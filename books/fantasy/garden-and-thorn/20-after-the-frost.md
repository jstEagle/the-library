## 20. After the Frost

The thaw came in the night, without ceremony, the way forgiveness sometimes does.

Sera Vane stood at her window in the huntresses' barracks and watched the ice on the courtyard flagstones go soft and weep itself away. All across Vharrow, she knew, the same thing was happening — icicles letting go of eaves, snow sliding from rooftops with a sound like a long exhale held for three years. Somewhere beneath it all, the Thornwood was breathing again.

She had not slept. None of them had. The palace bells had rung until dawn — first the alarm, then the all-clear, then, unbelievably, something no one under forty had ever heard: the joy-bells, the ones cast for coronations and the ends of wars. A king waking. A realm unbound. A green light going out of the world like a candle cupped by gentle hands.

Her hands were still shaking. She kept them wrapped around a cup of tea that had gone cold an hour ago, because holding something helped.

*He's alive,* she thought, for perhaps the four-hundredth time since sunrise. *Dominic is alive.* She'd seen him carried out of the throne room on his grandmother's arm, gray-faced and empty as a drained cup, and for one terrible quarter-hour she had believed — but no. He breathed. His heart beat. It simply did so without the thing that had always hummed underneath it, the guild-gift he'd never been allowed to name. He was magicless now, and alive, and when they'd laid him down in the infirmary he had looked up at her with eyes that were only his own and said, hoarsely:

"Well. I suppose I'm just a charm-seller now."

And then, before she could decide whether to laugh or cry: "The charms never worked anyway. You knew that, right?"

She had kissed him instead of answering. It seemed like the correct procedure.

* * *

They brought Corvane to judgment on the third day of the thaw, in the great hall where the king's council had once sat around a table slowly being strangled by ivy.

Sera stood witness among the huntresses, in her grays, her oath-medallion heavy at her throat. The hall was full — courtiers who still bore faint vine-scars beneath their collars, Warden officers who had served Corvane without knowing what they served, common folk let in through the doors because the new decree said the crown's justice belonged to the crown's people. Maren sat near the front, thin from her weeks in a cell, arms crossed, wearing the expression of a woman prepared to be deeply unimpressed.

Corvane walked in unchained.

That had been his own request, Sera learned later. He came up the aisle straight-backed, gray-haired, his Warden blacks brushed and buttoned, and if there was anything left of the Verdant in him it showed only as a certain terrible clarity in his eyes. He stopped before the dais, bowed to the king — a precise, correct bow — and waited.

King Aldric looked older than his years now, which was its own kind of honesty. He sat without the crown. It lay on a cushion beside him, and Sera noticed that everyone in the hall gave it a wide berth, the way you'd avoid a sleeping animal of uncertain temper.

"Warden-Captain Corvane," the king said. "You are accused of treason, of murder, of the deaths of forty-one citizens burned under false writs, and of serving a power not of this realm against your sworn sovereign. Do you deny any of it?"

"No, Your Majesty." Corvane's voice filled the hall without effort. "I would add only this: I did not know what wore you. I felt it. For six years I felt it — the cold coming off you in council, the way the seasons bent wrong — and I told myself it was grief, illness, the burden of rule. I chose not to look closer, because looking closer would have demanded action, and action might have cost me my position." He paused. "Cowardice is also a crime. I plead guilty to that one first."

A murmur ran through the hall. Beside the dais, Dominic sat with his grandmother — who had appeared at the palace gates two days ago leaning on a staff and daring anyone to comment — and Sera saw his jaw tighten. Forty-one burnings. He had spent his whole life hiding from those fires.

"You hunted mages," the king said quietly. "On my writs."

"I did. And the writs were ash in more ways than one, Majesty, because the man signing them had a forest growing behind his eyes." Corvane turned, slowly, and his gaze crossed the hall — crossed Sera, and stopped.

She made herself meet it.

"Huntress Vane," he said. "You testified to the Queen's Guard that you saw something move beneath the king's skin. Four years ago. In autumn."

"I did." Her voice came out steady. She was almost proud of it.

"And what happened to that testimony?"

"It was buried," Sera said. "By me. I withdrew it. I told my commander I'd been mistaken — that I'd seen candlelight through wine glass." She heard her own voice flatten. "I was afraid. I had already given the Wardens one person I loved. I could not — " She stopped. Started again. "I chose my fear over my duty. That testimony might have begun all this four years early. Whatever judgment falls on me for it, I'll bear it."

The hall went very quiet. Corvane regarded her for a long moment, and then — horribly, wonderfully — he nodded, once, like a soldier acknowledging a clean report.

"There it is," he said, to the whole hall. "That is what the Verdant fed on. Not power. Fear. Every one of us who saw and said nothing, who suspected and looked away — we built its winter plank by plank. I built more planks than most. Judge me accordingly."

The king pronounced sentence that afternoon: Corvane would live, stripped of rank, sentenced to labor rebuilding what the Long Winter had broken — roads, granaries, the flood-drowned villages along the Thawline. "You will spend the rest of your life repairing," Aldric said, "the things you helped break. If you find that merciful, good. It isn't meant to be easy. It's meant to be *useful*."

Corvane accepted it with another of those precise bows. Passing Sera on his way out, he paused.

"The brother," he said, low, so only she heard. "Tomas Vane. Hedge-craft, hearth-charms, nothing harmful. I signed the writ myself. I have signed a great many writs." He met her eyes. "When the ban lifts — and it will lift — see that his name comes off the rolls of criminals. He should never have been on them."

Then he walked out into the sunlight, and Sera stood very still, and discovered she was crying, and did not entirely mind.

* * *

The lifting of the ban happened on the seventh day, in the same hall, before a smaller crowd — though somehow the important people had all found their way in. Dominic sat beside Sera, still pale, still moving carefully, as though testing whether his body belonged to him. His grandmother occupied a chair that had clearly been fetched specifically so she wouldn't insist on standing.

King Aldric rose. He did not wear the crown for this either; Sera wondered if he ever would again.

"Sixty years ago," he said, "this kingdom outlawed magic, because we were afraid of it. We were right to be afraid of power. We were wrong to believe fear could keep us safe." He looked out over the room, and his gaze found Dominic and held there. "For six years, something ancient bound itself to my crown and drank this realm dry while I slept inside my own body. I do not remember most of it. I remember enough. And I know that the order we outlawed — the guild we burned and scattered and drove into cellars and sewers — is the only reason I am standing here a free man."

He stepped down from the dais. The whole hall held its breath.

He stopped in front of Dominic Delacroix, charm-seller, and — to the visible horror of three protocol officers — knelt.

Not fully. But enough. Enough that gasps went around the room like wind through wheat.

"I am told the guild's medallion passes down a line," the king said. "I am told you carry the last one." Dominic's hand went, reflexively, to his collar, where the medallion hung — the old silver sigil, worn smooth by three generations of hidden thumbs. "I cannot restore what was taken. I can only return what was stolen: your right to exist. The ban on magic is ended. The guild's crimes are struck from the rolls — every name, every record, every family. And to the guild, and to you, and to your grandmother—" here his voice did actually waver, and Sera squeezed Dominic's hand hard enough to hurt— "I ask forgiveness. From a dead woman's order, and from the last man carrying its name. I ask it knowing I have no right to it."

Silence. Long enough that Sera could hear the meltwater dripping somewhere beyond the windows, patient as a heartbeat.

Dominic looked at his grandmother. His grandmother looked at the ceiling with the expression of a woman deciding whether a king was worth the effort of a nod. Then she nodded — one sharp dip of the chin, a queen granting a favor — and Dominic turned back to the kneeling king, and Sera watched him struggle visibly between awe and the overwhelming urge to make a joke.

What he said was: "Get up, Your Majesty. You'll catch cold. It's spring, but barely."

And the hall laughed — a rusty, disbelieving sound, the first true laughter Sera had heard in the palace in years — and the king got up, and embraced a charm-seller in front of God and the court, and it was done.

Afterward, in the corridor, Dominic leaned against the wall and shook for a while, and Sera held onto him and let him.

"A school," he said finally, into her shoulder. "She wants to build a school. Out in the Thornwood. She says the guild died hiding, so the new one has to live in the open. She wants to teach hedge-craft to farm kids. *Farm kids*, Sera. Kids whose parents will hand them over to be taught by the same order that used to be burned." He pulled back and looked at her, and his face was doing several things at once. "And I can't cast so much as a warming-cantrip anymore. I poured all of it into the unbinding. Everything. What am I? What kind of master of a guild can't—"

"Dominic." She took his face in both hands. "You talked a dying courier's map out of a locked city, survived frost-wraiths, out-argued my entire chain of command, healed a child with borrowed craft, severed a god, and made a king laugh in his own throne room. You are going to be *insufferable* as a teacher."

He laughed — wetly, but he laughed.

"And for the record," Sera added, "I've decided something. When we go out to the Thornwood — and we're going, aren't we, don't give me that look — I'm coming. I resigned this morning."

His eyes went wide. "Sera. Your order. Your whole life—"

"My whole life was penance," she said. "I'm done doing penance. I'm told the guild forgave worse." She smiled, and meant it, and felt the truth of it settle into her bones like warmth after the long cold. "Besides. Someone has to teach the students how to fight. Your grandmother scares them, you can't demonstrate anything, and Maren refuses to wake up before noon."

"Maren's joining?"

"Maren negotiated a title. 'Mistress of Practical Applications.' She says it means she gets to blow things up for pedagogical purposes."

* * *

Evening found them on the palace roof, where the whole city lay spread below, steaming gently in the sunset like a loaf fresh from the oven. The snow was going fast. Somewhere a cart-driver was cursing the mud, cheerfully, at volume — the first honest complaint about ordinary weather Vharrow had heard in three years, and it sounded like music.

Dominic turned the medallion over in his fingers. Catching the last light, the sigil threw a small bright coin of gold onto his cheek.

"I keep waiting to feel empty," he said. "Everyone keeps looking at me like I should. Like I gave up an arm." He turned it over again. "But mostly I feel — lighter? Is that awful? The Verdant thanked me, Sera. It *thanked* me, while it faded. And the land is coming back, and the king is weeping with gratitude, and I'm sitting here thinking the worst part is that I'll never again feel what it felt like when the magic stirred." He glanced at her, sheepish. "Does that make me a fraud?"

"It makes you someone who paid a price and lived," Sera said. "Frauds don't pay."

He was quiet a moment. Then: "My grandmother says the medallion was never really about the casting. She says it marks the keeper. The one who holds the guild's memory when everyone else forgets." He looked down at it. "I used to think that was just something old people say to comfort the crippled ones."

"Says the man who's about to teach children that magic is a garden, not a weapon."

"A garden," he agreed softly. "With thorns. There's always thorns. That's rather the point of the book, isn't it?" He slipped the medallion back under his collar, where it rested against his heart, warm from his hand. "First students arrive next month. Grandmother's already drafted a curriculum. Lesson one: how to hide nothing, because hiding is how the last guild died."

Below them, the city glowed — ten thousand hearths lit against the last cool nights, smoke rising straight and unafraid. Somewhere in that sprawl, a boy was lighting a candle with a snap of his fingers and freezing in terror, waiting for the knock on the door that would never come again.

Sera leaned her head on Dominic's shoulder and watched the smoke rise.

"We should write to that boy," she murmured. "Whoever he is. Tell him he can come to the Thornwood. Tell him the garden's open."

Dominic smiled against her hair. "We will. First letter the new guild ever sends." He paused. "Right after grandmother's list of supplies. Which is apparently eleven pages long."

And far to the north, past the walls and the fields and the recovering pinewastes, the Thornwood stood bare-branched and patient in the softening air — waiting, as gardens do, for someone to come and plant something in it.
