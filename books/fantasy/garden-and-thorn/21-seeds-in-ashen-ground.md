## 21. Seeds in Ashen Ground

The first frost of the new year came on a night in late autumn, and Dominic stood in the garden to watch it fail.

It touched the bean rows and slid off them. It silvered the fence posts and the old well cover and the crooked gate that Sera kept promising to fix, but when it reached the beds where the guild's seedlings slept under straw, it simply stopped — a thin white line drawn across the earth like a border no one had agreed to honor. Beyond that line, the soil stayed dark and soft, breathing its slow green breath into the cold air.

"The land remembers," his grandmother had said, back when she'd still been alive to say such things, back before they'd buried her beneath the rowan at the garden's edge. She'd said it the way she said most things: as though it were obvious and everyone else was being deliberately slow. "You don't have to teach it. You just have to stop telling it to forget."

Dominic pulled his coat tighter and crouched by the frost line, close enough that his breath fogged over the warm ground. He could not feel what the students would soon learn to feel. That was the strange arithmetic of his new life: he had poured everything he was into the unbinding, every scrap of guild-gift in his blood, and woken the next morning empty as a drained cup. He could not coax a sprout. He could not warm a stone. He could not so much as make a dandelion wilt out of spite, which he had tried, once, on a particularly smug dandelion growing through the flagstones by the kitchen door.

But he could see the frost line. He could read the way the Thornwood's recovering shade leaned toward the school like a curious animal. And he could teach.

* * *

The school had begun, as most reckless things do, with a conversation at a kitchen table.

"You want to do *what*," Sera had said, setting down her tea with the deliberate care she usually reserved for loading a crossbow.

"A school." Dominic had spread his hands over the table, over the charred remains of Maren's map, which Maren insisted on keeping visible "for humility's sake." "Not a guild. No oaths, no secrets, no medallions hidden under floorboards. A school. Out here, in the open, where anyone can walk up to the door."

"Anyone," Sera repeated. "Including people who think hedge-craft is a crime."

"It isn't a crime anymore. The king lifted the ban."

"The king lifted the ban six months ago, after spending a year being strangled by a plant. People are slow to forgive the thing that nearly ate their court, Dom. Half of Vharrow still burns candles outside Warden houses like it's a festival."

"Then we start small," he'd said. "Three students. Four. The ones nobody else will take. Hedge-wives' daughters. Shepherd boys who talk to their flocks a little too well. Kids who got reported by their neighbors and are now very confused about why the Wardens haven't come."

Sera had been quiet for a long moment. He knew what lived in that quiet — a brother's name, a pyre, a confession made by firelight on a snow road that felt like another lifetime. When she spoke again, her voice was steady. "Someone has to vet them. Someone has to know which ones are running from something worse than the law."

"That's why I asked you first."

"And Maren?"

"Maren gets to be in charge of everything and complain about it, which is her love language."

From the corner of the room, where she'd been ostensibly mending a net and actually eavesdropping for the last ten minutes, Maren had snorted. "My love language is threatening to leave. But fine. Fine. Someone has to keep you two from naming it something ridiculous."

They had named it something ridiculous anyway. Or rather, the students had. The sign above the gate — carved by a fourteen-year-old named Pip who had more enthusiasm than chisel technique — read THE GARDEN AND THORN SCHOOL, with the T slightly larger than everything else because Pip had run out of room and refused to start over. Dominic had offered to fix it. Pip had looked at him with such wounded pride that he'd pretended the asymmetry was intentional, a lesson about imperfection, probably.

* * *

The first students arrived on a gray morning in early spring, three of them, walking up the forest road from Vharrow with their belongings in bundles.

There was Pip himself, all elbows and questions, whose gift was so modest it barely qualified — he could make cut flowers last a day longer than they should — but whose hunger to learn was bottomless and occasionally alarming. There was Odile, a fisherman's daughter with a burn scar down one arm and a habit of watching doors, whose magic moved water the way other people breathed, without thought or permission, and who flinched whenever anyone praised her for it. And there was Bram, who was forty years old, a widowed farrier with forearms like ham hocks, who had spent his whole life hiding the fact that iron told him things — where it had been, what it wanted to be — and who had walked three days to get there because his late wife had made him promise, before the ban lifted, that if it ever did, he would go.

Dominic met them at the gate, and the sight of them nearly undid him. Three strangers, carrying nothing but bundles and fear and hope, standing where a Delacroix had once been dragged away in chains. His grandmother's medallion hung under his shirt, warm against his chest, and he found himself gripping it like a talisman before remembering that he was the talisman now, or something like one.

"Welcome to the Garden and Thorn," he said. "The T is bigger on purpose. It's a lesson about imperfection."

Pip beamed. Odile glanced at the treeline, counting exits. Bram shook his hand with crushing sincerity and said, "Your grandmother burned my master's forge-cleanse sigil into an anvil once, back before the Sundering laws. Best work I ever saw. I've been trying to copy it for twenty years."

"She'd be insufferable about that," Dominic said, "if she were alive. Come in. We'll feed you first. Everything goes better fed."

* * *

Teaching, it turned out, was nothing like casting.

When he'd had the gift, magic had been instinct — a language he'd spoken without translating, inherited in the blood the way his grandmother's stubbornness had been. Now he had to stand beside Odile at the stream and explain, in ordinary words, how to ask the water to hold still instead of commanding it, and half the time his explanations were wrong, and she would frown at him and then do it perfectly by some route he couldn't perceive, and he would laugh and say, "Well. Don't listen to me, then. Listen to the water."

He learned to teach failure. That was the heart of it, he discovered — not the techniques, which changed from student to student like handwriting, but the relationship with failure. Pip's little workings collapsed constantly, and Pip took each collapse like a personal insult from the universe. Dominic sat him down after the third ruined attempt at a preservation charm and told him about the courier.

"I killed a man's last hour with clumsy magic," he said. "I was trying to ease his pain and I pushed too hard, and I'll carry that forever. The gift doesn't care how sorry you are afterward. So you learn to be careful *before*. Every collapse teaches you where your edges are. You're not failing, Pip. You're mapping yourself."

Pip had gone red-eyed and nodded furiously and gone back to work, and by summer he could keep a rose alive for a full week, and he presented it to Sera with the gravity of a knighthood, and Sera — who had once arrested people for exactly this — accepted it with a straight face and put it in water and let it die naturally, which Pip declared the proudest moment of his life.

Bram was slower, deeper. Iron spoke to him in long, patient sentences, and he didn't need technique so much as permission. Dominic gave him the forge on the second week and mostly stayed out of the way, and by midsummer Bram had re-hung the school's gate so that it swung true in any wind, singing a low note when it closed, and when Dominic asked him how, Bram shrugged and said, "It wanted to."

Odile was the hard one. Her gift frightened her — it had drowned things, once, when she was small and angry, and she had never forgiven herself or it. She practiced alone, at dawn, thinking no one watched. Dominic never let her catch him watching. Instead he left books where she'd find them, and one evening she appeared at his door, jaw set, and said, "Show me how to make it gentle. Not small. Gentle. There's a difference."

"There is," Dominic said, and felt his grandmother's hand on his shoulder across the years. "That might be the whole lesson, actually. Sit down."

* * *

Maren ran the practical side of the school with the efficiency of a woman who had spent decades running illegal safehouses and now found legal logistics almost insultingly easy. She kept accounts. She negotiated with Vharrow merchants who remembered her fondly and feared her appropriately. She cooked for eleven people without breaking stride and complained about every single meal in a tone that made clear the complaints were ceremonial.

She also, Dominic noticed, had taken to sitting with Bram in the evenings while he worked the forge, saying nothing, sharpening tools that did not need sharpening. When he raised an eyebrow at her about it, she said, "I'm supervising," and dared him with a look to make anything of it, and he wisely made nothing of it, and Sera made everything of it later, privately, with great delight.

The school grew the way gardens grow — unevenly, with weeds. By autumn there were nine students. A Warden came, once, officially, to inspect them, and turned out to be a hollow-cheeked woman named Captain Ives who had served under Corvane and surrendered with the rest, and who stood in the classroom doorway for a long moment before saying quietly, "Corvane used to say magic was a rot. He said it every day. Toward the end he had vines growing under his collar and he still said it." She left a list of names — children the Wardens had flagged over the years and never burned, because even Corvane's machine had sometimes hesitated. "They'll come eventually," she said. "Be ready."

Dominic pinned the list above his desk, next to the map, next to the sketch of the guild's lost sigil that Pip had drawn and gotten subtly wrong, which everyone agreed was fitting.

* * *

On the evening of the first frost-that-failed, the whole school ended up in the garden, because Pip had spotted the frost line from his window and shouted about it loud enough to wake the dead, possibly including the one buried under the rowan.

They gathered at the border of it — nine students, Maren with her arms crossed against the cold, Sera with a lantern — and stared at the impossible line where winter stopped.

"Is it the Verdant?" Odile asked carefully. After everything, none of them said the name casually. "What's left of it?"

"No," Dominic said. "The Verdant's gone. Freed and faded — it thanked me and went out like a candle. This is older and smaller than that." He crouched and pressed his palm flat against the warm soil, feeling nothing magical, feeling only warmth, which was enough. "This is the land remembering what we did. What the guild did, for four hundred years, and what it cost us to finish. Ground keeps accounts. It knows who paid."

"Can we learn to do that?" Pip asked. "Make a place remember?"

"You already are," Dominic said. "Every working you cast kindly, every time you mend instead of command — you're writing on it. In fifty years, some child will stand on this spot in winter and wonder why the beans never freeze, and there won't be anyone left who remembers why, and it won't matter. That's what a guild is, Pip. It's not the medallion. It's the ground remembering."

He hadn't meant to say it that loudly. The students had gone quiet in the way people do when they've been handed something heavier than expected. Maren cleared her throat and announced that supper was getting cold, which broke the spell as it always did, and they trooped inside in a noisy clatter, all except Sera.

She stayed by the frost line, lantern swinging gently from her hand, looking down the dark road toward Vharrow — toward the capital, where a mortal and chastened king ruled a realm still learning to trust itself, where Corvane awaited judgment behind walls that no longer whispered, where the queen's empty chair had finally been given a proper mourning.

"You do that," Dominic said, coming to stand beside her. "Look down the road like it owes you money."

"Old habit." She leaned into him, shoulder to shoulder, the way she'd learned to instead of the way she'd once forced herself not to. "A rider came today while you were teaching. From the palace."

Dominic went still. Old reflexes died hard; for a heartbeat he was back in a burning shop with a dying courier's blood on his hands. "Bad news?"

"Depends." Sera turned the lantern so its light fell on a letter sealed with the royal crest — but stamped beside it, newly cut, was a second seal: a tree with a single thorn. "The king's established a commission. To study what happened. To study *him* — what was done to him, whether anything of the Verdant survived in the land, in the bloodlines, in the old binding sites. They want records. Guild records." She paused. "And they want a Delacroix to advise them. Formally. With a seat at court."

The wind moved through the Thornwood above them, and somewhere in its recovering shade, a bird sang a winter song it had no business knowing.

"They also want a huntress," Sera said. "Someone the old order trusts. Someone who's seen both sides of the law." She looked up at him, firelight and lantern light mixing in her eyes, and her mouth curved into the expression he had learned meant she'd already decided and was only waiting to see if he'd catch up. "They want us, Dom. Both of us. In Vharrow. Where it started."

Dominic looked back at the school — at the windows glowing with lamplight, at the sound of Maren bullying nine children and one farrier toward the supper table, at the warm dark line in the earth where winter was politely refused.

"Well," he said. "It seems the ground remembers us too."
