## 22. Garden and Thorn

The morning of the planting came in gold and grey, the kind of autumn light that Vharrow had almost forgotten how to make. Dominic Delacroix stood at the edge of the guild's new garden with a spade in his hands and dirt already under his nails, watching the frost retreat from the grass like a tide going out.

A year ago, frost had not retreated from anything.

He still caught himself marveling at ordinary weather. Rain that fell because clouds wanted it to fall. A spring that arrived on schedule instead of trickling in green and wrong, out of season, fed by something hungry beneath the crown. Even now, walking the rows of the garden he and Sera had broken ground on back in the thaw, he half-expected the soil to go grey under his boots, to feel that faint wrongness in the air like a held breath.

It never did. The land simply breathed.

"You're staring at the dirt again," Sera said behind him. She came up the path carrying two mugs of cider and her sword at her hip, because some habits were load-bearing. "Maren says if you keep doing that, the turnips will get self-conscious."

"The turnips should be so lucky." He took the mug. It was warm, spiced with cloves Maren had hoarded through three bad winters and was now spending recklessly, as though she'd decided scarcity was a superstition she could finally afford to drop. "I'm not staring. I'm listening."

"And what does the dirt say?"

"That we planted the beans too close to the wall again."

Sera snorted into her cider. "The dirt is right. Your grandmother would have said the same thing, only louder, and with more swearing."

Dominic smiled, but the smile settled into something quieter as his eyes went to the far corner of the garden — the corner they'd left empty all year. The grass there grew long and pale, untouched by the hoe, waiting for what today would give it.

His grandmother's grave.

She'd been buried at midwinter, when the ground was iron and the whole city had come out to watch them lower her — the last master of the guild, the woman who'd spent forty years in exile guarding a blade no one remembered how to use. Half of Vharrow had stood in the snow. Old charm-sellers who'd bought hedge-craft from her before the Sundering. Warden veterans who'd once hunted her and now took off their hoods in her honor. The king himself had come, mortal and grey and weeping quietly, and had said nothing at all, which Dominic had understood to be the most honest eulogy anyone offered that day.

They'd marked her with a stone and nothing else, because she'd left instructions: *no monument until the garden grows. Bury me where you'll plant the tree. If I'm going to be useful, I may as well be useful.*

Typical of her. Even dead, she'd found a way to assign him homework.

"Spade," Sera said, holding it out. "Before Maren finishes the eulogy and starts assigning us chores."

* * *

Maren's eulogy lasted eleven minutes and covered, in order: the guild's founding; the guild's destruction; the guild's rebirth; the correct depth for planting an oak; and a pointed reminder that the school's first class began in nine days and neither of them had finished building the desks.

"She'd have hated every word," Maren concluded, dabbing her eyes with a sleeve already stiff with garden mud. "Which is why I'm saying it. She only ever tolerated praise when it was inaccurate enough to be funny."

"She did," Dominic agreed. His throat was tight. "Once, a merchant told her she was the finest hedge-witch in the northern provinces, and she laughed so hard she pulled a muscle in her neck."

"And then charged him double for the love-charm," Sera said.

"Triple. It was a terrible love-charm. She made it deliberately, to punish him for flattery." He crouched at the edge of the empty plot, working the spade in. The earth turned dark and rich, smelling of rain and rot and growing things — the honest smell of it, nothing green and hungry underneath. Just soil. Just the world, minding its own business.

They dug together, the three of them, taking turns, and it felt less like a funeral than like the work itself: the same work they'd done all year, clearing the Thornwood's edge, hauling stone for the schoolhouse, teaching the first brave few — a baker's daughter with a knack for warming bread, a former Warden recruit whose hands shook until Maren put a seed-charm in them and they went quiet — that magic was not a crime but a craft, and crafts could be learned by anyone patient enough to fail at them properly.

When the hole was deep enough, they lowered the young oak. Its roots were wrapped in burlap, and its branches were bare and crooked, and it looked, frankly, unimpressive — a stick with ambitions.

"It'll grow," Sera said, catching his look.

"I know it'll grow. I just wish it looked less like a fence post."

"Your grandmother looked like a fence post with opinions, and she grew on everyone eventually."

He laughed — really laughed, the kind that loosened something in his chest — and knelt to pack the soil around the roots with his bare hands. That part he wanted to do himself. No gloves. No magic, even if he'd had any. Just hands and earth and the slow, stubborn work of putting something in the ground and believing it would come up.

Halfway through the packing, his fingers brushed something at the root's edge.

Small. Smooth. Warm.

He drew it out carefully: a single shoot, no taller than his thumb, two leaves unfurled like small green hands. It hadn't been there when they'd dug. He was certain of it. And around its base, the soil was soft and dark and utterly free of frost — though the morning's cold lay white on everything within a handspan of it.

The leaves trembled in a wind that didn't reach the rest of the garden.

"Oh," Maren said softly. She'd gone very still. "Oh, you clever old woman. You planned this."

"What is it?" Sera asked, though Dominic suspected she knew. He knew. He'd known since the moment he touched it — known the way you know a voice in another room.

"The Verdant's last gift," he said. "From the unbinding. When it—" His voice caught. A year later, and he could still see it: the vast green consciousness fading from the king's body, unraveling into light and leaf-mold, and the moment before it went, when it had pressed something small and warm into his hands — into his *magic*, into the last of it — and he'd felt gratitude, enormous and ancient and gentle, pass through him like water through a net.

*For the mercy,* it had seemed to say. *For choosing the harder kindness.*

Then it was gone, and so was his gift, poured out entirely into the severing, and he had woken in the ruins of the throne room magicless for the first time since childhood — hollowed and grieving and, somehow, free.

But the Verdant had left one thing behind. One seed of itself, tucked somewhere safe. And apparently, it had chosen to spend it here.

"It waited," Dominic said wonderingly. "All year. It waited until we planted the tree. Until the grave was dug. Until—"

"Until it had somewhere to belong," Maren finished. She knelt beside him, careful not to touch the shoot. "That's what the old texts never understood about it. Not evil. Not good. Just starving, and bound, and bargaining for a home. Your grandmother figured that out at the end. I think that's why she softened toward the unbinding, near the last."

Dominic looked at the little shoot — the thing that had worn a king, that had frozen a realm, that had nearly ended everything — and saw only two green leaves turning gently toward the sun.

"We can't leave it here," Sera said. "Can we? It's still—"

"It's a shoot," Dominic said. "One shoot. Whatever it becomes, it becomes slowly, and honestly, and in plain sight of everyone who tends this garden." He glanced up at her, and the ghost of his grandmother's humor crossed his face. "Besides. Every garden needs a thorn. Otherwise how do you know the roses are worth the trouble?"

Sera rolled her eyes, but she was smiling. "You've been saving that line."

"For a year."

* * *

They built a low ring of stones around the shoot — around both of them now, the oak and the Verdant's remnant, sharing one grave and one patch of earth. Maren pronounced it botanically reckless and symbolically perfect, and went off to yell at the desk-builders.

By late afternoon the rest of the day's work was done, and the garden had filled with the quiet traffic of the guild's new life: students hauling water, arguing about moon-phases, a boy from the river district showing off a cantrip that made dandelions sneeze. Openly. In daylight. With a Warden liaison — Sera's old second, Tam, gray-haired and reformed — watching from the gate with an expression of profound relief that none of it was happening in cellars anymore.

Dominic sat on the schoolhouse step with Sera as the light went long and amber. Her shoulder rested against his. Somewhere past the wall, Vharrow rang its evening bells, and the sound carried clean across a city that no longer flinched at shadows.

"Do you ever miss it?" she asked. "The magic?"

He considered the question honestly, because she deserved honesty and because lying to a huntress was a poor survival strategy even in peacetime.

"Sometimes. Like missing a language you used to dream in." He turned his medallion over in his fingers — his grandmother's guild sigil, worn smooth now from a year of handling. "But mostly I miss it the way you miss a fever. It was brilliant while it burned. And then it was gone, and what was left was just... me. And it turns out me is enough. Especially—" he nudged her shoulder with his, "—especially with backup."

"Backup," Sera repeated. "You severed an ancient entity from the soul of a king, and I'm *backup*."

"You kept Corvane busy for twenty minutes. That's basically a saga."

"He broke my nose."

"A memorable saga."

She laughed, and leaned into him more fully, and for a while they watched the students fumble their way toward competence in the failing light. The oak stood bare against the sky. Beside it, the little green shoot glowed faintly in the dusk, unhurt by the coming cold, patient as anything that has learned to wait.

"Nine days," Sera said. "First class. Are you nervous?"

"Terrified. I can't cast a single spell. What kind of teacher does that make me?"

"The kind who knows exactly what it costs." She said it simply, without pity, and it landed like a blessing. "The best ones always do."

Down in the garden, the boy with the dandelion cantrip sneezed spectacularly, and his classmates cheered, and Maren's voice rose above it all scolding someone about proper seed storage, and the bells rang on, and the frost crept up the wall and stopped — stopped dead — precisely at the ring of stones where the Verdant's last gift kept its small eternal spring.

Dominic watched it a long moment. Then he reached into his pocket, took out the seed-charm Maren had given him a year ago at a price he'd gladly paid, and pressed it into the soil at the ring's edge — a first enrollment, of sorts.

The shoot's leaves turned toward it, almost like a nod.

"Well," he said quietly. "Welcome to the guild."

Above the grave, the bare oak — impossibly, gently, in defiance of every season and every law Dominic had ever learned — put out a single new bud.

And in the morning, there would be desks to finish.
