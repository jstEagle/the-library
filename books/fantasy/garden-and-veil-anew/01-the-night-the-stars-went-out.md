## 1. The Night the Stars Went Out

The night market of Greyhollow smelled of tallow smoke, wet wool, and the particular desperation of people who had learned to want small things.

Emrys Ashgrove knew that smell the way they knew their own heartbeat. Twenty years behind a stall in Lantern Row, and the city's hunger had become as familiar to them as the grain of the counter beneath their palms—worn smooth by generations of sellers who had come before, all of them peddling hope in one form or another. Emrys sold it in the shape of charms: knotted red thread against bad luck, river-stones drilled and strung for safe travel, little glass beads that caught lamplight and threw it back in colors no honest flame could make.

None of it was real magic. That was the point. That had always been the point.

"Charm for a toothache," said a woman with flour ground so deep into her knuckles it looked like frost. She didn't wait for an answer; she never did. She set her coin on the counter and took the little cloth bag Emrys handed over without looking at their face. Nobody looked at faces on Lantern Row. That was another kind of safety, and Emrys had spent two decades learning to be grateful for it.

"Blessings on your house," Emrys said, because that was what charm-sellers said, and the woman grunted and moved along into the crowd.

*It isn't even a blessing,* Emrys thought, not for the first time. *It's dried yarrow and a prayer I don't say out loud.* But the toothache would ease anyway—yarrow was good for that—and the woman would sleep, and tomorrow she'd tell her neighbor the charm-seller on Lantern Row had fixed her up proper, and the small economy of belief would turn another revolution. It was harmless. It was invisible. It was exactly the life Emrys had built for themselves, stone by careful stone, in a city where the Warden's edicts were painted on every wall in letters tall enough to read from horseback:

*MAGIC IS THEFT. CASTERS WILL BE FOUND.*

Twenty years since Emrys had last called anything. Twenty years since the storm. They kept the count the way other people counted coins or sins or children's birthdays—one number, always climbing, always there when they woke at three in the morning with the wind rattling the shutters like fingers on glass.

Tonight the wind was doing exactly that.

Emrys noticed it between customers—the way the air had gone strange, pressing against the market's noise like a listener leaning close to a door. The tallow flames in the hanging lanterns burned too still, as if the fire itself were holding its breath. Emrys's hands, sorting river-stones into neat rows, went quiet without any decision from their mind.

They knew this feeling. They had known it once, catastrophically, and had sworn on everything left of their name that they would never mistake it again.

*Pressure,* the old storm-callers had called it. The sky remembering what it wanted. A gathering, somewhere far off or very near, of all the wild charge that lived between the world's skin and its bones. Most people felt nothing. Hedge-witches felt a prickle. Storm-callers felt it the way a deaf man feels thunder through the floorboards—in the teeth, in the marrow, in the scar tissue of old grief.

Emrys felt it now, faint but unmistakable, threading up through the cobbles of Greyhollow like water finding cracks in a dam.

"No charm tonight," said a voice, and Emrys looked up into the round, worried face of Old Tammas the pie-man, who occupied the stall two down and had been selling questionable meat in trustworthy pastry for longer than Emrys had been in the city. "Got a question instead. You feel that?"

"Feel what?" Emrys said, mild as milk. Their hands, below the counter's edge, had closed around the edge of the wood hard enough to whiten the knuckles.

"The air." Tammas squinted upward, past the awnings, past the rooftops, to where the sky should have been showing its usual handful of winter stars through the smoke-haze. "Like the whole city's holding its breath. My dog wouldn't come out tonight. Wouldn't cross the threshold. Just stood there looking at me like I was mad for trying."

"Dogs are sensible," Emrys said. "Cold night. Buy a warming charm?"

Tammas bought a warming charm he almost certainly didn't need, because Tammas was kind in the way of men who had decided long ago that the charm-seller on Lantern Row ate poorly, and moved off into the crowd shaking his head at the sky.

Emrys exhaled slowly and made themselves count the stones again. One row of seven. Another. The pressure wasn't growing, exactly. It was *settling*, the way snow settles, silently and everywhere at once, until the branch snaps under a weight that fell flake by flake.

*It's nothing,* they told themselves, in the flat tone they used for such lies. *Wards hum. Cities hum. You've felt a hundred nights like this and the stars came up on schedule every time.*

Except they hadn't felt a hundred nights like this. They had felt one, twenty years ago, on a green hill under a wide sky, and the memory of it lived in Emrys's body the way a burn lives in a hand: not thought about, but consulted, constantly, by every movement.

A child stopped at the stall—a boy of maybe eight, wrapped in an adult's coat, holding a coin in his fist like something he'd been told to guard with his life. Behind him hovered a mother with the gray, stretched look of someone working double shifts in a dye-house.

"He wants the blue one," the mother said. "The one that glows."

"It doesn't glow," Emrys said gently. "It catches light. There's a difference."

"What's the difference?" the boy asked, solemn as a magistrate.

Emrys held the bead up between two fingers. Lamplight slid through the glass and came out the other side green-gold, trembling. "A thing that glows makes its own light," they said. "A thing that catches light only shows you what's already there."

The boy considered this with his whole face. Then he leaned close, so his mother couldn't hear, and whispered: "My gran says you're a witch."

Emrys's heart performed a slow, cold somersault. Their face did nothing at all. Twenty years of practice could do remarkable things with a face.

"Gran says a lot of things," Emrys said, handing the bead to the mother, taking the coin. "Tell her the yarrow's fresh this week."

"She says the wards keep witches out but they can't keep 'em from being *born* here." The boy delivered this with the relish of forbidden knowledge. "She says the sky's going to fall someday and then we'll see."

"The sky's held up fine for a hundred years," the mother said sharply, tugging him away. "Thank you. Come along, Ollie."

Emrys watched them go and let the breath out through their nose, carefully, the way one releases a bird one has been strangling by accident.

*The wards,* they thought. And there it was again, that pressure, and now that they had a name to hang on it, they could not stop feeling it: the great ward-lattice that ringed Greyhollow like a cage rings a songbird, humming its flat, dead note day and night, the note that had smothered Emrys's own gift into silence for two decades. Everyone in the city lived inside that hum without hearing it, the way fish live inside water without knowing the word for wet.

But storm-callers heard it. Storm-callers had always heard it. And tonight, for the first time in twenty years, the hum sounded—

Thin. Stretched. Like a rope bearing more weight than it was tied for.

Emrys looked up.

Between the rooftops, in the gap above Tanner's Lane where the smoke thinned enough to see through, the winter stars hung in their familiar scatter. The Shepherd's Crook. The pale eye of Vessel. The little tight cluster the dockhands called the Seven Sisters' Knitting. Emrys had charted them nightly for twenty years, not from love—from vigilance. Stars were the same in every realm; the Veil ran beneath them both. As long as the stars shone, the world's floor was solid.

They were shining now. Ordinary. Indifferent.

Emrys nodded once, to themselves, and turned back to the stall, and was reaching for the knotting cord when the pressure *broke*.

Not faded. Not eased. Broke—like a note held too long on a cracked bell, the sound going wrong all at once. Emrys's hand froze mid-air. Down the row, a lantern flame stood perfectly upright, rigid as a nail. Somewhere a dog began to howl, high and continuous, and was joined by another, and another, until the whole dark edge of the city was singing with them.

And Emrys felt—felt with the marrow-deep sense they had spent twenty years starving—the wards *shatter*.

There was no sound a normal ear could catch. But Emrys felt it pass through the city like a wave through a sheet shaken at both ends: the great lattice, the Warden's pride, the cage that had held them prisoner and safe for twenty years, tearing along some seam too deep for any eye to find. It didn't collapse. It *unraveled*. Emrys felt each thread part, felt the flat dead hum gutter and die, and underneath it rose a sound—or a sensation, or a grief—that Emrys had not heard since childhood and knew instantly, the way one knows a mother's voice:

The Veil. Singing. Out of tune.

"Oh," Emrys said aloud, to nobody, in the old tongue of a country that had exiled them. "*Oh, no.*"

Then the stars went out.

All of them. At once. No flicker, no dimming, no slow fade like clouds crossing. One heartbeat the Shepherd's Crook hung bright above Tanner's Lane; the next the sky was a single unbroken black, total and smooth, as if the night had been painted over while no one was looking.

The market did not go silent all at once. Silence spread through it like ink through water. First the hush of voices faltering mid-word. Then a woman's sharp laugh cut short. Then the pie-man's stall going quiet, and the knife-grinder's wheel spinning down, and somewhere near the fountain a dropped cup breaking with a sound too loud by half.

Then everyone looked up.

Emrys looked up with them. They stood among the crowd—shoulder to shoulder with dyers and drovers and thieves and children—and for the first time in twenty years Emrys Ashgrove was exactly as visible as every other soul in Greyhollow, all of them staring upward together into a starless black, and the invisibility they had built their life upon dissolved around them like sugar in rain.

Because Emrys knew what empty skies meant. Every hedge-witch's granddaughter knew the stories, and Emrys knew the truth beneath them: the stars were not lights. They were *stitches*. The points where the Veil between realms was sewn tightest, pinned through both worlds like a hem through cloth. When the stitches held, the sky showed its little lights and the worlds lay side by side, separate, safe.

When the stitches failed, the hem came loose. And what came through a loose hem was the long winter, the hungering dark, the cold that ate memory first and light second and left cities standing full of people who had forgotten their own names.

*That's a story,* Emrys told themselves. *That's a fireside story for frightening children, and the wards just flickered, and the stars are behind cloud—*

There was no cloud. Emrys could feel the air, dry and clear and bitter-cold, and feel—oh, they wished they couldn't—the vast thin *tear* overhead, running from horizon to horizon like a seam ripped open, bleeding cold into the world.

Around them the market found its voice again, and the voice was fear.

"Witchcraft," someone spat. "This is caster work—"

"The Warden will fix it. The Warden's got engines, the Warden's got—"

"My stars. Where are my *stars*—" A young woman was crying, hands pressed to her mouth, staring up as though the blackness might blink and give them back.

"Everyone inside! Curfew'll come early tonight, mark me—"

Emrys stood in the middle of it, numb, their stock of charms spread out before them—knots and stones and glass beads, twenty years of harmless nothing—and felt the old power stir in their chest like a sleeper turning over. It had waited patiently behind the ward-hum for two decades. Now the hum was gone, and it woke hungry, and it recognized the tear in the sky the way a dog recognizes its master's killer.

*No,* Emrys told it. *No. We don't. We had our one storm. We paid for it. We do not—*

Above Tanner's Lane, in the absolute black, something flickered. Not a star. Too low, too green, too brief. A ripple, like light on the underside of ice.

Only Emrys saw it. Only Emrys understood it. The Veil, thin as paper in that spot, flexing—showing, for half a heartbeat, the *other side*. Whatever waited there. Whatever cold was already leaning its weight against the worn places in the world's hem.

The crowd saw nothing. The crowd was busy being afraid of the wrong things—of casters, of curfews, of the Warden's wrath. In a few days, Emrys knew with sick certainty, the inquisitors would be through Lantern Row with their iron censers and their lists, hunting someone to blame for the darkened sky. Someone residual. Someone suspicious. Someone whose grandmother whispered about witches.

Someone like a charm-seller whose hands shook too much tonight, whose eyes kept going to the sky, who had gone white as whey while everyone else merely panicked.

Emrys began, mechanically, to pack up the stall. Stones into the box. Cord coiled. Beads counted twice—they lost count, started again, lost it again. Their hands knew the motions; the rest of them was somewhere else, standing on a green hill twenty years distant, watching lightning walk on legs of white fire and hearing a voice cry out their name in a way that meant the world had ended.

*Invisible,* they had promised themselves. *Small. Harmless. A life so quiet the Warden's own hounds would walk past it.*

The stars were out. The wards were gone. The Veil was torn. There was no quiet left anywhere in the world to hide inside.

Emrys closed the box, latched it, and stood a moment longer with their palm flat on the lid, head bowed, while the starless cold settled over Greyhollow like a hand.

Down the row, Old Tammas was blowing out his lanterns. "Going to be a long winter," he called over, attempting cheer. "Best get home, friend. Sky's acting unnatural."

"Aye," Emrys said. Their voice came out steady, which surprised them. "Long winter."

They lifted the box and turned toward the narrow stair that led up to their rooms above the shop—and stopped.

Frost was creeping across the cobblestones. Fast. Far too fast for a cold snap, racing outward from the fountain in feathered white branches, silvering the stalls, the awnings, the abandoned cups. The crowd gasped and shrank back from it, and Emrys understood why: the frost was moving *deliberately*, spreading in patterns too regular for weather, spirals and interlocking lines like script, like writing, like—

Emrys went cold in a way that had nothing to do with the air.

Because they recognized the pattern. Deep in the oldest room of their heart, in the place where a mother's voice still lived, they recognized it: frost-script, the calling-geometry of the northern covens, the shapes their mother had traced in window-ice to teach them letters before they could hold a pen.

The frost reached the base of Emrys's stall and stopped. Spread itself flat. Held.

And in the silvered cobbles at Emrys's feet, written in characters twenty years out of use, in a hand Emrys would know anywhere in any world, the dying Veil spoke its first word to the last storm-caller in Greyhollow:

*Emrys.*

Their childhood name. Their true name. The name no living soul in this city had ever spoken.

Emrys stood very still, the charm-box heavy in their arms, and did not run, though every instinct screamed at them to run. Around them the crowd was fleeing the frost, streaming toward the lit streets, shouting for the Warden, for the watch, for anyone. Above them the sky stayed black and torn and bleeding cold.

Emrys looked down at the name written in ice by a hand twenty years buried, and felt the last wall of their carefully hidden life crumble to nothing.

Whatever came next, invisibility was over.
