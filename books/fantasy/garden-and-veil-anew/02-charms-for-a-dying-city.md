## 2. Charms for a Dying City

Greyhollow woke wrong.

Emrys knew the city's mornings the way a sailor knows the pull of tides—the groan of shutters on Rope Lane, the first clatter of the fish-carts down from the harbor terraces, the particular quality of grey light that came through the smoke-haze and turned everything the color of old pewter. For twenty years that rhythm had been their clock and their cover. A city with a predictable heartbeat was a city in which one small charm-seller could go unnoticed, unremarked, unseen.

But this morning the heartbeat had gone arrhythmic. Emrys stood at the shuttered window of their rooms above the shop, hands wrapped around a cup of barley tea gone cold, and listened to Greyhollow scream in its sleep.

Not screaming, exactly. Not yet. It was the sound before screaming—the sound of a city trying to decide whether last night had been real. Doors opening and closing too quickly. Voices pitched low and urgent, the way people spoke when they didn't want to frighten themselves by hearing their own fear aloud. Somewhere down on Tallow Street, a woman was telling someone, over and over, *I saw it, I tell you, I saw them go out, all of them at once, like snuffed candles—*

Emrys drank the cold tea anyway. Ritual mattered. Routine was armor, and this morning they needed every plate of it they could buckle on.

The stars were still gone. Emrys had checked three times through the night, standing at the window like a mourner at a graveside, watching the black where the sky's familiar constellations should have been—the Shepherd's Crook, the Weeping Sisters, the pale scatter of the Lantern that sailors steered by. Nothing. Not cloud-cover; the night had been knife-clear, hard as hammered tin. The stars hadn't been hidden. They had been *extinguished*, all together, all at once, the way a lamp is extinguished—with intent.

And beneath that, fainter than memory, Emrys had felt the other thing: the border wards going down. Not broken—*dissolved*, unraveling thread by thread like a sweater pulled from its hem. They had felt it in their teeth, in the storm-glass amulet against their sternum, which had grown so cold it had burned. Twenty years since Emrys Ashgrove had touched true magic willingly, and last night the world had reached out and touched *them*, without permission, without mercy, whispering: *the Veil is dying, and you are still stitched to it, little storm-caller, whether you like it or not.*

They set down the cup. They tied on their shop-apron, the one with the frayed pocket where they kept the copper tokens for children who couldn't pay. They went downstairs to open the shop, because if there was one thing Greyhollow would forgive, it was a merchant behaving exactly as merchants always behaved.

*Invisibility is a discipline,* their mother had once told them, back when such words were spoken freely under their family's roof. *It is not the absence of self. It is the mastery of it.* Emrys had spent two decades mastering themselves into a person of no consequence—a charm-seller of modest talent selling luck-knots and hearth-blessings and love-charms that worked only because belief was half the cure anyway. They had made themselves small. They had made themselves boring. They had gotten very, very good at it.

This morning, smallness felt like drowning.

* * *

By midmorning, Tallow Street had stopped pretending nothing had happened.

The market quarter opened around them in ragged pieces—half the stalls shuttered, the other half manned by vendors who kept glancing upward even though the sun was up now and there was nothing to see but flat, starless blue. The bread-line outside Ferren's bakery stretched twice its usual length and moved at half its usual speed, because no one in it was talking about anything except the dark. Emrys heard fragments as they arranged their wares: a dockhand swearing the tide had come in wrong, too high, too saltless; a grandmother insisting her joints had known something was coming for a week; a pair of apprentices from the Guildhall whispering that the Wardens had sealed the archive tower at dawn and no one would say why.

And everywhere, threaded through it all like wire through wool: fear. Fear has a smell, Emrys thought. Sour and metallic, like a storm about to break over copper roofs. Twenty years ago, Emrys had stood in another crowd that smelled like this, the night the storm came ashore at Merrow's Reach, the night three people died and one name was struck from every ledger in the province. They knew what fear did to crowds. Crowds wanted someone to blame. And in Greyhollow, in a city where magic had been outlawed for a generation, there was only one direction blame ever pointed.

"Charm-witch." Old Maren the netmender planted herself at the stall's edge, her voice carrying further than she probably intended. Her hands, knotted with rope-scars, gripped a luck-knot she'd bought from Emrys a dozen times before. "Tell me straight. You're the closest thing to a wise-woman this street's got. Did the charms fail? Did *we* do something? Is this—" She lowered her voice, and the crowd leaning in lowered theirs to match. "Is this a casting? Someone's casting?"

Every eye on the street turned toward the stall. Toward Emrys.

Here it was, then. The moment Emrys had rehearsed in nightmares for twenty years—not the inquisitors at the door, not the pyre, but this: a neighbor's question, asked in innocence, that could end everything depending on the answer.

They let the silence sit just long enough to seem like thought. Then they smiled the small, apologetic smile of a tradesman delivering bad news about goods.

"Maren. My charms are knots and prayers and a bit of showmanship, and you know it. If I could put out the stars, don't you think I'd have charged more?" A ripple of uneasy laughter moved through the little crowd, and Emrys leaned into it, gentle as a hand on a frightened horse. "The sky's been strange before. Remember the year of the red moon? Everyone swore the world was ending then, too, and instead we just got a bad herring season." *Lie smoothly,* they told themselves. *Lie kindly.* "Go home. Bar your doors tonight if it makes you feel better. But don't turn on each other. That's how winters get long."

Maren held their gaze a moment longer than was comfortable—old eyes, sharp as gull-beaks—and then nodded slowly and shuffled off, and the crowd broke apart into murmuring clusters, and Emrys stood behind their counter with their heart slamming against the cracked storm-glass at their chest and thought: *That was close. That was closer than it has ever been.*

Because it wasn't entirely a lie, and Maren had perhaps smelled the truth underneath it. Emrys's charms *were* mostly knots and showmanship—but not all. There was a warming charm in the knotwork, a whisper of it, enough to take the chill out of a drafty room. There was a finding-spell braided into the lost-child tokens. Small things. Safe things. Things that had kept Emrys's hands remembering their craft across twenty years of deliberate forgetting, the way a musician taps out rhythms on a table to keep from losing the music entirely.

And this morning, with the Veil bleeding out somewhere beyond the horizon, those small things were humming. Every charm in the shop had woken at dawn faintly warm, faintly alive, straining toward the wound in the sky like plants toward light. As if the magic itself knew something Emrys refused to know.

As if it were saying: *soon*.

* * *

The child came in an hour past noon, when the cold arrived.

It came suddenly—that was the wrong thing, the thing that made even the hardened market-vendors stop and stare at each other. One moment the day was merely bleak, the flat grey chill of any Greyhollow autumn. The next, frost bloomed up the sides of buildings like white fire, breath hung in clouds, and the puddles in the cart-ruts froze solid with a sound like snapping fingers. In minutes. In *seconds*. Cold that did not drift in but *arrived*, purposeful, as though the winter had stepped through a door.

Emrys stood in the doorway of the shop and watched frost crawl across Tallow Street and felt the amulet against their chest pulse once, hard, like a second heart.

Then they heard the crying.

It came from the fountain square—a thin, tearing sound, a child's cry in the register that bypasses thought and goes straight to the marrow. Emrys crossed the street before they had decided to move. The crowd parted around a knot of people gathered at the fountain's rim, and there in the middle of them sat a girl, maybe six years old, in a coat far too thin for the season, and her hands—

Her hands were wrong. Frostbitten, badly, the fingers waxy-white and bloodless to the second knuckle, and she'd been holding onto the fountain's iron rail when the cold hit, some game of dares with other children scattered and fled, and now she couldn't let go, couldn't feel her own grip, and her mother was kneeling in the frozen slush trying to pry the small hands free while the girl screamed and screamed.

"Don't pull," Emrys heard themself say. Their voice came out level, quiet, the voice they used for frightened animals and frightened customers. "You'll tear the skin. Warm water, not hot—you know that. Get her inside."

"There's no time," the mother said wildly. She was young, hollow-cheeked, wearing the grey smock of a dyer's assistant with hands stained indigo to the wrist. "Look at her fingers, look at—they'll take them, the physickers will take them, we can't afford—" She caught herself, looked around at the gathering crowd, and Emrys watched her do the arithmetic every poor parent in Greyhollow did: the cost of a physicker against the cost of a child's hands, and no good answer anywhere in the sum.

The frost was spreading up the girl's wrists as they knelt. Emrys could see it—not metaphorically, not as a figure of speech, but *actually see it*, the way they had been able to see weather all their life before they buried that sight: threads of cold moving in the girl's flesh like ink dropped in water, hungry, reaching for the warmth of her heart.

Twenty years of rules rose up in Emrys like a wall. *Never heal. Never cast where eyes can see. Never be the miracle on the street corner, because miracles have names attached, and your name is already written in one book you never want opened.* They had walked past suffering before. Of course they had—everyone in Greyhollow walked past suffering daily; the city ran on it like a millwheel. They had learned to fold their compassion small and carry it folded, a letter never sent.

But the girl looked up at them through her tears, and her eyes were brown and ordinary and terrified, and she said, in the small reasonable voice of a child negotiating with the world: "Make it stop hurting. Please. You're the charm-man. Charms make things better."

*You're the charm-man. Charms make things better.*

Later—much later, in another life—Emrys would understand that this was the moment the twenty years ended. Not at the Stones, not at the climax of anything grand, but here: kneeling in frozen slush beside a fountain, looking into a child's face, feeling the folded letter of their own heart crack open along its crease.

"Everyone," Emrys said, rising, pitching their voice to fill the square, "give us air. Go on—nothing to see. Inside, all of you, before the cold takes your noses off." And because they said it with authority, because a crowd will always obey the person who sounds least afraid, they cleared a space. Almost. The mother stayed. And old Maren, damn her gull-sharp eyes, lingered at the edge of the square, pretending to examine her nets.

Emrys took the girl's ruined hands in theirs.

The cold in them was deep, deeper than flesh—it had the taste of that *other* cold, the one that had arrived with intent, and touching it was like putting their fingers into the wound in the sky. Emrys breathed out slowly. Beneath their ribs, the storm-glass amulet pulsed, eager, starving, and Emrys did the thing they had sworn off for two decades: they reached for the storm.

Not lightning. Not the roaring thing that had torn the sky open over Merrow's Reach. Just the smallest thread of it—the warmth at a storm's heart, the static charge that lives in the moment before rain. They drew it up through the amulet, through their palms, and let it flow into the girl's hands like sunlight poured into dark water.

The frost retreated. He watched it go—saw the threads of cold unwind and dissolve, saw color flood back into the small fingers, pink and living, saw the girl's sobbing stutter and change key, from pain to simple overwhelming relief. The mother made a sound that was half prayer and half disbelief.

"There," Emrys said softly. "Wiggle them. All ten?"

The girl wiggled all ten, and then, being six, seized Emrys's sleeve with both restored hands and inspected it gravely. "Your hands are sparky," she informed them.

"Do you know," Emrys said, and their voice nearly failed, "I believe they are."

They got the girl and her mother pointed toward home with instructions—warm water, wool, no rubbing—and stood alone in the emptying square with frost melting around their boots and their heart hammering a rhythm they hadn't heard in twenty years.

*What did you just do.*

They had cast. Openly. On a public street. In a city where the penalty for casting was the Warden's white wagon and a cell below the Bastion and, for repeat offenses, the long drop from the Traitor's Gate. Any one of thirty witnesses might talk. Maren had *seen*—or suspected—and Maren had a tongue like a bell-rope.

And yet. Emrys flexed their hands, which tingled with borrowed storm, and found—to their horror and their wonder in equal measure—that they could not summon a single regret. Twenty years of folding their compassion into a letter never sent, and one child's cry had undone the whole careful origami of it in the space of a heartbeat. The warmth was still in them. It wanted to be used. It had *always* wanted to be used, and they had starved it until it had become a kind of death.

"I'm getting careless," they murmured to the empty square.

"You're getting *stupid*," said a voice behind them, dry as old paper, "and worse, you're getting noticed. Which in this city, friend, amounts to the same thing."

Emrys turned.

A woman stood at the mouth of the lane with a leather map-case slung across her back and a look on her face like a blade that had been sharpened specifically for this conversation. She was tall, wind-burnt, with ink-stained fingers and grey eyes that did not blink often enough. Emrys had seen her before—everyone on Tallow Street had; she rented the garret above the chandler's and was known for maps of astonishing beauty and for opinions delivered at volume.

She crossed the frosted cobbles without hurry, stopped an arm's length away, and looked Emrys up and down with the frank appraisal of someone reading a document.

"Frostbite healed in under a minute, no salve, no physicker's arts," she said. "Hands glowing like a lantern-fish. And yesterday evening, charm-seller, when every star in the sky went out at once—I watched your face from my window. You weren't surprised. You were *grieving*." She tilted her head. "So. Who are you really, and what do you know about the Covenant Stones?"

The name went through Emrys like ice water.

No one said that name. No one had said it in twenty years—not in the provinces, not in the cities, not anywhere the Wardens' reach extended, which was everywhere. The Covenant Stones were a story told to children and then carefully untold, a place erased from maps so thoroughly that even Emrys, who had grown up on their mother's tales of them, could not have found them on any chart printed in the last two decades.

And this stranger had spoken the words aloud on a public street as easily as asking the time.

"I think," Emrys said carefully, "you've mistaken me for someone interesting."

"Oh, I don't think so." The woman smiled, and it was not a comforting smile. She reached into her coat and drew out a folded sheet of paper—no, a map, dense with fine ink lines—and held it up between two fingers like a warrant. "My name is Sable Vane. I make maps for a living, which means I notice what's missing from them. And last night, charm-seller, I started noticing *everything* that's missing—including, I suspect, most of your story." Her grey eyes glittered. "We should talk. Somewhere with a door. Because unless I miss my guess—and I never miss my guess—the Wardens are going to be very interested in whatever it is you just did to that child. And I'd rather hear your side of it before they hear theirs."

Behind her, far off across the rooftops toward the Bastion hill, a bell began to ring. Not the hour-bell. The alarm.

Sable Vane didn't even glance at it. She just raised one ink-stained eyebrow, and waited.
