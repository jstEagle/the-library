## 3. The Cartographer Who Knew Too Much

The morning after the stars went out, Greyhollow woke up angry, and Emrys Ashgrove did what they had done every morning for twenty years: they opened the shutters of the charm stall, set out the little trays of trinkets that were mostly glass and wire and wishful thinking, and waited for the city to come to them.

It came quickly.

The night market crowd was thinner than usual—curfew rumors already crawling through the quarters like damp through plaster—but the faces that pressed close to the stall were harder, hungrier. A fishwife bought a luck-knot without haggling. Two dockhands split the cost of a salt-charm against drowning, though the harbor had frozen over three days past and no ship had moved since. An old man with a soldier's bearing laid down a copper for a warding bell and asked, quietly, whether it would keep out "whatever took the sky."

"It's just a bell," Emrys said, in their flat market voice. "But it's a good one."

He looked at them a moment too long, then nodded and shuffled off into the grey.

Emrys turned to rearranging the trays and let their hands shake where no one could see. *Just a bell.* The lie sat in their chest like a swallowed stone. Every charm on this table was a small act of cowardice—real craft sanded down to superstition, real power folded so many times it fit inside a thimble. That had been the bargain: stay small, stay dull, stay invisible. Twenty years of selling people pretty lies because the truth would get them all burned.

And last night the wards had shattered like a dropped plate, and every star in the sky had gone dark at once, and invisibility had stopped being something Emrys chose.

They were counting the luck-knots for the third time when she arrived.

* * *

She came down Candlemaker's Row like weather—not walking so much as arriving, a woman in a long oilskin coat the color of wet slate, a leather map-case slung across her back and an ink-stained bandolier of pens at her hip. She was perhaps forty, with close-cropped iron-grey hair and a face built for scowling, and she moved through the market crowd the way a knife moves through bread: parting it, unbothered by it.

She stopped at the stall. Looked at the charms. Looked at Emrys.

"You're the charm-seller," she said. Not a question.

"Depends who's asking." Emrys kept their voice pleasant, their hands busy, their eyes anywhere but hers. "Luck-knots are two coppers. Warding bells three. I don't do readings."

"I didn't ask for a reading." She pulled a folded paper from her coat and slapped it down on the counter between a tray of glass beads and a jar of river-stones. "I asked for you."

Emrys glanced down. It was a map—or the ghost of one. Hand-drawn, dense with annotations in a sharp, slanted hand, showing Greyhollow and the borderlands beyond, the whole eastern reach of the Marches. And across it, in red ink gone brown with age or repetition, someone had drawn lines. Converging lines. Dozens of them, radiating inward from points along the old ward-boundaries, all meeting at a single marked place deep in the high country, far north and east, where the mountains began.

At the center of the convergence, written in that same slanted hand, were two words:

**COVENANT STONES.**

Emrys's blood went cold and stayed there. They did not touch the map. They did not look at it longer than a bored merchant would. "Pretty," they said. "Is it for sale?"

"It's not for sale. It's evidence." The woman leaned in, and her eyes were pale grey, the color of the sky before hail, and they did not blink enough. "Three nights ago, every ward-line from here to the Salt Road flickered. Not failed—*flickered*, like a lamp before the oil runs dry. I know, because I've spent eleven years mapping those ward-lines. Don't ask me why; the answer's boring and illegal. The point is, I felt it happen. Then last night—" she tapped the map, once, hard, "—every star goes out. Simultaneously. Not cloud cover. Not ash. *Simultaneously*, which means whatever killed them didn't travel. It happened everywhere at once, which means it happened somewhere that isn't anywhere, if you follow me."

"I really don't," said Emrys.

"You do." She straightened up and looked around the market with theatrical patience—at the fishwife pretending not to listen, at the dockhands lingering by the pickle-barrel, at the gray light pressing down on the city like a lid. "Every line converges there." She touched the center of the map again. "The Covenant Stones. Which is interesting, because according to every official record in the Warden archives—and I checked, believe me, I checked—the Covenant Stones are a myth. A story hedge-witches tell their brats. There's no such place. So either I've spent eleven years triangulating a fairy tale, or somebody very powerful went to a great deal of trouble to make sure nobody ever looked for it. And now the sky's gone black, and I find myself leaning toward the second option."

Emrys picked up a luck-knot and began, with great deliberation, to retie its fraying end. "Ma'am, I sell charms. Glass and wire. If you want your fortune read, there's a woman two rows over who does tea leaves, and she'll tell you anything you like for a copper."

"Yes," the woman said. "You do sell charms. Glass and wire." Her gaze swept the stall slowly, cataloguing. "Your knotwork is Aldermoor style—coastal witch-craft, three centuries out of fashion, and done left-handed, which means you learned it from someone who learned it left-handed, which means you didn't learn it from a book. Your river-stones aren't river-stones; they're creek-bed quartz from high ground, tumbled in snowmelt, and there's exactly one watershed in the Marches that produces that particular milky veining. Your warding bells are cast bronze with a core of cold-iron filings, which is a storm-caller's trick for grounding lightning, except you've filed the clappers flat so they can't actually ring—which means you built them to work and then deliberately broke them." She paused. "Also, you flinched when I said *storm-caller*. Just now. Barely. But I've spent my life reading terrain, and terrain includes faces."

The market noise seemed to recede. Emrys became aware of their own heartbeat, slow and enormous, like something turning over in deep water.

"My name," the woman said, "is Sable Vane. I make maps. Good ones. And I am telling you, charm-seller, that whatever you're hiding under all that glass and wire, the time for hiding it is over, because the Veil is dying, the winter is coming in wrong, and I need to find someone who knows what the Covenant Stones actually are before the Wardens figure out that I know. Because when they do—and they will—they will burn this entire quarter looking for everyone who ever so much as sneezed sideways at a ward-line. Starting, I'd imagine, with the person whose craftsmanship gave you away."

Silence. Somewhere down the row, a child laughed, bright and oblivious, and the sound hurt.

"You're mistaken," Emrys said quietly.

"I'm never mistaken about maps," Sable Vane said. "And people are just maps, charm-seller. Roads taken, roads avoided, places burned behind them. You're a map of a person who used to be somebody, wearing a map of a person who doesn't exist. It's a good forgery. I'll give you that. But I've caught better forgeries than you in archives older than this city, and every single one of them made the same mistake you're making right now."

"And what mistake is that?"

Sable smiled. It was not a comforting sight. "You looked at the Stones on my map before you looked away. Everyone else looks at the red lines first. Curious people look at the lines. Only someone who already knows what's at the center looks at the center first."

* * *

Emrys closed the stall early. They told the fishwife—Maren, who had bought charms from them for six years and never once asked a personal question—that they were feeling ill, and Maren looked at them with those shrewd fish-market eyes and said, "Aye, well. Whole city's ill, love," and pressed a smoked herring into their hands as payment-in-kind, and walked away.

Then Emrys led Sable Vane through three alleys, two courtyards, and a doorway that smelled of tallow and cat, up four flights of stairs to the rented room above the dyer's shop that had been home for nineteen of their twenty years in Greyhollow. Not because they trusted her. Because the market was full of ears, and because some conversations, once started, could only get worse in public.

The room was small and ruthlessly tidy: a bed, a stove, a shelf of books with their spines defaced so no title could identify them, a window overlooking the dye-vats and, beyond the rooftops, the empty black strip of sky where the stars should have been. Emrys lit the stove because their hands needed something to do.

Sable stood in the middle of the room, turning slowly, reading it the way she'd read the stall. "You've lived here a long time," she said. "But you haven't *lived* here. No photographs. No letters. No keepsakes. Everything in this room could be abandoned in ten minutes." She looked at the defaced book spines. "You've been running longer than you've been hiding."

"Sit down," Emrys said, "or leave."

Sable sat, on the one chair, with the map-case still slung across her back like she expected to run at any moment. Emrys remained standing by the stove, arms crossed, and for a long moment neither of them spoke, and the silence filled up with the sound of the wind worrying the eaves.

"The Covenant Stones," Emrys said finally. "Where did you hear that name?"

"Nowhere. That's rather the point." Sable unlaced the map-case and drew out more papers—dozens of them, years of work, fanning them across the bed like a hand of cards. "I heard nothing. I *derived* it. Old survey records mention 'boundary cairns' in the high country, pre-Warden, purpose unknown, location scrubbed. Folk songs from the valley villages reference 'the singing stones' and then stop referencing them, abruptly, around two hundred years ago—as if the singing stopped. And the ward-lines themselves, the ones I've been mapping for eleven years? They're not Warden work. They're much older, and they all point somewhere. Every ward in the Marches is a spoke, charm-seller. Something built them as a wheel, and the hub is up in those mountains, and two nights ago the wheel started coming apart." She looked up. "So. You know what's at the hub. I can see it in your face, which, incidentally, is the worst-crafted thing about you. What is it?"

Emrys stared at the window, at the starless dark waiting beyond the rooftops even in daylight, a bruise on the sky.

"If I tell you," they said, "you'll be in danger. Real danger. Not market-gossip danger. The kind that ends with Warden irons and a pyre."

"I've been in danger since the day I decided the Warden Survey's maps were wrong on purpose," Sable said. "You think I carry eleven years of illegal cartography in a leather tube because it's comfortable? Everything worth knowing is dangerous, charm-seller. That's how you know it's worth knowing." She gathered the maps back into a neat stack, precise as a surgeon. "Tell me or don't. But if you don't, I go alone, and I'll die alone in the high country, and the Stones will stay lost, and the sky will stay black, and the winter will keep coming in wrong until it comes in *forever*. I've accepted that arithmetic. Have you?"

Emrys closed their eyes.

Twenty years. Twenty years of smallness, of glass and wire, of filing the clappers flat. Twenty years of being no one, and it had bought them nothing—because the sky had gone out anyway, and the wards had broken anyway, and the thing they had run from had found them anyway, wearing the face of a sharp-tongued mapmaker with ink on her fingers.

"There was a covenant," Emrys said. The words came out rusty, like a door opening after decades. "Between this world and the other one. The world behind the sky. Long ago—before the Wardens, before the purges—the two realms were bound together at a ring of standing stones in the high country. The covenant wasn't a wall. People think of magic as a wall, something to keep the other side out. It wasn't. It was more like..." They searched for it. "*Roots.* One tree, roots going down into both soils. The Veil wasn't a barrier; it was a graft-point. Things passed through it the way sap passes through wood. Memory, mostly. The realms remembered each other, and that remembering is what held everything up. Including the stars. The stars are—" they stopped, hearing their mother's voice saying it, and had to start again. "The stars are the other realm shining through. Windows, not fires."

Sable had gone very still. "And the Stones broke."

"The Stones broke. Or were broken. I don't know—I was a child when the stories stopped being told. What I know is that the wards we have now are scars, not skin. The Wardens built them on top of the old covenant-lines, using the old geometry, because you can't build new geometry, only steal it. And when the wards failed two nights ago, they didn't fail randomly. They failed along the old lines. The covenant isn't just damaged, Sable. It's dying. And when it dies fully, the Veil closes forever, and both worlds go dark, and the winter—" Emrys opened their eyes. "The winter won't end. Not in a season. In ever."

Sable looked down at her maps for a long moment. When she spoke again, the sharpness had gone out of her voice, replaced by something quieter and more frightening: certainty. "Then we go to the Stones."

"*We* don't go anywhere. *I* haven't agreed to anything."

"You closed your stall early for me. You brought me to your home. You just lectured me on pre-Warden cosmology." Sable began rolling her maps with brisk efficiency. "In my experience, charm-seller, that's agreement. You're just doing it slowly, so it feels like your idea."

"That's not—" Emrys pinched the bridge of their nose. "You don't understand what you're asking. The high country in winter is lethal. The Wardens will be mobilizing—they'll seal the passes within days, maybe hours. And if anyone finds out what I am—"

"What *are* you?" Sable asked. Simply. Directly. The question landing in the small room like a stone dropped in a well.

Emrys said nothing.

"Storm-caller," Sable said, answering herself. "Left-handed coastal knotwork. Lightning-grounded bells. You flinched at the word. And last night, when the wards fell—Maren the fishwife says the lamps in your stall guttered in a spiral pattern. Clockwise. That's not a ward-failure signature. That's a *calling* signature. Something reached for you, or you reached for it, and either way, the storm knows your name." She stood, shouldering the map-case. "I don't care. Whatever you did, whoever you were—that's your map, not mine. I need the territory. The territory is the Stones. Come with me or don't, but decide soon, because the Wardens are already asking questions in the low quarters, and questions in Greyhollow have a way of becoming bonfires."

She was at the door when Emrys spoke.

"Why me?"

Sable paused, hand on the latch. "Because you're the only person in this city who knew what the Stones were before I showed you the map," she said. "And because you're terrified, and you're staying anyway. That's the rarest thing I know how to read." She glanced back, and for one moment the scowl softened into something almost like respect. "Get your affairs in order, charm-seller. We leave within three days. Sooner, if the snow comes early."

The door clicked shut behind her.

* * *

Emrys stood alone in the small room for a long time. Below, the dyer's vats steamed; a cart rattled past on the cobbles; the city went on making its ordinary noises, unaware that the sky above it had died.

They went to the window. Pressed their palm flat against the cold glass. Beyond the rooftops, the bruised dark of the starless sky stretched from horizon to horizon, and somewhere far north and east, under all that darkness, the Covenant Stones waited—roots of a dead tree, hub of a broken wheel, the place their mother had named only in whispers, if she had named it at all.

*I could refuse,* they thought. *Stay small. File the clappers flat. Sell glass and wire until the winter takes the city, takes the market, takes Maren and her smoked herrings and the laughing child down the row.*

But the storm knew their name. And for the first time in twenty years, Emrys Ashgrove found that they wanted to answer.

They crossed to the loose floorboard beneath the bed—the one board in the whole room that was theirs, truly theirs—and lifted it. Inside lay a bundle wrapped in oilcloth, and inside the bundle, a pendant of storm-glass on a leather cord, its silvered surface swirling faintly, restlessly, like weather trying to remember itself.

Emrys had not touched it in twenty years.

Their fingers hovered above it, trembling.

From somewhere below, muffled through floorboards and distance, came the first tolling of the Warden bells—not the hour-bells, but the deeper ones, the ones reserved for proclamations and purges. Once. Twice. Three times.

Calling the city to order.

Calling, Emrys thought, the hunters out with the hounds.

They closed their hand around the storm-glass, and it was cold as the space between stars, and it pulsed once against their palm—like a heartbeat. Like recognition. Like a door, twenty years locked, beginning, at last, to open.
