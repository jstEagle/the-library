## 4. Wardens at the Door

The knock came at dawn, and it was not a knock so much as a verdict.

Emrys had been awake for hours—awake all night, truthfully, lying on their narrow bed above the charm shop with the shutters open, watching a sky that held nothing but grey. No stars. Not one. The city below had spent the dark hours murmuring like a hive struck with a stick, and now the murmur had hardened into something with edges.

The sound that woke the street was iron on wood: three measured blows from a Warden's mallet, spaced with the patience of people who knew no door in Greyhollow would stay shut against them.

*Residual casters.* The word had come down with the morning criers, shouted through speaking-horns in voices flattened by official dread: *By order of the High Warden, all residents of the Lanternmarket will present themselves for attestation. Residual casters are reminded that concealment of arcane aptitude is a capital offense. Cooperation will be noted.*

Cooperation would be noted. Emrys stood at the top of the stairs with their hand pressed flat against the wall and thought: *they always say it like that, as if mercy were a ledger they might kindly balance in your favor.*

Twenty years in Greyhollow had taught them the grammar of raids. There were two kinds. The first was theater—a dozen grey-cloaks marching through the market square, tipping over a fruit cart or two, confiscating a hedge-witch's dried lavender because someone's cousin had dreamed badly about her. You endured those by being boring. By being *furniture*. Emrys had made an art of furniture; it was why the shop sold love-charms that were only rose oil and suggestion, luck-knots that were only pretty knots, wards against bad dreams that worked exactly as well as chamomile tea. A fraud, the other exiles would have sneered. Emrys preferred *survivor*.

The second kind was not theater.

They heard the boots before they saw them—heavy, synchronized, too many—and the particular wet-hiss of ward-lanterns being lit. Ward-lanterns burned cold blue and screamed when they came near active magic. Emrys had watched one scream a spice-merchant into shackles eleven years ago because the man's grandmother had been a river-speaker and something of the tide still slept in his blood. He'd been selling saffron. He'd done nothing. The lantern hadn't cared.

Emrys moved.

The storm-glass amulet hung where it always hung, beneath three layers of wool, against skin gone cool with morning. It was warm. It was always warm now—it had been warm since the night the stars went out, a small fevered heartbeat against their sternum, and every hour Emrys told themselves it was nothing, and every hour the glass inside its silver cage swirled a little darker, like weather deciding.

There was no time for cleverness. Cleverness took minutes they did not have. They wrenched up the floorboard behind the counter—the third one, loose since the reign of the last Warden's last purge, a gift from the shop's previous owner, who had also been hiding from something—and dropped the amulet into the cavity between the joists, into the nest of dust and mouse bones and a dead man's secrets.

The glass flared as it fell. Just once. A pulse of pale light, quick as a gasp.

"No," Emrys breathed, "no, *quiet*—" and pressed the board down over it as the front door shuddered.

Not knocked this time. Opened. The lock gave with a crack of splintering wood, because Warden law held that doors which locked themselves were doors with something to hide, and Emrys's lock had been a perfectly ordinary lock, and it broke anyway.

* * *

Four of them came in. Three grey-cloaks with ward-lanterns swinging from staves, and one inquisitor.

You could always tell the inquisitors. The rank-and-file wore their authority like borrowed coats, too big, shrugging under the weight of it. Inquisitors wore theirs like skin. This one was a woman perhaps fifty, silver at her temples, with the unhurried eyes of someone who had looked into a great many faces and found most of them guilty of something. Her cloak bore the eye-and-key of the Office of Attestation stitched in thread so fine it seemed embroidered in frost.

"Proprietor," she said. Not a question. She already knew whose shop this was; she had read the tax rolls, the lease, probably the name Emrys had lived under for two decades—*Ash*, they called themselves here, Ash the charm-seller, a name chosen for its dullness, its ashiness, its perfect forgettability. "You'll stand away from the counter."

Emrys stepped away from the counter. Their heart was doing something obscene against their ribs, and they made their face do what it had learned to do: nothing. Mild. Slightly confused, the way honest people are confused. Twenty years of practice, and it fit like an old glove.

"Is there trouble?" they asked. Their voice came out right too—roughened with sleep they hadn't gotten, pitched low, unremarkable. "I've only just opened. I don't open till the bell, usually."

"The bell didn't ring this morning." One of the grey-cloaks said it flatly, and Emrys felt the words land somewhere beneath their skin. True. The temple bells of Greyhollow rang the hours, and this morning, at dawn, every bell in the city had hung silent. The bell-ringers swore the ropes had gone stiff as iron. Nobody could explain it. Nobody wanted to try.

"The lanterns will do the talking," the inquisitor said. "Stand in the corner, please. Touch nothing."

The grey-cloaks began at the doorway and worked inward with terrible method. Ward-lanterns passed over shelves of dried flowers, over bundles of knotwork, over the little brass scales Emrys used to weigh out rose petals like an apothecary weighing medicine. Blue light washed everything. The lanterns stayed silent as grave-goods.

Emrys stood in the corner and counted their own breaths and thought about the floorboard.

It was four feet from where they stood. Four feet of old pine and dust, and beneath it a storm-glass amulet that had once belonged to their mother, that had sung in their hand the night they called the storm, that held—if the old stories were true—a sliver of the sky itself, bound in glass. It was warm. It was *alive*. And if one of those lanterns drifted low enough, if the blue light sank through the floorboards the way water finds a crack—

Don't think about it, they told themselves. Thinking pulls. That was the first lesson of concealment, older than the Wardens, older than the covenant: magic answers attention. Think at a thing and the thing feels your thinking, the way you feel someone staring at the back of your neck.

So Emrys thought about bread. About the price of flour. About the leak in the roof over the storeroom that needed patching before real winter came, if winter was going to be what everyone now feared it would be. Mundane thoughts, mortar-thick, laid brick by brick between themselves and the floor.

The grey-cloaks reached the counter.

One of them knelt. Emrys watched his lantern-hand drift along the underside of the counter's lip, along the drawers, and then—slowly, terribly—down toward the floor. Toward the boards. Toward the third one.

The lantern hummed.

It was very soft. A mosquito-note, almost. But in the silence of a raided shop it was a shout, and the kneeling grey-cloak went still, and tilted his head the way hounds tilt, and the inquisitor turned from the window she'd been studying and said, quietly, "What is it?"

"Floor's singing, ma'am. Maybe. Could be pipes."

"There are no pipes under the Lanternmarket," the inquisitor said. "Dig it up."

* * *

Later—much later, years later, in another life—Emrys would try to remember deciding. They never could. There was no decision, no moment of choice, just a gap in the record like a skipped stitch, and then their hand was moving and the candle-flame on the counter—left burning from the pre-dawn, forgotten, a stub of tallow no bigger than a thumb—was *leaping*.

Not much. That was the horror of it, how little it took. A flare, a lean, a tongue of fire stretching sideways off the wick like a dog straining its leash, and the curtain beside the counter—old linen, dry as August—caught.

Fire in a shop full of dried flowers and paper charms and twenty years of carefully accumulated boredom. It went up the way gossip goes up a staircase.

"Fire!" someone shouted—one of the grey-cloaks, his composure cracking into something younger and more human. "Fire in the back—"

The inquisitor did not shout. She turned, saw the flame crawling up the curtain with an appetite no draft could explain, and her frost-fine eyes went from the fire to Emrys standing in the corner with their hands at their sides, and Emrys watched her watch them, and understood with a clarity like falling that she had already begun to suspect, and that suspicion, once lit, burned longer than any curtain.

Then the neighbor came through the smoke.

Old Marta Hollis from the dye-shop next door, seventy if she was a day, built like a rain barrel, with arms stained permanent indigo to the elbow. She should have been in the square for attestation like everyone else; instead she came through the connecting door with a bucket of dye-water—madder-red, steaming—and threw the whole thing over the burning curtain without a word, without hesitation, the way she'd have swatted a fly off her own grandchild.

The fire died in a hiss and a bloom of pink steam. The shop filled with the smell of scorched linen and wet wool and, absurdly, roses.

And the floorboard stayed silent. Because in that half-second of flame and shouting, every lantern in the room had swung toward the fire, and the blue light had swept *away* from the third board, and whatever note the storm-glass had been humming drowned in the roar of the burn.

Marta stood there dripping, hands on hips, glaring at the inquisitor as if *she* had started the fire. "Well? Are you going to help or are you going to stand there being important? Man's livelihood, this. Half the street buys their luck-charms here, not that they work, mind—" a meaningful sniff, "—but they're *pretty*, and pretty matters in hard times."

"Marta," Emrys managed. Their voice almost held.

"Don't you 'Marta' me, Ash, you left a candle burning, I've told you a hundred times, you're lucky I keep odd hours—" She was still scolding when the grey-cloaks herded them both out onto the step, still scolding while the inquisitor's lantern-men pried up the third floorboard and found, beneath it, dust and mouse bones and a dead man's secrets and *nothing else*, because Emrys, in the gap where the decision should have been, had done something they had no memory of doing—had reached, somehow, through four feet of pine and panic, and folded the amulet's light closed like a hand closing around a moth.

They found out later that they'd done it. Found out from the ache. Magic answered attention, and Emrys had paid attention with everything they had, and the storm-glass had obeyed—and now their left hand trembled and would not stop, and somewhere behind their eyes a headache bloomed like ink in water.

The inquisitor crouched at the open floor cavity for a long time. She touched the dust. She rubbed it between finger and thumb. Then she stood, and brushed off her knees with the unhurried precision of a woman who never wasted a motion, and looked at Emrys across the ruined counter.

"Ash, is it."

"It's on the sign, ma'am."

"Hm." She glanced at the scorch-mark on the wall, the sodden curtain, the bucket. "Candles are dangerous things. In a city full of frightened people, a single careless flame can do more harm than any caster." Something moved behind her eyes—not warmth, nothing so kind, but a kind of professional interest, the interest of a fisherman seeing a large shadow under the boat. "You'll come to the Hall tomorrow for attestation. All of the Lanternmarket will. But you especially, charm-seller. I find I'm curious about you."

"I sell knots," Emrys said. "Ma'am."

"Yes," she said. "That's what concerns me."

She left then, boots ringing on the cobbles, grey-cloaks trailing, ward-lanterns guttering out one by one like a procession of small funerals. The street watched them go with the fixed, careful blankness of people memorizing faces for later.

* * *

Afterward there was the mess, and messes were good, messes were merciful. Emrys swept scorched linen into a pail. Marta wrung out her apron and complained about the state of the world with the fluency of long practice. The neighbors drifted past the broken door offering sympathy that was mostly curiosity, and Emrys accepted it all with the mild nodding face of a man whose shop had briefly caught fire, which was, after all, true.

"You saved my shop," Emrys said at last, quietly, when the street had emptied.

"I saved my own wall," Marta said. "Your fire doesn't get to eat my dye-shop." But her voice had gone gentle around it, the way rough cloth can be gentle if you hold it right. She looked at Emrys's trembling left hand, and then very deliberately at the middle distance, and said nothing about it at all. "Attestation tomorrow. You'll go?"

"I'll go."

"They took the Fenwicks last spring. Attestation, they called it. Old man Fenwick came home three days later and never spoke again, just sat by the window. His wife says they asked him questions he couldn't answer about things he'd never done." Marta gathered her empty bucket. "Be boring, Ash. You're good at boring."

She went out through the connecting door, and Emrys stood alone in the smoke-smelling ruin of their careful life.

The trembling wouldn't stop. They sat down on the floor amid the wreckage, in the space where the third board gaped open like a missing tooth, and put their head in their hands, and let the shaking have them for a while.

*Nearly.* That was the word that kept circling. Nearly caught. Nearly burned. Nearly—gods, nearly *burned Marta*, because the fire had leapt toward the connecting door before it climbed the curtain, had leaned that way with an eagerness that Emrys recognized with sick certainty as their own eagerness, twenty years deep, the want to break out, to be seen, to stop pretending—and Marta had walked into it. Had thrown her bucket into it. Seventy years old and indigo to the elbows and she had walked into a fire that Emrys had made, to save a fraud's shop, and she would never know how close the thing had come to taking her eyebrows, her lungs, her life.

This was the cost. Not the raids, not the fear, not even the loneliness—those you could carry, those became furniture like everything else. The cost was this: that staying hidden meant letting danger walk past you onto other people. That every day of survival was bought with a small coin of someone else's risk. If the fire had taken Marta, Emrys would have stood in the crowd and watched her burn and said nothing, and the saying-nothing would have been the worst thing they'd ever done, worse than the storm, worse than the night that had driven them here, because the storm at least had been an accident, and this—this daily arithmetic of cowardice—was a choice, renewed each morning, paid each dusk.

*I could leave,* they thought. The thought came the way it always came, patient as frost. Walk out the broken door, take the salt road south, vanish into the borderlands where names didn't matter. Twenty years ago they'd promised themselves they'd never use the storm-glass again, and they'd kept that promise the way you keep a wound: barely, painfully, with constant attention. But the stars were out. The Veil was dying—that was what the frost on the window had spelled last night, in letters of their own childhood name, *Emrys, Emrys*, written in a dead language by a dying sky—and whatever was coming for both worlds would not be stopped by a charm-seller's caution. Hiding had kept them alive. It had not kept anyone else safe. Perhaps it never had.

From the cavity in the floor, faintly, the storm-glass pulsed. Warm. Waiting. Like a heart that remembered what it was for.

Emrys reached down and lifted the board and took it out.

Up close, in the grey light, the glass had changed. It had always held weather—that was its nature, a captive sky—but now the swirls inside it moved with purpose, gathering, organizing, and at the center of the swirl something bright was trying to form, some pattern pressing against the inside of the glass like a face against a window. Emrys turned it in their fingers and the light shifted, and for half a heartbeat they saw it clearly:

A map. Or the ghost of one. Lines like roads, converging on a point far north and east, beyond the city, beyond the ashline, out where the old stories said the Covenant Stones had stood before the Wardens tore them down.

And beneath the lines, so faint it might have been imagination, a pressure against their mind that was not quite a voice:

*Come.*

The shop door creaked.

Emrys's hand closed around the amulet—too late to hide it, no time, the movement obvious—and they looked up with their heart in their throat, ready to lie or run or, gods help them, fight.

It was a girl. Sixteen maybe, lamplighter's coat two sizes too big, a brass lighting-pole taller than she was, soot on her chin and fury in her eyes. She stared at the amulet in Emrys's bare hand—at the light still fading from it, the map-lines dissolving—and Emrys watched her understand exactly what she was looking at.

"You're a caster," the girl said. Not a question either. Everyone in this city was asking questions they already knew the answers to.

Emrys's mouth opened. Twenty years of practiced denial rose to meet the moment—*it's glasswork, it's a trick of the light, child, run along*—and died there, unused, because the girl's eyes were wet and furious and desperate in a way that Emrys recognized from the inside, and because outside the broken door the sky was still starless, and because the amulet in their hand was warm as a living thing and pulling north.

"What do you want?" Emrys asked, and heard their own voice come out strange: not the charm-seller's dull rasp, but something older. Tired. Honest.

The girl planted her lighting-pole on the floorboards like a knight grounding a lance.

"My mother's dying," she said, "and I need you to teach me."
