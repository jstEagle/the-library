## 5. The Lamplighter's Daughter

Pip had counted the lamps of Greyhollow since she was seven years old, and she knew them the way other children knew the faces of their mothers. There were two hundred and eleven in the market quarter alone—two hundred and twelve if you counted the broken one on Tanner's Row that nobody ever fixed, which Pip did count, because a lamp unlit was still a lamp, just like a person forgotten was still a person.

Tonight she lit them in the dark.

That was the joke of it, the ugly joke the whole city lived inside: since the stars went out, Greyhollow burned more oil than it ever had. The Wardens called for curfew and then discovered that a city with no stars and no moonlight was a city that would break its own legs walking home blind, so the lamplighters worked double shifts now, double pay if you could get it, and Pip—small, quick, fourteen and a half, with hands that smelled forever of lamp oil and tallow—worked triples when her mother's cough let her sleep and quadruples when it didn't.

She went up her ladder at the corner of Candlewick and Ash, struck her flint against the striker, and touched flame to the wick the way her father had taught her before the fever took him: *cup your hand around it, little bird, like you're keeping a secret warm.* The lamp caught. Light spilled gold across the cobbles, across the shuttered fronts of the charm-sellers' stalls, across the frost that had crept up from the gutters and lay on everything like a skin of salt.

Frost in autumn. Frost that thick. Pip had been lighting lamps through frost every winter of her life and this was wrong, this was wrong the way a silence is wrong where there should be breathing, and everyone in Greyhollow felt it and nobody said it out loud because saying things out loud in Greyhollow got you questions, and questions got you the white cloaks.

She cupped her hand around the next flame. Kept the secret warm.

Her mother's secret was a different kind of warmth, and it was dying.

* * *

Maren Quill had been a hedge-witch the way some women are singers—not professionally, not proudly, but because something in her simply would not keep quiet. She could coax a fever down with boiled willowbark and a hummed note. She could find water under dry ground by holding a spoon on a string. When Pip was small she'd pressed a sprig of rosemary into her daughter's palm and said, *This is for remembering, birdie. Hold it when you're scared you'll forget something that matters,* and the rosemary had gone warm as a held hand, and Pip had known, the way you know your own heartbeat, that her mother carried something the city said didn't exist.

Magic. Small magic. Kitchen magic. The kind that never hurt anyone and got people hanged anyway.

Now Maren Quill lay in their rooms above the tannery on Ropewalk Lane, coughing in a wet, rattling way that had started in autumn and deepened with every night the stars stayed dark, and the doctor—Warden-licensed, Warden-paid—had looked at her lungs through his brass tube and pronounced it *damp-lung, chronic, treatable with rest and good air,* and charged four silver for the visit, and left.

Good air. In Greyhollow. Where the smoke from ten thousand chimneys sat in the streets like a lid on a pot.

Pip had tried everything her mother had ever taught her, every trick passed down whisper to whisper along the lamplighters' route: willowbark and honey, mustard plasters, a poultice of comfrey that made the whole room smell like a pond. She'd even done the thing with the spoon and the string over her mother's chest, hunting for the sickness the way her mother hunted for water, and the spoon had spun wild, spinning spinning spinning, pointing nowhere, pointing everywhere, and Pip had stood in the cold room holding a trembling spoon and understood that whatever was inside her mother was bigger than kitchen magic. Bigger than rosemary and humming.

It needed real magic. And real magic, in Greyhollow, meant one person.

Everyone in the market quarter knew about the charm-seller on Candlewick Row. Quiet shop, quiet keeper, charms that were mostly tricks—lucky copper pennies, knotted red thread against headaches, dried lavender sewn into sachets for bad dreams. The kind of harmless nonsense the Wardens tolerated because persecuting it would be admitting it mattered. But Pip lit the lamps on Candlewick Row every evening, and lamplighters see things. That was the whole truth of being a lamplighter: you were invisible, a boy or girl with a ladder whom nobody looked at twice, and so you saw *everything.*

She had seen the charm-seller pause, one evening last spring, over a woman's bruised wrist—and seen the faint blue shimmer rise off the seller's fingertips like heat off summer stone, there and gone, so fast you'd think you dreamed it. She had seen the seller stand in the doorway during the first hard frost, head tipped back toward a sky with no stars in it, lips moving, and seen the frost on the windowpane beside them curl into shapes too deliberate to be accident—a spiral, a branching, almost like writing.

And three nights ago, when the wards had shattered and every star in the sky had died at once, Pip had been up her ladder on Candlewick Row, and she had seen the charm-seller come out into the street and stand very still among the panicking crowd, face turned upward, and Pip would swear on her father's grave that the air around them had *smelled like lightning.* Like the moment before a strike. Ozone and iron and storm.

So tonight, with her mother's coughing audible through the floorboards of the whole tannery, Pip finished her round early, hung her unlit lantern on its hook, and knocked on the door of the charm shop on Candlewick Row.

* * *

The knock took a long time to be answered. Long enough that Pip nearly lost her nerve twice, rehearsing and discarding openings—*I know what you are* (too much like blackmail), *please help me* (too much like begging, though begging was exactly what it was), *my mother is dying* (true, but it opened doors she couldn't close). She settled finally on standing straight, chin up, lamplighter's posture, hands loose at her sides the way you stood when you wanted to be underestimated.

The door opened. The charm-seller looked down at her.

Up close, Emrys Ashgrove was younger than Pip expected and older than they looked, which she couldn't have explained to anyone but felt absolutely: a face that was maybe thirty and eyes that had watched a great many more years than thirty go by. Dark hair going grey at the temples. Hands stained with ink and dried herbs. They wore the flat, careful expression of someone who had practiced not being interesting for a very long time.

"Shop's closed," they said. Their voice was low and even, an accent from somewhere far north that Pip couldn't place.

"I'm not here to buy." Pip lifted her chin another inch. "I'm here because I know."

A pause. The kind of pause that has weight, that has temperature. Somewhere down the street a dog barked at nothing, at the starless dark, and stopped as if embarrassed.

"You'd better come in," Emrys said, "before someone hears you saying foolish things in the street," and Pip understood that she had just been given both a warning and an invitation, and that the two were the same thing.

* * *

Inside, the shop smelled of lavender and beeswax and, underneath it, something else—something green and electric, like the air after rain that hadn't fallen yet. Shelves lined the walls, crowded with jars and bundles and little drawers labeled in a neat, cramped hand. A single lamp burned on the counter. Emrys closed the door, shot the bolt, and stood with their back against it for a moment, looking at Pip the way a person looks at a trap they've walked into with their eyes open.

"So," they said. "You know. Tell me what you know, exactly, and who else you've told."

"Nothing. Nobody." Pip made herself hold those grey eyes. "I know your charms aren't all tricks. I saw—" and she told them, fast and low, about the wrist and the shimmer, the frost that wrote spirals, the smell of lightning on the night the stars went out. Emrys listened without moving, without blinking, and Pip realized halfway through that they weren't listening like a person deciding whether to believe her. They were listening like a person measuring how much danger she was.

When she finished, Emrys was quiet a long moment. Then: "You should forget it. Whatever you think you saw. And you should never say any of it aloud again, not to me, not to anyone. Do you understand? Not as a threat. As the only advice that will keep you alive."

"My mother is dying."

The words came out of Pip harder than she meant them to, cracking in the middle, and she hated the crack, hated the tears that pricked hot behind her eyes, hated being fourteen and small and powerless in a cold shop full of harmless nonsense while upstairs on Ropewalk Lane her mother coughed her life out into a rag.

"She's got damp-lung, the doctor says, but the doctor's wrong, I can feel it's wrong, the spoon spins and won't point, and she taught me things, little things, hedge-things, and none of them are enough, and you—" Pip's voice shook. "I saw what you are. You're a real one. A real caster. And my mother is dying, and the winter's coming down on this city like a lid, and I am asking you. I'm asking you to teach me. Or heal her. Or anything. Please."

Emrys had gone very still. Stillness, Pip would learn later, was what Emrys did instead of flinching.

"No," they said.

Just that. No anger in it, no cruelty. A door closing, gently, all the way.

"Why?" It came out as a wail and Pip dragged it back down to a whisper. "Why not? Are you afraid? Of course you're afraid, everyone's afraid, but she's *dying*, do you understand what that—"

"I understand exactly what it is." For the first time something moved behind the grey eyes, something old and drowned. "I understand better than you could. And the answer is no. If I use what I am—if I teach anyone, heal anyone, be anyone—the Wardens find out, and then it isn't one grave in Greyhollow, girl, it's several. Yours. Your mother's. Mine. Go home. Light your lamps. Forget my face."

"I light your lamps," Pip said stupidly, and then, because grief makes children cruel and clever at once: "Every evening. Cup my hand around the flame. You watch me do it. Don't tell me you don't—I've seen you watching from the window. So don't tell me to forget your face, because I couldn't forget it if I tried, and neither could you."

Silence. The lamp on the counter hissed softly, its flame leaning as though listening.

Emrys closed their eyes. When they opened them again, something had shifted—not softness exactly, but a crack in the wall where softness might get through, and Pip, who had spent her life reading flames and frost and her mother's silences, saw it plain.

"Where's your mother now?" Emrys asked quietly.

"Ropewalk Lane. Above the tannery. Third floor."

"Go home to her," Emrys said. "Sit with her. Keep her warm." They turned away, began straightening jars on a shelf that didn't need straightening. "I'll come by after midnight. If anyone asks, I'm delivering a charm order. Lavender sachets. You ordered lavender sachets."

Pip's heart slammed once, hard, against her ribs. "Is that—does that mean—"

"It means," Emrys said, not turning around, "that no one should die in a city with two hundred and twelve lamps just because everyone in it is afraid. Now go. And girl—" they glanced back, and their face was already the careful mask again, but the voice underneath it wasn't quite, "—if anyone follows you here, if anyone asks you anything about this shop, you ordered lavender sachets and nothing else. Swear it."

"On my father's grave," Pip said, "and on my mother's memory, and on every lamp on Candlewick Row."

Something flickered across Emrys's face at that—approval, or pain, or both—and they waved her out, and Pip ran home through the starless streets with her heart going like a hammer and the frost crunching silver under her boots, and she did not once look behind her, because lamplighters know better than anyone: you never look behind you in the dark. You just carry the flame forward.

* * *

Midnight came down on Ropewalk Lane like a held breath.

Pip sat by her mother's bed with the blanket pulled up to the thin shoulders and the comfrey poultice cooling useless on the nightstand, and Maren Quill slept in shallow snatches, waking to cough, coughing until her face went the color of tallow, sleeping again. Her hands above the blanket were translucent-thin. Pip held one of them and thought about how her mother's hands used to be able to find water under the earth, and how now they couldn't even hold a spoon steady.

At half past midnight, three soft knocks sounded on the street door below. Not the pattern of the Warden patrols—they always knocked loud, entitled, twice and then the rattle of the handle. This was gentle. Almost apologetic.

Pip went down and let Emrys in. They came up the stairs quietly, carrying a canvas satchel, and paused at the threshold of the sickroom, and Pip watched them take in the room—the peeling walls, the single candle, the row of dried herbs hanging from the beam that any Warden informant would read instantly for what it was—and watched something in their expression settle, like a man seeing a country he'd sworn he'd never return to.

"Hedge-work," Emrys murmured. "Real hedge-work. Who taught her?"

"A woman in Saltmarsh, before we came to the city. My mother says the old ways pass hand to hand." Pip hesitated. "Are you going to heal her?"

"I'm going to look first." Emrys set down the satchel. "Healing's not a tap you turn. And I need you to understand something before I touch her at all." They crouched by the bed, close to Maren's labored breathing, and spoke without looking up. "What I do tonight, I do carefully, and small, and hidden. Not because I'm stingy. Because every working leaves a trace, and traces are how the inquisitors hunt. Do you understand?"

"Yes," Pip whispered.

"Do you *understand*? That your mother lives tonight possibly because I stay small, and dies certainly if I show off?"

"Yes," Pip said again, and meant it, and Emrys nodded once and turned to the bed.

What happened next, Pip watched with her whole soul.

Emrys drew back the blanket and laid both hands flat on her mother's chest—not pressing, just resting, the way you'd rest your hands on a drum to feel it breathe. Their eyes half-closed. Their lips moved without sound. And the candle flame on the nightstand leaned toward the bed, leaned and stretched and stood up tall and thin like a dancer en pointe, and the air in the room changed—it warmed, actually warmed, Pip felt it on her cheeks—and beneath Emrys's palms something began to glow. Faint. Blue-white. The color of frost, the color of lightning at a distance, the color of the shimmer she'd seen over the bruised wrist. It sank into her mother's chest like water into cracked earth.

Maren Quill sighed. A long, deep, whole-body sigh, the sigh of someone setting down a weight carried for miles, and her breathing changed—the wet rattle thinning, easing, the rhythm smoothing out like a wrinkled cloth drawn flat.

Emrys worked in silence for what Pip guessed was a quarter hour, though it felt like a season. Sweat stood on their forehead despite the cold room. Twice they swayed slightly and steadied themselves against the bedframe. And Pip, kneeling on the floorboards, watching the light move under those careful hands, understood suddenly and completely why they had said no in the shop. This wasn't a trick. This cost. You could see it costing, drawn up out of them like thread from a spool.

At last the glow faded. Emrys sat back on their heels, gray-faced, breathing hard, and Maren Quill slept on—but differently. Easily. Her chest rose and fell like tide instead of struggle.

"The infection's drawn back from the lung-root," Emrys said hoarsely. "I've bought her time. Weeks, maybe a season. But hear me, and hear me well: I can't cure this. Damp-lung rooted this deep needs more than one night's work, and I—" they stopped, jaw tightening. "There are things I can't spend anymore. Not safely. Not now."

"Then teach me," Pip said.

It came out quiet this time. No begging in it. Just a fact, laid on the table between them like a coin.

Emrys looked at her—at the soot on her cheeks, the oil-stained hands, the eyes that had watched and remembered and refused to forget—and Pip watched the war happen behind their face, watch after watch of it, want and fear tearing at each other like dogs.

"Teaching you puts a target on you," they said at last. "You understand that. The moment you can do more than rosemary and spoons, you're not a lamplighter anymore. You're a residual caster, and the white cloaks burn residuals in the square on feast days, and they make everyone watch."

"My mother's already dying," Pip said. "The winter's already eating the city. The stars are already dead. What exactly is there left for you to put a target on?"

Emrys stared at her. And then—abruptly, shockingly—they laughed. A short, rough, rusty sound, like a hinge unused for years, and they pressed a hand over their eyes and laughed again, quieter, and said something under their breath in that northern language that Pip didn't know but understood anyway, the way you understand a lullaby without the words.

"All right," they said. "All right, you stubborn little candle. Here's how it will be. One lesson. Tomorrow night, after your rounds, in the shop, door locked. I'll teach you the first thing my own mother taught me—which is not a spell at all. It's how to listen. If you have the knack, we'll see. If you don't, you go home and you never speak of this again, and I'll keep coming to tend your mother regardless, because that part isn't conditional. Agreed?"

"Agreed," Pip breathed.

"And Pip." Emrys rose, shouldered the satchel, and at the door they paused, silhouetted against the stair-light, and their voice dropped into something almost gentle. "Your mother taught you rosemary for remembering. That was wise of her. Remembering is going to matter, in the days coming. More than you know." A beat. "Keep your lamp lit, little bird. Even when they tell you to put it out. Especially then."

Then they were gone, down the stairs and into the starless dark, and Pip stood a long time in her mother's room, listening to the easy tide-sound of her breathing, watching the candle flame stand tall and steady as a soldier.

Outside the window, over the rooftops of Greyhollow, the sky was black and empty as a closed eye. But somewhere north, past the burned borderlands where the wards had fallen, Pip felt—knew, the way you know your own heartbeat—that something was moving toward the city through the dark. Something with white cloaks and cold hands and a hunger for people like her, like her mother, like the quiet charm-seller on Candlewick Row.

Tomorrow night, her first lesson. Tonight, she climbed into bed beside her mother's warmth and held the sprig of rosemary from the windowsill pot until it went warm in her palm, and she made herself a promise by every lamp she'd ever lit:

*I will learn. I will not be afraid. And I will remember everything.*

Far to the north, beyond the Ashline, the frost was already spelling names on the stones of ruined villages—and one of the names was hers.
