## 6. What the Frost Remembers

The frost came in the night, and it did not come the way frost should.

Emrys knew cold. They had grown up with it, before exile, before Greyhollow, back when winter was a season and not a sentence. Cold had rules. It crept from the north, pooled in low places, silvered the inside of windowpanes where breath met glass. It was dumb weather, honest weather, the kind of thing a storm-caller learned to read the way a sailor reads water.

This frost broke every rule they knew.

It began at the center of the window above their workbench and spread *outward*, feathering against the direction of the draft, curling into spirals no ice crystal had any business making. Emrys stood in the dark of their shop with a candle stub in one hand and watched it grow, and the hair on their arms rose beneath three layers of wool, because the patterns were not random. Frost was never random, exactly—it had its fern-shapes, its hexagonal grammar—but this was something else. This was deliberate. This was legible.

And then it spelled their name.

Not *Ash*. Not the false name stitched into the collar of their coat, the name they'd bought for two coppers from a forger in the Saltmarket twenty years ago. The letters that bloomed across the glass in slow, glittering strokes were older than that. Deeper.

*Wren.*

Emrys's candle guttered. Their hand did not shake—they had trained that out of themselves long ago, trained the flinch out of their fingers the way one trains a dog not to bark at thunder—but something behind their ribs went very still, the way a rabbit goes still when the hawk's shadow crosses the grass.

No one alive called them Wren. No one *should* know the name. It belonged to a kitchen with a crooked chimney and a mother who sang off-key, to a brother who'd chased them through summer meadows shouting it like a taunt and a challenge both—*Wren, Wren, catch me if you can*—to a life that had ended on a hillside under a sky full of their own making. Tam was dead. Their mother was dead. The village that had named them was ash and rumor, and everyone who might have spoken that name into the world had been silenced by time or by grief or by Emrys's own careful, twenty-year campaign of erasure.

So who—or what—was writing it on their window in ice?

They told themselves the obvious lies first. A trick of the light. A leak in the roof letting warm air draw strange shapes. Sable Vane playing games—the cartographer had an unsettling sense of humor and an even more unsettling habit of appearing wherever she wasn't wanted. But the frost kept growing while they stood there inventing explanations, and it grew with *intention*, each letter finished before the next began, and when the last stroke of the *n* curled into place the whole pane lit faintly from within, blue-white, like moonlight trapped in glass.

Then, beneath the name, smaller, the frost wrote a second line.

*COME HOME.*

Emrys set the candle down very carefully on the workbench, because their hand had finally begun to shake after all.

* * *

They did not sleep. Of course they didn't sleep. They sat in the chair by the cold hearth with a blanket around their shoulders and watched the message slowly fade as the shop's banked warmth ate at the edges of it, and by the time grey dawn seeped through the shutters the window showed nothing but ordinary fog and the ghost of what might, if you squinted, have been condensation.

But Emrys had spent twenty years learning to see what magic left behind, because seeing residue without touching it was how you stayed alive in a city that hanged casters. And there was residue on that glass. Faint as breath on a mirror, but unmistakable once you knew the texture: realm-stuff. Veil-stuff. The same wrongness that had prickled along their skin the night the stars went out, the same hollow note that hummed now beneath every ward-line in Greyhollow like a cracked bell.

The Veil itself had spoken to them. Or something wearing the Veil's voice had.

Emrys made tea they didn't drink and considered the shape of their fear. It was an old companion; they knew all its faces. There was the fear of discovery, which lived in their chest like a tenant and paid its rent in vigilance. There was the fear of their own power, which slept coiled at the base of their skull and dreamed of hillsides and screaming. And there was a third fear, rarer, one they usually managed to go months without meeting: the fear of being *known*. Of the past putting its hand through the wall of the present and saying, I remember you. I never stopped remembering you.

That was the fear with teeth, and it had just walked through their window.

Because here was the truth Emrys had built their whole gray little life on top of: names were threads. In the old craft—the craft their mother had practiced, the craft the Covenant had been woven from—a true name given freely was a door. Whoever held *Wren* held a handle on everything Emrys had buried. And the dying of the Veil meant the old doors were swinging open whether anyone locked them or not.

If the Veil remembered their name, the Veil remembered everything else too. The storm. The hillside. The three bodies the searchers carried down through the rain, and the fourth body they never found, and the lie Emrys had told themselves every day since—that Tam had simply been swept away, that death by drowning in a flash flood was cleaner than death by your sister's lightning.

*Come home.* Home was a word with a corpse in it.

A knock at the shop door made them flinch hard enough to spill the tea.

* * *

"It's me," said Pip's voice through the wood, pitched low, followed immediately, because Pip had never met a low pitch she could maintain, "obviously it's me, who else knocks like a normal person, the Wardens kick doors."

Emrys opened it and the lamplighter's daughter slipped inside carrying a paper sack that smelled of fried dough and a look on her face that Emrys had already learned to dread, because it was the look of a girl who had decided something.

"You didn't come to the corner," Pip said. "Yesterday evening. You always come to the corner when the lamps need trimming, you pretend you're selling me a luck-charm but really you check my wick-work, and you weren't there." She dumped the sack on the counter—two honey buns, Emrys noted, bought with money the girl could not afford—and frowned up at them. Her mother's illness had sharpened her face lately, carved the roundness away, left her eyes too big and too old. "And now you've got your 'nothing's wrong' face on, which is worse, because your 'nothing's wrong' face is the loudest face you own."

"My face is quiet," Emrys said.

"Your face is a locked door with a welcome mat," Pip said. "I've been reading people's faces since I was six, it's how you survive asking the rich streets for lamp-oil money. So." She crossed her arms. "What's wrong?"

The easy answer was *nothing*, delivered flat, and Pip would have pushed for exactly four more minutes before letting it go with a scowl. That was the dance they'd been doing since Emrys had broken their own first rule and healed the frostbitten child, since they'd broken the second rule and agreed—God help them—to teach this stubborn, brilliant, reckless girl the first shapes of hedge-craft. Rules were made to be broken by Pip specifically; she seemed to operate on that assumption about all rules everywhere.

But Emrys looked at her—sixteen, sharp-eyed, standing in a dead woman's shop in a city whose sky had gone black—and heard the frost say *come home*, and found they did not want to lie. Not entirely. Lying entirely had cost them twenty years, and the interest was coming due.

"The stars went out," Emrys said. "You know that. Everyone knows that. What nobody knows is why, and I—" They stopped. Started again, quieter. "Last night, something wrote on my window. In frost. My childhood name."

Pip went still in a way that was almost eerie, all that restless energy suddenly gathered and pointed like a lamp-beam. "Your real name?"

"My real name." Emrys turned toward the window, where the morning fog was already burning off. "One I haven't heard spoken aloud in twenty years. One that exactly zero living people know." They hesitated over the next part and gave it anyway, because Pip had earned it, and because saying it aloud felt like testing a bruise. "It said *come home*."

"Home where?" Pip asked. Then her eyes widened, and Emrys watched the same conclusion arrive behind them that had been sitting in Emrys's own chest all night. "The Stones. Sable says the wards failed at the Covenant Stones. If the Veil's... if whatever's left of it is talking..."

"Then it's talking to someone it thinks is listening," Emrys finished. "Which is a problem, because I have spent two decades making sure I am extremely deaf."

Pip was quiet for a moment—an event rare enough that Emrys marked it. When she spoke again, her voice had lost its bounce and gained something steadier underneath. "My mum used to say the dead don't haunt houses. They haunt unfinished things." She shrugged, awkward with her own tenderness. "Maybe the Veil's got unfinished business with you."

"Maybe the Veil should have finished it," Emrys said, sharper than intended, and saw Pip take a half-step back, and hated themselves for it. They softened. "I'm sorry. It's only—there are things I buried for good reasons. Reasons with graves attached. If the past is digging itself up, it isn't going to stop at my window. It'll keep going until it's dug up everything, and some of what's down there will bury us all."

"Then we'd better know what's coming," Pip said simply. "That's what Sable says. She maps things so they can't sneak up on you." A pause, and then, with the terrifying directness that was rapidly becoming familiar: "You should tell her. About the window."

"I should absolutely not tell her about the window."

"She traced the ward-failure across four provinces with a broken compass and a bad knee," Pip said. "She figured out you're a storm-caller in about nine seconds flat, which, by the way, you should know your disguise is terrible, you flinch every time anyone mentions weather. She's the smartest person either of us knows and she's already decided you matter, so telling her isn't giving anything away. It's just... catching up."

Before Emrys could construct the seventeen objections lined up behind their teeth, the shop bell rang—not the door bell, which announced customers, but the small brass bell on the shelf that Emrys had warded years ago to shiver when someone with official intent came within fifty paces of the shop. It shivered now, a thin urgent tremolo.

Both of them froze.

"Wardens?" Pip whispered.

"Or Sable," Emrys murmured, though their stomach had dropped through the floorboards. Through the shutters' slats they could see the street: early market traffic, a fish-cart, a knot of children chasing a dog. Ordinary. And at the far corner, where Lamplighters' Row bent toward the square, three figures in slate-gray coats moving with the unhurried certainty of people who owned the ground they walked on.

Inquisitors. Not searching—*walking a route*. Sweeping. The way they'd swept the market quarter last week, hauling off the hedge-witch Osla and two innocent tinker brothers who'd had the misfortune to sell her copper wire.

"They're doing rounds," Pip breathed. "They've never done rounds up here before. We're too poor to be interesting."

"We were too poor to be interesting until the sky went dark and they needed someone to blame." Emrys moved without thinking, hands already doing the old practiced work: the charm-racks tilted to display only the harmless trinkets, the love-knots and traveler's blessings, the drawer of real workings sliding shut beneath the false bottom. The storm-glass amulet at their throat they tucked under their shirt, its familiar weight suddenly enormous, a heartbeat hanging against their sternum. "Back room. Now. If they ask, you're a customer who bought a luck-charm for your mother, which is even true, which makes it the best kind of lie."

Pip grabbed the honey buns off the counter—priorities—and slipped through the curtain. Emrys arranged their face into the dull, slightly put-upon expression of a small merchant interrupted during inventory, and had just enough time to notice that their hands were steady, that twenty years of practice held even now, before the knock came.

Three knocks. Measured. The kind of knock that expected the door to open itself.

Emrys opened it.

The lead inquisitor was a woman with a face like a folded map—all creases and hidden routes—and eyes the pale gray of old ice. She smiled without engaging any part of her face above the mouth. Behind her, the other two fanned out with professional boredom, one drifting toward the charm racks.

"Merchant Ash," the woman said, consulting a slate. "Charm-seller, licensed, licensed, and—" her eyes flicked up, "—licensed. You renew promptly. That's rare, in your quarter."

"Fear's a fine motivator," Emrys said, keeping the voice level and dull. Inside, beneath the shirt, the storm-glass pulsed once, warm, like a second heart noticing danger. *Steady,* Emrys told it silently. *Steady.*

"Mm." The inquisitor stepped inside uninvited, boots leaving wet prints. She picked up a luck-charm, turned it over. "Sky troubles everyone these days. Stars gone. Wards gone. People say the old times are coming back." She set the charm down with a small click. "Old times had old names in them, merchant Ash. Names the archives remember, even when people would rather forget." Her pale eyes settled on Emrys with the mild weight of a hand on a scale. "You'd be surprised what the archives remember."

Every drop of blood in Emrys's body went cold and stayed cold, and they heard themselves say, in the flat Greyhollow accent they'd worn like a coat for two decades: "Archives aren't much use to me. Can't read past my own ledgers."

"No," the inquisitor agreed pleasantly. "I imagine you can't."

She let the silence stretch exactly long enough to be a message, then touched her hat brim and withdrew, her colleagues folding in behind her. The door closed. The footsteps faded. Somewhere down the street, a fishmonger resumed shouting his prices, and the world pretended to be ordinary again.

Emrys stood in the middle of their shop and breathed, and breathed, and breathed.

*Names the archives remember.* Coincidence? The Wardens swept every poor quarter eventually; that was procedure, not prophecy. And yet. The frost on the window. The inquisitor's eyes. The feeling, crawling up the back of their neck like a draft from a door they'd sworn was sealed, that the past was not merely refusing to stay buried—it was being *dug up*, deliberately, by hands that knew precisely where to dig.

Pip emerged from the back room, white-faced, clutching the honey buns like a talisman. "Did they—"

"They were delivering a message," Emrys said quietly. "Whether they knew it or not." They looked at the window, bare of frost now, showing only the gray street and the gray sky where stars should have been. *Come home,* the ice had said. And the Wardens were walking the streets saying, in their way, *we remember old names too.*

Two forces, closing from opposite directions, and Emrys Ashgrove standing in the narrow space between them with a stolen name and a buried past and a girl holding breakfast.

"Pip," they said. "Go find Sable Vane. Tell her I need to see her maps. All of them. Tell her—" They stopped, and heard their own voice change, dropping the merchant's dullness for something older and rawer. "Tell her I think I'm done hiding. Tell her I want to know the road to the Stones."

Pip stared at them. Then, slowly, she grinned—the first real grin Emrys had seen on her in days, bright as a struck match—and shoved one of the honey buns into their hand.

"Took you long enough," she said, and was out the door and running before Emrys could remember why they'd ever wanted to stay invisible at all.
