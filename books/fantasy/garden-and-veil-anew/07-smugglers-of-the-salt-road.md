## 7. Smugglers of the Salt Road

Sable Vane had drawn maps for kings, cartographers' guilds, and once—for six disastrous weeks—a pirate crew that couldn't read. She had learned, in twenty years of charting coastlines and mountain passes and the small dishonest spaces between borders, that the most important features on any map were never inked at all. They were the places where a road bent out of sight. The places where someone was waiting.

The Salt Road bent out of sight three miles past the Ashline checkpoint, behind a shelf of black rock the locals called the Deacon's Jaw, and Sable would have bet her good compass—her *only* compass—that someone was waiting there.

"Stop fidgeting," she said to Pip, who had asked her some question about the rock formations and then abandoned it halfway through Sable's answer to fidget instead.

"I'm not fidgeting."

"You've checked your bootlaces four times."

"They were loose."

"They're boots, Pip. They're meant to be loose." Sable adjusted her satchel strap and kept walking. "Besides, if you keep looking down you'll miss it when they show themselves, and I want witnesses when I'm right."

Behind them, Emrys walked with their hood up and their hands in their pockets, which was how Emrys did everything: contained, folded away, a person determined to take up as little of the world as possible. Marrow's inn was two days behind them now, and its warmth still clung to Sable like woodsmoke in wool. She kept turning over what Marrow had said—or hadn't said—at the door that morning. *The Brambles run the Salt Road past the second ridge. Tell them the innkeeper still owes for the winter of the red snow.* Then he'd pressed a wrapped parcel of bread and hard cheese into her hands and shut the door before she could ask what the winter of the red snow was.

Everyone on this road owed everyone something. It was, Sable supposed, a kind of currency. In Greyhollow the coin was silence; out here it was debt.

The land itself was wrong in a way no map could capture. She'd tried, the night before, by lamplight, sketching the borderlands from memory, and had ended up staring at her own work with something close to nausea. The hills were the right shape. The riverbeds were where riverbeds should be. But the color was draining out of everything the farther east they walked, as though the world were an old engraving left too long in the sun—the grass gone grey-green, the sky the color of dishwater, the distant mountains fading into a suggestion of mountains. Sable had spent her life believing that places had essences, that a mapmaker who paid attention could catch them. This place felt like it was being *erased*, patiently, from the edges inward.

"It's getting worse," Emrys said quietly. Not a question.

"The color? Yes." Sable didn't look back. "I mapped this stretch eleven years ago. There was a shrine about half a mile ahead—little thing, roadside, travelers used to leave ribbons. If it's still there, we can gauge how fast it's spreading."

"And if it isn't?"

"Then I'll be unhappy, and you'll all have to live with that."

Pip snorted. Even that small sound seemed loud in the drained landscape, where nothing sang and nothing buzzed and even the wind moved as if embarrassed to be noticed.

They crested a low rise, and Sable saw the place where the road bent behind the Deacon's Jaw, and she stopped so abruptly that Pip walked into her back.

The shrine was there. But every ribbon that had ever been tied to it hung motionless—and colorless. Dozens of them, silk and wool and braided grass, all of them grey as ash, all of them frozen mid-flutter as though the wind had simply stopped agreeing to touch them. And beneath the shrine, sitting on the milestone like it was a garden bench, was a woman eating an apple.

"Well," said Sable. "Right on schedule."

* * *

The woman was broad-shouldered and freckled, with copper hair cropped short and a knife she wasn't bothering to hide strapped to her thigh. She watched them approach with the unhurried attention of someone who had already decided how the next ten minutes would go and was merely waiting for everyone else to catch up.

"You're a long way from your map table, Vane," she said.

Sable stopped six feet away. "I don't know you."

"No." The woman took another bite of apple. "But you know my sister's work. That route through the Fenmark marshes you sold to the grain merchants three years back—the one that shaved two days off the crossing?" She tapped the milestone with her boot heel. "Bramble route. My sister cut it. You traced it, cleaned it up, and charged the merchants a fortune without so much as a thank-you note."

Sable felt her ears go warm. In her defense, the route had been *beautiful*. "I improved it."

"You stole it."

"I *improved* it," Sable repeated, "which is what one does with raw material. Who are you?"

The woman stood, tossed the apple core into the grey grass, and stuck out a hand callused like tree bark. "Rowan Bramble. The pretty one is my sister Alder, and she's currently deciding whether to rob you or feed you, so do try to be charming."

Alder Bramble stepped out from behind the black rock of the Deacon's Jaw as if she'd been part of it until that moment—tall where Rowan was wide, with the same copper hair worn long and braided, and a crossbow held with the relaxed grip of someone who'd never once needed to hurry. Her eyes went over each of them in turn: Sable, Pip, and lastly Emrys, on whom they lingered a beat too long.

"Four travelers," Alder said. "No pack animals. Heading east on the Salt Road, which doesn't go anywhere anymore except the old Stones country, which nobody goes to unless they're running from something or toward something worse." She smiled, and it was a nice smile, which somehow made it worse. "And one of you is wearing a storm-glass amulet under a charm-seller's coat, which is either very brave or very stupid, and my money's on both."

The silence that followed had weight. Sable heard Pip's breath catch. Beside her, Emrys had gone very still—the particular stillness of a person calculating distances to cover and whether running would help.

"How—" Pip started.

"Your coat hem's wet with meltwater and it's been below freezing since the pass," Alder said mildly. "Storm-glass runs warm. Anyone who's handled one knows. Also you walk like someone listening to weather that hasn't arrived yet." She shrugged. "We're smugglers. Noticing things is the whole trade."

Rowan cracked her knuckles cheerfully. "So! Robbing or feeding. Sister, thoughts?"

"Depends." Alder lowered the crossbow—not all the way, but enough. "On what they're carrying besides the amulet."

"Nothing worth taking," Sable said quickly.

"That's not what I asked."

Sable weighed it. This was the moment, she knew—the hinge of the whole enterprise, the bend in the road made flesh. She could lie, and these two would see through it inside a day, because people who ran contraband past Warden checkpoints for a living could smell a concealed truth the way sharks smelled blood. Or she could tell the truth, which was more dangerous and also, annoyingly, the only strategy she'd ever seen actually work in the borderlands.

She looked at Emrys. Emrys gave the smallest nod, hood shadowing everything but the set of their mouth.

"We're going to the Covenant Stones," Sable said. "All the way. And we need to get past three checkpoints and whatever Corvess has got crawling over the passes, and Marrow says you owe him for the winter of the red snow, and frankly I think that's leverage we should all be able to work with."

Rowan whistled low. "The Stones. Nobody's been to the Stones in—"

"Forty years," Alder said. Her smile had faded into something sharper and more thoughtful. "Since the Sundering. Since the Wardens sealed the high country and called it protection." She looked at Emrys again, longer this time, and whatever she saw there made her shoulders drop a fraction, the crossbow finally swinging to hang at her side. "You're really doing it, then. You're really trying to fix it."

"We're going to try," Emrys said. Their voice was quiet, roughened by disuse, and Sable heard Pip lean in slightly just to hear it. "Whether we can fix anything is another matter entirely."

Alder and Rowan exchanged a look—one of those sibling conversations conducted entirely in eyebrow positions, which Sable had always envied and never managed to learn. It lasted perhaps three seconds.

"Feeding," Rowan announced. "Definitely feeding. You can't rob people who look like that. It'd be like kicking a stray dog that's already apologized to you."

"I don't look like a stray dog," said Pip.

"You absolutely do, sweetheart," said Rowan, already digging in her pack. "You've got the big eyes and everything. Sit down. We've got smoked trout, black bread, and a jar of pickled onions that Alder swears by and I swear *at*, and if you're crossing the Ashline proper tomorrow you'll want to eat like it's a religion."

* * *

They ate around a fire built in a hollow lined with slate, screened from the road, burning wood so dry it crackled almost silently. Sable noted, professionally, that the twins had chosen the spot with a surveyor's eye—wind direction, sight lines, two escape routes—and filed it away under *things that will keep us alive*.

"So here's the situation," Alder said, tearing bread. "Checkpoint at the Ashline crossing is doubled since the stars went out. Wardens on the bridge, Wardens on the ford, and a new lot—grey cloaks, no insignia—who don't talk to the others and give Sable here the shivers, judging by her face."

"My face is fine."

"Your face is doing arithmetic and coming up frightened. It's all right. Mine does sums too." Alder pointed the bread at her. "Grey cloaks started appearing about a month ago. Before the dark. Which interests me, because everyone official is pretending the dark was a surprise."

Sable sat forward despite herself. "Before?"

"Mm. Rowan and I move goods through the pass country year-round. We know every patrol pattern like you know your rivers. Six weeks back, the rotation changed. New routes, new timing, and a standing order nobody would explain: no traffic east of the third ridge, no exceptions, shoot first and file the paperwork after." Alder's eyes were steady on hers. "Somebody knew the Veil was failing, Vane. Somebody knew early, and they put soldiers on the roads to make sure no one got curious about why."

The fire popped. Out beyond the slate-lined hollow, the colorless evening was settling over the land like a lid.

"That matches," Emrys said softly. Everyone turned. They had barely spoken through the meal, sitting with their hands wrapped around a tin cup of tea they weren't drinking. "The wards didn't fail. They were *unwoven*. I felt it happen—I've felt ward-work my whole life, and what broke over Greyhollow that night wasn't decay. Decay frays. This was picked apart, thread by thread, by someone who knew exactly what they were holding." A pause. "Someone patient."

"Patient and well-funded," Sable said. "Which narrows the list considerably."

"Corvess," Pip said, and then flinched, as if saying the name aloud might summon her.

"Maybe. Maybe Corvess is just the hand." Rowan leaned back against her pack, arms crossed. "Hands get replaced. Whatever's driving this has been driving a long time."

Sable found herself watching the twins as they talked—watching the easy rhythm between them, the way they finished each other's tactical assessments without seeming to notice they were doing it. She had worked alone for most of her career. It had seemed efficient at the time. Sitting here, she felt the peculiar vertigo of realizing that efficiency might not have been the actual reason.

"You've decided to help us," she said. "I notice neither of you has actually said so."

Rowan grinned. "Haven't we?"

"You've fed us and told us useful things. That's hospitality, not commitment."

"Sable," Alder said, and there was something new in her voice—not sharp now, but level and serious, the voice of a woman reading terrain. "Six weeks ago we watched soldiers seal the passes against a disaster nobody had announced. Three weeks ago our cousin in Hollowmere stopped writing letters, and when Rowan went to check on her, half the village was standing in their gardens staring at nothing. Last week the stars went out, and every honest road east became a Warden shooting gallery." She turned her tin cup slowly in her hands. "You four are the first people we've met who are heading *toward* the problem instead of away from it. What exactly were we going to do—stay home and pickle more onions?"

"There's a market for pickled onions," Rowan said.

"There is not a market for pickled onions, they're vile—"

"They're *acquired*—"

"*Children* spit them out, Rowan, children, whose palates are famously undiscriminating—"

Sable let them argue. It was, she suspected, partly performance—a pressure valve, humor pumped into the space where fear wanted to pool. She recognized the technique because she used it herself, constantly, usually with footnotes. But underneath the bickering she could see the other thing, the true thing: these two had been waiting for a errand worthy of them, and had known it, and here it came walking up the Salt Road with a stolen route-map reputation and a hidden amulet.

"All right," Alder said finally, when Rowan had conceded that children were indeed unimpeachable critics. "Here's the offer. We take you through the Ashline tomorrow at first light, via the dry wash north of the bridge—Wardens can't see into it from the road, and the grey cloaks don't patrol it because there's officially nothing there. We move at night from there on. Rowan knows the old shepherd tracks; I handle the talking if we're stopped, because I'm prettier and more believable."

"You are neither of those things."

"I'm *believable*, which is different from honest." Alder looked around the fire, at each of them in turn, and her voice dropped into something plain and unadorned. "Terms are simple. You follow instructions on the road—no heroics, no improvising, and especially no magic unless someone's about to die, and then only the minimum required to prevent it. We've moved casters before. The ones who died were the ones who thought a spell could solve what patience couldn't."

Emrys nodded slowly. "Agreed."

"Just like that?" Pip blurted. "You'll take us all the way?"

"To the foothills below the Stones, yes. Past that, nobody goes—not us, not anyone. The high country's..." Alder hesitated, glancing at her sister, and for the first time since they'd met, she looked uncertain. "Wrong. Worse than the lowlands. The color drains out of things, but up there it's more than color. Rowan scouted within a day's walk of the Stones last summer, before all this, and came back three days late and wouldn't say why for a week."

Everyone looked at Rowan. Rowan, for the first time all evening, wasn't smiling.

"Frost," she said shortly. "There was frost on the rocks in midsummer, and it wasn't shaped like frost. It was shaped like—" She stopped. Shook her head. "Writing. Old writing. And when I got close enough to read it, I remembered things I'd never done. A house I'd never lived in. A dog I'd never owned. Sat down on a rock and cried about a dog that never existed for the better part of an hour." She picked up her cup and drank, decisively, as if to wash the sentence away. "So. Foothills yes, Stones no. After that you're on your own, and may every god anyone remembers have mercy, because I suspect most of them forgot this country years ago."

The fire settled. Somewhere far off, wind moved through the grey grass with a sound like paper turning.

Sable pulled out her journal, mostly to have something to do with her hands, and found she'd already sketched the Deacon's Jaw and the hollow and the slate-lined firepit without noticing. Beneath it, her pen added a line she hadn't planned:

*Bramble, R. & A., smugglers. Owed: everything, probably.*

"Get some sleep," Alder said, rising. "First light. And Vane—" She paused at the edge of the firelight, silhouetted against the colorless dusk. "That route through the Fenmark. It saved my sister's life once, during the flood year. She's never mentioned it, because she's proud, but I'm telling you so you know the ledger runs both ways."

She walked off into the dark to take first watch, leaving Sable staring at her own handwriting with her ears warm again and absolutely no professional justification for it whatsoever.

Beside her, Emrys had finally drunk their tea. Pip was already asleep, curled against her pack with her lamplighter's pole within arm's reach, dreaming, Sable hoped, of lit streets and unextinguished stars.

Tomorrow they crossed the Ashline. Tomorrow the real country began—the wounded country, the written-in-frost country, the country that was forgetting itself one village at a time.

Sable closed her journal, and in the last light of the fire, she caught Emrys watching the eastern darkness with an expression she'd begun to recognize: not fear, exactly. Recognition. Like a person hearing footsteps in a house they'd once lived in.

"Emrys," she said quietly. "What's at the Stones? Truly. What are we walking into?"

Emrys was silent long enough that she thought they wouldn't answer.

"A door," they said at last. "One my mother helped build. And I think—" their hand rose, unconsciously, to the amulet hidden beneath their coat, "—I think it's been knocking."

Above the black line of the ridges, where stars should have been, the sky stayed empty. But far off, so faint it might have been imagination, the frost on the high rocks was beginning, very softly, to glow.
