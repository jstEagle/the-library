## 8. Twenty Years of Quiet

The charm-seller's stall smelled of cedar oil and dried lavender, and Emrys Ashgrove had spent twenty years making sure it smelled like nothing else—no ozone, no rain-on-hot-stone, none of the scents that lived in the back of their throat like a name they refused to say.

They were restocking the luck-knots when the memory came, the way it always did: not invited, but not turned away either.

* * *

It had been summer in the Ashgrove valley the year everything ended. Emrys was nineteen, and the sky over the high pasture had gone the color of a bruise by mid-afternoon, green-black at the edges where the clouds stacked themselves into towers. The whole village knew what that meant. The whole village knew who that meant.

"Emrys." Their mother had stood in the doorway of the bindery, flour on her hands, her voice carefully light. "The Merrow farm's hay is still out. Old Bram says it'll rot if it doesn't come in tonight."

"I know."

"And you'll be careful."

"I'm *always* careful." They had said it with the careless arrogance of nineteen, shrugging on their storm-coat, the one their mother had stitched with silver thread along the seams because she believed—falsely, sweetly—that thread could hold a person together the way it held cloth.

Tam had been waiting for them at the gate, sixteen and all elbows, his hair sticking up where he'd slept on it wrong. He carried two apples and handed one over without being asked, which was how he said most things.

"You don't have to come," Emrys told him.

"You don't get to decide that." Tam bit his apple, talked around it. "Besides, someone has to count the lightning for you. You always lose track after the third strike. You go somewhere else. Your eyes do this—" he waved the apple core vaguely at his own face, "—*thing*."

Emrys had laughed. That was the detail that returned most often, in the years after, arriving unbidden at the worst moments: they had laughed, easy and bright, standing in a field that would become a grave.

The calling itself had been simple. A storm already gathering; a storm-caller only needed to ask it to fall *elsewhere*, to bend its shoulder away from the hay and the harvesters and pour it out over the empty moor instead. Emrys had done it a hundred times. They were good at it—the best the valley had produced in three generations, people said, and Emrys had believed them, had let the belief sit warm under their ribs like a swallowed coal.

They remembered raising their hands. They remembered the taste of copper at the back of their tongue, the pressure drop, the way every hair on their arms lifted toward the sky as if the storm were a lover leaning close. They remembered Tam counting—"one, two, *three*"—his voice thinning as the wind rose, and then the wind took his voice entirely, and Emrys went *into* it, into the wide white silence behind their own eyes where there was no field and no brother and no body, only the storm's vast indifferent joy.

That was the thing no one understood about calling weather. It wasn't commanding. It was *consenting*. You opened yourself and let something enormous move through you, and for as long as it moved, you were not entirely a person. You were a door.

And doors cannot see what walks through them.

* * *

"—Master Ashgrove? The charms?"

Emrys blinked. A customer stood at the stall, a broad woman with chilblains on her knuckles, holding up a luck-knot and looking at them with mild impatience.

"Two coppers," Emrys said. Their voice came out steady. Twenty years of practice made even grief sound like small talk. "For travel, hang it facing east. For luck at cards, don't bother; nothing helps at cards."

The woman laughed, paid, went on into the grey morning of Greyhollow. Emrys stood among their wares—fever-charms, find-your-way-home knots, little glass beads that fogged before rain—and pressed both hands flat against the counter until the shaking stopped.

Three people. That was the arithmetic they carried everywhere, sewn into them like the silver thread in an old coat. Three people dead in the Merrow field: old Bram Merrow himself, struck down beside his own hay; a hired girl named Sefa Oduya, seventeen, who had run toward the barn instead of away from it; and—

And Tam.

They had never found him. That was the cruelty inside the cruelty. Bram and Sefa lay where the lightning left them, but Tam was simply *gone*, no body, no coat, nothing but one apple core half-buried in the mud, and Emrys had searched that field until their knees bled through their trousers, calling his name into a silence that had no weather left in it at all.

The tribunal had been merciful, in the way that mercy is sometimes just violence wearing softer gloves. Storm-calling was licensed work in the valley lands; a caller who killed was not hanged but *unmade*—stripped of license, of name-rights, of place. Exile. The elders had called it compassion. *You may live,* they said. *Only not here. Only not as yourself.*

Their mother had walked them to the border stone. She had not cried, which Emrys understood later was the bravest possible thing, because crying would have made leaving harder and staying impossible. She had pressed something into their hand at the parting: a small amulet of storm-glass, sea-green and cloudy, threaded on a leather cord.

"It was your grandmother's," she'd said. "It holds what you are. Not what you did. Don't lose it, and don't lose the difference."

Then she had turned around and walked back down the road, and she had not looked back, and Emrys had stood at the border stone until dark, learning the first lesson of exile: that the people who love you can survive losing you, and that this is somehow worse than if they couldn't.

* * *

Greyhollow in the early afternoon was all wet slate and woodsmoke, the city hunched under its low sky like an animal trying to make itself small. Magic was outlawed here—not regulated, not taxed, *outlawed*—and so the city ran on a thousand small hypocrisies: charms sold openly as "keepsakes," hedge-remedies labeled as folk medicine, everyone agreeing not to see what everyone saw. Emrys had chosen it precisely because of the hypocrisy. A city full of people pretending was a city where one more pretender vanished easily.

Twenty years of quiet. They had built it brick by brick, rule by rule.

Rule one: never call anything larger than a candle-flame of power. Rule two: never stay anywhere longer than five years, because five years was how long it took people to start asking about your past. Rule three—and this was the load-bearing wall of the whole structure—never, ever learn anyone's true name or give them yours, because names were hooks, and hooks were how you came to matter to people, and mattering was the first step on the road back to the field, back to the counting, back to *one, two, three*.

They had broken rule three twice. Once for a woman in a river town whose name Emrys still deliberately did not think about. And once, four years ago, for a sharp-tongued mapmaker who had barged into their stall demanding to know why the "keepsake" compasses actually worked, and who had looked at Emrys's face for exactly three seconds too long before saying, "Huh. Fine. Keep your secrets, Master Nobody. But my maps are honest, and someday yours will have to be too."

Sable Vane. Emrys had spent four years regretting the acquaintance and four years failing to end it.

They were thinking about Sable—about the way she'd looked at the sky last night, when the stars went out, with an expression Emrys recognized from mirrors—when the frost crept in at the edge of their vision.

Not real frost. Memory-frost. It happened sometimes, in the years since exile: cold blooming across the mind's eye, and in the cold, images printed themselves like breath on glass. The field. The bruise-colored sky. Tam's apple core in the mud.

But this time the frost brought something new.

This time it brought the sound.

For twenty years, Emrys's memory of the storm had been silent after the third strike—a hole in the recollection, smooth-edged, which they had assumed was trauma's mercy. Now, in the middle of a Greyhollow afternoon, with the stars dead overhead and the wards of the world lying in ruins, the hole filled in.

Wind first. Then a voice, thin and torn by gale but unmistakably alive, unmistakably *his*:

"*Emrys! EMRYS! I'm here—I'm HERE—don't stop, keep calling, I can't see you—EMRYS!*"

Emrys gripped the counter. The stall tilted. Somewhere a customer was saying something about fever-charms and Emrys heard none of it, because underneath twenty years of quiet, a door that had been sealed was now standing open, and through it came the truth like cold air into a warm room:

Tam had been alive. Tam had been *calling for them*. Tam had survived the storm—and Emrys had walked away from that field believing otherwise, had built an entire life on the foundation of a death that never happened.

No. No, that couldn't be right. They had searched. They had torn the field apart blade of grass by blade of grass. If Tam had been alive, if he'd been shouting—

Unless he hadn't been shouting in the field.

The thought arrived with the gentleness of a landslide. Unless the voice in the memory was coming from somewhere else. From *after*. From whatever happened between the lightning and the tribunal, those nine days Emrys could barely remember—grief-drunk, sedated with poppy and valerian, half-mad, while the elders gathered testimony and the Warden's men, who had jurisdiction over licensed callers even out in the valley lands, conducted their interviews.

Testimony. The tribunal had run on testimony. Bram was dead; Sefa was dead; the only living witness to what happened in that field was the storm-caller who had caused it, and a storm-caller in shock is not a credible witness to anything, least of all to their own innocence.

So whose word had condemned them?

Emrys tried to remember the tribunal itself and found the memory frosted over, blurred, the faces of the elders smudged like thumbprints on glass. That was wrong too. That was *newly* wrong. Memories didn't usually decay in neat shapes; they decayed all at once, like fruit. Something had eaten the center out of that week and left the rind.

Something—or someone.

They became aware, gradually, that the market sounds had changed. Fewer voices. More boots. At the far end of Lantern Row, near the ward-post where the city's anti-magic bells hung, a knot of grey cloaks was moving stall to stall, and the crowd was bending around them the way water bends around a stone.

Inquisitors. Of course. The night the stars died, the whole city had felt the wards shatter, and the Wardens' answer to a dying sky was always the same: find someone to blame, and make sure the someone was small enough to arrest.

Emrys's hands moved on their own, the way hands do when the body has learned survival better than the mind. Luck-knots straightened. Fever-charms angled to hide the faint true shimmer in the glass. The storm-glass amulet slipped beneath their shirt collar, cool against the sternum, holding what they were—not what they did, their mother had said, though some days the difference felt like a paper wall in a flood.

*Keep calling.* Tam's voice again, echoing in the frost. *Don't stop.*

*I didn't know,* Emrys wanted to tell the ghost, the memory, the maybe-living man. *I didn't know you were alive. I built twenty years on top of your grave and the grave was empty and I never once looked inside—*

Because looking inside meant hoping, and hope was heavier than shame. Shame you could carry. Hope carried *you*, wherever it liked, over cliffs.

A shadow fell across the stall. Grey cloak. The inquisitor was young, with a clipboard and a bored mouth, and asked the question every charm-seller in Greyhollow had answered a hundred times: "Licensed goods only? Nothing residual?"

"Keepsakes and folk remedies," Emrys said. "I sell memories of good weather, officer. Nothing more."

The young man almost smiled, wrote something, moved on.

Emrys stood in the grey light of a starless noon, in a city pretending not to be magic, holding a secret that had just doubled in weight. Because it was no longer only *what happened in the field*. It was *who took the memory of the tribunal*, and *why*, and whether the same hand that had hollowed out nine days of Emrys's life had also hollowed out the sky.

Somewhere across the rooftops, the anti-magic bells began to ring—not the alarm peal, but the deeper, slower cadence that meant a proclamation. The whole city paused to listen, faces tilting upward like flowers toward a sun that wasn't there anymore.

Emrys closed their hand around the storm-glass amulet and listened too, heart hammering out a rhythm older than exile, older than shame:

*One. Two. Three.*
