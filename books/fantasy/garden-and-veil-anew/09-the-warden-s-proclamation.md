## 9. The Warden's Proclamation

Pip had never seen Greyhollow so quiet.

She stood on the corner of Lamplighter's Row with her brass taper-pole in hand, the way she did every dusk, and the silence pressed against her ears like snowfall. No cart wheels on the cobbles. No fishwives arguing over the last of the herring. Even the gulls had gone somewhere else, as if the whole city were holding its breath beneath a sky that gave nothing back.

The stars had been gone for eight days now. Eight days of a sky like a slate wiped clean, and Pip had learned that people could bear almost anything except an empty ceiling. They walked faster. They spoke less. They lit candles in windows that faced the wrong direction entirely, because there was no longer any direction worth facing.

Her mother was the same way. Worse, maybe. The fever had settled into her chest like a tenant who'd decided to stay through winter, and every morning Pip woke to hear that wet, rattling breath and thought: *not yet, not yet, not yet.* She'd taken to leaving the lamp outside their door burning all night—not because her mother needed light, but because Pip needed to believe something was still burning somewhere.

"Oi, Pip." Old Hessa from the chandlery, wrapped in three shawls, her breath fogging. "You hear? They're ringing the Bell of Office at full dark. Full dark, mind. Not curfew bell—*Office* bell."

Pip's stomach dropped. The Bell of Office hadn't rung since before she was born. It only rang when the Wardens wanted the whole city listening at once.

"I heard," she said, though she hadn't. "What's it about?"

Hessa's eyes went sideways, down the row, up again. "Nobody knows. But they've been nailing papers to doors since noon. Warden's men. Didn't say a word, just *bang bang bang* with the hammer, like they were nailing shut a coffin." She pulled her shawls tighter. "My boy read me a bit of one before I shooed him inside. Said it was about attestations. Purity attestations."

"Purity," Pip repeated flatly.

"That's what he said." Hessa looked at her then, really looked, and there was something in the old woman's face that made Pip's skin prickle—a question being asked without words. Hessa bought charms from Emrys Ashgrove's stall. Half the street did, half pretending they didn't. And everyone knew Pip ran messages for the charm-seller, and everyone knew what charm-sellers were, and nobody said anything, because that was how Greyhollow survived: by knowing everything and saying nothing.

"You should be home before the bell," Hessa said quietly. "Whatever it says."

"I've got lamps to light."

"Lamps can wait one night."

"They can't." Pip lifted her chin. "That's rather the point of lamps, isn't it?"

Hessa sighed, muttered something about stubborn girls, and shuffled back toward her shop. Pip watched her go, then turned to face the long row of iron posts stretching away into the gloom, each one waiting for its flame.

Lighting the lamps was the only thing she did anymore that felt honest. Her hands knew the work—the taper cupped against the wind, the wick catching with that soft *whump*, the glass panes fogging briefly with warmth before clearing. Twelve lamps on her route, thirteen if she counted the one outside the Ashgrove stall, which wasn't officially hers but which she lit anyway because Emrys worked late and the alley behind the market quarter was the kind of dark that swallowed people.

She was crouched at the seventh lamp when the bell began to ring.

The sound rolled over the rooftops like a wave of cold iron, deep enough that she felt it in her teeth, in the soles of her boots, in the lamp post under her palm. Once. Twice. Three times, and then a fourth, and it did not stop. It went on and on, and doors opened along the Row, and faces appeared in doorways pale as the paper notices nailed beside them, and everyone came out into the streets because no one dared not to.

Pip left her taper burning in the lamp's cradle and followed the crowd toward Candler's Square.

* * *

Candler's Square had been built for market days, for festivals, for the autumn lantern-floating that Pip remembered from when she was small and her mother still had the strength to lift her onto her shoulders. Tonight it held four thousand people packed shoulder to shoulder, breath rising in a single white cloud, and at the far end, on the steps of the Wardenhouse, torches burned in iron brackets and a platform had been raised overnight.

High Warden Corvess stood upon it.

Pip had seen Corvess maybe five times in her life, always at a distance, always in procession—tall and straight-backed in grey-and-silver, her face the kind of handsome that made you think of locked doors. Up close she was worse. Up close you could see that she never blinked quite when you expected, and that her eyes moved across the crowd the way a moneylender's eyes moved across a ledger: counting, assessing, finding what was owed.

Beside her stood a line of inquisitors in their bone-white cowls, and behind them, banners. Pip had grown up under those banners—the crossed keys and the closed eye—and had never once thought about what they meant until this moment, when they hung above a city with no stars and looked, suddenly, less like heraldry and more like a threat.

Corvess did not shout. She didn't need to. A young man beside the platform worked some kind of speaking-trumpet arrangement, brass pipes and leather, and her voice arrived everywhere at once, level and clear, the voice of someone reading a list of things that had already been decided.

"People of Greyhollow."

The crowd stilled. Somewhere near the front, a baby started to cry and was quickly hushed.

"Eight nights ago, the sky above this city went dark. Not clouded—darkened. Every star extinguished at once, in defiance of nature, in defiance of God, in defiance of the order that has kept this city safe for two hundred years." She paused, letting the words settle like ash. "I know what you have been told. That it is an omen. That it is the end of days. I am here to tell you it is neither. It is an *attack.*"

A murmur moved through the square like wind through wheat.

"The Veil—that ancient boundary between our world and the unquiet realms beyond—has been wounded. Torn open. And what comes through such a wound is cold, and hunger, and forgetting. You have seen the frost that does not melt. You have heard of villages gone silent. This is the work of the Sundering, and the Sundering is the work of casters."

The word dropped into the crowd like a stone into a well. Pip felt it fall past her, past everyone, down into some deep place where the whole city kept its oldest fear.

"For generations we have tolerated the small magics," Corvess continued. "The hedge-charms. The luck-knots. The love-sachets and fever-tinctures sold in your night markets by smiling merchants who assure you it is all harmless. Harmless." Her mouth did something that was almost a smile. "There is no harmless magic. Every working draws from the same well. Every charm, however small, drinks from the Veil. And the Veil is dying. Your comforts have a cost, and the cost is being paid now, in the only coin the dark accepts."

Pip's hands had gone cold around the strap of her taper-pole. She thought of Emrys's stall, the little glass bottles catching lamplight, the way Emrys's fingers moved when they tied a knot-charm—precise, careful, reverent, almost. *Drinks from the Veil.* Was that true? Could it be true? Emrys healed a frostbitten child just last week, right there in the market, quick and quiet, and the child had laughed—

And the sky had already been dark for six days by then.

No. Pip shoved the thought away. Whatever was eating the stars, it wasn't a woman selling fever-cures for two coppers. It couldn't be.

"Effective tonight," Corvess said, "a full curfew shall be observed from the evening bell to the dawn bell. No person shall be abroad without a Warden's writ. Effective within the week, every resident of Greyhollow shall present themselves for a purity attestation—an examination conducted by my inquisitors to determine exposure to residual casting. Attendance is mandatory. Refusal will be interpreted as confession."

The murmur turned ugly at the edges. Someone near Pip hissed, "*Attestation*, what in the hells—" and someone else shushed them frantically, glancing at the white-cowled figures on the platform.

"I know what you are thinking," Corvess said, and her gaze swept the crowd, and for one horrible instant Pip was certain it passed directly over her, certain the High Warden saw the taper-pole, the lamp-oil stains on her cuffs, the hedge-witch's knot-charm hidden under her collar where her mother's fever-tincture recipe was written out in a code only Pip could read. "You are thinking: *I have done nothing wrong. I have bought a charm or two, perhaps. My grandmother whispered over the kettle. Surely I am not what she means.*" 

She let the silence stretch.

"But the Sundering does not distinguish between the knife and the hand that holds it. The Veil does not distinguish between the storm-caller and the woman who buys a luck-charm to keep her roof from leaking. All of it drains. All of it feeds the wound. And so I am asking—not asking, *instructing*—every citizen of Greyhollow to look at their neighbors. Look at the shops that stay open too late. Look at the strangers who ask strange questions. If you know a caster, you will report them. If you have purchased a charm, you will surrender it. Those who come forward freely will be treated with mercy."

Another pause. Longer. The torches crackled.

"Those who do not," Corvess said, "will be treated as the cause."

Then she stepped back, and the banners shifted in the windless air as if stirred by something underneath the world, and the crowd began, slowly, horribly, to disperse—not angry, not shouting, but quiet, heads down, scattering to their homes like water finding cracks. That was the worst part, Pip thought. Not the proclamation itself. The quietness afterward. Four thousand people swallowing their fear at once made a sound like nothing at all.

She stayed rooted at the edge of the square until the crowd thinned, until the inquisitors descended the steps and began moving through the remaining stragglers with their ledgers and their questions. Then she turned and ran—not toward home, toward the market quarter, taper-pole bouncing against her back, lungs burning with cold air.

Because there was one thing she knew that Corvess didn't, one thing she'd promised herself she'd never use, and the promise was breaking apart in her chest with every stride.

* * *

The night market was shuttered. Shutters banged in the wind, awnings lashed down, the whole lane stripped bare as a picked bone. Only one window showed light: the narrow glow of Emrys Ashgrove's back room, seeping gold through the gap in the curtain.

Pip hammered on the door with the side of her fist. Once. Twice. Three times.

The light moved. A shadow crossed the curtain, paused—she could practically hear Emrys deciding whether to answer—and then the bolts slid back and the door cracked open a hand's width, warm lamplight spilling over the step.

"Pip." Emrys's voice was low. Their eyes went past her shoulder, scanning the empty street, the dark windows. "Do you have any idea what time—"

"You have to leave." The words came out in a rush, tumbling over each other. "Or hide, or—I don't know—but you can't be here. She announced it tonight, the High Warden, curfews and attestations and they're going door to door with lists, and she said—" Pip's throat tightened. "She said anyone who knows a caster has to report them. Everyone's scared, Emrys, scared people do stupid things, and everyone knows your stall, everyone—"

"Pip. Breathe."

"I *am* breathing—"

"Slowly." Emrys opened the door wider and pulled her inside, bolting it behind her. The back room smelled of dried lavender and lamp oil and the faint ozone tang that Pip had learned meant magic had been worked here recently, carefully, secretly. Emrys steered her to a stool and stood over her, arms folded, and in the lamplight Pip could see how tired they looked—more tired than usual, worn thin as paper, like someone who hadn't slept properly in eight days either.

"Now," Emrys said. "Start from the beginning. What exactly did she announce?"

So Pip told her. The bell, the square, the crowd, all of it—curfew, attestations, *report your neighbors*—and she watched Emrys's face as she spoke, watched the stillness settle over them like frost forming on glass. When she got to the part about mercy for those who came forward, Emrys closed their eyes briefly, and Pip understood, with a small cold shock, that Emrys had known this was coming. Had maybe been waiting for it for twenty years.

"There'll be informers by tomorrow," Emrys said quietly. "There always are. Fear makes people generous with other people's names." They moved to the window and parted the curtain a finger's width, looking out at the dead-dark sky. "The attestations are cleverer than they sound. They're not hunting casters, not really. They're hunting *connections*. Who bought what. Who visited whom. A web, drawn tight."

"Then burn your ledger," Pip said. "The sales book—you keep one, I've seen it—"

"And leave my customers' names in a hundred memories instead?" Emrys shook their head. "It doesn't work that way, little lamp. Fear remembers better than ink."

Pip gripped the edge of the stool. Her heart was going like a hammer on nails, *bang bang bang*, coffin-lids. "Then what? You can't stay. They'll come here first, everyone knows the charm-seller, and when they find out what you actually are—" She stopped herself, but too late; the word was already in the air between them. *Storm-caller.* She'd guessed it months ago, from the way storms seemed to lean toward Emrys, from the amulet they wore under their shirt that hummed on bad nights. She'd never said it aloud. Saying it aloud made it real, and real things could be reported.

Emrys turned from the window. For a long moment they just looked at her, and Pip had the strange sensation of being measured—not for guilt, not for usefulness, but for something else. Trustworthiness, maybe. Or the cost of trusting.

"You shouldn't have run here," Emrys said at last. "If anyone saw you—if anyone asks why the lamplighter's daughter came pounding on a caster's door after curfew—"

"Nobody saw. I took the tanner's alley."

"Pip."

"*Nobody saw.*" She stood up, and to her own surprise her voice didn't shake. "Look, I don't care what you are. I don't care about the Veil or the Sundering or whatever the Wardens think is happening. My mum is dying, Emrys. Fever in her chest since the frost came, and the physickers want three silvers a visit and the temple wants a tithe and neither of them can actually *do* anything, and you—" Her voice cracked and she hated it, hated the weakness in it, pushed through anyway. "You fixed Hessa's hands in ten minutes last month. Ten minutes. So I'm not here to warn you out of the goodness of my heart. I'm here because I'm selfish, and scared, and I need you to teach me. Before the attestations come. Before the winter takes her. Teach me what you know."

The silence that followed was the longest of Pip's life. The lamp flame bent in a draft. Outside, far off, a Warden patrol rattled past, boot-heels and chain-mail, and both of them listened until it faded.

"No," Emrys said softly.

"You haven't even—"

"It doesn't matter what I feel about it. The answer is no." Emrys's voice was gentle, which somehow made it worse. "Not because I don't want to help your mother. Because teaching is how this starts. One student, then two, then a cellar full of children whispering over candle flames, and then the inquisitors find the cellar, and everyone in it hangs. I have seen it before, Pip. I won't build the pyre myself."

"Then teach me and send me away! Teach me and forget me!"

"It doesn't work that way either." Emrys crouched to her level, and their eyes—grey-green, storm-colored, tired beyond counting—held hers. "Magic isn't a recipe, little lamp. It's a relationship. And relationships don't end just because you want them to. If I open that door in you even a crack, the cold will find the crack. Everything finds the crack. That's what tonight was about, if you listened."

"I *did* listen." Pip's fists were shaking at her sides. "Corvess listened too, and you know what she heard? An excuse. Twenty years of excuses, probably, all written down nice and legal, and now she gets to blame the sky on people like you while the real reason rots wherever it rots. And everyone will nod along, because it's easier to hang a charm-seller than to admit nobody's in charge of anything." She swiped at her eyes with her sleeve, furious at the tears. "You can say no. Fine. Say no. But don't tell me it's for my protection, because my mum is upstairs coughing her lungs into a rag, and no one protecting *her*."

She grabbed her taper-pole and stormed to the door, wrenched the bolts back, and had one foot on the step before Emrys spoke.

"Pip."

She stopped. Didn't turn.

"Your mother's fever," Emrys said quietly. "Is it the wet kind? Rattling, worse at night, blue at the fingertips?"

Pip turned. "...Yes."

"Come back tomorrow. After the dawn bell, before the market wakes. Use the tanner's alley again." Emrys's face was unreadable, but their hands—those precise, careful, reverent hands—had gone to the shelf where the fever-tinctures lived. "I can't teach you. But I never said I wouldn't treat the sick. That rule I broke a long time ago."

Hope hit Pip so hard she nearly staggered. "Thank you—"

"Don't thank me. Thank me by being careful." Emrys met her eyes one last time. "And Pip—whatever you heard tonight, whatever Corvess promises—remember that fear is a tool. Somebody forged it into a weapon on purpose. Our job is to figure out who's holding the handle."

Pip nodded, not fully understanding, and slipped out into the frozen dark.

The curfew bell would ring soon. She had eleven lamps still unlit on her route, and she was going to light every single one of them, curfew or no, proclamation or no. Let the Wardens write her name in their ledgers. Let them come with their questions and their white cowls.

Somebody in this city had to keep the lights burning. And as she ran through the empty streets, taper flaring against the wind, Pip made herself a promise in the space where fear used to live:

When they came for Emrys Ashgrove—and they would come—they'd have to go through the lamplighter's daughter first.
