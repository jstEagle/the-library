## 10. Crossing the Ashline

They left before dawn, when the checkpoints changed hands and the Warden patrols grew drowsy with cold. Sable had mapped the gap between the second and third watchtowers down to the width of a cart-track, and the Bramble twins had sworn—crossing their hearts and spitting for luck—that no one had patrolled the old shepherd's road in years.

"Years," Emrys repeated, as they climbed over a fallen wall into the gray hour before morning. "That word again."

"Everything worth knowing is years out of date," Rook Bramble said cheerfully, hauling his pack higher on one shoulder. His sister Thorn walked ahead of them all, silent as her namesake, reading the ground the way Sable read maps. "That's why it's cheap."

Pip stumbled along beside Emrys, her lamplighter's pole repurposed as a walking staff, her breath coming in little silver flags. She hadn't slept well. None of them had. The night before, camped in the twins' smuggler's cellar among crates of untaxed brandy and smuggled salt, Emrys had woken to find frost creeping across the floorboards in patterns that were almost—but not quite—the shape of a name. They had not told anyone. Some griefs you carry until they're ready to be spoken, and some you carry until they bury you.

The city fell behind them by degrees: first the walls, then the smoke of the tanneries, then the last stubborn lamp of a farmstead where someone was already up, milking a goat in the dark because the dark no longer respected clocks. And then there was only the road, and the hills, and the sky overhead like a lid on a pot.

Emrys kept glancing up. Old habit. Twenty years of looking at stars without letting themselves be seen looking. Now there was nothing to look at—a blackness so complete it felt less like absence than like something pressed flat against the world, heavy, attentive. The first few nights after the wards fell, folk in Greyhollow had stood in the streets pointing up, waiting for the stars to come back the way they came back after clouds passed. By the fourth night they had stopped pointing. That was the thing about catastrophe, Emrys thought. It didn't have to kill you. It only had to become ordinary.

"Stop fidgeting," Sable said, falling into step beside them. She carried her map case slung across her chest like a soldier carries ammunition, and she'd been touching it every quarter-hour since they set out, checking, reassuring herself it was still there. "You walk like a guilty man."

"I am a guilty woman."

"You know what I mean." Sable lowered her voice. Her eyes went to Pip, twenty paces ahead, then to the twins, then back. "Once we cross the Ashline, there's no pretending anymore. No charm stall. No cover story. Whatever you are out there, you'll be it in the open."

Emrys watched the horizon. The borderlands lay ahead, a low smudge of broken country where the realm's edge met the realm's edge, stitched once upon a time by wards older than any kingdom. "Maybe that's the point," they said quietly. "Maybe the disguise stopped working the night the stars did."

Sable snorted. "Poetry. Wonderful. I'll be sure to carve it on your headstone if the Wardens catch us." But she didn't move away, and her shoulder stayed near Emrys's shoulder for another mile, and that, from Sable Vane, was practically an embrace.

* * *

They smelled the Ashline before they saw it.

It was a smell like a hearth swept out three days ago, like burnt bread left in its tin, like lightning that had struck and struck and never stopped striking. Pip noticed it first—of course she did; she was young and her senses hadn't yet learned to look away from ugly things—and she stopped dead in the road with her nostrils flaring.

"What is that?"

"Burned ground," Thorn said. Then, after a moment, more honestly: "Burned *something*. Not wood. Not flesh either. I've smelled both." She spat. "This is neither."

They crested the last ridge as the sun tried and failed to rise properly behind them, and there it was.

The borderlands stretched out below like a page torn out of the world. Everything beyond a certain line—the line, Emrys realized, where the great ward-stones had once stood in their ancient procession, each one a day's ride from the next—was drained. Not scorched. Drained. The grass was grass-shaped but colorless, a pale ghost-green fading to ash-gray. The trees stood bare though it was barely autumn, their branches like scratches on slate. A river ran through the middle distance, and it ran *wrong*—not frozen, not dry, but moving without glitter, without shine, water the color of pewter, making no sound they could hear from the ridge. Even the wind seemed muffled here, arriving late, as if it had to cross something first.

And above it all, the sky came down lower. Emrys felt it rather than saw it: the weight of the starless dark pressing closer to the earth over the wound, like a hand laid flat over a bleeding cut.

"Oh," Pip said. Just that. One small syllable, and then she put her hand over her mouth.

Rook whistled low and tuneless. "Well," he said. "That's new."

"It's not new," Emrys heard themselves say. Their voice came out strange, distant, the voice of someone standing slightly outside their own body. "I've seen this before. In miniature. When a ward dies—when one dies properly, all at once—it takes the color with it. The magic in living things isn't separate from them. It *is* them. Drain the magic and you don't get a dead meadow. You get a meadow-shaped hole."

Sable had her map case open and was sketching fast, her pencil scratching, her eyes bright with the terrible joy she always found in accurate horror. "The whole border," she murmured. "Every ward-stone line. Look at the extent—" She broke off. "Emrys. How far does it go?"

Emrys looked. North, south, as far as the eye could reach, the colorless country ran along the old covenant line like a scar along a wrist. Somewhere out there, days away, the Covenant Stones stood shattered at the center of it all, and everything between here and there was this: a land with the life wrung out of it, holding its shape the way a glove holds the shape of a hand.

"We're going to walk through that," Pip said. Not a question. Her chin had come up, the way it did when she was frightened and refusing to let the fright vote.

"We're going to walk through that," Emrys agreed.

Thorn unslung her pack and began distributing oilskins without being asked. "Then we walk fast, we don't touch anything, and nobody eats or drinks what we find in there. Colorless berries are lying berries." She looked at Emrys, and her flat dark eyes held something that in a softer face might have been worry. "You've crossed dead wards before. You'll feel it. Best you tell them now, so they don't panic."

"Feel what?" Pip asked.

Emrys took a breath. "Like the world has stopped noticing you," they said. "Wards are how the land pays attention. Inside a dead one, you're—" They searched for the word and hated every candidate. "*Unwitnessed.* Your footsteps don't echo right. Your fire burns small. Your own name feels far away. It passes, once you're through. Mostly."

"*Mostly,*" Rook repeated, delighted. "Oh, I do love a professional guarantee."

But he shouldered his pack, and they all did, and together they went down off the ridge and into the gray.

* * *

The first thing Emrys lost was birdsong.

There had been larks on the ridge behind them, faint but persistent, stitching the morning together. Ten steps past the line of the old ward-stones—marked now only by stumps, by circles of paler earth where the stones had been pried up or toppled—the larks were gone. Not flown away. Gone, as if the sound had been subtracted from the air. Emrys stopped and listened hard and heard instead a silence with texture to it, a silence like wool packed in the ears, and beneath it, very faint, a hum. Low. Patient. The sound of a wound the world was trying and failing to close.

Their storm-glass amulet stirred against their chest. Emrys pressed a hand over it instinctively, under the coat, and felt it answering the hum—faint flickers of charge, like a cat's hackles rising. The glass wanted something. Or feared something. With storm-glass it had always been hard to tell the difference.

"Keep moving," Thorn said softly. "Don't linger at the line. Things notice lingerers."

"What things?" Pip whispered.

"If I knew their names, girl, I'd sell them to a ballad-maker and retire."

They walked. The colorless country closed around them, and it was worse inside than it had looked from the ridge, because inside it, the wrongness got personal. Emrys's shadow was fainter here—pale gray on pale gray, a suggestion of a shadow. Pip's red scarf, knitted by her mother in better years, had gone the color of old dishwater, though when Emrys touched it, it was still soft, still warm from her neck. Color hadn't died; it had been *taken*, siphoned upward into that pressing dark like smoke into a chimney.

"The Veil," Emrys said aloud, mostly to keep their own courage fed. "People think it's a wall. It's not. It's a membrane. Both worlds breathe through it—ours and the other one, the deep one, the one the old stories call the Garden. Every green thing here is green because the Garden's light comes through. Every dream, every hunch, every moment of luck. It all leaks through the Veil, gentle as rain through a roof." They gestured at the drained fields. "This is what happens when the roof comes off. The light stops leaking. The rain stops falling. And both sides start to freeze."

"You talk like you learned it at your mother's knee," Sable said, watching them sidelong.

"I learned it at my mother's knee," Emrys said. "Among other places."

Sable let it go, which was unlike her, and Emrys understood why an hour later, when they crested a low rise and found the farmhouse.

* * *

It stood alone in a field of gray grass, stone-walled, thatch long collapsed, and it was the most horrifying thing Emrys had seen in twenty years of hiding from horrors—because it wasn't ruined. Nothing had knocked it down. Time itself seemed to have skipped it, the way the river ran without shining. The door hung open on a kitchen where a clay pot still sat on a cold hearth, and a wooden spoon rested against its rim, and a doll lay on the floor by a chair, and everything—the pot, the spoon, the doll, the chair, the table with its single unwashed bowl—was rendered in shades of ash and bone, as if someone had painted the room using only the colors of forgetting.

Nobody spoke. Thorn circled the house with her knife out and came back shaking her head. "No bodies. No blood. No sign anyone left in a hurry." She nodded at the spoon against the pot rim. "Or slowly."

"They didn't leave," Emrys said. Their voice sounded thin in the wool-silence. "Look at the dust. There isn't any. This place hasn't been abandoned. It's been *paused*."

"Paused," Rook said. "Right. And where do paused people go?"

Emrys didn't answer, because they had knelt by the doll, and the doll's stitched face had done something to them that they couldn't explain and couldn't shake. They thought of Hollow Bells—not yet, they didn't know that name yet, wouldn't for days—but they knew, in the marrow way, in the storm-caller's way, that somewhere ahead of them people were standing mid-task in villages just like this one, alive and empty, their memories drunk down by the dying Veil or by whatever was drinking the Veil. The frost on their cellar floor had spelled a childhood name. The world was trying to tell them something, and the message was written in everything it had stopped bothering to finish.

Pip crouched beside them. She picked up the doll, turned it over in her colorless hands, and set it gently back in the exact spot, facing the same way. "We'll come back for them," she said fiercely, to the room, to the doll, to Emrys. "After. Whatever we fix at the Stones—we'll come back and unpause everyone. Right?"

Emrys looked at her—at her chapped hands and her soot-stained cuffs and her absolute, unearned, magnificent certainty—and felt something crack in their chest that had nothing to do with storm-glass.

"Right," they said. It was a lie and a promise, and they gave it anyway, because some lies are load-bearing.

* * *

They made camp that night in the lee of a fallen ward-stump, and the fire burned exactly as small and apologetic as Emrys had promised. Rook complained about it. Thorn told him to eat his stew cold and be grateful. Sable spread her maps by firelight and marked their progress with a pin, and the pin's brass head was the only bright thing in the whole visible world, a tiny gold eye winking in the gray.

Emrys took the second watch, because they didn't trust themselves to sleep here. They sat with their back against the stump and their knees drawn up and the amulet in their cupped palms, and they watched the starless dark press down over the wounded land, and they listened to the hum.

Up close, over hours, the hum resolved. It wasn't random. It had structure—phrases, almost. Repetitions. A rhythm like breathing, if breathing could grieve. Emrys had spent twenty years refusing to listen to magic, plugging their ears with commerce and anonymity, and now here they sat in the dead heart of the borderlands with their eyes closed, listening the way they hadn't listened since they were a child on a mountainside, learning to hear weather before it was born.

And beneath the hum, threaded through it, thin as wire:

*Ashgrove.*

Not spoken. Not quite. More remembered than said—the way frost remembers a name, the way a scar remembers a blade. The Veil's wound, humming its grief into the colorless dark, and somewhere in the grief, their mother's name for them, their true name, the one they'd buried under two decades of *just a charm-seller, friend, nothing special*.

Emrys opened their eyes. The amulet was glowing. Faintly, fitfully, blue-white as summer lightning, pulsing in time with the hum—and around them, impossibly, the gray grass at the edge of the firelight had begun to frost over, delicate ferns of ice spreading outward from where they sat, and in the frost-ferns, over and over and over, in a child's careful handwriting:

*Emrys. Emrys. Emrys.*

"Pip," they said. Their voice cracked on it. "Pip, wake up. Wake up—all of you. It knows we're here."

Out in the dark beyond the firelight, something vast and patient shifted its attention, the way a sleeper turns toward a sound—and the hum changed key, and every hair on Emrys's arms rose, and far to the north, where the Covenant Stones waited, a single point of light flickered in the starless sky.

Not a star returning.

A lamp being lit. Someone—or something—was awake at the end of the road, and it had just seen them coming.
