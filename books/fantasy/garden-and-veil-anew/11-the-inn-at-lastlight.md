## 11. The Inn at Lastlight

The inn appeared the way salvation always did in Sable Vane's experience: too late, half-ruined, and smelling faintly of lies.

They had been walking since before dawn, following the salt road through country that had forgotten how to be a country. The Ashline stretched behind them like a scar across a page—Sable had mapped it in her head as they crossed, because mapping was what she did when she was afraid, and she had been afraid for six days now. Every mile she charted was a mile she could hold between herself and the dark. The land on this side of the burn was better, or at least it pretended to be: grey pines, grey fields, a grey river running with water the color of old pewter. But there were no birds. That was the thing she kept noticing and refusing to notice. No birds, no crows, not even the fat winter sparrows that should have mobbed any traveler foolish enough to carry bread.

And the color. She'd stopped trusting her own eyes around midday. The heather should have been rust and violet even dead; instead it lay in the ditches like ash that had decided to be plants again out of habit.

"We're stopping," Emrys said.

It wasn't a question, which was unlike them. Six days on the road and Sable had learned that the charm-seller asked permission for everything—asked to take first watch, asked if the fire was too smoky, asked Pip twice whether the girl's boots were warm enough as though boots could be warmed by inquiry. But now Emrys had stopped in the middle of the road, one hand pressed flat against their own sternum like someone holding a door shut from the inside, and their face had gone the particular shade of paper that Sable privately called *about-to-collapse*.

"You're swaying," Sable said.

"I'm standing."

"You're standing *while swaying*. There's an inn." She pointed with her chin, because her hands were full of map case and cold. Down where the road bent toward the river, a building crouched against the treeline—a long, low structure of black timber, its roof furred with frost, its windows lit with a light that was almost but not quite the color of lamplight. A sign swung above the door on a bracket gone green with age. Even at this distance Sable could read the lettering, because she read everything, compulsively, the way other people breathed: **THE LASTLIGHT**. Beneath it, smaller, in a different hand, as if added later by someone with a grudge against optimism: *Rooms. Stew. No Questions.*

"No questions," Pip repeated, reading over Sable's shoulder. The girl's voice cracked on the second word. She was fourteen and had been pretending not to be terrified for six days, which Sable respected enormously and would never admit to respecting. "That's either very good or very bad."

"In my experience," Sable said, "it's usually both."

Emrys was already walking.

* * *

The Lastlight's common room was warm in a way that felt less like hospitality than like argument—the stove winning, barely, against a draft that came in under the door with the smell of the river. Perhaps a dozen tables, three of them occupied: two drovers asleep in their bowls, a woman mending a net by touch alone while she stared at the wall, and an old man playing solitaire with a deck missing what looked like most of its face cards. Behind the bar stood a person built like something meant to hold up bridges—broad, weathered, bald except for a fringe of iron-grey—and polishing a mug with the slow, circular patience of someone who had been polishing that same mug, Sable suspected, for years.

The room went quiet when they entered. Not hostile-quiet. *Assessing*-quiet. Sable had grown up in border towns; she knew the difference. Hostile-quiet reached for things. Assessing-quiet counted your exits.

"Four bowls," Emrys said, setting coins on the bar. "And whatever you've got for a room. One room is fine."

The barkeep's eyes moved over them one at a time. Over Pip, small and sharp-eyed and trying to look taller. Over Sable, whose map case she shifted self-consciously behind her hip. Over the Bramble twins' cousin Rook, who had joined them at the crossing and mostly communicated in shrugs. And then over Emrys, and stopped.

Sable saw it happen. She doubted anyone else in the room did, because it happened in the space of a blink—but she had spent her whole life reading faces the way she read terrain, looking for the places where the lines didn't match the story, and the barkeep's face *stuttered*. Recognition, quick as a fish turning under ice. Then something else laid over it, smooth and deliberate: blankness, poured like wax.

"Coins are wrong," the barkeep said.

Emrys blinked. "I'm sorry?"

"Old mint." The barkeep turned one of the coins over with one enormous finger. "These haven't circulated in—" A pause, precise as a blade finding a joint. "Twenty years. Give or take."

The silence stretched. By the stove, the sleeping drovers snored on, blessedly oblivious. Sable's hand drifted toward the knife at her belt without consulting her, which was the trouble with hands.

"They're family money," Emrys said evenly. "I keep them for luck."

"Luck." The barkeep set the coin down with exaggerated gentleness, the way you set down something that might go off. Then those pale eyes came up and fixed on Emrys, and all the wax slid off, and what was underneath was not hostility. It was something worse. It was *grief*, worn smooth as river stone. "Storm-caller."

Pip made a small sound. Rook's chair creaked as his weight shifted forward. Sable stepped in before anything could detonate, sliding herself into the space between the bar and Emrys the way she'd once seen a sheepdog put itself between a flock and a cliff edge.

"All right," she said brightly, in the voice she used for customs officials and other natural disasters. "Let's all be very calm about whatever this is. My friend here sells charms. Buttons, mostly. Love charms, which don't work, which I've told him is false advertising, but he says the hope does the work and the charm just gives it somewhere to sit—"

"I know who they are," the barkeep said quietly. Not unkindly. That was the terrible part. "Ashgrove. From the western holds. I'd know that face anywhere. I spent four years of my life memorizing faces like that one." They set down the mug. Their hands, Sable noticed, were shaking—not much, just at the fingertips, like a struck bell after the sound has supposedly ended. "My name is Marrow. I was Third Lance, Warden Inquisitorial, stationed at Coldspring garrison. Eleven years. I walked away nine winters back, and I have regretted both halves of that sentence every day since."

Nobody moved. The woman with the net had stopped mending. The solitaire player had frozen mid-deal, one card hanging from his fingers.

"You're going to tell me," Sable said slowly, working it through, "that you're not going to report us."

"I'm going to tell you," Marrow said, "to sit down before you fall down, because your storm-caller has been bleeding magic since they walked through my door and I can smell it from here, and then I'm going to feed you, because you look like the last four people in the world, and I don't ask questions. It's on the sign."

"The sign also says rooms," Sable said, because sarcasm was the only tool she had left that still worked.

"And you'll get one. Upstairs, end of the hall, window faces the river." Marrow was already moving, hauling a pot off the hook, ladling something dark and steaming into bowls with the brisk economy of a person who had fed soldiers and knew exactly how. "Stew's barley and river-fish. Don't ask what kind of fish. The river doesn't answer questions either lately."

Emrys hadn't sat. They were staring at Marrow with an expression Sable had never seen on them before—six days of road, and she thought she'd catalogued all of the charm-seller's faces: the careful pleasantness, the tired kindness, the locked-door look when talk turned to the past. This was none of those. This was a person looking at a ghost that had come back wearing an apron.

"You knew," Emrys said. "When we walked in. You knew immediately."

"I told you. Faces." Marrow pushed the first bowl across the bar, toward Pip, because even deserters apparently had instincts about feeding children first. "I ran the registry desk at Coldspring for two years. Exile rolls, watch-lists, the sealed files. Your name was in the sealed files. Ashgrove, E. Storm-calling, third degree. Sentence: exile in perpetuity, ward-marked, shoot-on-sight order suspended by special instruction." Those pale eyes flicked up. "Special instruction. Signed by Corvess herself, before she was High Warden. You want to know why? Because they wanted you findable, not found. There's a difference. I filed enough paperwork to know the difference when I saw it."

Sable felt the floor tilt slightly under her certainties. She'd known Emrys was hiding—had clocked it within ten minutes of meeting them, had said so with some satisfaction at the time—but there was a wide gulf between *hiding from neighbors* and *a High Warden's personal signature on a twenty-year-old manhunt*. She looked at Emrys sideways. Their face had gone very still, the way water goes still right before it freezes.

"Eat," Marrow said again, gentler now. "All of you. Then sleep. Nobody rides for Coldspring from here; nearest garrison's two days north and nobody from it has bought a drink in this room since summer. You're safe tonight. That's all I'm selling. Safe tonight, stew included, and the rest of it—" they shrugged, mountains moving under wool, "—the rest of it isn't mine to sell."

* * *

Later, when the bowls were empty and the drovers had staggered up to their beds and the solitaire player had conceded defeat to himself and gone the same way, Sable stayed at the corner table with the last of the barley tea and watched Marrow bank the stove.

She should have slept. Her body had been sending increasingly strident memos on the subject for hours. But she'd learned long ago that the hours after a near-disaster belonged to her mind, not her body, and her mind wanted to work. So she worked. She took out her maps—not the good ones, the practice sheets, the ones she let people see—and redrew the day's route from memory, annotating distances, water sources, the place where the color had failed along the eastern hedgerow. And she thought.

"You're the cartographer," Marrow said, without turning around.

"Sable Vane. Mapmaker, surveyor, occasional forger of documents I'm not proud of." She said it easily, watching for the flinch. It didn't come. "You're the deserter. That's a rarer trade."

"Commoner than you'd think, lately." Marrow came and sat across from her uninvited, which Sable decided to allow, mostly because the alternative was being rude to someone with forearms like that. Up close, the ex-Warden's face was a topography of old decisions: a scar through one eyebrow, deep lines around a mouth that had clearly done more grimacing than smiling, eyes that had the flat patience of someone who had waited in a lot of cold places for bad things to finish happening. "You're taking them to the Stones."

It wasn't a question. Sable kept her own face pleasant and empty, which took effort. "We're travelers."

"Mm." Marrow wrapped both hands around the tea Sable hadn't offered and drank anyway. "I was at Coldspring when the wards fell. We all felt it—you felt it, everyone felt it, like the world's spine cracking. Half the garrison knelt down and started praying. The other half started sharpening things. I did neither. I got drunk, and then I got sober, and then I got out, because I remembered something." They looked at her over the rim of the cup. "I remembered the sealed files. There's a file in that archive older than the Wardens themselves. Older than the *office*. It's called the Foundational Record, and it's the only document in the whole Warden library that's chained shut, and I once asked my commanding officer what was in it, and he looked at me like I'd asked him to open his own chest, and he said, *Marrow, some things are only true if nobody reads them.*"

"That's a hell of a sentence," Sable said.

"It's a hell of an organization." Marrow set the cup down. "I don't know what you're doing. I don't want to know—that's not modesty, that's survival, mine and yours. But I'll tell you two things for free, cartographer, because you've got the look of someone who writes things down, and written things survive people." They held up one thick finger. "One: the road past Thornmere is washed out, but the shepherd's track above it is passable if the ice holds, and it will hold maybe another week. Take the track. Two:" the second finger rose, and here Marrow's voice dropped until it was nearly part of the stove's murmur, "if you reach the Stones, and if whatever you're planning there involves the old covenant—don't trust anything the Wardens ever wrote about it. Including what I just told you. Especially what I just told you. We were built on a story, cartographer. All institutions are. Ours just happens to be the one holding up the sky."

Sable sat very still. Somewhere upstairs, a floorboard creaked—Emrys pacing, probably, or Pip having a nightmare, or Rook sleeping the way large men sleep, like furniture falling over slowly. The stove ticked. Outside, beyond the frost-furred glass, the starless dark pressed against the inn like a tide against a seawall.

"Why help us?" she asked finally. "Really. No questions means no questions, fine, but you recognized them on sight and you fed us anyway. People don't do that for free. I've met every kind of person there is, and none of them do that for free."

Marrow was quiet for a long moment. When they spoke, they looked at their own hands, as though the answer might be written there in old ink.

"Because in eleven years I signed forty-one exile orders," they said. "Forty-one names. I told myself each time that it was law, that it was order, that the alternative was the wild magic and the burning years. And then the stars went out, and the wards fell, and do you know what I realized, sitting in that garrison with my prayers and my sharpening?" They laughed, short and airless. "The burning years never ended. We just hired someone to write them down neatly." They stood, joints cracking like green wood, and collected the cups. "Bed, cartographer. Dawn comes early and the ice won't wait."

At the foot of the stairs, Marrow paused. Didn't turn around.

"For what it's worth—I never opened your friend's file past the first page. Some things I didn't want to know." A beat. "But the second page has a list of next of kin on it. In case you're the type who reads things."

Then they were gone into the kitchen dark, and Sable sat alone with the banked stove and the words *next of kin* ringing in her skull like a struck bell.

* * *

She climbed the stairs quietly. The end-room door stood ajar, lamplight leaking through in a thin gold seam, and through it she could hear voices—low, blurred, the shape of conversation without its bones. Pip's voice, young and insistent. Emrys's, answering, softer than she'd ever heard it.

Sable stood in the hall with her hand on the doorframe and did not go in.

Through the gap she could see them: Pip cross-legged on the bed, hair loose, gesturing with the intensity of someone reenacting a battle; Emrys sitting against the wall with their boots off and their storm-glass amulet cradled in one palm, watching the girl with an expression that Sable now, finally, had a name for. It wasn't warmth exactly. It was hunger. The specific hunger of someone who had spent twenty years rationing themselves down to nothing, suddenly seated in front of a feast they didn't believe they were allowed to touch.

Marrow's voice in her head: *next of kin.* A list. Twenty years old. Whoever was on it—if anyone was—was either dead or had testified, and given the sealed-file architecture of the whole miserable business, Sable's money was on testimony. On betrayal, dressed up as duty. She thought of Emrys's locked-door silences on the road, the way they changed the subject whenever talk drifted westward, toward the holds, toward home. She thought: *there's a brother or a sister or a lover out there who sold them to the Wardens, and our charming button-seller has been carrying that wound so long they've forgotten it isn't part of their body.*

She would find out. Of course she would. She was a cartographer; unmapped territory offended her on a spiritual level. But not tonight. Tonight the ice was holding, the stew had been good, and the sky outside was a black lid pressing down on a world that had forgotten how to shine—and tomorrow they would walk back out under it, toward Thornmere and the shepherd's track and the Covenant Stones, carrying secrets that were getting heavier by the mile.

Downstairs, very faintly, she heard Marrow begin to sing. An old song, wordless, more hum than melody—the kind of song you sang to a stove, or to a grave, or to a memory you couldn't put down. Sable leaned her forehead briefly against the cool wood of the doorframe.

Then she went in, told Pip in no uncertain terms that elbows were not limbs designed for sharing beds, and slept—dreamlessly, mercifully, for almost four hours.

She woke before dawn to the sound of Emrys's voice downstairs, low and urgent, and Marrow's answering, and one word rising through the floorboards clear as a crack in ice:

"*Corvess.*"
