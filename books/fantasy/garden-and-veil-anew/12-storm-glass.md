## 12. Storm-Glass

The crack came in the night, a sound like a knuckle popping inside Emrys's own chest.

They woke in the loft above Sable's map shop with their hand already pressed to the amulet at their throat, the way they had pressed it every morning for twenty years without thinking—except that now they were thinking, and the glass beneath their palm was wrong. Warm. It had never been warm before. Storm-glass should hold the chill of the sky it remembered, and this piece held the chill of nothing at all.

Emrys sat up in the dark and worked the amulet over their head, holding it to the gray sliver of window light. There, running from crown to base like a river drawn by a careless child: a hairline fracture, so fine they could have imagined it. They did not imagine the smell, though—the faint ozone bite rising off it, like the air after lightning, like the air after *her*, once.

"Ah," Emrys said softly, to no one, because saying anything louder would have made it real. "Ah, no."

They had bought the glass from a peddler in Vell's Landing two decades ago, back when exile was still a wound instead of a habit. The peddler had claimed it was cut from a storm that fell on the Salt Road a hundred years past, bottled mid-strike, and Emrys had known even then that half of what peddlers said was weather-worn lies—but the glass had sung true against their skin, and a storm-caller knows her own instrument when she holds one. It had been their anchor ever since. A focus. A reservoir. When the calling rose in them like floodwater, the glass took the overflow and kept it, kept them small enough to pass as a charm-seller with clever hands and no craft at all.

Now it was cracked, and everything it had been holding was leaking out into Emrys's blood like ink into water.

They felt it immediately, now that they knew to feel for it: a restlessness under the skin of their forearms, a pressure behind the eyes, the old familiar itch of weather wanting out. Twenty years of discipline, and one hairline fracture, and their body had gone back to being a struck bell.

Below them, through the floorboards, Pip's voice rose in argument with Sable about the proper orientation of contour lines, bright and stubborn as a wren. Emrys closed their fist around the amulet and thought: *I cannot let her see me like this.*

Then, because lying to themselves had never been among their talents: *I cannot let anyone see me like this.*

* * *

They went down anyway. Of course they went down. That was the trouble with found family—it made hiding geometrically impossible.

The map shop smelled of ink and tallow and, this morning, of the rosemary tea Sable brewed whenever she was worried and pretended she wasn't. She stood over her great table with a compass in one hand and a look on her face that meant she'd been awake all night, which meant she'd found something in the ward-failure patterns she didn't like, which meant Emrys didn't want to know yet. Pip perched on a stool, legs swinging, a stub of pencil behind each ear.

"You look terrible," Sable said, not looking up.

"I slept wonderfully."

"Liar. Your face does a thing." She finally glanced up, and her sharp cartographer's eyes went straight to Emrys's throat, where the amulet hung outside their shirt as it always did—and stopped there. Stopped, and narrowed. "What's wrong with your necklace?"

"Nothing."

"It's glowing."

Emrys looked down. A faint blue shimmer pulsed along the crack, slow as breathing, brightening with each beat of their heart. So the leak ran both ways, then. Lovely.

"It's old," Emrys said. "Old things glow when they're offended."

"Mm," said Sable, in the tone of a woman filing a lie away for later dissection.

Pip slid off the stool and crossed the room before Emrys could step back, and there was no stepping back from Pip; approaching her was like approaching weather. She reached up and touched the glass with one finger, and her whole face changed—the wonder stripped of childishness, replaced by something older and more careful. Hedge-witch's sight, Emrys realized. The girl could *feel* it.

"It's full," Pip whispered. "It's full and it's breaking. Like an egg."

"Pip—"

"Like an egg that's been sitting too long near a fire." She looked up, and her eyes were enormous. "You've been holding it in. All this time. All these years. You've been holding *yourself* in, and now the thing you're holding yourself in with is cracking, and—" She stopped, visibly reorganizing her thoughts, which she did with her whole body, shoulders squaring like a little general. "You have to let some out. On purpose. Controlled. Or it'll come out uncontrolled, and I've seen what uncontrolled looks like, haven't I? We all have. The whole sky's uncontrolled right now."

"She's not wrong," Sable said quietly. She set down her compass. "I don't know what you are, Ashgrove. I've stopped pretending I don't know you're something. But if you're a kettle, and the kettle's cracking, we find somewhere to open the spout."

Emrys stood in the rosemary-smelling light and understood, with the particular clarity that arrives only when a decision has already been made somewhere below the level of consent, that they were going to say yes.

Not for themselves. For the girl. Because Pip had asked them to teach her once and they had refused, and refused again, and the refusal had cost more each time, and here was the universe presenting the bill with interest: *teach her, or break.*

"There's a place," Emrys heard themselves say. "Up past the tannery ruins. Old quarry, stone all around, nothing to burn. Tomorrow, before dawn. Bring nothing you love."

"Why nothing I love?"

"Because first lessons go badly."

* * *

The quarry at dawn was a bowl of blue shadow and pale stone, ringed with frost ferns that the dying Veil had painted overnight in patterns too regular to be natural—spirals, lattice-works, letters in a script no living person read. Emrys tried not to look at them. Looking at them made the ache behind their sternum worse.

They set the rules out loud, because rules spoken aloud were harder to abandon than rules kept in the head.

"Small callings only. Static, breath-of-wind, a candle's worth of heat. Nothing that would make a Warden look twice if they saw it from the road. And we stop the moment either of us feels the pull toward *more*. More is how people die. Do you understand me?"

"Yes," Pip said, bouncing on her heels in the frost.

"*Do you understand me.*"

"Yes," Pip said again, slower, and this time she meant it, and Emrys thought: *oh no, she actually might be teachable,* and something in their chest that had been locked for twenty years turned over in its sleep.

They began with static. Emrys showed her how to find it—not in the sky but in herself, the tiny friction of being alive, the spark between intention and act. *Rub your awareness against itself,* they signed and whispered both, because sound carried strangely in the quarry. *Feel the itch. Now don't scratch it. Hold it. Hold it until it hums.*

Pip held it. Of course she did; she was a lamplighter's daughter raised on patience with flame, and within a quarter hour her fingertips were throwing sparks like a cat's fur in winter, and she was laughing, high and disbelieving, sparks dancing off her teeth.

"I'm doing it! I'm—look, I'm *doing* it—"

"I'm looking," Emrys said, and found they were smiling, and the smile hurt, the way unused muscles hurt.

It was when they demonstrated—when they drew a thread of their own static off the air to show her the shaping, the gentle cupping of energy into form—that the amulet flared.

The crack blazed white-blue. Emrys felt the glass drink deep, deeper than it had drunk in years, gulping down the overflow of a real working for the first time since Greyhollow—and then it gave something *back*, and that was the part they hadn't braced for. A returning tide. The glass had spent twenty years storing not just power but *self*, all the small pieces of Emrys that the discipline of hiding had pressed into it like flowers in a book, and now the crack let those pieces seep back out into the open air of the world, and the world kept them.

Emrys swayed. Caught themself on the quarry wall. The stone was very cold and very real and for one vertiginous moment they could not remember why they had come to a quarry at dawn, and then it returned, all of it, like water finding its level—but something else did not return. Something small. Something they couldn't name, which was precisely the horror of it: a hole shaped like a memory, smooth-edged, painless, *gone*.

"Emrys?" Pip's sparks died. "You went strange. Your face went strange."

"I'm fine."

"You're doing the thing where you lie."

"The amulet takes a toll," Emrys said carefully. Each word placed like a foot on ice. "It always has. Lately the toll has gotten... higher."

"What kind of toll?"

And here was the crossroads, plain as a marked trail. Lie, and keep the mask on, and watch it crack anyway. Or tell the truth to a fourteen-year-old in a frozen quarry, and lose another piece of the invisibility that had kept them alive for twenty years.

"Toll," Emrys said, "of remembering."

* * *

They practiced three mornings, then four. Small callings only, exactly as promised: static, warmth, a breeze coaxed across still water in the flooded lower pit. Pip learned the way dry ground learns rain—greedily, deeply, everything soaking in. And each morning the amulet drank, and each morning it gave back less than it took, and Emrys learned to inventory themselves afterward the way a merchant inventories stock, running fingers down the shelves of the mind.

The name of their first teacher: present. The color of the door of the house they grew up in: present, though it flickered, though it had begun arriving with a slight delay, like a word on the tip of the tongue. The sound of Tam's laugh—

Present. Still present. They checked it every morning, twice, the way other people checked for purses in crowds. *Tam's laugh, Tam's laugh, still there.* Whatever else the glass took, it seemed to leave the load-bearing walls alone. Perhaps even a cracked storm-glass knew what a person could not stand to lose.

But the small things. The small things were going like snow in spring.

The fourth morning, walking back through the gray streets with Pip chattering about conductivity, Emrys passed the baker's on Cordwainer Lane, and the smell of fresh bread rolled out warm and golden into the cold, and Emrys stopped dead in the street.

Nothing.

Not disgust, not indifference—*nothing*. The smell registered as information. Bread, baked, wheat, warm. They knew all the words. They knew that bread was good, that people loved it, that a baker's morning was a holy sort of labor. But the smell passed through them like wind through a broken window, touching nothing, catching on nothing, and somewhere behind that smooth-edged hole in their memory a truth sat waiting to be read:

*I have lost something. I do not know what. I will not know what. That is how it works.*

"Emrys?" Pip had doubled back, tugging their sleeve. "You stopped. Is it the bread? Do you not like bread?"

"I used to," Emrys said.

"That's not an answer."

"No," Emrys agreed. "It isn't."

They walked on. Three streets later, without deciding to, they heard themselves speak.

"When I was young—we're talking younger than you—my mother baked twice a week. Dark loaves, rye and a little honey, and she'd score the tops with a leaf pattern, oak leaves, because we lived under oaks, and she said the bread should know its neighbors." The words came steadily, which surprised them; grief usually stuttered. "The whole house filled up with the smell. It got into the walls. Years later, long after, I'd walk past a bakery in some border town and the smell would hit me and I'd be eight years old again, standing on a stool to watch her hands work the dough, flour to her elbows, humming. That smell was—" They searched for it. Reached for it the way you reach for a stair in the dark that isn't there anymore. "That smell was the shape of being safe. And this morning I walked past that baker's, and it was just a smell."

Pip was quiet for a long moment. Lamplighter's daughter, hedge-witch, child of a sick mother in a dying city—she understood economy of loss better than most adults.

"The amulet took it," she said. "When you were teaching me."

"Yes."

"So teaching me costs you." Her chin came up, that little-general set to her jaw. "Then we stop. I'll learn from books. Marrow might know things. I'm not—I won't have you paying with—"

"Pip."

"—with *yourself*, that's not fair, nobody asked you to—"

"*Pip.*" Emrys crouched, there in the street, in front of God and the fishmonger and everyone, and took the girl's chapped hands in theirs. "Listen to me. I spent twenty years paying a different price. The price of never using it at all. Of letting the gift rot in its sheath while the world went dark around me. I told myself it was prudence. It was fear wearing prudence's coat." Their voice dropped. "This morning I lost the smell of my mother's bread. It's gone. Truly gone—I can't even grieve it properly, because grieving needs the memory, and the memory is what's missing. And do you know what I felt, standing in that street?"

Pip shook her head, eyes wide.

"Relief," Emrys said. "Terrible relief. Because it means the price is real, and if the price is real, then the teaching is real, and if the teaching is real then none of it—all those years of hiding—none of it was the only way to live." They squeezed her hands. "A storm-glass is a jar for holding weather. Mine's cracked. Everything I keep in it wants out. Maybe that was always true, and the glass just made it easy to pretend otherwise."

"But your mother's bread—"

"Is paid forward," Emrys said. "Into you. Into whatever you become. I can live with that arithmetic." They stood, knees cracking, and managed something that was mostly a smile. "Besides. If the Veil dies and the long winter comes, we'll all be losing a great deal more than bread."

"That's supposed to make me feel better?"

"No. It's supposed to make you feel *urgent*. Better comes later, if we earn it."

* * *

That night Emrys sat alone in the loft with the amulet in their cupped palms, turning it in the lamplight. The crack had widened—a thread's breadth now, maybe two. Blue light breathed in it, slow, patient, like something sleeping with one eye open.

They thought about setting it down. Leaving it on the table, walking away, letting twenty years of stored self simply evaporate into the world. They thought about wrapping it in wool and burying it in the garden behind the shop, some symbolic nonsense about planting the past.

Instead they did what they had done every night for twenty years: they held it, and they listened to it, and this time—for the first time—they listened *back*.

And the glass, cracked open, seemed almost to whisper. Not words. Weather. A pressure change, a promise of storms far off over mountains they had crossed in another life. The sense—unmistakable, absurd, undeniable—that the amulet was not emptying but *changing state*, the way fog becomes rain becomes river. That what leaked from it wasn't being destroyed at all.

It was being *spent*.

Emrys sat with that until the candle guttered, and then they lay down and did not sleep, and near dawn they heard boots in the street below—many boots, moving in formation, and the iron knock of a Warden's knocker on doors up and down the lane, and Sable's voice low and sharp through the floorboards:

"Ashgrove. Get down here. Something's happened to the village on the north road. Nobody's come to market, and the milk cart just came back *empty*, and the driver—" a pause, and Sable's voice, for the first time since Emrys had known her, sounded genuinely afraid. "The driver says the whole village is standing in the streets. Just standing. Not moving. Not speaking. Every soul of them, frozen mid-task, like puppets with the strings cut."

Emrys was already pulling on their coat, the cracked amulet burning cold against their chest, and they thought of the frost-ferns in the quarry, spiraling in that unreadable script, and thought: *the winter isn't coming for our bodies first.*

It was coming for our minds.
