## 13. Hollow Bells

Pip had learned to read a village by its bells.

Greyhollow rang them at dawn and dusk, at market, at funerals, at the Warden's curfew—so many bells that you stopped hearing them, the way you stopped seeing your own hands. But out on the salt road, in the little towns strung like beads along the frozen river, bells meant something simpler. They meant someone was awake. Someone was tending the fire, winding the clock, ringing the hour because the hour deserved it. A village with bells was a village alive.

The village of Marrowgate—for that was what the crooked signpost said, though half the letters had been pried off for firewood years ago—had three bells. One in the chapel tower, one above the smithy, one small brass thing hung over the door of what had probably once been a bakery. All three were silent.

That wasn't strange, not by itself. It was past midnight, and the company had been pushing hard since the Ashline, sleeping in ditches and moving at dusk like criminals, which technically they were. Silent villages at midnight were normal.

What was strange was the light.

Every window in Marrowgate glowed.

Not flickering firelight—steady, unwavering, the flat amber of lamps left burning until their oil ran dry. Dozens of them. Every house on the lane, every shuttered window, lit from within like a row of lanterns set out for a festival no one had come to.

"They've all gone to bed and forgotten to blow them out," Sable said, but her voice did the thing it did when she didn't believe herself—the sharp edge going soft, like a knife wrapped in cloth.

"Nobody forgets every lamp," Emrys murmured. "In every house."

Pip stood between them in the shadow of the last fence post, her lamplighter's pole cold in her grip even through her mittens, and felt something she couldn't name settle over her like wet snow. She'd walked dark streets her whole life. She knew every kind of darkness there was—the friendly dark of a city asleep, the mean dark of an alley, the deep dark under the bridge where the river ice groaned. She had never seen darkness shaped like this: a whole town lit up inside and utterly, perfectly still.

No smoke from the chimneys. No dog barking. No creak of a windmill turning in the wind that came down off the hills smelling of iron and old snow.

"No smoke," she whispered. "Emrys. There's no smoke."

* * *

They went in anyway. Of course they went in. Pip was beginning to understand that this was simply what her life was now: walking toward the thing everyone sane would walk away from, because Emrys walked toward it, and because leaving people unlooked-after sat in Pip's chest like a swallowed stone.

The first house's door wasn't locked. It wasn't even latched. It swung open at Sable's touch with a long wooden sigh, and the warmth of the room spilled out over them—actual warmth, banked stove, kettle on the hob—and Pip's skin prickled up her arms.

Inside, a woman stood at the washbasin with her hands in a basin of water gone grey and cold.

She was maybe forty. Her sleeves were rolled to the elbow. Her hair was pinned up hastily, one pin slipping, a strand hanging against her cheek. And she was standing absolutely motionless, staring at nothing—not the wall, not the window, but *nothing*, the way you stare when your eyes are open and nobody is home behind them. Water dripped from her cupped hands back into the basin. Drip. Drip. Drip. The rhythm of it was the only sound in the whole house, and Pip thought she might go mad if she heard it much longer.

"Mistress?" Sable said, gentle, the way you'd speak to a spooked horse. "Can you hear me?"

Nothing. Not a blink. The drip went on.

Pip made herself step closer. Up close she could see the woman was breathing—slow, shallow, automatic, like a body remembering how without anyone operating it. Her lips were slightly parted. Frost had crept up the inside of the windowpane in ferns and feathers, beautiful and wrong, and where it touched the glass nearest the woman's face it had grown thickest, as if the cold came *out* of her.

"Pip." Emrys's hand landed on her shoulder, light as a moth. "Don't touch her skin."

"Why? What's wrong with her?"

"I don't know yet." Emrys's voice had a crack in it, thin as ice on a puddle. "But I've felt this before. Last night, in the frost on my window. It's the Veil. It's—" They stopped. Started again. "It's drinking them."

"Drinking what?" Pip demanded, because she needed it said plainly or she would start screaming.

"Memories." Emrys crossed to the woman and held their palm a careful inch from her forehead, not touching. Their storm-glass amulet, hanging at their throat, had gone cloudy as dirty ice. "Look at her eyes. There's nothing being looked *with*. She's not asleep, she's not sick, she's not dead. Everything that made her *her* has been pulled out through the top of her head like thread off a spool."

The drip of water into the basin. Pip counted eleven drops before she could make her voice work.

"Is she going to die?"

"I don't know." Emrys said it quietly, honestly, and somehow that was worse than any lie.

* * *

They found thirty-one people in Marrowgate.

Sable counted. That was the sort of thing Sable did—turned horror into arithmetic so it could be carried. Thirty-one souls, standing in thirty-one houses, frozen mid-task. A man halfway through feeding his chickens, grain scattered on the frozen ground around his boots, hens pecking obliviously at his ankles. An old fellow sitting in a chair with a pipe gone out between his teeth, a book open on his knee. Two children in a loft bed, lying side by side with their eyes open, and Pip had to go outside after that one and put her head between her knees and breathe until the world stopped tilting.

A baker stood at his oven with a peel in his hands, loaves blackening inside. A schoolteacher stood at a blackboard, chalk raised mid-word. The word was *remember*. She'd gotten as far as *remem*.

"That's not funny," Pip said out loud, to no one, in the empty schoolhouse, and her voice bounced off the walls and came back sounding smaller than she'd ever heard it.

It was Emrys who worked out the pattern—or part of it. They moved through the village like a physician through a plague town, quick and grim, holding their palm near each frozen face, and near each one the amulet clouded a little more.

"It's not random," they said finally, in the square, under the dumb chapel bell. "The ones who've been taken longest are deepest. The baker's been standing two days—I can tell from the bread. The children went under tonight. It's spreading outward from somewhere, like ripples. Like something landed here and is drinking in circles."

"From where?" Sable asked.

Emrys turned slowly, and Pip watched their face do something complicated—dread and recognition fighting for the same ground.

"The well," Pip said.

They all looked at it. The village well sat in the center of the square, stone-lipped, rope and bucket still attached, snow drifted against one side. It looked exactly like a hundred wells Pip had drawn water from. Except that the frost around its rim grew inward instead of outward, thick and blue-white, spiraling down into the dark like fur on the inside of some enormous throat. And when Pip leaned over the lip—against every screaming instinct, because she was a lamplighter's daughter and curiosity was the family curse—the cold that rose out of it didn't feel like weather.

It felt like *attention*.

Something far below noticed her looking.

She jerked back. Her heel caught the bucket; it clattered against the stones, absurdly loud, and every person in the company flinched, and down in the well, in the black, something exhaled—a long, slow movement of air that smelled of deep winter and, faintly, horribly, of *bread*. Fresh bread. The smell of the bakery where the man stood forever waiting with his peel.

"Oh," Pip said. Her voice came out very small. "Oh, that's cruel. That's *cruel*."

"What did you smell?" Emrys was beside her instantly, gripping her arm.

"Bread. Warm bread." Her eyes stung. "It's wearing what they loved. It's—it took everything they remembered and now it's just *sitting* in it, like a maggot in fruit."

Emrys went very still. When they spoke, their voice had dropped into the register Pip privately called the storm-voice, low and resonant, the voice of someone who had once argued with thunder and won. "This isn't the Veil dying passively. This is a wound, and something is feeding at it. Or—" they glanced at Sable, "—something is *harvesting*."

"For Corvess," Sable said flatly. "For the bloodline. Stolen memories, woven into power. We read the archive, Emrys. This is what it looks like when the theft gets industrial."

Down the street, in a lit window, the woman with her hands in the cold water dripped and dripped and dripped.

"We can't carry thirty-one people," Sable said. "We can't feed them, we can't wake them, we can't stay. Every hour we linger is an hour Corvess gains on us, and I will not say that gently because somebody has to say it at all."

"I know." Emrys's jaw was tight. "I know. But we're not leaving them to freeze either. Pip—"

"Yes." She was already moving. "Lamps. If we can get fires down and doors shut and oil rationed, the houses hold heat till morning. Lamplighter's work." She heard her own voice steady itself around the task, and blessed her father for every freezing night he'd taught her that work was a rope you could climb down on. "Give me twenty minutes and a lot of matches."

* * *

It took nearer two hours, and it was the worst work Pip had ever done.

Not because it was hard—shutting dampers and banking stoves was child's play—but because of the small mercies involved. Tucking a blanket over the shoulders of a man who would never know. Turning the old fellow's chair away from the drafty window. Taking the blackened loaves out of the oven so the smell of burning wouldn't fill the house, and setting them on the sill, and then standing there like an idiot because taking bread from a sleeping man felt like theft even when he'd never wake to miss it.

At the schoolhouse she paused. Chalk still balanced between the teacher's fingers. *REMEM* on the board.

Pip picked up a fresh stick of chalk and finished the word. Then, beneath it, in her best round lamplighter's capitals, she wrote:

*WE FOUND YOU. WE ARE GOING TO FIX THE SKY. HOLD ON.*

It was a stupid thing to write. The woman couldn't read it. Probably no one would ever read it. But Pip stood back and looked at it, at the two handwritings side by side—one frozen mid-word, one stubbornly complete—and decided that stupid things done in hope were still worth doing. Maybe especially then.

On her way out she noticed the schoolhouse bell rope, frayed but sound, hanging in its corner. On impulse she pulled it. Once. Twice.

The bell spoke over the silent village, bronze and round and shockingly alive, and for three rings Marrowgate sounded almost like a place where people lived. Somewhere in the third ring, Pip could have sworn the frost on the well, far off across the square, shivered.

Then she stopped, because she wasn't sure whether she was ringing for the villagers or calling to the thing in the dark, and she didn't want to learn which.

* * *

They camped that night in the chapel, on the far side of the square from the well, with the door barred and Emrys awake on watch. Pip lay on a pew under three blankets and listened to the building tick and settle, and tried not to think about her mother's face.

She failed, of course. She thought about it the way you press a bruise.

Her mother was sick—had been sick since autumn, lungs full of wet gravel, coughing in the Greyhollow dark while the stars went out one impossible night and never came back. And Pip had left her. Had run off down the salt road with smugglers and mapmakers and a storm-caller, chasing a cure that lived at the end of a story, and every mile put more frost between them. And now here was a whole village of people whose faces had been scooped out like gourds, and Pip lay in the dark doing the arithmetic she couldn't stop doing: *the Veil eats memories, Mama is forgetting things already, she called me Rosemary twice before I left, she called me Rosemary, that was Mama's mother's name—*

"You're crying," said Emrys's voice softly, from the watch by the door.

"I'm not."

"All right."

A pause. Snow ticked against the windows. Then, because Pip was fifteen and tired and frightened and had run out of pride somewhere around the second frozen child:

"If it takes her," she whispered, "even if they fix everything, even if the Veil heals and the stars come back—if it takes her memories before we get there, then fixing the sky doesn't give her back to me. Does it?"

Emrys was quiet a long time. Long enough that Pip thought they wouldn't answer.

"No," they said at last. "Some things given away don't come back. Believe me—I'm counting on knowing that better than anyone." A log shifted in the little stove. "But listen to me, Pip. What the winter takes, it takes whole. What people *give*—that's different. That roots. Your mother gave you her name and her temper and her terrible singing, and none of that lives in her head alone anymore. It lives in you. The Veil can drink a mind dry and it still won't get all of her, because part of her is walking around in your boots, lighting lamps and finishing strangers' sentences on chalkboards."

Pip stared into the dark, sniffling, furious at how much she wanted that to be true.

"How do you know?" she managed.

Another silence. When Emrys answered, their voice had gone somewhere far away, back down twenty years of road.

"Because someone gave me something once, and I lost it anyway, and I have spent twenty years learning the difference between losing a thing and never having had it." A breath. "Sleep, Pip. Morning's coming, and tomorrow we walk faster."

* * *

Morning came grey and windless. They stood in the square one last time, packs shouldered, breath steaming, thirty-one lit windows watching them with no eyes at all.

"We'll send help," Emrys told the village, aloud, as if it could hear—as if *they* could hear. "When the covenant stands again, we'll send everyone we can find. Hold on."

Sable was already consulting her maps, lips moving, compass out. "There's another town upstream. Threewells, eight miles. Bigger. If the ripples spread along the water—"

She stopped.

Pip followed her gaze north, up the frozen river road, and her stomach dropped through the ice of the world.

Frost was coming down the road toward them. Not weather-frost. It crept along the ground in a slow fan, glittering, deliberate, a tongue of white reaching from the northern hills like something tasting the air—and where it touched the road, the snow itself seemed to lean toward Marrowgate, toward the well, toward the spiral throat in the square.

Toward *them*.

"It knows we're here," Pip breathed.

Emrys's hand closed around their cracked amulet, and the storm-light that answered, faint and blue and angry, was the brightest color Pip had seen in days.

"Then we run," Emrys said. "*Now.*"
