## 14. The Founder's Lie

The archive had been a cathedral once, or something shaped like one—Sable could tell by the ribs of stone fanning overhead, by the way her boots echoed in a space built to make every footstep feel like an intrusion into someone else's holiness. Now the vaulted ceiling had half fallen, letting snow sift down through the breach in slow, ceremonial spirals, dusting the shattered shelves below like a blessing nobody had asked for.

She'd been mapping for three days.

Emrys and Pip were camped outside in the lee of a collapsed colonnade, keeping a fire small enough to hide from the road, and Sable had told them she needed until dark. What she hadn't told them was that she wasn't sure she wanted to find what she was looking for. Cartographers liked clean lines. Coastlines that met the sea where the sea actually began. Roads that went where the map said they went. She had built her whole stubborn life on the belief that the world was legible—that if you looked hard enough, measured carefully enough, everything would resolve into sense.

The world, lately, had stopped cooperating.

* * *

It had started with a marginal note.

Three shelves deep in the flooded lower stacks, where the paper had swollen into soft gray bricks and the ink had bled into ghosts of itself, Sable had pried apart a waterlogged ledger with a knife and found, scrawled in a hand nothing like the clerk's careful script: *He lied about the Sundering.*

Four words. No signature. Whoever wrote it had pressed so hard the quill had torn the page, and beneath the tear, faint as a bruise, was the impression of more writing—a letter written on the sheet above it, long since rotted away, leaving its shape behind like a footprint in ash.

Sable had sat on a broken shelf in the dark and read those four words perhaps forty times.

Because everyone knew the story of the Sundering. Children learned it before they learned their letters. A hundred and sixty years ago, the realms had been bound together by the Covenant—an accord sealed at the Stones, woven between this world and the other one, the one beyond the Veil—and then, one terrible winter night, the covenant had *broken*. Shattered. And magic had poured into the world uncontrolled, wild and dangerous, burning villages and drowning valleys, until the Wardens rose to contain it. That was the founding myth. That was why magic was outlawed in Greyhollow and every city that flew the Warden's grey banner. Magic had already destroyed the world once; the Wardens were the dam against the second flood.

Sable had never much cared for myths. But she had cared very much about maps, and maps did not lie, and the maps she'd spent the last month making—the ward-failure lines, the places where the border wards had died first, the pattern of the darkness spreading across the sky—all of them pointed at the Covenant Stones. Not away from them. Not at some wild surge of unbound magic loose in the hinterlands. At the Stones themselves, as though whatever had broken a hundred and sixty years ago had broken *there*, and was breaking there again.

And now, in a drowned archive in a town whose name had worn off its own signposts, a dead clerk's anonymous correspondent had torn a page telling her the same thing.

*He lied about the Sundering.*

*He.* Sable knew who *he* was. Everyone did. Aldric Veyra, First Warden, the man on the statue in every city square, the name carved over every garrison door. The hero who had contained the catastrophe. The saint who had given the world order after chaos.

She got back to work.

* * *

By midday she had found the clerk's index, which was the single most useful object in the ruin: a catalog of holdings, organized by subject, and among the subjects—one entry, nearly illegible—*Covenant materials (restricted). Sub-cellar. By order of V.*

Restricted. Sub-cellar. *By order of V.*

Sable allowed herself one dry laugh that echoed off the vaulting and came back sounding like someone else laughing at her.

The sub-cellar was behind a door that had been bricked over in a hurry—she could tell because the mortar was different, newer, slapped on without care, the mason's equivalent of a slammed door. It took her two hours and most of her patience to open a gap wide enough to squeeze through, and when she finally stood inside, holding her lantern up, she understood why they had bricked it instead of simply locking it.

They hadn't wanted anyone to find this room even if they found the building.

Shelves again—but these were iron, not wood, and the documents on them were wrapped in oilcloth and waxed leather, preserved with a deliberateness that made her skin prickle. Someone had expected these papers to matter for a long time. Someone had expected people to come looking.

The first bundle she opened was correspondence, dated in the years after the Sundering. Warden administrative traffic. Ration ledgers, patrol rotations, the dull machinery of a new government finding its feet. She almost set it aside—almost—before she noticed that certain letters bore a private seal she recognized from her history lessons: the tower-and-thread sigil of Aldric Veyra himself.

She spread them out on the floor in chronological order, the way she'd chart coastlines, and began to read the way she read terrain: patiently, looking for what didn't fit.

What didn't fit arrived within the hour.

* * *

*"The narrative holds,"* wrote one of Veyra's early lieutenants, in the spring after the Sundering. *"The people accept that the covenant failed of its own age. But the questions persist in the eastern provinces. They ask how a binding woven by both realms, renewed each generation, could fail in a single night. I recommend we move faster on the attestation edicts. Questions are easier to outlaw than answers."*

Veyra's reply, in his own hand—tight, precise, a hand that reminded Sable unpleasantly of High Warden Corvess's proclamations:

*"Let them ask. An answer exists only if we permit it to exist. Burn the eastern archive at Marrowford. I want no record surviving that predates my account."*

Marrowford. Sable closed her eyes. Marrowford's great library had burned—every schoolchild knew it, another crime laid at magic's door, another wound in the long indictment against the unregulated gift. She had walked past the charred foundation stones of it twice in her life and felt the grief everyone was taught to feel.

She opened the next bundle with hands that were not quite steady.

* * *

The deeper she read, the colder the room seemed to grow, and it was not the sub-cellar's damp.

There were work orders. That was the horror of it, the thing that made her sit back on her heels and stare at the lantern flame: the sundering of the covenant had not been a catastrophe. It had been a *project*. There were requisitions. There were schedules. There was a labor roster assigning teams of artificers to the Covenant Stones over a period of eleven months, and a series of payments drawn from a treasury account under a cipher she eventually cracked because cartographers are, at bottom, people who cannot leave a code alone.

Eleven months of work to break a binding that the histories said shattered in a night.

And threaded through all of it, in Veyra's increasingly confident hand, a vocabulary that made her stomach turn. He did not write of destroying the covenant. He wrote of *harvesting* it. Of *threads*. The covenant, it emerged from the correspondence the way a coastline emerges from fog, had not been a contract between rulers. It had been a weaving of memory—memory freely given, realm to realm, generation to generation, each renewal requiring the participants to offer up something true of themselves. That was what held the Veil taut. Not force. Not law. *Gifts.* Freely given memory, the willing surrender of what you loved most, offered so that two worlds could keep faith with each other.

And Aldric Veyra had found a way to take instead of give.

The technical documents were harder—artificer's shorthand, diagrams she could only half parse—but the gist was unmistakable, and it was worse than theft. He had not merely stolen the memory-threads of the covenant. He had *woven them into himself*. Into his bloodline. There was a passage, copied in a scribe's careful hand from something older, that Sable read four times before she let herself believe it:

*"The bearer shall hold what the realms surrendered. The power of the covenant shall pass not to the worthy but to the heir. Thus is order secured: not by the consent of the many, but by the custody of the one."*

Custody. Sable laughed again, and this time it came out ugly. He had stolen the freely given memories of two worlds and called it *custody*, the way thieves call burglary *retrieval*.

She thought of Emrys's face when they spoke of the Veil dying—not fear exactly, something older and sadder, the look of someone watching a parent fade. Emrys had grown up believing the covenant broke by accident, the way everyone had. Had carried twenty years of exile under a sky that went dark because the world's oldest promise had failed. And the promise hadn't failed.

It had been picked clean, thread by thread, by a man who then built a religion of control on top of the empty socket.

* * *

She worked until her lantern guttered, and then she worked by candle stub, and then she worked by the gray light coming through the breached ceiling, because she could not stop. There was a final bundle, wrapped separately, sealed with wax stamped by the tower-and-thread, and labeled in Veyra's own hand: *For my successors. Read once. Burn after.*

Whoever was supposed to burn it had kept it instead. Sable decided she would be grateful to that particular coward for the rest of her life.

Inside was a single document, part testament, part instruction manual, and entirely damning. In it, Veyra addressed the future Wardens of Greyhollow directly, and his voice on the page was so reasonable, so weary with the burden of genius, that Sable had to remind herself she was reading the confession of the greatest thief in history.

*"You will be tempted to restore the covenant,"* he wrote. *"Do not. A restored covenant requires gifts, and gifts require givers, and givers require freedom, and freedom is the wound we were raised to heal. The threads I hold are sufficient for ten generations. Guard them. Renew them from your own stock. Let the bloodline be the covenant, and let no one—no witch, no storm-caller, no sentimental fool with a theory of consent—convince the people they ever had a right to what I safeguard."*

Ten generations. Sable did the arithmetic the way she plotted distances, automatically, terribly. A hundred and sixty years. Call it five generations per century, generously. Ten generations was two hundred years.

The math had run out. The stolen threads were spent—or nearly—or failing, and the Veil was dying because the machinery built to mask the theft was finally grinding to a halt. The stars weren't going out because the covenant had broken.

They were going out because the *bill* had come due.

And somewhere in the Warden's line, someone named Corvess—whoever she truly was, whatever she truly knew—was hunting for a solution. For a covenant-heir. For blood that might renew what blood had stolen.

Sable sat in the freezing sub-cellar amid two hundred years of lies filed in perfect order, and understood, with a cartographer's certainty, that every map she had ever trusted had been drawn on top of a forgery.

* * *

She climbed out through the gap in the brickwork stiff and half-frozen, clutching the documents she'd dared to carry—she'd taken only what fit inside her coat, and choosing had been agony—and crossed the snow-dusted nave toward the gray daylight. Her legs ached. Her eyes burned. She had a violent, fleeting urge to burn the whole archive down, and she recognized the impulse for what it was: Veyra's own instinct, inherited by anyone who'd just learned what records can do.

Outside, the light was the color of dishwater. Pip was awake, feeding the fire, and jumped up when Sable appeared, relief and worry fighting openly on her young face. Emrys rose more slowly, snow in their dark hair, and Sable watched them come toward her with that careful, guarded walk they had—twenty years of practiced invisibility in every step—and felt something crack open in her chest that had nothing to do with the cold.

They think the Sundering was an accident. They've built their whole shame on top of it. On top of *his* lie.

"Sable?" Emrys said. "You've been gone three days. You look like you've seen a ghost."

"Worse," Sable said. Her voice came out rougher than she intended. She pulled the oilcloth packet from inside her coat, and her hands, usually so steady over a drafting table, would not quite hold still. "I've seen the founder."

Pip glanced between them, sensing the weather change. "The founder of what?"

"The Wardens." Sable looked at Emrys, and made herself say it plainly, because plainness was the only mercy she had left to offer. "Aldric Veyra didn't contain the Sundering, Emrys. He *caused* it. Eleven months of planned work at the Stones. He harvested the covenant—stole the memory-threads, the freely given ones, generations of them—and wove them into his own bloodline. Every Warden since has been guarding the theft and calling it guardianship." She swallowed. "The covenant didn't break. It was robbed. And the sky going dark isn't the wound reopening—it's the debt coming due. His heirs have been spending what he stole, and they've nearly spent it all."

The silence that followed was long enough for snow to fall between them.

Emrys had gone very still, the way water goes still before it freezes or floods. When they finally spoke, their voice was quiet and strange. "Twenty years," they said. "I exiled myself for a storm I thought proved the covenant was failing. I thought the world was *breaking*, and I was part of the breakage." Their eyes lifted to Sable's, and there was something raw and dawning in them, grief changing shape in real time. "If it was never broken—if it was *taken*—then the exile, the hiding, all of it—"

"Was built on his lie," Sable finished softly. "All of ours were. Every law, every attestation, every child taught to fear their own gifts—they're all standing on a forgery he signed."

Pip had gone pale. "Then Corvess—" the girl started, and stopped, because she was quick, quicker than any of them gave her credit for. "Corvess is his heir. If the power passes by blood, then she's not trying to stop the dark. She's trying to *refill* it. She needs—"

"Threads," Sable said. "Memories. Gifts." She looked at Emrys, and hated what she was about to say, and said it anyway, because a map that flatters the traveler is worthless. "Or a covenant-heir. Someone with old blood in them. Someone the Stones might still recognize."

The wind moved through the ruined cathedral behind them, stirring the snow on the shattered shelves, and somewhere in the deep of the sub-cellar a rotted shelf finally gave way with a sound like a bone breaking.

"We have to move," Sable said. "Today. Because if I could find this truth in a drowned cellar, so can she—and Emrys, I don't think she's searching for it." She tucked the packet back inside her coat, over her heart, where the important things go. "I think she already knows. And I think she's coming to the Stones to collect."
