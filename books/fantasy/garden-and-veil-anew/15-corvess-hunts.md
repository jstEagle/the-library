## 15. Corvess Hunts

The map room of Wardenhold had not been warm in forty years, and High Warden Sable Corvess preferred it that way. Cold kept the mind honest. Cold reminded her that comfort was a currency her family had never been permitted to spend—not while the sky above them was a wound, not while the stolen light in her veins dimmed by the season like a lamp running out of oil.

She stood at the great table with its relief of the northern provinces carved in pale ashwood, her gloved fingers resting on the place where Greyhollow sat like a knot in the grain. Around her, the room's high windows showed a sky without stars. It had been eleven days since the stars went out, and the city below had begun to pray to her.

That was the part no one understood—no one but the dead. They thought she had ordered the darkness. They whispered it in the lower wards: *the Wardens broke the sky; the Wardens ate the light.* Let them whisper. Fear was a tool she had inherited along with everything else, and tools were meant to be used.

But the truth was worse than their whispers, and she carried it alone.

*The power is failing.*

She felt it the way one feels a tooth going bad—slowly, then all at once, then constantly. The threads Aldric Veyra had torn from the covenant four generations ago and woven into his bloodline were unraveling. Her grandfather had been strong enough to stop a river with a word. Her father could still call frost from a summer sky. Corvess herself could do—what? She could sense casters through stone walls. She could make a grown man confess with a look. Small magics. Administrative magics. The interest on an inheritance being spent down to nothing.

And when it was gone entirely, the Veil would finish dying, and the long winter would come for both worlds, and every soul in every realm would learn what her family had done to buy their centuries of order.

Unless she found what she needed.

"Report," she said, without turning.

Inquisitor Halloway crossed the room with the particular care of a man who had seen colleagues vanish mid-sentence. He was young for his rank, ambitious, and terrified of her in exactly the correct proportion. He laid a leather folder on the table beside the model of Greyhollow.

"The market quarter sweep, High Warden. Three residual casters taken. A candle-dowser, a luck-broker, and—" he consulted the page, "—a woman who was blessing fish."

"A woman who was blessing fish."

"She claims it was a prayer, High Warden."

"It was a prayer until I decide otherwise." Corvess opened the folder. Three names, three addresses, three small lives about to be fed into the attestation process. None of them mattered. None of them were what she wanted. She closed the folder with two fingers. "And the storm-signature?"

Halloway hesitated. That hesitation told her more than the report would have.

"Speak."

"We've confirmed it, High Warden. On the night the wards fell, there was a discharge in the night market. Storm-calling. Old lineage work—the kind they say died out with the Ashgroves." He swallowed. "It matches the resonance pattern in the founder's private records. The covenant-heir line."

There it was. The words she had been hunting for twenty years, delivered by a man too frightened to understand what he was saying.

Corvess turned back to the map. Her reflection ghosted across the glass case over it—a tall woman in grey wool and black iron, silver at the temples though she was barely forty, eyes the color of a frozen lake just before it cracks. People said she looked like a statue of some stern saint. They didn't know how right they were. Statues were what remained when something living gave itself away piece by piece.

"The Ashgrove girl," she said quietly. "Or boy. Or whatever shape the heir has chosen to wear." She had read the old files so many times she could recite them. *Emrys Ashgrove, presumed dead in the exile-storm of the borderlands. Body never recovered.* Presumed dead, because dead was tidier, and because the Wardens of that era had preferred not to think too hard about what they'd driven into the wilderness. "Twenty years hiding under our noses. Selling charms, if the informant reports are worth anything. Playing at being nothing."

"Do you believe the heir caused the ward-failure, High Warden?"

"No." She let the word fall flat as a dropped coin. "The ward-failure happened *because* the covenant is dying, and the covenant is dying because my ancestor gutted it. The heir's little storm was a symptom, not a cause. A door rattling in its frame before the house comes down." She traced the borderlands on the map, the burned scar of the Ashline where the wards had stood for four hundred years. "But the heir can feel the Veil. Can hear it. Which means the heir can find the Stones—and the Stones are where I must go, and what I must have."

Halloway's brow furrowed. He was clever enough to be dangerous and loyal enough to be useful, which meant she would eventually have to decide between those qualities. Not yet.

"You mean to reforge the covenant, High Warden?"

"I mean to *renew* it." She chose the word with care. Reforging implied equality, exchange, all that sentimental nonsense about freely given memory that the original text went on about. Renewal implied continuity. Implied that what had been built by theft could be maintained by theft, provided the thief was competent. "Aldric Veyra made a mistake. He took memories by force and wove them raw into the bloodline, and raw thread frays. Four generations later, here we are. But the principle was sound. Power drawn from the covenant itself, held by one hand, wielded for order. He simply lacked patience. He lacked craft." She smiled, and saw Halloway flinch, and noted it. "I am very patient, Inquisitor. And I have had a great deal of time to study his craft."

"And the heir's blood?"

"Is the key the founder never possessed." She moved her hand across the map, north, toward the mountains where the Covenant Stones slept under snow and ruin. "Aldric stole from the lattice. He never touched the living line of the covenant-makers themselves. The Ashgrove blood remembers how the binding works—not in records, not in ritual, but in the body. In the marrow. With an heir willing or unwilling, I can anchor the threads properly this time. Permanently. No more slow leak. No more watching my own strength gutter like a cheap lamp."

She did not tell him the rest. She did not tell him that the renewal required memory given at the moment of binding—that even Aldric's notes, mad and arrogant as they were, agreed on that single non-negotiable term. *Freely given,* the old covenant read, and the lattice did not lie, and neither did the founder's marginalia, scrawled in a hand gone shaky with rage: *it must be offered. It cannot simply be taken. This is the flaw in the design. I will find a way around it.*

He hadn't. Four generations of Veyras and Corvesses hadn't. But Corvess believed she knew a way, and it was ugly, and it required an heir who could be made to offer—truly offer, in the depths of their soul—while believing they were doing something else entirely. Grief, she had learned, was the most reliable forge for genuine offering. Break a person down far enough, take enough from them, and they will give you anything just to make the taking stop. And the lattice, she suspected, did not check the reasons behind a gift. Only its truth.

So she needed Emrys Ashgrove alive. Afraid. Grieving. And finally, at the last, willing.

"Double the patrols on the Salt Road," she said. "Every checkpoint from Greyhollow to the Ashline. If the heir runs north, they'll need smugglers, and smugglers run routes, and routes can be watched." She pulled a pin from the map's edge and set it in the pass country. "And bring me the lamplighter girl."

Halloway blinked. "High Warden?"

"Pip Thistlewick. Sixteen. Lamplighter's apprentice, third district. Her mother is ill; her mother owes debts; her mother will say yes to anything that buys medicine." Corvess had spent two nights reading the residue reports from the night market, cross-referencing the small warm signatures of hedge-magic against census rolls, and the girl's name had surfaced twice. Once for unlicensed charm-work. Once for standing too close to the storm-discharge and walking away unburned. "The heir has a weakness. They always do. Twenty years alone makes people soft toward children who remind them of themselves. Find out if the girl has been seen talking to any charm-seller in particular, and then leave her be—for now. A hook is only useful if the fish doesn't know it's in the water."

"Yes, High Warden." Halloway gathered his folder. At the door he paused, the way subordinates always did, gathering courage for the question that would get someone else promoted. "May I ask—why does it matter so much to you personally? The renewal. Some of the Council say we should simply fortify the cities and endure the winter. Ration the dark. They say—forgive me—they say the old power was never ours to hold."

Corvess considered him for a long moment. Then she crossed to the window and looked out at the starless sky, and for once she answered honestly, because honesty, deployed rarely and precisely, was more persuasive than any speech.

"When I was nine years old," she said, "my father took me up to the observatory tower and showed me the founder's vault. There's a window in it, sealed behind iron, and through the iron you can see—just barely—a fragment of preserved Veil-light. One of the threads Aldric didn't use. It's the most beautiful thing I have ever seen. It moves like water remembering it used to be a river. And my father told me: *this is what we are custodians of. This is why our family endures what other families never could. Because someone must hold the wound closed, and holding it hurts, and only we have learned to bear it.*" She turned. "He died three winters later, hollowed out, spending the last of the bloodline's strength keeping the eastern wards standing while the Council thanked him and cut his budget. So yes, Inquisitor. It matters to me personally. My family has paid for this order in ourselves, generation after generation, and the world we bought with that payment is now calling us monsters and praying to us in the same breath. I intend to finish what Aldric started. Properly. Permanently. And then no Corvess will ever again have to stand in a cold room watching the light die and pretend she doesn't grieve it."

Halloway stood very still. "You'll forgive me, High Warden. That sounded almost like—"

"Duty. Yes. It does sound like duty. That's what makes it dangerous." She waved him out. "Go. Checkpoints by dawn. And Halloway—if the heir is taken alive, no one touches them but me. Anyone who harms so much as a hair delivers their whole company to the attestation blocks. Am I understood?"

"Perfectly, High Warden."

He left. The door boomed shut, and the silence came back, and Corvess allowed herself, at last, to stop performing.

* * *

Alone, she went to the cabinet.

It was a plain thing, iron-banded oak, locked with three keys she wore on separate chains—one at her throat, one at her wrist, one sewn into her boot. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth, lay the founder's journal: Aldric Veyra's own hand, recovered from the ruined archive at Lastlight before the deserters could burn it all. She had killed two men to get it. She thought of it as the most honest book ever written, because its author had never expected anyone else to read it.

She unwrapped it and turned to the pages near the end, where the handwriting deteriorated from script into something closer to claw-marks.

*The lattice speaks tonight. It called me thief. As if the realms were not already bleeding out through the crack, as if the old covenant were not a leash on every strong soul born under it. Freely given, they wrote. Freely given! As if anything free has ever held. Order is not given. Order is taken, and held, and paid for.*

*I took ninety-one threads. I wove them true. But the weave pulls. Every year it pulls tighter, and every year I feel myself thinning, and I begin to suspect the truth I will not write aloud: that the theft does not replace the giving. That the lattice knows the difference. That I have built my house on a foundation of borrowed light, and my children's children will inherit the debt.*

Below that, in fresher ink, in her grandmother's careful hand:

*Debt confirmed. Seeking alternative anchors. Blood of the maker-line may substitute for gift. Unverified.*

And below that, in her father's blunt strokes:

*Unverified. Tested once. Subject died screaming. Do not attempt without an heir who consents. Finding such a fool is the task of generations.*

Corvess closed the book gently, the way one closes a coffin.

Her family had known, then. All of them. Had known the power was borrowed, the foundation rotten, the debt compounding—and had chosen, each generation, to keep spending rather than confess. She could almost admire it. It took a certain terrible discipline to pass a lie down four generations intact, polishing it each time, adding locks and laws and purity attestations to keep anyone from looking underneath.

She was going to be the one who ended it. Not by confession—confession would destroy the Wardens, and the Wardens were the only wall between these frightened cities and the dark—but by payment. A final payment, large enough to cover four generations of theft. An heir's freely given memory, harvested at the Stones, woven where the stolen threads had frayed away. The books balanced. The wall rebuilt. The long winter turned back at the border of the world.

That someone would suffer for it was unavoidable. Someone always suffered; that was the price of order, and pretending otherwise was the sentimentality that had rotted the old covenant in the first place. But she would keep the suffering as small as she could make it. One life, one memory, one grief—weighed against two worlds. Any honest accountant would sign off on that trade.

She was an excellent accountant.

* * *

Dawn came grey and starless over Wardenhold, and with it came the courier.

The man was half-frozen, his dispatch satchel crusted with road-ice, and he knelt in the map room's doorway breathing hard. Corvess took the dispatch tube, cracked the seal, and read.

Her informants in Greyhollow's night market had finally earned their bribes. A charm-seller. Stall at the corner of Tanner's Row, twenty years established, quiet, unremarkable, beloved by neighborhood grandmothers. Known to the locals as *Ash*. And on the night the stars went out, this charm-seller had been seen standing in the middle of the emptying market with their face turned up to the dying sky, and the frost on the stalls around them had bloomed into patterns no natural cold could make—spirals, sigils, the old covenant-script that hadn't been written anywhere in four hundred years.

And this morning, per the second report, the charm-seller's stall was shuttered. Neighbors said *Ash* had gone to visit a sick friend. Neighbors said a cartographer had been asking questions. Neighbors said a lamplighter girl had been seen slipping out of the shop before dawn, carrying a bundle, crying.

Corvess read the reports twice. Then she set them down on the map table, precisely aligned, and stood looking at the north of the map for a long time.

They were running. Of course they were running. Straight toward the only place in the world that could answer the questions the dying Veil was shouting at them. She could almost respect it. The heir had spent twenty years playing small, and the moment the sky broke open, they had turned toward the wound instead of away from it. There was courage in that. There was even, she supposed, a kind of nobility.

It changed nothing. Nobility was just another resource to be spent.

"Halloway," she said, and the Inquisitor materialized from the corridor as if he'd been waiting with his ear to the door, which he probably had. "Ready the fast column. Twelve riders, no banners. We ride for the Ashline within the hour." She pinned a second marker to the map, north of the first, closing the pincer. "And send word ahead to Captain Ashgrove."

Halloway went still. "The captain, High Warden? He's stationed at the Graywater checkpoint. That's directly on the heir's likely route."

"I'm aware of where my own captain is stationed, Inquisitor." She picked up the founder's journal and held it a moment, feeling through the oilcloth the weight of four generations of debt. "Captain Tam Ashgrove is the finest tracker in the Wardens, the brother of the person we're hunting, and the man whose false testimony sent her into exile in the first place. Whatever he feels when he sees her again—guilt, love, shame—it will make him relentless. Relentless men catch runners." She smiled thinly. "And if he should hesitate instead... well. Then I'll learn something useful about him too. Either way, I win. That is generally how I arrange things."

"Yes, High Warden." A pause. "Will that be all?"

"One more thing." She crossed to the window, where the first grey light was failing to make the sky anything but a bruise. Somewhere beyond it, in the burned borderlands, a charm-seller with a storm in their blood was running toward her with every mile. "Have the observatory staff monitor the Veil-residue readings hourly. If the heir attempts any significant working on the road, I want to know within the hour. Every time they reach for the storm, the lattice will sing, and I will hear it." She watched the lightless dawn. "They think they're escaping me. Let them. Every step they run north is a step toward the Stones, and the Stones are mine. By the first snowfall, Inquisitor, I will be standing among the Covenant Stones with an heir on their knees and the sky in my fist—and this family's debt will be paid in full."

Halloway bowed and withdrew. The door boomed. The cold held.

Corvess stood alone in the map room a moment longer, her hand flat on the ashwood north, on the tiny carved mountains where her fate waited under snow. Then she rang for her horse, and Wardenhold began, all around her, to wake and arm and move—quietly, efficiently, like a machine built over four generations for exactly this purpose.

The hunt was on.
