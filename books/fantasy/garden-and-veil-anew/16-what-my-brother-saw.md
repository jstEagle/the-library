## 16. What My Brother Saw

The storm came down off the mountains two days after they left Lastlight behind, and by the time they reached the inn again—Marrow's inn, the one with the crooked sign and the hearth that never seemed to burn less than a full blaze—they were half-frozen and out of arguments about which pass Sable's map promised would hold.

Emrys stood in the doorway with snow crusted in their lashes and felt something they had not felt in twenty years: the instinct to knock.

It was a small thing. A foolish thing. They had slept under this roof once already; Marrow had fed them stew and asked nothing, had looked at Emrys with those gray, patient eyes and said only, *You'll want the room at the end of the hall. The window sticks, but it keeps the cold honest.* And yet here they stood on the threshold like a beggar, hand raised, because some old wound in them still believed that shelter was a thing you had to earn back every time, that it could be revoked without warning, that the people who gave it would one day look at you and see what you truly were and turn you out into the weather.

Pip shoved past them, snow melting off her red nose, already shouting for hot cider. Sable followed, muttering about the map being *perfectly accurate* and the mountain being *personally spiteful*. And Emrys knocked—lightly, once—on the doorframe of an unlocked door, and heard Marrow's boots on the floorboards before the light spilled out warm and yellow across the snow.

"You're dripping on my step," Marrow said. "Come drown yourself in the fire instead."

* * *

The evening arranged itself around the hearth the way good evenings do: Pip asleep sitting up with a mug of cider balanced precariously against her knee until Sable rescued it; the Bramble twins' absence a quiet ache none of them named aloud, though Emrys caught Sable touching the folded map in her coat pocket where she'd marked the pass they'd held—the pass that had cost them. The wind worried the shutters. The fire talked to itself in its low, contented voice.

Emrys sat closest to the flames, as they always did now. Old habit. Storm-callers ran cold; their mother used to say they carried winter in the hollow of their bones to make room for the thunder. They had not thought of their mother's sayings in years. Lately they thought of little else.

Marrow settled into the chair opposite with the deliberate slowness of a large person conserving energy, and for a long while said nothing. That was Marrow's way. They had been a Warden once—Emrys had known since that first night, had seen it in the way the ex-deserter moved, economical and watchful, a body trained by an institution rather than a trade—and yet Marrow asked nothing, pried nothing, offered nothing but food and dry beds and a silence so complete it felt like a courtesy.

Tonight, though, the silence had weight. Emrys felt it settle over their shoulders like a second cloak.

"You keep looking at me," Emrys said finally, without opening their eyes. The fire painted the inside of their lids orange. "You've been looking at me since the night we arrived."

"I have."

"Say whatever it is, then. I'm too tired to be frightened of it."

Marrow was quiet a moment longer. When Emrys opened their eyes, the big woman was leaning forward, elbows on her knees, hands loose between them—scarred hands, Warden-issue gauntlets long traded away, knuckles like river stones.

"Do you know why I deserted?" Marrow asked.

Emrys blinked. Of all the openings they had imagined—and they had imagined several, in the sleepless hour past midnight when the wind screamed loudest—this was not among them. "I assumed—" They stopped. Assumed cowardice, or conscience, or some private grief. Everyone who fled the Wardens fled from something.

"Everyone assumes," Marrow said, and there was no bitterness in it, only a kind of worn smoothness, like a stone the river had given up arguing with. "They assume I couldn't stomach the work, or I stole from the quartermaster, or I loved someone the purity attestations disapproved of. All fine reasons. Some of them are even true of me." She turned her hands over, studied the palms. "But the true reason is smaller than any of those. Twenty-two years ago I was stationed at a border garrison called Vantage Post, in the Ashgrove hills. And I watched a tribunal decide a girl's whole life in an afternoon, and I said nothing, and I have been saying nothing ever since, and last month the stars went out, and I found I couldn't do it anymore."

The fire popped. Somewhere upstairs a floorboard creaked under settling snow.

Emrys's mouth had gone dry. "Ashgrove," they repeated. Their own name, spoken back to them from another life. "That's—"

"That's your family's name. Yes." Marrow's eyes came up, gray and steady and terribly kind. "I know who you are, Emrys Ashgrove. I've known since you walked through my door with frost in your hair and your hood up like hiding is just how you wear a coat. I knew your face before you ever crossed my threshold, because I spent three days guarding a tribunal tent while they decided what to do with it."

Sable, in the corner, had gone very still. She did not pretend to read her maps anymore when conversations like this began; she simply watched, sharp-eyed, cataloguing. Emrys felt her attention like a second pair of hands on their shoulders.

"The exile-storm," Emrys said. The words came out flat, rehearsed, the litany they had recited inwardly ten thousand times. "I called a storm I couldn't control. It killed three people. One of them was my brother."

"No," Marrow said.

The word landed like a dropped glass. Emrys heard it break somewhere inside themselves and felt nothing for a moment—not denial, not hope, just a strange ringing silence, the way the air goes after lightning.

"No," Marrow said again, gentler now. "Tam Ashgrove did not die in that storm. Tam Ashgrove walked out of it alive, and stood in front of a Warden tribunal, and told them his sister's storm killed three people—one of whom was himself."

* * *

Afterward, Emrys could never reconstruct the order of things. Whether Marrow rose first or they did; whether anyone spoke during the long minutes when Emrys stood at the window with their palms flat against the freezing glass, breathing fog onto it and wiping it away and breathing more. The room continued. Pip slept on. Sable poured something amber from a bottle behind the bar and set it near Emrys's elbow without comment, and it sat there untouched, catching firelight.

"Tell me," Emrys said at last. Their voice sounded borrowed. "All of it. Don't soften it. I've had twenty years of softened versions—I wrote most of them myself."

Marrow nodded slowly, as if this were the right answer, the answer she'd hoped for, and resettled herself.

"I was a lance-warden then. Young. Posted to Vantage because I was competent and unimportant, which is the combination institutions prize most." A ghost of humor crossed her face and died. "The storm hit on the eve of the harvest fair. You'd have been—what, seventeen?"

"Seventeen," Emrys whispered. Seventeen, and standing in their mother's garden showing Tam how to feel the pressure change in his back teeth, laughing, the sky going green-black at the edges far too fast—

"The official record says a rogue caster summoned a tempest that destroyed the fairground pavilion, killing three attendees." Marrow's voice took on the flat cadence of a report, and Emrys understood suddenly that she had carried this report in her head for two decades, word-perfect, the way soldiers carry orders they failed to obey. "What the record does not say is that the pavilion was struck by a single massive discharge at the storm's leading edge—that the rest of the storm, the part that lasted hours, behaved strangely. Contained. As if someone were holding it away from the town all night."

Emrys's breath fogged the glass. They had held it. They remembered holding it—knees in the mud of the north field, arms flung wide, screaming words their mother had taught them and words she'd never gotten the chance to, dragging the storm's whole writhing weight up off the fairground and the village and grinding it down over empty pastureland until dawn, until their gift burned out of them like a wick burning to nothing. They had never told anyone that part. In the tribunal's version, and in every version they had built since, there was only the strike, the deaths, the guilt. They had let the guilt swallow the rest. Guilt was simpler. Guilt required nothing of anyone.

"Three dead," Emrys said. "That part was true. Three people died. I saw—" Their throat closed. "I saw the bodies carried out. I saw Tam's—"

"You saw a sheet over someone," Marrow said quietly. "And the Wardens let you believe it was your brother, because a grieving caster confesses cleanly. Grief is a very efficient tool. We were trained in it, though we didn't call it training." She leaned back. "Two of the dead were real—a chandler named Odell Fenn and his daughter, caught under the pavilion beam. The third name on the list was Tam Ashgrove. Except Tam wasn't dead. Tam had been pulled from the wreckage half-drowned, concussed, and taken into Warden custody before your family even knew he'd survived. And the next morning, he testified."

The fire cracked. Wind pressed at the shutters like something wanting in.

"He testified that he saw you call the storm deliberately," Marrow went on. "That you had threatened to unmake the town if the covenant-wardens didn't release you from the registration rolls. He described your casting stance. Your words of calling. Details only a brother would know." Her gray eyes did not waver. "He was fourteen years old, Emrys. He had a head wound and he was alone in a tent full of inquisitors who had already written their verdict, and I have spent twenty-two years telling myself that whatever they did to make him say those words, he was a child and they were professionals, and that the shame belongs to the men in gray robes and not to the boy on the witness stool."

"But he said them," Emrys said. The words tasted like copper. "Whatever they did—he said them. And I was exiled, and Mother—" Their mother had died two winters later, alone, her name on a Warden register of *associated persons*, her letters returned unopened. Emrys had learned of it years afterward, thirdhand, in a port town, and had gotten drunk for the first time in their life and sworn off storms forever.

"And Mother lost everything," Marrow finished softly. "Yes. I stayed at Vantage another year after the tribunal. Long enough to see the paperwork move. Long enough to understand that the verdict had been decided before the storm ever fell—there was a writ, sealed from the capital, authorizing the *removal and binding* of an unregistered storm-caller of the Ashgrove line. They needed a crime. The storm gave them one. Your brother's testimony gave them the shape of it." She paused. "There was a name countersigned on that writ. I didn't understand its significance then. I do now, given what's happened to the sky. Corvess. Then a junior inquisitor. Now High Warden."

Sable made a sound—small, involuntary, the sound of a puzzle piece dropping into place. Emrys barely heard it. The world had narrowed to the window glass and their own reflection ghosted upon it: a charm-seller's plain face, a stranger's face, and beneath it, surfacing like a drowned thing after twenty years, a seventeen-year-old kneeling in the mud with her arms flung open, holding the sky off everyone she loved.

*He lived.* The thought arrived and kept arriving, wave after wave, each one breaking over the last. *He lived. He lived and I mourned him. I built my whole life on his grave and the grave was empty.*

"Why?" The question tore out of them raw. "Why would he—Marrow, he was *kind*, he was a child, he used to fall asleep in the hay barn listening for thunder with me—why would he say those things? What could they possibly have—"

"I don't know," Marrow said. "I've wondered for twenty-two years. But I'll tell you what I saw, the one time I saw him after. A year later, in the garrison yard at Vantage. He'd been taken into the Wardens' training program—you don't survive testimony like that and get sent home; they keep what they've made. He was drilling with the other cadets. Gray uniform, shaved head, posture like a drawn blade." She looked into the fire. "He was the best drill-ground soldier I ever saw. And he was the only one who never once looked at the sky."

* * *

Later—it might have been midnight, might have been nearer dawn—Emrys sat on the floor beside the banked hearth with their knees drawn up, and Sable sat down beside them without asking, which was its own kind of mercy. For a while neither spoke. Outside, the snow had stopped. The silence was enormous.

"I need to say it out loud," Emrys said eventually, "or it won't be real."

Sable said nothing. Waiting. She was good at waiting; it was the cartographer in her, the patience of someone who knew that terrain reveals itself to those who stay.

"I spent twenty years believing I killed my brother." Emrys's voice came out low and level, which surprised them; they had expected it to shatter. "Not just that night. Every day since. Every charm I sold with my head down, every rule I kept, every time I healed someone in secret and hated myself for the risk—because I'd decided I deserved the smallness. I deserved Greyhollow. I deserved to be nobody, because somebody was the person who killed Tam." They laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "And he was never in the grave at all. He was in a gray uniform the whole time. My shame had a heartbeat. My shame grew up and learned to drill and stopped looking at the sky."

"Emrys—"

"They made him do it." The fury arrived late, the way fury does, flooding in behind the numbness. "Fourteen years old, head bleeding, alone with inquisitors—and they *made* him, and then they kept him, they *kept* him like a—like a tool, like a thread they'd cut from the loom and wound onto their own spindle—" Their hands were shaking. Storm-light flickered faintly along their knuckles, blue-white, unbidden, and they clenched their fists until it died. Twenty years of discipline, and it nearly slipped for a boy in a hay barn. "Corvess. The writ had Corvess's name on it. She signed my exile before I ever committed a crime, and now she's hunting us, and Tam is—" 

"You don't know that he's with her," Sable said carefully.

"Don't I?" Emrys stared into the coals. "Best drill-ground soldier she ever saw. The only one who never looks at the sky. Where else would the Wardens keep what they made of him?" They pressed the heels of their hands against their eyes. "I have to find him, Sable. Before the Stones, before any of it—I have to know what they did to him. I have to tell him I never blamed him. God help me, I have to tell him I never even *thought* to blame him, because I was too busy blaming myself, and he's been carrying it alone since he was fourteen—"

Sable's hand closed over their wrist, firm, grounding, a cartographer pinning a map flat against the wind.

"Then we go to the Stones," she said, "and we take the truth out of Aldric Veyra's stolen threads, and we make sure the whole world hears what was done to your family—including Tam. Including Corvess. Every name on every writ." Her grip tightened. "But Emrys. Listen to me. If Corvess holds your brother, then she knows exactly what he is to you. She has had twenty years to think about what a storm-caller will do for the last piece of her family she has left."

Emrys went cold in a way that had nothing to do with the dying fire.

Outside, far off across the white valley, a bell began to ring—once, twice, urgent, the alarm pattern of a village watch. Hoofbeats followed, muffled by snow, many of them, moving fast along the road toward Lastlight.

Gray cloaks rode at the head of them. Emrys could not see them yet. But they could feel it in their back teeth, in the hollow of their bones where the winter lived: the pressure change of a hunt closing in, and at the center of it, drawing steadily nearer through the dark, a heart that beat in rhythm with their own.
