## 17. The Brambles Stand

The pass at Wolfjaw Notch had a smell to it, and Sorrel Bramble knew that smell the way she knew her own hands: old snow, cold iron, and the particular sourness of men who had been standing too long in one place waiting for something to hate.

She lay on her belly on the ridge above the road, elbows dug into a drift that had crusted over like sugar, and counted the Warden tents below. Nine. Cookfire smoke standing straight up in the windless cold. A picket line of horses stamping steam. And strung across the narrow throat of the pass itself, where the rock walls pinched down to scarce two carts' width, a barrier of black-lacquered stakes wrapped in chain — and hanging from the chains, swaying gently, a dozen small glass bells.

*Ward-bells.* Her brother's breath went out of him beside her, slow and controlled, the way he breathed when he was doing arithmetic he didn't like. Thorn Bramble had a face that never changed much — flat brown eyes, a mouth built for chewing rather than talking — but Sorrel had spent twenty-six years reading him the way other folk read weather.

"Those are new," he murmured. "Since the dark came."

"Everything's new since the dark came." She kept her voice under the wind. Below them, an inquisitor in grey walked the line of bells with a tuning fork of pale metal, touching it to each bell in turn. When the fork sang clean, he moved on. When it buzzed wrong, he marked the stake with chalk. *They're listening for casters,* she thought. *Listening for anything that hums against the Veil.* And what was Emrys Ashgrove, huddled three miles back at the ruined shepherd's cot with Pip and Sable Vane and a storm-glass amulet that cracked a little more every time it was used, if not the loudest humming thing left in the world?

Thorn shifted his weight off his bad knee — the one he'd never let anyone treat properly, because treating it would mean admitting it was bad — and said nothing for a long moment. That was how they talked, the two of them. In the spaces between words. Their mother had called it *the twins' tongue*, and their father had called it maddening, and both had been right.

"Road through the high scree is closed," Thorn said finally. "Avalanche took it Tuesday. I saw the fan from the ridge."

"River gorge?"

"Frozen solid, but the ice sings when you walk it. Whole gorge'd ring like those bells." He chewed on a strip of dried plum, working it thoughtfully with his back teeth. "So there's the road, or there's nothing."

"There's us," Sorrel said.

He looked at her then. It was a long look, the kind they'd been giving each other since before either of them could remember, and in it passed everything that needed passing: *I know what you're thinking. I know you're right. I wish you weren't.*

"Two of us," he said. "Nine tents. Ward-bells. And four soft-footed friends who can't fight their way past a picket line without lighting up the sky like a festival."

"Three of us can't be caught carrying them through." She nodded down at the barrier, at the chalk-marked stakes, at the inquisitor's patient grey back. "You heard what Marrow said. Corvess has Ashgrove's name now. Real name, real face, real trail. They're not sweeping for smugglers anymore, Thorn. They're hunting specific."

Thorn spat a plum pit into the snow. "Then we make ourselves very interesting," he said, "and very much not specific."

* * *

They told the others around the shepherd's cot's dying fire, and it went the way Sorrel had known it would go, which was badly.

"No." Sable Vane stood with her arms crossed, map-case slung across her chest like a bandolier, and her chin set at the angle that meant she'd already argued with herself and won. "Absolutely not. We find another way."

"There isn't another way," Sorrel said. "I've sat on that ridge all day. The scree's down, the gorge rings, and every shepherd track south funnels into that notch eventually. It's the notch or it's nothing."

"Then we wait them out. Armies get bored."

"Wardens don't." It was Emrys who said it, quiet, from the corner where they'd been sitting with their hood half up out of habit even indoors. Firelight caught the storm-glass amulet at their throat, and Sorrel watched the light move inside it — slow, sullen, like weather deciding. "Corvess doesn't get bored. I knew her predecessor's people. I know the breed." Their voice did something complicated on the word *knew*, and stopped.

Pip was fourteen and had the particular genius of fourteen-year-olds for cutting straight through an argument everyone else was enjoying. "If the twins stay behind," she said slowly, "and we go ahead — how do we get past the barrier without them? They're the ones who know the countersigns, the toll-words, the—"

"We don't go past the barrier," Thorn said. "That's the whole point. You go *around* it. There's a drainage cut under the old tollhouse — smugglers' work, my grandfather's generation, maybe older. It comes up inside the picket line, forty yards past the stakes, behind the horse lines. Narrow. One at a time, crawling, in the dark."

"And while we're crawling," Sable said, "you two do what, exactly? Sing a shanty?"

"We make noise," Sorrel said. "Big noise. Wrong-direction noise. A fire on the north slope, wagon wheels, shouting — enough that every eye in that camp points away from the tollhouse while five ghosts crawl under it." She grinned, and knew it was the grin that made people underestimate the Brambles, and used it anyway. "We've done harder jobs for less reason."

"You've never done this job for people," Sable said.

And there it was. The room went quiet the way rooms do when someone says the true thing by accident. Sorrel felt it land in her chest and sit there.

Because that was the thing, wasn't it. Twenty years of running salt and charms and unlicensed hope through Greyhollow's cracks, and the Brambles had never once bled for cargo. Cargo didn't have names. Cargo didn't teach a lamplighter girl to whistle through her fingers, didn't argue about maps until midnight, didn't carry a grief around its neck in cracked glass. Somewhere between the night market and the ashline, between Marrow's inn and the hollow village where the people stood frozen mid-task with winter eating their minds, *cargo* had turned into *family*, and neither twin had said it aloud, and here was Sable saying it for them.

"Well," Thorn said, into the silence, in the flattest voice he owned. "First time for everything."

Emrys stood up. They crossed the room, and Sorrel braced herself for an argument, a refusal, some noble nonsense about no one being left behind — but instead Emrys just took her hand, and then Thorn's, and held them a moment with fingers that were colder than they should have been. Storm-caller's fingers. Weather lived in them.

"I buried a brother once," Emrys said. "Or believed I had. I'm not going to stand in a hole like that twice if I can help it." Their eyes were steady and terrible. "Don't die. That's not a request. That's the price of passage, and I'll pay it however you name it later."

"Cheaper than your usual rates," Sorrel managed, because if she didn't joke she was going to say something true, and true things were dangerous in a room with a fire this low.

* * *

They worked through the afternoon with the efficiency of people who had stopped pretending the plan might change. Sable drew the tollhouse cut from Thorn's description, cross-hatched and annotated, muttering about gradients and crawl-clearance. Pip sat with Emrys, learning — Sorrel caught fragments of it, the low murmur of teaching, *breathe from below the ribs, feel for the weight in the air before you ask it to move* — and pretended not to watch, and watched anyway.

At dusk they split the gear. The twins took the loudest things: a spare oilcloth, the signal lamp, Thorn's collection of firepots, and the cart axle they'd carried disassembled since Lastlight for exactly this kind of evening, though none of them had said so.

At the edge of the trees, Pip caught Sorrel's sleeve. Up close the girl smelled of lamp-oil and hedge-magic, that green crackle underneath, and her eyes were too bright.

"When this is done," Pip said, "you're both coming after us. Swear it. Because I'm keeping a list of everyone I'm supposed to end up knowing, and you're both on it, and I don't cross people off."

Sorrel laughed, rougher than she meant to. "Wouldn't dream of it, sprout. Now get gone before I say something soft."

Pip went. They all went, five shapes folding into the blue dusk toward the tollhouse, and Sorrel stood watching until Thorn touched her shoulder, and then the night belonged to the two of them and the work.

* * *

The trick to a good diversion, their father had taught them, was that it had to be worth believing in. A small lie invites inspection; a large one invites awe. So they built awe.

On the north slope, in a fold of rock where the camp's sightlines converged, they raised the cart axle upright and dressed it in the oilcloth so that, in bad light, it might be a figure. Around its feet they stacked firepots with their fuses braided together, and Thorn ran the fuse-line back along a game trail with the patience of a man laying out a wedding feast. Meanwhile Sorrel climbed the southern ridge with the signal lamp and a coil of copper wire and her own two hands, which were, whatever else could be said of the Bramble twins, extremely good hands.

The plan was simple. At the appointed hour, Thorn lit the fuse. The firepot stack went up as one bloom — flame and sparks and the axle-figure silhouetted against it, a caster-shaped shadow thrown huge on the snow. Simultaneously, Sorrel would strike the lamp and flash it in the old smuggler pattern that meant *raid from the ridge, multiple runners*, a code every Warden deserter in the borderlands still half-remembered. Between the false caster and the false raid, the camp would turn itself inside out pointing north and east, and five crawlers would slip beneath its blind side like thread through a needle's eye.

Simple. It was even working.

The bloom went up at moonrise, gorgeous and obscene, a second false dawn. Sorrel flashed her lamp — three long, two short, three long — and below her the camp detonated into motion, horns blaring, men spilling from tents with drawn steel, horses screaming at the fire-light. Every lantern swung north. Every crossbow pointed at a burning scarecrow.

And that was when Sorrel saw the second party.

They came up the south trail on foot, six of them, moving in the loose practiced file of inquisitors on a hunt — and they weren't reacting to the fire at all. They were *past* it somehow, or had never been part of the camp's confusion, and they were heading directly for the treeline. For the tollhouse. For the drainage cut.

For their family.

Later, Sorrel would understand it: the ward-bells hadn't only been listening for casters. They'd been listening for *movement against the Veil* — and Emrys, teaching Pip in that ruined cot, breathing from below the ribs, feeling for the weight in the air, had rung every bell in the pass like a dinner chime. The inquisitors hadn't taken the bait because they'd never been looking at the fire. They'd been following a sound only they could hear, and it led south.

There was no time to signal Thorn. There was barely time to think. Sorrel did the arithmetic she'd been doing her whole life — two of us, six of them, our people in a hole in the ground with their backs to the sky — and the sum came out the same way it always came out for Brambles.

She slid down the scree toward the trail, drawing her hatchet, and put two fingers in her mouth and whistled the falling note that meant, in the twins' tongue, *now, brother, now.*

* * *

What followed was not a battle. Battles have lines and phases and some dignity of shape. What followed was a knife-fight in the dark between rocks, all elbows and breath and snow kicked up blinding, and Sorrel remembered it afterward the way you remember a fall — in flashes, out of order.

The first inquisitor went down to Thorn's weighted net and didn't get up cleanly. The second reached Sorrel and learned what her hatchet was for, though not well enough, not fast enough, and she took a slice along her forearm that she wouldn't feel until much later. The third and fourth tried to flank toward the tollhouse and met Thorn coming downhill like a landslide with opinions, and somewhere in the crush of it a crossbow discharged far too close, and the bolt took Thorn high in the side, punching through the quilted coat and out the back bloody.

Sorrel heard him make a sound she had never heard him make. Not a scream. Worse — a *surprise*. Twenty-six years of reading her brother, and she had never once heard him surprised by pain.

She killed the fifth man almost without seeing him. The sixth broke and ran for the camp, and she let him, because chasing him meant leaving Thorn, and there was no version of any world where she did that.

Her brother was sitting against a boulder with his hand pressed over the wound, and blood was coming up between his fingers in a slow pulse that matched hers, that had always matched hers, and his face was finally, terribly, expressive.

"Don't," he said, before she could speak. "Don't make the face."

"What face?"

"The one Ma made. When Da's leg—" He stopped. Breathed. The breath whistled a little. "It's a through-and-through. I've seen worse. I've *had* worse."

"You have not."

"I have absolutely had worse." But his hand was shaking, and the blood was not slowing, and when Sorrel peeled the coat back the entry wound was ugly and low, and she thought with a cold clarity: *lung, maybe. Maybe not. Maybe.* Above them the false dawn was guttering out over the north slope, and the camp's horns had gone quiet, and somewhere in the dark five crawlers were either clear of the picket line or not, and none of it mattered, none of it had ever mattered, next to the wet sound of her brother breathing.

"Sit forward," she said. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her. "Press here. I'm packing it."

"Sorrel."

"Packing it, Thorn."

He sat forward. She packed the wound with snow and strips of shirt and the pressure of both hands, and he gripped her wrist hard enough to bruise, and neither of them said a word, because everything that needed saying had always traveled in the spaces between.

* * *

They found them an hour later — Emrys first, materializing out of the treeline with that uncanny storm-caller's silence, then Sable, then Pip, whose face when she saw the blood went white and then set like mortar. The crawlers had made it through; the cut had delivered them past the stakes as promised; the plan had worked perfectly, except for the part where it hadn't.

Emrys knelt in the snow beside Thorn and pulled back the dressing, and Sorrel watched their face do the thing faces do when they're measuring a life. Then Emrys looked up, and their eyes found the amulet at their own throat — cracked, hairline-fractured, each use a memory paid out forever — and Sorrel saw the whole arithmetic happen behind their eyes. *Heal him, and lose something irreplaceable. Don't, and—*

"No," Thorn rasped. "Whatever you're pricing. No."

"It's not yours to refuse," Emrys said softly.

"It's my blood."

"And it's my choice." Emrys's hands hovered over the wound, trembling — not with fear, Sorrel realized, but with the effort of holding something enormous back, the way a person holds back a sneeze or a sob. "Twenty years I've hidden what I am and let people freeze around me. I'm done rationing myself. But—" and here their voice caught, honest as a wound, "—I don't get to choose what it takes. So if I stop mid-healing, if I forget why I'm kneeling here — someone tell me. Someone keep the count."

"I will," Pip said instantly. "I'm keeping the list anyway."

Emrys closed their eyes. The air above the wound shimmered, faintly, like heat over summer stone — the ghost of a storm called down small and careful — and Sorrel felt the pressure change in her ears, felt the fine hairs on her arms lift, and understood that she was watching something that had once split a sky open now threading itself delicately through her brother's torn flesh.

When it was done, Thorn breathed deep and whole, and the bleeding had stopped, and Emrys swayed on their knees with a look of vast, quiet loss — reaching for something in themselves and finding the shelf empty.

"What did it take?" Sable asked gently.

Emrys frowned, searching. "I don't know," they said. "That's the point, isn't it."

But Pip was already digging in her pack, pulling out a stub of pencil and the little journal she'd started keeping since Lastlight, writing fast, lips moving. *Keeping the count.* And Sorrel, kneeling in the trampled snow with her brother's blood on her hands and her sister-of-choice forgetting pieces of herself to buy pieces of her brother, understood at last what Sable had meant by the fire, what Emrys had meant by the price of passage, what the whole ragged company had been building without ever naming it.

They got Thorn up. He leaned on Sorrel's shoulder and complained about it, which was how she knew he'd live. South, Sable said — there was shelter in the high country, an old mine adit she'd mapped years ago, defensible, off every road. Snow was beginning again, fat and silent, filling their tracks behind them.

Halfway to the treeline, Thorn stopped. He was looking back at the pass, at the dead fires and the scattered tents, and his flat brown eyes had gone strange.

"Sorrel," he said. "The sixth one. The runner." He swallowed. "Before he broke, he shouted something back to the camp. I've been turning it over." His hand pressed briefly against the healed wound, as if checking it was real. "He shouted a name. *Ashgrove.* And then he said — 'the captain will want them alive.'"

Sorrel looked at Emrys, walking ahead through the falling snow with Pip's small hand tucked in theirs, and felt the night close around them like a hand.

"They're not hunting us anymore," she said quietly. "They're herding."

Nobody answered. The snow kept falling, and the mountain waited, and somewhere behind them the ward-bells hung silent in the dark, listening.
