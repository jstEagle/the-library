## 18. Snowbound Confessions

The storm found them an hour past the last waymarker, and by the time Sable spotted the black seam of a cave mouth in the cliffside, the wind had learned their names and was shouting them back at them like accusations.

Emrys went first, one hand on Pip's shoulder, the other raised against the white roar, calling—not lightning, not yet, just a thin thread of warmth pulled from the amulet's fading heart. The stone under their boots was slick with old ice. Inside, the cave opened wider than it had seemed from without: a hollow throat of granite going back thirty feet, its floor scattered with the dry droppings of mountain goats long gone, its ceiling hung with frost-ferns that caught the grey light and multiplied it.

"Fire," Pip said through chattering teeth. It wasn't a request so much as a prayer.

"Fire." Emrys knelt and pressed both palms to the ground. The warmth came grudgingly now—everything came grudgingly now—and when the little ring of heat bloomed beneath them, melting a circle into the frozen floor, Emrys felt the cost of it like a drawer sliding open somewhere behind their eyes. They reached instinctively for something to check the loss against. A name. A face. The color of—

*The color of what?* There had been something. A door in a house they'd once known, painted some color that mattered. They stood there for three full seconds, hand still pressed to the stone, groping for a color that was no longer there.

"Emrys?" Sable's voice, sharp as her drafting pens. "You've gone strange."

"I'm fine." The lie came easily after twenty years of practice. "Sit. Both of you. Before the cold gets into your bones and stays."

They sat. Pip curled small near the warmth-ring, knees to chest, her lamplighter's coat steaming where the snow melted off it. Sable remained upright, cross-legged with the rigid posture of someone who refused to be comfortable until she understood her surroundings, her map case hugged to her chest like a child. Outside, the storm screamed. Inside, the firelight made amber islands of their faces.

For a long while nobody spoke. There was only the wind's howl, the tick of ice expanding in cracks overhead, and the small wet sounds of Pip unwrapping the last of the Brambles' salt-pork.

It was Pip who broke first. She always broke first; it was one of the things Emrys loved about her, though they'd have died before saying so. "Do you think the twins are all right?"

"They held that pass against six Warden regulars," Sable said. "Brambles don't fall easy. And Thorn got the wound bound before we left—he'd have argued about the stitching otherwise, and he never argues about stitching."

"He argues about everything else," Pip said, and laughed, and the laugh cracked halfway through and became something else, wetter.

"Hey." Emrys shifted closer. "Alder and Thorn knew the risk. They chose it. That's what family does—it stands in doors you need walked through."

"You're not my family," Pip said, too fast, and then, miserable, "I mean—you are. That's what I mean. You are, and if the winter takes any of you I don't—" She pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes. "My mum used to say the mountain doesn't care who you love. It just takes."

"The mountain doesn't care," Emrys agreed quietly. "But people do. That's the whole trick of being alive, Pip. The mountain doesn't care, and we care anyway."

Sable snorted softly. It was, from Sable, nearly tenderness.

The fire settled. The storm outside rose to a pitch that rattled pebbles down the cave walls, and Emrys knew then—knew in the marrow-deep way of someone who had read storms since before they could write—that this was not a night's weather. This was a day's weather at minimum. Possibly two. They were sealed in, the three of them, with nothing but salt-pork, half a skin of watered wine, and each other.

And the truth, which Emrys had been carrying up this mountain like a stone in a pack, growing heavier with every mile.

Pip dozed first. She was sixteen and she had called her first lightning four nights ago and she had not stopped moving since, and her body simply collected her, mid-sentence, folding her down against her pack with her thumb hooked in the strap like a child holding a toy. Sable watched her sleep with an expression Emrys couldn't parse—something between exasperation and fierce protection.

Then Sable looked at Emrys, and her expression changed.

"You've been carrying something since the inn," she said. No preamble. Sable didn't believe in preambles; she believed maps should start where you were standing. "Since Marrow told you whatever Marrow told you. You've been walking around with your face shut like a ledger, and I've been waiting for you to open it, because I'm tired of navigating a person I can't read."

"Sable—"

"No. Listen." Her voice stayed low, pitched under the wind, careful of the sleeping girl. "I traced your ward-failure across four hundred miles of borderland. I mapped the Veil's wound before anyone believed it existed. I have spent eleven weeks in your company, Emrys Ashgrove—or whatever your name is, because I've known since the night market that *Ashgrove* is a coat you wear over something else—and I have asked you nothing. Nothing. Do you understand what restraint that has cost me? But we're sealed in a hole in a mountain, and tomorrow we climb to the Stones, and if you die up there carrying whatever it is you're carrying, I will be *furious*, because I'll have carried it too, for nothing."

The fire popped. Somewhere above them, ice groaned and resettled.

"What did Marrow tell you?" Sable asked.

Emrys looked at their hands. The hands hadn't changed much in twenty years—the same burn-scar across the left palm, earned the night everything ended—but the person looking at them felt like a stranger wearing familiar gloves.

"You said you've known since the night market," Emrys said. "That I'm not—" They stopped. Started again. "What gave me away?"

Sable's mouth curved, thin and dry. "Nobody sells charms that well and hates doing it that visibly. Your wards were flawless. Your smile was terrible. Also, when the stars went out, every soul in that market panicked except you. You looked at the sky like a man reading a letter he'd been dreading for years." She tilted her head. "So. Who are you?"

And Emrys, who had rehearsed a hundred evasions across twenty years of exile—who had built the charm-seller persona plank by plank like a dock over deep water—found that the planks had rotted through somewhere on the Salt Road, and there was nothing underneath but water, and the water was rising.

"My name is Emrys Ashgrove," they said. "That part's true. The rest is—" A breath. "The rest is a story I've never told anyone whole. Not once. Not even to myself, really. I've told it in pieces, the way you eat something bitter—one bite at a time so you can pretend you're not swallowing it."

"Then tell it whole," Sable said. "We've got a storm and a night. Use them."

---

Emrys fed the fire another thread of warmth—smaller this time, more careful, having learned the price—and began.

"I was born in Highmoor, in the hill country east of here, before the Wardens held it. My mother was a covenant-tender. Not a hedge-witch, not a storm-caller—a *tender*. One of the last people alive who still kept the old rites at the boundary markers, the ones that predated the Wardens' charter by a thousand years. She used to say magic wasn't a weapon or a tool. She said it was a *conversation*. You didn't take power from the world; you asked, and the world answered, and the asking changed you a little each time, and that was the point. That was the *price*, and it was a fair one."

Pip stirred in her sleep but didn't wake.

"There were two of us. Me and my brother. Tam." The name came out of Emrys's mouth like a tooth being pulled—clean, sudden, leaving a hollow. "Tam was older by two years and braver by ten. He was the one who climbed things. I was the one who stood at the bottom and called warnings up at him, and he always waved like the warnings were applause. When my mother taught us the rites, Tam learned the words and forgot them by supper. I learned them and couldn't stop hearing them. Mother said I had the deeper ear. She said the storm in me was loud but the listening was louder, and that someday the listening would save more lives than the storm ever endangered."

Emrys laughed, short and joyless. "She was wrong about that. Or right, depending on how the next few days go."

Sable said nothing. She had produced, at some point, one of her stub pencils and a scrap of paper, and Emrys realized she was mapping the story—literally drawing its shape, landmarks and distances, the way she did with terrain. It should have been infuriating. Instead it was a mercy. It meant Emrys could look at the paper instead of Sable's eyes.

"I was nineteen. It was midwinter, the kind of cold that makes wood smoke fall straight down. A storm came down off the high passes unlike anything in living memory—black-bellied, double-walled, the kind the old songs call a *widow-maker*. And it turned toward Highmoor. Toward the valley. Three hundred people. Two hundred of them children, if you counted the way villages count, everyone under fifteen."

"The ward-stones were dead. Mother had been warning the council for years—the old network was failing, the rites neglected, the Wardens' new charter forbade 'unlicensed workings' and drove every trained tender out or underground. So when the widow-maker came, there was no one. Except me. Nineteen years old, deeper ear than sense, and a valley full of people about to die."

"You called the storm," Sable said. Not a question.

"I *asked* it," Emrys said, and heard the old wound open in their own voice. "That's what nobody ever understood, what the tribunal wouldn't hear. You don't command a storm. You offer it something. A shape. A path. You say: *here is grief, here is fury, here is a valley of sleeping children—go around.* And the storm listens, or it doesn't. Ours listened. Mostly." Their hands had closed into fists without their noticing. They made themselves open them. "I turned it. I turned a widow-maker, alone, untrained past what my mother could teach me in a kitchen, and I turned it *away from the village*—and it came down on the shepherd's road instead. On the high pasture where the winter flocks were sheltering."

The fire hissed. Outside, the wind found a new register, lower, almost conversational, as though the mountain itself had leaned in to listen.

"Three shepherds died. And Tam." Emrys's voice did something strange on the name—flattened, guarded, a door pulled shut even as it opened. "He'd gone up that morning to help bring the flocks down. He was supposed to be back by noon. I turned the storm at midday. I killed my brother with the same breath I used to save two hundred children, and I have done the arithmetic of that every single day for twenty years, and it has never once come out clean."

Silence. Even the storm seemed to hold itself.

"The tribunal convened within the week. The Wardens had just consolidated the hill country; Corvess's predecessor wanted a spectacle, a demonstration of what happened to unlicensed casters. They put me in irons in front of the whole valley. And Tam—" Emrys stopped. Pressed the heels of their hands against their eyes. "Tam testified. That's what I believed for twenty years. That my brother, dying or dead—I never saw the body, they buried the shepherds fast in frozen ground and Tam with them—had somehow spoken against me. Because the tribunal record said a witness named T. Ashgrove confirmed the accused had acted recklessly, willfully, *knowing the flocks were on the road*. Knowing. As if I'd chosen. As if I'd looked at that storm and thought, *yes, the sheep-road, that's the spot.*"

"But Marrow told you different," Sable said.

"Marrow was there." Emrys lowered their hands. Their eyes were wet and they let them be; there was no longer any percentage in hiding it, and something in them had passed, on this mountain, beyond shame's jurisdiction. "Marrow was a young Warden-lieutenant assigned to the tribunal. He watched the testimony get taken. And he told me, in that inn at Lastlight, that the record was forged. That Tam survived the storm—barely, half-frozen, brought down off the pasture two days later—and that the tribunal needed a witness statement from the family to seal the verdict, and when Tam refused to condemn me, they took it anyway. They wrote what they needed and put his mark on it. He was nineteen—no, twenty-one, he was twenty-one, concussed, snow-blind, half-dead—and they used him. And I spent twenty years hating a dead boy for a lie that men in grey coats wrote with his frozen hand held to the page."

Emrys's voice finally broke. Not dramatically—quietly, the way ice breaks, along lines that had been forming for years.

"I built a whole life on that lie, Sable. I built the exile on it. I built the silence on it. Every rule I ever made—don't heal, don't help, don't be seen, don't use the gift for anything bigger than a luck-charm—I built it on the belief that my gift had killed my brother and that using it again would kill someone else. Twenty years of making myself small enough to fit inside that belief. And it was *forged*. It was ink and cruelty and a bureaucracy that needed a monster, and I volunteered. I sentenced myself harder than the tribunal did."

"That's why the healing of the frostbitten child nearly undid you," Sable said. Not unkindly. "In Greyhollow. The morning after the stars went out."

"That's why everything undid me." Emrys wiped their face with the heel of one hand, rough, impatient. "Every kindness I let myself feel was a betrayal of a dead boy. And now he's not dead. Now he's—" A sound that was half laugh, half sob. "Now he's Corvess's warden-captain, if the Brambles' intelligence holds, riding at the right hand of the woman who wants my blood to renew her stolen power. My brother is alive and he is the enemy's sword arm, and I don't know which of those facts breaks me more."

Pip was awake.

Emrys knew it before they turned—knew it by the quality of the silence behind them, the held-breath stillness of a listener trying not to exist. When they did turn, she was sitting up, firelight on her tear-tracked face, and she didn't apologize for eavesdropping and Emrys didn't ask her to.

"You saved two hundred children," Pip said.

"I killed four people."

"You were *nineteen*." Pip's voice cracked across the cave like a whip-crack, indignant on Emrys's behalf, fierce as her first lightning. "You were nineteen and you turned a widow-maker *alone* and it worked, it mostly worked, and they took your brother's name and wrote lies with it and you punished yourself for twenty years for a crime somebody else committed. That's not arithmetic, Emrys. That's just cruelty. You were cruel to yourself the way the Wardens are cruel to everyone, and you let them win twice."

"Pip—"

"No!" She was crying openly now, furious and weeping at once, sixteen years old and absolutely certain in the way only the young and the grieving can be certain. "You healed my feet the second night out, when the blisters went bad and I was too proud to say. You taught me the listening even though it costs you memories every time you call anything bigger than a candle-flame. You're giving up pieces of yourself *right now*, every mile up this mountain, and you're still— you keep *choosing* us. So don't sit there and tell me what you are is a killer. I know what you are. I've been watching you be it for weeks."

The fire burned low. Emrys looked at this furious, fearless child who had decided, without consultation, that Emrys Ashgrove was worth defending—and felt something shift in their chest that had been locked for two decades. Not healing. Healing was too clean a word. More like a door that had rusted shut swinging loose on its hinge, groaning, letting in a draft of air that tasted of a world larger than guilt.

"Sable," Emrys said hoarsely. "You asked who I am. That's who I am. A storm-caller who stopped calling storms. A sister who mourned a brother she was told betrayed her, and who now has to climb a mountain and face him alive in the service of the woman who forged his testimony. And in two days, maybe less, I'm going to stand in front of Tam Ashgrove, and I don't know if I'm going to fight him or beg his forgiveness or simply stand there like a fool and let him strike me down, because every version of that conversation ends with me deserving whatever he chooses to give."

"No," Sable said.

Emrys blinked. "No?"

"No." Sable set down her pencil. In the firelight her eyes were very clear, very grey, the eyes of a woman who dealt only in true bearings. "You don't deserve whatever he chooses. That's the whole lesson of your own story and you still haven't learned it. You were a child who did an impossible thing badly because there was no good option, and liars profited from it, and you've paid their debt for twenty years. The account is closed, Emrys. What happens with Tam is a *new* account. And you don't walk into it already conceding." She picked her pencil back up. "I map roads. That one's a dead end. Take another."

Emrys stared at her. Then, helplessly, they laughed—a real laugh, wrecked and wet and astonished, the first in twenty years that didn't taste of ash.

Outside, the storm raged on, but inside the cave the fire held, and Pip crept close and put her head against Emrys's shoulder the way she must once have put it against her mother's, and Sable pretended to study her maps, and none of them slept much, and none of them minded.

Near dawn, the wind changed. Emrys felt it before they heard it—a subtle easing in the pressure, the storm's great shoulder turning away—and sat up, alert, listening with the deep ear their mother had promised would one day save more lives than the storm ever endangered.

Because beneath the wind's retreat, faint but unmistakable, rolling up the mountain passes from the valley below:

Bells. Warden bells. And the measured, distant tread of many boots on frozen stone.

Corvess had found the mountain.
