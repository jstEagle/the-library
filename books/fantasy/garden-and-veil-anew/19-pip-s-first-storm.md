## 19. Pip's First Storm

Pip had counted the ways a person could die on this mountain eleven times since dawn, and she was fairly sure she'd missed a few.

There was cold, obviously — the slow kind that started as a friend and ended as a stranger wearing your body. There was falling, because the pass Sable had mapped wound along shelves of black rock where the snow lay like a lie over empty air. There was the Veil-sickness itself, which had already started picking at the edges of her mind; she'd woken that morning reaching for her mother's face and found only the shape of it, warm and gone, like a window breathed upon. And now, as the light went thin and violet between the peaks, there were the eyes.

They'd been following them for an hour.

She'd noticed first because lamplighters notice light — or the lack of it. Way back down the trail, in the blue shadow under an outcrop, two small green coins had blinked open, then shut. Then another pair. Then three more, spaced with terrible patience, like lamps being lit one by one along a street she very much did not want to walk.

"Pip." Emrys's voice came low from ahead, where they stood with their boots planted in the drifts, head tilted the way they always did when they listened to something no one else could hear. "How many?"

"Seven," Pip said. "Maybe eight. They're not hunting us."

Sable snorted, breath steaming. "That's a comfort."

"They're *herding* us," Pip said, and hated how steady her voice came out, because inside she was all rattling windows and blown candles. "Off the trail. Down toward the hollow by the frozen falls. That's where they want us to go."

Emrys looked at her then — really looked, the way they'd started doing since the cave, since the night of snowbound confessions when Pip had learned that the charm-seller with the careful hands had once been someone who could split the sky open, and that the sky had taken payment in blood. Something moved behind their eyes that Pip couldn't name yet. She'd know its name soon enough.

"You've been watching how I listen," Emrys said.

"Since the inn," Pip admitted. "You tilt your head left for wind-borne things and right for ground things. You breathe through your mouth when you're counting. You count everything twice."

A ghost of a smile crossed Emrys's chapped lips. "And how many times have you counted the wolves?"

"Three times each. Eight total. One's limping on the left forepaw — the big one, the leader. It's hungry enough to be desperate, which means the rest are hungrier than that."

The silence that followed had weight. Sable glanced between them, map forgotten in her mittened hands, and Pip saw the cartographer do the thing she did when a route turned out to be real: a small, fierce satisfaction, quickly buried.

"Well," Sable said. "It seems my apprentice problem has become an apprenticeship."

"I'm not—" Pip started.

"*Later*," said Emrys, and the word cracked like ice shifting on a lake. Because the green coins had stopped blinking. All eight pairs were open now, and closing.

* * *

The wolves came off the rocks the way avalanches come: quietly at first, a hiss of displaced snow, and then all at once, everywhere.

Pip would remember it afterward in pieces, the way broken things are remembered. The gray blur of the first wolf off the ledge above. Sable shouting and swinging her pack like a flail, maps scattering into the wind like dying birds. Emrys stepping in front of her with both arms spread wide, storm-glass amulet bared against their chest — and the amulet *flaring*, light crawling through its cracks like lightning trapped in dirty ice, and Emrys crying out and staggering, because every calling cost them now, cost them memories they could never get back, and Pip watched them pay that price without hesitation and thought: *they'd forget me. They're willing to forget me.*

No. Not that. She'd think about that later, or never. Right now—

Right now the lead wolf hit the snow ten feet away and turned, and up close it wasn't a monster, it was just hunger with ribs, gray and gaunt and shivering, and that was somehow worse. Its pack fanned out around them in a crescent, eight shapes in the failing light, and the limping one made a sound low in its chest that wasn't quite a growl. It sounded almost like an apology.

"They're starving," Pip heard herself say. "Emrys, they're starving, the winter's eaten everything—"

"I know." Emrys's voice was tight. Their hand hovered over the amulet and didn't close around it. "If I call, it costs me. If I don't—"

The crescent tightened. Snow sprayed. Somewhere behind them, the drop toward the frozen falls yawned, blue-black and waiting, exactly where the wolves had wanted them all along.

And Pip — Pip who had spent her whole life tending other people's flames, lighting other people's streets, keeping other people's houses warm while her mother coughed in the dark — Pip stepped forward.

"No," Emrys said.

"Yes," said Pip.

"Pip, you've called *sparks*. Candle-flames. You froze a teakettle once and we don't speak of the teakettle—"

"And you'll forget your brother's face if you call!" It burst out of her louder than she meant, loud enough that the nearest wolf flattened its ears. "I *heard* what it costs, you told us in the cave, every time you use it you lose something you can't buy back, and you were going to do it anyway, right now, for us, without even—" Her voice broke. She let it. "So it's my turn. Teach me. *Now.*"

The wolves were five feet away. Four.

Emrys looked at her — and Pip saw the exact moment the teacher became something else. Not a partner yet. But the door to it, opening.

"Storm doesn't come from anger," Emrys said, fast and low, words tumbling like the ones they'd used in the cave, in the inn, all the places truth had leaked out of them lately. "Everyone thinks it does. It comes from *wanting the sky to answer*. From asking so hard the air can't refuse. Feel the charge first — it's already there, Pip, it's always there, in the dry cold, in your own blood. Find it. Don't grab it. *Invite* it."

"That's not instructions, that's poetry—"

"Three seconds, Pip!"

The lead wolf lunged.

Pip threw up her hands like she was pushing open a door in a gale, closed her eyes, and asked.

* * *

Here is what she had expected: fire. Power. The clean hot roar of a thing answering.

Here is what actually happened: nothing, for one endless heartbeat, and in that heartbeat she felt utterly naked, a girl standing in the snow with her arms up, about to die stupidly far from home with her mother's face half-forgotten — and the grief of that, the raw *unfairness* of it, rose in her like floodwater.

*Wanting the sky to answer.*

Oh, she thought. *Oh.* It was never about the technique at all.

The world went white.

Not light — *sound*, first, a crack so vast it wasn't a sound anymore but a change in what sound was, and then the light arrived late and sideways, a fork of brilliance leaping from the low clouds straight down through the falling snow to the point where Pip's raised hands met the air. It didn't strike the wolves. It struck *between* them — a tree-trunk of lightning splitting the crescent in two, slamming into the packed snow with a thunderclap that rolled back and forth between the canyon walls like the mountain itself clearing its throat.

Snow vaporized into steam. The whole slope lit blue-white. For one suspended instant Pip saw everything perfectly: the wolves mid-leap, transformed into statues of terror; Sable flat on her back with her mouth open; Emrys silhouetted against the glare, and on their face —

On their face was pride. Undiluted, unguarded, blazing pride, the kind Pip had spent sixteen years waiting to see aimed at her, and it was aimed at her, and she was crying, she realized, though her eyes weren't cold at all, couldn't be cold, because heat was pouring off her skin in waves and the snow within six feet of her had melted to bare wet stone.

Then the thunder finished rolling, and the wolves ran.

All eight of them, even the limping leader, even the desperate ones, fleeing downslope with their tails low, and Pip understood dimly that they hadn't been driven off by fear of death. Wolves knew death. They fled because they had seen something older than death, something that belonged to the sky, standing in a girl's body with her arms still raised and steam rising off her shoulders.

Her knees buckled. Emrys caught her before the ground did.

"I did it," Pip said. Her voice came out tiny and enormous at once. "I did it, I actually — Emrys, I did *it*—"

"You did it." Emrys was laughing, or maybe crying too, or maybe both; it was hard to tell in the wind. "First try. Do you know how long it took me? Do you know what I *destroyed* learning that?" They pulled back to arm's length, gripping her shoulders, and their expression shifted into something stern that fooled neither of them. "Never alone again. Never without me beside you. Promise me. The storm chooses people, Pip, and once it chooses you, you don't get to be careless with yourself. That's the bargain. That's the *whole* bargain."

"I promise," Pip said, and meant it, mostly, in the way of sixteen-year-olds meaning things — completely now, renegotiable later.

Sable picked herself up out of the snowdrift, retrieved two of her scattered maps, glared at the third as it sailed over the edge toward the frozen falls, and stalked over.

"For the record," she said, brushing snow off with tremendous dignity, "I had a plan."

"You were on your back," said Pip.

"I was *assessing terrain*." But Sable's eyes were bright, and she looked at Pip the way she looked at a passage through mountains that shouldn't exist but did. "Eight wolves. First calling. Untrained." She shook her head slowly. "Your mother's going to want the full story. Every detail. Twice."

The mention landed like a stone dropped into still water. Pip's triumph dimmed at the edges. Her mother, three days' travel behind them, coughing in a room Marrow had paid for with money he claimed he didn't miss. Her mother, whose face Pip could no longer fully see behind her eyelids. Her mother, who might be forgetting Pip right back, in some sick symmetry of the dying Veil, and wouldn't that be a mercy, some cruel part of her whispered, and she hated that part and kept it anyway, because it was hers.

"We keep moving," Emrys said quietly. "Light's nearly gone. There's shelter past the saddle — Sable?"

"Old waystation, if my map's honest and the mountain hasn't eaten it." Sable squinted at the remaining charts. "Beyond that, the valley opens. Three days, maybe four, and we're looking down on Veyra's capital."

Nobody said anything for a moment. They all carried the same picture of it: the abandoned city from Sable's research, the monuments to stolen glory, the citizens wandering memoryless among marble. The place where the founder's lie had been carved in stone so large no one had thought to read it.

"The wolves will tell the mountain we're here," Pip said. She was surprised by her own voice — steadier now, deeper somehow, like a bell recast. "Word travels. If Corvess has ears in these valleys—"

"She does," Emrys said. "She always did. My mother warned me about the mountain listening, twenty years ago, and I laughed at her." A pause. "I'm doing that less lately."

They climbed. The saddle path switchbacked upward through deepening dusk, and Pip walked in the middle, between Sable's practical grumbling and Emrys's watchful quiet, and felt the new thing sitting in her chest like a second heartbeat. Not sparks this time. Not candle-tending. A storm, sleeping, hers.

Once — only once — she caught herself tilting her head to the left, listening for wind-borne things, and found she could hear them: the creak of the glacier, the mutter of the wind over the crags, and far, far below and behind, faint as a memory of a memory, the long slow breathing of weather coming down out of the north. Weather that was not natural. Weather that moved with purpose, toward the Stones, toward them.

She didn't say anything yet. She was newly chosen; she was allowed one secret.

But at the top of the saddle, with the first stars pricking through — *real* stars, returned stars, the covenant holding, thank all the small gods — Pip stopped and looked back the way they'd come, down the darkening pass toward the south and the world beyond it.

Lights. Many lights. Moving in formation through the valley they'd have to cross tomorrow, orderly as a rosary, patient as wolves.

"Sable," she said. "Your waystation. How solid are its walls?"

Sable followed her gaze. So did Emrys. The silence stretched, filled only by wind.

"Those aren't villagers," Sable said at last. "Villagers don't march in columns."

Emrys's hand drifted to the cracked amulet at their throat, and Pip saw them weigh it — saw them calculate, in the space of a breath, exactly how much of themselves each flash of it would cost, and begin spending anyway.

"Waystation," Emrys said. "*Now.* And Pip — whatever comes over that ridge tomorrow—"

"I know," said Pip, and felt the storm turn over in her chest, waking. "Ask the sky nicely."
