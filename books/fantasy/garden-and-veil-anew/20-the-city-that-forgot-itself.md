## 20. The City That Forgot Itself

Sable Vane had mapped a great many ruins in her life—collapsed watchtowers, drowned villages, the bone-yards of old battlefields—but she had never mapped a city that was still breathing.

They crested the ridge an hour past dawn, and there it was below them: Veyrastad, the capital of the founder's dream, spread across the valley like a silver coin dropped in white fur. Even from this distance Sable could read it the way she read all cities, by its bones—the great axial road running spine-straight from the southern gate to the citadel on its hill, the concentric rings of avenues like ripples in frozen water, the monuments rising at every intersection, tall as trees and pale as teeth. It had been built to be seen from above. Built to be *read*. Every line of it said: *look what Aldric Veyra made.*

"Is anyone living there?" Pip asked, huddled into her borrowed coat, breath steaming.

Sable raised her glass—a cartographer's spyglass, brass and honest—and swept the streets. People moved through them. That was the strange part. Not corpses, not ghosts. People. A woman sweeping a step that did not need sweeping. A man standing at a fountain, hands in his pockets, watching water fall. Children—gods, children—walking in slow circuits around a monument with their faces tipped up toward its stone face.

"There are people," she said, and heard how her voice flattened. "Hundreds, maybe."

Emrys took the glass from her without asking. Their fingers brushed; Sable pretended not to notice that she noticed. Through the lens Emrys went very still, the way they always went still before saying something true.

"They're not going anywhere," Emrys said quietly. "Look at their feet. They're not going anywhere at all."

* * *

The Bramble twins had left them at the tree line two days past, Ferren's arm bound and splinted, both of them insisting with identical grins that a checkpoint ambush was hardly worth missing a festival over. So it was only the three of them now—Sable, Emrys, and Pip—who came down out of the hills as the light thickened, following the great road through orchards heavy with frost-killed fruit no one had picked.

The gate stood open. Of course it stood open. Gates were for keeping things out, and nothing had come here for twenty years except snow.

Inside, the silence was the worst thing. Sable had expected ruin—smoke, scavengers, the honest decay of abandonment. Instead the city was *kept*. Streets swept. Windows unbroken. Lamps trimmed and ready though none were lit. It was as if the whole place had been paused mid-breath, a held note that no one remembered starting.

And the people.

They passed a baker's row where ovens sat cold and a man in a flour-dusted apron stood in his doorway, looking down the street with an expression of mild, patient attention, like a man waiting for a customer who would never come. He did not look at them. He looked *through* them, or rather through everything, his gaze resting on the middle distance the way Sable's rested on a map when she was thinking.

"Sir?" Pip called softly. She was already moving toward him, because Pip moved toward every wound in the world like a moth with a hero complex. "Sir, are you all right?"

He turned to her. He smiled. And Sable felt her stomach drop, because the smile was warm and whole and utterly empty—a smile with no one behind it, a lamp lit in a house where the furniture had been carried out years ago.

"Good morning," he said pleasantly. "Lovely day."

It was not a lovely day. It was grey and bitter and the sky had been starless for weeks. But he said it with such serene conviction that Pip actually glanced upward, half-hoping.

"What's your name?" she tried.

He considered this. Sable watched him consider it—watched something move behind his eyes like a fish under ice, circling, finding no hole to rise through.

"I'm sure I knew once," he said, still pleasant. "It'll come back to me." And he turned back to his doorway, to his patient vigil, content as a man who has mislaid a glove.

Pip came back to them white-faced. "His eyes," she whispered. "There wasn't anything wrong with his eyes. That's what got me. He looked *healthy*."

"He is healthy," Sable said. Her voice sounded far away to herself, the way it did when she was working a hard problem. "That's the horror of it. Whatever took them didn't touch the body. It went in after something else."

"The Veil-sickness," Emrys said. They had stopped walking. They were staring up—at the monuments now, close enough to see them properly, and Sable followed their gaze and understood why Emrys had gone quiet.

Each monument was a figure in stone, thirty feet tall, rendered in that self-important heroic style that Sable privately thought of as *look-at-me architecture*. Aldric Veyra appeared again and again: Veyra with a raised hand, Veyra with a codex under his arm, Veyra crowned in stylized flames, Veyra holding a thread of stone between his fingers like a seamstress holding silk. At the base of each statue ran inscriptions, deep-cut and gilded, and Sable walked to the nearest one and read it aloud because someone had to:

*HE GAVE US ETERNITY. HE ASKED NOTHING IN RETURN.*

She laughed. It came out wrong, cracked in the middle. "Asked nothing," she repeated. "Emrys. Look at this city. Look at what he *asked*."

* * *

They found the archive of names in what had clearly been a census hall, its doors standing open onto a rotunda lined floor-to-ceiling with ledgers. Sable could not help herself; she was inside before the others finished arguing about whether to go in, her fingers already walking the spines. Census records. Birth rolls. Tax lists. Twenty years of meticulous bureaucratic memory, ending—she pulled volume after volume, checking dates—ending abruptly, nineteen years ago, mid-year, mid-entry. The last ledger simply stopped. Halfway down a page, a clerk's neat hand recording the birth of a girl named Odile Ferrance, and then nothing. No more entries ever. As if the whole city had set down its pen to answer a knock at the door.

"It happened here first," Emrys said from the doorway. They hadn't come in. They stood with one hand pressed flat against the doorframe, and Sable realized they were steadying themselves. "This is where he wove them in. His own people. The first threads he stole went into the ground under his own capital."

"You can't know that."

"I can feel it." Emrys's hand curled against the wood. "Can't you? The whole city is a wound dressed up as a garden. He took their memories and he—" they gestured vaguely outward, at the swept streets, the tended lamps, the smiling man at his door— "he left the shapes of them. Like pressing flowers. You keep the form and lose the living part."

Sable wanted to argue. It was her nature to argue; a map that agreed with you too easily was a map that hadn't been surveyed properly. But she had been reading the ledgers, and the ledgers told a story she couldn't refute. She'd found the maintenance rolls. For nineteen years, some authority—Warden garrisons, presumably, or whatever passed for governance here—had paid for the city's upkeep. Sweeping. Lamp oil. Grave-tending. Line item after line item for the care of a population that produced nothing, bought nothing, needed nothing but tending. The last entries were four months old. Then those stopped too.

"Someone was paying to keep them comfortable," she said slowly. "Or keeping up appearances. And even that's ended."

"Corvess doesn't need appearances anymore," Emrys said. "She thinks she's about to win."

* * *

They made camp that night in a caravanserai near the central square, a high-walled inn whose stable roof still held, and Sable climbed to its flat top while the others slept—or pretended to sleep; she'd heard Emrys talking softly with Pip well past midnight, the low murmur of a teacher and a student, and had pretended not to listen, which was its own kind of lie she was getting practiced at.

From the roof, the city lay open under the dead sky, and Sable did what she always did: she drew it. Charcoal on the back of a supply list, quick and sure, the avenues radiating, the monuments pinned like nails. But halfway through, her hand slowed.

Because she had finally understood what the map was telling her.

The monuments weren't scattered. They formed a pattern. Eight figures, eight intersections, arranged in a ring around the central plaza—and at the center of the plaza, visible even from here as a pale column against the dark, stood a ninth structure, taller than the rest, that she had taken for another statue. It wasn't. Through her glass she could see it was a pillar of fused stone, twisted like a candle burned down in layers, and around its base the memoryless citizens had gathered in their dozens, standing in the cold, faces upturned, motionless as the man in the doorway. Waiting. Worshipping, maybe. Or maybe just drawn, the way water finds a low place, to the one hollow deepest than themselves.

Eight points and a center. She'd seen that geometry before—in the sketches Marrow had given them of the Covenant Stones, before the sundering. A lattice. A binding diagram the size of a city.

"Oh, you vain, monstrous old man," she breathed. "You built your theft into the streets."

"What theft?" Pip's voice, below and behind her. Sable hadn't heard her climb up. The girl settled beside her, pulling the coat tighter, and looked out at the plaza with her young-old face. "What are we looking at?"

"A confession," Sable said. "Written so large nobody read it." She tapped the charcoal ring. "Veyra didn't just steal the realm-memories, Pip. He anchored them here. Wove them into the city itself—into stone and street and monument—so the power would stay in his bloodline's reach. These people aren't sick. They're *harvested*. Their memories are the mortar. Every one of these statues is probably threaded through with thousands of lives."

Pip was quiet a long moment. Below them, in the plaza, the crowd stood in the freezing dark, patient as furniture.

"So if we break the statues," Pip said, "or the pillar—do they get their memories back?"

Sable opened her mouth. Closed it. Because the honest answer was that she didn't know, and the more-than-honest answer was worse: she suspected not. Threads cut from a tapestry didn't leap back into the cloth. What had been held for twenty years in dead stone might be beyond any weaving, even Emrys's—even the Stones' own. Some thefts were simply permanent. That was the arithmetic of it, and she had never yet found a map that could route around arithmetic.

"I don't know," she said, because Pip deserved honesty more than comfort. "But I know someone who might be able to find out. And I know that Corvess is riding here eventually, because this is the vault of everything she's heir to. If we're going to understand what she holds—and what we can take back from her—it's through that pillar."

"And Emrys?" Pip said quietly. "Have you told them?"

"Told them what?"

Pip turned and looked at her, and in the starless dark her eyes were very bright. "That they're going to stand in the middle of all this and feel responsible. That they're going to want to fix every single person down there, right now, tonight, even if it kills them. Have you told them that you see it coming?"

Sable looked back at the plaza. Somewhere down there a woman was humming to herself, tunelessly, endlessly, a song with no melody left in it.

"No," she said. "But I will. Before it breaks them." She hesitated, then added, because the night made her brave: "That's what I'm for, I think. Seeing the shape of the route. Including the parts people don't want on the map."

Pip bumped her shoulder, companionably. "Emrys says you pretend you're only in this for the surveying."

"Emrys talks too much."

But she stayed on the roof long after Pip went down, drawing by feel now, adding the last lines of the pattern, and when she finally slept she dreamed of a city full of doors standing open, and behind every door a room swept clean, and in every room a lamp burning for no one, and her own name being called from somewhere she could not find on any map she'd ever drawn.

* * *

They left at first light, taking the north road—the road to the Stones, the road that climbed. But at the edge of the plaza Sable stopped, because Emrys had stopped, standing before the tallest monument of all: Veyra robed and crowned, one hand extended in eternal benediction, and beneath his feet, carved small among the grandiose dedications, a single line Sable had missed the night before:

*I REMEMBER FOR YOU.*

Emrys reached up and laid their palm against the stone. For a moment nothing. Then frost bloomed outward from their fingers—not the random lace of cold, but letters, deliberate, forming along the statue's base in Emrys's own childhood hand, the hand the Veil had been writing to them in since Greyhollow:

*NOT ALL WAS TAKEN. SOME IS HIDDEN. COME AND SEE.*

Sable read it twice, her heart doing something complicated and unwelcome in her chest. Hope, probably. She'd forgotten the symptoms.

"Well," she said, when Emrys turned to her with an expression she'd never seen on them before—wonder and terror sharing a face. "Looks like the old thief kept a few things in a drawer after all." She shouldered her pack and nodded up the north road, where the mountains waited, white and vast and patient. "Let's go make him tell us where."
