## 21. Corvess Offers a Bargain

They came down out of the Veyran capital at dusk, and the dusk was wrong.

Emrys had grown used to wrongness in the last weeks—the colorless fields, the frost that spelled names, the silence of hollow villages—but this was a different order of wrong. The light itself seemed to be leaving the world the way water leaves a cracked bowl: slowly, then all at once. Behind them, the capital's monuments stood pale as bone against a sky the color of old pewter, and ahead, the road to the Covenant Stones climbed into hills where no birds sang and no smoke rose from any chimney.

"We should make the ridge by midnight," Sable said, unrolling her map across a fallen milestone without stopping her stride. She'd taken to walking with the map half-open, one hand always on it, as if it were a railing on a ship in rough seas. "There's a shepherd's bothy past the second cairn. Stone walls. Defensible."

"Defensible against what?" Pip asked. She was walking close to Emrys tonight, closer than usual, and Emrys knew why. Pip had woken twice in the night reaching for her mother's face and finding only a warm blankness where it should have been. She hadn't said so. She didn't have to. Emrys could feel it coming off her like heat off a hearthstone.

"Against whatever Corvess sends," Sable said flatly.

"She won't send soldiers," Emrys said quietly.

Sable looked up. In the failing light her eyes were sharp as awls. "You know something."

"I know her." Emrys pulled their cloak tighter. The cold had teeth now; it bit through wool and charm-warding alike. "Twenty years ago I thought I knew everything about the Wardens from the outside. But Corvess—" They stopped. Started again. "She doesn't waste tools. If she wanted us dead, we'd be dead. She wants something else."

"What could she possibly want that she can't take?"

Emrys looked at the mountains. Somewhere up there, under centuries of ice and grief, the Covenant Stones waited—shattered, memoryless, humming with the ghost of their mother's work. And somewhere behind them, an army moved through the ashline with a warden-captain at its head who wore Tam Ashgrove's face.

"She wants me willing," Emrys said. "Or she wants me to believe I'm willing. There's a difference, and it matters."

* * *

The trap sprang at the bothy.

It was a good place, as Sable had promised—low stone walls, a turf roof furred with frost, a single door that opened onto a single room with a hearth long gone cold. The Brambles would have laughed at it; Marrow would have called it honest. Emrys built a fire with a whisper of calling, just enough warmth to thaw their fingers, and hated themselves for the small taste of memory it cost—a summer afternoon, gone now, traded for heat. They were learning to spend carefully. They were learning to be poor.

Pip slept first, curled against the wall with her lamplighter's pole within arm's reach even here, even now. Sable sat sharpening her cartographer's knife by firelight, though they all knew the knife was for maps, not men. And Emrys sat by the door, because Emrys always sat by the door, because twenty years of exile teaches you that doors are where the world comes in.

The knock, when it came, was almost gentle.

Three taps. Even. Patient.

Sable was on her feet before the third tap finished, knife reversed in her grip, and Emrys raised a hand—*wait*—because they already knew. You cannot ward a door against someone who has already decided to enter, and some presences announce themselves not through sound but through pressure, like the stillness before lightning. The air in the bothy had gone taut. Every hair on Emrys's arms stood up beneath their sleeves.

"Open it," Emrys said. "She's alone."

"You can't know that."

"I can." Emrys stood. Their legs felt like borrowed things. "That's what frightens me."

They lifted the latch.

High Warden Corvess stood in the snow with her hood down, letting the flakes settle on her bare grey hair like a crown she couldn't feel. No guards ringed the bothy—but Emrys felt them anyway, a constellation of lanterns and steel scattered down the slope, patient as stars. She had brought the army after all. She had simply chosen to arrive as a single woman asking admittance, which was somehow worse.

"You look tired, Ashgrove," she said. Her voice was low and even, the voice of someone who had never once needed to raise it. "May I come in? It's cold enough to crack stone, and I've walked a long way to say something you'll want to hear sitting down."

"Say it standing."

Something flickered across her face—not anger. Respect, maybe, or its counterfeit. "Standing, then."

Emrys stepped back just far enough. It was not hospitality. It was arithmetic: better her inside these walls where Emrys could see her than outside them where she could see everything.

Corvess entered the way water enters a hull—quietly, completely, changing the nature of the vessel. Her eyes went to Sable, to the sleeping shape of Pip, to the dead hearth, cataloguing everything with the unhurried competence of a woman reading a ledger she had written herself. Then they settled on Emrys, and there was something in them Emrys had not expected.

Weariness. Genuine, bone-deep weariness, worn as openly as a scar.

"You've seen the capital," she said. It wasn't a question. "Then you've seen what my family built. Monuments to memories we don't have. A dynasty of thieves who stole so much they forgot what they stole it *for*." She removed her gloves, finger by finger. "I found my great-great-grandfather's private archive when I was nineteen. Aldric Veyra's own hand. Do you know what he wrote at the end of his life, alone in that palace, with all the realm-memory of two worlds woven into his blood?"

"No," Emrys said. "But I think you're going to tell me."

"*I have everything,*" she quoted, "*and I remember nothing.*" She folded the gloves away. "He went mad, of course. They all did. Every heir since. We inherit power built on stolen recollection, and stolen things don't sit quiet in a person. They squirm. They *hollow*. I have spent my whole life feeling other people's memories rot inside me like fruit in a sealed jar, and I am *tired*, storm-caller. I am so tired."

Pip stirred in her sleep. Corvess glanced at her, and Emrys saw the calculation move behind her eyes like a fish under ice—and saw, too, the moment she chose not to act on it. That choice frightened Emrys more than any threat could have.

"So here is my offer," Corvess said. "Not a threat. An offer. Help me reforge the covenant at the Stones—but reforged under structure. Under law. Under Wardens. Memory given freely, yes, but *held* freely too, governed, stewarded, so that no Aldric Veyra can ever strip-mine it again. A covenant with teeth. With custodians." She spread her hands. "And in exchange—"

"Don't," Emrys whispered.

"—your brother lives."

The words landed in the room like a dropped anvil. By the fire, Sable had gone very still. Outside, the wind worried the eaves.

"He's alive now," Emrys said. Each word cost effort, as if spoken through deep water. "He's your captain. He marched with you."

"He marched with me because I let him, and he serves me because he believes his false testimony bought your survival twenty years ago—which, incidentally, it did, though he doesn't know I know that either." Corvess's voice remained maddeningly level. "But the covenant is dying, Ashgrove. When it dies fully, the Veil goes, and everything touched by realm-magic unravels—including every thread of stolen memory my bloodline ever wove into itself. Including the thread that holds a certain warden-captain's life together." She tilted her head. "You've felt it, haven't you? How the winter eats minds first. Hollow Bells wasn't an accident. It was a preview. Tam stands closer to that lattice than anyone alive. When the Stones fail, he will not die quickly. He will simply stop having ever been."

Emrys's knees nearly buckled. They caught themselves on the doorframe, wood cold and real beneath their palm, and hated how much they needed it to be real.

"You're lying."

"I'm many things, but I have never once needed to lie to people who could check." Corvess picked a fleck of frost from her sleeve. "Refuse me, walk up that mountain, reforge the covenant wild and free and ungoverned—and yes, perhaps the stars relight, perhaps the Veil heals. And Tam Ashgrove, whose existence is currently propped up by threads my ancestor stole, becomes collateral damage of your beautiful freedom. Or accept my terms, and I divert lattice-current around him while the reforging happens. He lives. He remembers. He even gets to hate you properly, which I suspect he'd prefer."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I grieve a good captain," Corvess said simply, "and you carry it forever. Again."

* * *

The silence afterward lasted a hundred years or a heartbeat; Emrys couldn't have said which.

They became aware, distantly, that Sable had moved—had placed herself between the hearth and the High Warden, knife held low, body angled in the particular way of someone preparing to do something stupid and necessary. They became aware that Pip's breathing had changed, that she was awake behind closed eyes, listening, her small fists clenched in her sleep like a child fighting a nightmare she hadn't consented to.

And Corvess stood waiting in the middle of the bothy with the patience of a woman who had already run this conversation a thousand times in her head and knew every branch of it.

"You want me to build your cage," Emrys said at last. Their voice came out steadier than they felt. "Dress it up however you like—custody, stewardship, law. My mother gave her memory *freely*. Freely means no one holds the key. Not even well-meaning Wardens. Especially not well-meaning Wardens."

"My ancestor proved what happens without governance."

"Your ancestor proved what happens when one man decides he deserves to hold the key." Emrys pushed off the doorframe, forced themselves upright, forced their spine straight. "You're not offering me a covenant, Corvess. You're offering me a bargain with the same shape as his theft, just wearing kinder clothes. Give me control, and I'll spare what you love. That's not stewardship. That's ransom."

Something moved behind the High Warden's eyes—hurt, maybe, or the memory of hurt. "I am trying to save *everyone*."

"I know." And Emrys did know, and that was the cruelest part. "So was he, probably. At the start."

Corvess was quiet for a long moment. Snow ticked against the window. In the hearth, the fire Emrys had bought with a summer afternoon settled into embers, and Emrys thought absurdly of bread—of a taste they still had, of a taste they might yet lose—and understood suddenly, wholly, what they were standing inside of.

Every covenant is a bargain. Every bargain costs. The only question was who paid, and whether they chose to.

"You need time," Corvess said finally, and there was no mockery in it. Only exhaustion. "Take until the Stones. Take the mountain climb. Decide there, at the source, where you can feel what's left of the lattice. I'll hold my army below the tree line." She drew her gloves back on, finger by finger, sealing something away. "But understand me, Ashgrove. I am not a patient woman pretending to be one. I am a desperate woman pretending to be patient, and the difference is that desperation has a deadline. If you reach the Stones and refuse me, I will do what must be done, and I will live with it, because that is what my family does. We live with things."

She paused at the door. Looked back once.

"For what it's worth—I hope you find a third answer. I've been looking for one my whole life." A ghost of something that was almost a smile. "I haven't found it yet. But you people—you smugglers and hedge-witches and stubborn mapmakers—you keep finding answers in places I'd never think to look."

Then she stepped out into the snow, and the dark took her, and the constellation of lanterns down the slope began, slowly, to withdraw.

* * *

For a long time nobody spoke.

Sable sheathed her knife with a click that sounded very loud. Pip sat up, eyes red-rimmed, pretending she hadn't heard everything, fooling no one. The fire had burned down to almost nothing, and none of them moved to feed it.

"She's right about one thing," Sable said finally. Her voice was rough. "If the Stones fail, and Tam's tied to the lattice—"

"I know."

"Emrys." Sable crossed the room and took their face in both hands, the way she did when precision mattered more than comfort, forcing their eyes to hers. "Listen to me. Whatever you're building in your head right now—some grand sacrifice, some trade of yourself for him—stop. I've watched you spend memories all the way up this mountain like coins. I've watched you give away tastes and summers and your own mother's voice. And I am telling you, as your cartographer, as the person whose entire profession is knowing where things actually are: you cannot navigate your way out of this by erasing yourself. There won't be anyone left holding the map."

"The alternative is Tam."

"The alternative is *her terms*." Sable's grip tightened. "And her terms are just Aldric Veyra with a softer voice. You said it yourself."

From the wall, Pip's voice came small and steady. "My mum used to say you can't buy back a thing with the same coin that lost it."

Emrys looked at her—at this girl who had lost her mother's face and kept walking anyway, who had offered her light to strangers before anyone asked—and felt something shift in their chest, some load-bearing wall settling.

"There's a third answer," Emrys said slowly. "She said she hoped I'd find one. Which means she hasn't. Which means she doesn't know what happens if the covenant is reforged *freely*—truly freely, the way my mother did it. Because no one's done it in a thousand years." They turned toward the window, toward the black bulk of the mountain waiting under the starless sky. "Aldric stole. Corvess wants to govern. But giving—" Emrys's breath fogged the glass. "Giving isn't either of those. Giving changes the giver. Maybe it changes what the lattice *is*."

"That's not a plan," Sable said. "That's a prayer."

"I'm a storm-caller, Sable. Prayers are mostly what I've got left."

Outside, the first snow of the true winter began to fall—fat, silent flakes, falling straight down through windless air, as if the whole world were holding its breath. And high above, where no stars had shone for weeks, Emrys saw—or dreamed they saw—a single point of light flicker, gutter, and hold.

Tam's face floated up in their mind: younger, laughing, soaked in rain that Emrys had called down. *Come on, Rys. Show me the big one.*

*Hold on,* Emrys told the dark, to the mountain, to whatever was left of a brother threaded into a dying lattice. *I'm coming. And I'm not choosing between you and the world. Not this time.*

Above them, the faint light flickered again—

and stayed.
