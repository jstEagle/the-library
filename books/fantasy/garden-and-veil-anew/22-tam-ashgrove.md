## 22. Tam Ashgrove

The sword was the first thing Tam Ashgrove had ever owned that fit him properly.

He had thought about that once, years ago, standing in the armory of the Warden citadel while a quartermaster with ink-stained fingers measured his reach and pronounced him "a natural grip." Everything before—the cot in the border garrison, the boots with someone else's name scratched inside, even his own body for a stretch of those first starving months—had been borrowed, secondhand, too big or too small. But the blade they gave him sat in his hand like it had been poured there.

He had not thought about it in a long time. He thought about it now because he was holding it wrong—loose, angled away from the woman across the training yard—and because his hands had begun to remember things his mind had spent twenty years burying.

"Again," said Corvess.

She stood at the edge of the yard in her grey traveling cloak, no armor, watching him drill forms against a pell with the particular stillness she wore like other people wore rank. Around them the citadel of Veyra's Reach hummed with preparation: couriers crossing the flagstones at a run, wagons being loaded under tarpaulins, the forge-song of smiths working through the night shift. The army would march within three days. Everyone knew where. The Covenant Stones. Everyone knew why, too—or thought they did. Corvess had told them the truth the way she told all truths: precisely enough to be unimpeachable, shaped just enough to serve her.

*A storm-caller has been sighted on the Salt Road. A covenant-heir. The one who will restore what our founder broke.* She had let the word *restore* do its quiet work in the ranks. What she meant was *harvest*.

"Your guard is drifting," Corvess said.

Tam reset his stance. "Captain's privilege to say so."

"You're not a captain right now. You're a soldier who can't hold a form." She came closer, boots silent on the frost-rimed stone. Up close she smelled of cold iron and something faintly sweet, like the inside of an old book—a smell he had never been able to place and had stopped trying to. "You've been wrong since the report came in from Lastlight."

"I've been tired since Lastlight."

"You've been *something* since Lastlight." Her eyes were pale as winter water. Twenty years he had served this woman, and he had never once seen her blink when it would have been useful to. "Tell me."

He ran the form again instead. Cut, recover, cut. The pell's leather face split along an old seam, straw bleeding out into the snow. He watched it fall and felt something in his chest split along the same seam.

Because the report from Lastlight had carried a description. Grey cloak, hooded. Charm-seller's tray. And beside the storm-caller, a girl with lamplighter's soot still under her fingernails, and a cartographer whose maps were banned in four provinces, and—

And a name. Not spoken aloud. Marrow had not spoken it; Marrow was too careful, and besides, Marrow had looked at Tam across the inn's common room with an expression Tam had spent two decades learning to read on soldiers' faces, and it had not been fear. It had been *pity*. Pity, and a question: *Do you know who she is?*

Tam knew who she was. He had known since the moment the word *storm-caller* crossed the dispatch table, because there was only one storm-caller left in the world who mattered, and he had helped make sure of it.

"Emrys," he said.

Corvess said nothing. She let him say it again, alone, into the cold.

"My sister," Tam said. "Emrys Ashgrove. My sister is alive, and she's walking toward your Stones, and you mean to bleed her."

*

He had testified on a Tuesday. That was the detail that came back to him now, always now, always at the worst hours—the ordinariness of it. Not a night of storm and terror. A Tuesday morning, a bare room in the Warden garrison at Hollowfen, a clerk with a dry cough and a stenographer's pen, and a Warden examiner named Doss who had bought him bread and honey first.

He had been seventeen. His hands had been bandaged where the lightning had kissed them—strange marks, Lichtenberg figures, ferns of silver burned into the skin, and the garrison surgeon had called them *evidence* with a weight that made the word mean *guilt*. Three dead in the market square at Hollowfen. A granary roof collapsed. A carter crushed beneath his own horse. And in the middle of it, a figure in the rain with their arms raised and the sky coming down like the end of the world.

His sister. His little sister, who had cried at the slaughter of chickens, who had once spent a whole summer teaching a lame crow to fly again, who had stood in that square with the storm answering her like a hound answers its master.

That was what Doss had shown him. Not lies—Doss hadn't needed lies. He had shown Tam the square, the shrouded bodies, the weeping survivors, and then he had shown him the alternative. *Storm-calling is a hanging offense under the Accords. For the caller. And by extension, for kin who sheltered the caller's training, who concealed the talent, who—* and here Doss had paused, delicately, over the list of Tam's own small crimes, the letters he'd smuggled, the coin he'd passed to hedge-teachers, the years he'd known and said nothing.

*Or,* Doss had said, *you tell us what you saw, and we understand that the girl was coerced. Groomed by a cabal of residual casters operating out of the Ashgrove farm. A victim, Tam. Victims go to the Reclamation Houses. They're corrected. They live.*

They had shown him Emrys's confession afterward. It wasn't hers—he'd known that even then, known it in the marrow the way you know your own name—but it bore her mark, her signature traced by another hand, and it named names, and the names burned down through the borderlands like a fuse. Twelve arrests. Three hangings. One farm seized and salted.

And Tam Ashgrove, seventeen years old, hands wrapped in linen, had sat in a bare room on a Tuesday morning and sworn that he saw his sister call the storm deliberately. That she looked at the carter before the bolt fell. That she *chose*.

He had told himself the story for twenty years, the way you keep a fire banked: that he had saved her life. That the Reclamation Houses were terrible but not fatal. That a corrected sister in a House was better than a hanged sister on a scaffold, and that someday—someday—he would find which House held her, and buy her out, and take her somewhere the Wardens' writ didn't run, and beg forgiveness on his knees.

Then, eight years into the telling, he had learned there was no record of Emrys Ashgrove in any Reclamation House in the province. Then twelve years in, he had learned what happened to unregistered casters who vanished between the garrison and the Houses, and he had stopped asking questions, because the answers were the kind that made men desert, and deserters from the Wardens ended up in ditches with their throats opened and their uniforms folded neatly beside them.

So he had kept serving. He had risen. He had become the thing the uniform promised he could be: competent, trusted, decorated. Captain of the High Warden's own retinue. Keeper of her correspondence. The man who read her letters before she did and burned the ones that might trouble the ranks.

And every year the story thinned. Every year the Tuesday morning grew more vivid and the justification grew more pale, until one day—not long ago, perhaps a year, perhaps less—he had woken in the citadel and understood that he had not saved his sister at all. He had sold her. Cheaply. For bread and honey and the promise of his own clean hands.

*

"You mean to bleed her," he said again, and made himself look at Corvess while he said it.

She watched him with that unhurried attention, and then, unexpectedly, she smiled. It was not a cruel smile. That was the horror of Aldric Veyra's bloodline—they had inherited not cruelty but *certainty*, polished smooth as river stone, and certainty could smile kindly while it sharpened the knife.

"I mean to renew the covenant," Corvess said. "The covenant requires memory freely given—that much the old texts are clear on, whatever else the archives lost. Blood freely given works. It has worked, in my family, for generations; how do you think we kept the wards fed after the sundering? Why do you think the Veil held at all, these twenty years, while lesser lines failed?" She turned her hand over, studied the palm as though reading it. "But blood is a blunt instrument. It holds the door shut; it doesn't open it. Memory opens it. And a covenant-heir's memory—" she looked up at him, mild as milk, "—a heir's freely given memory would open it wide enough to last another age."

"And if she doesn't give it freely?"

"Then I will obtain it by the means available." She said it without heat, the way she ordered supply columns rerouted. "Tam. You have served me faithfully for twenty years. You have earned honesty, so hear it: I do not want to hurt your sister. I want what she carries. If she gives it gladly, she walks away whole, and richer than she knows. If she resists—" a small shrug, "—then the winter takes us all, and her resistance will have killed more people than any storm she ever called."

*Any storm she ever called.* The words went into him like a splinter finding old wood.

"You know about the storm," he said.

"I know everything in your file, Captain. Including the parts you don't know are in it." She stepped close, and lowered her voice, and for one strange moment she sounded almost gentle. "I also know what Doss did to you. I have read his methods. I inherited his archive." Her pale eyes held his. "You believe you lied. You may even believe the lie. But I have seen the original testimony of the survivors of Hollowfen, Tam—all of it, including the testimony that was struck. And I am going to tell you something now, because I need you steady on the road ahead, and a man at war with himself is not steady."

She waited until he nodded.

"There was a witness that day. A granary hand, half-blind, hiding in the doorway of the cooper's shop. Close enough to see everything. His account was struck from the record because it contradicted the narrative Doss required." Corvess's voice stayed level, almost bored. "He swore the storm began before your sister raised her arms. He swore it came down the valley like a wall—natural, vast, wrong-seasoned—and that she stood in it with her arms up trying to *hold it back*. Trying to turn it. And that it turned anyway, because a storm that size does not care, and the carter died, and the roof fell, and your sister knelt in the wreckage screaming at the sky to stop."

The yard tilted. Tam put a hand against the pell and the straw and the split leather swam in front of him.

"That's not true," he heard himself say. "Doss showed me—"

"Doss showed you what served Doss. Your sister's trial was theater, Captain. The verdict preceded the evidence by a week. All Doss ever needed from you was legitimacy—a brother's word, a family's shame, a clean story to hang the purges on." She straightened his collar with two fingers, a gesture horribly like their mother's. "Whether you choose to believe me is your affair. But consider: I gain nothing by lying to you. I need you functional, not comfortable. And consider further—" the gentleness drained away, leaving the winter water eyes, "—that whether the storm was hers or heaven's, she is still the most dangerous caster alive, walking toward the most dangerous place alive, in the company of smugglers and hedge-witches and a deserter who fed her at my own table. Whatever you feel about her, your duty hasn't changed. Bring me the heir. Or stand aside, and be dealt with as anyone who stands aside."

She turned and walked toward the citadel, grey cloak moving like smoke, and left him alone in the yard with the broken pell and the falling snow.

*

He did not sleep that night. He sat in his quarters with a lamp turned low and took out the thing he kept in the false bottom of his campaign chest, the thing he had carried through nineteen postings and two promotions and one occasion when he'd had to abandon baggage under fire and had gone back for it.

A wooden bird. Crude, whittled by a child's knife, one wing longer than the other so it flew crooked forever. Emrys had made it for him the winter before everything, when he was fourteen and she was eleven and their mother was still alive to scold them both for wasting good pine. *It's a kestrel,* she'd said. *Kestrels hover. They hold still in the wind when everything else gets blown around. Like you.*

Like him. God, the irony of it had teeth.

He turned the bird in his fingers. Somewhere below, the forge still sang. Somewhere south, past the Ashline, past the colorless country where the wards had died, his sister was walking toward the Stones with people who loved her—which was more than he could claim, though Marrow had fed her, and Marrow had looked at him with pity, and pity was at least a kind of seeing.

*Trying to hold it back.*

If it was true—if Doss's archive held what Corvess claimed—then everything was inverted. Not his shame alone, but hers. She had spent twenty years believing she had killed three people with her own hands, believing herself a monster in exile, and the monster had been the weather, and the crime had been his. His testimony. His Tuesday morning. His bread and honey.

And she was walking toward a woman who meant to open her like a letter and take what was inside.

Tam set the bird on the table. He looked at it for a long time, the way you look at a map of a country you're about to cross illegally.

There were guards on the south postern he had personally assigned—Hale and Osk, both of whom owed him, both of whom slept badly and drank worse. There was a fast horse in the third stable that no one logged. There was a pass through the eastern fells that appeared on exactly one map, and he knew the cartographer who'd drawn it, because he had read every document that crossed Corvess's desk, and Sable Vane's work was unmistakable.

Twenty years of service. Twenty years of the uniform fitting perfectly.

He took off the sword belt and laid it on the chest, on top of the false bottom, and found that his hands did not shake at all.

The kestrel went into his coat, over his heart, flying crooked forever. Tam Ashgrove blew out the lamp and went down the stairs into the snow, southward, toward the Stones, toward his sister—carrying nothing but a bird, a truth he wasn't yet sure he believed, and the last words he would ever speak as a Warden, muttered to the empty corridor like a prayer:

"Hold still in the wind. I'm coming."
