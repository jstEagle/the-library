## 23. The Road to the Stones

The mountain did not want them.

Emrys had known it from the first switchback, when the wind came down the slope like something with opinions, shoving at their shoulders, plucking at their hems, trying its patient best to turn them back the way they'd come. Twenty years of hiding had taught Emrys to read weather the way other people read faces, and this weather was not merely cold. It was *suspicious*. It circled them. It tested the seams of their coats and the gaps in their resolve and found both wanting.

They climbed anyway.

Behind them, Greyhollow had shrunk to a smudge of smoke against a gray horizon, and beyond it the Ashline burned faintly even now—a thread of dull ember-light where the wards had died, where the land itself had been drained of color. They had crossed that wound four days ago. Sable still wouldn't speak about what she'd seen in the ash fields, but she'd started sleeping with her map case strapped to her chest instead of beside her bedroll, and Emrys understood. Some things you kept close because letting go of them felt like admitting they could be taken.

"You're doing the brooding thing again." Pip's voice came from below and to the left, breathless, indignant. "I can hear it. It sounds like a kettle deciding whether to scream."

"I'm walking," Emrys said.

"You're walking *and* brooding. It's very efficient of you."

Pip scrambled up over the lip of the trail, lamplighter's pole slung across her back like a pikestaff, her braid crusted with ice where her breath froze before it finished leaving her mouth. She was fifteen and stubborn as frost-heaved stone, and three days ago she had begun forgetting things—small things first, then larger ones. The name of the innkeeper at Lastlight. Which pocket she kept her flint in. Her mother's face, for one terrible hour on the second morning, until Sable talked her through describing it aloud, detail by detail, stitching it back into place with words.

The Veil-sickness. It moved faster up here, closer to the Stones, where the covenant's wound gaped widest. Emrys had stopped pretending otherwise. Every hour they climbed, they climbed against it.

"Where's Sable?" Pip asked.

"Ahead. Being insufferable about elevation."

"She's always insufferable about elevation. Last week she corrected a shepherd about his own hill."

Emrys almost smiled. Almost. The cold took smiles away before they finished forming; it had been doing that since the Ashline, snatching warmth out of the air and out of the body with equal indifference. They crested the ridge and found Sable standing at the top with her boots planted wide and her map spread against the wind, weighted at the corners with stones she'd carried up specifically for this purpose, because Sable Vane did not trust the world to hold anything in place unless she held it first.

"Well," Sable said, without turning around. "It's worse than I thought."

"That's your favorite sentence," Emrys said. "You say it every morning like a prayer."

"And every morning I'm right, which is why it's a good prayer." She turned, and Emrys saw what the wind had been hiding: the valley beyond the ridge, and the road—or what had been a road—swallowed whole. Snow lay over the pass in a single unbroken sheet, smooth as poured milk, featureless from edge to edge. No track. No cairn. No shadow of the old pilgrim way that every map in Greyhollow swore ran through this col toward the Covenant Stones. Just white, and above the white a sky the color of dirty wool, starless, sunless, lit by nothing so much as the memory of light.

"The road's gone," Pip said flatly.

"The road isn't gone," Sable said. "Roads don't go anywhere. Roads are ideas. The *snow* is new." She knelt and pressed her palm flat against the drift, and Emrys watched her do the thing she did—the thing that had no name in any guild registry, the thing that made her less a cartographer than a kind of translator between the land and anyone fool enough to travel it. Her fingers spread. Her eyes went distant. "Underneath. There's a wall here. Old retaining stone, left side. And a culvert—" her hand twitched, tracing something invisible— "there. Water still runs under this snow. If water runs, the channel isn't iced solid, which means the grade is steep enough to keep it moving, which means we're on the right line."

"You can feel that?" Pip whispered.

"I can feel where the world has *edges*," Sable said, standing, brushing off her knees. "Everything else is negotiable." She rolled the map with the crisp violence of someone who had already made three decisions while everyone else was still having one feeling. "We cross the col on the bearing of the culvert. Single file. Rope. And nobody steps where I haven't stepped."

* * *

They crossed the col in the last usable hour of light, which was to say the hour when the gray became slightly less gray, and it took everything.

Emrys went second, behind Sable, with the rope around their waist and their storm-glass amulet cupped in one bare hand beneath their mitten. The crack in the glass had widened since the cave—since Pip's lightning, since the wolves, since everything—and Emrys had learned to read it the way they once read clouds. Each small calling cost. A warming charm: the smell of woodsmoke, gone. A shield against the wind: the color of Tam's coat, gone, though they hadn't known its color was still in them until it wasn't. They were spending themselves in small coins, and the purse was nearly empty, and the largest payment waited at the top of the mountain.

They did not tell the others. They suspected Sable knew anyway. Sable knew most things and was tactless about all of them except the ones that mattered.

Halfway across, the snow gave a sound Emrys would remember longer than most things now promised to stay: a low, vast *whump*, like the mountain exhaling, and the slope above them shifted. Not an avalanche—not yet—but a settling, a slow slither of tons of white testing whether it wanted to move.

"Down!" Sable roared, and Emrys dropped, and the rope snapped taut, and for three heartbeats the whole world was the pull of two other people holding them against the slide of the drift, Pip's boots braced on stone Sable had told her to find, everyone screaming without much sound because the wind ate sound the way the winter ate everything else.

Then it stilled. The mountain reconsidered. Somewhere under the snow, water went on running through its culvert, indifferent, faithful, keeping the only promise on the mountain.

"Note for the revised edition," Sable said shakily, hauling them up. "Col crossing: *inadvisable*."

"Noted," Emrys gasped.

"I'm adding it in red."

"You don't have red ink."

"I'll acquire some. There are standards, Emrys."

And that—that absurd, furious pedantry, offered at the top of a dying world with numb lips and shaking hands—was the thing that got Emrys back onto their feet. Not courage. Not duty. Sable complaining about ink. Pip laughing, a wild cracked laugh, and immediately slipping and being caught by the rope, and laughing again. The found family holding, the way it had held since a night market and a shattered ward and a girl who'd seen through a charm-seller's disguise in the space of a single conversation.

They made the far side of the col as the dark came down, and camped in the lee of the retaining wall Sable had promised was there, and it was there, exactly there, ancient pilgrim-masonry furred with ice, and Emrys sat against it and let Pip press a tin cup of melted snow into their hands and pretended, for everyone's sake, that their hands weren't shaking from anything but cold.

* * *

The supplies failed on the fifth day.

It was not dramatic. That was the terrible part. Emrys had imagined, in the long nights, that the end of their journey would arrive with wolves or Wardens or the mountain itself rising against them. Instead it arrived at breakfast, if a heel of black bread and the scraping of a nearly-empty honey jar could be called breakfast, when Sable turned out the ration sack on a flat stone and counted, aloud, in the tone she used for bad news delivered precisely:

"Four days of food. Three, if we're honest about portions, and we're always honest about portions." She looked up. "The Stones are six days away. Seven, in this snow."

Nobody spoke. The wind spoke for all of them, worrying at the wall above their heads.

"We could turn back," Pip said, in the voice of someone who did not mean it and knew no one would believe her.

"To what?" Sable asked—not cruelly, just factually, which was worse. "Greyhollow's under curfew. Corvess knows we're coming; she's had scouts on the pass since the checkpoint. Your mother's village is memory-drained, and the lowlands will follow by spring. There is no *back*, Pip. There's only forward and the shape forward takes." She began repacking the sack, each item placed with terrible care. "So. We go up. We eat half rations. We move fast while the weather holds, and if the weather doesn't hold—"

"It'll hold," Emrys said.

Everyone looked at them.

Emrys heard their own voice as if from a distance, calm in a way they hadn't earned. "I've been listening to this mountain for five days. Storms have a rhythm. This one's holding its breath—it's saving itself for the summit. We have a window. Two days, maybe three, of hard, clear cold." They flexed their fingers inside their mittens, where the storm-glass sat cracked and hungry. "I can feel it. Weather was always the one language I never had to learn."

Sable studied them for a long moment, her sharp face unreadable, wind-reddened, older than her years in the way of people who carry maps of places that no longer exist. "Two days of clear cold," she repeated. "You're certain?"

"No," Emrys said honestly. "But I'm certain enough to bet our breakfast on it, and I've never once bet wrong about a storm. Only about people." They thought of Tam, and did not say the name, and saw Sable notice the silence and, mercifully, leave it alone. "We move at first light."

They moved at first light.

* * *

The window held, and it was worse than a storm, in its way—the deep cold that comes after wind, when the air goes still and the stars' absence becomes a presence, a great blank dome pressing down. They climbed through a silence so complete Emrys could hear their own heartbeat, and Pip's, and the tiny crystalline tick of the snow settling around them, and beneath it all, fainter than any of these, something else.

A hum. Felt more than heard. In the teeth. In the old scar tissue of Emrys's palms.

"What is that?" Pip whispered on the second morning, stopping mid-stride, one mittened hand pressed to her sternum.

Emrys had felt it too, growing stronger with every thousand feet of ascent, and had been refusing to name it. Now, with the summit ridge finally visible ahead—a black serrated line against the pale sky, and above it, impossibly, a shimmer in the air like heat-haze in dead winter—they could refuse no longer.

"The Stones," they said. "Or what's left of them. The covenant isn't broken the way a rope breaks. It's broken the way a bone breaks—wrongly set, still trying to knit. What you're hearing is the realms grinding against each other." They looked at Pip, at her chapped face and her bright frightened eyes, at the girl who had given her light once already and would give it again, who taught them all what free giving meant without ever once making a speech about it. "We're close enough to hear the wound, little lamp. That's how close we are."

Pip nodded slowly. Then, because she was Pip: "Does it hurt? The mountain, I mean. When it grinds."

Emrys considered lying. Considered the kindness of lies, the twenty years they'd spent wrapped in them like blankets. "Yes," they said. "I think it does."

"Then we'd better hurry," Pip said simply, and walked on.

* * *

That night, camped in the mouth of a shallow cave with the summit a day away and the food reduced to a handful of oats and a scrap of salt pork they'd been carrying since Lastlight, Sable sat apart with her maps and did not sleep, and Emrys went and sat with her.

For a while neither of them spoke. Below them the world fell away in folds of blue-white shadow. Above them the empty sky. Between those two emptinesses, their small fire, fed on the last of the dwarf-pine scrub, burning low and gold and stubborn.

"You should know," Sable said eventually, not looking up from the map she was annotating by firelight, "that I've mapped us a route to the summit that doesn't exist on any chart. Not the Warden charts, not the pilgrim charts, not the merchant charts my father sold his soul to compile." She tapped a thin inked line that wandered along the eastern face where every other map showed sheer cliff. "There's a shelf. Old lava flow, under the snow. I felt it yesterday—broad as a wagon road, if you trust your feet and don't look down. It cuts two days off the standard approach."

"Every other cartographer missed it?"

"Every other cartographer trusted the maps." She smiled thinly. "Maps are arguments about the world. I prefer to interrogate the world directly." She rolled the chart, and then, uncharacteristically, didn't put it away. Held it across her knees. Looked into the fire. "My family mapped the borderlands for six generations. We drew the wards' boundaries. We drew the lines Corvess's people used to hunt people like you." Her jaw tightened. "I've been waiting my whole life to draw a line they can't use."

"Sable—"

"Don't." She said it quietly. "Don't thank me, don't absolve me, don't do the thing where you make it noble. I'm doing this because it's mine to do. Because somebody in my line has to hold the pen for the other side, for once." She finally looked at him, firelight in her eyes. "Tomorrow we take the shelf. You walk where I walk. All of you. And when we get to the top, whatever's waiting there—Corvess, the army, the winter, your brother—" Emrys flinched; she didn't pretend not to see it— "whatever's waiting, we hold. That's the whole plan. That's the entire strategy. We hold."

Emrys looked at her—at this sharp-tongued woman who had seen through a charm-seller's disguise in a night market and decided, without ceremony, to burn her family's six generations of complicity into a new map—and felt something in their chest that had no name in any of the languages they knew, living or dead.

"We hold," they agreed.

From the cave's depths, Pip stirred in her sleep and murmured something—her mother's name, maybe, or the taste of bread, the things she was fighting to keep—and both of them turned toward the sound, together, the same instinct, the same fierce tenderness, and Emrys thought: *this*. This is what I spent twenty years forgetting existed. Not safety. Not power. This—the rope, the shared watch, the map drawn for the other side.

Above them, in the blank and starless dark, the shimmer over the summit pulsed once, slow, like a heartbeat heard through a wall.

Like something waiting.

Like something that knew their name.
