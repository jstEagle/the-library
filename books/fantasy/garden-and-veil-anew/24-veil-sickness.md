## 24. Veil-Sickness

Pip had always thought of her mother's face as the one thing in the world she could never lose.

Not because it was beautiful—though it was, in the way that tired things are beautiful, all soft edges and laugh-lines worn deep as riverbeds—but because Pip had looked at it every morning of her life. Fifteen years of mornings. Fifteen years of a face bent over the washbasin, over the stove, over the little tin lamp Pip's father had left behind when the fever took him, back when Pip was small enough to still believe lamplighters were a kind of magic all their own.

She knew that face the way she knew her own hands. The crooked tooth on the left side when Mama smiled wide. The scar above her eyebrow from a kettle, years ago, that she'd never let anyone charm away because—"it's how your gran finds me in dreams," she'd said, and laughed, and Pip had laughed too, and that was that.

So when Pip woke on the cold floor of Emrys's shop with frost on the inside of the windows and her mother's name sitting strange and heavy on her tongue, she did not understand at first what was wrong. She only knew that something was.

* * *

They had made camp in the ruined chapel two nights past, after the silent village, after Hollow Bells, after Sable had stood in the empty square with her maps spread out on the frozen fountain and said, quietly, "We're close now. Three days, maybe four, if the weather holds." The weather did not hold. It never held anymore. But they had found the chapel with its collapsed roof half-sheltering the old altar, and Bramble-the-elder—Fen, the one who'd taken the spear through the shoulder at the checkpoint and refused to lie down for it—had finally slept without groaning, which everyone pretended was a good sign and no one quite believed.

Pip had taken first watch because she liked first watch. She liked being the one awake while the others breathed slow and even under their blankets; she liked the dark and the responsibility of it. Back home in Greyhollow she'd climbed her ladder every dusk with her lighting-pole and touched flame to each lamp along Candlewick Row, and there was a pride in that—a small, steady pride, like a coal you cupped in both hands. Being the one who kept the light going. She had thought, when she ran off to follow Emrys Ashgrove into the wild white end of the world, that she was carrying that same coal with her.

Now she sat against the altar with her knees pulled up, watching the snow come down through the broken roof, and tried to remember her mother's face.

And could not.

It started small. That was the terrible thing about it—the smallness. She could remember that Mama had a scar above her eyebrow. She could remember the kettle, the steam, the sound of her own crying afterward and Mama laughing it off. But when she reached for the face around the scar, it was like reaching into fog. There was a shape where the face should be. A warmth where the smile should be. The crooked tooth—left side, she was sure, mostly sure—it floated in her mind with nothing attached to it, a single detail adrift in a blankness that should have been full.

"No," Pip said out loud, too loud, and Fen stirred by the fire and muttered something in his sleep, and she clapped both hands over her mouth.

Because she knew. Of course she knew. She'd seen the village. She'd walked those silent streets between people standing frozen mid-task—a woman with her hands still buried in bread dough, a boy halfway up a ladder with a hammer raised, an old man at a well-rope with his mouth open on a word he would never finish—and Sable had explained it in her flat cartographer's voice, the voice she used for things too big to say any other way: *The Veil is thinning. What leaks through isn't light. It's memory. The long winter eats minds first.*

Eats minds first. And then what? Pip hadn't asked. She hadn't wanted to know. She'd been fifteen and terrified and trying very hard to believe that the sickness was a thing that happened to other villages, other people, strangers on a road—people who hadn't spent their whole lives one street over from a storm-caller, people whose mothers were safe behind them in Greyhollow with a lamp lit in the window.

But her mother wasn't behind her anymore. Her mother was three days' hard travel south, alone in a city gone dark, and Pip was here, and the face was going, and there was nothing—*nothing*—she could do about either of those things except sit very still in a ruined chapel and not wake anybody.

The snow came down through the broken roof. Somewhere out in the dark, something howled—not wolves, or not only wolves. The wind had learned new sounds since the stars went out. Pip pulled her blanket tighter and began, deliberately, methodically, to list everything she remembered about her mother, the way Sable listed landmarks on a route. Out loud, in a whisper, so the words would fix themselves in the air.

"Her name is Wren. Wren Joubert. She has brown hair going grey at the temples, and she ties it back with whatever's nearest, usually my old bootlace, which is disgusting. She laughs before she finishes jokes. She burns the porridge every single morning and blames the stove. She hums when she sews—wrong notes, all wrong notes, she doesn't care. She smells like lamp oil and lavender because she says a person ought to smell like their work and their hope."

Her voice cracked on the last one. She said it again anyway. *Lamp oil and lavender.* She said it five times. She said it until the words stopped sounding like words.

Then, in the gray hour before dawn, she crept to her pack and took out the stub of charcoal she used for marking lamp-posts, and on the back of one of Sable's spare maps—the one of the Salt Road, which they didn't need anymore—she drew her mother's face.

Or she tried to.

The eyes came easy. Brown, warm, always a little crinkled at the corners like paper folded too many times. The mouth came easy too, the crooked tooth peeking out. But the space between—the whole architecture of the face, the nose and cheeks and the particular way her brow sat—wouldn't come. Pip drew it three times. Each version was her mother and none of them were, and the worst one was the third, because by then she couldn't tell which parts were memory and which parts were invention, and a face built half of guessing was worse than no face at all.

She wrote underneath it, in careful letters: *This is my mama. Her name is Wren Joubert. If I forget, read this to me.*

She folded it small and put it in the inner pocket of her coat, over her heart, where the amulet Emrys had given her used to sit before it cracked.

* * *

Emrys noticed at breakfast.

Of course they did. They noticed everything—that was the maddening, wonderful thing about them. Pip had watched them work the night market for a year before she ever worked up the courage to speak: the way Emrys's eyes moved over a crowd like water finding cracks, cataloguing griefs and hungers nobody spoke aloud. Twenty years of hiding had made them a scholar of other people's faces, because faces were the only things they'd been allowed to study.

So when Pip sat down by the fire with her bowl of barley porridge and couldn't meet anyone's eyes, Emrys set down their own bowl and said, gently, "Pip. Look at me."

"I'm eating."

"You're stirring. You've been stirring for a minute. Look at me."

Pip looked. And it was a mistake, because Emrys's face was full of concern, and concern was the one thing she couldn't bear right now, and her chin did the wobbly thing she hated, and then she was crying into the porridge, great ugly sobs, while Fen awkwardly patted her shoulder with his good arm and Sable pretended very hard to study a map.

"It's my mum," Pip got out. "I can't—I went to draw her and I can't—her face is *gone*, Emrys, it's just gone, like someone scooped it out, and I know her name but the face won't come, and the village people, they just *stood there*, and what if that's me, what if I'm next, what if I forget her completely before we even get to the stupid Stones—"

"Breathe." Emrys crouched in front of her, and their hands—scarred, clever, always faintly smelling of ozone and dried herbs—took hers and held them still. "Breathe. In. Again. Good." They waited until the sobs came slower. "Tell me exactly what's missing. Not the fear. The facts."

"The facts are the fear," Pip whispered.

"Maybe. But facts we can fight." Emrys's thumb traced a small circle on the back of her hand—an old habit, Pip realized, some gesture from a life twenty years closed. "Memory isn't one thing, little lamp. It's a thousand threads. Some go, some hold. Which threads are left?"

Pip sniffed, and thought, and it was easier somehow to answer when the question was shaped like work. "Her name. Her hair. The scar. The tooth. She burns porridge. She hums wrong. Lamp oil and lavender."

"Lamp oil and lavender," Emrys repeated, and something flickered across their face—there and gone, quick as heat lightning. "That's a strong thread. Stronger than you think. Smell holds longer than sight, in everyone. In hedge-witches especially." They hesitated, and Pip saw them weigh something, the way they weighed everything now, ever since the amulet cracked and they'd lost the taste of their mother's bread. Ever since they'd started counting costs. "There's a working. Small. A fixing-charm, really—you anchor a memory to an object, and the object holds the thread when your mind can't. Hedge-witches used to stitch names into buttons and lockets. It's children's magic, mostly. Safe."

"But?"

"But it needs a focus that already carries the memory. Something of hers. Something she wore or handled often." Emrys's eyes went to Pip's coat, to the inner pocket, and Pip understood before they said it. "You don't have anything of hers. You left in the night with a lighting-pole and a stolen apple."

Pip's stomach dropped. "I didn't think I'd need—I didn't think I'd be *gone* this long—"

"Nobody thinks." Emrys said it without judgment, which somehow made it worse. Then they were quiet a moment, turning something over, and Pip watched them decide. "All right. We adapt. Give me your hand."

"What are you—"

"A variation. Older. My mother's line, actually." A ghost of a smile. "You'll be the focus. Hold the threads yourself instead of giving them to an object. It's harder, and it won't last forever, but it will last until the Stones—if the Stones do what we think they'll do."

Emrys closed their eyes. When they opened them, there was a faint grey shimmer in the air between their palms, like breath on a winter window, and Pip felt the working settle over her—not heavy, not cold, but *attentive*, the way a blanket feels when someone tucks it in around you. Words in a language she almost recognized, older than Greyhollow, older than the Wardens. Emrys's voice dropped low and rough, and for a moment the ruined chapel smelled impossibly of woodsmoke and rain, and Pip thought: *this is what they lost. This is what they've been hiding for twenty years. And they're spending it on me.*

The shimmer sank into her chest like warm water.

"There," Emrys said, and their voice was thinner than before. Pip saw them press two fingers to their temple, briefly, the way you do when the room tilts. "The threads you named—they're knotted now. Name, hair, scar, tooth, porridge, humming, lavender. They'll hold. They may fray at the edges, but they'll hold."

"What did it cost you?" Pip asked, because she'd learned to ask, because everyone in this ragged family had learned to ask.

Emrys smiled and lied beautifully. "A song I barely liked anyway."

"That's not true."

"No," Emrys admitted. "It's not. But it's mine to spend, and I choose to spend it on you. That's the whole of hedge-witchery, Pip. Everything else is commentary."

* * *

Sable found her at midday, out on the chapel steps with the map-face in her lap.

The cartographer lowered herself onto the step beside Pip without asking, which was its own kind of speech. Sable Vane did not share silence easily. Silence was empty space on a map, and Sable abhorred empty space.

"You drew this?" she said, nodding at the charcoal face.

"This morning. Before I forgot more."

Sable studied it for a long moment. Pip braced for critique—Sable critiqued everything, coastlines and handwriting and the way Fen chewed—but it didn't come. Instead Sable said, "My father's face went first."

Pip turned. "What?"

"When the Veil started thinning—years ago, before anyone had words for it. He was a surveyor. Worked the borderlands. Came back from a mapping run one autumn and couldn't remember my mother's face. Just hers. Everyone else fine." Sable's voice stayed level, flat as her plains, but her thumb was moving along the edge of the map in Pip's lap, back and forth, back and forth. "He used to stand at the window at night holding a miniature of her and crying without knowing why. He said it felt like homesickness for a house he was standing in."

"That's—" Pip's throat closed. "That's exactly it. That's exactly what it feels like."

"I know." Sable finally looked at her, and behind the sharpness there was something old and tired and kind. "Here's what I learned watching him, little lamp, and you can take it or leave it. The forgetting doesn't take everything at once. It takes the picture first. The picture goes, and you panic, because you think the person's gone. But they're not. The picture was never the person. The threads underneath—what she smelled like, what she sounded like, what she burned every morning—those go last. Those are roots. Pictures are leaves."

"Leaves fall."

"Leaves fall," Sable agreed. "Roots hold through winter. That's rather the point of them." She tapped the charcoal drawing. "Keep this anyway. But don't trust it over the list. Drawings lie. Porridge doesn't."

Pip laughed—a wet, hiccupping laugh, but a real one. "You're comforting me with botany."

"I'm comforting you with cartography. Botany is Fen's department. He'll tell you the roots metaphor is stolen from turnips." Sable stood, brushing snow from her coat, and paused. "Three days to the Stones, Pip. Maybe less. Whatever frays between here and there—we hold the rest together. That's what a crew is. That's what a map is. Nobody carries the whole route alone."

She walked back toward the fire, and Pip sat a moment longer, looking at the imperfect face on the map. Then she took out the charcoal and added, beneath the written list, one more line:

*She smells like lamp oil and lavender, because she says a person ought to smell like their work and their hope.*

* * *

That night the wind changed, and with it came the dream.

Pip dreamed she was back on Candlewick Row with her lighting-pole, going lamp to lamp, but the lamps wouldn't light. She'd touch the flame to the wick and the wick would drink the fire and give nothing back, and behind every dark window stood her mother, faceless, waiting for the light that Pip could not make. And in the dream Pip understood—with the terrible lucid logic of dreams—that the lamps weren't broken. They were *full*. Full of everyone's forgotten faces, all the memories the long winter had drunk from the world, and they would never hold flame again because there was no room left.

She woke gasping, heart slamming, and for a moment didn't know where she was—and that frightened her more than the dream, because she had always known where she was, always, even half-asleep, even as a baby according to Mama, who claimed Pip could find her own cot blindfolded out of pure stubbornness—

*Mama.* The name came. The threads held. *Wren Joubert. Lamp oil and lavender. Burns the porridge.*

Pip pressed her hand flat against her chest, over the pocket with the folded map, and breathed until the world steadied.

Then she noticed the light.

It was coming through the broken roof—faint, silver, trembling. Not moonlight. There was no moon anymore; the sky had been a blank black plate since the night the stars went out, and everyone had stopped looking up because looking up hurt. But this light moved, and shimmered, and fell across the chapel floor in long rippling bands like the bottom of a pool.

Pip crawled to the gap in the roof and looked up.

The sky was tearing.

High above the mountain, far to the north where the Covenant Stones waited, the darkness had split along a seam no wider than a knife-cut—and through the tear poured a light that was not starlight, not moonlight, not anything Pip had a name for. It was the color of the moment before a storm breaks. It pulsed, slow, like breathing. Like something enormous on the other side of the sky had opened one eye.

Behind her, she heard Emrys rise. Heard the sharp intake of breath. Heard Sable whisper, "That's not on any map I've ever drawn."

And heard Fen, hoarse from his sickbed by the fire, say the thing they were all thinking:

"It knows we're coming."

The tear in the sky pulsed once more—slow, patient, vast—and high above the mountain, at its northern edge, a single point of light kindled in the darkness.

One star. The first in weeks.

It hung there, trembling, like a lamp-flame cupped in two hands.

Like a promise. Or a summons.
