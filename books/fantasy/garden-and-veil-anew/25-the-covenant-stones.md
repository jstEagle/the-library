## 25. The Covenant Stones

The mountain did not want them there.

Emrys felt it in their teeth before they felt it in their boots—a low, grinding reluctance in the stone itself, as though the whole mass of the peak were a jaw set against them, and every switchback of the trail a step between its teeth. The wind came down from the summit in long, keening gusts that carried no snow at all, only cold, only silence, only the faint mineral smell of things that had been broken a very long time ago and never mourned.

They had been climbing for three days.

Three days since the burned capital with its wandering, memoryless citizens. Three days since Sable had stood in the middle of Veyra's grand processional avenue, map cases clutched to her chest like armor, and said, quietly, "We keep going north," in a voice that dared anyone to argue. No one had argued. There was nothing left in that city to argue about.

Now the trail had thinned to a suggestion—a seam of darker rock winding up through the scree—and the world had gone white and blue and utterly still. The ever-deepening winter that had followed them across the Ashline reached its fullest expression here. Frost sheathed every boulder in feathers and ferns. Their breath froze in their lashes. Pip's lamplighter's coat, patched and re-patched, had gone stiff as bark, and she walked with her arms wrapped around herself, her lips moving constantly—counting steps, Emrys suspected, or reciting names. Her mother's name. Her own. The small litany of a girl determined not to be forgotten by herself.

"Tell me again what we're looking for," Pip said, for perhaps the ninth time that morning. Not because she'd forgotten. Because saying it aloud kept it true.

"The Covenant Stones." Emrys shifted the strap of their pack higher on their shoulder. The storm-glass amulet lay beneath three layers of wool against their sternum, and even through the wool Emrys could feel it—warm, faintly, the way a hearthstone stays warm after the fire dies. It had cracked in the cave below the pass, and each small calling since had cost them something they could not name until it was gone. "Twelve of them, if the old maps are right. Set in a ring on the summit plateau. Older than Greyhollow. Older than the Wardens. Older than the border, maybe."

"Older than the border," Sable repeated behind them, in the tone of a woman who had spent four days arguing with that phrase and lost every time. "Borders are drawn by people, Emrys. People with ink and armies and opinions."

"These weren't drawn." Emrys glanced back at her. Sable Vane climbed like she did everything else—jaw first, fury second, competence third and always. Ice had built little white bridges across her eyebrows. She looked, Emrys thought not for the first time, like a woman carved out of pure stubbornness by someone with a very fine chisel. "They were *agreed*. That's what the word means. Covenant. Not a wall. A promise."

Sable snorted, but her eyes went, briefly, northward—to where the sky should have been and wasn't. Four days without stars now. Four days of a heavens like a sheet drawn over a corpse. "Then let's go read the fine print."

* * *

They crested the final ridge as the light was failing—not sunset, because the sun had become a pale coin behind gauze days ago, but the slow dimming that passed for evening now—and stopped, all three of them, as if the ridge itself had put out a hand.

The plateau opened below them like a held breath.

And the Stones were broken.

Emrys had imagined them whole, without deciding to: twelve pillars standing in their ancient ring, weathered but proud, the way ruins look in ballads. What lay below was worse than ruin. The Stones had not merely fallen; they had been *shattered*—each pillar burst from within, it seemed, split into shards that leaned against one another like mourners, or lay flat in the snow like bones arranged by some enormous careless hand. The tallest still stood, barely, half its height remaining, its fractured crown tilted toward the dead sky at an angle that looked less like architecture and more like supplication.

Frost covered everything. But it was not the frost of the mountain. Even from the ridge, Emrys could see that the ice on the Stones moved—crawled, slowly, in patterns too regular to be weather, spiraling inward toward the center of the ring and then outward again, breathing.

"Oh," Pip said softly. "Oh, it's *alive*. It's still—" Her voice caught. "It's still trying."

"It's starving," Emrys said.

The word left them before they understood it, the way the truest words always did. They started down the slope, boots punching through the crust, and the amulet against their chest grew warmer with every step, and colder, both at once, like a hand gripping and releasing, gripping and releasing.

The ring was larger than it had looked from above—forty paces across, fifty. Snow lay deep between the shattered pillars, but it did not lie evenly. It lay in channels, in whorls, following lines beneath the surface that no eye could see but that Emrys's feet found anyway, turning them gently, inevitably, toward the center. Toward the place where a twelfth stone should have stood and did not. In its place there was only a socket in the living rock, empty, its edges melted smooth and then refrozen, over and over, into glassy ripples.

Like something had been torn out. Like something had been torn out *recently*, and often, and would be again.

"Emrys." Sable's voice had changed. All the sharpness gone out of it, replaced by something careful and quiet, the voice she used for unexploded things. "Come look at this one."

She was crouched beside the half-standing pillar—the great one, the supplicant—and her bare hand, glove stripped off despite the cold, hovered an inch from its surface. Emrys joined her and saw what she saw, and forgot how to breathe.

The stone was covered in writing.

Not carved. *Grown*—that was the only word for it, letters raised in the rock the way veins rise under skin, flowing and interlaced down every face of the pillar, and where the frost touched them the letters lit, faintly, a green-gold color like the last light through spring leaves. Emrys knew the script. They had known it once, as a child, tracing it in their mother's grimoire while she laughed and said *no one reads High Covenant anymore, little storm, you'll make the archivists weep.* Twenty years of exile had buried the fluency but not the shape of it, and the shape of it rose in them now like water finding an old channel.

"It's the covenant," Emrys heard themselves say. "The original. Word for word."

"Read it," Pip whispered. She had come to stand on Emrys's other side, and her breath was coming fast and shallow. "Please. Out loud. I want—I need to hear how it was supposed to sound."

So Emrys read.

Their voice cracked twice on the old formal phrases, and they did not stop. The language of the covenant was not the language of law—that much became clear with the first line, which was not a clause but an address, a greeting exchanged between equals. *To our neighbors across the veil, who were strangers, who are now kin.* And then the terms, and the terms were not what any Warden catechism had ever taught.

Emrys had grown up on the Wardens' version, everyone in Greyhollow had: the covenant as a leash, magic bound and licensed and permitted, the realms sealed apart for their mutual safety, the Wardens as the seal-keepers, the lock-masters, the necessary jailers of power. Corvess's proclamations rang with it still. *The Veil protects. Obedience preserves.*

But the words on the stone said:

*Let the passage between the worlds be held open not by force, for force corrodes, nor by blood, for blood is taken and taking breeds debt, but by memory freely given. Let each realm give to the other its remembering—its seasons, its songs, its griefs and its harvests—so that neither world shall forget the other, and in remembering, remain one garden under many skies.*

*What is given freely cannot be stolen. What is remembered together cannot be sundered.*

*This is the whole of the law: that no door between kin shall be locked, and no gift between kin shall be compelled.*

Pip made a small sound, like something breaking gently.

"That's it?" she said. "That's all it is? It's not—it was never a cage. It was a *handshake*." She turned to Emrys, and her eyes were wet and furious in the green-gold light. "They taught us it was a prison. My whole life they taught us magic was the thing that had to be chained, and it was—it was people *giving each other presents*. Remembering each other. On purpose."

"Freely given memory," Sable said slowly. She had pulled out one of her journals and was sketching the script with numb, clumsy fingers, refusing to wait for warmth. "That's what Veyra stole. Not power. *Remembering.* Realm-memories. He wove them into his bloodline so his heirs would hold the covenant's anchor-points without ever having given anything for them." She looked up, and the wind pushed her pale hair across her face, and she didn't bother to move it. "You can't steal a gift and have it stay a gift. It becomes something else. It becomes a *debt*. A debt twenty generations deep, accruing interest." Her gaze went to the empty socket at the ring's center. "And the debt's come due."

Emrys barely heard her.

Because understanding had arrived—not gradually, not piece by piece, but all at once, the way a storm front arrives, the whole weight of it visible on the horizon a heartbeat before it breaks—and Emrys stood in the middle of the shattered ring with the covenant's words still warm on their tongue and knew, with terrible clarity, what the Stones were asking.

The anchor-stone at the center had been torn out. The lattice of freely-given memory that had once flowed between the realms—season to season, song to song, grief to grief—had been cut at its heart and its threads stolen, generation after generation, to feed the Veyra line. The Veil was dying because the remembering had stopped. The stars had gone out because no one, on either side, was holding the other in mind anymore. The long winter was not a punishment. It was an *amnesia*, vast and total, closing over two worlds like water over a drowned lamp.

And the covenant could be reforged. The words said so—they could feel it in the script, in the way the letters leaned toward the empty socket like stems toward sun. The lattice could be rewoven. But it could not be forced, and it could not be bought, and it could not be taken from anyone, because the moment complicity entered it, it would corrupt exactly as Veyra's theft had corrupted. It required what the first covenant had required.

Memory. Freely given. Given *all the way*—not a token, not a tithe, but true remembering, surrendered into the stone with open hands, to become part of the shared fabric forever.

Emrys looked down at the cracked storm-glass amulet in their palm, and understood that too. It had been preparing them. Every small calling since the cave, every little lightning-work practiced with Pip in the blue dark, had been a lesson in the grammar of loss. The taste of their mother's bread: gone. The name of the street they'd lived on as a child: gone, a blank where a certainty used to be. They had thought the amulet was failing.

It was teaching.

"Emrys?" Pip's hand on their sleeve. "You've gone somewhere. Where'd you go?"

Emrys closed their fingers over the amulet. The glass bit coldly into their palm, hairline fractures catching the green-gold light like tiny rivers seen from very high up.

"I know what has to happen here," they said.

The wind dropped, all at once, as if the mountain were leaning in to listen.

"The covenant runs on given memory. Real memory, freely surrendered. The first givers gave their whole remembering of the other realm—willingly, gladly, into the stone, so the door would stay open. Someone has to do that again. Someone has to stand at the center and give—" Emrys's voice caught, and they steadied it the way they had learned to steady everything, with practice, with silence, with twenty years of hiding the tremor. "Give enough. Give truly. The lattice will take what's offered and weave it back through the Veil, and the remembering will flow again, and the stars will come back."

"Then we figure out what to give," Sable said briskly. "There are six of us—or four of us, counting what's left of the Brambles, and Marrow if he—" She stopped. Recalculated. Emrys watched her do it, watched the arithmetic of sacrifice run behind her eyes, and loved her fiercely for how fast she moved to stand between her people and the bill. "We pool it. Everyone gives a little. A memory each. Fair shares."

"No." Emrys said it gently. "It doesn't work in fractions. I can feel it in the stone—it wants a whole. One remembering, complete, given entire. Anything less and the weave has holes, and holes are how Veyra got in the first time." They turned in a slow circle, looking at the shattered pillars, the breathing frost, the empty socket with its glassy, melted-smooth rim. "One person gives everything. Everything they remember of—of what matters. Of the life the covenant is meant to protect."

Silence, except for the wind returning at the edges of the plateau, tentative, like a listener deciding whether it had heard correctly.

"No," Pip said.

"Pip—"

"*No.*" Louder now, cracking. "I know what you're doing. I've watched you do it for months. You do the math with yourself on one side of the scale every single time. You healed my mother's hands when you swore you didn't heal anymore. You took the watch every night so the rest of us could sleep. You're already choosing, aren't you? You're already—" Her voice broke entirely. "What would you even give?"

Emrys looked at her—at this fierce, frightened, lamp-hearted girl who had called her first lightning to save them all and then cried about the wolves afterward—and told the truth, because the place demanded truth, because the stones themselves seemed to lean in and require it.

"My exile," Emrys said. "Twenty years of it. Every day of Greyhollow. The market, the shop, the disguises, the running. The night the wards fell. The frost that spelled my childhood name. All of it—every memory I made in the dark, given back to the light that made them necessary." A breath. "And Tam. The memory of my brother. What he looked like. What his laugh sounded like. What it was to grow up beside him. If I give my exile, I give him with it, because he's woven all the way through it. I'd stand here afterward and not know why my hands were shaking. Not know whose face I was grieving."

The words came out steady, and that steadiness was the most frightening thing Emrys had ever produced.

Sable had gone very still. When she spoke, her voice was rough. "There has to be another way."

"There isn't. You know there isn't. You've known since the archive." Emrys met her eyes. "Veyra stole remembering and built a dynasty on it, and the worlds went dark. The only thing that ever held the door open was someone giving it away for free. That's the whole of the law. *No gift between kin shall be compelled.* Nobody can order this. Nobody can volunteer somebody else. It has to be—" they almost smiled— "*freely given.*"

"And you're free to give it," Sable said, "because you've been erasing yourself for twenty years already, so what's one more—"

"Sable."

"—no. No, I'm not done. You don't get to dress suicide up as generosity and call it the covenant. I have *journals*, Emrys. I write everything down. Everything. I have your mother's bread recipe in my bag right now, transcribed from your own mouth in a cave full of wolves, and if you think I'm going to stand here and let you walk into that socket and come out the other side a stranger to the people who—"

She stopped. The wind filled the pause.

"To the people who what?" Emrys asked quietly.

Sable's jaw worked. Behind her, Pip had both hands pressed over her mouth, and the frost-light moved on her face like water.

"To the people who love you," Sable said at last, furiously, as if the words cost money. "There. I said it. Are you happy? Now the dramatic gesture is ruined, because you know exactly what it costs, and it isn't yours alone."

Emrys hadn't known. That was the terrible, tender truth of it—they had calculated the price in memories and never once in mourners. They stood in the broken ring of the oldest promise in the world and felt the full weight of what Sable had just handed them, heavier than any pack, warmer than any amulet.

"I know," Emrys said. "I know it isn't mine alone. Nothing ever was. That's rather the point of a covenant." They looked around the ring—at the shattered pillars holding each other up, at Pip's wet furious eyes, at Sable clutching her journal like a shield made of paper. "I'm not deciding tonight. I want that understood. We rest. We eat. We read every word on every stone, because there may be something we've missed—there's always something we've missed, you taught me that. And tomorrow—"

The amulet flared.

Not warmly. Not gently. It blazed against Emrys's chest like a struck match, and the green-gold light in the covenant-script guttered all at once, every letter dimming as if a shadow had passed across a window, and the breathing frost of the ring stopped breathing.

Emrys spun toward the empty central socket.

The air above it had begun to move. Not wind—something older, something deliberate, gathering out of the frozen ground like heat-shimmer in reverse, coalescing into a shape that was almost a man: tall, robed in the fashion of an age no living person remembered, his features composed and composed again out of drifting frost, patient as architecture. Around his throat hung a chain of woven light—threads of gold-green memory, hundreds of them, thousands, glinting like a net full of captured stars.

He looked at them with eyes like the space between constellations, and when he spoke, his voice was the voice of a library burning slowly, page by page, and enjoying it.

"So," said the echo of Aldric Veyra. "Heirs arrive at last. Let us see what you are willing to believe."
