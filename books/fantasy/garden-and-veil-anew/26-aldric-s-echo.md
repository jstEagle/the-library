## 26. Aldric's Echo

The Stones did not want them there.

Sable felt it before she crossed the threshold of the shattered ring—a pressure against the skin of the world, like pressing her palm flat against a door that something on the other side was holding shut. The mountain wind died at the boundary as if sliced off with a knife. Beyond it, the air hung still and warm and wrong, smelling faintly of ink and candlewax, of old libraries, of a house she had not thought about in eleven years.

She stopped walking.

Emrys noticed at once. They had developed that habit between them over the long road—Emrys reading her hesitations the way she read contour lines, each of them fluent now in the other's silences. "What is it?"

"Do you smell that?" Sable said.

Emrys lifted their face to the dead-still air. Pip, a few paces behind with her hood up and her hands jammed in her pockets against a cold that had stopped bothering to be weather and become something more like intent, wrinkled her nose. "Smells like somebody's gran's parlor," she said. "Lavender and dust."

"Smoke," Emrys said quietly. "Beeswax. And underneath—" They didn't finish. Their hand went to the cracked storm-glass at their throat, an unconscious gesture Sable had catalogued weeks ago: *the amulet, when they're afraid; the amulet, when they're lying; the amulet, when they don't know which they're doing.*

"The archive smelled like this," Sable said. "The one where I found Veyra's ledgers. Which makes no sense, because we burned half of it getting out, and because we are four hundred miles from it, and because air does not carry memory." She heard her own voice go flat and precise, the cartographer's voice, the voice she used when the map disagreed with the ground. "Unless here it does."

They stood together at the edge of the ring of Covenant Stones—or what remained of them. She had imagined, from Emrys's descriptions and from the fragments in the archives, something like a temple: columns, altars, the ordered geometry of deliberate ruin. It was nothing so tidy. The Stones were vast slabs of a dark stone shot through with veins of something that caught the light and refused to give it back, driven into the mountainside at angles that suggested they had been standing when some enormous grief struck the world. Several had fallen. One lay split down its center, its two halves leaning against each other like tired travelers. And everywhere across their surfaces, where the strange veined light ran, Sable could see—she had to squint, had to turn her head, had to look the way you look at stars at the edge of vision—*threads*. Fine as spider silk, thousands upon thousands of them, running from stone to stone and up into the black sky where no stars answered.

The lattice. The covenant's body, cut open and left to bleed for two hundred years.

"It's beautiful," Pip whispered, and then, horrified at herself: "It's horrible. It's both. How is it both?"

"Because it was made with love and broken with greed," Emrys said. "Those leave different scars, but the same shape." They stepped forward, toward the threshold where the wind stopped, and Sable caught their sleeve without deciding to.

"Wait." She hadn't meant to say it aloud. All the way up the mountain she had been the one arguing for speed—*we're exposed on the switchbacks, Corvess's outriders were a day behind us at the pass, every hour we lose is an hour she gains*—and now here she was, the anchor dragging. But the smell of beeswax and lavender sat in her chest like a swallowed stone. "Something lives in there. Something that already knows us. Did your mother's records say anything about a guardian?"

Emrys was quiet a moment. Snow had gotten into the grey at their temples over the past weeks, or perhaps the grey had simply spread; either way, in the flat light of the starless sky they looked older than Sable knew them to be and younger than they pretended. "My mother wrote that the covenant was not a wall but a conversation. That the Stones listened. She never wrote about anything that *answered*." A pause. "But my mother also wrote that Aldric Veyra wove himself into the covenant's bones when he broke it. If any part of him survived the sundering—if he left a lock on the door he picked—" They looked at Sable, and their eyes were steady and very tired. "Then yes. Something answers."

"Great," said Pip. "Love that. A haunted rock circle guarded by the ghost of history's worst man. Really glad we walked three weeks uphill for this."

"You didn't have to come," Sable said, out of reflex, and Pip gave her the look—the sixteen-year-old look, the one that said *you keep saying that and I keep ignoring it, at what point do we acknowledge neither of us believes it.*

"Yeah, all right," Pip said, and shouldered past them both into the stillness.

* * *

Inside the ring, sound behaved strangely. Their footsteps made noise but the mountain did not carry it back; there was no echo, as if the Stones drank every sound the way the veins drank light. Sable found herself walking close to Emrys, closer than the terrain required, and told herself it was tactical.

The center of the ring held a basin of that same dark stone, shallow as a wading pool, dry as ash. Around its rim, characters were carved in a script older than any Sable had ever charted—and she had spent her life charting scripts, dead languages pressed into borderstones and ruined bridges, the graffiti of vanished peoples. These characters moved. Not much. A shiver, a slow rotation, like letters seen through deep water.

Emrys knelt at the rim and went very still.

"What does it say?" Pip asked, crouching beside them.

"I can't—it's not reading like language. It's reading like—" Emrys's breath caught. "Like someone talking in their sleep. Like it's dreaming." They reached out, hesitated, and Sable watched them weigh the cost of every touch these days, the amulet's crack a tax collector always at their shoulder. Then they laid two fingers against the rim.

The threads above them shivered.

Every vein of light in every Stone flared at once—not bright, exactly, but *present*, the way a room becomes present when a lamp is lit—and the stillness inside the ring turned over like a sleeper. Sable felt it against her skin, a attention, a vast and patient regard settling over the three of them the way snow settles: slowly, totally, from all directions at once.

And then a voice, from nowhere, from the stone, from inside her own skull, warm as a hearth and smooth as poured wax:

***Welcome home.***

Sable's whole body went cold.

She knew that voice. Not the words—she had never heard the words—but the *warmth*, the practiced geniality of it, the tone of a man who has spent decades learning how to make a room trust him. She had read enough of Veyra's correspondence in the ruined archive to know the shape of his sentences even stripped of sound. This was that shape. This was Aldric Veyra's warmth, preserved like a fly in amber, wearing the covenant's bones as a mouthpiece.

"Oh," said Pip, very small. "Oh, I hate this."

"You are weary travelers," the echo said, and now it seemed to come from everywhere, from the stones, from the ground, from the space just behind Sable's ears, "and you have climbed so far, and given so much. Let me give something back. It is the least hospitality requires." A pause, delicate as a held breath. "Let me show you what you want most."

"No," Emrys said sharply. "We know what you are. We've read what you did. We're not—"

But the world had already changed.

* * *

Sable kept her eyes open. That was the first thing, the discipline she clung to: *eyes open, feet planted, name yourself.* She was Sable Vane. She was thirty-four years old. She was standing in a ring of stolen light on a mountain that had forgotten winter, and everything that happened next was a lie with excellent craftsmanship.

It didn't help.

Because the lie was Saltmarsh.

Not the Saltmarsh of the maps—she had drawn that town a hundred times from memory and from other people's accounts, and her versions were always slightly wrong, the harbor too neat, the gull-cries missing—but the real one, the one that existed only in the sealed room behind her ribs. The crooked lane up from the quay where the cobbles wore smooth as whalebone. Her father's chandlery with its blue door gone soft and chalky from salt wind. The smell of pitch and tarred rope and, under it, the bread from Mrs. Callow's oven that her father bought on Sundays and carried home balanced on one forearm while she walked backward in front of him asking questions about everything.

Her father stood in the doorway of the chandlery.

He was mending a net. He was always mending a net; his hands were never empty, knots moving through his fingers like water. He looked up when she came into the square—he looked up the way he used to, without surprise, as though she had only been gone an hour, as though eleven years and a continent and a war of silence between them were nothing but the length of a walk to the well—and he smiled, and said, in the voice she had been trying for a decade to stop hearing in dreams:

"There you are, love. You'll want to see the charts I found. Charts of the far coast. Proper ones. I knew you'd come back for them eventually."

*Eyes open,* Sable told herself. *Feet planted. Name yourself.*

But oh, the craftsmanship. The gulls wheeled and cried exactly right. The light came off the water at the exact angle of late afternoon in autumn, gold going to copper, the tide halfway out so the boats leaned on the mud with their reflections folded under them. Somewhere a dog barked twice and stopped. Her father's hands moved through the net-mending, and there was the scar on his thumb from the gutting knife, the one she'd traced with a finger when she was six and asked if it hurt, and he'd said *only when I'm stupid, and I'm stupid about once a week.*

She had left him a letter. She had left him a letter and taken the coach north with her satchel of maps and her scholarship and her certainty that a girl who wanted to chart the unmapped edges of the world could not do it from a town whose edges were all water, and he had written back once, stiff and hurt and loving, and she had written back twice, and then the roads closed, and then the years closed, and then the silence got so heavy that neither of them could lift it alone and neither would ask.

He set down the net. He came toward her across the cobbles with his arms slightly open, the way he'd greeted her since she was small enough to run the last ten steps, and Sable—

Sable did not move.

That was the whole of her victory, and it cost everything she had. She did not move. She stood in the false gold light of a false afternoon in a town that existed now only in her head and in the memories of people who might be dead, and she let her father walk toward her and she did not take a single step to meet him, because she understood—with the terrible clarity that the echo's craftsmanship itself provided—that this was not her father. That her father, alive or dead, faithful or estranged, would never stand in a doorway mending nets while his daughter starved on a mountain. That love which asks nothing of you except to stay is not love; it is bait.

"You're crying," her father said gently, stopping an arm's length away. Close enough to touch. The echo had calculated that distance perfectly, the cruelest possible distance, near enough that refusing was violence. "Come inside, love. There's soup on. There's always soup on. You never have to leave again."

"That's how I know you're not him," Sable said. Her voice came out wrecked and she hated it and she kept talking anyway. "My father would never tell me I never have to leave. He'd tell me the tide tables and ask when I was sailing." She wiped her face with the heel of her hand, furious at the tears, furious at the echo for finding the exact seam in her and driving its wedge in. "You took the best thing I have and you made it a trap. That's all you ever did, isn't it? That's the whole of you. You find what people love and you weave it into chains and call it a covenant."

The false afternoon flickered. Just at the edges. The gull-cries stuttered like a skipped stitch.

Somewhere beyond the illusion, faintly, she heard Pip scream.

* * *

Sable tore her eyes open—when had they closed?—and the ring of Stones snapped back around her, black and veined and starless, the false Saltmarsh peeling away like wet paper. Her knees nearly went. She locked them. Across the basin, Emrys was on their feet with frost crawling off them in spirals, shouting at the air, and Pip—

Pip was on her knees in the snow with her hands pressed over her ears and tears cutting tracks down her face, and the air around her shimmered with something Sable couldn't see, some vision tailored to a sixteen-year-old lamplighter who missed her mother, and Sable understood with a lurch that the echo wasn't finished, that it was working them one by one, that it had started with her precisely because she was the skeptic and breaking the skeptic first made the others easier—

"Pip!" She crossed the basin in six strides and dropped beside her and gripped her shoulders. "Pip, look at me. It's not real. Whatever you're seeing, it's not real, it's a lure, it's Veyra's ghost fishing with your own heart as bait—"

"She's *better*," Pip sobbed. "She's not sick, she's up and she's making tea and she asked me to fetch the lamp oil and she *smiled at me*, Sable, she hasn't smiled since before—" Her voice cracked apart. "I know it's not real. I *know*. Why does knowing not help?"

"Because it's built from the true thing," Sable said fiercely, shaking her once. "Listen to me. It can only copy what's real. The fact that it hurts this much means your mother is real and loved and worth missing. Don't let him counterfeit her. Don't let him spend her like coin. She's not his to spend."

Pip's breath hitched—once, twice—and then she dragged in a lungful of the cold mountain air and screamed at the top of her considerable lungs, at the stones, at the echo, at two hundred years of thieved light:

"SHE'S NOT YOURS!"

The shimmer around her shattered like ice on a pond.

* * *

For a moment the ring was silent, truly silent, the silence of held breath. The threads overhead hung motionless. Sable knelt in the snow with her arm around Pip's shaking shoulders and watched Emrys stand at the basin's rim, frost melting off their sleeves, their expression unreadable—and she realized she didn't know what the echo had shown them. She didn't know what shape her friend's deepest want wore, and Emrys, who had spent twenty years practicing invisibility, was not going to volunteer it.

"What did it offer you?" Pip asked hoarsely, wiping her face.

"A version of me that never called the storm," Emrys said quietly. "A Greyhollow where I stayed invisible forever and everyone I've met on this road stayed safely strangers." A pause. "It was very peaceful. That's how I knew it was a lie. My life has never once been peaceful, and I wouldn't trade the noise."

"And you?" Pip turned to Sable, red-eyed, merciless in the way of the young. "What did it show you?"

Sable looked at the fallen Stone, at the split halves leaning together, and decided that after the mountain they had all climbed, they could survive one truth. "Saltmarsh," she said. "My father. Mending nets in the door of the chandlery, asking me to stay." She swallowed. "I told it he'd never ask me to stay. I hope to God I was right. I haven't spoken to him in eleven years, and if I was wrong—if I invented a braver version of him to hide behind—" Her voice failed, and she finished anyway. "When this is done, whatever happens with the covenant, I'm going south. I'm going to find out."

"That's not temptation," Pip said. "That's just being brave later."

"Most courage is," Sable said.

Then the stones spoke again, and the warmth was gone from the voice. What remained was colder and older and infinitely more tired, and it said:

***Two hundred years, and no one has refused me before.***

The veins of light gathered, drew inward, condensed—and out of the basin rose a figure woven entirely of thread and dim glow, tall and robed, its face a suggestion rather than a feature, its posture the posture of a man who had ruled the world and found even that insufficient. It regarded them with what might have been curiosity, or hunger, or the last flicker of something that had once been human.

***You seek to reforge what I unmade. Very well. But the covenant takes a price, little thieves, and I intend to make certain you understand the ledger before you sign it. Come. Let me show you what your friend Emrys's mother actually agreed to—***

The figure turned, threads whispering, and began to sink back down into the basin, drawing the light with it like a tide going out, and beneath the basin's rim the shifting characters resolved at last into words that Sable could read, and the first line of them made her blood run cold:

*Herein is recorded the true account of the sundering, set down by Aldric Veyra, who confesses what none living dare—*

"He kept a confession," Sable breathed. "He wrote it *down*." She was already moving toward the rim, already pulling out her journal, the cartographer's reflex overriding every alarm. "Emrys—your mother searched for proof her whole life. It's been sitting under our feet the entire time."
