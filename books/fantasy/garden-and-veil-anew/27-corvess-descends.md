## 27. Corvess Descends

The mountain had a name once. Corvess had read it in a ledger so old its ink had gone the color of dried blood: *Karvenhal*, the Warden tongue's clumsy rendering of something older, something that meant *the place where two skies touch*. The founders' maps still labeled it that way, in faded copperplate, as though cartography could hold what the covenant could not.

She stood at the head of her army on the frozen shoulder of the pass and looked up at it, and thought: *I will give you back your name, old thing. I will give everything back its name.*

Four thousand soldiers climbed behind her in a river of grey wool and black iron, their breath rising in a single plume that hung over the column like a banner no herald had designed. Lanterns bobbed along the line all the way down into the valley, where the baggage trains crawled, where the siege engines groaned on sledges, where the outriders with their signal-mirrors flashed white pulses against the darkening slopes. Four thousand. She had wanted more. The Council had flinched at the cost, at the audacity of marching an army out of Greyhollow while the city starved and muttered—but Corvess had not become High Warden by asking permission twice.

And besides, the Council did not know what she knew. The Council still believed the darkened sky was weather. That the vanished stars were smoke, or omen, or the gods turning their faces away in disgust at hedge-witches and charm-sellers. Let them believe it. Belief was a leash, and Corvess had spent her whole life learning how leashes were made.

"High Warden." Captain Dree reined up beside her, snow crusted in his eyebrows, his voice pitched low enough that only she could hear it over the wind. "Scouts report movement near the summit. Small party. Six, perhaps seven. They've taken shelter in the old pilgrim caves."

Corvess did not turn her head. She watched the summit instead, where the last light was dying off the broken crown of Karvenhal, where the shattered Covenant Stones must have stood for twenty years like pulled teeth in a jaw. Somewhere up there, Ashgrove was climbing toward them with a smuggler, a child, and whatever ragged truth they'd scraped together from the ruins of the archive at Vellmark. Sable Vane's name had surfaced in the interrogations three days ago—a mapmaker, of all things, a woman who drew lines for a living and had apparently drawn one straight through the heart of the Warden's oldest secret.

"They'll come down to us," Corvess said. "Or we'll go up to them. Either way, they are already mine."

"And the brother?"

The question landed exactly where Dree intended it to land, which was why she kept him. He was not clever enough to be dangerous and just clever enough to be useful, and he understood that loyalty in the Wardens was not a virtue but a survival strategy worn openly, like armor.

"Captain Ashgrove is with the vanguard," she said. "Where he has been since Vellmark. Where he will remain until I release him." She paused, then added, because even a leash should be checked for fraying: "He serves. Whatever he feels about it is his own affair, carried out in private, like grief."

Dree grunted and turned his horse back down the line, bawling orders about campfires and watch rotations. Corvess let him go.

*Whatever he feels about it.* She almost smiled. Twenty years ago, a boy of nineteen had stood in a bare room in the Hall of Inquiry with frostbite eating his fingers and terror eating everything else, and he had said the words he'd been given to say, and those words had sent his sister into exile and bought the Wardens the silence they needed. Corvess had read the transcript a hundred times. She knew the pauses in it better than she knew her own confession. There was a moment—recorded faithfully by some clerk who probably never thought about it again—where Tam Ashgrove was asked whether he saw his sister call the storm, and the transcript read simply: *[pause]*. Eleven seconds of nothing. Then: *Yes.*

Eleven seconds. She had built an entire strategy around eleven seconds. Because a man who hesitated for eleven seconds before condemning his own blood was a man who could be moved, and a man who could be moved was a man who would do anything—*anything*—to keep from being moved again. Guilt made the finest garrison stone. She had walled Tam Ashgrove up inside his own lie for two decades, and he had held, because holding was the only thing left that felt like penance.

Now his sister was climbing the same mountain he was, and Corvess intended to be standing between them when they met.

* * *

They made camp at dusk in the bowl below the final ridge, four thousand fires blooming across the snow like a second, uglier sky. Corvess walked the perimeter alone, as she always did on the first night in new ground. Soldiers nodded as she passed; some bowed. A few looked away quickly, and she catalogued each of those faces without breaking stride. Fear was fine. Fear was fuel. But the ones who wouldn't meet her eyes tonight were the ones who'd heard the stories—the village of Hollowmere where every soul stood frozen mid-task, the colorless country beyond the Ashline, the bells that rang with no one to ring them—and were beginning to wonder whether the woman leading them into the high winter was marching toward the enemy or toward the thing that had eaten the enemy whole.

Let them wonder. Wonder kept men awake, and awake men kept watch.

At the northern edge of the camp she stopped, because the wind had changed.

It came down off the summit carrying something that was not cold—or rather, something that was cold in a way cold had never been before, a cold with texture, with intention, like a hand pressed flat against the back of her neck. The sentries nearest her shifted uneasily. One of them, a boy hardly older than Tam had been in the inquiry room, whispered a prayer to saints whose names the Wardens had officially struck from the rolls forty years ago. No one corrected him.

Corvess closed her eyes and reached inward, down through the layers of herself, to the place where the threads lived.

They were restless.

She had inherited them the way one inherits everything in the Wardens—not by gift but by succession, passed from mother to daughter in a rite disguised as an ordination, woven into her bloodline so deep that she could not remember a time before she could feel them. Threads of memory, hundreds of them, thousands: the taste of bread from a farm that burned before her grandmother's grandmother was born, a lullaby in a language no living throat remembered, the exact blue of a sky over a realm that was not this one. Aldric Veyra had torn them from the covenant when he sundered it, and he had woven them into himself and his heirs as one weaves stolen gold into a hem—unseen, unspent, but heavy, always heavy, and warming.

For thirty years they had been her strength. When she faced the Council, she drew on the thread of a dead queen's unshakable certainty and spoke with a voice that brooked no argument. When she interrogated, she wore the patience of a monk three centuries drowned. When she needed to be feared, she borrowed the memory of a storm-god's wrath from a realm whose name existed now only in her blood.

Tonight they were singing. All of them, faintly, discordantly, like wires in wind. And beneath the song she felt the other thing she had felt since Vellmark, growing stronger with every mile north: a pulling. As if somewhere above her, at the top of this mountain, something was reeling in lines that ran through her body, and her body was leaning toward it whether she willed it or not.

*The Stones,* she thought. *They're trying to remember themselves. And I am wearing their memory like a coat.*

Her hand went to her collarbone, to the sigil there, cold through the leather of her glove.

Then let them try. She had not carried this weight thirty years to set it down on a mountainside because the weight had opinions. If the covenant wanted its threads back, it could negotiate. Everything negotiated. Everything had a price, and she had learned prices at her mother's knee, along with the family's first law, spoken over candlelight in a room the servants were forbidden to enter:

*What is freely given can be freely taken. Guard therefore what you have taken.*

* * *

Tam found her at the fire after midnight.

She heard him coming—she always heard him coming; the man had the grace of a falling bookcase and had never once managed to surprise her—and she did not turn until he stood at the edge of the light, snow in his dark hair, his cloak thrown open because Tam had never learned to dress for weather, only for war.

"You sent for me," he said.

"I sent for you hours ago."

"I was walking the line." He came closer and lowered himself onto the stone beside her fire, uninvited, which was another thing about Tam: he took small freedoms everywhere, as if freedom were a ration he was owed and perpetually shortchanged. Up close she could see the sleeplessness on him, ground into the skin under his eyes like soot. "The men are frightened. Hollowmere shook them. Half my company thinks we're riding to fight a witch and half thinks we're riding to feed us all to whatever emptied that village, and both halves are wrong, and neither half will tell me what they actually think, because I'm—" He stopped. His jaw worked. "Because of what I am to you."

"Ashgrove," Corvess said quietly. "Say what you came to say."

He stared into the fire. For a long time the only sound was the wind worrying the tent-ropes and, far above, that strange textured cold sliding down the slopes like water finding its level.

"She's up there," he said finally. "Emrys."

"Yes."

"You've known since Vellmark. You've known for weeks, and you didn't tell me."

"I told you what served the mission."

"*Served the mission.*" He laughed, and it was an awful sound, dry as old paper. "Twenty years I've served the mission. Do you know what the mission is, High Warden? The mission is that I get up every morning and put on this uniform and I don't think about a girl with lightning in her hands standing in the rain outside our house. The mission is that I don't think about the transcript. Eleven seconds—I know you know about the eleven seconds, everyone in the Hall knows, they probably teach it—eleven seconds where I tried to find any other word than yes, and couldn't, because Inquisitor Hale was looking at me the way you look at a door you're deciding whether to break." His voice cracked and he hated it, she could see him hating it, hating her for witnessing it. "You took that from me and you made it a career. And now she's here, and you're going to use her, and you're going to use me to do it, and you want to know the worst part?"

"Tell me," Corvess said, and her voice did not change at all, though the threads beneath her breastbone had gone tight as bowstrings.

"The worst part is I'd still do it. Whatever you ask. Because if I don't, you'll kill her, and if I do, maybe—" He stopped again. Pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes. "Maybe there's a version of this where she walks away. That's what you sold me in Vellmark, isn't it? Her life, for the Stones. That's the bargain you're building."

Corvess considered him for a long moment. The firelight moved on his face and made him briefly, unbearably young—the boy from the inquiry room, nineteen and frostbitten and drowning in a word he couldn't unsay.

"Yes," she said. "That is the bargain. And you will keep your part of it, Captain, because the alternative is that I take the Stones with her blood and yours spilled on them together, and nothing I have promised anyone in thirty years has ever failed to come due. Go and sleep. We climb at first light."

He rose. Stood a moment with his back to her, a big man making himself very still, the way men do when stillness is all that stands between them and something worse. Then he walked out of the firelight into the dark, and Corvess sat alone with the flames and the wind and the singing threads, and allowed herself, for exactly the length of one breath, to feel what she felt.

Then she put it away, as she put everything away, in the vaulted dark where the stolen memories lived, and went to give the order that would move four thousand soldiers up a holy mountain at dawn.

* * *

First light came grey and grudging, the sun a pale coin behind cloud, and the army of the Wardens began to climb.

They went up the pilgrim road, or what remained of it—steps cut into the rock a thousand years ago by hands that had believed two realms could be married, widened since for gun-carriages, scarred by avalanche, patched with timber. The column wound along the mountainside like a black vein. Banners snapped overhead: the crossed keys of the Wardens, the eye-and-wall of the Veyra line, Corvess's own standard, a silver thread knotted through a black ring. Signal-mirrors flashed from peak to peak. Drums kept the step, muffled with cloth so the sound rolled soft and enormous, like a heartbeat under the snow.

Corvess rode near the front, and the higher they climbed, the louder the threads sang.

By midmorning she could no longer pretend it was imagination. They pulled at her with every switchback—grief-stricken, joyful, furious, a chorus of ten thousand lives all straining toward the summit like iron filings toward a lodestone. Once, rounding a shoulder of black rock, she caught a scent on the wind that stopped her horse dead: woodsmoke and rosemary and rain on warm stone, a smell from a courtyard in a realm she had never seen, and her eyes filled with tears she had not authorized, and it took her a full minute to master her own face while her honor guard pretended very hard to study the horizon.

*They know,* she thought. *The threads know where home is, and I am dragging them up the mountain in chains.*

Good, she answered herself. Let them strain. Chains that strain are chains that hold.

Near noon the scouts came down at a gallop with news: the caves below the summit were empty. Cold ash, abandoned gear, boot-prints heading upward—six sets, maybe seven, fresh within the day. And carved into the ice above the cave mouth, in letters that had not melted though the afternoon sun had beaten on them for hours, a single word in a language the scouts did not know and Corvess, wearing a dead queen's memory, read without effort:

*COME AND SEE WHAT WAS TAKEN FROM YOU.*

She looked at it a long time. The wind moved her cloak. Below her, four thousand men waited on the word; above her, the broken crown of Karvenhal stood black against the white sky, and beyond it, past the last ridge, she could feel it—the Stones, the sundered Stones, humming like a struck bell that refused to finish its note.

Ashgrove was up there. The mapmaker, the smugglers, the lamplighter child. Her brother. And whatever truth they had dug out of the archive at Vellmark, sharpened into words, waiting.

Corvess drew her glove tighter and gave the order.

"Sound the advance. All companies. We take the summit by nightfall."

The drums changed. The column surged forward, and the army of the Wardens rang around the mountain like a noose drawing closed—and far above, at the top of the world, something ancient opened what remained of its eyes, and waited for her to arrive.
