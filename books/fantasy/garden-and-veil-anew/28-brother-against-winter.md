## 28. Brother Against Winter

The mountain had stopped pretending to be a mountain. Somewhere above the cloud-line, the Covenant Stones stood in their broken ring, and everything between Emrys and that ring was Warden steel—torches in ordered rows, siege-lanterns burning white, the long blue line of Corvess's army drawn across the saddle of the pass like a scar across a throat.

Emrys stood at the mouth of the last defile with snow to their knees and Pip's small hand gripping the back of their coat, and watched one rider come down out of the ranks.

He came alone. That was the first cruelty of it. No escort, no herald, no banner—just a man on a grey horse picking his way through the drifts with the unhurried care of someone who had done this ride before, in worse weather, for worse reasons. His armor was Warden-issue but unadorned: no rank-chains, no purity-seals, none of the gilded vanity the officers wore. The wind took his hood back as he descended, and there he was.

Tam.

Older than the brother in Emrys's memory, because memory had kept him seventeen forever—the lanky boy who could whistle through two fingers, who once carried Emrys three miles home on his back when they sprained an ankle on the salt flats, who had stood at the edge of the storm-field that night with his face lit orange by burning barley. This Tam was broad through the shoulders and hollow through the cheeks. There was frost in the creases around his eyes, though he couldn't have been more than thirty-seven. A captain's sword hung at his hip, and he wore it the way tired men wear tools.

He reined in ten paces away. Dismounted. Stood.

"Hello, Emrys," he said.

His voice was the same voice. That was the second cruelty. Twenty years, a false testimony, an army at his back—and underneath it all, the same low rasp that had read aloud to them through winter nights, stumbling over the hard words in the old tales, refusing to skip ahead even when Emrys begged him to.

Pip's grip tightened. "That's him?" she whispered. "That's your brother?"

"That's Captain Ashgrove," Emrys said, because it was easier than saying *yes*, and because some part of them wanted to see if the name would make him flinch.

It did. Barely. A twitch at the corner of his mouth, quickly mastered.

"You should send the girl back up the trail," Tam said. "What comes next isn't for children."

"I'm fourteen," Pip said, "and I've called lightning, so you can stuff what comes next."

Something moved behind Tam's eyes—not quite a smile, not quite grief. He looked at her a moment too long, the way men look at things they remember being. Then he turned back to Emrys, and the softness shut like a door.

"Corvess offers terms. You know them already. Come down off this mountain, give your blood to the Stones under Warden authority, and your brother—" He stopped. Corrected himself, flatly: "*I* live. Refuse, and I'm hanged at dawn for treason against the covenant I was sent to protect. She's made the arithmetic very simple for both of us."

"She's good at that," Emrys said. "Making simple arithmetic out of other people's lives."

"Yes." No denial. No defense. Just the word, dropped into the snow between them like a stone into a well, falling a long way before it stopped making sound.

Behind Emrys, Sable's boots crunched closer. They felt her presence at their shoulder—sharp, watchful, her map-case slung across her chest like armor. She said nothing. None of them did. The whole mountain seemed to be holding its breath; even the wind had dropped, and the silence of the high pass pressed in with a weight that had nothing to do with snow.

Tam took one step forward. Then another. Close enough now that Emrys could see the details the distance had hidden: a scar splitting his left eyebrow, new since the memory-version of him. Chapped lips. And his hands—his hands were shaking, just slightly, and he kept them fisted at his sides as if he could hold the shaking in by force.

"You look like her," he said suddenly. Roughly. "Mother. Around the eyes. I haven't—I don't let myself think about that, usually, and then you come walking out of a snowstorm looking like a ghost I helped bury, and I don't know what I'm supposed to do with that."

"You could start by telling the truth," Emrys said.

The words came out quieter than they'd intended. Not an accusation. Almost gentle. And that gentleness hit him harder than any blade could have—they saw it land, saw his jaw work, saw the fist at his right side open and close.

"The truth," he repeated. "You want to talk about truth. Fine. Here's mine: I was nineteen years old, and three people were dead in a burned field, and Warden inquisitors had me in a room with no windows for six days. Six days, Emrys. They didn't beat me. That's the thing nobody understands—they never touched me. They just *talked*. For six days. About Mother. About what would happen to her if the investigation concluded the storm was called deliberately by the Ashgrove children. About how a confession naming one guilty party—a single storm-caller acting alone—would close the case, protect the family, protect *her*. All I had to do was say the words they'd already written." His breath came out ragged, smoking in the cold. "And I believed them. Gods help me, I *believed* them. I thought I was saving us."

"I know," Emrys said.

"You don't—you can't—"

"Marrow told me. At the inn." Emrys stepped forward, and the snow took them to mid-shin, and they didn't care. "He told me about the six days. He told me you recanted it later—twice—and they threatened you into silence both times. He told me you've spent twenty years wearing their uniform trying to change what it does from the inside, and that you're the reason half the border villages still have their hedge-witches, because Captain Ashgrove keeps losing the paperwork on 'residual casters.'" Their voice cracked, and they let it. "I know, Tam. I've known for nine days. And I have been carrying twenty years of shame built on a lie, and you have been carrying twenty years of guilt built on a truth you weren't allowed to speak, and I am so *tired*, brother. I am so tired of us both carrying things we were never meant to carry alone."

Tam stared at them. The wind chose that moment to return, moaning up the defile, and somewhere far below, the army shifted—a sound like a giant turning over in its sleep, ten thousand small movements of metal and impatience.

"You should hate me," he said at last. It came out almost pleading. "It would be simpler if you hated me. Corvess expects you to hate me. The whole shape of her plan requires a brother who hates you, or a sister who hates me—one of us has to play the villain so the other can be leveraged, and I have spent three weeks trying to decide which role I'd rather die in."

"What if neither of us plays?"

"Then she kills us both and finds another heir."

"Then she kills us both," Emrys agreed, "and the covenant dies with us, and the long winter takes everything, and none of it will have mattered—not her stolen memories, not your sacrifice, not my exile, not Pip learning to call lightning in a cave while wolves circled. All of it wasted, Tam. Every cost paid for nothing." They spread their empty hands. "Or—we walk up this mountain together, and we put the truth where it belongs, in the Stones, in the lattice, in front of her whole stolen inheritance. And whatever happens after that, at least it happens *true*."

For a long moment Tam didn't move. Snow gathered on his shoulders. On the epaulettes he'd polished every morning for twenty years. On the Warden crest at his breast, the silver tower-and-veil that meant law, order, the suppression of wild magic—all the machinery that had eaten their family alive and called it justice.

"I testified against you," he whispered. It wasn't an argument anymore. It was a wound, opened at last after two decades of careful bandaging. "I signed the paper. I watched them put you on the exile-wagon. I *watched*, Emrys, and I told myself it was mercy, I told myself exile was better than the censure-block, I told myself—"

"I know what you told yourself." Emrys closed the last of the distance between them. Up close, he smelled of woodsmoke and cold iron and, faintly, impossibly, of the same soap their mother had used, the kind sold in the market at Harrow's Bend, and something in Emrys's chest tore cleanly along an old seam. "I told myself versions of it too. That the storm was my fault. That I'd earned the wagon. That if I'd been stronger, or slower, or better—twenty years, Tam, I built a whole life out of those sentences. Sold charms in a city that outlawed everything I was, and told myself invisibility was a choice." They reached out—slowly, giving him every chance to step back—and laid their palm flat against the Warden crest over his heart. Beneath the cold silver, his heart was hammering like a trapped bird. "But here's what I finally understand, standing here with the Veil dying overhead: shame is just love with nowhere to go. That's all it ever was. Yours and mine. Love, turned inward, eating us alive, because we were never allowed to say it out loud."

Tam made a sound. Small, broken, utterly unlike anything a Warden captain should make. His hand came up—hovered—then closed over Emrys's wrist, gripping hard enough to bruise, the way he'd gripped their wrist crossing the flooded ford at Millbrook when they were children, *hold on, little storm, hold on, I've got you.*

"I've got you," he said now, and didn't seem to know he was saying it.

"I know," Emrys said. "You always did. Even when it looked like betrayal. Especially then."

Above them, a horn sounded from the army's line—one long note, impatient, a question asked in brass. Tam's head snapped toward it, and Emrys felt the moment break over him like cold water: the captain surfacing through the brother, duty dragging him back under.

"They'll advance without me if I don't signal," he said hoarsely. "Corvess gave me until the horn sounds twice. After that, she comes down herself—with the vanguard, and the binding-chains, and she will not negotiate, Emrys. Whatever you think you can do up there at the Stones, you have minutes, not hours."

"Then help us have them."

"If I turn my back on that army, I'm a traitor twice over. If I don't—" His face twisted. "If I don't, I'm the man who chained his own sister on the mountain where our mother bound the worlds together. There is no version of the next hour where I come out clean. Don't you understand? There never has been. That's what I tried to tell the inquisitors, in the end. That's why they stopped talking and started writing."

"Then come out dirty," Emrys said. "Come out *human*. You've been clean for twenty years, Tam, and look what it bought you—frost in your face at thirty-seven and a heart that shakes worse than your hands. Come stand in the snow with us, on the wrong side of every law she wrote, and be my brother again. That's all I'm asking. Not forgiveness—I gave that away on the road, somewhere between the Hollow Bells village and the cave, when I finally understood you'd been a child too. Just—stand with me. One hour. One mountain. Let it be true."

The horn sounded again. Twice this time.

Down the slope, the army began to move—a slow, grinding shift, torchlight rippling along the ranks like fire through dry grass. Somewhere in that machine, Corvess was watching. Emrys felt it the way they felt storms: pressure behind the eyes, a taste of iron on the air. She had seen her captain dismount. She had seen him stand unarmed in the snow with the traitor storm-caller. Whatever happened in the next sixty seconds, she had already begun recalculating.

Tam looked at the advancing lines. Then at Emrys. Then—at last, longest—at Pip, who had crept forward during the conversation and now stood at Emrys's elbow with her chin up and her fists glowing faintly, stubbornly blue, exactly the way a lamplighter's daughter ought to stand when the dark got too big.

"You called lightning," he said to her. "In a cave. Your first time."

"Wolves," Pip said. "There were a lot of wolves."

"And you're fourteen."

"Fifteen in spring."

Tam laughed. It was a terrible sound—half sob, half wonder, wrenched out of him like a splinter from deep flesh—and he pressed the heel of his hand against his eyes for a moment, and when he lowered it, something had changed in his face. Something had come loose. Twenty years of held breath, leaving all at once.

"Mother would have liked you," he told Pip gravely. "She liked stubborn."

"Everyone says that like it's a compliment," Pip muttered, but she was grinning.

Tam turned back to Emrys. Slowly—so slowly it hurt to watch—he drew the captain's sword from its scabbard. Emrys tensed; beside them, Sable's hand went to her knife. But Tam only drove the blade point-first into a snowdrift, deep, until the crossguard stood bare above the white like a grave-marker. Then he pulled the Warden crest from his breast—the silver tower-and-veil, the sigil of everything that had broken them—and held it a moment in his open palm, snowflakes settling on the metal, melting, running off like small tears.

"Aldric Veyra stole the covenant," he said. Loudly. Clearly. In the carrying voice of a man who had commanded border garrisons for two decades and knew exactly how far sound traveled on a mountain wind. "I have seen the archives. I have seen the threads woven into the Veyra bloodline, generation upon generation, memories that were never given—only taken. And I, Tam Ashgrove, testify before these Stones and before every soul on this mountain: the testimony I gave at nineteen was coerced, false, and written by the Wardens' own hands. Everything built on it is rotten. Everything built on *that*—" he hurled the crest down into the snow at the army's edge, where it landed with a bright, ringing sound that somehow carried to the front rank, "—is theft wearing a uniform."

The army had stopped moving. Ten thousand soldiers stood in the trampled snow, staring up at their own captain standing weaponless beside the exiled storm-caller, and the silence coming off them was not the silence of obedience. It was the silence of people hearing, for the first time, a thing they had suspected in the privacy of their own hearts for years.

Far up the slope, at the head of the vanguard, a figure in white-and-silver raised one gauntleted hand, and the front ranks parted like water around a blade.

High Warden Corvess began to descend.

"Oh no," Pip breathed. "Oh no, oh no—"

"Sable." Emrys didn't take their eyes off the descending figure. Their heart was slamming, but their hands, strangely, had gone calm—the specific calm of the storm-caller standing at the center of the weather, where nothing moves except everything. "Take Pip. Start up the trail. Tam—"

"I know." He was already pulling off his gauntlets, dropping them in the snow beside his sword. His bare hands, when he flexed them, had stopped shaking. "Buy you time. However much I can. Go, little storm. Climb."

They climbed. All of them—Sable hauling Pip by the sleeve, Tam striding at the rear with his empty hands open at his sides, facing down the slope, facing the descending white-and-silver figure and the army behind her, a single unarmed man planted in the snow like the last fence post before the flood.

And Emrys ran upward through the screaming wind toward the broken ring of the Covenant Stones, toward the dying Veil, toward the mother-shaped absence at the top of the world—carrying, at last, nothing but the truth.

Behind them, faint beneath the wind, they heard Corvess's voice reach Tam, cold and clear as the ice it resembled:

*"Captain Ashgrove. Step aside."*

And Tam's answer, thrown back into her teeth with twenty years of held breath behind it:

"No."
