## 29. The Stolen Threads

Sable Vane had spent her whole life believing that maps were the truest form of memory—that ink on vellum held a place more faithfully than any mind could, because paper did not grieve and did not forget. She had drawn coastlines that no longer existed and called it history. She had charted the Ashline where the wards fell, mapping a wound in the world as though cartography could make sense of it.

Nothing had prepared her for the inside of the covenant lattice.

They descended into the Stones through a fissure Emrys's storm-glass opened—a seam of pale light splitting the black granite like a vein of frozen lightning—and what waited below was not a chamber but a sky. That was the only word Sable's mind would accept. They stood on a ledge of translucent stone above an endless dark, and through that dark ran threads.

Thousands of them. Tens of thousands.

They hung in the void like the rigging of some impossible ship, silver-white filaments stretching from horizon to horizon, each one faintly luminous, each one humming when the air moved. Some were thick as bridge cables, braided from hundreds of strands. Others were so fine they seemed spun from breath. And woven among them—this was the part that made Sable's hands go cold on her satchel strap—were knots. Clusters of dense, tangled light, like birds' nests built from stolen thread, each one anchored to the lattice by hooks of darker material that did not belong.

"That's not damage," Pip whispered beside her. The girl's lamplighter calluses were white-knuckled around her staff. "That's *added*. Someone wove those in."

"No," Emrys said quietly. Sable heard the sound they made—the small, controlled exhale of someone holding a door shut against a flood. "Someone *took*."

* * *

Sable had always been the one who looked closer. It was the cartographer's curse and gift: while others saw a forest, she saw the surveyor's error; while others saw a coastline, she saw where the mapmaker had fudged the inlet to flatter a duke. So while Emrys stood at the lattice's edge reading its architecture the way she read terrain, and while Pip hovered anxious and bright-eyed, Sable did what she always did.

She got closer. And she looked.

The nearest knot hung within arm's reach, a fist-sized tangle of light bound in dark cording. Up close, the threads were not abstract. Each filament carried texture—flickers of image moving along its length like reflections sliding over water. A woman's hands kneading dough in a kitchen with blue tiles. A boy running down a hill with his arms out against the wind. An old man dying in a bed while snow pressed against the window, and someone weeping, and the specific quality of that weeping, worn smooth by repetition—

Sable snatched her hand back.

"They're memories," she said. Her voice came out flat, which was how it went when she was most afraid. "Actual memories. Whole ones. Not echoes, not impressions—look at the grain of them. These are people's lives."

Emrys came to stand beside her. In the lattice-light their face looked carved from something older than flesh, the storm-caller's gray eyes reflecting ten thousand threads. "The covenant was memory freely given," they said. "Realm to realm. That was the binding. You give what you love about your world, and the other realm gives what it loves about yours, and the exchange is what holds the door open between them." Their hand rose toward the nearest dark-corded knot and stopped short of touching it. "These aren't given. Look at the bindings. They're *cut ends*. Someone harvested these threads and spliced them into the lattice to hold it together after they broke it."

"Veyra," Sable said.

"Aldric Veyra," Emrys agreed. "Four hundred years ago."

Pip made a small sound. When Sable glanced over, the girl had gone pale as the threads themselves. "But the histories say he *saved* the realms. The Concordance. He found the covenant failing and—he rebuilt it. That's what they teach. That's what the Wardens *are*." Her voice cracked on the last word, because Pip still wore her hedge-witchery like contraband, because every lesson she'd ever been taught had come wrapped in Warden authority. "He rebuilt it out of *stolen lives*?"

"He rebuilt it out of whatever was closest to hand," Sable said, and hated how steady she sounded. "Powerful men rarely run short of materials."

* * *

They worked along the ledge for what felt like hours, though time moved strangely under the Stones; Sable's pocket-watch ran backward twice and then simply stopped, its hands trembling like a compass near iron. Emrys taught them to read the lattice the way Sable read maps—by pattern, by flow, by the places where the design betrayed its maker.

And the design betrayed Aldric Veyra everywhere.

"The original covenant is still here," Emrys said, standing before a section of lattice where the threads ran clean and unbroken, silver all the way through, no dark cording anywhere. "Feel it. This part sings." And it did—when Sable laid her palm near those threads, a chord moved through her sternum, low and warm, a sound like a hearth in a house she'd lost. "Freely given memory has a different weight. It doesn't resist. It *holds*. But here—" they moved thirty paces down the ledge, to where the dark-spliced knots clustered thickest, "—here it groans. Here it's held together with grief and theft, and it's been straining for four centuries. This is why the Veil died. Not age. Not accident. Rot. The rot Veyra planted."

Sable pulled out her journal—she'd begun keeping a second one since the Ashline, a record of everything they found, because records were the only immortality she trusted—and began to sketch. The lattice defied ordinary notation. She ended up inventing symbols as she went: a circle for the clean covenant-threads, a barbed hook for the dark bindings, a spiral for the knots. Her margins filled with questions she couldn't answer and measurements she wasn't sure meant anything in a space where distance kept changing its mind.

It was on her third page that she noticed the dates.

Not dates, exactly—but sequences. The dark knots weren't random. They occurred in clusters, and the clusters repeated at intervals, and when she mapped the intervals against the timeline she'd reconstructed from the ruined archive two weeks ago—the archive where she'd first found proof of Veyra's sundering—the correspondence was exact.

"Emrys." She didn't look up from the page. "Come look at this. Now."

* * *

What she'd found was this: the thefts hadn't happened once. They happened every generation.

Each cluster of stolen threads corresponded to a moment of crisis in the historical record—a Veil-thinning, a famine attributed to "realm-sickness," a border war, a purge. And after each crisis, new knots appeared in the lattice, fresh-cut threads spliced into the structure, reinforcing it, keeping it standing a little longer. Keeping the *claim* standing.

"It's not a single crime," Sable said, and her voice finally shook, because this was the shape of it now, vast and terrible and perfectly ordered. "It's an inheritance. Veyra stole the first memories to build his power after breaking the covenant—and then he built the theft into the system itself. Every Warden generation since has fed it. Every crisis, every 'purge of residual casters,' every atrocity the Wardens ever committed—it was harvest. They weren't protecting the realms from magic. They were *farming* it."

The silence that followed had weight. Somewhere far off in the lattice-dark, a thread sang a broken note.

Pip sat down hard on the stone. "My mother," she said. Sable turned. The girl was staring at her own hands. "When I was seven, the Wardens came through our district doing 'attestations.' They took my grandmother in for questioning. She came back three days later and she never—" Pip's breath hitched. "She never remembered my name again. They said it was realm-sickness. They said the dying Veil takes minds sometimes. Everyone said that. It happens all over Greyhollow, old people forgetting, young people forgetting—" She looked up, and her eyes were wet and furious. "They've been *eating* us. For four hundred years. My grandmother's face is in there somewhere. Woven into their power like a *thread in a rug*."

"Pip—" Emrys started.

"Don't." The girl stood, wiping her face with the heel of her hand, fierce and fourteen and lit from within by something that had nothing to do with lightning. "Don't tell me to be calm. Don't tell me we'll fix it. Just—let me be angry for one minute, and then we'll keep going, because that's what you do, isn't it? You keep going." She picked up her staff. "One minute. Then we find the rest of it."

Sable thought: *I would follow this child into anything.* She wrote it in the margin of her journal, because it was true, and true things belonged in records.

* * *

They found the heart of it an hour later—or what Sable's watch, dead in her pocket, would have called an hour.

The central knot was not a knot. It was a *tree*. That was the only image her mind offered: a great dark trunk of braided cording rising through the lattice's center, roots plunging into depths below the ledge, branches spreading up into heights above it, and hanging from every branch, clusters of stolen memory like fruit gone silver with frost. The trunk pulsed slowly, rhythmically, and Sable realized with a lurch that it was beating. Beating like a heart. Like something alive that should never have been alive.

"This is the claim," Emrys said. Their voice had gone very quiet, the quiet of deep water. "This is what Corvess inherits. It's not just power—it's a *structure*. A living argument, woven from everyone they've ever taken. As long as this stands, the covenant can't be reforged honestly, because the lattice itself is poisoned. Any new binding would have to grow through this."

"Then we cut it down," Pip said immediately.

"And kill every memory inside it?" Sable asked, sharper than she intended. She pointed at the nearest branch-cluster, where images flickered along the threads: a wedding, a funeral, a child learning letters, a fisherman mending nets in gold morning light. "Look at them, Pip. Those are people. Dead people, mostly, by now—but their lives are *in there*, whole, preserved. Cut the trunk and we don't free them. We unmake them. There's no one left to give them back to."

Pip's face crumpled and reformed. "Then what do we do?"

Sable looked at Emrys. Emrys was staring at the tree with an expression Sable had learned to recognize on the road—the expression they wore when the cost of something was becoming visible, when they were counting in the only currency they had left. Since the storm-glass cracked, every calling took a memory. Sable had watched them lose the taste of bread. She'd written it down, hating herself a little for the relief she felt at having recorded it—because if Emrys wouldn't keep themselves, someone had to.

"There's another way," Emrys said slowly. "There has to be. The covenant's original principle—freely given memory—if that's the load-bearing truth, then the theft is the flaw. The whole structure fails wherever the theft is exposed. We don't cut the tree. We *name* it. We take the stolen threads back out of the weave and return them—"

"To whom?" Sable asked gently. "Most of them are dust, Emrys."

"Then to the lattice itself. To the covenant. Freely returned instead of freely given—the same current, reversed." Emrys's hand closed around the storm-glass amulet, and Sable saw the hairline crack in it catch the light. "It will cost. Everything costs. But it might work."

*Might,* Sable thought. *There's the word that kills people.* She said nothing. She opened her journal instead and began to draw the tree—all of it, root to crown, every cluster she could count, every sequence, every date-correspondence. If they failed, the record would remain. If they succeeded, the record would remain anyway. That was her faith, the only one she'd ever kept: that what was written down could not be wholly taken.

She was halfway through the crown of the tree when she saw it.

At the very top, newer than the rest—the cording still bright, the splices fresh—hung a cluster unlike the others. The threads there were not generations old. They were recent. Decades, perhaps less. And woven through them, unmistakable in the lattice-light, ran images Sable recognized: Greyhollow's night market. The charm stall with its crooked awning. A figure in a hooded coat selling luck-knots and weather-charms, face half-hidden, hands quick and careful.

Her own journal pages. Her own maps of the ward-failure. The interior of Marrow's inn, the Salt Road checkpoints, the cave where they'd sheltered from the wolves.

"Oh," Sable said. The word fell out of her like a stone down a well. "Oh, no. It's not just the dead. It's not just the past." She grabbed Emrys's sleeve. "Emrys. Look at the top of the tree. *Look at it.*"

Emrys looked. Sable watched the understanding arrive in their face the way frost arrives on glass—slowly, then all at once, spreading from the eyes outward until every line of them went rigid.

"She's been watching us," Emrys said. "Since Greyhollow. Since the market. Every step we've taken—"

"Not watching." Sable's mouth was dry. She was a cartographer; she understood instruments, and she understood what it meant when the instrument was also the trap. "Harvesting. She let us come. She's been *growing* this cluster the whole way—our route, our plans, our—" Her voice caught. "Our names. Our faces. We're not the rescue party, Emrys. We're the next crop."

Far above them, through miles of stone and snow, something shifted. Sable felt it in her teeth before she heard it: a tremor in the mountain, rhythmic and deliberate, like footsteps. Like many footsteps, descending.

Pip's head came up. "Do you feel that?"

The lattice around them dimmed—not evenly, but in a wave, darkness rolling inward from the edges of the void like a tide going out. And in the sudden half-light, the great dark tree at the center of everything beat faster, its stolen hearts hammering, as if it too knew what was coming.

Sable closed her journal over her drawings of the tree, of the fresh cluster, of the names and faces woven into its newest branches, and said the thing none of them wanted said:

"Corvess is here. And she's brought the whole harvest home."
