## 30. Pip Gives Her Light

The Stones were colder than anything Pip had ever known, and she had grown up on rooftops in Greyhollow winters, where the cold came down the chimney like a debt collector and sat in your bones until spring paid it off. This was different. This cold had *intent*. It pressed against her skin the way a hand presses against a door, testing, patient, certain that sooner or later everything opens.

She stood between Sable and Emrys at the base of the shattered Covenant Stones, her breath pluming out in little ghosts that did not drift away but hung in the air, frozen mid-curl, as if even her breathing was being remembered by the mountain and filed away.

There were seven Stones once. She could see the stumps of them in the snow like broken teeth. Three still stood, leaning toward each other across a gap where the fourth had fallen, their surfaces carved so deep with script that the grooves held shadows even though there was no sun to cast any. The dark sky above—starless for weeks now, starless since that first terrible night in the market—pressed down close, close enough that Pip kept expecting to feel it on the crown of her head like a lid.

"It's worse here," she said, because someone had to say it. "The forgetting."

Nobody answered right away. Nobody needed to. They all felt it—the way the Veil-sickness thickened the closer they came to the wound in the world. Pip had lost her mother's face three days ago. She knew the shape of that loss now: a smooth place in her memory where something beloved used to be, like a patch of wall where a picture hung for years and left only paler paint behind. She knew her mother's face was gone from her mind, and she knew she had loved it, and the knowing hurt worse than the losing.

Emrys stood closest to the Stones, one hand raised, palm out, the way you'd approach a horse that might bolt. The storm-glass amulet at their throat caught no light because there was no light to catch, but Pip could see the crack running through it now, jagged as a river delta seen from above. Every time Emrys called even a whisper of storm, that crack widened, and somewhere behind Emrys's eyes another room emptied out.

"We need a beginning," Emrys said quietly. "That's what the covenant says. Look." Their finger traced the deep-carved lines without touching them. "*Memory freely given, realm to realm, thread to thread.* Freely given. Not taken. Not bought. Not—" their voice caught, just slightly, "—not stolen, the way Veyra stole it. The lattice is empty. Dead. It needs a first thread to start the weave, and I don't think mine will do. Mine's too tangled with guilt. Guilt isn't giving. Guilt is keeping."

"Then whose?" Sable asked. Her voice was flat, practical, the voice she used when she was frightened and refusing to show it. She'd been mapping the Stones for two days, sketching every groove, every fracture, cross-referencing the archive pages she'd nearly died for in Veyra's ruined capital. "Whose memory starts a covenant meant to bind two worlds?"

Pip looked at the empty lattice.

She didn't decide, exactly. That was the strange part, thinking back on it later—or trying to, because of course she couldn't, not all of it, not anymore. It was more like the moment when you're standing on a rooftop edge about to jump a gap between buildings, and your body decides before your fear can file an objection. She had spent her whole life lighting lamps other people depended on. Lamplighters' work was never about the lamp. It was about everyone walking home in the dark who needed to see the door.

"I do," she said.

* * *

They argued. Of course they argued. Sable argued loudest, which was how Pip knew Sable loved her; the cartographer had a whole taxonomy of sharpness, and the sharpest edges were reserved for the people she couldn't bear to lose.

"You're sixteen," Sable said. "You are *sixteen*, and you want to feed your memories into a dying piece of rock because—"

"Because it needs a bright thread," Pip said. "Emrys said so. Listen—you've read the covenant a hundred times. It says *freely given*. It doesn't say big. It doesn't say important. It says given." She turned to Emrys. "What kind of memory does it need? Tell me true."

Emrys's face did something complicated. Twenty years of hiding lived in that face, and Pip had learned to read it the way she read weather: the tightening around the eyes meant a storm of grief coming, the small twitch at the mouth corner meant a joke being strangled before it could escape. Now both happened at once, and neither won.

"Bright ones," Emrys said finally. "The lattice is choked with stolen things—memories ripped out of people, woven under duress. Corvess's whole claim is built on theft. To restart the weave, the first thread has to be clean. Joyful. Given without reservation." A pause. "It will take more than one. But one clean thread might buy us time until—"

"Until Corvess comes," Sable said bitterly. "Which is soon. Which is why we should be planning the defense, not—"

"There's no defense," Emrys said gently. "You know that. An army is coming up this mountain, and we have no army. We have truth, and we have each other, and we have whatever the Stones will accept. Time is the only weapon we have left. Time for Tam. Time for Corvess to be offered what she actually wants, if she even knows what that is anymore."

Pip raised her hand like she was in school, which made Sable make a sound that was half laugh, half sob.

"What does it cost?" Pip asked. "Exactly. Don't soften it. You softened it with me about the wolves and I wet myself anyway, so softening clearly doesn't work."

Emrys almost smiled. Then the smile went away, and what was underneath was old and tired and honest.

"You choose what to give. The Stone takes it whole—not the memory itself, exactly, but your *holding* of it. You'll know you had it. You'll know it mattered. You'll remember that you gave it. But the warmth of it, the color—that goes into the covenant. Forever. And it can't be undone, and it can't be taken back, and I won't lie to you: some givers, in the old days, gave too much and hollowed themselves out. The village in the valley, the frozen one? That's what happens when the taking isn't free. When it *is* free—" Emrys spread their hands. "I honestly don't know. No one's ever done this on purpose. That's rather the point."

Silence, except the wind moving through the broken Stones, making a sound like slow breathing.

"I'll do it," Pip said again.

"Pip—"

"Sable. Sable, look at me." Pip took the older woman's hands. They were chapped and ink-stained and shaking slightly, and Pip held them the way she'd hold a lamp flame cupped against wind. "My mum's face is already gone. The Veil took it while we were walking, and I didn't get to choose, and I didn't get to say goodbye to having it. That's what the long winter *is*—it takes and takes and nobody gets to give anything back. Well." Her voice cracked, and she let it. "I'm sixteen years old and I'm going to be the first person in twenty years to give something back. On purpose. Because I choose to. You can't map a path for me out of this one, Sable. I've already found it."

Sable pulled her into a hug so fierce it cracked a rib feeling, and whispered into her hair, in the tone of someone making a threat and a promise in the same breath: "I am writing down *everything*. Everything you give. Every detail anyone remembers of it. So that someday, when the world is fixed, someone can hand it back to you."

"That's cheating," Pip said into her shoulder.

"That's *cartography*," Sable said. "Now tell me what you're going to give, so I can start."

* * *

They built a small fire at the base of the central Stone, because Emrys insisted that giving should happen in warmth, surrounded by witnesses, the way covenants were meant to be made—not alone in the dark like thefts. Marrow would have liked that, Pip thought. The ex-Warden deserter had stayed behind at Lastlight to hold the pass, buying them hours, and Pip sent a silent wish up the mountain to him: *hold on, we're doing it, we're starting.*

Tam was somewhere below with Corvess's army. That thought sat in Pip's chest like a swallowed stone. Emrys's brother, alive all along, carrying twenty years of a lie he'd been forced to tell. Pip had watched Emrys learn that truth and then watch Emrys refuse to hate him, and she still didn't fully understand it, but she understood it was the bravest thing she'd ever seen, braver than wolves, braver than lightning.

She knelt before the Stone. Up close, the carvings moved—not really, not physically, but her eyes kept sliding off them the way eyes slide off a star when you look straight at it. The lattice inside was visible now that she knew to look: a vast dim webwork threading through the Stone and out through the ground and up into the dead sky, threads snapped and frayed and gray as old cobwebs.

"How do I start?" she asked.

"Put your hand on the Stone," Emrys said. "And give. That's all. There's no word, no ritual. The old covenant was made by farmers and fisherfolk and children, Pip. Ordinary people, giving ordinary joys. That's what it was *for*. It was never meant to be a king's working. It was meant to be everyone's."

Pip put her hand on the Stone.

The cold bit through her palm, then stopped biting, and became instead a kind of attention—a vast, patient listening, like pressing your ear to a door and hearing something enormous breathe on the other side.

*Give,* said something that was not a voice, or was every voice at once, worn smooth by ages of use.

Pip closed her eyes.

She went looking for her brightest things, and found them easily, because she had always kept them polished. Here: the morning her mother let her light the first lamp of her own route, seven years old, standing on tiptoe on the lamplighter's ladder, and the wick catching with a sound like a tiny satisfied sigh, and her mother's hand steady on the ladder, and the whole street waiting for her light, and her lighting it, *her*, and the windows of the houses warming gold one by one as dusk came down. Here: the night of the Frost Fair, dancing with Sable on the ice while the smugglers cheated tourists at a rigged ring-toss and everyone pretended not to notice. Here: the first time she'd called a spark into her bare hand, hedge-witch magic, illegal and secret and *hers*, a little captive star trembling between her fingers while her heart hammered with the purest joy she'd ever felt.

Here: her mother's laugh. Her mother humming while she stirred porridge. Her mother's hands kneading bread, flour to the wrists—

No. That one was already gone, taken by the Veil-sickness, a pale patch on the wall. She couldn't give what she no longer had. Grief flickered through her and she let it pass through, because grief wasn't what the Stone wanted, and holding onto it here felt like bringing mud into a temple.

*I give these,* she thought, and meant it with everything in her, every scrap of her stubborn lamplighter soul. *Freely. First. Before anyone makes me. Before winter takes them the way it took Mum's face. They're mine and I give them away and that makes them stronger, not weaker. That's the trick, isn't it? That's the thing nobody knew.*

*Light isn't lessened by being shared. That's the whole secret of lamps.*

She pushed.

The Stone drank.

It was not pain. It was the opposite of pain, which was somehow worse—warmth flooding up her arm, golden and gentle, and with each pulse of it something lifted out of her, unhurried, almost tender, like a parent lifting a sleeping child. She felt the first lamp-lighting go. She felt the Frost Fair dance go. She felt the little captive star in her palm go, and with it went the *color* of those moments, the warmth, the taste—and what remained were the shapes, the facts, the knowledge that she had been happy, that she had given gladly, that it had mattered.

She heard herself making a sound. Singing, maybe. Or laughing. Or crying. Probably all three.

Somewhere far away Sable was shouting her name, and Emrys was saying something urgent, and Pip thought: *no, wait, I'm fine, I'm fine, look—*

Because the lattice was waking up.

Thread by thread, the gray webwork flushed with color—her colors, lamplight-gold and frost-fair blue and hedge-spark white—running outward through the Stone, down into the mountain, out along lines she could suddenly feel the way she felt the routes of her lamp rounds, familiar paths in a dark city. The webwork reached for the sky. It couldn't reach it yet—it strained and fell short, a bridge missing its last span—but oh, it *glowed*, and the glow pushed back the pressing dark by a handspan, a wingspan, a held breath.

And the cold hand against the door of the world paused. Tested again. Found the door *warmer* than expected.

Pip came back to herself on her knees in the snow, Sable's arms around her, Emrys's hand on her forehead. Her body felt wrung out and light, like laundry after a hard day's work. She looked up at the Stone, blazing softly with borrowed gold, and at the lattice running out into the dark like roots of fire, and she started to laugh, breathless and disbelieving.

"It worked," she said. "Did you see? Did you *see*?"

"I saw," Emrys said, and their voice was wrecked, and they were crying without any shame about it, tears freezing on their cheeks. "Pip. Child. What you just did—no covenant-heir, no founder, no Warden ever did that. They all tried to *take* the power. You *gave* it. Do you understand what you've shown us? What you've shown *me*?" They gripped her shoulders. "I've been preparing to sacrifice. To pay. To atone. And you just taught me it was never a payment at all."

"What was it, then?"

"A gift," Emrys said. "It was always supposed to be a gift."

Sable was already writing, furiously, by the Stone's new light, narrating as she wrote so Pip would have it later: "The first lamp, age seven, the wick catching, the street going gold window by window—" Her voice broke. She steadied it. "Frost Fair. Dancing. The smugglers cheating. Your first spark, in your bare hand, the little star." She looked up, eyes fierce and wet. "It's all here. Every color. Held for you. That's the deal, right? That's what we agreed."

"That's the deal," Pip said.

But even as warmth spread through the waking lattice, even as the three of them huddled together in the small circle of gold, Pip felt the edges of it—the strain. The bridge reaching for the sky and falling short. One child's bright handful of memories was a single lamp lit in a dark country. Beautiful. Not enough. The cold outside the circle was already leaning in again, patient, testing, and far down the mountain, faint on the wind, came a sound that did not belong to wind at all.

Bells. Marching bells. The army was climbing.

Emrys rose, and the storm-glass amulet at their throat caught the Stone's golden light, and the crack in it gleamed like a lightning-bolt drawn in miniature.

"They're here," Emrys said quietly. "Whatever happens next—we hold the circle. Whatever she offers, whatever she threatens—" they looked at Pip, and their eyes were full of her borrowed light, "—we remember what you taught us. We give. We don't pay. We *give*."

Down through the dark, the bells rang closer, and the first torches of Corvess's army crested the lower ridge like a scatter of false stars rising from below to meet the true ones that no longer shone.
