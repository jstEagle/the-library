## 31. Corvess Unraveled

The mountain had stopped pretending to be a mountain.

Corvess felt it as she climbed—the Stones' lattice humming through the soles of her boots, through the iron of her gauntlets, through the stolen threads coiled in her blood like sleeping serpents. For thirty years she had carried the Veyra inheritance without questioning its weight. Power was power. It did not ask where it came from. She had learned that lesson at her grandmother's knee, in a hall hung with the portraits of ancestors whose eyes all seemed to follow you, whose faces all seemed to share something—a sameness, a smoothness, like wax pressed from the same mold.

Now, standing at the threshold of the shattered Covenant Stones with an army at her back and the storm-caller Ashgrove before her, Corvess understood that the mold itself was the lie.

"You could have run," she said to Emrys. Her voice came out flat, official, the voice she used for proclamations. "I offered you your brother's life. That was more generosity than this family has ever been shown."

Emrys stood among their ragged company—the cartographer with frost in her lashes, the lamplighter girl swaying on her feet with that terrible new hollowness behind her eyes—and they did not look afraid. That was the thing Corvess had not prepared for. Twenty years of hunting residual casters, and she had catalogued every flavor of fear: the wet-eyed begging, the frozen defiance, the animal scramble. Emrys Ashgrove looked at her the way one looks at weather. Inevitable. Almost pitying.

"Your generosity," Emrys said quietly, "was built on a theft. You should know what it is you're defending before you defend it."

"I know exactly what I'm defending." Corvess spread her hands, and the stolen threads answered—cold light crawling up her forearms, the lattice groaning beneath her like a ship changing tack. "Order. Walls between worlds so the winter stays *out there*. You want to unmake the Veil because you think memory is some sacred commons. I've read what happens when realms bleed into each other, Ashgrove. I've seen the hollow villages."

"You've seen what *your founder* made," said the cartographer—Vane, sharp even half-frozen. "We found his archive. We found the ledgers."

"Ledgers." Corvess almost laughed. "You'd be amazed what rebels can forge when they need a villain."

But her certainty rang wrong in her own ears, a cracked bell. Because she had felt the ledger's truth already, hadn't she? Felt it in the way the lattice flinched beneath her feet. In the way the threads in her blood had grown restless these past months, tugging toward the Stones like dogs toward a burning house.

It was the girl who moved first. Pip—she knew the file, lamplighter's daughter, minor hedge-talent, flagged twice for unlicensed charm-work—who stepped forward on legs that barely held her and pressed both palms flat against the nearest Stone.

"I gave mine freely," the girl said. Her voice was thin but it carried, the way small bells carry. "That's how it works. Nobody told me. I just knew. Freely given, realm to realm. That's the whole covenant. That's *all* it is." She turned her head, and her eyes were fever-bright. "You can't hold it shut with stolen things, Warden. Stolen things don't knit. They just sit in you, heavy, waiting to go home."

Corvess raised a hand to signal her wardens forward—and hesitated.

It was a hesitation of half a heartbeat. But the lattice felt it. The threads in her blood surged toward the Stones, and for one vertiginous moment she saw them from the inside: thousands upon thousands of memories woven into the covenant's broken frame, generations deep, each one bright and specific and *grieving*. A woman's hands kneading dough in a kitchen that no longer existed. A boy running through summer grass under stars that still burned. A name spoken over a cradle, again and again, in a language dead three hundred years.

They were not hers. They had never been hers.

"What did you do to me?" she whispered, and hated the crack in her own voice.

"Nothing," Emrys said. "Aldric Veyra did it, thirty years before either of us was born. He wove the covenant's memory-threads into his bloodline and called it strength. And every heir since has carried strangers' lives in their chest and mistaken the weight for their own greatness." Emrys took one step closer, unarmed, snow gathering on their shoulders. "I can give them back. All of them. Your ancestors' true memories—not the polished legend, the *truth* of what he took and what he was. Freely offered. You only have to take them."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then you'll keep carrying them until they tear out on their own. The Veil is dying, High Warden. The threads are going home whether you release them or not. The only question is whether you're standing in the doorway when they leave."

Behind her, four hundred wardens waited on her word. Tam Ashgrove stood at their head, his hand nowhere near his sword, watching her with an expression she had never permitted herself to read before.

Corvess looked at the Stone. At the light moving beneath its surface like fish under ice.

She thought: *I am the last of the Veyra line. Everything I am was built to hold this.*

And then, quieter, in a voice that sounded horrifyingly like a child's: *What if nothing I am is mine?*

"Show me," she said.

* * *

Taking the memories was not like reading a book. It was like being read.

The first thread entered her and she was kneeling in a snowfield three centuries old, and Aldric Veyra was young, and afraid, and *right*—that was the horror of it, he had believed himself right—the long winter pressing at the borders, the realms bleeding sickness into each other, and the Covenant Stones glowing with a solution he refused because it required giving. Memory, freely given, realm to realm. He had watched the old covenant-keepers do it, watched them laugh as they poured their dearest recollections into the lattice like wine into shared cups, and he had thought: *weakness. Sentiment. A wall built by people too soft to build a door with a lock.*

So he had cut instead. She felt his hands do it—felt the Stones scream, felt the threads come away wet and writhing, felt him weave them into his own blood while his fellow founders wept and cursed him and were, one by one, silenced. She felt his triumph, and underneath the triumph, so faint he never once acknowledged it in sixty years of rule: the loneliness. A man who owned ten thousand lives and belonged to none of them.

The second thread was her grandmother, and here Corvess made a sound she did not recognize as coming from herself.

Because her grandmother—the stern monument, the portrait with the following eyes—had *known*. Had lain awake at night knowing, had traced the seams of her borrowed power and found other people's fingerprints all over her own soul. Had written letters she never sent. *Mother, I dreamed a dream that wasn't mine again. There was a garden in it. Whose garden is it?* And had buried the knowing deeper each year, because the alternative was admitting that everything—the name, the authority, the iron certainty Corvess had inherited like a spine—was scaffolding around a theft.

Thread after thread after thread. Farmers and poets and midwives. A warden who had deserted rather than swear to Veyra and whose desertion had been rewritten into treachery. Children. So many children's first days of school, first snowfalls, first words—all of it hoarded in a bloodline like coins in a miser's mattress, doing no one any good, least of all the ones who carried it.

Corvess fell to her knees on the ice-slick stone. Her wardens surged forward; Tam Ashgrove raised a hand and held them, and she would think about that later, about who he had chosen in that moment without being asked.

"Stop," she gasped. "Make it stop—"

"It doesn't work that way," Emrys said, kneeling across from her, close enough to touch. Their voice was gentle, which was worse than cruelty. "Truth doesn't stop. You can put it down or carry it. Those are the options. They always were."

"I built my life—" Her voice broke apart. "There are laws with my seal on them. There are people I burned. I *knew*, Ashgrove, I knew I was right—"

"You knew what you were given to know." Emrys's eyes were steady. "That's not the same as evil. It's not the same as innocence, either. But the woman who burned people believing herself righteous and the woman who learns tonight what righteousness was built on—they're both you. Truth doesn't unmake you, Corvess. It just takes away the story you were hiding inside."

The last thread was the strangest. It was not a memory at all but an absence shaped like one: Aldric Veyra's final hour, recorded in the lattice because the lattice remembered everything, even what its thief tried to withhold. He had died alone in his capital, surrounded by monuments to himself, calling for heirs who feared him, and in his last moments he had finally understood what he had spent a lifetime refusing to understand—that the covenant-keepers' laughter as they gave their memories away had not been weakness at all. It had been the sound of people who trusted the world enough to put pieces of themselves into it. He had died trying to speak an apology to no one, and the lattice had caught the shape of it and kept it, all these years, waiting for a Veyra brave enough to finish the sentence.

Corvess finished the sentence.

"I'm sorry," she said—to the Stones, to the threads streaming out of her now like embers rising, to the farmers and poets and children whose lives had warmed a stranger's ambition for three hundred years. "I'm sorry. Take them home. Take them home."

And the lattice took them.

* * *

Afterward there was silence, and cold, and the strange lightness of a body emptied of its stolen weight.

Corvess knelt in the snow and felt—for the first time since childhood, since before, since ever—the true boundaries of herself. She was small. She was tired. She was fifty-three years old and her knees ached and her hands, bare now, her gauntlets fallen off somewhere in the unraveling, were just a middle-aged woman's hands with ink stains on the right forefinger from thirty years of signing death warrants.

Her power did not explode. It simply left, the way a tide leaves—drawn back into the lattice, into the reforging that was already beginning behind her, Emrys Ashgrove standing at the Stones with their arms open and their face lit from within. Corvess watched the first star relight above the mountain and felt nothing but a vast, terrifying clarity.

"Ashgrove," she said. Her voice sounded like someone else's. Younger. "The purity attestations. The burnings. I signed those orders believing—"

"I know what you believed."

"The ledgers in my vaults. The archive keys. Everything Veyra hid, you'll find it all documented—I'll give testimony, whatever tribunal you—"

"There will be a reckoning," Sable Vane said, crouching to Corvess's level with the unsentimental efficiency of a woman mapping terrain. "But not tonight, and not by you deciding its terms. Tonight you're going to walk down this mountain ahead of us, in front of your wardens, and tell them the truth yourself. Every word of it. That's the first payment."

Corvess looked past her to the army on the mountainside—four hundred men and women who had followed the Veyra name their whole careers, who were watching their High Warden kneel in the snow with empty hands. Tam Ashgrove stood among them, and his face, when she met it, held no triumph. Only a grief she recognized now, having just lived three hundred years of it: the grief of a person learning that a story they loved was never true.

"Will they even listen?" she asked.

"They listened to a lamplighter's daughter," Emrys said. "They'll listen to you. Whether they forgive you is another matter, and not yours to demand."

Corvess Veyra—last of her line, first honest woman in three hundred years of dishonest ones—pushed herself to her feet. Her legs held. That surprised her. She had expected, somehow, to dissolve along with the power.

She turned to face her wardens, and began to speak.

And high above, one by one, patient as dawn, the stars came home.
