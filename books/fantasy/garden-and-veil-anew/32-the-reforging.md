## 32. The Reforging

The mountain had stopped trying to kill them. That was how Emrys knew they had arrived.

For three days the winter had clawed at them—wind like a whetted knife, snow that fell sideways and then upward, as if the sky itself could not decide which world it belonged to. But now, in the bowl of the summit where the Covenant Stones stood, there was only stillness. A silence so complete that Emrys could hear their own heart, and beneath it, fainter, another rhythm: a slow pulse rising out of the broken stone, like something vast turning over in its sleep.

The Stones were nothing like the drawings in Sable's journals.

Those had shown seven pillars in a ring, each tall as a watchtower, carved with script that predated every alphabet the cartographer had ever catalogued. What remained was ruin. Six of the pillars had fallen, snapped at varying heights, their upper halves lying in the snow like the fingers of a buried hand. Only the seventh still stood whole—and even it was wrong, its surface scarred with channels gouged deep into the living rock, channels that had once held light and now held nothing but frost. Where the carvings remained, they were defaced. Not weathered. *Defaced*—deliberately, methodically, by someone who had wanted the words unreadable.

Aldric Veyra's work. Of course it was.

"Emrys." Sable's voice came hoarse behind them. She had not stopped shivering since the capital, since the memoryless city with its monuments to stolen glory, and her lips were nearly blue. "Tell me you feel it too."

"I feel it." Emrys knelt in the snow at the ring's center and pressed both palms flat against the ground. Through their gloves—or rather through the storm-glass amulet against their chest, cracked now into a spiderweb of white lines—the pulse came up through the mountain like a second heartbeat laid over their own. Out of sync. Out of tune. A song whose singer had forgotten the melody. "It's still alive. Whatever Veyra did to it, he didn't kill it. He *starved* it."

Pip crouched beside them, and Emrys saw the girl sway before she caught herself on the fallen pillar. Her face was wrong. Not frightened-wrong or exhausted-wrong, though she was certainly both—but *thinned*, as if someone had taken a fine blade to the edges of her and pared away what didn't seem essential. Three days ago she had stopped being able to picture her mother's face. This morning, on the last ascent, she had asked Sable what her own laugh sounded like, because she couldn't remember that either.

And still she had walked. Still she had climbed. Still, when the wolves had come on the second night, she had stood in the snow with her hands shaking and called lightning down out of a starless sky, screaming the whole time, and then laughed afterward—a laugh she no longer recognized—at the smoking craters and the smell of singed fur.

"You're sure," Pip said. It wasn't a question so much as a hand held out in the dark. "You're sure this will work. You're sure I'll get them back. My mother's face. My laugh."

Emrys looked at her and did not lie.

"I'm sure of what the covenant asks," they said. "Memory freely given, realm to realm. That's what the original words say—I read them in the standing stone while you slept. Freely given. And freely given means it can be freely returned, when the covenant is whole again. That's the whole point of it, Pip. It was never meant to be a theft. It was meant to be a *gift*, passed back and forth until neither realm could tell which memories were theirs alone." They paused. "But I won't pretend the giving doesn't cost. You know better than anyone what it costs."

Pip nodded slowly. Then, with the terrible practicality of the young, she said, "Then we should start. Before I forget why I came."

Behind them, at the rim of the summit, Corvess lay in the snow where the truth had set her down.

They had all watched it happen an hour ago. They had watched Sable open the lattice-thread she'd torn free from the standing stone—the thread woven from generations of stolen memory, the Veyra bloodline's hoarded inheritance—and hold it out to the High Warden like a mirror. *These are your ancestors' true memories,* Sable had said. *Every one they stole. Every life they emptied to build their power. Take them back, and see what you actually are.*

And Corvess—who had crossed a continent with an army to claim these Stones, who had offered Emrys their brother's life in exchange for obedience, who had spent her whole existence certain down to the marrow that her power was her *right*—Corvess had reached out and taken it.

Emrys would remember the sound she made for the rest of their life, however long that turned out to be. Not a scream. Something worse: a long, unraveling exhale, like a knot coming undone, like a wall discovering it had been made of paper all along. She had staggered. She had wept without seeming to know she was weeping. And then she had folded down into the snow among her soldiers—who had not moved to help her, because half of them had felt the threads snap too, had felt the borrowed certainty drain out of them and left only the cold question of what they had been serving—and she had not risen.

Tam stood near her. Not touching her. Not looking at her. Looking at Emrys.

His sister. His captor's captain. The brother Emrys had mourned for twenty years, alive and armored in Warden black, his face older than it should have been and carved with everything he'd carried. There would be time for him. Emrys had told him so, there on the mountainside with the wind between them: *There is a truth about that night, Tam, and it isn't the one either of us has lived with. But first I have to finish something. Watch me. Please just—watch me.*

He was watching now. That was enough. That had to be enough, for now.

* * *

Sable had mapped the ritual the way she mapped everything: obsessively, beautifully, in ink that would outlast the paper.

"The standing pillar is the anchor," she said now, walking the ring with her boots crunching, her breath streaming. "Veyra gutted the others to feed it—or rather, to feed himself, through it. The channels are empty conduits. If we can fill them—" she tapped the nearest fallen pillar with her toe, "—the covenant reweaves itself. It wants to. That pulse you're feeling? That's it trying to remember its own song with six strings broken."

"And the strings are memories," Emrys said.

"The strings are memories." Sable stopped and looked at them, and for once—for the first time since a sharp-tongued mapmaker had cornered a charm-seller in Greyhollow's night market and seen straight through twenty years of disguise in under a minute—her voice was gentle. "Yours specifically. We've been over this."

"We've been over this," Emrys agreed.

Pip had already given hers. Two nights ago, in the cave, wrapped in blankets with frost on her eyelashes, she had put her small hands on the cracked amulet and *poured*—her first day lighting lamps, the smell of lamp-oil and her father's coat, the secret pride of her first hedge-charm working, a hundred small bright things offered up without hesitation, without bargaining, the way a child gives. The covenant lattice had drunk her light and flared, briefly, gold along the dead channels, and everyone had understood at once both how close they were and how far.

Because a child's bright handful of memories was a candle. What the covenant needed was a conflagration. It needed the thing Emrys carried, the thing they had carried for twenty years like a stone sewn into their chest:

Exile. All of it.

Twenty years of Greyhollow. Twenty years of selling charms to people who couldn't know what they were buying, of keeping their head down and their hands cold and their name unspoken. Twenty years of watching the door of their shop and flinching at Warden boots. Twenty years of loneliness so thorough it had become a personality, a craft, a way of moving through the world. The night market where they'd stood when the stars went out. The frostbitten child whose fingers they'd warmed, breaking their own rules. Pip begging to be taught. Sable seeing through them. The Salt Road. Marrow's inn. The Ashline. The cave. The confession. All of it—the whole slow, painful, miraculous thaw of a frozen heart.

That was the price. Not the exile's misery—that they would give gladly. The price was the *thaw*. The price was the found family, held in memory, and the covenant taking it as fuel.

"You could choose differently," Sable said quietly. "We've been over that too. You could give the misery and keep the rest. The lattice might accept a partial binding. It might hold for a decade. A generation." Her jaw tightened. "I hate this. I want that on the record. I have written three separate objections in my journal and I am going to show them to you later, when you're yourself again, and you are going to read every word."

"Sable."

"Don't *Sable* me."

Emrys smiled. It hurt. Everything hurt, and had for days, and would for a little longer, and then—either way—it wouldn't.

"When I was a girl," they said, "my mother told me that the old covenant wasn't built by heroes. She said it was built by neighbors. People who lived on either side of a fence and decided, one year, to stop guarding it. Each of them gave the other something they couldn't bear to lose. That was the whole magic of it. Not power. *Trust.*" They looked around the ring—at the broken pillars, the gouged channels, the sky above, black and starless and waiting. "Veyra broke it because he couldn't understand that. He thought memory was property. He stole instead of giving, and everything since has been the interest on that debt. I can't unsteal what he took. But I can pay it back the way it was meant to be paid."

"With interest," Sable said dryly, and her voice cracked on the word.

"With interest."

Pip took Emrys's left hand. Sable, after a moment, after visibly deciding that dignity was a luxury for warmer climates, took their right. And far off at the rim of the summit, a figure in Warden black took one step closer and then stopped, fists at his sides, watching.

Emrys lifted the storm-glass amulet over their head.

It had been their mother's. Cracked now, webbed with white fracture-lines, each crack earned: the taste of bread gone, the color of their childhood kitchen gone, small tolls paid up the whole length of this road. In its cloudy heart, a spark still moved—the last of the storm they had been born carrying, the gift and the curse and the name.

"Storm-caller," Emrys said aloud, to the mountain, to the Veil, to whatever listened. "Ashgrove. Daughter and son of Liria Ashgrove, who bound what she loved and lost what she bound. I come to pay a debt twenty years owed."

The pulse beneath the snow quickened.

They knelt at the base of the standing pillar and set the amulet against the stone, in the deepest of the gouged channels. Then they closed their eyes and began to open themselves the way they had taught Pip to open herself—not reaching outward, not calling, but *unclenching*. Letting go of the grip they had kept on their own history for two decades. And the memories rose.

* * *

They came gently at first, almost shyly.

The gates of the old country closing behind them. Snow on a road with no end. The first night in Greyhollow, counting coins in a rented room, telling themselves it was temporary. The first forged papers. The first false name. The shop with its crooked sign—*Charms & Curios, Nothing Magical Guaranteed*—and the joke of it, the bitter private joke, that had kept them laughing at themselves for years.

The lattice drank, and the channels began to glow. Faintly. A thread of gold crawling along the gouges like dawn along a horizon.

Emrys kept giving.

The years blurred past, and they let them. The neighbor woman who left bread on their step every midwinter for a decade and never learned why the charm-seller always wept at midwinter. The inquisitor raid, the amulet hidden in the flour jar, the terrible heat rising in their blood and the neighbor's curtain beginning to smolder and the horror of it, the *horror*, the proof that hiding was its own kind of violence. The frostbitten child. Pip's face the first time she called a hedge-charm and got it right. Sable's voice saying *I know what you are* and the strange, terrifying relief of being *seen*.

The glow spread. Channel to channel, pillar to pillar. One of the fallen stones shuddered in its bed of snow and righted itself with a groan that Emrys felt in their teeth, gold light climbing its broken height like sap rising in spring.

More. It needed more. Emrys could feel the covenant's hunger now, vast and patient and *righteous*—not greedy the way Veyra had been greedy, but hungry the way a starving thing is hungry, hungry for exactly what it had been promised. And Emrys had promised everything.

Here, then. The hard part. The part they had known was coming since the cave, since the confession, since Pip had said *you're not alone anymore* and Emrys had believed her.

The night market. The wards shattering. The stars going out one by one overhead while they stood frozen among their charms, understanding, knowing, knowing—

No. Keep that. Give it anyway. *Freely given.*

The Salt Road, the Bramble twins' laughter, the checkpoint ambush and the blood in the snow—

Give it.

Marrow's inn, the stew, the ex-Warden deserter's kindness that asked nothing—

Give it. Give it. Give it.

The cave. The fire. Pip asleep against Sable's shoulder. The whole story spoken aloud for the first time in twenty years, the mask coming off, the air on their bare face—

*Give it.*

It was like pulling out their own spine one vertebra at a time, and doing it *lovingly*. Because that was the trick of it, the terrible beautiful trick: the covenant would not take. It would only receive. Every memory had to be handed over whole, examined one last time, *cherished*—and then released. You could not give away a thing you hated. You had to give away things you loved. That was what made it a covenant and not a theft. That was what Aldric Veyra had never understood, and what his heir had finally learned in the snow an hour ago, and what a lamplighter's daughter had known in her bones from the very first night.

Emrys gave, and gave, and gave.

The light roared up the standing pillar. The remaining fallen stones dragged themselves upright one by one, gold flooding the gouged channels, and the script that Veyra had defaced began to *regrow* across the surfaces like frost forming in reverse, like a wound remembering it was ever whole. The pulse beneath the mountain steadied, deepened, became a rhythm, became a *song*—two rhythms braided now, this world and the other, falling into step after centuries of stumbling.

And Emrys, kneeling in the center of it, felt themselves growing quiet.

Not empty. That was the miracle they would never be able to describe afterward, because they would not remember describing it. Not empty. *Light.* As if all those twenty years of weight had not been subtracted but *transmuted*—burned as fuel, become warmth, become the gold pouring through the Stones, become the thinning of the black above them.

Because the sky was changing.

Through the roaring light, through the song, Emrys heard Pip scream—not in fear, in *joy*—and opened eyes they didn't remember closing.

Above the summit, in a sky that had been blind for weeks, a single star kindled.

Just one. A pinprick. A needle-hole punched through black cloth. It hung there trembling, as if unsure of its welcome, and then a second star caught beside it, and a third, and a fourth—not all at once, not in a flood, but *one by one*, each ignition a small silent detonation of silver, each one landing somewhere in Emrys's chest like a footstep on a stair. The Veil was relighting. Somewhere beyond it, another sky was answering this one, star for star, the two realms trading light across the mended seam like neighbors passing candles down a dark lane.

Pip was crying and laughing and pointing, naming constellations she'd memorized from books because she'd never been allowed to see them. Sable had her journal out—of course she did, of course she was *sketching the stars coming back*, the impossible woman—and her hand was shaking so badly the lines wobbled. Down at the rim of the summit, the army of the Wardens stood in the snow with their helmets off, their faces tipped upward, soldiers and inquisitors alike struck dumb by the simple ancient fact of starlight.

And the seventh channel filled. The last of the gold sank home into the standing stone, and the song resolved into a single clear chord that rang through the mountain, through the ground, through the soles of everyone's boots, and faded—not ending, Emrys understood, but settling. Becoming background. Becoming the way things were.

The covenant stood reforged. Memory freely given, realm to realm. Neighbors again, on either side of a fence no one guarded.

Emrys stayed kneeling a moment longer, breathing, listening to the new silence—which was not silence at all anymore, but fullness, a hum beneath the world like a hearth-fire in the next room.

Then they looked down at their own hands, at the amulet lying cool and quiet in the snow, its cracks sealed over with seams of pale gold, and they tried to remember why they were crying.

They were crying. Their face was wet. Their chest ached with a grief that had no address, a homesickness for a place they could not name, a love with no object—as if someone had handed them a letter written in a language they no longer spoke, and every line of it was tender.

"Emrys?" Pip's voice, small and careful. She knelt in front of them, searching their face, and Emrys saw with distant curiosity that she was afraid of what she'd find there. "Do you—do you know me?"

Emrys looked at this girl—this stranger, this child with frost in her lashes and starlight in her eyes and tear-tracks freezing on her cheeks, who was holding their hands as if afraid they might blow away—and felt something enormous and warm rise in their chest, entirely unattached to any memory, needing none.

"No," Emrys said honestly. And then, because it was true, and because truth was the whole of what they had just spent themselves on: "But I think I'd very much like to."

Behind the girl, a tall man in Warden black was walking toward them through the snow, slowly, taking off his helmet as he came—and Emrys watched him come with the open, unguarded curiosity of someone meeting the world for the first time.
