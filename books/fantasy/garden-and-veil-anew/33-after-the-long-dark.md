## 33. After the Long Dark

The snow had stopped falling, but the mountain had not stopped listening.

Tam Ashgrove knelt in it because his legs would no longer hold him upright. That was the truth of it—unglamorous, unheroic—and he clung to the truth the way a drowning man clings to driftwood, because for thirty years he had built his life on everything *but* the truth, and now that it was all inside him at once, every stolen thread Corvess's lattice had given back, he could barely breathe under the weight of it.

The army behind him stood silent. Two hundred wardens in grey-and-silver, their formation broken open like a dropped plate, watching their High Warden unravel into nothing on the stones above and not one of them moving to stop what came next. Tam could hear their breathing. He could hear the creak of leather and the tick of cooling armor. Somewhere down the slope, a horse stamped and snorted, breath pluming white.

And above him, on the shattered Covenant Stones, the stars were coming back.

They returned one by one—not all at once, the way they had gone out, but slowly, like lamps being lit by someone walking through an endless dark house. Each new star made a small sound, or seemed to; a chime felt more than heard, deep in the sternum. The light fell across the snow and turned it silver, then blue, then something warmer than either, and Tam watched it happen with tears freezing in the creases of his eyes because he did not dare blink. Blinking might mean missing one. Missing one might mean this was all a dream, and if it was a dream, he did not think he would survive waking.

*Emrys,* he thought. *Emrys, Emrys, Emrys.* The name beat in him like a second heart.

He remembered everything now. That was the terrible gift of it—the lattice had not asked his permission, exactly, but when the threads had come loose, when Corvess had taken her ancestors' true memories back into herself and been unmade by them, the whole rotten architecture had come down, and everything Aldric Veyra had hoarded had poured out like water from a cracked dam. Into the sky. Into the Stones. Into Tam, who had carried a splinter of it in his blood all along without knowing, heir to a theft he'd never chosen.

He remembered the night of the storm.

Not the version the Wardens had fed him, polished and useful: *your sister called the lightning, your sister killed them, sign here and we will make the pain manageable.* No. He remembered standing in the doorway of the Ashgrove cottage with rain hammering the thatch, sixteen years old and furious about something small—a quarrel over a borrowed coat, gods help him, a *coat*—and he remembered Emrys in the yard with their arms raised and their face lit blue-white, screaming words he hadn't understood then and understood now: not a curse, not an attack. A *plea*. They had been trying to turn the storm aside. They had been trying to hold the sky off the village the way you'd hold a door shut against a flood, and they were fifteen, and they were losing.

He remembered the lightning striking the mill. He remembered the screams. He remembered running toward the fire instead of away from it, because he was sixteen and brave in the way that gets people killed, and he remembered the beam coming down—

—and he remembered waking three days later in a Warden infirmary with a man in a grey hood sitting beside his bed, patient as winter, saying *we know what your sister is, boy. We know what she tried to do. The question is whether you're going to help us find her before she does it again.*

He had said yes. That was the fact at the bottom of everything, the stone in the well: they had offered him a story where his sister was a monster and his survival was a miracle, and grief and concussion and fear had made the story fit, and he had said yes. He had testified. He had watched them read out the charges while Emrys stood bound between two inquisitors, searching the crowd for his face, and he had looked down at his boots. He had let them carry his sister out of the only home either of them had ever known, and he had told himself for thirty years that she was dead, because dead sisters didn't need saving and didn't come back to ask why.

Except she hadn't died. She had gone to Greyhollow and sold charms and made herself small and quiet for twenty years, and he had climbed the Warden ranks on the strength of a lie he'd helped tell, and last month he had stood in a war room reading reports of a "residual caster" in the market quarter and felt something in his chest go cold and certain, and he had said nothing. He had led the pursuit. He had cornered her on the mountain with an ultimatum—*help Corvess or your brother dies*—never knowing, until the lattice broke, that the brother in question had already betrayed her once, thoroughly, at the beginning of everything.

And she had refused anyway. She had looked at him across the snow with Corvess's blade at his throat and said, *I won't trade one cage for another*, and then she had walked up onto those stones and given away twenty years of her own memory to relight the sky, and somewhere in the giving she had lost him. Lost all of it. The exile, the loneliness, the fear—every hour of the twenty years he had caused and she had endured, poured out freely so that two worlds would keep turning.

She had paid for his sins with her past.

Tam made a sound that was half sob and half laugh, and pressed his forehead against the snow, and stayed there, kneeling, asking nothing. He had no right to ask anything. He knew that with a clarity that felt almost holy. Whatever happened next—whether she ever spoke to him again, whether she spat at his feet, whether she simply looked through him as though he were a stranger on a road—that would be more than he deserved, and he would take it and be grateful.

The snow was cold enough to burn. He let it.

Footsteps crunched nearby. Not warden-boots; these were lighter, uneven, accompanied by the tap of a walking staff and a low voice muttering something about *the state of these switchbacks, honestly, I mapped this ridge twenty years ago and it was never this steep.* Sable Vane, then, the cartographer, descending from the Stones with a lantern she didn't seem to need anymore under the returning starlight. She stopped a few paces away. Tam felt her looking at him—at the warden-captain's insignia still on his shoulders, at the man kneeling in the snow beneath it—and heard her exhale slowly through her nose.

"Well," she said. "Look at this. The mighty captain of the Grey Wardens, doing penance in a snowdrift." Her voice wasn't cruel. It was tired, and sharp at the edges the way a good knife is sharp, and underneath both there was something almost gentle. "For what it's worth, I've seen a lot of maps of this mountain, and I've never once seen a route marked *redemption*. People always want me to draw one. There isn't one. You just walk."

"I know," Tam said. His voice came out ruined, scraped raw. "I'm not—I'm not asking for a route."

"No," Sable agreed. "I don't suppose you are." She was quiet a moment. Then: "She's coming down. She wanted to see the valley first. Said she couldn't remember why the valley mattered, but that her hands went tight on the railing when she looked at it, so it must have." A pause. "That's how it works now, apparently. The body remembers what the mind gave away."

Tam lifted his head from the snow. His heart was going so hard and fast it hurt.

"Does she—" He stopped. Started again. "Is she all right?"

"She's laughing," Sable said, and there was wonder in it, unwilling and unmistakable. "Do you understand? Twenty years of exile, gone out of her like breath on glass, and she's standing up there laughing at the stars like a person who's never been hurt in her life. Pip's holding her hand. Marrow's crying, which Marrow will deny to their grave." She looked at him a long moment, and whatever she saw in his face made her sigh. "I'm going to walk down ahead of her, captain, and you're going to stay right there, because if you get up and start toward her I'll break your kneecap with this staff and I'll do it *accurately*. She should meet you standing still. She should meet you however she meets you. Understood?"

"Understood," Tam whispered.

Sable crunched away down the slope, lantern swinging, muttering something about *kneecaps* and *accuracy* and *gods, I need a drink.*

And then, after a time that stretched like taffy, Tam heard laughter coming down the mountain.

It floated ahead of her the way birdsong floats ahead of dawn—light, unguarded, astonished at itself. He kept his eyes on the snow in front of his knees. He listened to the footsteps grow closer: hers, quick and light; smaller ones beside them, Pip's, chattering breathlessly about *did you see the third one, the green one, I've never even read about a green star*; the heavy measured tread of Marrow bringing up the rear, sniffling with tremendous dignity.

The footsteps stopped.

"*Oh*," said Emrys Ashgrove.

Tam's whole body flinched. He couldn't help it. He kept his head bowed, snow melting against his brow, staring at the small dark circle his kneeling had pressed into the white, and waited for hatred, or contempt, or the warden-voice he knew she must remember him using—*help reforge the covenant or your brother dies*—waited for the reckoning he had earned thirty years ago and again last week and again an hour ago.

What he heard instead was curiosity. Bright, unarmored, wide-open curiosity, the kind children have.

"You're the one who was fighting," Emrys said. "On the slope. Before the—" A gesture he heard rather than saw; the rustle of a sleeve sweeping toward the sky, the Stones, the impossible. "Before all *that*. You were shouting at the woman in the black-and-gold. You told her to stop. Why did you tell her to stop?"

Tam's breath left him in pieces.

Because it was true. In the last moments before the lattice broke, before Corvess had unraveled, he had drawn his sword—not against Emrys, but against his own High Warden, against thirty years of inherited theft, and shouted at her to stand down, and the memory of it was already becoming the only clean thing he owned.

"Because she was wrong," he said hoarsely. "About everything. About the covenant, about magic, about—" His throat closed. He forced it open. "About you."

"Me?" Genuine surprise, warm and puzzled. "But she can't have known me. Did she know me?" A pause, and then, softer, with a strange gentleness that undid him worse than any blow: "Did *you* know me?"

The question hung in the starlit air.

Pip made a small strangled sound and started forward—"*Emrys, maybe we should*"—and Marrow's big hand settled on her shoulder, holding, waiting, letting it be what it was going to be.

"Yes," Tam said. He still hadn't looked up. He couldn't. Looking up meant seeing whether her face held his mother's laugh-lines, and he wasn't sure he'd survive it. "Yes, I knew you. A long time ago. Before—" His voice broke entirely. He knelt there in the snow, a grown man, a warden-captain, shaking. "Before I broke it. Broke *us*. And you gave away the remembering of it to save everyone, and I would give anything to give it back to you, and I can't, and I know that, and I'm not asking you to forgive me. I'm not asking anything. I just needed you to know that I remember. Someone has to remember, since you can't. So I'll remember it for both of us. For as long as I live. That's all. That's all I came to say."

Silence, except the wind and the far-off chime of another star lighting.

Then snow crunched—two steps, three—and a hand, small and scarred and steady, came into his field of vision. Held out. Open.

"Hello," said Emrys Ashgrove, his sister, who did not know him. "I'm Emrys. I sell charms. Well—I *sold* charms. I appear to have recently done something enormous and I don't fully understand what, and everyone keeps looking at me like I'm a festival bonfire." The hand stayed open. Waiting. "You look like you've been kneeling in the snow a long time. That seems cold and unnecessary. My name is Emrys, and I don't know yours—but my hands went tight on the railing when I looked at you, the same way they went tight looking at the valley. So whoever you were to me, I expect you mattered." A breath. "Would you like to start there?"

Tam raised his head at last.

His sister stood above him in the starlight, older than his memory of her and younger than her years, her face washed clean of twenty hard winters, her eyes bright with a future she couldn't yet see the shape of. She looked, impossibly, like their mother. She looked, impossibly, like no one he had ever hurt.

He took her hand.

"Tam," he said. "My name is Tam."

"Tam," she repeated, tasting it, and frowned slightly—not with pain, he realized, but with the ghost of pain, the body's memory moving through her like weather. Then the frown cleared, and she smiled, and it was their mother's smile entire. "Well, Tam. It's a long way back to Greyhollow, and I'm told I used to know the road, and I'd very much like to hear how a warden-captain ends up kneeling in snowdrifts telling strangers the truth." She squeezed his hand once, firm and warm. "Walk with me? You can tell me everything. I'll believe you. I'm very good at believing people—it may be the only thing I have left."

Behind them, Pip burst into tears. Marrow pretended not to. Sable, ten paces down the slope, called back over her shoulder that if everyone was quite finished being devastating, the inn at Lastlight had beds, and she, personally, intended to claim two.

And high overhead, unhurried, certain, the stars went on lighting—one by one by one—as if the sky itself had all the time in the world, and meant to spend every minute of it watching them walk down together into the valley, out of the long dark, toward morning.

* * *

They made camp that night in the lee of the mountain's shoulder, because Sable's map of the descent had a flaw she refused to acknowledge and Marrow's knee had opinions, and by the time the argument resolved itself the dark had come down thick and soft, and no one wanted to navigate switchbacks by starlight alone. So they built a fire—Pip conjuring the flame with a snap of her fingers and then looking around, startled, as if expecting to be arrested for it, and no one arresting her, and her sitting down very fast as though her legs had briefly forgotten their job.

Tam sat at the edge of the firelight. He knew his place. He had chosen it deliberately: near enough to be counted among them, far enough to make no claims. He had surrendered his sword to Marrow without being asked, hilt-first, the old deserters' gesture, and Marrow had weighed it in one hand, looked at him for a long moment, and said only, "Good balance. Shame to waste it," which Tam suspected was the longest welcome he would get tonight, and found he treasured it anyway.

Emrys sat close to the fire, wrapped in a blanket Pip had bullied them into accepting, watching the flames with the particular intensity of someone reading a letter written in a language they almost speak. Every so often their hand drifted to their collarbone, where some amulet used to hang—Tam remembered the storm-glass, remembered it cracking, remembered what each use had cost—and found nothing, and drifted back.

"Ask me things," Emrys said suddenly, to the fire, to all of them. "While we walk tomorrow. While we sit. Ask me about myself. Everyone keeps *not* asking, tiptoeing around the holes in me like they're graves, and it's making me feel like a haunted house." A crooked smile. "I'd rather be a house people live in."

"I have questions," Sable said immediately, producing a journal from somewhere in her coat with the inevitability of sunrise. "I have *forty pages* of questions. I've been sitting on them for hours out of politeness, which has been physically painful."

"Sable," Marrow rumbled.

"No, let her," Emrys said, delighted. "Forty pages. Go on, then. First question."

Sable opened the journal, wet her pencil, and looked at Emrys over the top of it with the expression of a woman preparing to enjoy herself immensely. "When you healed the frostbitten child in the market—before all this—you broke your own rule against using magic. Why? You'd kept the rule for twenty years. What was different about that morning?"

And Tam, at the edge of the light, went very still.

Because he knew the answer. He had read the report—he had signed off on the report, gods forgive him, the incident file on a "residual caster" in Greyhollow's market quarter, frost-mending, minor, unsanctioned—and he had read the witness statements, and one of them had described the child. Blue-lipped, shivering, a lamplighter's kid from the look of the coat. And Tam had read it in a warm war room a hundred leagues from that market, and thought: *she hasn't changed. Twenty years hiding, and the first thing she does is spend her safety on a stranger's child.*

"Because," Emrys said slowly, feeling along the inside of the question the way you feel along a wall in the dark, "the rule was keeping me alive, and the child was dying, and I looked at those two facts side by side and discovered they weren't the same size. One of them was bigger." They glanced around the fire, almost shy. "Is that right? Did I really do that?"

"That's right," Pip said softly. "I was there. You fixed her fingers and told her to keep her gloves on and you were scared the whole time, but you did it anyway."

"The rule was keeping me alive," Emrys repeated, wondering, "and I decided something else was bigger." They looked into the fire for a long moment. "I think I'd like to be the person who decides that. Going forward. Whoever I was before, I'd like to keep *that* part."

Tam's chest ached so badly he had to breathe around it, slow and deliberate, like breathing around a knife. She had lost everything and the thing she reached for first, out of all of it, was her own kindness. As if instinct had sorted through the ruins and saved the load-bearing wall.

Marrow threw another branch on the fire. Sparks climbed toward the stars, which crowded the sky now, dense and glittering, a whole city of them where for weeks there had been nothing. "Rest," Marrow said, in the tone of a woman whose word had ended arguments in deserter camps for a decade. "Long walk tomorrow. And the boy—" a nod at Tam, not unkind, "—takes first watch. He looks like a man who needs something to guard."

Tam blinked. "I—yes. All right. Yes."

"It's not absolution," Marrow added, settling back against her pack. "It's a job. Absolution you earn slower."

"I know," Tam said. And found, to his surprise, that he was smiling—small and cracked and real. "Thank you. I'd like a job."

The fire burned down. Pip slept curled against Emrys's side like a comma against a word, and Emrys sat stroking her hair and humming something—some half-remembered lullaby, maybe, or maybe just a tune the body kept when the mind let go—and Tam took his watch at the perimeter with his back to the fire and his face to the stars, and stood guard over the sleeping family he had betrayed, and had not yet earned, and was beginning, cautiously, unbelievably, to hope he might.

Toward dawn, a sound woke him: Emrys, sitting up in the grey half-light, looking east, where the sky was going pale at the rim.

"Tam," they said quietly, not turning. "Come sit. I can't sleep, and I want to know things, and you strike me as a man with answers he doesn't know how to give. We have time. The road's long." Now they turned, and the first thin light caught their face, and they smiled at him—open, curious, unbearably kind. "Tell me who I was. Start anywhere. I'll believe you."

Tam crossed the camp and sat down beside his sister, who did not know him yet, and began.
