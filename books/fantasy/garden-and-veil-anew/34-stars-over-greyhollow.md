## 34. Stars Over Greyhollow

Pip saw the first star from the crest of the Ashline road, and she thought at first her eyes had finally broken.

They'd been walking for three days since the Stones—three days of Emrys moving through the world like a person made of paper, smiling at things they couldn't remember why they should smile at, and Pip carrying the weight of that smile in her chest like a stone she'd volunteered to hold. Three days of Tam trailing a half-mile behind them because no one had asked him to come and no one had asked him to go. Three days of Sable muttering bearings and Bramble—one Bramble, the other one carried in a cart Marrow had lent them, bandaged and feverish and swearing with impressive creativity—and the whole ragged procession of them trudging south toward a city none of them knew how to return to.

And then the sun went down behind them, and the sky ahead did not stay empty.

"There," Sable said. She stopped so suddenly that Pip walked into her back. "Pip. *There.*"

Pip looked up.

One star. Then two. Then a scatter of them, like sparks off a struck flint, kindling across the black in ones and twos and then in dozens, until the whole eastern sky was stitched with light and the light was falling downward, faint as breath on glass, and the snowfields below them caught it and held it, and the whole world turned silver.

Pip had never seen stars. Not real ones. She was sixteen years old and she had been born under the Veil's long dimming, under the Warden lamps and the curfew bells, and the sky of her entire life had been a ceiling of grey felt with maybe three stubborn points showing on the clearest winter nights—the ones the lamplighters' guild called the Three Sisters, though nobody remembered which sisters or why. She had grown up thinking that was the sky. That grey felt was the whole of it.

Now there were thousands. Tens of thousands. They came out in waves, constellation after constellation waking up like windows being lit one by one down a long street, and Pip stood in the road with her mouth open and her lamplighter's pole still slung across her back where she'd refused to leave it behind, and she cried without any dignity whatsoever.

"Emrys," she said, and her voice cracked in the middle. "Emrys, *look*."

Emrys looked. And Pip watched their face do something complicated—watched them search for something in all that light and not find it, watched them grieve a memory they no longer owned, and then watched them choose, deliberately, visibly, to be glad anyway. It was the bravest thing Pip had ever seen anyone do, and she had recently seen people bleed out on a mountain pass for strangers.

"I don't remember what they looked like before," Emrys said quietly. "But I think—I think they look right."

"They look right," Sable confirmed, and her voice was rough too, though she'd have denied it under torture. "That's Cassiopeia. That's the Salt Road star, the one we navigated by—" She stopped. Corrected herself. "The one *they* would have navigated by. Before."

Bramble, from the cart, raised his head off the blankets. "Are those actual stars or am I dying? Because if I'm dying I want it noted that I died looking at something nice for once."

"You're not dying, you great lump," his brother said, though his hands were shaking as he checked the bandage. "You're going to live to complain about it for fifty years."

"That's my girl," Bramble said contentedly, and lay back down.

They walked the last miles into Greyhollow under a sky none of them could stop looking at, and every time someone stumbled on the rutted road because they were staring upward instead of at their feet, somebody else laughed—not the careful, muffled laughter of the city, but the loud kind, the kind that echoed, and the echo didn't get anyone arrested, and that alone felt like a miracle stacked on top of the miracle.

* * *

Greyhollow woke up wrong, in the way that cities wake up wrong after anything enormous happens: slowly, suspiciously, like a sleeper prodded by a stranger's hand.

Pip felt it the moment they came through the Salt Gate. The gate guards weren't checking papers. They were standing outside their post, both of them, faces tipped up at the sky, and when the company filed past them—one storm-caller in a charm-seller's coat, one wanted mapmaker, one Warden captain out of uniform with his insignia torn off and his hands in his pockets, one smuggler pushing a cart with another smuggler in it, and one lamplighter's apprentice who had technically been missing from her rounds for six weeks—the guards didn't even glance at them. One of them was crying. The other was trying to explain constellations to him and getting them wrong.

"The bells haven't rung curfew in four days," the second guard said, apropos of nothing, as Pip passed. "Nobody knows who's supposed to ring them. Nobody knows if there *is* a curfew." He laughed, a little wildly. "Half the wardens went home. The other half are standing around the Citadel asking each other who's in charge, and nobody is."

"What happened to Corvess?" Sable asked.

The guard's face did something strange—puzzlement layered over fear layered over pity. "She came down off the mountain alone," he said. "Walked into the Citadel great hall in front of everyone and just... started talking. About her grandfather. About what he did. About what *she* did, trying to finish it." He shook his head. "She talked for hours. Some of it didn't make sense. Some of it made too much sense. Then she gave up her signet and walked out, and nobody stopped her, and nobody's seen her since. There's a writ on the Citadel door dissolving the Office of Purity. Signed by her. Half the city thinks it's a trick. Half the city's already tearing down the attestation boards."

Pip looked at Tam. Tam was looking very hard at the middle distance, at nothing, at everything, and his jaw was working like he was chewing on words he couldn't swallow. He had loved Corvess once, Pip understood—or served her, which in the Wardens might have been the same thing—and whatever had happened to her on that mountain, whatever truth had unmade her, had left him standing in the wreckage of a life he'd built on her orders.

"She's not dead," Tam said finally. "I'd know."

"Would you?" Sable asked, not unkindly.

"No," Tam admitted. "But I'd hope harder."

They made their way down into the Lamplight Quarter as the true dark settled, and here was the thing Pip hadn't expected, the thing that lodged under her ribs and stayed there: the city was lighting its own lamps.

Not waiting for the lamplighters. Not huddling indoors behind shuttered windows until the official light came. People were out in the streets with candles and lanterns and little oil-burning hand-lamps, hanging them on hooks and doorposts and window ledges, passing flame from wick to wick down the whole length of Candlemaker's Row, and the light spread through the quarter like gossip, like good news, like something catching. An old woman stood on a chair to hang a lantern over her shutters, and when it swung alight, the whole street cheered her. Cheered. For a lamp. In a city where six weeks ago lighting an unsanctioned flame after dusk could get your door marked.

"It's not just the sky," Pip said. Her throat was tight. "Look at them. They're not scared of the dark anymore."

"They're scared of plenty of other things," Sable said practically. "Don't go mistaking one night for a new world. But—" she paused, watching the old woman climb down and get embraced by three neighbors at once, "—no. You're right. Something's loose in this city tonight, and I don't think the Wardens can put it back in its box."

Because that was the complication, wasn't it. That was the knot underneath the joy, the one Pip kept putting her thumb on and finding sharp. The Veil was mended. The covenant burned again in the Stones, freely given, realm to realm, and the stars proved it overhead every hour. But the law hadn't changed. The writ on the Citadel door was one woman's signature on a piece of parchment, and half the wardens had gone home but the other half were still armed, still confused, still standing in their garrisons waiting for someone to tell them what magic-users were now—criminals, citizens, weapons, neighbors. Nobody knew. The wardens didn't know. The hedge-witches didn't know. Pip herself, standing in the street with a candle in her hand and a hedge-witch's training in her fingers and a storm-caller's inheritance somewhere in her blood, didn't know what she was allowed to be tomorrow.

Victory, it turned out, was not tidy. Victory was a city full of people holding lit candles and looking at each other sideways, wondering who was about to say *actually, wait*.

* * *

The shop was exactly as they'd left it, which somehow made it worse.

Dust on the counter. The little bell above the door that gave its tired jangle. Emrys's cot in the back room with the blanket folded, the way you fold a blanket when you expect to come back, or when you're trying to convince yourself that you will. On the workbench, half-finished charms sat where twenty years of habit had arranged them: luck-knots, fever-salves, a jar of salt-iron beads for keeping nightmares honest. Everything small. Everything harmless. Everything built by a person who had spent two decades making themselves so quiet that even their own magic whispered.

Emrys stood in the doorway a long moment. Pip watched them take it in—the dust, the cot, the small safe life laid out like a costume they'd outgrown without noticing.

"I don't remember leaving," Emrys said. "Sable told me I locked the door. I don't remember the door." Their hand rose to the cracked storm-glass amulet at their throat, the one that had cost them the taste of bread and the sound of rain and twenty years of exile, given away thread by thread until the binding took. "I keep reaching for memories and finding smooth places. Like river stones."

"We wrote it all down," Pip said. "Everything. Every night on the road, Sable made me dictate and she wrote, and Tam added the parts we didn't see, and Bramble—" she smiled despite herself, "—Bramble dictated his entire fever-dream from the pass, twice, and Sable says it's actually useful medical testimony. It's all in the journals. Whenever you want it. However much of it you want. Or none of it. It's yours either way."

Emrys touched the shelf where the journals now sat, seven of them, fat with Sable's cramped handwriting, and didn't open them. "Tomorrow," they said. "Tonight I want to stand in my own kitchen and not know why it feels like coming home, and let it feel like that anyway."

So they swept the floor, all five of them plus one convalescent smuggler giving directions from a cot, and they lit the stove, and Sable found a bottle of plum wine behind a false panel that she claimed she'd always known about and everyone else claimed she absolutely had not, and they ate bread and hard cheese and pickled onions out of jars, and it was the best meal Pip had ever had, and she'd had better food. She knew she'd had better food. It wasn't the food.

It was the window. Because Emrys's shop had a front window, and the front window faced east, and the east was full of stars, and nobody drew the curtain. Twenty years of drawn curtains, and tonight the glass stayed bare, and the starlight came in and lay across the floorboards like spilled water, and Emrys sat in it, in plain view of the whole street, eating bread, visible.

A knock came near midnight. Everyone froze—old habits—hands drifting toward knives and amulets, Tam's hand actually closing around a poker before he caught himself.

But it was only a neighbor. Mrs. Halloway from the dye-shop, in her nightdress with a shawl over it, holding a candle, and behind her in the street a dozen more neighbors, holding candles, and Pip's heart went up into her throat.

"We saw the lights," Mrs. Halloway said to Emrys through the glass, hesitant, bold, terrified, brave, all at once. "We saw the shop lit. And we heard—" she glanced back at the crowd, embarrassed, "—well. We heard things. On the road, they say. About the Stones. About who mended the sky." Her chin came up, and Pip recognized the expression, because she wore it herself most days: the look of a person deciding, right now, in this exact moment, to stop being afraid. "My granddaughter has the knowing. Little touches. Cold-hands, the midwives call it. We've hidden it her whole life. I've hidden it." Her voice shook. "Is it true? Is it—is it not against the law anymore?"

And Emrys Ashgrove, exiled storm-caller, charm-seller, the person who had taught Pip that the first rule of survival was *be small, be dull, be nothing*, looked at the crowd of neighbors at their door, and then at Pip, and something passed between them that Pip would carry for the rest of her life.

"The law hasn't caught up yet," Emrys said carefully. "I won't lie to you. There are wardens who'll say it's still crime tomorrow morning. There may be trouble. There will almost certainly be confusion, and arguments, and worse than arguments before the city decides what it wants to be." A pause. The starlight moved on the floorboards. "But the sky isn't waiting for permission. And neither, I think, are any of us. So yes. Bring her. Bring her tomorrow, or bring her tonight. I'm done teaching in whispers."

Mrs. Halloway's candle trembled. Behind her, an old man Pip half-recognized from the fish market let out a breath that sounded like twenty years leaving his body, and someone started clapping, quietly at first and then not quietly, and the sound rolled down the street and woke more windows, and more lights, until the whole quarter glittered like the sky had decided to move indoors.

* * *

Later—much later, when the neighbors had gone and Bramble was snoring and Tam had taken the cot by the stove without anyone having to ask him, which was its own small treaty—Pip climbed the narrow stair to the shop's roof with her lamplighter's pole, because some habits deserved to survive the revolution.

From up here Greyhollow spread out below her, and she did what she'd done ten thousand times: she looked for the dark spots, the dead lamps, the streets that needed her. Old instinct. But tonight the dark spots were few, and the ones she found weren't dark because of fear. A widow had let her lamp burn out because she'd fallen asleep watching the sky. Two boys had hung their lantern in a tree and were throwing stones at it, badly, laughing. Down by the river, someone had set a hundred floating candles on the water, and they drifted out toward the sea-wall like a slow golden procession, and Pip could hear singing, several songs at once, none of them matching, all of them glad.

Her mother's lamp was lit. Of course it was. Her mother had probably never let it go out, all these weeks, burning oil she couldn't afford, keeping a flame in the window for a daughter who'd run off with storm-callers and smugglers and one extremely rude mapmaker. Pip's chest squeezed. Tomorrow she'd go home. Tomorrow she'd sit by her mother's bed and hold her hand and start telling her everything, and her mother would cry and scold and feed her, and Pip would show her what her hands could do now, really do, and her mother would be terrified and proud in equal measure, and it would be the hardest and easiest conversation of Pip's life.

But tonight she stood watch, because the city was changing faster than its laws, and changing things needed watching. Somewhere out there, wardens were sitting in garrisons reading Corvess's writ and arguing about whether obedience to a resigned commander counted as obedience at all. Somewhere out there, Corvess herself was walking under the same restored stars, carrying truths that had unmade her, alive or dead or somewhere in between. Somewhere out there, people who had spent lifetimes hiding their gifts were deciding, one kitchen at a time, whether to light their own lamps.

And somewhere out there—Pip felt it in her teeth, in the fine cold hum along her skin that had lived there ever since the Stones—a storm was gathering. Not a bad one. Not like the old stories. Just weather, real weather, the first honest weather Greyhollow had seen in a generation, rolling in off the mountains with lightning in its belly, and every hair on her arms stood up to meet it, and her hands itched with wanting.

*First storm of the new sky,* she thought. *Somebody ought to greet it properly.*

She lifted her lamplighter's pole, and reached, gently, the way Emrys had taught her, the way the covenant itself was written: freely given, freely received.

Far to the north, over the mountains, the clouds answered—a single soft flicker, like a nod, like a wave from a friend across a crowded room.

Pip grinned at the dark, at the stars, at the whole uncertain, unfinished, brand-new world, and rang her pole against the rooftop rail like a bell.

Below her, in the lit window of the charm shop, Emrys looked up.
