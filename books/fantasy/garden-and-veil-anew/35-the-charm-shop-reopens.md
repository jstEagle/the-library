## 35. The Charm Shop Reopens

The sign had been hanging in the back of Emrys's storeroom for twenty years, wrapped in oilcloth like a body they couldn't bring themselves to bury.

They had bought it in their first winter in Greyhollow, back when the shop was a rented alcove with a broken shutter and a smell of damp stone that never quite left the charm-bottles no matter how much lavender Emrys burned. *Ashgrove Charms & Small Mercies*, the sign read, painted by a sign-writer who had charged them three coppers extra for the ampersand and done it beautifully anyway. Emrys had hung it for exactly one day before the first Warden patrol came down Lantern Row, and then it came down, and stayed down, and became one more thing folded away in the dark of a life spent folding things away.

Now it hung above the door again, crooked because Pip had insisted on doing the honors and Pip's ladder technique could best be described as *enthusiastic*.

"It's leaning," Sable observed from the street, arms crossed, morning light catching the ink stains on her fingers. She had not stopped carrying her satchel since the mountain; Emrys suspected she slept with it now, the way other people slept with knives.

"It's characterful," said Pip from the top of the ladder.

"It's going to fall on a customer."

"Then it'll be a memorable shop."

Emrys stood in the doorway with a broom they were using mostly as a prop, watching the two of them argue in the cold gold light of a Greyhollow morning that actually felt like morning—felt like something beginning rather than something endured. Above the rooftops, the stars' absence was a pale blue wound healing into day, and above *that*, if you knew where to look, the faint seam of the Veil shimmered like heat off summer stone. Whole again. Or near enough. Near enough was a phrase Emrys was learning to live inside.

"Leave it," Emrys said. "Crooked signs bring luck."

"That's not a real superstition," Sable said.

"I invented it just now. I'm allowed. I'm a witch."

Pip crowed from the ladder and nearly fell, which settled the argument about the sign's structural integrity more decisively than any measurement could have.

* * *

They opened at the ninth bell, which was a lie of a name because Greyhollow's bells had been recast after the long dark and none of them kept proper time anymore. The city was still learning its own rhythms back. For months during the winter, the whole of Greyhollow had moved like a sleepwalker—shops shuttered, streets empty by dusk, people huddled around whatever warmth they could buy or steal while the frost crept further up the walls each week and the memory-sickness took names, faces, birthdays, whole neighborhoods' worth of small human history. Emrys still flinched sometimes at doorways, half-expecting to find someone standing frozen mid-task, eyes open and gone.

But spring had come early this year—the covenant's first gift—and the city had come out of its burrows blinking and suspicious and hungry. Hungry was the word Emrys kept returning to. Not hungry for bread, though there was that too. Hungry for *knowing*. For the thing that had been taken from them twice over: once by law, once by the dying Veil itself.

By midmorning there was a line.

Emrys had expected perhaps a dozen curious souls. What they got was Lantern Row backed up to the corner of Tallow Street, a queue of people shuffling and stamping their feet and craning to see through the freshly washed windows, and Emrys standing behind their own counter feeling like a storm caught in a bottle.

"Right," Sable murmured, appearing at Emrys's elbow with a ledger she had absolutely appointed herself keeper of without discussion. "We do this in order. Names in the book. Complaints go to Pip. Medical emergencies—" she glanced at the queue, where a woman was holding up a child whose arm was wrapped in a sling made of dish towels, "—apparently skip the line entirely."

The child had fallen from a wall. Ordinary fall, ordinary break, but the bonesetter on Cooper Lane wanted four silver and the mother had two, and so here they were, in a charm shop, in a city where six months ago the word *witch* spoken aloud could get your door kicked in.

Emrys looked at the child's arm. Looked at the mother's face—that particular exhausted defiance of a parent who has decided humiliation is cheaper than helplessness. And felt the old rules rise up in them like scar tissue: *don't draw attention, don't use what can be traced, don't be seen being what you are.*

The rules were dead. Emrys had killed them themselves, on a mountaintop, with every star in the sky as witness.

"Well," they said, and heard their own voice come out steadier than they felt. "Let's have a look at you, sprout."

The mending took less time than the explaining. That was the strange part—not the magic, which flowed through Emrys these days like water through a channel they'd finally stopped damming, but the *witnessing*. Twenty years of working small and sideways, of healing under cover of "a good poultice" and "luck," of letting people believe anything except the truth. Now the mother watched, openly, as Emrys laid bare hands on the crooked arm and called the bone home with a few words in the old tongue, and the child giggled at the tickle of it, and nobody screamed, and nobody ran for a Warden, because there were no Wardens anymore—or none with teeth—and the whole street outside pressed closer to the window to watch.

"There," Emrys said, when it was done. "Keep it still for two days. It'll itch. That means it's knitting."

"How much?" the mother asked, already reaching for her coins.

Emrys looked at the two silver in her palm. Thought of a certain smuggler twin currently eating them out of house and home in the back room. Thought of Marrow's inn, and the meal Marrow had fed five fugitives without taking a coin, and the lesson in that that Emrys had been turning over all winter like a stone in the pocket.

"Tell you what," Emrys said. "You owe me a favor. Someday, someone will need something you know how to give, and you'll give it free, and we'll call it even."

The mother stared. Then her face did something complicated and wet, and she nodded once, hard, and left with her child walking beside her holding his mended arm up like a banner.

Sable wrote something in the ledger. Emrys didn't ask what. They suspected it was either *first debt logged* or *our witch has gone soft*, and they weren't sure they wanted to know which.

* * *

The afternoon brought the harder questions.

A man named Odell—grey-bearded, boat-builder's hands, the wary look of someone who had spent decades keeping his head down—came in just past the fourth bell and didn't buy anything. He stood at the counter turning his cap in his hands and finally said, "My wife. She was taken by the frost-sickness. In the winter." His voice went careful, the way voices did around that season now. "She's better. Most of her came back. But she looks at me some days like she's trying to place me, and our anniversary's coming, and I don't—" He stopped. Started again. "I heard you're teaching. That you take students. Anyone who asks."

The rumor had spread faster than Emrys had anticipated, which was naive of them; everything in Greyhollow traveled fast, gossip most of all, and the reopening of a charm shop by the storm-caller who had relit the stars was not gossip so much as civic news. But they hadn't expected this shape of it. Not *come sell me a luck-charm*. Rather: *teach me.*

"I don't teach charms," Emrys said gently. "Not the way you mean. Hedge-work isn't something you buy or borrow. It's—" They paused, searching for the words, and found them somewhere unexpected: in the memory of Pip's face the first time she'd called lightning, terrified and triumphant, lit blue-white in a snowbound dark. "It's a relationship. With the small magics. The ones that live in hearth-smoke and hedgerows and the space between neighbors. You don't command them. You get to know them. And they decide whether to help you."

"And would they? Help me?" Odell asked. "For her?"

Emrys studied him. There was nothing remarkable in his face—no gift blazing out of him, no bloodline hum. Just an ordinary man with an extraordinary want, which was, Emrys had come to believe, the truest soil magic grew in.

"Come Thursday evenings," Emrys said. "Bring nothing. We'll start with listening."

He blinked. "Listening to what?"

"To everything. That's the whole first lesson. Most people never get past it."

* * *

By the time the light went amber and long across the floorboards, Emrys had healed a burned hand, unhexed a well (the hex turned out to be a dead cat upstream, but the ritual satisfied the neighborhood and the cat got a burial), sold eleven luck-charms, two honesty-knots for a merchant who swore it was for his business partner and not his wife, and one love charm—which Emrys had refused, and then replaced with a courage charm and a frank conversation about how the woman in question deserved to be approached sober and in daylight.

Pip had handled complaints, as assigned, with escalating delight. Her favorite, she reported breathlessly, was the man who demanded a refund on a luck-charm he'd bought *before* the long dark, on the grounds that it had clearly failed. "I told him," she said, perched on the counter with her lamplighter's boots swinging, "that surviving the death of the entire sky was probably the luck working overtime, and he got so offended he bought a second charm out of spite."

"Spite-purchases are the backbone of commerce," Sable agreed, not looking up from her maps. She was drawing again—she was always drawing again—but differently now. The old maps had been defenses, ways to know terrain faster than danger could know her. These new ones were something else. Lately she'd been charting the Veil itself, its seams and shallows, the places where the world's new mending showed through thin as old scars. Emrys had watched her work on one such map for three nights running, and had noticed that she kept returning to a blank space at its western edge, a region she'd marked with a single small symbol Emrys didn't recognize, and that whenever Emrys came near, she angled the paper away.

He let it be. Everyone carried their locked rooms. He of all people knew that.

Bramble arrived at closing, as Bramble did, letting himself in through the front door with the air of a man who had personally invented doors. He was thinner than he'd been before the pass—one arm still bound tight to his chest where the Warden blade had found him, the wound slow to close in a way that made Emrys's hands itch with wanting to fix it and Bramble's pride itch harder against being fixed—but his grin was intact, which was the part of Bramble that mattered.

"Heard you turned away a love charm today," he said, dropping onto a stool. "Word travels. There's a woman on Tallow Street telling everyone the great Ashgrove believes love should be earned."

"The great Ashgrove believes love charms are a coward's tax," Emrys said.

"Mm. And yet you gave the woman a courage charm instead, which is arguably worse. Courage gets people into terrible trouble." He said it lightly, but his eyes went briefly distant—to a frozen pass, to a brother bleeding in the snow, to the months of recovery that had taught all of them what courage cost when it was spent for family. Then he shook it off, because Bramble shook everything off; it was his whole architecture. "Anyhow. I'm not here to critique your sales philosophy. I'm here because there's a cart at the loading door with my sister's handwriting on the manifest, and she says if you don't inventory it tonight she's coming in tomorrow, and you know what she's like before her tea."

"What is it?"

"Supplies. Glassware. A truly obscene quantity of dried nettle." He paused, and his grin softened into something rarer. "And a crate marked *books*. From the archive folk. They've started copying out everything Veyra hoarded. The real records. What the covenant actually was, before him." He tapped the counter once. "People want to learn, Ashgrove. Properly. Not just charms for sore backs. The old knowledge—all of it. And they keep asking where they're supposed to go, and everyone keeps saying the same thing."

Emrys looked around their shop—at the jars and bundles, the counter worn smooth by twenty years of quiet trade, the crooked sign visible through the window against the deepening blue of evening, the first star of the night pricking through above the rooftops like a pinhole in dark cloth.

"They're saying here," Emrys said quietly.

"Everyone's saying here."

* * *

They closed late. Pip fell asleep in the reading chair with a primer of hedge-herbalism open on her chest, and Emrys covered her with a blanket and stood a moment watching her breathe—this fierce, stubborn girl who had knocked on their door a lifetime ago begging to be taught, and who had given her brightest memories to a mountain so that strangers might keep theirs. Some of those memories had come back with the reforging, imperfectly, the way rivers return after drought. Some had not. Pip spoke of her mother's face now the way one speaks of a beloved house one lived in long ago: with love, and detail drawn partly from other people's telling. Sable kept a journal of it, at Pip's request. *So I can relearn myself,* Pip had said, practical as ever. *If I'm going to forget things, I want them written down somewhere braver than my head.*

Emrys thought about that journal more often than they admitted.

They were blowing out the lamps when Sable appeared in the doorway of the back room, satchel over her shoulder, clearly dressed to leave and equally clearly not leaving.

"You have a crowd problem," Sable said.

"I have a line problem. Crowds imply chaos. Lines imply hope."

"You have both. Word's spreading beyond the city. There were travelers in the queue today—I counted three accents from over the Ashline, and one woman who I'd wager my best pen came from across the Veil entirely." Sable's mouth twitched. "The realms signed your covenant, Emrys. Envoys are already circling like gulls. People are going to come looking for the person who did it. And you've gone and put a *sign* up."

"A crooked one."

"A crooked one. Which is worse. Crooked things look honest." She hesitated then, and Emrys saw her hand drift toward her satchel—the gesture she'd been making for weeks, the almost-touch, the thing she picked up and put down. Tonight, apparently, she picked it up and did not put it down.

"There's something else," Sable said. "About the journals. Mine. The ones I've been keeping since the mountain." Her jaw worked, the way it did when she was deciding whether to say a thing precisely or at all. "You should know that I haven't only been mapping the Veil. I've been writing down... other things. Things from before. Things that were lost." She met Emrys's eyes, and behind the sharpness there was something unguarded, rare as a green shoot in February. "Everything, Emrys. Everything anyone in this company gave up. Written down, witnessed, dated. Because a record isn't the same as a memory. But it's not *nothing*, either. And I think—" she stopped, exhaled, started over with the bluntness she used to hide tenderness, "—I think you especially ought to see what's in mine. When you're ready. Not tonight."

The lamp Emrys had just blown out left a ghost of smoke curling in the air between them, and above the shop, through the window, the stars were coming out one by one over Greyhollow—stars Emrys had bought back with twenty years of their own life, though they could no longer remember the price, only feel its shape the way a missing tooth is felt.

"Not tonight," Emrys agreed softly.

But all through the washing of the charm-jars, all through the banking of the hearth, all through the long quiet walk home beneath the returned sky, the thought glowed in them like a coal: *she wrote it all down.* Every given thing, held safe in someone else's keeping. And tomorrow, or the day after, or whenever they grew brave enough, there was a journal waiting with their name in it—and inside it, perhaps, the taste of bread, and a brother's laugh, and everything else they had paid without remembering the coin.

Emrys stood a moment in the middle of Lantern Row, looking up at the crooked sign glowing faintly in the starlight, and laughed—a small, startled sound, alone in the empty street, at the sheer terrifying generosity of being known.

Then they walked home, and slept, and dreamed of a door standing open.
