## 36. What Was Given Away

Sable Vane had never trusted a memory she couldn't hold in her hands.

Maps were better. A map admitted its own incompleteness—here be marshland, here the river shifted after the flood of '61, here the surveyor's hand grew tired and guessed. A map was honest about what it didn't know. But memory, unrecorded memory, was a cartographer's nightmare: coastlines that redrew themselves in the night, mountains that migrated, whole cities sunk beneath the waterline of forgetting without so much as a dotted line to mark where they'd been.

She had learned that lesson twice over. Once as a girl, when her family's house burned in Veyra's purges and no one had thought to draw it first. And once again on the mountain, when Emrys Ashgrove had poured twenty years of exile into the Covenant Stones like wine into a cracked cup, and woken the next morning smiling at strangers.

Sable had started writing that same night.

* * *

The journals lived in a locked chest under her bed at the charm shop—or what had been the charm shop, and was now something larger and harder to name. Three volumes of good rag paper, bought with the last of the coin Marrow had pressed into her palm at Lastlight. She had filled them by lamplight while the others slept: every use of storm-glass, every small calling, every cost itemized with a surveyor's precision. The taste of bread, gone on the fourth night of practice. The name of the village where Emrys had hidden their first winter, gone by the mountain pass. The sound of Tam's laugh, gone before they ever reached the Stones—and Sable knew that one mattered most, because Emrys had mentioned it exactly once, offhand, on the road through the ashline, and then never again, because there was nothing left to mention it *with*.

She had written down things Emrys hadn't said, too. Things she'd observed. The way Emrys still turned left out of doorways, always left, some old habit of a city they could no longer name. The way they flinched at the smell of ozone. The lullaby they hummed without knowing they were humming it—a tune Sable had traced, eventually, to a cradle-song of the northern valleys, the kind mothers sang during calving season when the storms came in off the ice.

Twenty years of a life, reduced to three volumes and a margin full of questions.

For two months she had done nothing with them. That was the agreement she'd made with herself, the only oath she took anymore: *I will keep it. I will not decide for them.* Because there was a difference between holding a map for someone and drawing their route for them, and Sable Vane had watched enough powerful people confuse the two to last several lifetimes.

But winter was ending. The thaw had come up the valley like a slow tide, and Pip was due back from the lowlands within the week, and Bram was walking again without the crutch, and every morning the shop opened its doors to hedge-witches and charm-seekers and one extremely persistent dairy farmer who wanted a blessing for his cows. The world was refilling itself. And Emrys went about in it cheerful and curious and light as a blown egg, greeting each day like a stranger at a fair, and Sable could not shake the feeling that she was standing on a fault line holding a survey no one had asked to see.

So on a grey-gold morning in late thaw, with the gutters running loud outside and the smell of wet stone coming through the shutters, she carried the chest downstairs and set it on the counter between the dream-charms and the display of weathered bells.

"Emrys," she said. "I need to show you something. And I need you to sit down first."

Emrys looked up from a jar of moonwort they were labeling in their careful, slightly crooked hand. "That's an ominous sentence."

"It's meant to be." Sable set two stools at the counter, sat on one, and put her palms flat on the chest's lid. "Do you remember the night we came down off the mountain?"

"No." Emrys said it easily, without flinching; they had long since stopped flinching at the shape of their own absence. "Pip says I cried. I find that hard to picture, but she swears by it."

"You did. Quietly. Into my shoulder, which was soaked through and freezing, and you apologized eleven times." Sable heard her own voice go dry and precise, the way it did when feeling got too close. "And when you woke the next morning, you asked me who I was. And I told you. And then—" She stopped. Started again. "Then I went and bought paper."

Emrys set down the label brush. Slowly, the way people move when the ground has begun to speak to them.

"Sable. What did you write?"

"Everything." She lifted the lid. The three volumes lay inside, wrapped in oilcloth against damp, spines already softening with use. "Every memory you spent. Every one you gave. Everything I saw you lose and everything I could infer, cross-referenced, dated. I kept records, Emrys. It's what I do. It's the only thing I know how to do when the world starts eating itself."

The silence stretched. Outside, a cart rattled past; someone called a greeting to someone else; the ordinary machinery of a waking city ground on, indifferent.

"You've had these," Emrys said at last, "for two months."

"I have."

"And you didn't tell me."

"I didn't tell you." Sable met their eyes. Hers were grey as a coastline in fog; she'd been told they gave nothing away, which was a lie, but a useful one. "Because this isn't mine to give. It's yours. It was always going to be yours. I just—I held it. The way you'd hold a map for someone crossing country they couldn't see. You don't walk the route for them. You don't even point. You wait until they ask where they are."

Emrys reached out and touched the top volume's cover with one finger, lightly, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal you weren't sure wouldn't wake angry.

"What's in them?" they asked. "Truly. Not the inventory. What's *in* them?"

Sable took a breath. This was the part she had rehearsed and abandoned and rehearsed again, and in the end she decided the only honest version was the plain one.

"A brother who laughed like a drainpipe in rain. A mother whose bread tasted of caraway and woodsmoke. A village called Harrow's Bend where you hid your first winter and taught the miller's daughter to whistle up wind. A name you were called as a child—Rys—that only your family used. Twenty years of loneliness, catalogued. Twenty years of exile, transcribed." Her voice caught on the last of it, and she let it. "You gave all of that away to relight the stars. And I wrote it down so it wouldn't stop existing. Because a thing that exists in record can be found again. That's all a map is, in the end. Proof that somewhere was real."

* * *

They read for three hours.

Sable had expected tears. She had braced for them, arranged herself for them, positioned the tea and the good linen handkerchief with the tactical foresight of a woman who had once planned supply routes through a war zone. But Emrys read the way they seemed to do everything now—with an open, unhurried attention, turning pages as if each one were a window into a neighbor's garden. They laughed twice. Once at the entry about the miller's daughter (*taught her to whistle up wind; consequence: entire village of Harrow's Bend unable to keep hats on for a fortnight*). Once, unexpectedly, at the account of their own crying on the mountainside.

"Eleven apologies," they murmured. "That sounds like me."

"It was insufferable," Sable agreed, and her voice was not entirely steady, and she let that stand too.

It was near midday, the light slanting gold through the shutters, when Emrys closed the second volume and sat for a long moment with both hands flat upon it, as though taking its measure.

"Can I ask you something strange?" they said.

"I'd be disappointed otherwise."

"When you lost your family's house. When it burned." Emrys looked up. "Did anyone write it down? Did anyone keep a record, so it could be—found again?"

Sable had not expected the question to land where it landed. Something old and unmapped shifted in her chest.

"No," she said. "No one thought to. We weren't record-keepers, my family. We were *goers*, movers, people who assumed there'd always be another horizon. And then the Wardens burned the horizon too, and there was nothing left to hold. For years afterward I'd try to remember the kitchen—the blue tiles, the crack in the third one shaped like a river delta—and I couldn't tell anymore what I actually remembered and what I'd invented. That's the worst of it, Emrys. Not losing it. Losing the ability to tell the difference between what was true and what you built to fill the hole." She tapped the journal under Emrys's hands. "So no. No one wrote it down. Which is why I wrote down you."

Emrys was quiet a moment. Then, gently: "You built a library around a person."

"I built a *survey* around a person. Libraries are for people with more shelving."

That earned the smile Sable had come, without quite admitting it, to arrange her days around—the one that started crooked and finished warm, the one that belonged to someone who had traded away their grief and was still learning what faces were supposed to do with the space it left behind.

"So," Emrys said. "What happens now? Do I read them all and become myself again? Is that how it works? I read the words and the memories come home like pigeons?"

"No." Sable shook her head slowly. "I've thought about this until my head ached, and I'll tell you what I believe, for whatever belief is worth. The memories are gone. Truly gone—you spent them, they went into the lattice, they're holding up stars now or feeding roots or doing whatever given things do. Reading won't bring them back. What reading can do is—" She searched for it, hands sketching shapes in the air, cartographer's hands always drawing even when idle. "It can give you the *shape* of what you lost. The outline. You'll know there was bread, and that it mattered, and why. You won't taste it. But you'll know the taste existed, and that it was yours, and that someone loved you enough to bake it. That's not nothing. That's a coastline drawn from hearsay. Less than the truth. More than blank water."

Emrys looked down at the volumes for a long time.

"And if I choose not to read them?" they asked quietly. "If I choose to leave it blank water?"

"Then I burn them in front of you and we open a bottle of Marrow's terrible plum brandy and never speak of it again." Sable said it flatly, and meant it completely. "That's the whole point of what I'm offering. It's not the journals, Emrys. It's the *choice*. You didn't get to choose what you gave. Someone should get to choose what comes back."

* * *

They did not decide that day. Sable hadn't expected them to; decisions of this weight moved at glacier speed, and she was, professionally speaking, a great respecter of glaciers.

What happened instead was smaller and stranger. Over the following week, Emrys began asking questions—not about the big entries, the brother, the mother, the twenty hollow years, but about the margins. About the small things.

"Tell me about the hats," they said one evening, elbow-deep in a delivery of dried nettle. "Harrow's Bend. The fortnight of hats."

So Sable told it, reconstructing from her own notes and the interview she'd once conducted with a garrulous tinker who'd passed through the village in Emrys's hiding years. The miller's daughter, Wren, fourteen and furious at the world, who'd begged the quiet charm-seller to teach her just one trick, just one, please, I'll sweep your floor for a month. And Emrys—who had broken their own rules exactly once before and would break them exactly once more—had taught her a calling so small it barely counted as magic: a whistle that coaxed the wind into playing. And Wren had practiced on the village green until the wind fell in love with her, and for two weeks no hat in Harrow's Bend stayed on any head, and the parson preached a sermon about vanity that was interrupted four times by his own sermon-notes escaping across the churchyard.

Emrys laughed until they had to sit down among the nettles.

"I don't remember it," they said, wiping their eyes, "but I *remember* it. Does that make sense? It's like hearing a story about yourself that fits so well it must be true. Like trying on a coat and finding your initials in the lining."

"That's exactly what it is," Sable said. "A coat with initials in the lining. I can't give you the body that wore it. But I can prove it was yours."

Piece by piece, that was what the week became. Sable read aloud from the margins while Emrys worked, or cooked, or mended the shutters that had finally, finally stopped rattling now that the wards above Greyhollow were singing again instead of screaming. The entries about Tam, Emrys listened to in silence, eyes closed, one hand pressed flat over their sternum as though holding a door shut—or perhaps holding it open. They did not ask to hear those twice. But they did not ask Sable to stop, either, and on the third evening, when she finished the entry about the drainpipe laugh, Emrys said, very quietly:

"He used to laugh at everything. Even things that weren't funny. Especially things that weren't funny. Mother said he laughed at his own funeral it would serve him right if no one else did." A pause. "I don't remember saying that. But I know I said it. You can tell, can't you? You can tell the difference between a story and a scar."

"Yes," Sable said. "Yes, you can."

On the sixth day, Emrys stood in the middle of the shop after closing, the three journals stacked on the counter between them, and said: "I want to keep them. All of them. Not to read straight through—but to have. To know they're there. Some nights I think I'll want the bread entry. Some nights I think I couldn't bear it. I want to be able to choose *then*, not now."

"Then they're yours," Sable said. "Though I'm keeping the originals. These are copies."

Emrys blinked. "When did you copy them?"

"While you slept, the first month. I'm not handing a person their whole lost life in documents that could burn. I'm careless, Emrys, but I'm not *that* careless." She allowed herself the thin, sharp smile that had frightened customs officers on three continents. "There are copies in Marrow's strongroom at Lastlight, copies with the Brambles, and one sewn into the spine of a completely boring atlas of southern drainage basins that no human being will ever voluntarily open."

"You hid my life inside drainage statistics."

"The safest place on earth. Nothing guards a secret like tedium."

Emrys laughed—that new laugh of theirs, unburdened, a little startled by itself—and then went quiet, and crossed the room, and did something Sable had not prepared for at all. They hugged her. Briefly, firmly, arms around her shoulders, cheek briefly against her hair, smelling of nettle and lamp-oil and the particular clean cold of someone who had recently been outdoors being happy.

"Thank you," they said into her ear. "Not for the journals. For waiting. For not deciding for me. Do you know how rare that is? Everyone else in my life—" They stopped, stepped back, and their eyes were bright but level. "Everyone else loved me loudly enough to make my choices for me. You drew me a map and handed me the compass. That's the kindest thing anyone's done since I woke up."

Sable, who had survived blizzards, betrayals, and one memorable encounter with a territorial snow-wyrm without losing her composure, discovered that she had absolutely nothing to say. She nodded. She busied herself with the bell display. She was aware, distantly, that her eyes were doing something inconvenient, and she elected to blame the lamp-oil fumes, and Emrys had the grace to pretend to believe her.

* * *

It was two mornings later, in the blue hour before opening, that the hook came.

Sable was at the counter with her current project—a proper survey of the restored borderlands, the first honest map of that country in thirty years—when Emrys came down the stairs two at a time, hair uncombed, holding the second journal open in both hands like something rescued from a fire.

"Sable. The bread entry. Read it again. Aloud."

Frowning, she took the book and found the page. *Entry 14. Memory spent: taste of mother's bread (caraway, woodsmoke, crust that sang when torn). Context: lost during practice session with P., night four.* She read it aloud, puzzled. "Caraway, woodsmoke, crust that sang when torn. Why? What's wrong?"

"Nothing's wrong." Emrys's voice had gone strange—wondering, almost frightened, lit from inside. "That's what's wrong. Or right. I don't—" They pressed both hands to the counter and leaned there, breathing. "Last night. Pip baked. She's been learning, she made a loaf from a recipe she found in one of Mother's old books that Tam sent down from the capital, caraway and woodsmoke, and I ate a piece, and Sable—" They looked up, and their eyes were wide and wet and shining. "It sang when I tore it. The crust *sang*. And I tasted it. Not remembered it. Tasted it, for the first time, like anyone tastes bread. And I've been sitting upstairs for an hour trying to understand, and then I remembered what you said. Given things don't vanish. They go *somewhere*. Into the lattice. Into the covenant. Into the—" 

"Into the roots," Sable whispered. The pen had slipped from her fingers. "Into the garden. Oh, Emrys. You didn't lose it. You *planted* it."

Outside the shuttered window, in the little plot behind the shop where Emrys had been turning soil all week—where something green and impossible had begun, against all sense, to push up through the last of the thaw—the morning light was coming up gold, and the stars' faint daytime ghosts were fading overhead, and somewhere beneath the stones of the world the covenant hummed like a hive in spring.

Emrys grabbed their coat.

"Where are you going?"

"To dig," Emrys said, wild-eyed and laughing, already at the door. "Sable, get your journal. Get *all* your journals. If things planted in the Veil can grow back—if what I gave away is coming up out of the ground—then I need to know every single thing I gave. Every entry. Starting now."

The door banged open onto the gold morning, and Sable, heart hammering a surveyor's quick count, swept the three volumes off the counter and ran after them.
