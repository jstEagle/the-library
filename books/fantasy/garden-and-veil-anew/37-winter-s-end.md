## 37. Winter's End

The thaw came to Greyhollow the way forgiveness comes—slowly at first, then all at once, and then everywhere.

Emrys noticed it first in the gutters outside the charm shop. For months they had rung with ice, a hard bright music that piped through the shutters at night and made the whole street sound like a struck bell. Then one morning in late Thawmoon, Emrys opened the door to sweep the step and heard instead a thin, industrious dripping, water finding its old paths down the cobbles, running silver in the early light. Above the rooftops, where for half a year the sky had been a bruised grey ceiling, there was blue—actual blue, unbroken, so ordinary and so miraculous that Emrys stood on the threshold with the broom forgotten in their hands until their eyes stung.

They had not seen that particular shade of sky since before the exile. Or since after it. The distinction no longer mattered; the journals had given them back everything worth keeping, and what remained lost was only the shape of loneliness itself, which was perhaps the kindest thing anyone had ever taken from them.

"Em!" Pip's voice came from up the lane, breathless, boots slapping wet stone. She rounded the corner of the tanner's with her lamplighter's pole abandoned somewhere behind her and a bundle clutched to her chest like contraband. "Em, you have to come *now*, the river ice is going out—the whole thing, all at once, Marrow says it sounds like the world cracking its knuckles—"

"The world," Emrys said mildly, "does not have knuckles."

"It does now. Everything does now. That's sort of the whole point of the last year." She grabbed their sleeve and hauled, and Emrys let themselves be hauled, laughing, the broom left leaning against the doorframe for anyone to steal and no one likely to, because everyone in Lantern Row knew whose step it was.

* * *

The river broke at midmorning, and half of Greyhollow turned out to watch.

It began as a groan underfoot, felt more than heard, a shudder traveling up through boot-soles into knees. Then the ice downstream sheared away from the banks in great grinding plates, and the whole frozen sheet of it began to move—a slow, ponderous, glittering procession, floes tilting and shouldering past one another, catching the new sun and throwing it back in shards. The crowd on the embankment gasped as one body. Somewhere a child shrieked with pure delight. An old man who had fished the Grey his whole life took off his hat, which in Greyhollow was as close as most people came to prayer.

Emrys stood among them, shoulder to shoulder with strangers who were no longer strangers, and felt the sound move through their chest like something remembered from very far away. Water. Moving water. For twenty years they had lived beside this river and never once heard it run free.

"There you are." Sable appeared at their elbow, map case slung across her back even though there was nothing left within fifty miles that needed mapping—or so she claimed, though Emrys had seen her sketching the thaw-line along the northern hills three days ago, muttering about how the land would settle differently now that the Veil was whole, how every road might shift a handspan east or west, and someone had better be ready to draw it. "I've been looking all over. The Brambles are asking for you."

"How are they?"

"Wren's walking again. Briar's pretending he isn't still using the crutch when he thinks no one's watching, which fools absolutely no one, least of all Wren." Sable's mouth did the thing it did instead of smiling—tugged sideways, wry, the ghost of a smirk she'd never fully surrendered even now. "They're holding court at Marrow's inn. Well. Wren's holding court. Briar's holding the fire."

"They held a pass against thirty Warden regulars with two crossbows and a mule cart," Emrys said quietly. "I think they've earned a little court."

"They earned more than that." Sable looked out at the river, and for a moment her sharpness went soft at the edges, the way it only ever did when she thought no one was looking. "We all did."

The ice went out for another hour, and nobody moved. It was, Emrys thought, the first time the whole city had gathered for anything other than fear since the night the stars went dark. They had assembled in terror under Corvess's curfews; they had assembled in hunger during the worst of the winter; but this—they had chosen this, freely, to stand together in the cold bright morning and watch the world remember how to move.

Pip found them near the end of it, cheeks pink, hair escaping its cap in copper wisps. She didn't say anything. She just leaned against Emrys's arm, the way she'd done since the cave, since the wolves, since the night she called her first lightning and shook for an hour afterward while Emrys held her hands and told her the shaking meant she understood what she'd been given. They watched the last floes grind past together, and neither of them needed words, which was its own kind of covenant.

* * *

Marrow's inn smelled of woodsmoke, wet wool, and the particular deep-boned warmth of a building that had decided, sometime over the winter, to become the center of everything.

The Lastlight had changed since autumn. Marrow had hung new lamps—not lamplighter's oil but small witch-lights, hazed green-gold, the kind hedge-witches had made in secret for generations and could now make openly, badly, joyfully. Half of them flickered. One of them, above the bar, occasionally emitted a faint smell of lavender for reasons no one had determined. Marrow refused to fix it. "Character," she said, whenever anyone complained, in the flat voice that permitted no argument.

She was behind the bar now, arms crossed, watching the room with the vigilance of a woman who had spent years watching doors for inquisitors and had not yet taught her shoulders to stand down. But when she saw Emrys come in with Pip and Sable, something in her face eased—the smallest loosening, gone almost before it arrived, but Emrys had learned to read it. From Marrow it was an embrace.

"You're late," Marrow said. "Wren's told the story of the pass four times and each version gives her more scars."

"I heard five," said a voice from the corner, "and in the fifth one I killed six men with a soup ladle."

Briar Bramble sat propped by the hearth, leg stretched out, crutch leaning against his chair exactly where he could reach it without admitting he needed it. He was thinner than he'd been in autumn—the wound had gone bad twice before a proper healer could see to it, and there had been weeks in deepest winter when Emrys had sat by his bed calling warmth into the fevered air and wondering, in the small hours, whether the cost of the working was worth it, and knowing every time that it was—but his eyes were the same: quick, amused, missing nothing.

"In the fifth version," Wren said from beside him, "you also serenaded the enemy into surrender."

"That part's true."

"That part is *not* true."

"The ladle's true, though," Briar said to Emrys solemnly. "Never underestimate kitchenware. It's how we survived the Salt Road all those years."

Emrys pulled up chairs and sat, and the table rearranged itself around them with the easy frictionless motion of people who had done this a hundred times now—Sable claiming the seat with sightlines to both doors, Pip wedging herself between Emrys and the fire, Wren sliding a cup of cider across the wood before anyone asked. Outside, the river ran loud and free. Inside, the witch-lights hummed. And Emrys sat in the middle of it with their hands around a warm cup and thought: *this*. Not the Stones, not the reforging, not even the stars coming back one by one over the mountain—this, the ordinary miracle of a table full of people who had bled for each other and stayed anyway.

"You're quiet," Wren observed. "You get quiet when you're happy. It's very annoying. Most people smile."

"I'm told my smiles are unsettling."

"They are," said Sable, Pip, and Briar, in unison.

Marrow set down a plate of bread and cheese without ceremony, and then, in the manner of a woman paying a debt she'd rather die than name aloud, added a second plate—the good honey-cakes, the ones she saved—and pushed it toward the center of the table. Nobody commented. Everybody understood.

"To the ice going out," Wren said, raising her cup.

"To the ladle," said Briar.

"To the ones who held the pass," Emrys said, and the table went briefly, gently silent, and then drank.

* * *

Pip's graduation was three days later, and it was, by any formal measure, a disaster.

There was no formal measure. That was the problem. Hedge-witchery had never had rites—it had been illegal for so long that its traditions survived only in fragments, in grandmothers' whispers, in things passed hand to hand under counters. When Emrys had proposed marking Pip's passage from apprentice to journeywoman, they'd had to reconstruct the ceremony from memory-threads the covenant lattice had returned to the world: a candle lit by the student's own calling, a word spoken by the teacher, a gift given by the community. Simple. Dignified. Achievable.

What actually happened was that forty-one people showed up to the shop.

Word had gotten out—word always got out in Greyhollow, moving through the city faster than any courier, riding gossip the way plague rode rats—and by dusk the little charm shop on Lantern Row was packed shoulder to shoulder with neighbors, tanners and fishwives and two off-duty city guards who kept their helmets on out of embarrassment, and old Fen the ratcatcher who had never once bought a charm but came to every gathering because, he said, the company was good and the wine was free. Marrow had closed the inn for the evening, which she announced as though it were a military decision, and brought the good wine. The Brambles came, Briar on a cane he wielded like a marshal's baton. Sable came early and spent the first hour rearranging the furniture to her satisfaction, which took some doing, because Sable's satisfaction was a rigorous standard.

And Pip stood at the front of the room in her best coat, which was her only coat, and tried very hard to look solemn, and failed completely, because she kept catching people's eyes and grinning.

"You're supposed to be nervous," Emrys murmured.

"I'm supposed to be *ready*," Pip whispered back. "That's the whole point. You said a journeywoman's the one who's ready."

"I said a journeywoman is the one who knows she'll never be finished learning."

"Same thing, basically."

"*Pip.*"

"Sorry. Nervous. Look how nervous I am." She was vibrating slightly, the way she did before a storm broke, and Emrys recognized it with a pang of tenderness: the girl was full of weather. She had been from the beginning. All the teaching had ever done was give the storm somewhere honest to go.

The candle was the hard part. A journeywoman's candle had to be lit by her own calling—no flint, no borrowed flame—and Pip had practiced for weeks in the yard behind the shop, striking sparks off the evening air until her fingers ached. Now she stood before the plain white taper, and the room went quiet enough that Emrys could hear the river, far off, still running.

Pip breathed out. Closed her eyes. Emrys watched her gather—not reaching outward, the way Emrys had learned as a child, hungry and grasping, but drawing inward, down into whatever well the girl had found inside herself, that bright stubborn place that had kept her mother alive through the worst of the winter when the healers' herbs ran short. Her lips moved. Her hands rose.

Lightning did not strike. What happened was smaller and stranger: a single thread of pale gold light slid from Pip's fingertips and touched the candle-wick, and the flame that bloomed was not orange but white-gold, steady as a star, and every person in the crowded shop felt it—the pressure of it, the rightness, like a chord resolving. Someone gasped. Old Fen took off his hat.

"The word," Emrys said softly, and gave it to her, the old word recovered from the lattice, the one that meant *go and keep going*: "**Vael-síora**."

"Vael-síora," Pip repeated, voice steady, and the white-gold flame bent toward her like a listening thing.

Then the gift, and here the plan dissolved entirely, because apparently everyone had brought one. Marrow gave her a knife, honed and oiled, "for cutting lampwicks and lies." The Brambles gave her a compass that pointed, Briar swore, "at trouble, mostly," and Wren swore just as firmly that it worked. Sable gave her a folded map of the northern hills with a single route inked in red and no explanation, saying only, "When you're ready. You'll know." Old Fen gave her a rat skull, which was received with grace. And the neighbors—dozens of them, one after another—brought small things: buttons, ribbons, a tin whistle, a jar of plum preserves, a child's drawing of Pip standing on a rooftop with lightning in her fist, rendered in charcoal with a ferocity that made the whole room laugh and made Pip turn away quickly and wipe her face.

Last, Emrys stepped forward, and the room hushed again.

They had thought for a long time about what to give. In the end the answer had been waiting in the journals, in Sable's careful handwriting, in the record of everything Emrys had given away at the Stones: twenty years of exile, page after page of empty roads and rented rooms and the long practice of being no one. Emrys had read those pages and felt the absence of them like a missing tooth, the tongue returning again and again to the gap.

So they gave Pip the opposite. A memory of their own—one of the few they had chosen to keep entire, hoarded through the reforging like a coin sewn into a hem. They pressed their palm to Pip's forehead, and gave.

Pip swayed. Her eyes went wide. And Emrys knew what she was seeing, because they had chosen it deliberately: a market day, decades ago, before everything—a woman with flour on her hands laughing, a boy chasing a dog between the stalls, a girl of seven or eight sitting on a counter swinging her legs, watching her mother sell charms and thinking, with the absolute certainty of childhood, *that will be me someday.*

"That's—" Pip's voice cracked. "That's your mother's stall. That's *you*."

"That's what a beginning looks like," Emrys said. "Yours starts tonight."

Pip threw her arms around them hard enough to crack ribs, and the room cheered, and the white-gold candle burned on, and outside the window, above the rooftops of Greyhollow, the first stars were coming out—real stars, named and unnamed, the old constellations restored and a few new ones besides that the astronomers were already fighting about. Emrys held on to their journeywoman and looked at the lights over the city and thought that if someone had told them, a year ago, that this was what the end of the long winter would look like, they would not have believed it. They would not have dared.

* * *

Later—much later, when the neighbors had gone home singing and the shop smelled of wax and spilled wine and Pip had fallen asleep in the armchair with her new knife still clutched in one hand—Sable stayed behind to help bank the fire, and Marrow lingered in the doorway with her coat half on, and the three of them stood in the wreckage of the evening in comfortable silence.

"She'll be better than you," Sable said at last. "You know that."

"I count on it."

"Mm." Sable looked around the shop—at the shelves of charms now sold openly, at the sign Emrys had repainted in letters visible from across the square, ASHGROVE CHARM-WRIGHT & HEDGE-CRAFT, LESSONS ENQUIRED WITHIN. "There's a letter came for you today. I forgot, with everything." She dug into her map case and produced it: heavy paper, sealed with grey wax, bearing a crest Emrys hadn't seen in twenty years and had hoped, in some cowardly corner of themselves, never to see again.

Not the Wardens'. Those days were done. This was older—the seal of the Covenant Council, the new one, the one being argued into existence in a dozen cities, the one that had already written twice asking, politely, insistently, whether Emrys Ashgrove would consent to preside over the signing at the Stones in high summer.

Emrys turned the letter over in their hands. Third request. The envoys were arriving from beyond the Veil now—through the mended crossings, cautiously, wonderingly—and they wanted a storm-caller's name at the head of the document. They wanted the person who had paid the price.

"They won't stop asking, will they," Emrys said.

"No," said Sable. "And honestly? They shouldn't."

Marrow, from the doorway, said nothing at all, but her silence had a distinct shape, and the shape was agreement.

Emrys broke the seal. Read. Set the letter down on the counter beside the sleeping girl and the guttering white-gold candle, and looked out the window at the stars, and felt the old dread rise and, for the first time, find nothing in them to take hold of.

"All right," they said quietly. "Tell them yes."

Sable smiled her sideways smile. "I'll start drawing the route."
