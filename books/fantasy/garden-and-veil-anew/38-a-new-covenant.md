## 38. A New Covenant

### Emrys

The Stones had learned to sing again.

Emrys heard it first as a hum in the soles of their boots as they climbed the last switchback of the mountain road, then as something deeper—a resonance that lived in the ribs rather than the ears, the way thunder lives there, the way their mother's voice had lived there once. By the time they crested the ridge and saw the Covenant Stones standing whole against the morning sky, the sound had become unmistakable: seven great pillars of veined grey crystal, each one threaded through with light that moved like water under ice, all of them humming in a chord that no single voice could hold alone.

They had not looked like this when Emrys first saw them, half-dead and frost-blind, on the worst night of their second life. Then they had been broken teeth jutting from a wound in the world, black-veined, silent, weeping cold. Now frost laced their bases in patterns that were almost—almost—writing, and between them the air shimmered faintly, like heat-haze in reverse, like a curtain being held closed by hands that were learning to be gentle.

"You're staring," Sable said beside them, breath steaming. "Stare later. There are envoys."

There were envoys.

Emrys had known they were coming—had spent three months of letters and negotiations and one disastrous attempt at a shared translation spell making sure of it—but knowing was different from seeing. The meadow below the Stones, still patched with snow though spring had come to the valleys weeks ago, had become a small city of tents and banners and carefully arranged nothing-that-could-be-mistaken-for-a-weapon. Envoys from across the Veil's two sides stood in loose clusters, eyeing one another with the wary courtesy of cats introduced too soon.

From Greyhollow and the lowland cities: magistrates in sober wool, hedge-witches in every color, Pip's lamplighters' guild in their brass-buttoned coats, Marrow towering at the back of the Warden delegation with his deserter's badge worn openly now, polished, almost defiant. From beyond the Veil—the other side, the realm that had spent twenty years dark and starving as the covenant failed—figures Emrys had only read about in Sable's recovered archives: the tide-speakers of the drowned coast, tall and grey-skinned, their robes moving as if underwater; the root-bound elders of the deep forests, bark-skinned, patient, moss flowering in the seams of their shoulders; and, standing slightly apart from all of them, a delegation of the star-folk themselves, or what remained of them—slender, luminous, their skin holding the faint afterimage of constellations, like people who had been lamps for so long they could not fully stop glowing.

One of them turned as Emrys came down the hill. The star-folk envoy's face was difficult to look at directly—not because it was blinding but because it kept shifting, settling, shifting again, as if it could not decide which version of itself to wear. When it spoke, its voice was many voices speaking just barely out of sync.

*Ashgrove.* The name landed like a hand on the shoulder. *You are smaller than your mother.*

"So I've been told," Emrys said, and found, to their surprise, that they could smile. "She'd have said the same thing, probably louder."

The envoy made a sound that might have been laughter in any language. *We have come to sign,* it said. *And to ask what you will ask of us. We are told you preside.*

"I'm told that too." Emrys looked out over the meadow—at the tents, the banners, the Warden delegation standing stiffly under a banner that had been redesigned twice since winter, Corvess's sigil struck through and replaced with something newer, plainer, an open hand instead of a closed fist. At Tam, who stood at the head of that delegation in a captain's coat he had not yet decided whether to keep wearing, watching Emrys with an expression Emrys was still learning to read: hope wearing armor.

Twenty years ago, Emrys Ashgrove would have run from this meadow. Ten years ago they would have hidden in it. Even six months ago, standing before these same broken Stones with the long winter closing in, they had believed—had needed to believe—that the covenant should be reforged by someone else, anyone else, someone who hadn't lost three people in a storm and twenty years to exile and the taste of their own mother's bread.

But the Stones had chosen differently. Or perhaps they had simply waited, patient as mountains, for everyone else to finish refusing.

"Then let's begin," Emrys said.

### Sable

Sable Vane had drawn maps of places that did not exist, maps of places that existed but wished they didn't, and one memorable commission—a map of a wealthy merchant's grudges, rendered as a nautical chart—that had gotten her banned from three ports. She considered herself an expert on territory, borders, and the lies people drew around both.

So she watched the signing ceremony the way she watched everything: looking for where the lines actually ran.

The formalities took most of the day. Emrys stood at the center of the Stones' circle in a coat Sable had bullied them into—deep green, embroidered at the cuffs with lightning-work in silver thread, because "you're presiding over the rebinding of reality, you are not doing it in that charm-seller's apron"—and read aloud, line by line, the covenant as the original drafters had written it. Not the Warden's version. Not the blood-and-obedience version Aldric Veyra had twisted into his family's inheritance. The real one, recovered from the lattice inside the Stones themselves, transcribed by Sable's own aching hand over eleven sleepless nights.

*Memory freely given, realm to realm. What is offered cannot be taken. What is taken cannot bind. Let each who signs remember why they came, and let that remembering be the door.*

It was, Sable thought, watching the envoys step forward one by one to press their palms against the nearest Stone, a remarkably good contract. Short. Clear. No loopholes she could find, and she had looked. The old drafters had understood something the Wardens never had: that a covenant built on compulsion was just a cage with better manners, and cages always eventually broke—or worse, didn't.

The tide-speakers signed first, offering a memory of the first rain that ever fell on their coast. The Stone drank it in, and for a moment the pillar bloomed with images of a young ocean, and every person in the meadow felt, briefly, salt on their lips. The root-bound elders followed, giving up the memory of the first tree that learned to dream. The star-folk gave something that made the whole circle flare white—Sable caught only a fragment of it, a sense of vast slow joy, of light that loved the darkness it moved through—and when her vision cleared, several people were crying without knowing why, herself included.

Then the human delegations. Marrow signed for the deserters, offering the memory of the day he'd first doubted—an ordinary memory, a stolen loaf of bread shared with a Veil-touched child, doubt as small and holy as that. The magistrates signed. The guilds signed. Pip went up with the lamplighters, chin high, and gave the memory of the first lamp she'd ever lit, and Sable saw Emrys's face do something complicated and tender as the Stone showed it back to all of them: a girl of nine on a ladder too tall for her, flame cupped in shaking hands, absolutely certain she was holding back the dark.

Which left the Wardens. And Tam Ashgrove.

Sable had mapped this moment too, in the way she mapped everything she was afraid of—in precise, unsentimental detail, over and over, until the fear wore grooves she could walk in. Tam stepped forward. He had refused, in the weeks of negotiation, to say what memory he would give. He had refused to say whether he would give anything at all. The covenant required free offering; it could not be compelled, not even by the woman presiding, not even by a brother.

He pressed his palm to the Stone and said, clearly enough for the whole meadow to hear:

"I give the memory of my testimony."

The silence that followed had weight, like snowfall.

"The night of the storm," Tam went on, and his voice did not shake, which Sable suspected cost him more than shaking would have. "I said my sister called it deliberately. I said I saw her raise her hands against our neighbors. That was a lie. I said it because men in Warden coats told me my sister would hang either way, and that my lie could at least buy her exile instead of a rope. I carried that lie for twenty years and built a career on top of it, and I am done carrying it, and I am done building on it. Take it. It was never mine to keep."

The Stone flared—and this time the memory it gave back was not Tam's but Emrys's, the true night, the storm tearing loose from a frightened girl's grief, the neighbors running not away from malice but toward a collapsing house, the lightning striking where it struck because the sky was breaking and a child's power answered it the way a scream answers a wound. Everyone saw it. Everyone heard Tam's younger voice reciting the words men had fed him. And everyone saw Emrys, seventeen years old, standing in the ruins, reaching for her brother and finding only Warden coats between them.

When the light faded, Tam was still standing at the Stone, head bowed. And Emrys walked across the circle—not as the presiding authority, Sable noted, but simply as a person, boots crunching frost—and stopped in front of him.

"I remember giving you up," Emrys said quietly. Not the old words. Not the new ones. Something in between, in the voice of someone relearning a language. "I don't remember why. But I remember that it hurt, and I remember choosing it anyway. So." They held out their hand. "Whatever you gave up—we'll trade. Piece by piece. However long it takes."

Tam looked at the hand for a long moment. Then he took it, and whatever he said was for the two of them alone, and Sable, professional to the last, looked away and pretended to check her instruments and got frost in her eyelashes for her trouble.

### Pip

Pip had expected the hard part to be the magic.

That was the embarrassing truth she'd carried home from the mountain and never quite confessed: somewhere in the back of her mind, she'd thought the covenant ceremony would be mostly lightning. Big dramatic weather. Maybe the sky opening. Definitely something you could tell stories about afterward in the lamplighters' hall until the story grew a proper tail of exaggeration.

Instead it was mostly waiting, and standing very still, and watching grown-ups be brave in extremely quiet ways. Which, Pip was beginning to understand, was what most real magic looked like.

Her favorite part—her actual favorite, the part she'd already started telling and would keep telling until it grew that tail—was the evening after the signing, when the envoys stopped being envoys. Someone produced food. Of course someone produced food; the Bramble twins had arrived two days early with a cart specifically provisioned for diplomatic incidents, and one of them—Brenna, the one with the scarred eyebrow—had appointed herself master of ceremonies for the feast with the same brisk competence she'd once used for smuggling contraband past checkpoints. Her brother Colm sat nearby on a bench, his leg propped on a crate, still walking with a stick months after the pass at Ashline, complaining cheerfully about the quality of diplomatic cheese.

And the star-folk danced.

Not the way humans danced. They moved in slow spirals, and where they passed, the frost on the grass lit up in patterns—constellations, Pip realized, actual constellations, the ones that had come back to the sky in the weeks since the reforging, drawn glowing on the ground for everyone to walk through. Children from the Warden delegation chased the lights. A tide-speaker taught a Greyhollow magistrate a song that required humming underwater, which the magistrate attempted with catastrophic results and tremendous dignity. Somewhere near the fires, Marrow was teaching root-bound elders a drinking song, and the elders were learning it with the grave deliberation of people who intended to outlast the song's original civilization.

Pip sat on a crate with her teacher and watched all of it.

"They're going to mess it up," she said. "Aren't they. The new covenant. Somebody's going to find a loophole, or hoard memories anyway, or start a war about whose constellation is whose."

"Oh, definitely," Emrys said.

Pip blinked. "You're supposed to say something hopeful. You're the presiding authority."

"I'm also a charm-seller who spent twenty years watching institutions eat people." Emrys turned the storm-glass amulet over in their fingers—whole now, uncracked, but Pip noticed they still handled it like something breakable. "They'll mess it up. They'll find loopholes. In fifty years some magistrate will invent a way to take memories 'for the common good,' and there'll be a scandal, and maybe a war, and someone will have to fix it all over again." They shrugged. "That's not failure, Pip. That's just what covenants are. Living things. They need tending. That's why the old drafters wrote *let each who signs remember why they came.* Not so it would work forever. So that when it breaks, people remember it was worth fixing."

Pip chewed on that for a while, watching the star-folk spiral, watching frost-light constellations bloom and fade under running feet.

"That's why you're teaching," she said finally. "Isn't it. The shop. The classes. You're not just being nice. You're tending."

Emrys smiled—the real one, the one that had gotten less rare all spring. "Don't tell the magistrates. They think I'm being institutional."

### Tam

Tam Ashgrove had attended perhaps four hundred ceremonies in his years as a Warden captain. Investitures, purges, attestations, the annual rite where High Warden Corvess had renewed her claim on the city's fear. He knew how ceremonies worked: they were machines for making power look like order.

This one kept failing to be a machine. People kept laughing. Delegations kept dissolving into conversations that had nothing to do with jurisdiction. An elder whose people had been enemies of humanity for longer than Greyhollow had existed spent an hour showing Tam's youngest ensigns how to fold shadow into shapes, and the ensigns were terrible at it, and nobody cared.

He kept waiting for the trap. That was the residue of twenty years of Corvess's service—you learned that generosity was bait, that openness was positioning, that every gift had a hook in it. He had watched his former commander take the ancestors' memories back into herself and unravel, had seen certainty die in her eyes mid-sentence, and he had thought he was done with that world. But the habits stayed. Trust, for Tam, remained a foreign country he was mapping slowly, badly, one border at a time.

On the second evening, the star-folk envoy sought him out.

It found him at the edge of the firelight, apart, the way he still instinctively stood apart. Its shifting face settled—for him, he realized, deliberately—into something almost human, almost familiar.

*You gave up the lie,* it said. *The others gave gifts. You gave a wound.*

"It wasn't doing me any good."

*No,* the envoy agreed. *Wounds rarely do. But listen, warden-captain, because this is why I crossed the dark to stand in your cold meadow.* Its many-voice dropped, became almost private. *My people kept the covenant's far side for a thousand generations. We remembered what your founder stole, because remembering is what we are. And in the long dark, when your side forgot us entirely, do you know what we feared most? Not your armies. Your forgetting. A realm that forgets its debts becomes capable of anything.* The shifting face tilted. *Today your brother gave back a debt. Publicly. Freely. With witnesses. My people saw it. So I will tell you what we will tell our councils: the humans are learning. Slowly. Badly. As all young things learn. But learning.*

Tam found, to his considerable discomfort, that his throat had gone tight.

"And if we stop?" he managed. "If we forget again?"

*Then you will have to be reminded,* the envoy said, and its voice carried, suddenly, all the many voices at once, warm and terrible, *and we are very, very good at remembering.*

It bowed—a gesture it had clearly studied from humans and gotten slightly wrong, the way one gets a borrowed language slightly wrong—and drifted back toward the fires, leaving Tam standing in the dark with his heart hammering and the strange, unfamiliar sensation of having been, for the first time in his life, on the right side of a reckoning.

### Emrys

On the last morning, before the delegations packed their banners and began the long roads home, Emrys climbed alone to the tallest Stone.

The covenant lattice hummed beneath their palms, steady now, a heartbeat rather than a fever. Through it they could feel the threads—thousands of them, millions, freely given memories braided realm to realm, each one a small light in the weave. Their mother's thread was there somewhere. Pip's lamp-flame. Marrow's stolen bread. Tam's surrendered lie, transformed now into something that glowed like a scar healing. And somewhere in the braid, twenty years of exile that Emrys had given without knowing what they gave, held safe, held *for* them, the way Sable held the journals.

They stood there a long time, listening.

Below, the meadow was waking—tents coming down, wagons loading, Brenna Bramble loudly negotiating transit fees from three governments simultaneously, Pip racing the dawn to relight the boundary-lamps the old-fashioned way, by hand, because she said the traditional way mattered even when the traditional way was obsolete. Sable stood at the meadow's edge with her charts spread on a folding table, already sketching the routes the envoys would travel home, already arguing with a tide-speaker about coastal currents, already, unmistakably, planning the next journey.

Because there would be one. Emrys could feel it in the lattice the way they'd once felt storms coming: a thread at the far edge of the weave, thin and flickering, running out past the mapped realms into a darkness the covenant had never reached. Something else was out there. Something older than the old drafters. And it had begun, very gently, to pull.

Emrys closed their eyes and let the Stones sing through them, and thought: *Let it wait until after breakfast.*

Then they went down the mountain to find the others.
