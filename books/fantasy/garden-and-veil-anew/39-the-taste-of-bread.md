## 39. The Taste of Bread

The kitchen behind the charm shop had never been meant for baking. It had been meant for storage—jars of river-salt and bundled rowan, coils of copper wire, the dusty paraphernalia of a life spent pretending to be smaller than it was. But in the weeks since the stars had come back, since the covenant had been reforged and the long winter broken, the room had been slowly, stubbornly converted. Pip had scrubbed the shelves herself, humming, her hedge-witch's apron already singed in three places from lessons that had gone enthusiastically wrong. The Bramble twins had carried up an iron stove from the Salt Road inn they'd bought at auction, grumbling the whole way up the stairs and then refusing any thanks for it. And Sable Vane had claimed the corner by the window as hers alone, dragging in a worktable, a mortar and pestle, and a growing stack of journals that smelled of dust and old ink and, lately, of flour.

Emrys woke that morning to the smell of it.

Not flour, exactly. Not yeast, though both were there, warm and living in the air. It was something underneath—caraway, maybe, and a sweetness like honey that had been heated just to the edge of patience, and beneath all of it a ghost of woodsmoke that had no business being in a city oven. Emrys lay still beneath the blankets and let it drift up through the floorboards, and felt something move in their chest that they had no name for. It was like hearing a voice call a name you no longer answered to. It was like standing in a doorway of a house you'd never seen and knowing, absolutely, which floorboard creaked.

They got up. They dressed. They went down the stairs with their heart beating strangely, quickly, the way it had beaten on the mountain when the first star had relit.

The kitchen was chaos in the way that had become, without anyone announcing it, the shape of their family. Pip sat cross-legged on the counter, a bowl of glaze in her lap that she was supposed to be stirring and was instead eating with one finger. One of the Brambles—Emrys still couldn't always tell them apart when they were being quiet, which was rare—was attempting to repair a cabinet hinge with more enthusiasm than skill. And Sable stood at the worktable with her sleeves rolled to the elbow and flour on her sharp cheekbones like war paint, bent over a journal with an expression Emrys knew well: the one she wore over maps, over riddles, over anything that resisted her and was therefore, by her reckoning, worth conquering.

"You're up early," Sable said, without looking up.

"You're baking," Emrys said. "You don't bake."

"I contain multitudes." Sable turned the journal toward herself again. "Sit down. Don't look at what I'm doing. If you look, you'll see the recipe, and then it won't be a surprise, and I've spent three days on this surprise, and I will not have it ruined by your face."

"My face has ruined nothing."

"Your face ruined the solstice market. You looked at the fireworks Pip made and cried in front of the entire east quarter. People talk, Emrys. The Charm-Seller Who Weeps at Pretty Lights. You have a reputation now."

"I have a reputation," Emrys said, sitting, "because I stopped hiding. That was the entire point."

Pip snorted glaze. The Bramble said, without turning from the hinge, "He's got you there, Vane."

Emrys let the noise of them wash over them and tried to be ordinary. It was harder than it used to be. For twenty years, ordinary had been a discipline—head down, hands still, feelings filed away in the same locked drawer as the storm-glass amulet. Now the drawer stood open all the time. Now feelings arrived uninvited, in the smell of bread, in the sound of Pip's laughter, in the particular way Sable's voice went gentle when she thought no one was listening. Emrys had traded away twenty years of memory to light the stars, and what remained sometimes felt like a house with all its windows suddenly unshuttered—light pouring into rooms that had been dark so long the light itself was almost painful.

The smell came again, stronger now. Woodsmoke. Caraway. Honey at the edge of burning.

Emrys's hands, resting on the table, had begun to tremble.

They didn't know why. That was the strangest part—the not-knowing, the way grief could arrive without its story attached. The journals Sable had spent months compiling held everything: every memory Emrys had given to the Stones, written down in Sable's precise cartographer's hand so that it wouldn't be lost entirely, so that Emrys could one day choose what to take back. Emrys had read some of them. Not many. It was an odd thing, reading about your own life like a stranger's—*the subject wept at the border of the Ashline; the subject could not sleep for a week after learning of the brother; the subject, aged eleven, called their first lightning into a copper kettle and was scolded for a fortnight.* They knew these things the way they knew the history of a country they'd visited once. True, but not theirs. Not yet. Maybe not ever.

But the body remembered what the mind had given away. The body had kept its own journal, written in flour-smell and woodsmoke and the specific ache behind the sternum that had no name.

"Emrys." Sable's voice, closer now. "You've gone somewhere."

"Nowhere," Emrys said, which was a lie, because they had gone somewhere—they had gone to a kitchen they couldn't picture, in a house they couldn't name, where someone they couldn't remember had stood at an oven with her sleeves rolled up, and the whole house had smelled like this, exactly like this, and Emrys had been—how old? Small. They had been small. They knew they had been small the way you know the sun will rise.

Sable studied them for a long moment with those gray eyes that had seen through every disguise Emrys had ever worn, including the ones Emrys hadn't known they were wearing. Then she went back to her worktable and said, briskly, "It'll be ready within the hour. Pip, if you eat any more of that glaze I'm docking your wages."

"I don't get wages."

"Then I'm docking your dessert."

"You can't dock my dessert from a dessert—"

"I can dock it *metaphysically*."

Emrys sat at the table and let them argue, and watched the light move across the floor, and waited for whatever was coming the way you wait out a storm—not with fear, exactly, but with the deep animal attention of someone who has learned that weather always means something.

* * *

The loaf came out of the oven at midmorning, round and dark-crusted, split along the top in the way that good bread splits when the baker has scored it with confidence. Sable set it on the worktable to cool and would not let anyone touch it, which nearly caused a diplomatic incident with Pip. The whole shop filled with the smell until it seemed the walls themselves were made of it—woodsmoke and caraway and honey, and under that, faint as a watermark, something green and growing, like a garden after rain.

Emrys stood by the window and tried to breathe normally.

"Right," Sable said at last. She wiped her hands on her apron. She had put on a clean apron for this, Emrys realized. She had done her hair. Sable Vane, who had once crossed the Ashline in a hailstorm complaining about the state of her boots, had dressed for this the way other people dressed for coronations, and the understanding of what that meant settled into Emrys's chest like a stone into still water.

She cut three slices. Only three. She set one before Pip, one before Emrys, and kept one for herself, and then she stood there with her hands flat on the table, and for the first time since Emrys had known her, Sable Vane looked like she didn't know what to say.

"You wrote it down," Emrys said quietly. "In the journals. My mother's recipe."

"She talked the whole time she baked, according to her sister—your aunt, who kept the letters. I got the recipe from a letter your aunt wrote in the year you were nine. There were measurements missing. There were *instructions* missing. 'Bake until it's done, and don't you dare open the oven to check, Aldara, I mean it.' Your mother wrote like she talked, from the sound of it." Sable's mouth twitched. "I tested it eleven times. The Brambles ate the failures. They have complaints about the fourth one."

"The fourth one was *fine*," said the Bramble from the doorway, where both twins had silently gathered, because of course they had. Neither of them would say why they were here. Neither of them had to.

"You don't have to eat it," Sable said to Emrys, and now her voice had gone gentle, the gentle she thought no one heard. "I want to be very clear about that. I didn't do this to make you feel something. I did it because—" She stopped. Started again. Sable Vane, who could talk her way past any checkpoint in the nine provinces, stopped and started again. "Because a person shouldn't lose the taste of their mother's bread. That's all. That's the whole of it. Whatever's in the journals, it's yours to take back or leave forever. But the *bread*—the bread should exist in the world regardless. It was good. Things that were good should keep existing."

Emrys looked down at the slice.

The crust was dark and split just so. Steam rose from the cut face in a slow, curling ribbon, and in the steam was the smell, the whole of it, the woodsmoke and the honey and the green garden rain, and Emrys's hands were shaking so badly they had to press them flat against the table.

"I don't remember it," Emrys said. "I want you to know that. I don't remember her kitchen. I don't remember her face. I gave—" Their voice caught. They tried again. "I gave all of it. Twenty years. It bought the stars back, and I'd do it again, and I'm not sorry. But I don't remember."

"I know," Sable said.

"So whatever happens when I eat this—it won't be memory. It can't be. There's nothing there to remember *with*."

"I know," Sable said again. "Eat it anyway."

Emrys picked up the slice.

The crust crackled under their fingers, warm as a living thing. They brought it to their mouth. They hesitated—one breath, two, the way you hesitate at the edge of cold water—and Pip, uncharacteristically silent, reached over and rested her hand briefly on Emrys's wrist, and that small weight was somehow the thing that let them go forward.

They bit.

And the world—

The world *opened*.

It was not memory. It was not the past returning, not the lost years flooding back, not any of the things that Emrys had braced for or longed for or feared. It was something stranger and more terrible and more tender than all of that. It was the taste itself, arriving whole and complete into a body that had been starving for it without knowing it was starving—caraway and honey and woodsmoke and the green ghost of a garden, and beneath it all a warmth that was not the warmth of the oven but of the hands that had shaped the dough, and Emrys was *small*, they were small and safe and the kitchen was full of morning light, and a voice was singing low and off-key about the rain coming to the barley, and none of it had a face or a name or a place in time, and all of it was *true*.

The bread was gone. Emrys didn't remember eating it. They were standing—the table pushed back, the chair toppled, when had they stood?—with their hands over their mouth and tears coming down their face in a flood that had no bottom, great silent sobs that bent them double, that wracked them like a storm they had no power to call or stop.

They did not know why they were weeping.

That was the truth of it, the plain and unbearable truth: they wept and did not know why. The grief was real and enormous and *orphaned*—a grief for someone they could not name, over something they could not recall, in a kitchen that existed now only as warmth in the mouth and light on the walls of a house that had been dust for twenty years. They wept for the taste. They wept for the not-remembering. They wept because the bread was good, and their mother had been good, and both of those things had nearly been lost to the world forever, and one stubborn cartographer with flour on her face had refused to let them be.

Pip was crying too. Pip was always crying at things lately, and proud of it. She had her arms around Emrys's middle, her cheek pressed against their shoulder, and she was saying, over and over, "That's her. That's her, that's her, you can feel her, can't you, you can feel her—" and Emrys could, that was the thing, they *could*, not as memory but as presence, the way you feel the sun on your face on the first warm day of spring without being able to say where the light has been all winter.

The Brambles had turned to face the wall, both of them, shoulders shaking, pretending with tremendous dignity to be deeply interested in the cabinet hinge.

And Sable—Sable stood on the other side of the table with her own slice untouched and her own eyes bright, and when Emrys could finally speak, when the storm had spent itself down to a fine rain, what came out of them was not a word at all but a question, small and wondering, aimed at no one and everyone:

"What was her name?"

The kitchen went still.

"Aldara," Sable said softly. "Aldara Ashgrove. She grew barley and charms and one extremely stubborn child. She scored her loaves in a spiral, because she said the spiral kept the luck from leaking out." A pause. "I put the spiral on. I hope I did it right."

Emrys looked at the loaf. The cut in the crust curled around the crown of it in a slow, unbroken line, a spiral, a small galaxy baked into bread, and the laugh that came out of them was half a sob and entirely helpless.

"You did it right," Emrys said. "I don't know how I know, but I know. You did it right."

"You know because your hands know," Sable said. "The journals can hold the words, Emrys. But some things live deeper than ink. That was the whole lesson of the covenant, wasn't it? Memory freely given doesn't die. It just—changes hands. Roots somewhere new." She pushed her own slice across the table toward Emrys. "Eat the rest. There's a second loaf proving. And a third. I intend to make this every week until it stops making you cry, and then I intend to make it every week after that, because I'll have gotten good at it, and I refuse to waste competence."

Emrys sat back down. They ate the second slice slowly, deliberately, letting each bite arrive and be felt—the caraway, the honey, the smoke, the garden—and this time they did not weep, or rather, they wept the whole time, quietly, steadily, the way rain falls on a field that needs it, and no one in the kitchen thought anything of it at all.

Pip licked glaze off her thumb and said, "When I'm a journeywoman, I'm learning this recipe first. Before any storm-calling. I mean it. Storms can wait. This can't."

"Your mother said the same thing about my cooking," the Bramble said.

"Your cooking once gave Sable visions."

"Visions of *flavor*, Pip. There's a difference."

The noise rose again, warm and argumentative and alive, and Emrys sat in the middle of it with the taste of their mother on their tongue and their tears drying salt on their cheeks, and understood, in the way that you understand things at the end of a long road, that this was what they had bought with twenty lost years. Not the stars. Not the covenant. This. A kitchen full of people who would learn a dead woman's bread recipe and argue about glaze and stand facing the wall so their friend could grieve in privacy. The stars had been the payment. This was the change returned.

Later, when the loaves were eaten and the kitchen quieted, when Pip had gone to light the lamps and the Brambles had gone to argue with a delivery cart, Sable lingered by the worktable, stacking her journals with unusual care.

"There's one more thing," she said, not quite looking at Emrys. "In the journals. About your mother. Something your aunt wrote, near the end of her letters. I've been deciding for three days whether to tell you now or let you find it."

Emrys's heart, which had only just settled, began again its strange quick beating. "Tell me."

Sable pulled a single journal from the stack—a thin one, its cover worn soft—and set it on the table between them, but did not open it. "Your aunt wrote that Aldara kept a garden. Behind the house. She said your mother used to say that the covenant between the realms was like a garden—that it didn't want a warden, it wanted a *gardener*. That it didn't need to be guarded." Sable's gray eyes came up, and they were shining. "It needed to be *planted*, and tended, and given freely, and it would feed everyone who came to it. Emrys—she wrote that down twenty years before the Stones ever shattered. Your mother knew. Maybe she always knew."

Emrys looked out the window, at the small yard behind the shop where the last of the snow was pulling back from the dark earth, where the ground lay open and waiting and the light came down gold through the new-lit stars' old familiar sky.

"Sable," Emrys said slowly, "how do you feel about helping me dig a garden bed tomorrow?"

Sable Vane smiled like a woman who had already drawn the map. "I was hoping you'd ask."
