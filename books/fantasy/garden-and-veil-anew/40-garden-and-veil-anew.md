## 40. Garden and Veil Anew

The soil behind the charm shop had not been turned in living memory—or at least not in Emrys's memory, which these days amounted to nearly the same thing.

They knelt in the cold spring earth with a spade that Sable had sharpened twice because she couldn't stand a dull edge, and they worked slowly, the way one does when there is finally time to be slow. The alley behind the shop was narrow and crooked, hemmed by Greyhollow's leaning rooftops, but above it the sky opened wide and clean, and the stars were out. All of them. Emrys still caught themselves counting sometimes, an old habit from the first weeks after the reforging, when every dawn had come with the small terror that the dark might return like a debt unpaid. But the stars held. They wheeled overhead in their patient courses, and the moon rose full over the eastern gables, and the night smelled of thawed earth and woodsmoke and, faintly, of the sea of grass beyond the Ashline, where the land was remembering its colors.

Emrys pressed the spade into the soil and turned it over. Worms writhed away from the light of the lantern Pip had hung on a nail—Pip insisted gardens needed lanterns, "for morale," though whose morale, hers or the seeds', she declined to specify—and Emrys worked them gently back under with two fingers, murmuring an apology so old it predated their exile, so old it was one of the few things that had survived intact.

*Sorry, friends. We're making room.*

That was the thing about giving something away, they were learning. Everyone assumed you ended up with less. Twenty years ago—if it had been twenty years; the journals said so, in Sable's precise hand, and Emrys believed her the way one believes a lighthouse—twenty years ago, Emrys Ashgrove would have told you that sacrifice was subtraction. That to give was to be hollowed. They had built a whole life on that arithmetic: give nothing, keep everything, stay small enough that the world's hunger could not find you. Sell charms that did almost nothing. Heal no one. Love no one loudly. Keep your name folded up small in your pocket where even you could not read it.

And then they had stood on the mountain at the Covenant Stones and poured twenty years of exile out of themselves like water from a cracked jug, and everyone had warned them—it had been *them*, hadn't it, who'd most feared it—that what remained would be an emptiness shaped like a person.

It had not been an emptiness. It had been a garden bed.

Not immediately. There had been terrible days at first, days when Tam had stood in the snow saying *Emrys, it's me, it's Tam* and Emrys had looked at this broad-shouldered stranger with his Warden-grey cloak and felt only the polite blankness one feels toward any unknown face, and Tam had wept, and Emrys had felt the wrongness of their own calm like a stone in the chest. There had been nights when they woke reaching for a memory of fear or loneliness the way a tongue seeks a missing tooth, and found instead a smooth new surface, and wondered what had grown there, and whether it was theirs.

But things grew. That was the miracle no one had promised. Grief, once given away, left room for grief's opposite. The space where twenty years of hiding had lived filled—not all at once, not cleanly, but steadily—with other things: the taste of Sable's terrible coffee, the sound of Pip laughing so hard she fell off the shop counter, the weight of Marrow's hand on their shoulder when he came down from the inn at Lastlight for market days, the sight of the Bramble twins arguing cheerfully across a breakfast table while one of them relearned to walk on a leg the healers swore should have been lost. The covenant ran through all of it now, thin and bright as a vein of silver in stone, and Emrys could feel it when they let themselves feel anything at all: the two worlds breathing together again, realm against realm, like two people sleeping back to back for warmth.

They turned another row of soil. Their hands knew this work. That was the strangest gift of all—the body remembered what the mind had surrendered. When Sable had baked the bread, months ago now, from the recipe recovered from the journals, Emrys had wept without knowing why, and their hands had known how to knead before their heart knew what it was mourning. Some things went deeper than memory. Some things rooted.

* * *

Pip came clattering down the back stairs well past midnight, as she always did, wrapped in a coat three sizes too large that had belonged—according to the journals—to Emrys, back when Emrys had owned such a coat. She carried a clay pot cradled against her chest like a sleeping animal.

"You're still digging," she said. "You've been digging since sundown. It's a garden, not a grave."

"Thank you for that image."

"You're welcome." She set the pot down on the upturned earth with exaggerated care. Inside it, a seedling trembled—a storm-bloom, silvery-leaved, its petals closed tight as fists. Hedge-witchery had come back into the open since the winter ended, unregulated and uncertain and occasionally chaotic, and Pip had thrown herself into it with the ferocity of someone making up for years of practicing by lamplight in a cellar. She was sixteen now, or claimed to be; she had been fifteen for so long that Emrys suspected she'd simply decided to stop counting until it became convenient. Her journeywoman's knot was tied at her wrist in green cord, fraying already, because Pip treated every symbol she earned as something to be worn hard.

"That's the last of the storm-blooms," she said. "From the cuttings off the ones at the Stones. If it takes, we'll have flowers that glow during thunderstorms. If it doesn't take, I'll have murdered the only cutting in Greyhollow and we'll never speak of it."

"It'll take," Emrys said. "Storm-blooms grow in ruined ground. It's practically their whole philosophy."

Pip crouched beside them, sleeves pushed up, and began turning soil with her bare hands in the way of the young and the impatient. "Sable says the envoys from the other side arrive next month. Through the Stones. Actual people, from the actual other realm, walking through actual standing stones into our actual city." She shook her head. "I keep trying to picture it and all I get is the Hollow Bells village, before. All those people standing frozen. And now there's going to be *traffic*."

"The bells ring again there," Emrys said quietly. They had gone, in the autumn, all of them, to see the village wake. It had been Marrow who suggested it—"you should see what you bought back"—and they had stood in the square while the frost lifted from the villagers' eyes and a baker finished a loaf he had begun forgetting a year before, and an old woman had looked at her own hands mid-task and laughed at nothing, at everything, at being returned to herself. The bells had rung vespers that evening for the first time in two years. Emrys had not been able to explain to anyone why they had needed to sit down afterward on the church steps and simply breathe.

"I know," Pip said. "I was there." A pause, and then, gentler, in the way she had learned to be gentle with them, which was its own kind of education: "I know you don't remember being there. I remember *for* both of us. That's the deal, right? That's what the journals are for."

"That's the deal."

She dug her fingers into the soil, made a hole, looked up. "Well? Are we doing this or are you going to philosophize at the dirt all night?"

Emrys laughed—a real laugh, unguarded, the kind they had not known how to make for twenty years and now made daily, wastefully, like a man spending coins he'd forgotten he had. They took the seedling from its pot, teased the roots loose, and settled it into the earth. Together, without needing to speak of it, they called a little water up from the deep ground—not magic, exactly, or not only magic; just attention, the oldest spell there is—and packed the soil close around the stem.

Above them, a star flickered. Just once. Not dimming—*winking*, if a star could wink, and lately Emrys had begun to suspect that it could, that the reforged covenant was not a machine but a conversation, and conversations had moods.

"Did you see that?" Pip whispered.

"I saw it."

"Do you think it means something?"

"I think," Emrys said, patting the soil flat, "that we planted a storm-bloom under an open sky on the night the veil between worlds runs thinnest, and something on the other side noticed us gardening. I think that counts as meaning something."

* * *

Sable found them an hour later, both of them filthy and triumphant, sitting on the back step sharing a heel of bread. She stood in the doorway with a lamp in one hand and a leather folio under her arm, and she looked at the turned earth, the lantern, the two of them, and her sharp face did the thing it did instead of smiling—which was to go carefully neutral, as though any strong emotion might be used against her in court.

"You've ruined my drainage plan," she said.

"There was no drainage plan."

"There was *always* a drainage plan. I drew it in autumn. You agreed to it in autumn."

"I have no memory of agreeing to it," Emrys said, and the joke landed the way it always landed now—softly, sadly, warmly, all three at once—because none of them had quite stopped flinching at that sentence, and none of them ever would, and they had all decided together that the flinching was a fair price.

Sable sat down on the step, set the lamp between them, and opened the folio across her knees. Emrys knew what it was before she touched it. She brought it out on certain nights, never announced, never explained. The journals. Forty-one volumes now, in her mercilessly neat cartographer's hand: everything Emrys had given away, recorded in ink so that the gift could be visited like a grave, or a garden.

"You don't have to," Emrys said. It was ritual. Both halves of it.

"I know I don't have to." Also ritual. She turned to a marked page. "It's the anniversary soon. Of the reforging. I thought you might want to choose something early this year. You always wait until the mountain, and then you cry on the mountain, and the mountain has enough weather of its own."

Pip snorted bread crumbs. Emrys elbowed her.

"What is it?" they asked.

Sable hesitated. In twenty years of exile, Emrys had learned to read hesitation like weather, and Sable's hesitation was rare enough to be its own season. "It's small," she said. "Which is either mercy or cruelty, depending. It's the taste of your mother's bread. You gave it up in chapter twelve—" she caught herself using the word *chapter*, an old joke between them about the book Pip kept threatening to write, "—on the road, practicing callings. You gave up the taste of it first, and the smell of it later, and the shape of her kitchen last. I wrote down what you told me about it, that night in the cave. Rye and caraway. Crust that cracked like ice on a puddle. She sang while she kneaded, badly, some song about a fox."

The words went into Emrys like rain into dry ground. They could hear the description. They could not hear the bread. Somewhere in them was a smooth place where that taste had been, and around the smooth place, everything else—Sable's voice, the lamplight, Pip's shoulder warm against theirs, the smell of turned earth and the star-silvered sky—pressed close and alive.

"Read it again," Emrys said. "The part about the song."

"*Some song about a fox,*" Sable repeated. "You never remembered the words. You said she got them wrong every time and you loved her more for it."

Emrys closed their eyes. Behind their eyelids, stars bloomed—afterimages, or perhaps not afterimages at all. Perhaps the covenant, keeping its accounts. Perhaps their mother, on the far side of a veil grown thin and kind, singing badly about a fox.

"No," they said at last. "Not tonight. Leave it in the book a while longer." They opened their eyes and smiled at Sable, and watched her fierce careful face decide to trust them with the decision. "Some things are better as seeds than as harvests. Ask anyone. I'm told I'm something of an authority."

Sable closed the folio. She did not smile, exactly. But she leaned, very slightly, until her shoulder rested against Emrys's, which from Sable Vane was a sonnet.

* * *

Later—much later, when Pip had fallen asleep against the doorframe and been carried upstairs, grumbling, to her cot; when Sable had gone home to her maps and her meticulous, unlabeled tendernesses—Emrys stayed in the garden alone.

The storm-bloom seedling stood in its fresh-turned bed, leaves silvered by starlight, and around it Emrys had set the rest of the night's plantings: foxglove, because some jokes deserved to be perennial; sage and rue for the hedge-witch apprentices who would come tripping down the back stairs within the year, because they always did; a row of plain brown beans, because Marrow had said a garden ought to feed somebody and not just mean things at them. Frost was long gone from the air, but Emrys breathed on their palms anyway, out of habit, and felt the small answering warmth of their own storm-blood, quiet now, domesticated, a hearth-fire where there had once been a hurricane.

They sat back on their heels and looked up.

The stars were all there. Every one the Wardens' founder had stolen, every one the covenant had died withholding, every one that had gone out over Greyhollow on the worst night of a bad century—they burned, steady and shameless, and between them the Veil shimmered like heat-haze in reverse, like the first breath of spring made visible. Somewhere beyond it, another world's gardeners were looking up at the same light from the other direction. Next month they would walk through the Stones and argue about tariffs and treaty language and whether hedge-witches required licensing, and it would be tedious and miraculous in equal measure, and Emrys would preside over it with dirt still under their fingernails, because someone had to, and because they had learned that the opposite of invisibility was not glory. It was presence. It was showing up, again and again, with your hands in the soil.

Twenty years of exile, given away. And look what had rooted in the empty rows: a brother learning to be a brother from scratch, patiently, the way one learns a difficult instrument. A city learning that magic was neither sin nor salvation, just weather, just work. A girl with a green knot at her wrist who would out-teach them all within five years and knew it. A mapmaker who had mapped a path through a dying world and then, without ever saying so, had begun mapping a way back through the country of a stranger's grief, one journal entry at a time, so that nothing given was ever truly lost—only held, the way soil holds water, the way a friend holds a truth you cannot carry yourself.

Some things, once given, root deeper than memory. Emrys had spent a whole life believing the inverse, and had been wrong, and the wrongness had cost a war's worth of winter, and the correction had cost them everything they were—and what grew in the space where they had been was better. Smaller-rooted, maybe. Needing watering. Needing company.

Needing, Emrys thought, rising, brushing the earth from their knees, exactly this: a narrow crooked garden behind a charm shop in a city that had forgotten itself and learned to remember, under a sky that had learned to stay lit.

They picked up the lantern. They paused at the door, as they always did, and looked back—one last count of the stars, one last check of the seedling, one last breath of the night air, which smelled of rye and caraway from Sable's kitchen window next door, though Emrys could not have said why that smell undid them slightly, every time, like a hand briefly closing around their heart in greeting.

Behind them, in the fresh-turned bed, the storm-bloom shivered though there was no wind.

And in the morning, when Pip came clattering down to check on it, she would find that overnight, impossibly, out of season, out of reason, it had opened its first flower—a small silver bell, tilted upward, glowing softly, pointed not at the sun but at the last fading star, as if listening.

As if answering.

As if something, on the far side of the veil, had seen a garden planted in good faith under an open sky, and had decided—freely, gladly, the way all the best covenants are made—to send one back.
