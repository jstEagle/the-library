## 1. The Ledger of Impossible Things

There is a particular way a man walks when he is trying to be furniture. I know it because I have practiced it for eleven years: shoulders rounded just enough to look apologetic, eyes down but not so far down that they seem shifty, hands occupied with something dull and official-looking. A ledger works beautifully for this purpose. Nobody suspects the clerk. The world has agreed, by some unspoken treaty, that clerks are beneath notice — which is precisely why I became one.

The Guild of Impossible Things occupies a narrow building on Candlemaker's Row, wedged between a chandler who swears his tallow screams when rendered (it doesn't; I've checked) and a widow who sells what she insists are relics of Saint Vessia's foot (there were four toes in her last shipment; saints, presumably, have ten). Our sign is painted in flaking gold leaf: *Appraisals, Acquisitions, Custody*. Beneath it, smaller, in someone's long-ago moment of honesty: *No Refunds on Miracles*.

We keep, in the guildhouse, an inventory that would get any of us hanged if the wrong magistrate ever read past the first page. A lantern that burns only in the presence of lies — it has been lit continuously since the reign of old King Ademola, which tells you something about the air at court. A jar of captured echoes from a cathedral that burned two centuries ago; shake it gently and you hear the choir, mid-hymn, forever interrupted. A key that opens no lock but instead closes doors that were never shut. And in the cellar, behind three locks and a door painted to look like damp brickwork, the things we do not list even in the deep ledger.

My name is Mara Oyelaran, and I am the keeper of those ledgers. Officially, I am a modest talent with numbers and a beautiful hand. Unofficially — unofficially is where I live, the way a fish lives in water, invisibly and always one breath from drowning.

* * *

That morning began the way all my mornings begin: with ink, bread gone slightly stale, and the low argumentative murmur of the guildhouse waking up around me. Sefu, our guildmaster, was already in his office pretending not to be awake, which is his own version of walking like furniture. He is a broad man gone soft at the edges, with a beard he trims himself and therefore trims badly, and he has run this guild for thirty years without once being arrested, which in this city is a form of genius.

"Mara." His voice through the door, before I'd even reached my desk. "The Ferro woman is coming about the sword. Be gentle with her."

"Am I ever anything else?"

"You're ever everything else," Sefu said, "but you hide it well."

I chose to take that as a compliment. It was probably a warning. With Sefu the two sound identical, which may be the most useful thing he ever taught me.

Widow Ferro arrived at the ninth bell, wrapped in grey wool despite the mild weather, carrying a long bundle the way you carry a sleeping child you're not entirely sure you want to wake. She had come to us twice before — once to appraise a ring that whispered her late husband's name when worn (we advised her to sell it; she kept it), and once simply to sit in our front room because, as she put it, "the objects here don't look at me like I'm mad."

"Master Oyelaran." She set the bundle on my table with exaggerated care. "It's started again."

"Started what again, Widow Ferro?"

"It remembers." She unwrapped the cloth with the reluctance of a woman opening a letter she already knows is bad news. Inside lay a short sword, plain-hilted, the scabbard leather cracked with age. "My husband bought it off a demobilized soldier, gods rest him, twenty years ago. It hung on our wall and did nothing, nothing, all that time. Then last month—" She stopped. Her hands were trembling, and she pressed them flat against the table as if pinning something down. "Last month I found myself standing in the kitchen at night, weeping, and I didn't know why. And then I did know why. I knew a name. Ossory. A boy called Ossory, seventeen years old, killed at the ford at Bren with this blade through him, and his mother's name was Ilsette, and she never learned what happened to him because the army wrote him down as *deserted*."

The room felt colder. Or perhaps I did.

"That's not possible," I said, in the tone I use for saying things that are entirely possible.

"I know it's not possible," she said fiercely. "But it's true, and I need you to write it down, Master Oyelaran, because I can't hold it all. There's more coming every night now. Names. Faces. The sword is *showing* me, and I'm seventy-one years old and I don't want to know how a boy dies. I want it written somewhere safe. I want it out of my head."

Here is the thing you must understand about me, before I go one word further: I should have said no. I should have appraised the sword at a fair price, recommended immediate sale to a collector in a distant city, and recorded in the ledger only the dry facts — steel, age, provenance, no unusual properties noted. That is what a careful man does. That is what I had done for eleven years, carefully, invisibly, while the gift sat coiled under my tongue like a swallowed coal.

Instead I pulled the ledger toward me, uncapped my pen, and said, "Tell me the names. All of them. In order if you can."

Because the truth — the secret I keep even from this page, though I suppose a page is exactly where secrets go to stop being secrets — is that I don't merely record impossible things. I *hear* them. When an object holds a memory, I feel it the way you feel a word forming on another person's lips across a quiet room. The guild believes I am thorough. Thoroughness is my disguise. When I write that a lantern burns near lies, it isn't because previous owners reported it. It's because I can hear the lantern *thinking*, a low smug hum like a priest at a confession.

And the sword on my table was not humming. It was *screaming*, quietly, the way grief screams — into a pillow, at night, so as not to wake the house.

* * *

I will not reproduce here everything Widow Ferro gave me that morning, because some of it belongs to the dead and not to my prose. But I recorded eleven names before her voice gave out. Eleven kills the sword remembered, each one complete — not just the dying but the living: Ossory's crooked tooth, the mud at Bren, a mercenary called Dagny who had laughed at jokes right up until the end, a conscript who begged in a language the sword's wielder hadn't understood and the sword had understood perfectly.

My hand shook only once, and I hid it by dipping the pen.

"You've written it all," she said at last, watching me. "Every word."

"Every word you gave me."

"And you believe me. You didn't once make the face." She meant the face people make at her, the small polite tightening that says *poor madwoman*. "Why not?"

Because I could hear your sword grieving from across the room, Widow Ferro. Because its memories arrive in my head in your voice, since you're the one carrying them, and I have been sitting here transcribing a dead boy's last thought while smiling at you like a competent clerk.

What I actually said was: "Madness usually wants attention. This wants only to be put down. That's the shape of truth, in my experience."

She looked at me a long moment, and I saw her decide not to press, the way people decide not to press when they suspect that pressing might break something they need. "What happens to it now? The sword."

"The guild will find it custody," I said. "A collection, or a scholar. Someone who understands what they're keeping." Someone far away, I did not add. Someone with high walls and no connection whatsoever to me.

"And the page?" She touched the ledger's edge. "May I see it?"

"No one sees the ledgers. Guild law." I softened it as best I could. "But I'll tell you this: as long as that book exists, Ossory son of Ilsette died at the ford at Bren, and no army's paperwork can say otherwise. That's what we do here. We are the counterweight."

She wept then, briefly and without noise, the way old people weep, economically, having learned the cost of tears. Then she paid her fee — too much, I gave some back — and left the sword with us, lighter by one burden and visibly unsure whether to be grateful or terrified. Both, probably. Gratitude and terror travel together so often in this city that they've likely married and had children.

* * *

I carried the sword down to the cellar myself, which was against protocol — acquisitions go through Tomo, our custodian, a cheerful mountain of a man who asks no questions because questions interrupt his lunch. But I wanted a moment alone with it. I wanted to apologize, though to whom, and for what, I couldn't have said.

The cellar stairs are forty-one steps. I counted them out of habit, as I count everything — bells, coins, the seconds between a knock at the door and Sefu answering it. Counting is a kind of prayer for people who don't trust prayers. Behind the false-brick door, our deepest inventory sleeps on shelves of salt-wood: the things too loud to keep upstairs. A mirror that shows the room as it was a hundred years ago. A bell that rings when someone within a mile decides to commit a crime (it rings often; we keep it muffled). And a locked case, iron-banded, that I have never opened and never intend to, because on the nights I stand nearest to it I can hear something inside breathing my name in my mentor's voice, and I made a promise about that voice, and promises are the only ledgers I cannot amend.

I set Widow Ferro's sword on an empty shelf. As my fingers left the grip, the memory rose in me one last time — not a kill this time, but older, deeper, a memory the sword had been holding the way a riverbed holds the shape of water: fire on the horizon, vast wings, a voice like a landslide speaking words I almost recognized, words with a rhythm I knew in my teeth before I knew them in my ears—

An oath. The sword remembered an oath.

I stood very still among the sleeping impossibilities, and the coal under my tongue stirred, and I pressed it down the way I have pressed it down for eleven years, since the night my mentor died teaching me that the surest way to survive a purge is to be a footnote.

Then I went back upstairs, entered the sword in the deep ledger in my best hand — *shortsword, steel, c. 60 yrs; property of W. Ferro; unusual properties: none noted* — and lied in ink, which is the most durable kind of lie there is.

* * *

The trouble with routine is that it teaches you the weight of things. Lift the same ledger every morning for eleven years and you notice, one day, when it's heavier by a single page. So it was that evening, closing up, that I noticed the small wrongnesses gathering at the edges of my ordinary day like birds before a storm.

Tomo had seen a man across the street, three times this week, reading nothing, buying nothing, standing outside the chandler's shop with the patience of someone paid to wait. Sefu had received a letter with a crown seal on it and burned it unread, which he would only do if reading it were more dangerous than ignoring it. And at the market bell that afternoon — I'd been out fetching ink — a crier had posted a proclamation on the Row's notice post, fresh vellum, royal blue ribbon, the kind of paper that costs more than the message it carries usually deserves.

I hadn't stopped to read it. I never stop to read proclamations; stopping is how faces get remembered. But I had heard the tail end of the crier's recitation as I hurried past with my nose in my collar, and the words had followed me home like a stray dog:

"...by order of His Majesty... the practice of swordspeech, being sedition and witchcraft both... all persons harboring, concealing, or failing to report..."

Swordspeech. The word itself is a wound. They outlawed it nineteen years ago, in the great forgetting after the border wars, along with dragon-cults, oath-binding, and a dozen other truths the crown decided had become inconvenient. They burned the speakers. They burned the schools. They burned, wherever they could find them, the books — and where they couldn't burn the books, they rewrote them, which is slower and cheaper and lasts longer.

I am the last oath-bound swordspeaker alive. I say that without pride; pride is a thing that gets people looked at. I say it the way you'd say *I am the last of a species hunted to the edge of the map*: quietly, and mostly to myself, and never, ever aloud.

That night, after the guildhouse slept, I sat at my desk with the lamp turned low and did the thing I do on bad nights: I reviewed my own invisibility the way a merchant reviews accounts. Name known to few. Face forgettable — genuinely, thank the quiet gods; I have the kind of features that witnesses describe incorrectly. Papers clean. Gift buried so deep that even the guild, shelterers of impossible things, believes their clerk is merely meticulous. Eleven years of careful nothing.

And yet.

I took out the page I'd written that morning — the fair copy of Widow Ferro's testimony, eleven names in my best hand — and I read it once, and I felt the coal shift under my tongue, warm, insistent, *awake* for the first time in months. The sword had sung to me, and songs like that don't stop. They wait. Somewhere in this city, other objects were waiting too, other memories pressing at other seams, and the crown had just nailed a proclamation to every post announcing that it intended to hunt down anyone who could hear them.

I fed the page to the lamp and watched eleven dead men's names curl into light and ash, and told myself it was prudence. Some testimonies are safer as smoke.

But as I ground out the lamp, my hand was not steady, and outside, far off toward the palace district, a bell began to ring — not the hour bell, not the alarm bell, but the market bell, rung at midnight, which means only one thing in this city:

They were calling everyone to hear a proclamation.
