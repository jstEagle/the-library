## 2. Proclamation at the Market Bell

The Guild of Impossible Things opened its doors at the eighth bell every morning, which was a lie we maintained with great discipline, because the doors never actually closed. They were too heavy to close — oak and iron, older than the city's walls, older possibly than the city's reasons for having walls — so instead we simply stopped pretending anyone might leave. Customers wandered in at all hours: insomniac scholars, drunk cartographers, a woman who came every Tuesday to ask whether we had acquired her husband's ghost yet (we had not; he was, by all accounts, still alive in Tarnholt, which made the request either hopeful or vindictive).

I mention this because it explains why I was already at my desk when the market bell began ringing wrong.

Bells have vocabularies. The morning bell speaks in round syllables; the fire bell screams in short, ugly bursts; the funeral toll drags its feet. What came across the rooftops that morning was none of these. It was a new word, one the bell had apparently been taught overnight, and it rang in a pattern I'd only read about in old municipal records: three strikes, pause, three strikes, pause, one long note held until the ringer's arm gave out. Assembly by decree. Every soul within earshot was summoned to the market square, and every soul within earshot included me.

I put down my pen mid-entry. The entry was for item forty-one in the autumn acquisitions: *one sword, provenance disputed, claims to remember its kills.* I had spent two hours on that entry the night before, mostly on the phrase "claims to," which was doing more work than any two words should. The sword sat in its crate behind me, humming faintly, the way a dog hums when it wants you to know it's awake.

"Not now," I told it.

The sword did not answer, which was a mercy, because if it had answered I would have had to write that down too, and there are limits to what even an impossible ledger should contain.

* * *

The market square of Lowmarsh holds perhaps four thousand people when it's full, and that morning it was full, because bells that ring wrong are very good at their jobs. I took my usual position: near the edge, half in the shadow of the chandler's awning, close enough to hear, far enough back that no crier would look at me twice. Invisibility is not a gift. It is a discipline, like penmanship. You practice it daily or you lose it.

On the scaffold where the wool-merchants usually displayed their fines, a herald stood in crown blue, flanked by four watchmen whose armor was newer than anything else in the square. New armor in Lowmarsh means someone upriver is paying attention to us, and no one pays attention to Lowmarsh unless they want something from it or want something gone from it.

The herald unrolled a proclamation with the crisp ceremony of a man who had rehearsed his pauses.

"By order of His Majesty Aldric the Third, Keeper of the Reconciled Realm, let it be known throughout the marches and holdings—" and here he paused, as rehearsed, "—that the practice of swordspeaking, being an abomination before the Crown and a corruption of the natural order of steel, is hereby declared high treason."

The square made a sound. Not a gasp exactly. More like four thousand people quietly deciding not to breathe at the same time.

I breathed. Someone has to.

"For generations," the herald continued, "the lie has persisted that certain men and women may bind their voices to blades, commanding metal as though metal were a servant. This lie ends today. Any person found to possess this gift, or to harbor those who possess it, or to conceal knowledge of it, shall be taken into custody of the Crown for examination." Another rehearsed pause. "Examination" is a lovely word. It sounds almost medical. It sounds like something you might survive.

"The Crown further declares a reward of fifty crowns silver for information leading to the identification of any swordspeaker, payable upon confirmation."

Fifty crowns. I did the arithmetic without meaning to — it's a ledger-keeper's disease — and arrived at the sum of roughly everything I owned, times three, plus the guildhouse roof. Fifty crowns could turn a neighbor into an informant faster than you could say neighbor. Fifty crowns could turn a brother into a brother-shaped problem.

The watchmen began distributing printed broadsheets through the crowd, and I watched the paper move hand to hand like something contagious. On it, I knew without looking, would be the telltales: how to spot a swordspeaker, the signs and symptoms. A certain stillness around bladed objects. Scars on the sword-hand. A habit of talking to tools. I catalogued my own body against the list with the calm of a man taking inventory and found myself guilty of all three counts and several I invented on the spot out of professional thoroughness.

And then the herald said the part I hadn't prepared for.

"Let it also be known that the gift may lie dormant in bloodlines thought clean. The Crown therefore calls upon all citizens of Lowmarsh to present themselves for assessment at the garrison, beginning tomorrow, in the order of their street."

Assessment. Not suspicion — assessment. Everyone. A census of the dangerous.

Beside me, under the chandler's awning, an old woman named Bess Ferrow clutched her basket a little tighter. I knew Bess the way you know everyone on your street: her candles burned crooked, her husband had died six winters back, and she had a small, faint, useless gift that everyone politely ignored, the way you ignore a limp or a bad singing voice. She could make needles hold an edge a day longer than they should. That was all. She'd once sharpened my letter-opener with a touch and a murmured word — not an oath, nothing with power in it, just a little domestic charm, the magical equivalent of spitting on a rag and polishing. Half the grandmothers in Lowmarsh could do something similar. It had never mattered.

It mattered now. I watched her do the same arithmetic I'd done, but hers ended differently, because Bess Ferrow was honest to the marrow, and honest people believe proclamations are invitations rather than traps.

"They'll just ask questions," she said, to me, to herself, to the basket. "It's only questions, isn't it?"

"Bess," I started, and got no further, because the watchmen chose that moment to demonstrate what questions looked like.

They didn't take her from the crowd. They went to her house first — I learned this later — and when she hurried home after the reading, they were waiting in her doorway with the courtesy of men who had done this in other towns already. But I saw the beginning of it, or enough of it: I saw her go pale when a watchman detached from the scaffold and began walking our way along the crowd's edge, checking faces against some list, and I saw her do the worst possible thing, which was to look at her own hands.

He followed her gaze. He followed her home. And an hour later, from the guildhouse window, I watched them walk her back through the streets — not dragged, not yet, walking between two watchmen with her basket still on her arm, because she hadn't wanted to leave the candles unattended, God help us all — and the whole street watched from doorways and windows and pretended, collectively, magnificently, to see nothing at all.

Nobody said a word. Nobody met anybody's eyes. The street performed invisibility the way I practiced it, four hundred people at once, and it worked about as well as it ever works, which is to say the watchmen saw everything and wrote down names anyway.

That night, they say, Bess asked the garrison examiner one question before they began: whether they'd found her husband's ghost yet, because she'd filed a request with the Guild of Impossible Things years ago and never heard back.

I don't know if that's true. I choose to believe it, the way you choose to believe anything about the dead: not because it comforts, but because it makes them larger than the hole they leave.

* * *

I got back to the guildhouse before Sefu did. Guildmaster Sefu Adisa ran the Impossible Things the way a lighthouse keeper runs a lighthouse: rarely seen, absolutely essential, and lit from somewhere nobody could inspect. He was in the records annex when the bell rang, and he stayed there, because Sefu's policy regarding crowns, kings, proclamations, and most forms of weather was that they passed, provided you kept the archive dry.

I did not share his philosophy that afternoon. I sat at my desk with the door barred — a formality; see above re: doors — and pulled the autumn ledger toward me, and read my own entries like a man reading a confession he'd forgotten writing.

Because here is the thing about ledgers: they are honest, and honesty is evidence. Item forty-one, the remembering sword. Item thirty-eight, a mirror that showed the buyer's mother's kitchen instead of his reflection (sold, mercifully, out of province). Item twenty-nine, a lantern that burned without oil, which I had dutifully recorded as "anomalous combustion, mechanism unknown," a sentence that would earn me a cell in the garrison if a watchman read it with fresh eyes. Page after page of impossible things, recorded in my careful hand, each entry a small signed confession that the world was stranger than the Crown permitted.

And threaded through them, invisible to anyone who didn't know how to look, was me. My descriptions were too precise. My marginalia noticed too much. When the remembering sword hummed, I had written *emits low tone when handled; source unresolved,* and any examiner worth his salt would resolve the source in about four minutes, with me in the room, holding the thing, unable to stop my hands from knowing it.

Because I am a swordspeaker. Was. Am. The oath sits in me like a swallowed coal — cold as long as I don't feed it, warm as long as I don't speak it, and burning, always, faintly, at the bottom of everything. My mentor made me swear never to speak it aloud, and I have kept that oath for eleven years, and keeping it has cost me less than breaking it would, probably, though lately the *probably* has been developing cracks.

The proclamation changed the arithmetic. Assessment meant every street, every house, every hand examined. My hands are scarred — ink-knife, quill-cuts, the ordinary wounds of a scrivener, but scars are scars, and examiners are paid to be curious. Sooner or later a man in crown blue would hold my palm open and ask me to grip a blade, and the blade would know me, and I would know it, and the coal in my chest would stir, and that would be that.

Unless there was nothing left to find.

I want to be clear about what I did next, because I've turned it over enough times since to know all its shapes. I did not burn the ledger out of courage. I did not burn it out of principle. I sat there in the grey afternoon light with Bess Ferrow's empty street outside the window, and I felt fear do what fear does best, which is to make itself sound like prudence, and I opened the ledger to item forty-one and decided that the safest swordspeaker in Lowmarsh was one whose own records couldn't betray him.

The page came out cleanly. Ledgers are bound to burn; it's practically their vocation. I fed it to the stove in the corner, the one I use to seal wax and dry ink, and watched the careful lines of my own handwriting curl and blacken. *One sword, provenance disputed.* Gone. *Claims to remember its kills.* Gone. Somewhere in the smoke was also the name of the widow in Tarnholt who'd sold it to us, and the price, and the date, and the small annotation in my margin — *do not let Veyra-class buyers near this one* — that I no longer remember writing, though I must have, because the handwriting was mine.

The sword hummed behind me while it burned. Louder, I thought. Or maybe that was the stove. Maybe it was me.

When the page was ash, I felt better for approximately nine seconds. Then I felt worse, and the worse had a shape I recognized, because it was the same shape as the feeling I'd had watching Bess walk between the watchmen with her basket on her arm. It was the feeling of watching something true get carried off to be examined, and doing nothing, and calling the nothing *survival.*

A ledger-keeper who burns his own records isn't safe. He's just smaller. And the Crown, I understood suddenly, sitting there with ash on my cuffs, wasn't hunting swordspeakers at all — not really. You don't need a census to find a handful of hidden gifted people; you need informants and patience, and the Crown had always had both. No. You need a census when you're building a list. When you want to know not just who carries the gift, but who might carry it, whose grandmother charmed a needle, whose cousin once spoke kindly to a knife. You build a list when you intend to use it for a long time.

You build a list when you're rewriting something, and you want to know who'll remember the original.

I didn't know that yet — not the shape of it, not the seal, not the war, not any of it. What I knew was smaller and colder: that the bell had rung a new word that morning, and the word was *everyone*, and that I had responded to it by making myself slightly harder to find and no harder to erase.

Sefu appeared in the doorway sometime after dark. He looked at the stove, at the ash, at my face, and being Sefu, he asked the only question that mattered.

"How many pages?"

"One."

"Whose?"

"Mine."

He nodded slowly, the way he nods at inventory that checks out. Then he said the thing that kept me in that guildhouse through everything that followed, and I record it here exactly as he said it, because some sentences deserve to survive even when their speaker doesn't mean them to:

"A keeper who burns his own page," Sefu said, "is a keeper who's realized the ledger matters. Welcome to the war, boy. Try not to burn the rest of it before you understand which side the fire's on."

I laughed. It came out wrong — too sharp, too much like the bell that morning.

And that was the night the burned woman came to our door.
