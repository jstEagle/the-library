## 3. The Guildmaster's Silence

Sefu summoned me at the hour when the guildhouse smells most like itself—lamp oil, old paper, and the ghost of whatever Adaeze had burned in the kitchen that morning, which I suspected was bread but could not prove.

I want to say I walked up those stairs with dignity. What I actually did was rehearse eleven different explanations on the way up and abandon all of them by the third step, because Sefu had that look. You learn the look if you work for him long enough. It's the look of a man who has already read your ledger, found the discrepancy, and is now simply curious how you'll try to explain it.

"The sword," he said, before I'd even sat down. "The one from the Aldermist estate. The remembering sword."

"It's catalogued," I said. "Shelf nine, wrapped in wool, quiescent."

"Quiescent." He turned the word over like a coin he suspected was clipped. "It spoke to you."

Not a question. Sefu didn't deal in questions when he already owned the answer; questions were for people he was still deciding about.

"It murmured," I said. "There's a difference. Murmuring is practically a clerical function. Some artifacts are chatty. You've told me yourself the mirror on shelf twelve won't stop commenting on everyone's posture."

"The mirror," Sefu said, "has never once told anyone how it died."

The lamp between us hissed. Somewhere below, the guildhouse went on with its evening—the creak of the stairs where Teslim always skipped the fourth step, the soft argument of two appraisers disagreeing about whether a haunted locket counted as jewelry or as a resident. Ordinary sounds. I held onto them the way a drowning man holds onto driftwood that is very obviously also pulling him under.

"Guildmaster—"

"Sit down, Mara."

I sat down.

* * *

Here is what you need to understand about Guildmaster Sefu Adeyemi, for the purposes of this conversation and possibly for the purposes of my eventual funeral: he is not a kind man. He is something rarer and more useful, which is a fair man, and fair men will do things kind men flinch from. He had taken me in eleven years ago, a boy of sixteen with no family, no trade, and a gift I'd been taught since birth to treat like a wound that must never be shown to light. He had never asked why I flinched from bells. He had never asked why I could stand in a room full of enchanted objects and tell him, without touching any of them, which ones were lying about their provenance.

He had simply hired me as a ledger-keeper, paid me fairly, and left me alone. For eleven years I had assumed this was mercy.

I was beginning, that night, to suspect it was policy.

"You knew," I said. It came out smaller than I intended. "You've known all along."

"I employ an archivist who can hear a sword confess its murders through three layers of wool," Sefu said, "and you're asking if I noticed." He leaned back. His chair complained; everything in the guildhouse complains eventually, it's practically our founding principle. "I knew before you signed your first page. You wrote your name into the intake book and the ink bent toward your hand. Ink doesn't bend, Mara. Not for ordinary men."

"Why keep me?"

"Because you were sixteen and terrified and the alternative was the street, and I am not in the business of feeding children to the crown." He said it flatly, like reading an inventory line. *One boy, frightened, retained.* "And because the Guild of Impossible Things has a longer memory than the crown likes to believe."

I waited. With Sefu, waiting was a skill, and I had gotten good at it. He let the silence run exactly as long as it took me to start composing a second question, and then he answered the first one anyway, which was his way of reminding me who controlled the room.

"There have been others," he said. "Oath-bound. Hidden here."

"How many?"

"Three, in my time. Four, if you count the woman who only stayed a winter and swore she wasn't anything special, which was a lie so bad the candlesticks used to snuff themselves when she walked past out of embarrassment." He almost smiled. Almost. On Sefu's face, an almost-smile is a major weather event, and I felt the room shift around it. "Before my time, more. The guild charter is older than the ban, Mara. We were keeping impossible things safe back when swordspeakers were heroes instead of heretics. Old habits survive. That's rather the point of us."

"And the others? The three in your time. Where are they now?"

The lamp hissed again. Below us, someone dropped a tray, and the whole building seemed to hold its breath on their behalf.

"Two left the city before the last proclamation cycle," Sefu said. "Changed names, changed trades. One writes very successful ballads in Ferrenholt under a name I promised never to write down, and I keep promises even to myself. The third—" He stopped. And here is the thing about Sefu: he does not stop. He is a man built of complete sentences. When he stopped, I felt it in my teeth.

"The third," I said.

"Was taken. Eleven years ago. During the last purge, which was smaller than this one will be, the way a match is smaller than a forest fire." His hands, folded on the desk, did not move at all. "His name was Ojo. He was our best restorer. He could talk rust off a blade. And one night the watch came with a list, and his name was on it, and we were a house full of clever people with a hundred hidden doors, and we opened none of them. Because opening one would have risked all of us, and the guild voted, and the vote was cowardice wearing a sensible coat."

I didn't say anything. There was nothing to say that wouldn't have been either cruel or useless.

"I signed the vote," Sefu said. "My signature is in the minutes. You've read the minutes. You file them."

*I file them,* I thought, and felt the floor tilt slightly under eleven years of assumptions. Every secret I thought I'd kept from this man, every careful invisibility I'd woven around myself like a second cloak—he had watched me weave it, approved the pattern, and stored the knowledge away against a night exactly like this one.

"So when you ask why I kept you," Sefu continued, "the honest answer is guilt. The practical answer is that you are the finest archivist I have ever employed and I am a selfish old man. And the answer I will deny under oath is that I have been waiting eleven years for the crown to make its next mistake, and I wanted a witness inside these walls who would recognize it when it came." He unfolded his hands at last. "Congratulations. It came."

* * *

"Then you know about the proclamation," I said. "The hunt. They dragged Tunde's boy out of the tanner's row yesterday for humming. Humming, Sefu. A tune that made the shutters rattle. They put him in a wagon like furniture."

"I know."

"They'll come here eventually. We're a warehouse of illegal sentiment. Half our inventory would be contraband if the assessors could read half our labels."

"They'll come," Sefu agreed, "and they will find ledgers. Ledgers, Mara, are the most powerful magic either of us owns, and they require no oath at all. A watch-captain can arrest a swordspeaker. He cannot arrest a receipt." Something moved behind his eyes then—not warmth, exactly, but the place where warmth would go if the building ever allowed it. "Which brings us to the actual reason I called you up here, because it isn't to reminisce about Ojo, though gods know he deserves the reminiscing."

"All right."

"You will not use the gift. Not here. Not in this city. Not while the proclamations are nailed to every bell-post from here to the river." He said each sentence separately, like laying stones. "No listening to the inventory. No readings for sympathetic clients, no matter how sympathetic. No murmuring conversations with shelf nine. If a sword speaks to you, you will look at it with the blank professional face you use on customers who think their grandmother's brooch is worth more than my roof, and you will catalogue nothing unusual."

"That's it? That's your plan? Hide harder?"

"My plan," Sefu said, "is that you remain alive and employed, which is a plan with an excellent historical success rate compared to the plans of every gifted fool who decided his conscience required an audience." He stood, which ended conversations the way a slammed door ends them, except Sefu never slammed anything. "You think I don't understand the pull. You think I haven't watched three people sit in that chair and promise me exactly what you're promising me right now with your face, silently, while planning to break my rule within the month."

"I'm not—"

"You are. It's all right. I did the same thing to my own master, once, about a matter involving a singing chest and a customs official, and I regretted it for reasons I will also take to my grave." He came around the desk, and for a moment he stood close enough that I could see how old he actually was, which he usually concealed through sheer force of posture. "Mara. Listen to me, because I am going to say this once and then we will both pretend I said nothing at all. Whatever the gift tells you—and it will tell you things, soon, because hunts like this one are never only hunts—you bring it to me before you act on it. Not after. Before. That is the price of staying under my roof. Not silence. Silence I already have. I want *first refusal*."

First refusal. As if my conscience were an artifact come up for auction and he meant to bid on it.

"And if I refuse to refuse?" I asked. Wryness is armor, and mine was getting thin, but there was still a plate or two left.

"Then you will be the finest archivist I ever employed," Sefu said, "right up until the moment you aren't. Dismissed."

* * *

I went down the stairs past the fourth step, because Teslim's habit had become mine somewhere along the years, and habits are how a house gets into your blood. The workroom was lamplit and warm and full of impossible things being quietly impossible: the mirror critiquing Teslim's posture, the locket sulking, shelf nine holding its wool-wrapped silence like a held breath.

I should have felt safer. That's the strange part. I had just learned that the man above me had known my whole secret for eleven years and chosen, repeatedly, to keep me—that the guild itself was older than the ban, that hidden beneath our receipts and our sensible coats ran a vein of shelter as old as the charter. Any reasonable person would have gone to bed lighter.

Instead I lay awake doing arithmetic.

Because here is what I couldn't stop turning over: Sefu had told me about Ojo. Voluntarily. Sefu, who dealt in complete sentences and closed accounts, had handed me a story designed to do exactly one thing—to frighten me into obedience, and to make the obedience feel like gratitude. And it *did* feel like gratitude. That was the trap of it. Gratitude and suspicion, poured into the same cup until you couldn't tell which taste was which.

A man who hides oath-bound members for eleven years is either a sanctuary or a collector. And I had no way to know which, because the one tool I owned for telling truth from performance was the one thing he had just forbidden me to use.

Clever, I thought, staring at the ceiling. Very clever, old man.

Somewhere below, near the front door, a shutter rattled. Just wind, probably. Just the ordinary night doing ordinary things. But I lay there and listened the way I hadn't let myself listen in years—with the gift uncoiling slow and cautious in my chest like a cat testing whether the room was safe—and the guildhouse murmured back at me, timber and iron and paper, all of it saying the same thing in a hundred small voices:

*Someone walked past the door twice tonight, and both times, they stopped.*

I didn't sleep much after that.
