## 4. A Woman Made of Ash

They tell you, when you are burning, that you will not remember it. They lie. I remember all of it: the pitch catching in the thatch, the way fire climbs a wall like it is reading it, my mother's hands on the door bar from the outside — no, from the inside; I have never once been able to keep that straight, and I have had three years of nights to practice.

What I do not remember is most of the road here. That is the mercy nobody warns you about. Grief keeps its ledgers faithfully; pain pays out in installments. The road from Emberfen to this city took eleven days by cart and should have taken four, and I recall perhaps two hours of it total: a boy selling plums who stared at my face too long, a river crossing where the water touched my ruined arm and I screamed so hard a bird fell off a fence post, and the moment, somewhere past the toll bridge, when I understood that I was being followed, and stopped counting anything else.

So understand that when I finally saw the guildhouse — narrow, crooked, wedged between a chandler's and a shop that sold nothing but locks, with a sign reading *GUILD OF IMPOSSIBLE THINGS* in letters someone had clearly painted as a joke and then, over the years, repainted in earnest — I did not feel hope. Hope is for people whose bodies still work. What I felt was arithmetic. *This door or no door.* My legs had voted to resign an hour ago. I had maybe forty steps left in them, and I spent thirty-nine getting across the street.

The last step I spent looking at the seal.

It sat in my palm like a sleeping animal: a scale the size of a coin, black-green, shot through with veins of gold that moved when I wasn't watching them directly. It had belonged to Adewale, our village's last swordspeaker, who had pressed it into my hands while his own were still smoking and said words I will get to later, because later is the only place I can afford to put them. Everyone else who carried a seal was dead. Everyone else who could read one was dead, or hunted, or both. Which meant the word inside it — the true name of what happened to us, the thing the crown's historians had spent three years scrubbing out of every record, every gravestone, every living mouth — meant the word inside it now lived in exactly two places: in this scale, and in me.

And I was, generously speaking, sixty percent alive.

I knocked. Or rather, I aimed my forehead at the door, which is a cousin of knocking, and the door opened before I arrived, which saved us both some awkwardness.

* * *

The man who caught me was old, broad, and built like a piece of furniture that has survived several house fires and developed opinions about them. He lowered me onto a bench just inside the entrance with the unhurried competence of someone who has caught falling things before.

"Guildmaster Sefu," he said, though I hadn't asked. "You're bleeding on the inventory."

"I'm mostly ash," I said. "The blood is incidental."

He looked at my face then — the whole ruin of it, the burn-scarred left side that makes children cry and priests pray — and he did not flinch, did not perform pity, did not do any of the small theatrical kindnesses people use to make themselves feel generous. He simply went and got a basin of water and set it beside me, within reach of my good hand, and said, "Drink or wash. Your choice. We don't ask."

That sentence — *we don't ask* — is why I am telling you this story instead of any other story. A guild that doesn't ask. In Emberfen we had a saying about institutions that don't ask questions, and the saying was not kind. But I was sitting in their doorway leaking, and the alternative was the street, and the street had eyes in it. I drank.

"You're the fourth impossible thing through that door this month," Sefu said, settling onto a stool across from me with the groan of a man negotiating with his knees. "Though the first on fire. Usually they wait until they're inside."

"Efficiency," I said.

Something moved behind him, in the shadow of the stairwell — a young man, ink-stained fingers, a ledger under his arm, watching me with an expression I would learn to know very well: the look of a careful person doing rapid, terrified calculations. He thought I couldn't see him. Most people assume the burned cannot see. We see fine. It's everyone else who stops looking.

"That's Mara," Sefu said, without turning around. "Our ledger-keeper. He's deciding whether you exist yet. Give him a moment; he's thorough."

"I exist," I said.

"Mm." Sefu's eyes went, briefly, to my right hand — the good one — which was still closed around the seal, because letting go of it had stopped being an option somewhere around day six. His face did something complicated and then settled into nothing at all, which is itself a skill, and I filed that away. "We don't ask," he repeated, more firmly, as if reminding himself. "But I'll say this once, free of charge: whatever you're holding, there are men in this city who pay gold for held things. If it's worth dying for, tell me now so I can raise the shutters. If it isn't, put it down and have some soup."

I almost laughed. It came out as a sound like a kettle giving up. "It's worth dying for," I said. "It's already been worth eleven deaths. I'm just the last one standing in line."

Behind him, the ledger-keeper went very still.

* * *

They gave me a room above the chandler's, because the guildhouse proper had no rooms, only shelves, and apparently even the Guild of Impossible Things drew a line at housing people among the inventory. The room smelled of tallow and dust and, faintly, of the sea, though the sea was a week away, which told me the chandler imported his smells along with his wax. There was a bed, a chair, a window with a view of a wall, and a bolt on the inside of the door that Sefu demonstrated twice without comment.

I slept for a day and a half. When I woke, the seal was still in my hand, and my hand had sweated around it enough that the scale had left its shape printed in my palm, gold veins and all, like a brand in reverse. I lay there a long time, listening to the city: carts, bells, a knife-grinder working his wheel somewhere below, the ordinary music of a place that had never burned.

Here is what I knew, lying in that bed, and I want it recorded plainly, because everything after this gets complicated:

Three years ago, the crown burned Emberfen. Not in war — there was no war. In policy. Eleven villages across the salt marches, every one of them home to a swordspeaker or a family of one, every one of them entered at night by soldiers carrying writs signed in a hand so elegant it could only belong to someone who had never watched a thatch roof catch. The official histories — I checked, later, in a library in Tarnholt, until they escorted me out — record those villages as lost to "the Marsh Fever." All eleven. In the same season. A fever, they say, that burned houses from the inside and left scorch marks shaped like boot heels.

My mother died of the Marsh Fever. So did Adewale, and his daughter, and the ferryman Ojo, and roughly two hundred other people whose names I carry in my head because there is nowhere else left to carry them. And the word — the oath-word, the swordspeaker's word, the one Adewale gasped into my ear with his lips cracking like riverbed clay — that word does not exist in any book the crown permits to exist. Which means that when I die, it exists nowhere at all.

Unless the scale counts. Nobody knows if the scale counts. That was rather the point of coming here.

* * *

On the third evening I came downstairs, because a body can only hide so long before hiding becomes its own kind of confession, and found the guildhouse in the middle of its evening ritual, which appeared to consist of Mara arguing with a sword.

Not metaphorically. There was a sword laid out on a cloth on the counting table, old, nicked, ugly, and Mara stood over it with a ledger open, reading aloud from it in a low voice while the sword — I want to be clear about this — the sword *answered*. Not in words. In heat. When he read a certain line, the blade warmed under his palm; when he read another, it went cold as well-water. He was taking its testimony. The sword remembered its kills, and he was writing them down.

I stood in the doorway and watched, and felt the seal shift against my sternum where I'd sewn it into my coat lining, warm as a held bird.

"It killed nine men," Mara said, without looking up. He'd known I was there; of course he had. "Eight of them it regrets. The ninth—" he paused, and his jaw worked, "—the ninth it remembers fondly, and I don't know what to do with that, so I'm writing it down anyway, because that's the job."

"And the job matters?" I asked.

He looked up then. Up close he was younger than the ink suggested, mid-twenties maybe, with the particular pallor of a man who has spent years being deliberately unremarkable in a building full of remarkable things. "Everything that happened, matters," he said. "Or nothing does. Those are the options. The crown prefers the second one lately."

"The crown," I said, "burned my village and called it a fever."

The silence that followed had texture. Sefu, sorting receipts at the far table, did not stop sorting, but he stopped *hearing* anything else, and I could feel it. Mara set down his pen with great care, the way you set down something when your hands want to shake and you won't let them.

"We don't ask," he said quietly. "House rule."

"I'm not asking you to ask. I'm telling you so you can decide whether to throw me out before it's inconvenient." I pulled the coat lining open, worked the stitches with my thumbnail — my hands were steadier than they'd been in days, adrenaline does that, adrenaline is a liar but a useful one — and lifted the seal out into the lamplight. "Because this is going to make it inconvenient either way, and I'd rather know sooner."

The scale caught the light and threw it back wrong. Gold veins crawling. Warm. Both men looked at it the way you look at a snake in a nursery: recognition and horror arriving in the same breath, and horror losing.

Sefu crossed the room faster than his knees had any right to allow. He didn't touch it. He leaned over it, close, and his face — that furniture-face, that nothing-face — cracked clean down the middle, and what was underneath was grief, old grief, load-bearing grief, the kind a building is secretly resting on.

"Where," he said, and his voice came out sanded flat, "did you get this."

"Off a dying man in a burning village. Why? Do you recognize it?"

"I recognize," Sefu said, "what it means that you brought it *here*." He straightened, and the crack sealed itself over, and he was furniture again, but I had seen the joists. "Mara. Counting room. Now."

"He should hear—"

"*Now.*"

* * *

They argued behind the counting-room door, which was a courtesy, since the door was thin and I had been a listener since before I had scars. Their voices came through in pieces, like a letter recovered from a fire.

"—can't be here, Sefu, you know what that is, you know what it *pulls*, it's not a trinket, it's a—"

"—it's a woman with no place else, boy, which is the founding condition of this entire institution, or have you forgotten the charter?"

"—the charter also says we don't harbor what the crown is actively hunting, there's a difference between impossible and suicidal—"

"—there is *nothing* suicidal about keeping one burned woman fed! There is something suicidal about the world we live in, which we did not write, and which I will not pretend we did!" A pause. Furniture creaked. When Sefu spoke again it was lower, and worse. "You think I don't know what she's carrying. You think I haven't seen one before. Nineteen years I've run this house, and in nineteen years, how many oath-bound have walked through that door? Count them in your ledger, Mara. Go on. Count."

Silence. Then Mara, very quietly: "...Four."

"Four. And how many did I turn away?"

Another silence, longer. "None."

"None. So we'll not start with the one missing half her skin." The door handle turned. I stepped back from it with the practiced ease of the guilty, and examined a shelf of cursed candlesticks with tremendous interest as Sefu emerged, followed by Mara wearing the expression of a man who has lost an argument and knows, horribly, that he'll win it again in a week.

"She stays," Sefu announced, to the room, to the candlesticks, to the world. "Under the same law as everyone: we don't ask, and you don't tell. Whatever you're carrying, it sleeps in your coat and speaks to no one. Agreed?"

"Agreed," I said.

"And the soup is not optional. You look like a scarecrow that lost an argument with weather."

I agreed to that too. It was, in fairness, excellent soup.

* * *

It was Mara who broke the rule first, which surprised me, and then did not surprise me at all.

It happened a week later, near midnight. I'd learned the house's rhythms by then: Sefu's lamp going out at ten, the creak of the third stair, the hour when Mara stayed up with the ledgers because — I understood this eventually, the way you understand a scar, slowly and all at once — because writing things down was not his job but his *religion*, and religions are practiced at night.

I found him at the counting table, and instead of pretending I hadn't, I sat down across from him and put the seal on the cloth between us.

He looked at it. He looked at me. "Sefu said—"

"Sefu said it sleeps and speaks to no one. Fine. Then it's asleep, and I'm not anyone, and neither are you, and in the morning none of this happened." I turned the scale over with one finger. The gold veins crawled toward my fingertip like roots finding water. "There's a word inside this. One word. A swordspeaker's word — the oath-word, the first one, the one they teach before they teach anything else. Adewale gave it to me with his last breath because a word in a dead mouth is a stone in a dead hand. Do you know it?"

"No," Mara said. Too fast. Too flat. The flatness of a man standing very still on a floor he knows is rigged.

"Then here's a stranger's question, asked by no one, answered by no one." I leaned forward. The lamp guttered between us. "If a word existed that could prove — not argue, not petition, *prove* — that the Marsh Fever never happened, that two hundred people were murdered by writ and signature, that the histories in every library in this kingdom are inked lies — if that word existed, and it lived in a scale and a burned woman, and the crown was hunting both — what would a house of impossible things do with it?"

For a long moment he said nothing. The lamp hissed. Somewhere above us the house ticked and settled, full of its impossible inventory, remembering everything and telling no one.

Then Mara reached out — slowly, the way you reach toward a fire you've been ordered never to touch — and laid two ink-stained fingertips on the edge of the scale.

The gold veins flared.

And he said, in a voice gone strange and resonant, a voice that was not entirely his own, a word I had heard exactly once before, in a burning doorway, from cracking lips —

and it was the word.

The true one. The first one. The one only a swordspeaker knows.

The lamp went out. Every lamp in the house went out. And in the dark, Mara breathed my mother's language like a man surfacing from deep water, and whispered:

"How long have you known what I am?"

I smiled, there in the dark, with the seal glowing between us like a small green dawn.

"Since the sword," I said. "You read its kills like scripture. Only oath-bound do that." I closed my hand over the scale, over his fingers, over the word still hanging in the air between us like smoke that refused to rise. "Now. Tell me everything you know about dragons — because this seal does, and it's been trying to show me for three years, and I think it's been waiting for exactly you."
