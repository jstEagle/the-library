## 5. What the Seal Remembers

I want to say I was careful. That I waited until the guildhouse slept, that I wrapped the seal in three layers of wool and a prayer to every disinterested god on the shelf, that I approached it the way you approach a sleeping dog with an uncertain temper.

I did some of that. The wool was real. The prayer was real, though I'm not sure the gods of bookkeeping accept prayers from men who falsify their own ledgers.

But here is what actually happened: I sat down at my desk after midnight with the seal in my lap like a cat that had decided to live there, and I told myself I would only look at it. Just look. Inventory it. Record its dimensions, its weight, its condition — *one dragon-scale seal, circular, warm to the touch, provenance unknown, value: do not think about* — and then I would put it in the strongbox and go to bed and be a sensible man who kept his head attached to his neck.

The scale had other ideas.

It wasn't warm anymore. It was hot — not burning, exactly, but the heat of a hand held over yours without touching, the heat of someone standing too close in a crowd. And when I pressed my thumb against its edge to test the temperature, which any competent appraiser would do and which I will continue to insist was appraisal and nothing else, the guildhouse fell away beneath me like a tablecloth pulled from under dishes that somehow don't break.

* * *

I stood on a hillside that smelled of rain and scorched iron.

That's the thing about visions nobody warns you about — they don't come with the dreamy softness of sleep. This was sharper than waking. I could count the veins in the leaves under my feet. I could feel wind moving through hair I haven't worn that long since I was nineteen. The light was wrong for any hour I knew: gold shot through with green, as if the sun were filtering down through water instead of air.

And the dragons.

I have read about dragons. Every child in this country has read about dragons, because every history primer opens with them — the great winged tyrants of the Age of Ash, the monsters our ancestors tamed or destroyed or drove into the mountains, depending on which edition your school could afford. I have catalogued four dragon skulls in my time with the Guild of Impossible Things, and each one came with paperwork disputing the others' authenticity.

None of the primers mentioned that dragons are beautiful.

The one above me was the color of storm-light on copper, vast enough that its shadow crossed the whole valley like a passing cloud, and it flew with the unhurried certainty of something that has never once needed to hurry. It banked, and the light caught along its wings, and somewhere below me people cheered.

Not screamed. Cheered.

I turned, because turning was all I could do, and saw the army.

They were drawn up across the hillside in ranks that went on past the edge of seeing — spears and banners and horses breathing steam, and among them, walking the lines like shepherds among sheep, figures in grey cloaks with swords at their hips. Oath-bound. I knew it before I knew how I knew it; the knowledge arrived the way the smell of rain arrives, whole and unquestioned. Swordspeakers. Dozens of them. More than I had ever imagined existing, more than the crown's proclamations implied had ever existed, standing openly beside kings.

Because there were kings. Three of them, on a rise at the center, crowned in gold and iron and something pale that might have been bone, and when the dragon passed overhead the king in gold raised his hand and the whole army roared.

I understood, in the way you understand things in dreams — completely and without evidence — that they were going to war together. Dragons and swordspeakers and kings. Allies. The oath-bound weren't hunted in this world. They were *deployed*. Honored. A king in bone-white put his hand on the shoulder of a grey-cloaked woman, and she smiled up at him, and he said something I couldn't hear but that made her laugh, and the sound of her laughter reached me across a distance no laughter should cross.

"This is the war," I said aloud, and my voice worked, which felt obscene. "This is the War of the Ashes."

Except it wasn't called that here. Even inside the vision I could feel the name sitting wrong, like a ledger entry where the columns refuse to balance. Here it was called something else. Something older. The words wouldn't come to me, but their shape did, and the shape was *our* war. *Their* war. A war fought by everyone, named by everyone, and later renamed by the winners while nobody who'd fought it was left alive to object.

I hate that I noticed that. I hate it even more that I noticed it and thought, *good catch, Mara, very thorough,* because apparently even inside a thousand-year-old memory I am still the man who checks the arithmetic.

* * *

The vision moved the way grief moves — not in order, but in weight.

One moment I was on the hillside; the next I was in a tent at night, watching the bone-crowned king and the grey-cloaked woman bend over a map by lamplight. She was arguing. He was listening. Both of those details seemed important, though I couldn't have said why. Around them the camp murmured, and outside the tent-pole a small dragon — a juvenile, dog-sized, iridescent — slept curled around a cookfire like the world's most dangerous housecat.

"They'll never hold the pass without us," the woman was saying. Her voice had the texture of river stones. "Your majesty can pretend otherwise at council if it pleases him, but the eastern lords will break, and when they break—"

"When they break, we send fire." The king tapped the map. "You speak as if the alliance is a favor we do one another. It isn't, Ishka. It's a marriage. And marriages survive on more than gratitude."

Ishka. The name landed in me like a coin dropped down a well. I never heard it again in the vision, but I carried it out with me, and I carry it still, and I have written it nowhere because there is nowhere safe to write it.

"Gratitude," Ishka said, "is exactly what marriages survive on. Everything else is furniture."

The king laughed — a real laugh, unguarded, the laugh of a man who has forgotten he is a king — and for one absurd moment I stood in a dead man's memory watching two dead people joke about marriage, and I felt something adjacent to hope.

Then the vision took the lamp away.

* * *

I saw the last battle. Of course I did. Visions are honest in the way wounds are honest; they show you the part that hurt.

It was winter, or the vision's memory of winter, white hills and black trees and a sky the color of dishwater. The enemy this time wore no colors I recognized — just shapes, pressure, a darkness at the far end of the field that the eye kept sliding off of. Against it stood the same alliance, thinner now. Fewer dragons. Fewer grey cloaks. The ranks had been bled by years I only glimpsed in flashes: a funeral on a rain-slick road, a quarrel in a marble hall, a harvest burned by frost rather than fire.

And I saw the moment it ended, and this is the part I will not manage to describe well, so forgive me, hypothetical reader of my private thoughts, whoever you turn out to be.

The darkness broke. Not fled — broke, like a wave breaking, dissolving into spray and gone. And the army stood in the sudden ordinary daylight and made a sound I have never heard soldiers make, a sound between a cheer and a sob, and the dragons landed among them and folded their wings, and it was over. They had won. Together. At terrible cost, but won.

Ishka found the bone-crowned king on the ridge. Her cloak was torn and her sword-hand was blackened to the wrist — the price of speaking, I understood, the price I know now in my own skin — and she was laughing and crying at once, and she embraced him, and he embraced her back.

Then he stepped away from her, turned to his marshals, and gave an order in a voice pitched to carry.

I didn't hear the words. The vision blurred them deliberately, the way a scribe blots a line he's been paid to blot. But I saw the marshals' faces. I saw them look at the grey cloaks scattered across the victory field — exhausted, sword-burned, half of them weeping with relief — and I saw the marshals look away, and arrange themselves, and begin, quietly, to move.

"No," I said. To the vision. To the king. To a thousand years. "No, you don't — you *won* —"

The vision did not care what I said. Visions never do. They're memories, and memories have already happened, and my objection was roughly as useful as objecting to weather.

What came next came in pieces, fast and merciless, the way the crown's historians must have received it and decided, page by page, not to write it down.

A grey-cloaked man waking in his tent to armed men and reaching for a sword that remembered him. A dragon roaring awake in the night as ropes and chains — chains, forged *when*, by whom, with whose money? — closed around its neck. The gold-crowned king signing something while a historian waited with fresh ink. Ishka running, always running, always looking over her shoulder for faces she'd saved, and finding none of them looking back.

And then the trials. I got the trials in full color, no mercy in the blurring at all. Grey cloaks in docks. The charge was not treason — treason would have been honest. The charge was *heresy against the natural order*, which is the charge you write when you need a crime that sounds old, that sounds like it was always illegal, that makes the accused seem mad for ever having believed otherwise. The oath itself was recast: not a covenant sworn in the war's first desperate year, witnessed by all three crowns, but a corruption, a sickness, a thing the swordspeakers had *stolen* from the fire rather than been entrusted with.

I watched a judge read the new history aloud from a brand-new book with a brand-new binding, and watched the audience nod, because the audience had been given the book before they'd been given anything else, and a story you hear first is the story that feels true forever.

The dragons lasted longer than the oath-bound. Barely. I saw the last of them — the copper storm-light one, older now, scarred — cornered in a mountain pass that I recognized with a lurch, because I have catalogued bones from that pass, and the paperwork said *cattle death, disputed origin.*

It looked at its hunters with what I can only call disappointment. Not rage. Disappointment, deep and patient, the look of a teacher watching a favorite student burn the library. And then it spoke — not in words, in meaning, meaning poured directly into the vision's marrow — and the meaning was:

*We were not your weapons. We were your witnesses. Remember us wrongly, and you will forget what you were.*

Then fire, then dark, then—

* * *

I came back to my desk the way a drowning man comes back to the surface: violently, gratefully, and already lying to himself about how long he'd been under.

The candle had burned to a thumb's width. My inkpot had a skin on it. The seal lay in my lap, cool now, ordinary, a circle of scale no bigger than a saucer, and I sat in the dark of the Guild of Impossible Things shaking like a man who has seen his own funeral and gotten the date wrong.

Here is what I had been taught, in primers and proclamations and the polite murmur of respectable company: the dragons were tyrants. The swordspeakers were their collaborators, corrupted by fire, and the kings of old delivered the world from both. The hunt the crown runs today is not persecution; it is hygiene. Every child learns it before they learn their letters properly. I learned it. I believed it the way you believe the floor will hold.

And now I had stood on the hillside where the alliance was celebrated. I had heard a king laugh at a swordspeaker's joke. I had watched the victors turn on their own allies before the mud of the battlefield dried, and I had watched the crime be rewritten into scripture with fresh ink and a straight face, and the vertigo of it was not the vertigo of learning something new.

It was the vertigo of learning that the floor was never there.

Everything balances differently once you see that. The proclamation at the market bell. The neighbor dragged away for a gift fainter than mine. The word *hygiene* doing its quiet work. None of it was policy. It was maintenance — the thousand-year upkeep of a lie, tended by every historian who preferred a clean ledger to a true one, funded by every crown that inherited the theft and called it law.

And the seal had shown me this. Why? Objects in my inventory don't show people things; objects sit on shelves and gather dust and occasionally bite. But this wasn't inventory. Ishola had carried it out of a fire, through a country hunting her, to the one door in the city that sometimes opened for impossible things. She hadn't brought me a relic.

She'd brought me a witness statement.

I looked down at the seal, and the seal — I swear this, and I know how it sounds — seemed to wait. Patiently. The way a ledger waits for an entry. The way a debt waits.

"I'm a bookkeeper," I told it. My voice came out rough. "I record things. I don't *do* things. There's a difference, and the difference is that one of us is still alive."

The seal said nothing, being a seal. But the silence had a texture, and the texture was Ishka's voice saying *gratitude is exactly what marriages survive on,* and the blackened sword-hands of every grey cloak on that field, and the last dragon's disappointment settling over me like ash.

Somewhere upstairs, a floorboard creaked. Sefu, perhaps, unable to sleep, or one of the apprentices, or — my stomach dropped — neither. Because I had spent one evening inside a true history, and I had learned the vision's coldest lesson without it being spoken: the crown does not merely hunt swordspeakers. It hunts *memory*. And I now possessed a piece of memory worth more than the guildhouse, more than my life, more — the thought arrived unwelcome and stayed — more than my silence.

The creak came again. Closer. Then footsteps on the stairs, unhurried, deliberate, and a knock at my door that I recognized, because I had heard it once before through a market square's noise, and hoped never to hear indoors.

"Mara," said a voice I didn't know. Polite. Almost kind. "Guildmaster says you keep the ledgers. I wonder if you might help me appraise something."

I put my hand flat on the seal, feeling its warmth wake again under my palm like a banked coal, and reached for the candle with the other.

"Come in," I said, and began, very quietly, to plan where a man hides a truth that burns.
