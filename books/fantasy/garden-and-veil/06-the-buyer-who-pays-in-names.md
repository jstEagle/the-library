## 6. The Buyer Who Pays in Names

The man arrived on a Tuesday, which I have always considered the most dishonest day of the week. Mondays announce themselves; Wednesdays at least commit to being terrible. But Tuesdays arrive wearing Wednesday's clothes and carrying Monday's debts, and so it was entirely fitting that a royal informant chose one to walk into the Guild of Impossible Things and ask to buy something that was not for sale.

I was in the front room, cataloguing a mirror that showed only rooms you had already left. It had come in from a widow in the Hill District who claimed her husband had spent the last decade of his life staring into it, visiting kitchens he'd eaten breakfast in as a boy. The guild's appraisal sheet listed it as *sentimental, minor, harmless*. I had privately upgraded it to *mildly haunted, do not hang opposite a bed*, but that was between me and the ledger.

The bell above the door rang once — a clean note, no hesitation in it. Visitors to our establishment usually fumbled. They paused on the threshold, uncertain whether they were entering a shop, a temple, or an elaborate joke. This man did not pause. That was my first warning.

My second was his boots. Good leather, city-made, but with road dust worked deep into the stitching. He wanted to look like a clerk who'd walked over from the Chancery. He had actually walked considerably farther than that, and recently.

"Good morning," he said. "I'm told you appraise unusual items."

"We appraise everything," Sefu said from behind the counter, before I could answer. The Guildmaster has a gift for inserting himself into conversations exactly when I would prefer he didn't, and never when I wish he would. "Whether we *buy* is another matter."

"I'm not here for the inventory." The man smiled. It was a well-constructed smile, load-bearing, designed by someone who understood smiles as architecture rather than expression. "I'm here for something that came through your door four nights ago."

The mirror across the room showed me a kitchen I had eaten breakfast in as a boy, and I confess I envied it. Four nights ago, a woman made of ash had collapsed against our door clutching a dragon-scale seal, and every hour since then I had been waiting for precisely this conversation. Waiting for it does not prepare you for it. Nothing prepares you for the moment the thing you dread walks in and orders tea.

"Something came through our door four nights ago," Sefu said mildly. "A great deal comes through our door. Rain came through our door. We had a leak."

"A woman," the informant said. "Burned. Carrying a seal of old dragon-scale." He set a purse on the counter, and the sound it made was the sound of a small fortune deciding to be modest. "I'm authorized to offer a fair price."

"And you are?" Sefu asked.

"Corvin," he said. "I pay in names, Guildmaster. It's what I deal in."

I want to be clear about what that sentence did to the room. A buyer who pays in coin is a merchant. A buyer who pays in names is a historian of the crown's particular kind — the kind that decides which people existed. Every name Corvin had ever purchased had been subtracted somewhere: struck from a tax roll, unwritten from a parish book, unremembered by everyone who'd ever said it aloud. He wasn't offering us money. He was offering us the privilege of never having been involved.

Sefu's face did nothing at all, which is how I knew he understood it perfectly.

"Dragon-scale is illegal to trade," Sefu said. "Has been since the Edict. You're asking me to commit a crime, Master Corvin."

"I'm asking you to *not* commit one." Corvin turned, slowly, taking in the front room — the shelf of bottled last words, the cage of migratory keys, the sword that remembered its kills sleeping in its case like a retired executioner. His eyes moved over everything with the unhurried appetite of a man taking inventory for someone else. "There are two ways this goes, Guildmaster. In the first, you sold me nothing because there was nothing to sell, and I leave, and perhaps I mention to certain offices that the Guild of Impossible Things remains a respectable institution. In the second—" he spread his hands apologetically, "—well. The second way involves more paperwork. For both of us."

"The second way," Sefu said, "sounds like it ends with a fire."

"Fire is very efficient," Corvin agreed pleasantly. "It's why the crown prefers it."

* * *

He drank his tea. That was the worst part, looking back. He sat in our chairs and drank our tea and complimented the honey, and all the while his gaze kept returning to the hallway that led back toward the infirmary where Ishola lay behind three locked doors and a warding charm Sefu had bought off a defrocked priest. He wasn't searching. Searching men look around. Corvin looked *through*, the way an auditor looks through a ledger to find the page that isn't there.

"You keep interesting records," he said to me, nodding at the ledger open on my desk. "Appraisals?"

"Inventories," I said. "Provenance. Condition. What things remember."

"What things remember," he repeated, tasting the phrase. "You believe objects remember?"

"I believe objects are the only witnesses that can't be threatened," I said. "Which makes them either useless or priceless, depending on who's asking."

His smile widened by a degree. "And which am I asking?"

"The dangerous kind," I said, before my caution could catch up with my mouth. "The kind that wants witnesses to be useless."

For a moment — just a moment — the architecture of his smile showed its scaffolding. Then it settled again, and he stood, and set another purse beside the first, and a third on top of those like a small obscene cairn.

"Consider the offer standing," he said. "I'll return in three days. And Master Oyelaran—" 

I had not given him my name. I want that recorded somewhere other than my own head, because in the days that followed I checked and rechecked and I know I never gave it. Sefu calls me archivist in front of strangers. The ledger says *M. O.* and nothing more. My name exists in this building in exactly two places, one of them locked, and neither of them spoken.

"—the honey really is excellent," Corvin finished, and let himself out.

* * *

The bell rang shut behind him and Sefu waited until the footsteps faded down Lantern Row before he spoke.

"He knew your name."

"Yes."

"How?"

"That's the question I've been asking since he walked in, and I keep arriving at answers I don't like." I went to the window, keeping myself behind the shutter's edge, the way I'd learned to stand near windows years ago — present enough to see, absent enough to be missed. "Either someone inside gave it to him, or someone outside already had it. Both options end badly."

"Or," Sefu said, "he read it off you. Informants of his stripe are trained to pull names out of the air. They watch how people defer, who keeps the ledgers, who the master trusts with the keys. You catalogued while he talked. Only the archivist catalogues."

That was generous, and I noted that it was generous, and I noted that I didn't believe it. But there was no profit in saying so. Instead I watched the street.

Lantern Row at midmorning should have been busy. It was busy — but wrong. There was a fishwife arguing with a cooper who had no fish barrels in evidence. There was a beggar outside the chandler's who had been outside the chandler's yesterday, and the day before, always in the same posture, always facing our door. There was a man reading a broadsheet upside down, which takes either remarkable commitment or remarkable instruction.

"Sefu," I said quietly. "How long has the cooper been repairing that cart?"

"Three days."

"And the fishwife?"

"Since the morning after she arrived." He had come to stand beside me, and his voice had gone flat and old. "I counted them before you did, archivist. Six regulars, two floaters, and a rooftop I don't like the angle of. We've been watched since the night she knocked."

The floor seemed to tilt a half-degree, the way it does on a ship when you realize the water underneath has opinions. Every errand I'd run. Every trip to the market with my hood up and my gift folded away like contraband — which, under the new proclamation, it literally was. Every time I'd slipped out the back to burn a page of my own ledger in the alley brazier, certain the dark concealed me. The dark had been furnished. The dark had staff.

"They're not watching for her," I said. "They're watching for whoever helps her. The hunt for swordspeakers is a net, and the crown doesn't care what it catches as long as it catches the *shape* of sympathy." I turned from the window. "Corvin isn't buying a seal. He's pricing us."

"Then we sell him nothing and give him no shape to catch." Sefu's hand landed on my shoulder, heavy as a door bar. "No errands. No market. No burning pages in alleys, Mara, in the name of every patient god, if you must destroy paper do it in the hearth like a civilized criminal. For three days, you are a man who appraises mirrors and minds his own affairs. Can you be that man?"

"I've been that man my whole life," I said. "He's exhausting."

* * *

I wish I could report that I obeyed. What I actually did was spend the afternoon in the memory-vault's antechamber, ostensibly re-shelving provenance scrolls, actually sitting with my ear against the inner door listening to Ishola breathe.

She slept most of the daylight hours now. Burns sleep like that — they take the body's whole budget. When she woke she asked questions in a voice like paper tearing: whether the seal was safe, whether anyone had come, whether the world still believed the war happened the way the histories say. I lied about the third one, gently, and hated myself for the gentleness, because gentleness delivered to a burned woman by a hidden man is just a smaller cage with softer bars.

On my way out I passed the case where the remembering sword sleeps, and I swear — I *swear* — the faint hum it gives off had changed pitch. Lower. Attentive. Swords that remember their kills are supposed to be the bloodthirsty ones, the ones you lock up and apologize to. But this one hummed the way a dog hums when it hears a stranger's step on the stair, and I found myself thinking: *it knows. The metal knows what walked through here today.*

Objects are the only witnesses that can't be threatened. I'd said it to Corvin as a flourish. Standing in the dim with the sword murmuring its low note, I understood it as a warning instead. Everything in this building remembers. Which means everything in this building can testify. Which means, if the crown ever gets its hands on our inventory, they won't need informants or interrogators. They'll need only time and a good listener.

Three days, Corvin had said. I had three days to decide what kind of witness I intended to be.

* * *

That evening I did the arithmetic I'd been avoiding.

The guildhouse has eleven ways in and out, if you count the coal chute and the roof hatch Sefu pretends not to know about. Of those eleven, I judged — watching the street patterns from the attic, where the rooftop watcher's silhouette blotted a patch of stars with the patience of a gargoyle — that maybe four remained genuinely unknown to the crown. The rest were observed, or observed often enough that using one would be a confession.

Eleven doors, four clean. One burned woman upstairs who couldn't walk to the privy without help. One Guildmaster whose love of this house is the largest and most frightening thing in it. One seal that hums against my palm at night like a second heartbeat, showing me kings with knives and dragons with honest eyes whenever I let my guard down. And one royal buyer coming back on Friday to collect either a purchase or a pretext.

Claustrophobia, I have decided, is not fear of small spaces. It is the discovery that the space was always small, and you were always in it, and the walls were simply polite enough to wait until now to lean in.

I sat in the dark of the archive with the seal in my hand and made myself a promise shaped like a plan: if Corvin's price came due, the guildhouse would not burn for me. I had watched one village burn already — through Ishola's telling, through the seal's cold recollection — and I knew now that fire is only half the crown's method. The other half is the ledger afterward, the tidy record proving nothing ever happened at all.

So if it came to choosing, I would choose the records. Not Sefu's forged ledgers, not the guild's careful fictions — the true ones. Mine. The pages I'd burned and the pages I hadn't yet. If they were going to take a history from this house, they would find the shelves already swept, and they would leave with nothing but ash and the maddening certainty that a story had been here, and been carried out through one of four clean doors, by a man whose name they thought they knew.

Because that was the thought I kept turning over, the splinter under the skin of the evening: *he knew my name.*

And then, past midnight, with the house asleep and the watchers on the street settling into their long patient shift, I heard it — faint, from the direction of the infirmary, threading up through the floorboards.

Ishola, awake, speaking in her torn-paper voice. Not calling for help. Not crying out.

Counting.

"...four hundred and six," she was whispering. "Four hundred and seven. Four hundred and eight..."

Names. She was reciting the names of her village's dead, the ones the crown's historians had erased, keeping them alive the only way left — one at a time, aloud, into the dark of a house surrounded by men paid to make sure no one ever said them again.

And I lay there in the claustrophobic dark, listening to a woman count the uncountable, and understood with perfect clarity that whatever Corvin was offering to buy on Friday —

she had already paid more than he could afford,

and I had not yet paid anything at all.
