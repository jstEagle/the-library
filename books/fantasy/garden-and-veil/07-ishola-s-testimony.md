## 7. Ishola's Testimony

The guildhouse kitchen smelled of cardamom and burnt honey, and I sat in it like a ghost that had been invited to stay.

Mara set a cup of tea before me. He did it the way he did everything—carefully, precisely, as though the cup might be catalogued later under *vessels, ceramic, chipped, provenance unknown*. He had that look about him, the archivist's look. I'd seen it before, on the faces of people who believed that if you wrote a thing down properly enough, it couldn't hurt you.

He was wrong about that. But I was in no condition to tell him so, not with my skin still weeping where the burns had healed wrong, not with the seal warm against my breastbone like a second heart.

"You don't have to," he said. "Tell me, I mean. Sefu says you should rest."

"Sefu says a great many sensible things." My voice came out rougher than I intended. Fire does something to a voice—it takes the softness out and leaves the smoke. "Sensible things didn't save Ashkome."

He flinched at the name. Good. That meant he hadn't heard it before. That meant the erasure was working exactly as designed.

"Where is Ashkome?" he asked, and then caught himself, hearing the shape of his own question, hearing what it proved.

"That," I said, "is rather the point."

* * *

I made him sit down across from me. I made him bring the lamp close. If I was going to do this—and some part of me had known since the moment I crawled through the guildhouse door that I would have to do this—I was going to do it properly, witness-style, the way testimony is supposed to be given: to one listener, by lamplight, with no hurry in it.

"My village was called Ashkome," I said. "It stood in the terraced hills south of the Veilwater, three days' walk from anywhere the maps bother to name. Four hundred souls, give or take the season. We grew millet and kept goats and wove cloth that traders carried as far as the coast. And for six generations, we kept a swordspeaker."

I watched his hands. Archivist's hands. They went very still when I said *swordspeaker*, stiller than they'd been all week.

"Not an oath-bound one," I added. "Those were all gone by then, or so we thought. Ours was what the old texts call a hearth-speaker. Small gift. She could touch a blade and know who had held it last, whether they'd come in anger or grief. Farmers used her to settle disputes about stolen goats. Brides used her to check whether their grooms' family swords had ever been drawn against kin. Trivial things. Village things."

"And the crown?" Mara asked quietly.

"The crown came on the ninth day of the Dry Month, four years ago." I could see it perfectly, which was its own cruelty—the fire had taken patches of my skin but none of my memory. Sometimes I thought that was the real joke of it. The crown burned the bodies and left the witnesses intact, assuming no one would listen to us. "They came with two wagons of historians and one wagon of soldiers, and the historians arrived first."

"Historians first," Mara repeated. His voice had gone flat in the way voices go flat when a man is trying not to feel something yet.

"Historians first. A woman named Mistress Adewale—she wore grey, she had ink-stained fingers, she was polite in the way a knife is polite—she set up a table in our square and began interviewing elders. Asking about the hearth-speaker. Asking about old oaths, old names, anything that touched the dragon wars. She paid silver for answers. People thought it was charity." I turned the tea cup in my burned hands, feeling the ridges of scar tissue drag against the glaze. "She stayed nine days. On the ninth day, the soldiers came down from the pass, and Mistress Adewale packed up her table and her silver and her notes, and she rode out ahead of them. And then the soldiers burned us."

"Why?" Mara asked. It wasn't an accusation. It was worse than that—it was genuine. He genuinely did not understand.

"Because we remembered," I said. "Because our songs had verses about the oath-bound fighting beside King Olorun the Third, and our graves had markers naming dragonspeakers among the honored dead, and our oldest house had a beam carved with the words *the flame remembers what the king forgets*. Because there were forty-one villages like ours along the Veilwater, and every one of them kept some piece of the true history, and the crown has spent thirty years collecting those pieces and burning them."

I let that sit. I watched him try to fit it into the world he knew—the tidy world of ledgers and inventories, where everything has a place and a price.

"The soldiers weren't cruel, if you can believe it," I went on. "That's the thing nobody believes. They were bored. They did it the way you'd clear a field. One of them helped my grandmother carry her goat out of the burning house before he lit the next one. He told her to walk north and not look back. He wasn't ashamed. You can't be ashamed of a thing you've been told isn't a thing."

"How did you survive?"

"I was in the drying shed with the year's cloth. The roof was thatch; it went up fast but the walls were stone, and I got into the cistern when the floor caught. Stayed under until my lungs gave out. Came up into a world that was mostly embers." I smiled, and felt the scars pull tight at the corners of my mouth. "Everyone tells survivors they're lucky. Nobody mentions that luck means being the one who wakes up."

* * *

"Now," I said, "the part that matters. The part I crawled three hundred miles to say out loud."

Mara leaned forward.

"After the fire, I walked. Weeks. I begged, I worked harvests, I kept moving south because north was where the soldiers had come from. And everywhere I went, I looked for Ashkome. Not the place—the name. In market records, in tax rolls, in travelers' talk. And here is what I found, swordspeaker, and I want you to hear it clearly because this is the crown's true weapon, sharper than any soldier's blade."

I took a breath. The smoke-voice almost broke. I wouldn't let it.

"Ashkome does not exist. Not in the tax rolls—not even the old ones, the ones in the magistrate's archive at Ilorin that predate the fire by a century. Not in the road surveys. Not in a single songbook, and I checked eleven. The trade routes bend around a valley that is now, according to every map in the kingdom, a lake. There is no lake. I have drunk from the well that stands where the lake should be."

Mara had gone the color of tallow.

"They didn't just burn us," I said. "They unmade us. Somewhere in the capital there are clerks whose whole work is going back through old records with clean pens, striking villages out, revising rolls, forging older documents to agree with newer lies. Mistress Adewale didn't come to collect history before the soldiers burned it. She came to make sure the version that replaced it was already written. The fire was just the punctuation."

"The seal," Mara said. His hand had drifted toward his coat, where he'd taken to keeping the dragon-scale seal wrapped in linen. "It remembers the war. The true war."

"It remembers more than the war. It remembers the kingdom that was, before the kings decided gratitude was inconvenient." I reached into my collar and drew it out, and even now, even knowing what it was, the sight of it stopped my breath—a scale the size of my palm, dark as deep water, with light moving inside it slow as weather. "This belonged to Ayomide the Last, who spoke the full oath at the Battle of the Red Ford and lived ninety years after, long enough to watch the kings she'd saved begin hunting her grandchildren. She hid this so that someday someone could prove the world used to be different."

"And you brought it to a guildhouse of impossible things," Mara said, with the ghost of a smile.

"I brought it to the only people mad enough to keep things no one else will admit exist."

* * *

He was quiet a long time after that. I drank my tea. Outside, the guildhouse went about its evening—one of the apprentices arguing with a cart-driver about a crate that hummed, the kettle singing, Sefu's footsteps overhead in his particular heavy patient rhythm.

Then Mara did something I didn't expect. He got up, went to the shelf where the current ledger lived, and took down a fresh book instead—a cheap one, string-bound, the kind merchants use for tallying fish. He sat back down and opened it to the first page, and his pen hovered there.

"What are you doing?" I asked.

"Starting a ledger," he said. His voice was steady but his pen-hand wasn't. "Ashkome. Population four hundred. Location, terraced hills south of the Veilwater. Destroyed ninth day of the Dry Month, four years past. Survivors known to the record: one." He looked up at me. "What was your grandmother's name?"

The question hit me sideways. Of all the questions—the seal, the purge, the conspiracy—he asked for her *name*.

"Iya Risi," I said. "Risi Adeyemi. She raised goats and buried two husbands and once threw a clay pot at a tax collector, and missed on purpose, because she wanted him to know she could have hit him."

Mara wrote it down. All of it. Every word, in that careful archivist's hand, and when he finished the entry he read it back to me aloud, slowly, the way you'd return something borrowed.

"There," he said. "Now Ashkome exists somewhere other than in you."

I meant to thank him. What came out instead was: "It won't be enough. One ledger, hidden in one guildhouse. They erased four hundred people with clean pens and polite historians, swordspeaker. They'll erase one book without breaking stride."

"Then we'll need more than one book," he said. "And more than one keeper."

And I saw it happen—I saw the grief move through him and come out the other side changed into something with edges. I'd seen that transformation before, in myself, in the weeks after the fire, when mourning finally got tired of being mourning and put on armor instead. Purpose looks like grief wearing its dead's clothes. But it walks, and it carries things, and it does not lie down.

"Forty-one villages," I said. "Along the Veilwater. I marked each one on my way south. Some burned, some emptied, some—worst of all—still standing, full of people who can't quite recall why their grandmothers' songs have holes in them."

"Names?" Mara asked. "Do you have the names of the others?"

"Some. Fragments. Survivors drift through markets like ash on wind—you learn to know them by how they flinch at the word 'historian.'"

"Then we'll find them," he said. "One name at a time. One entry at a time. Let the crown keep its clean pens." He tapped the string-bound book. "We'll dirty ours."

* * *

Later—much later, when the lamp had burned low and Ishola had finally gone to sleep in the room off the kitchen—I sat alone with two books in front of me.

The official ledger lay open on the left. *Inventory, Guild of Impossible Things, current quarter.* Safe. Sanctioned. A record of humming crates and remembering swords, strange enough to be harmless.

On the right, the new book. String-bound. Cheap. Already holding four entries in my hand: Ashkome. Iya Risi Adeyemi. The valley that was never a lake. The historian in grey with ink-stained fingers.

Two ledgers. Two versions of the world. And only one of them true.

I understood, sitting there in the guttering lamplight, that I had crossed a line I couldn't see behind me. Everything before tonight had been hiding—hiding my gift, hiding my name from the rolls, hiding in plain sight among the guild's beautiful impossibilities. Hiding keeps you alive. But writing Ashkome down wasn't hiding. It was *declaring*. Somewhere in the capital, clerks with clean pens were maintaining a world in which that village had never existed, and I had just committed treason with a fish-tally book and a borrowed pen.

My mentor's voice came back to me, the way it always did at moments like this: *A record is a promise, little one. Never write what you aren't prepared to die behind.*

I had sworn, at her grave, never to speak the oath aloud. I had kept that silence for eleven years, worn it smooth as a river stone. But she had never said anything about *writing*.

I dipped the pen again.

*Entry five,* I wrote. *Survivor's testimony, given by lamplight, ninth night after arrival. Witnessed by M. Oyelaran, keeper.* Then, after a moment, because the archivist in me demanded completeness and the man in me demanded it louder:

*She described her grandmother throwing a pot at a tax collector, and missing on purpose.*

Let the crown's historians make sense of *that*.

I was blotting the page when I heard it—a sound from the street below the kitchen window. Soft. Deliberate. The particular quality of a footstep that wants not to be heard, which is itself a kind of announcement.

I closed both ledgers and blew out the lamp, and in the dark I counted the steps: two, pause, three, pause. Pacing. Patient. The rhythm of a man measuring a building he intends to enter.

Someone was walking the length of the guildhouse frontage, slow as a clerk reading a ledger line by line.

And I realized, with a cold that had nothing to do with the night air, that we had already been entered into somebody's book.
