## 8. Oath-Bound Means Bound

There is a particular kind of silence that lives in a guildhouse at night, and I have catalogued it the way I catalogue everything else: by its weight, its provenance, and its resale value.

This silence was the heavy kind. The provenance was Ishola's door, closed since dusk. And the resale value, as far as I could tell, was nothing, because you cannot sell a thing that everyone in the building is pretending not to hear.

I sat in my alcove behind the counting room with the dragon-scale seal on the desk before me and a candle between us, like a chaperone at a courtship neither party wanted. The seal did not glow. It did not hum. It did none of the theatrical things artifacts do in bad ballads. It simply sat there, grey-green and dull as an old coin, and it *waited*, and waiting — I have learned — is far worse than glowing.

Three nights now it had been in the house. Three nights of the guild's ordinary noises rearranging themselves around it. Pip's laughter in the stairwell going quiet half a beat too soon. Sefu's boots passing my alcove twice when once would have done. And underneath all of it, that patient pull, like a hook set gently in the soft part of me, reeling by inches.

Swordspeakers feel things the way other people feel weather. That is what my mentor told me, back when telling me such things was still legal. *The gift isn't in your hands, boy. Your hands are just where it gets out.*

I had not spoken the oath in eleven years. I had not even mouthed it. Some nights I caught myself composing the words in my head and shut the thought down the way you'd slam a shutter on a lit lamp during a curfew — fast, guilty, certain someone saw.

But the seal didn't need me to speak. That was the trouble. It only needed me to *listen*.

* * *

My mentor's name was Doyin Adebayo, and he died on a Tuesday, which I have always felt was an insult added to injury. Great deaths should happen at dawn or in storm or at least on a day with some dignity to it. His happened in the flat grey light after market, in a barley field outside Ilorin-Under-the-Hill, with mud on his boots and his sword still sheathed because he never got the chance to draw it.

He was the last swordspeaker anyone had admitted to being, before me. Before the first edicts. Back then the crown's disapproval was still a rumor wearing a uniform rather than a policy wearing a crown.

I was seventeen. I had been his apprentice for four years, and in four years he had taught me everything except the oath. He taught me ledger-keeping — "a man who can't account for his own soul has no business accounting for anyone else's" — and he taught me the forms, the footwork, the way a blade remembers the hand that holds it if the hand is honest. But every time I asked about the speaking, the actual speaking, the words that turn a sword from a tool into a witness, he'd go quiet in a way I mistook for stubbornness.

It wasn't stubbornness. It was arithmetic. He was doing sums about how long we had.

The soldiers came on that Tuesday without heralds or banners, six of them in plain mail, led by a captain with a clerk's ink-stained fingers. Later I would understand why a clerk had come along. Even then, before the purges had a name, the crown understood that the point was never to kill swordspeakers. Anyone can kill a man. The point was to kill the *record* of him.

They came over the rise while we were sparring, and Doyin saw them first, because Doyin always saw things first; the gift runs ahead of the eyes sometimes, a pressure on the air, a wrongness in the light. He went very still, the way a heron goes still, and said, "Mara. Sheathe it."

"I haven't drawn it."

"You were about to. Sheathe it anyway." He smiled at me, and it was the worst smile I have ever been given, because it was warm, and warmth at a moment like that is a man telling you what he thinks the last thing you should remember looks like.

Six soldiers against one old swordspeaker should have been no contest at all, and it wasn't — just not in the direction the arithmetic suggested. I watched him hold them off without drawing steel, using only the forms, turning their blades with angles so precise it looked less like fighting than like correcting their posture. He could have ended it. We both knew he could have. One word — one syllable of the oath — and every sword in that field would have remembered whose side it was actually on.

He wouldn't spend it. Because spending it meant being *known*, and being known meant the clerks writing his name into the rolls, and the rolls becoming warrants, and the warrants becoming more Tuesdays in more barley fields, and somewhere down that road, me.

So he fought them mute. And mute men lose slowly.

I don't remember deciding to run. I remember the captain shouting "the boy! the crown wants the boy!" — they'd known about me all along, of course; apprentices are not secrets, they are investments — and I remember Doyin hearing it and changing everything. He stopped correcting their posture and started spending himself instead, buying ground, buying seconds, buying me a head start with the only coin he had left, which was the distance between his body and theirs.

The last thing he said to me carried across the whole field, because he put the gift behind his voice even without the oath, the way a dying fire will throw one last spark:

"*Bound means bound, boy. You'll want to speak it. Everyone wants to speak it. Don't.*"

Then the captain's sword went through him, and the clerk knelt beside the body with his little book, and I ran, and I have been running in place ever since.

Here is the part I have never written down, not even in the secret places of my mind where I keep the rest: the clerk didn't burn his book. He wrote in it. I saw him do it from the treeline, kneeling in the barley, dipping his pen, recording something about Doyin Adebayo that was not the truth. I couldn't read it at this distance. I have spent eleven years not reading it, which is a skill, and I am very good at it.

That is the inheritance. Not the gift. The silence. My mentor died to keep a lie intact, on the theory that a survivable lie beats a fatal truth, and he handed the theory to me along with his footwork and his penmanship, and I have kept both in good repair.

*Bound means bound.*

Some inheritances are chains. This one was a lock, and I was the door, and for eleven years I had kept myself shut.

* * *

The seal pulled.

Not dramatically. The seal does not do drama. But sitting there in my alcove with the candle burning down, I felt it the way you feel a name on the tip of your tongue — except it wasn't a word I was trying to recall. It was a word trying to recall *me*.

I put my hand flat on the desk, an inch from it. The scale was cool, then not cool, then cool again, a slow pulse like something breathing under a blanket.

And it showed me a fragment. Just a sliver, the way a locked chest shows you a corner of silk through a split seam. Not the war this time — I'd had enough of the war to last several lifetimes — but a smaller thing. A kitchen. A woman singing while she worked, and the song was in the old cadence, the oath-cadence, the rhythm that swordspeakers use for counting kills and kings' betrayals, and she was using it for a *lullaby*. Ordinary words. Sleep, child, the fire is banked, the swords are sleeping too.

The meaning of it hit me sideways: the oath was never a weapon. It was a *household*. It was something mothers sang. The crown hadn't outlawed a battle-spell. It had outlawed a lullaby, and called the outlawry prudence.

I snatched my hand back. The vision cut off mid-note, and the silence it left behind was enormous.

"That," I said aloud, to the seal, to the candle, to nobody, "is a dirty trick."

The seal, being a seal, declined to apologize.

I blew out the candle out of pure spite and sat in the dark, rubbing the hand that wanted, so badly, to reach back.

* * *

Sefu found me there an hour later. I heard his boots stop at the mouth of the alcove and knew he'd been standing longer than he'd let on, because Sefu believes in announcing himself the way cats believe in knocking.

"You're sitting in the dark," he said.

"It's fashionable."

"Mm." He came in anyway, which the alcove barely permitted, and lowered himself onto the stool that creaked under him the way everything in this building eventually creaks under Sefu. In the dark I could see almost nothing of him except the shape of his shoulders and the grey edge of his beard, which suited me fine. Some conversations go better as silhouettes.

"She told you what the seal is," he said. Not a question.

"She told me what it remembers. There's a difference."

"Is there?" He was quiet a moment. "When I was younger than you, there was a woman in this guild — before your time, before most of the current roof — named Adaeze. Keeper of odd lots, like you. And she had the gift, small, a whisper of it, enough to make a rusted hinge sing or a stuck drawer open. Nothing martial. Nothing dangerous. She hid it for thirty years the way you hide yours, and she was good at it, better than you, if I'm honest."

"What happened to her?"

"A tax assessor with a grudge. He suspected, and he was right, and he reported her, and the watch took her on a Tuesday—" he paused, and I felt him register the coincidence, or maybe he'd planned the pause, Sefu plans everything, "—and she was gone three days, and when they brought her back she wasn't Adaeze anymore. They hadn't killed her. They'd done something worse. They'd taken the memory of the gift out of her, some procedure the crown's sorcerer-clerks developed, and she lived another ten years in this house and never once knew what she'd lost. Kept reaching for things that weren't there. Used to stand at the window with her hand up, like she was holding something invisible, and cry without knowing why."

The dark was very complete around us. Somewhere below, the house settled, wood ticking as it cooled.

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because you're doing the arithmetic," Sefu said. "I can hear it. You're weighing the seal against the silence, the truth against the survival, and you think you invented the sum. You didn't. Every gifted soul who's ever slept under this roof has done it, and the ones who survived are the ones who arrived at the same answer Doyin Adebayo did." He stood, with effort, and the stool thanked him. "*Bound means bound.*"

"He died saying it."

"Yes. And it was still worth it." At the mouth of the alcove he stopped. "One more thing. The assessor with the grudge — the one who took Adaeze? He's back in the city. Older. Better dressed. He came asking after burned survivors and unusual inventory, very politely, and he left a card."

My stomach performed a maneuver I did not authorize. "A card."

"Suggesting the Guild of Impossible Things might wish to make a private sale. Of certain items of historical interest." Sefu's silhouette turned toward my desk, toward the place in the dark where the seal sat breathing its slow breath. "Sleep with your door barred, Mara. And keep your hand away from that thing. Curiosity is how they find us. It's always been how they find us."

His boots receded down the corridor, unhurried, a man who believed his walls would hold.

* * *

They would not hold. I knew it the way Doyin had known the soldiers were coming over the rise — not with evidence, but with that pressure on the air, that wrongness in the light of things.

Because here is what I did not tell Sefu, lying in my barred room with the seal wrapped in three layers of wool in a locked box under the floorboards, feeling it pull at me through all of it, gentle and patient as tide: the assessor's card was not the problem.

The problem was the vision. The kitchen. The lullaby.

Eleven years I had kept the silence by telling myself the oath was a weapon, and weapons can be left on racks. But it wasn't a weapon. It was a mother singing. It was a household. It was the sound of a world before the clerks got to it, and the crown hadn't outlawed it because it was dangerous to kings.

It had outlawed it because it was true.

I lay in the dark doing the arithmetic Sefu had heard me doing, and for the first time in eleven years, the sum came out differently. Not all the way. Not yet. Bound means bound; I could still hear Doyin's voice carrying across the barley, spending his last breath on my silence.

But under the floorboards, through wool and oak and lock, the seal waited, and it was no longer the only thing waiting.

Because as I finally drifted toward sleep, I heard it — faint, at the street level, at an hour when no honest cart rolls — the measured tread of boots. More than two. Fewer than ten. Pausing, just once, outside the guildhouse door, the way a man pauses when he is checking an address against a list.

Then moving on. For now.

I lay awake until dawn with my hand pressed flat against the floorboards, over the box, over the seal, holding it down the way you'd hold down a lid on something that has already begun to rise — and listening, the whole night, for the boots to come back.
