## 9. The Night the Watch Came

The knock came at the hour when honest people are asleep and dishonest ones are pretending to be.

Three knocks. Not the arrhythmic rattle of a drunk or the apologetic tap of a debtor. Three measured blows, spaced like a man counting coins into a purse. I lay in my cot above the ledger room and knew, before I'd finished drawing breath, exactly what they meant.

I had rehearsed this night for eleven years. I had imagined it so many times that when it finally arrived, my first feeling was not terror but a strange professional satisfaction — *ah, yes, this is how it goes* — followed immediately by terror, because satisfaction is a luxury for people whose names aren't written on lists.

Below me, I heard Sefu's door open. He didn't hurry. That was the first miracle of the night: Guildmaster Sefu, seventy years old and built like a wine cask, descended his stairs at the pace of a man going to answer a delivery of turnips.

I dressed in the dark. I had practiced that too — boots, coat, the small knife I wasn't permitted to carry and carried anyway. Then I went to wake Ishola.

She was already awake. Of course she was. Burned women don't sleep deeply; sleep is where fire lives. She sat upright on the narrow guild cot with the dragon-scale seal pressed against her sternum, her eyes two points of light in the dark room.

"The watch," I whispered.

"I know." Her voice was ash and gravel. "How many?"

"Couldn't tell. More than one."

She nodded, and began wrapping the seal's cord around her wrist, under her sleeve, the way you'd hide a scar. "Where do I go?"

And there it was — the question I had been dreading since the night she collapsed across our threshold smelling of smoke and old war. Because the guildhouse had a hundred hiding places for stolen candlesticks and mislabeled miracles, and not one of them was built for a woman the crown wanted badly enough to burn a village to find.

"Sefu will know," I said, which was cowardice dressed as delegation.

"Mara." She caught my wrist. Her grip was stronger than her body suggested; grief does that, packs everything down until it's dense as lead. "Whatever happens tonight — the seal doesn't leave this house. Do you understand? If they take me, it stays."

"If they take you—"

"*It stays.*" The word only swordspeakers know, the one she'd spoken bleeding on our doorstep, sat behind her teeth again, unspoken but present, like a drawn blade in a closed room. "Promise me."

I promised her. It was the second-easiest lie I ever told, because I fully intended to keep it, and promises made in terror have a way of surviving their makers.

Then we went downstairs to let the crown in.

* * *

There were six of them in the entry hall, which was four more than courtesy and two more than fear could comfortably hold. A lieutenant in royal blue stood at the front holding a writ with enough wax seals on it to sink a rowboat. Behind him, five watchmen arranged themselves through our doorway with the studied casualness of men who had kicked in softer doors before breakfast.

Sefu stood before them in his nightshirt and guildmaster's chain, yawning.

Actually yawning. I will remember that yawn until I die. It was the single greatest performance I have ever witnessed, and I once watched a woman sell the same haunted mirror to three different cousins in one afternoon.

"A writ of search," the lieutenant said. "Signed by the Lord Provost. We're looking for a fugitive. Burned woman, southern accent, possibly carrying stolen property of the crown."

"At this hour?" Sefu scratched his stomach. "Son, if you've come about the burned woman, you're a week late and a mile wrong. We had a chimney fire last month. Half the street saw it. You want to search for soot, be my guest."

The lieutenant's eyes did a slow circuit of the hall — past the taxidermied griffin that had never existed, past the shelf of bottled weather, past the sign over the desk that read THE GUILD OF IMPOSSIBLE THINGS: ALL CLAIMS VERIFIED, NONE BELIEVED. His lip curled the way educated men's lips curl at places like ours. Guilds of impossible things were tolerated in Veylmark the way beggars are tolerated outside temples: as an argument for the establishment's virtue.

"You keep records," he said.

"We keep records."

"Of inventory."

"Of everything that passes through these doors. It's the law."

"It is," the lieutenant agreed pleasantly. "So you won't mind showing me your ledgers."

And here is what I learned that night, standing on the stairs with Ishola's breath warm against my shoulder: Guildmaster Sefu had been keeping forged ledgers for longer than I had been alive.

He shuffled off to the ledger room and returned with a volume I had never seen. I know every book in that room. I have catalogued them, dusted them, repaired their spines with fish glue and patience. This one was new to me — fat, cracked, its corners soft with handling, its pages yellowed convincingly. Sefu opened it on the desk with the tenderness of a man showing photographs of grandchildren.

The lieutenant read. The watchmen fidgeted. And I read upside-down from the stairs, because a swordspeaker's gift is not only oaths and fire — it is attention, sharpened to a point, and mine was aimed at that ledger like a spear.

The entries were beautiful. Dated, itemized, witnessed. *Candlesticks, silver, six, sold to Madame Ostrava.* *Weather-in-a-bottle (fog), one, damaged in transit, discarded.* And there, in a careful hand dated nine days ago: *No persons admitted. No burned persons. No fugitives. Guildhouse empty of guests this season.*

Nine days ago, Ishola had been lying in our infirmary with her skin coming off in sheets.

He had forged a record of her absence before she'd finished her first fever. He had been keeping a false ledger against this exact night the way other men keep savings against a hard winter. And the forgery was good — better than good, it was *aged*, the ink matched, the hand varied page to page the way real hands vary — which meant it was not his first.

The lieutenant ran his finger down a column. Turned a page. Ran it back up.

"This says your guest wing has been closed since spring."

"Roof leaks," Sefu said. "You want to see the roof?"

"I want to see the roof."

They went to see the roof. All six of them, tramping up the main stair — *our* stair, past *my* cot, while I flattened myself and Ishola into the shadow of the memory-vault door like two coats left on hooks. The lieutenant paused at the landing. Looked at us. Looked through us, because Sefu had positioned a standing mirror directly opposite, and men who search houses always look at mirrors first, and mirrors show them themselves.

Then they were overhead, and we were alone in the dark, and Ishola's hand found mine and squeezed hard enough to grind bone.

"He lied for me," she breathed. "That man has never seen me before tonight, and he lied to six soldiers."

"No," I said, because I had done the arithmetic during the roof inspection, doing what I do best, which is accounting. "He's lied before. For others. That ledger's been kept for years, Ishola. There are older hands in it than his."

She stared at me. Above us, boot heels crossed the guest wing floorboards — the leaking, empty, never-occupied guest wing where she had slept for nine days.

"Your guild," she said slowly, "has been hiding people like me."

"People like us," I said, and heard myself say it, and did not take it back.

* * *

The search took three hours. They found nothing, because there was nothing to find, because Sefu's guildhouse was a machine built for making things unfindable. They poked the bottled weather. They sneered at the griffin. One young watchman picked up a remembering sword from the display case — not the sword from my first ledger entry, but its cousin, a blade that hummed faintly with the deaths it held — and the lieutenant snapped at him to put it down before it "did whatever cursed guild-things do."

At the end of it, the lieutenant stood in the entry hall and looked at Sefu for a long moment.

"You're very calm, guildmaster."

"I'm old," Sefu said. "Calm comes free with the price."

"The woman we're looking for. If she came here. If someone sheltered her." He smiled without warmth. "The penalty for harboring is the same as the penalty for being. You understand that."

"I understand that you haven't found her," Sefu said, "and that your writ covers one night, and that dawn is in two hours, and that my tea is getting cold. Good evening, Lieutenant."

And they left. All six, into the grey wet dark, their lanterns bobbing away down Copperlane like a string of dishonest stars.

I want to report that I felt relief. What I actually felt was the specific nausea of a man who has watched a tightrope walker cross a chasm and realized, mid-performance, that he is standing on the same rope.

Because the lieutenant had believed the ledger. But he had also, in the doorway, on his way out, said one thing too quietly for the watchmen to hear — though not too quietly for me, standing close, listening the way my gift taught me to listen:

*"The house is already bought, old man. Someone inside is selling."*

* * *

We gathered in the ledger room after dawn broke thin and colourless over the rooftops. Sefu poured tea nobody drank. Ishola sat with her bandaged hands wrapped around the cup anyway, for the warmth. I stood by the window watching the street, because someone had to, and because watching streets is the closest thing to prayer I know.

"That ledger," I said. "The false one. How long?"

Sefu looked into his tea. "Since the last purge. Forty-one years."

"Who else is in it?"

"Names you'd know if the crown hadn't eaten them. A weather-worker from the coast. Two oath-bound, before your time. A boy who could talk to locks." He said it flatly, an accountant reading figures. "Every one of them passed through this house. Every one of them left alive. That is the whole of my life's work, Mara, and I would thank you not to look at me like I'm a stranger."

"I'm not looking at you like a stranger. I'm looking at you like a man who let me believe I was the only secret in this building for eleven years."

"You needed to believe that," Sefu said. "Secrets multiply when they meet. Two secrets make a conspiracy. I couldn't afford a conspiracy. I could barely afford you."

"And now?" Ishola's voice cut between us like wire. "Now you can afford me?"

Sefu turned to her, and for a moment the wine-cask bluster fell away and I saw what was underneath: a tired man holding up a building with his shoulders. "Now I can afford nothing, madam, and am spending it all anyway. You'll stay. In the vault, if the watch returns. Not because I'm brave — I'm the least brave man in this room, and I include the griffin — but because turning you out would cost more than keeping you. Some prices you pay in coin. Some you pay in sleep. I've been paying in sleep for forty years and I've gotten used to the exchange rate."

He gathered the false ledger, hugged it to his chest like a child, and stopped at the door.

"One thing," he said, not turning around. "The lieutenant said something as he left. I heard it. 'Someone inside is selling.'" Now he turned, and his eyes went from Ishola to me and back. "I've fed that man three perfect lies tonight and he swallowed them whole. Men who swallow lies don't issue warnings. Warnings are what buyers give sellers when they want the goods delivered intact."

"So someone talked," I said.

"Someone talked," Sefu agreed. "Or someone is going to. Find out who, Mara. Quietly. Because next time the knock comes, there may be no ledger clever enough — and next time, they won't send six men. They'll send the ones who burn."

He left. Ishola looked at me across the cold tea and the morning light, and touched the seal hidden under her sleeve.

"Your guildmaster just saved my life," she said, "and told you he might sell it. Both in the same breath."

"Yes."

"Do you believe him?"

I thought about forty years of forged pages. About names eaten by the crown. About a man who kept a false ledger the way other men keep a loaded pistol under the bar — hoping never to need it, knowing the night would come.

"I believe him," I said. "That's what frightens me. Liars I could handle. But a man who tells the truth *that* completely—" I shook my head. "Truthful men have nothing left to lose. And men with nothing left to lose start making bargains."

Outside, the market bell rang the hour, and somewhere in Copperlane a shutter moved that had no business moving in the windless grey — a small sound, a domestic sound, the sound of a house adjusting itself around a watcher.

Ishola heard it too. Her hand closed over her wrist, over the seal, and she said, very softly, the word only swordspeakers know — not as an oath, not as power, just as a woman testing whether her voice still worked.

It worked.

And upstairs, faintly, the guildhouse answered — every remembered thing on every shelf humming once, like struck glass — and I understood that the vault below us had heard her too, and was waiting, patient as a grave, for one of us to come down and listen.
