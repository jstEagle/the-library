## 10. The Memory-Vault

The guildmaster's key was iron, old iron, the kind that has been held so long by so many hands that it has forgotten its own shape. Sefu did not hand it to me. He set it on the table between us and slid it across with one finger, the way a man slides a coin to a beggar he does not wish to look at.

"Down the stairs behind the wine racks," he said. "Third door. You touch nothing without Mara beside you."

"And if I do?"

"Then you will learn why we call it a vault and not a museum." He said it without heat, which frightened me more than shouting would have. A man who shouts is a man who still believes words can stop you.

I took the key. My hands were still wrong — I want to say they were burned, but that is a lie my skin tells for me. They were *healed* wrong, healed in a hurry by people who had more urgent patients than a woman who would probably die anyway. The fingers curled like question marks. I have learned to write with them anyway. Grief makes an excellent scribe; it never lets your hand go idle.

Mara was waiting at the top of the stairs with a lamp, looking like a man who had been ordered to escort a lit fuse through a powder store. Which, in fairness, was roughly what was happening.

"You don't have to do this," he said.

"Yes," I said, "I rather think I do."

* * *

The stairs went down farther than the building above could account for. That is the thing about the Guild of Impossible Things — from the street it is a narrow house squeezed between a chandler's and a shop that sells, or sold, umbrellas of dubious honesty. Inside it goes back and back, rooms folding into rooms like a hand closing. And beneath all of it, this: a corridor cut from the living rock, older than the guild, older than the city, older perhaps than the idea of cities.

"The founders didn't build this," Mara said. His voice had gone quiet in the way voices go quiet in churches and tombs, and I suppose the corridor was both. "They found it. Or it found them. Sefu says even the first ledger doesn't say which."

"What's down here?"

"Everything nobody else wanted to remember."

I laughed. It came out cracked, like a plate dropped on stone. "That's a lot of inventory."

"It keeps," he said. "That's the trouble with it. It keeps."

The third door was not locked. It was *sealed*, which is different — the wood had no lock, only a pattern of scales carved deep into it, and when I put my palm against those carvings the door exhaled. There is no other word. Air moved out of the seams, warm air, smelling of hot stone and something sweeter underneath, something like the inside of a fruit that has sat too long in the sun.

Mara made a small sound. I looked at him. He was staring at my hand on the door as though I had pressed my palm into wet clay and left a print that fit perfectly.

"That shouldn't happen," he said.

"Neither should I be alive," I said. "We're two impossibilities in a guild of them. Open the door."

He opened the door.

* * *

I had imagined a vault as a room full of boxes. This was a room full of *waiting*.

It was round, and its ceiling rose into darkness the lamp couldn't reach, and around its walls stood — I keep wanting to say shelves, but that is too domestic a word for what they were — plinths, tiers of them, spiraling up into the dark, and on each one rested a single object. A bell with no clapper. A child's shoe, leather gone stiff as bark. A spearhead green with age. A mirror turned to face the wall. A birdcage with its door open and a feather caught in the bars, still bright after what must have been centuries. Hundreds of them. Thousands. Each alone on its shelf like a word set down carefully by someone who intended to come back and use it later.

And the room was not silent. I want to be precise about this, because precision is all I have left. It was not silent the way a grave is not silent if you press your ear to the earth. Everything in that room was murmuring. Not words. Not yet. More like the sound a crowd makes before it decides whether to sing.

"They're memories," Mara said. "Or — vessels of them. The guild calls them reliquaries. We don't sell these. Ever. Not even the ones we could get a fortune for." He lifted the lamp. "Sefu says every object here belonged to something that ended badly, and the ending soaked into it. A sword that remembers its kills came from a case like this. Most of what we catalogue upstairs is just... ordinary impossible. This is where we keep the kind that bites."

"Why show me, then?"

He was quiet for a moment. In the lamplight his face did the thing it always did, the careful settling, the clerk arranging his features into order. But underneath it I saw what I had seen the night I collapsed at their door: a man holding a coal in his bare hand and pretending it isn't hot.

"Because you said a word," he said finally. "In the doorway. When you fell. You said *suthen-kai*. Nobody says that unless they've stood in an oath-circle. And Sefu decided — " he exhaled — "Sefu decided that if the crown is going to burn us anyway, you should at least see what it is they're burning us to protect."

"He has a generous soul."

"He has a terrified one. Those look similar from the outside."

I walked the spiral. I confess I touched things. Sefu had forbidden it, and I am told I have a talent for disobedience that survived the fire that took everything else. I let my fingertips graze the bell — and felt a wedding, a whole wedding, bells and rain and a bride laughing so hard she couldn't walk straight, and then the feeling curdled into absence, into a silence where laughter used to be, and I snatched my hand back as though the bronze had teeth.

"Don't," Mara said sharply. "Not the small ones. The small ones are the worst. A big memory you can stand apart from. A small one gets inside your clothes."

But I had already seen the shape of the thing, the way the objects worked, and I had also — I will admit this now, since I am admitting everything — already seen what I was looking for. Near the vault's heart, on a plinth of black stone taller than the rest, lay a scale.

A dragon-scale. Not the seal — the seal was sewn into the lining of my coat, where it had been since the night my village stopped existing. This was another scale, duller, its edges worn soft, mounted on a fragment of charred bone as though someone had once tried to make a talisman of it and lost their nerve.

It was warm. I knew it would be warm before I touched it. Everything about that room had been leading me there the way a river leads to the sea, patiently, without any single drop deciding anything.

"Ishola." Mara's voice, tight. "Don't."

"She said the same thing," I heard myself answer. "The keeper. Before the soldiers came. She said *don't touch it unless you're ready to carry it.*"

"Who said? Who are you talking about?"

I didn't know. That is the truth and I hold to it: I did not know whose voice was coming out of my mouth. The room knew things I didn't. Or the scale did. Or the dead do; I have stopped distinguishing.

I touched it.

* * *

Here is what I can tell you, and here is what I cannot.

I can tell you the color of the sky on the dragons' last night, because I stood under it. It was the color of a bruise healing, yellow-green going violet at the edges, and the mountains to the east were black cutouts against it except where the fires of the army below pricked them with orange, thousands of campfires, an army the size of a migrating nation.

I can tell you the smell: sulfur and cut grass and horse and, underneath everything, the smell of the oath itself, which I now know has a smell — hot metal and rain, a smell like the second before lightning.

I can tell you that dragons grieve the way old trees fall: slowly, groaning, taking everything nearby down with them.

I was — not Ishola. I was a swordspeaker named Adeyemi, tall, sun-dark, with a scar through my lip I kept touching because my wife had died giving birth three winters past and touching the scar was how I remembered her face, because the scar was hers, her needlework, done drunk on festival wine and proud of it. I stood in the last oath-circle with eleven others on a ridge above the dragon-roost, and our captain, a small grey woman named Bisan whose voice could crack stone when she spent it, was telling us what the king's herald had offered at dawn.

Pardon. That was the offer. Pardon for the oath-bound who laid down their swords and surrendered their gift — the gift stripped out of them, which the herald described the way you'd describe having a rotten tooth drawn. And death for the rest. And for the dragons, regardless: fire.

Because the war was over. That was the poison of it. The war we had bled nine years to win was *over*, the enemy broken, the borders drawn, and the king's historians had already begun writing the new chronicles, and in those chronicles there was no room for creatures that could burn a city in an afternoon and choose, every dawn, not to. A king cannot sleep next to that much restraint. Restraint is a debt, and kings hate debtors.

Bisan asked us to vote. Eleven swordspeakers, one dragon-elder — a creature called Vethremar, old enough that his scales had gone the grey of ash, standing behind us with his head lowered, listening to men decide his death in a language he had helped teach their grandmothers.

Three voted to run north, beyond the maps. Three voted to fight, and go down singing. Five voted to trust the pardon.

I voted to trust the pardon. Remember that. Whatever else the fire takes from me, make sure that stays: I voted to trust the pardon, because I was tired, because my wife was dead, because my daughter needed a father with both hands and no bounty on his name, because mercy was being offered by a man wearing a crown and crowns are very good at looking like faces.

Vethremar did not vote. He said — and I felt the words in my chest before I heard them, the way you feel thunder — he said: *I have watched your kind for six hundred years. You are the only species that apologizes to the thing it kills. Go home to your daughter. I will stay for the fire. Someone should witness it from the inside.*

We broke the circle. We walked down. The five of us who trusted knelt in the mud before the king's marshal and gave up our swords, and the stripping of the gift was exactly like the tooth, exactly like the herald said — a pulling, a hollow, a socket where something lived — and I remember thinking, in the last clear moment, *oh, it's true, it really is gone, I'm only a man now,* and feeling relief.

Then they killed us anyway. All twelve. The pardon was written in advance, dated, sealed, and read aloud over our bodies so that history would record that the oath-bound died pardoned — pardoned, do you understand, pardoned and dead, the document and the corpse displayed together, the first great masterpiece of the new school of historiography. And the dragons burned that night in their roost, and Vethremar sang while it happened, and the song entered the rock, and the rock remembered, and a shard of that rock with one scale fused to it sat on a shelf in a cellar under a guildhouse in a city that has forgotten all of it, waiting.

Waiting for a woman with burned hands to pick it up.

* * *

I came back to myself on the floor. Mara had hold of my shoulders and was shaking me, and my face was wet, and I was saying a name — Adeyemi, Adeyemi — that has never been mine, in a voice pitched lower than mine, and it took him calling my actual name four times to bring me back across.

When I could speak, I spoke. I told him all of it. The vote. The pardon dated in advance. Vethremar's song. The historians' ink still wet while the blood was still wet. I told him about the daughter, whose name I could not hold onto — it slipped away even as I reached for it, the way names do in dreams — and about the scar, and about the relief, the *relief*, of being emptied, which was the cruelest detail of all and the one I most needed him to hear.

"They didn't just kill them," I said. "They made the killing *tidy*. They wrote it so cleanly that nine hundred years later, the story everyone learns is that the dragons turned on the crown, that the oath-bound were traitors, that the burning was justice. Your own histories, Mara. What did they teach you?"

He was kneeling in the dust beside me and he had gone the color of tallow. "That the oath-bound started the war. That the dragons razed three cities before the king stopped them. That the swordspeakers' oaths were — " he stopped. Started again. "There's a verse. Children learn it. *The tongue that binds the fire binds the land.* It's a warning rhyme. We skip rope to it."

"Skip rope," I repeated. I laughed, and it was an awful sound, and I didn't care. "They skip rope to it. Oh, that's beautiful. That's *craftsmanship.* You don't ban a memory, Mara — banning makes people curious. You turn it into a nursery rhyme. You make it so small and so safe that no one ever thinks to open it up and look inside."

"The seal," he said slowly. "The one you carried. It showed me — " his hand went to his chest, unconsciously, the way you touch a bruise — "a piece of this. Enough to make me sick. Not enough to make me certain. This — " he looked up at the dark ceiling, at the thousand murmuring shapes — "this is certainty."

"Then you understand what I'm asking."

"I understand what you're asking," he said. "That's the problem."

He got up and walked away from me, three steps, four, and stood with his back to me among the plinths, and when he turned around his face had done the clerk's trick again, everything filed and squared, and I realized I hated that face, and realized too that I understood it better than I wanted to.

"You want me to speak the oath," he said. "Out loud. The full oath, the one that hasn't been spoken in nine hundred years, the one the crown is hunting people to the edge of the map to prevent. You want me to light a fire that burns false history out of the world's memory — and you've *felt* what that fire is. You were just inside it. It doesn't distinguish, Ishola. It burned the truth into the rock and it burned twelve people into ash in the same night. You want me to strike that match in a world with one flame left and no second chances."

"I want you to tell the truth," I said. "The fire is the truth's business, not ours."

"And my gift — " his voice cracked, just once, and he mastered it, and I could have wept at the cost of that mastery — "my gift survives because it's small. Because I've never spoken it. Every oath-bound who spoke, died. That's not legend, that's arithmetic. You're asking me to do arithmetic that ends in zero."

"Yes," I said. "I am. Because zero is what's coming anyway. I watched my village become zero, Mara. Forty-one people, and a well, and a shrine with a floor I helped my mother scrub every festival day, and now there is a field with no village in it and no record that there ever was one, and the king's historians have already written the field's biography. Zero is not a risk. Zero is the *schedule*. The only question is whether it arrives politely, one village at a time, or whether somebody stands up in front of the last fire on earth and makes it earn its meal."

The vault murmured around us. Somewhere above us, faintly, the guildhouse settled and creaked, ordinary sounds, the sounds of people sleeping in rooms full of catalogued impossibilities, believing themselves safe because the ledgers balanced.

Mara looked at the dragon-scale on its plinth for a long time. Then he looked at his own right hand, the sword-hand, and I saw him flex it, testing, the way you test ice before you commit your weight.

"There's a buyer coming," he said at last, quietly. "Day after tomorrow. A magistrate — one of the decent ones, if such a thing exists. Sefu wants to sell him a remembering sword. Test whether the law can be made to swallow a piece of the truth without choking." He said it flatly, a clerk reciting inventory, but his eyes weren't flat at all. "If the law works — if one honest man in a courtroom can force one true sentence into one official record — then maybe we don't need fire. Maybe there's a road through this that doesn't end in ash."

"And if it chokes?"

He didn't answer. He picked up the lamp and held the door for me, and I passed him, and just before we left the vault I heard him say — so softly I was meant to half-hear it, or maybe so softly he was meant to half-hear himself:

"If it chokes, then God help us, I'll open my mouth."

Behind us, in the dark, the dragon-scale sat on its plinth, warm as a banked coal, patient as a creditor.

And upstairs, at the top of the stairs, Guildmaster Sefu was waiting with a lamp of his own and a face like a closed ledger — and he had been waiting long enough, I would later realize, to have heard every word.
