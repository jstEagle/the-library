## 11. A Fair Price for a Sword

The Guild of Impossible Things had sold stranger things than swords. We had, in my three years behind the ledger desk, moved a mirror that showed its buyer's mother at seventeen, a jar of last words (three remaining), and — to a man from the salt coast who paid without haggling — a small brass bell that rang only when someone lied within earshot. The bell had been a mistake. It rang so often in his house that he'd smashed it inside a month and written us a furious letter we framed above the hearth.

But we had never sold a remembering sword. Not while I kept the books.

The sword in question lay on the green baize of the appraisal table like something asleep. It was old — older than the guildhouse, older than the city walls it had presumably once defended — with a blade the color of rain over iron and a grip wrapped in leather gone soft as chamois from centuries of hands. Its name, according to the inventory, was *Verdict*. Swords that remember their kills always have names. Nobody gives them; they grow them, the way scar tissue grows.

I had catalogued it myself, two winters past, when a widow from the hill districts brought it in a sack of turnips because she couldn't bear to keep it and couldn't bear to throw away anything her husband had touched. I had recorded its provenance, its weight, its condition, and then — because the ledger demanded a full accounting and my pen is an honest instrument even when I am not — I had recorded what it remembered.

Four hundred and eleven deaths. I will not pretend I wrote them all down. The sword showed me the first thirty or so before I learned to close my eyes behind them, the way you learn to look through a window without seeing the street. A boy soldier's death in a barley field. An old woman drowned in her own doorway by men wearing no colors at all. A duel between brothers that both of them lost. The memories came up through the grip like cold water through a well rope, and I sat there in the appraisal room with my hands on the hilt and wept for strangers who had been dead longer than the guild had existed.

That was the day Sefu found out about me. Or confirmed what he already suspected, which may be the same thing in a guildhouse where nothing stays secret long except the things everyone has agreed not to see.

"You held it," he said. Not a question. He stood in the doorway with his arms folded, a big man gone soft at the edges, wearing the expression of a father who has just watched his child juggle knives competently and cannot decide whether to be proud or start praying.

"I catalogued it," I said. "It's in the ledger."

"Show me."

I turned to the page. He read it standing, lips moving slightly, the way he read everything, though he'd deny it. When he reached the entry about the drowned woman he closed the book gently, as if it were sleeping.

"We don't sell this one," he said.

"It's inventory."

"It's a grave with a handle." He set the ledger back on the desk with exaggerated care, the way he did everything that frightened him. "Mara. Listen to me. There are objects in this house that are dangerous because they're cursed, and objects that are dangerous because they're valuable, and maybe six that are dangerous because they're *true*. That sword is all three. We keep it in the deep inventory and we let it sleep."

So it slept. Two years in the vault below the counting room, in a case lined with wool and warded with the particular silence the guild uses instead of locks, because locks invite picking and silence invites nothing at all.

And now Magistrate Adunbi Okafor wanted to buy it.

* * *

He arrived on a Tuesday, which felt appropriate. Tuesdays were our slow days, the days when the impossible things stayed quietly impossible and the ledger balanced and I could almost believe the world was made of columns that summed correctly.

He was not what I expected. I had imagined a magistrate as a kind of well-dressed knife — all edges and polish, arriving to cut something. Adunbi Okafor was a heavyset man in his fifties with ink stains on three fingers and the resigned posture of someone who had spent twenty years listening to people lie to him in increasingly creative ways. He wore his robes like a man wears a coat he's outgrown: faithfully, without affection.

"Master Oyelaran," he said, reading my name off the door plaque with the careful pronunciation of a man who checks his facts. "You keep the ledgers."

"I keep the ledgers."

"Then you'll know whether the guild holds a sword that remembers." He sat without being invited, which told me he was accustomed to rooms arranging themselves around him. "Not metaphorically. Not a sword with a famous history. A sword that *remembers*. I'm told such things exist. I'm told your guild exists precisely so that such things have somewhere to exist."

"And who told you?"

"A dying man," he said simply. "A witness in a case I lost eight years ago. On his last night he told me that if I ever needed the truth about a killing — the whole truth, not the shape of it that survives in courtrooms — I should come here and ask for the sword that remembers." He laced his fingers over his belly. "I thought he was delirious. Then last month I presided over a trial where a woman was convicted of murdering her husband on the evidence of one witness, and every hair on my arms stood up while the testimony was read, because I have heard that exact cadence of lying perhaps four hundred times, and I could do nothing. My hands were bound by procedure. Procedure is a wonderful servant and a terrible master, Master Oyelaran. So. Here I am."

I should have refused him. I want that on the record, since I keep records: some part of me, the part Sefu had spent three years training, knew that selling a remembering sword to a magistrate of the crown was like handing a lit lamp to a man standing in a powder store. The hunt was three weeks old. Proclamations on every corner. Swordspeakers dragged from their beds. And here was a functionary of that same crown asking, politely, to purchase the most dangerous true thing we owned.

But he had said *procedure*, with the bitterness of a man who had tried to be good inside a bad machine, and I recognized the tone. It was the tone of my own thoughts most mornings.

"The sword is not for sale," I said.

"No," he agreed pleasantly. "Very few things are, until their price is understood. What would it cost? Not in coin. In obligation. If I bought such a sword, what would I owe your guild?"

I looked at him for a long moment. Somewhere upstairs Ishola was sleeping, or pretending to sleep, in the room we'd given her under the eaves. Her burns had begun to heal into something that looked less like ruin and more like weathering. She had taken to sitting with me in the evenings while I worked, saying nothing, watching the way I entered figures, and once she'd asked, very quietly, why I bothered to write things down when writing could be burned.

"Because burning requires finding," I'd said. And she'd smiled with half her face, the half that still worked.

Now I heard myself say: "The price is use. You buy the sword, you swear before witnesses that you'll use it only in cases where the evidence conflicts — where the truth is contested. Not to settle private scores. Not to enrich yourself. And you record what it shows you, and those records enter the public archive of the magistrate's court, open to any citizen."

Okafor raised his eyebrows. "You want me to make its memories *law*."

"I want you to make them *matters of record*. Law can be repealed. Records are harder to kill. Believe me, I've tried to kill a few."

Something passed across his face — recognition, maybe, of a fellow clerk in the great disorganized bureaucracy of truth. "And if the crown orders me to stop using it?"

"Then you'll face the same choice every honest man faces eventually," I said. "And I'd like to know, before money changes hands, which way you lean."

He laughed. It transformed him entirely — twenty years fell away and left a tired, clever man who had once believed in something. "You drive a hard bargain for a shopkeeper. Very well. I accept your terms. Draw up the contract. I'll pay triple your appraisal and consider it cheap."

* * *

Sefu took the news the way stone takes rain.

"You're selling *Verdict*," he said. We were in his office, which smelled of pipe smoke and old decisions. "To a crown magistrate. During a purge of exactly the sort of people whose memories that sword carries."

"To a magistrate who hates what the crown is doing," I said. "Who came to us because a dead witness told him the truth had a price and he wanted to pay it."

"People change, Mara. Crowns lean on people until they bend."

"Then we sell him a sword that remembers *bending*," I said. "Sefu, listen. Ishola's village doesn't exist anymore. Not burned — *erased*. The maps have been redrawn. The tax rolls amended. Ask anyone in the capital where Ashfall was and they'll tell you there's never been such a place. She watched forty people die and the world has already forgotten how to grieve them. I can't give her village back. But if a magistrate starts ruling based on what actually happened instead of what was reported — if even one verdict goes the true way — then the law itself becomes a place where memory can survive. A crack in the wall. Things grow through cracks."

He was quiet for a long time. Outside his window, the market bell rang the hour, and we both flinched — an occupational hazard lately.

"You sound like the guildmaster who trained me," he said finally. "He used to say the guild's purpose wasn't to hoard the impossible but to *circulate* it — carefully, like medicine. Dose enough to cure, never enough to kill." He rubbed his eyes. "If this goes wrong, it won't go wrong the way you fear. Okafor won't betray us. He'll simply be removed. Replaced. And whoever replaces him will inherit a remembering sword and a contract with our seal on it, and they'll use it however the crown tells them to."

"So we build a condition into the sale," I said slowly, the idea assembling itself as I spoke, the way the best clauses do. "If the sword passes out of Okafor's hands for any reason other than death, ownership reverts to the guild. Automatically. No negotiation. The contract enforces itself."

"That's not how contracts work."

"In this house it is." I tapped the ledger. "Everything in this building works the way it's supposed to work. That's rather the point of us."

* * *

The sale happened on a Friday, in the appraisal room, with witnesses: Sefu, myself, Okafor's own clerk, and — at her own insistence, wrapped in a veil against the light — Ishola.

She hadn't spoken during the negotiations. But when I brought Verdict up from the vault and laid it on the baize, she made a sound I hadn't heard from her before. Small. Like a hinge turning after decades.

"You know it," I said.

"My grandmother's line carried them." Her voice came out sanded smooth by smoke. "Before the purges. Before my time. The oath-bound didn't just speak to fire, Mara. They *listened* — to blades, to stones, to the dead who had nowhere else to put their memories. The swordspeakers weren't warriors. They were archivists who happened to be armed." She reached out and did not quite touch the hilt. "This one is very old. Can't you feel it? It's tired."

"It remembers four hundred and eleven deaths," I said.

"Four hundred and twelve." She looked at me. "It was there when the war ended. It remembers the betrayal too. Kings signing peace with one hand and writing lies with the other. If your magistrate ever asks it about the ancient war—" She stopped herself. Looked at Sefu. Looked at me. "Be careful what questions you teach it to answer."

We drew up the contract. Triple appraisal, paid in weighed coin. The use-oath, sworn on Okafor's own warrant. The reversion clause, which I wrote myself and which Sefu sealed with the guild's stamp — the stamp of the Guild of Impossible Things, which is a small device showing a door that is both open and shut, depending on how you look at it.

When it was done, Okafor stood, gathered his robes, and paused at the door.

"One more thing," he said. "The dying witness who sent me. His name was Tunde. He was a swordspeaker — a minor gift, he could hear lies the way you hear rain. The crown's men took him last month. Before they came, he sent me a letter. It said: *Tell the guild the hunt isn't for swordspeakers. It's for something the swordspeakers are standing between the crown and.*" He met my eyes. "I don't know what that means. But you strike me as men who might."

The door closed behind him. The room was very quiet.

Ishola pulled back her veil. Her ruined face was pale beneath the burn-scarring, and her single working eye had gone bright and fixed, like a bird's.

"He knows," she said. "Or he suspects. Either way, word is going to reach the wrong ears that the guild sold a remembering sword to a magistrate who asks inconvenient questions." She picked up the coin chest, weighing it absently, a woman handling the instruments of her own endangerment. "How long do you think we have?"

I thought about Tunde's letter. About the hunt that wasn't a hunt. About the seal hidden in the memory-vault, warm as a living thing, dreaming its old dreams of dragons and treachery.

"Long enough," I said, and hated how much I sounded like a man whistling past a graveyard. "Long enough to matter."

That night I entered the sale in the ledger. Sold: one sword (*Verdict*), remembering, four hundred and twelve deaths, to Magistrate A. Okafor, with conditions. Price: fair. Risk: unpriced.

And beneath it, in the margin, in the small cipher only I could read, I added the thing I couldn't write plainly:

*First test of whether the law can hold memory. Result pending.*

Three days later, the result began to come in. Okafor used the sword in the retrial of the convicted widow — and the sword remembered the husband's death differently than the witness swore it. The verdict overturned. The crowd outside the courthouse cheered. And by nightfall, per the court clerks' gossip that reached even our kitchen, a courier had ridden from the courthouse straight to the palace, carrying a report of the proceedings and a description of the strange old blade that had decided them.

We had made our crack in the wall. Now the wall intended to do something about it.
