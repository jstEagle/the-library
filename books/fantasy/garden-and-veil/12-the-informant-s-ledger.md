## 12. The Informant's Ledger

There is a particular kind of silence a man makes when he is pretending not to be home. I have catalogued it, in my way. The guildhouse taught me early that people are inventory too — they come in, they get shelved somewhere, and if you're careful you can read their spines without ever pulling them down.

Master Corvel of the Salt Road was making that silence now.

He sat across from me in the guild's receiving room, in the good chair, the one Sefu saved for buyers who paid in coin rather than barter, and he was being very still about it. Still hands. Still shoulders. A stillness so deliberate it had its own gravity, pulling every eye in the room toward the things he wasn't doing.

"Tea?" I asked.

"Thank you, no." His voice was warm as a banker's handshake. "I'm expected back at the Gilded Scale by evening bell."

"Of course. Buyers always are." I set down the tray anyway, because hospitality is a ledger entry like any other, and because I wanted to watch what his hands did when they weren't being watched. "You said you were interested in provenance items. Seals, signets, things with official weight."

"Official weight." He turned the phrase over like a coin he suspected was clipped. "Yes. Particularly anything recovered from the eastern provinces. Ash Country, some call it now."

"Some do," I agreed. "The Guild of Impossible Things doesn't deal in recovered property. Recovered implies it was lost. Most of what comes out of the east was taken."

Something flickered behind his eyes — not offense. *Assessment.* The flicker of a man updating a figure in a column only he could see.

"And yet," Corvel said, "rumor insists your house received a delivery not three nights past. A woman, badly burned. Carrying something."

"Rumor," I said, "is the one commodity this guild refuses to stock. We'd go broke. Everyone would buy it."

He smiled. It was a good smile. It had been purchased at some expense, I imagined, along with the coat and the accent that put him somewhere between the capital and nowhere in particular. "Master Oyelaran. May I be frank?"

"You may be whatever you like. It's a free house."

"I represent collectors who pay for discretion with more than coin." He reached into his coat — slowly, the way a man does when he wants you to see exactly how unarmed he is choosing to be — and produced a small card of heavy cream stock. No name on it. Only an address in the capital's ledger district and a single embossed device: a crown over an open book. "Names, Master Oyelaran. My employer pays in names. The name of a debt forgiven. The name of a warrant withdrawn. The name of a man struck from a list no one admits exists." He laid the card on the table between us and did not push it toward me, which was its own kind of courtesy, or its own kind of trap. "If something of official weight should pass through your inventory, my employer would consider it a professional kindness to be told first. And would remember the kindness."

I looked at the card. Crown over open book. I thought: *that is either the royal historiographers' office or a very committed forger,* and then I thought: *in the capital those are increasingly the same profession.*

"I'll pass it to the Guildmaster," I said, which was true, and also meant nothing, because passing things to Sefu was like dropping stones down a well. You heard them land eventually, or you didn't.

"Of course." Corvel rose. At the door he paused, and for one unguarded half-second the warmth drained out of his face like water through a cracked jar, and what was underneath was tired. Genuinely, humanly tired. Then the warmth came back up like lamplight and he said, "A pleasure, Master Oyelaran. I hope we do business."

"I hope everyone does," I said, and saw him out, and stood in the doorway watching him cross the courtyard until he passed under the eave where the light failed, and I noticed — because noticing is the whole of my trade — that he did not turn left toward the market road. He turned right, toward the tannery lane, where there was nothing to buy but hides and trouble.

And I noticed something else. Something small enough that a less paranoid man would have let it go, and a more honest man would have been ashamed of what he did next.

Corvel's chair. When he rose, he'd rested his hand on the seat-back to push himself up. An old habit, a heavy man's habit, except Corvel wasn't heavy. And when he took his hand away, there on the wood, faint as breath on glass, was the ghost of a wax impression. Red wax. The kind that holds a seal.

He hadn't been carrying a seal. He'd been *using* one, pressed against the chair to leave a mark — a maker's mark, a tracker's mark, the kind of thing a spy leaves so a colleague walking the same room later knows the ground has been read.

The Guild of Impossible Things had just been inventoried.

* * *

Sefu listened to all of it without moving his face, which I have come to understand is his way of moving his face.

"A wax ghost," he said at last. "On my chair."

"On the buyer's chair. Which is worse, if you think about it. He marked the chair we give to people we trust."

"I don't trust anyone in that chair." Sefu turned his cup a quarter-turn on its saucer, a habit I'd catalogued long ago as meaning *I am thinking about something I will not say yet.* "Describe him again."

"Tall. Capital coat, provincial boots — new boots, broken in fast, he walks like a man whose feet haven't accepted them. Accent that slides. Hands soft except two calluses on the right forefinger, here and here—" I touched my own finger to show him. "Ink calluses. He writes more than he lets on. And he smelled of lamp oil and old paper. Not a collector's paper smell. Archive smell. Dust that's been *organized.*"

"Ink calluses." Sefu was quiet a moment. "Mara. What do you know about the man calling himself Corvel?"

"Nothing, which is itself a fact. No merchant in this city can place him. The Gilded Scale has never heard of him. I asked around this morning, carefully, the way you ask about weather."

"And the burned woman?"

"Ishola is in the vault wing, sleeping. She sleeps like someone repaying a debt. She hasn't left her room since she could stand."

"She should leave it less."

I blinked. "Guildmaster?"

"Nothing." He drank his tea. Quarter-turned the cup again. "Watch the tannery lane tonight. If your Corvel keeps appointments, I want to know with whom."

So that night I did what I am best at, which is being furniture. I took the long cloak with the hood that makes even tall men forgettable, and I went out through the coal cellar, and I stood in the shadow of the tannery's drying racks among the stink of lime and hide until the bells rang compline and the fog came off the river the way it always does, thick and smelling of everything the city would rather forget.

Corvel kept his appointment.

He met a woman under the third arch of the tannery bridge — a woman in watch grays, unbadged, which in this city means either very senior or very deniable. They spoke too low for me to catch words, but I didn't need words. I needed what happened after, which was this: she handed him a folio. Not papers loose. A *folio*, bound, with a clasp. The kind of thing you don't hand across a bridge in fog unless losing it would cost someone their post or their neck.

And then she handed him something else, small, wrapped in cloth, and he weighed it in his palm the way a man weighs a purse, and nodded, and they parted, and I stood in the lime-stink with my heart going like a drum in a parade and made the only decision available to a man built like furniture.

I followed the folio.

* * *

Here is a thing they don't tell you about spying: it is ninety percent bookkeeping.

Corvel went home to a rented room above a chandler's shop on Candlewick Row, and I know this because I spent two hours learning it, crouched in the doorway of a shuttered pie shop while the fog ate my knees. He lit no lamp. He was careful. But careful men are predictable in their care — they check the street twice, always the same direction, always counting the same beats — and after the second circuit he went up, and after a decent interval I went up after him, because the chandler's outside stair was old and the fourth step sang a note I'd already memorized, and because I have spent eleven years in a house full of impossible things learning that the most impossible thing of all is a lock that stays locked against a patient man.

His window latch gave to a knife blade and a prayer. Inside, the room was dark and smelled of lamp oil and organized dust, exactly as advertised.

I did not bring a lantern. I have ledger-keeper's eyes; I can read large print by moonlight, and I have learned to read by feel, and besides, lighting a lamp in a spy's room seemed like the sort of decision a man gets buried for. So I knelt at his writing desk in the dark and opened drawers by touch, the way I open the guild's strongboxes, and found:

Paper. Reams of it. And ink. And — my fingers stopped — a seal. Brass, not wax, warm from the room, and when I tilted it to catch the window's gray light I felt the design with my thumb before I saw it: a crown over an open book.

The royal historiographers' office. Or its forgery. In the capital, I reminded myself, the distinction was decorative.

And beneath the desk's false bottom — because of course there was a false bottom; there is always a false bottom; I have installed four of them myself in this very guildhouse — beneath it, a folio. Bound. Clasped. The twin of the one passed under the bridge.

I took it.

Understand what I did. I, Mara Oyelaran, keeper of ledgers, man whose entire survival strategy consists of being too boring to arrest, committed burglary against an agent of the crown in the middle of a purge that had already emptied three villages and filled the Iron House with people whose gifts were smaller than mine. If Ishola learned of it she would have called it courage. If Sefu learned of it he would have called it the end of the guild. Both would have been wrong. It was neither courage nor suicide. It was bookkeeping. Somewhere in that folio was an account, and accounts are how I understand the world, and I could not spend another night not knowing what was written in this one.

I copied nothing in the room — no time, no light — and I left everything else exactly as it lay, down to the angle of the brass seal, and I went home through the fog with the folio inside my coat, against my chest, where it felt less like stolen paper than like a second heartbeat.

* * *

I did not open it in my room. I opened it in the memory-vault, at the reading table, behind three doors and a wall of stone older than the city, with the vault's one candle lit and my hand not entirely steady.

The first pages were boring, which frightened me more than intrigue would have. Bureaucracy is never bored. Bureaucracy is the most passionate institution on earth; it simply expresses passion in columns.

*Disbursement schedule, quarter three.* Payments to informants in six towns of the eastern provinces. Payments coded by letter. *Item: retainer, subject C., Salt Road circuit.* Subject C. Corvel. My Corvel, drawing crown wages since before the proclamation was ever nailed to the market bell.

I turned the page and the cold started in my stomach and worked outward.

*Memorandum: re. Operation Ledger-Cleanse (provisional designation). Objective: recovery of the artifact designated SCALE, last confirmed in the possession of the order of oath-bound speakers, village of Tember's Ford and environs. Method: general proclamation against swordspeakers, to induce movement of the artifact toward sympathetic houses of refuge. Surveillance of listed refuges to follow. Note: the proclamation requires no arrests of consequence. The arrests are theater. The theater is the net.*

I read it twice. Three times. The candle guttered and I didn't notice.

They had never been hunting swordspeakers. Not primarily. The hunt — the proclamations, the dragged neighbors, the bonfires of confiscated books, the whole screaming machinery of fear that had made me burn a page of my own ledger with my own hands — was bait. A dragnet woven out of terror, cast wide across the provinces for one purpose: to flush the dragon-scale seal out of hiding and watch which burrows it ran to. Every refuge house in the east was on a list. Every refuge house was being watched. And the Guild of Impossible Things —

I found us on the fourth page. There was a map, hand-drawn, precise, with little marks in red wax — the same wax ghost as the chair, I realized, the same maker's mark, a whole private language of surveillance — and our guildhouse was marked with a symbol I didn't recognize until I found the key at the folio's back.

*Confirmed receipt of refugee, female, burned. Artifact probable. Do not seize. Observe. The seal must be taken with its bearer intact and the house compromised publicly, to justify the wider closures. Await instruction from H.*

Await instruction from H. H for Historiographer. H for whoever held the crown over the open book. They weren't going to raid us. They were going to *let us hang ourselves* — let us shelter Ishola long enough, deeply enough, that when the watch finally came through our doors the city would nod and say *well, they were hiding criminals, what did the impossible people expect.* Our shelter was being converted, page by page, into our indictment.

And beneath all of it, the coldest line, the one I copied out later word for word into the secret ledger I had begun keeping for Ishola, the one I hid inside the hollow spine of a songbook:

*Note on historical framing: upon recovery of SCALE, the revised chronicle may proceed. The war of the dragons to be recast as an uprising of beasts and their oath-bound abettors against lawful kings. All contrary testimony to be collected and destroyed under the authority of the present proclamation. Memory follows the archive. Whoever holds the ledger holds the past.*

*Whoever holds the ledger holds the past.*

I sat in the vault with the candle burning down and understood, with a clarity that felt less like revelation than like frost, that I had spent my whole life believing the danger of my gift was that it could be spoken aloud. That the crown feared swordspeakers because we could turn rivers to mist and walls to rubble. That the oaths were the crime.

It was never the oaths. It was the *record*. The gift was just fire, and fire is honest — it burns what's in front of it and says nothing after. What the crown feared was testimony. What the crown was hunting, house by house, village by village, was every ledger, every witness, every voice that remembered the war differently than the revised chronicle wished it remembered. They weren't erasing us because we were dangerous.

They were erasing us because we were *true*, and truth, unlike fire, survives its witnesses if you write it down.

Cold anger, Ishola had called hers once, describing the night her village burned. I had thought I understood her. Sitting there in the vault, with the crown's own arithmetic open on my knee, I finally did. This was not the hot anger that makes a man speak an oath and die gloriously. This was the anger of a man reading a balance sheet and finding his own name in the liabilities column. It did not want to burn. It wanted to *audit*.

I copied everything. Every disbursement, every code letter, every wax-marked map, in my smallest hand, in the secret ledger, and then I considered the folio itself.

Put it back? Return it to the false bottom, leave no gap, let Corvel keep his appointment with history? That was the careful play. The invisible-man play. Eleven years of training said: put it back.

But Corvel would return. Corvel would sit in the buyer's chair and smile his expensive smile and offer names for the seal, and behind the smile would be this folio, this map with our house marked in wax, this sentence — *the house compromised publicly* — waiting for its instruction from H.

And somewhere in this city, perhaps already on the road, was the watch captain who would receive that instruction.

I closed the folio. I did not put it back.

Some entries, once read, cannot be filed. Some debts, once seen, must be called. I would decide by morning whether to show Sefu, whether to show Ishola, whether to show anyone — whether knowledge this heavy was a thing to share or a thing to carry alone, the way I carried the gift, the way I carried everything.

I blew out the candle and sat a moment in the vault's total dark, listening to the stone hold its old memories around me.

That was when I heard, faint through the vault's inner door, footsteps. Unhurried. Familiar. Crossing the corridor toward the vault with the particular soft tread of someone who had walked this path many times and had no reason to believe anyone was watching.

Guildmaster Sefu's tread.

At this hour. Coming here. To the one room in the guildhouse where a man could speak without walls listening — or where a man could meet someone without the house knowing.

I stood very still in the dark, the stolen folio against my chest, and waited to learn which kind of secret my Guildmaster was keeping.
