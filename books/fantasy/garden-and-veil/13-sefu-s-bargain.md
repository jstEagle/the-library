## 13. Sefu's Bargain

I have kept the Guild of Impossible Things alive for thirty-one years, and in that time I have learned that survival is mostly a matter of arithmetic performed in the dark.

The guildhouse holds four hundred and six items of inventory, give or take the ones that wander. A ladder that leans toward yesterday. A jar of last winter's thunder, decanted and corked by some optimist two generations dead. A sword that remembers its kills — I had Mara log that one twice, because it asked him to, and because a thing that asks politely should be humored at least once before you lock it away. We are, on paper, a curiosity house for wealthy eccentrics. On paper we are harmless. On paper is where I do my best work.

The trouble with paper, though, is that it burns, and lately everything in this city smells faintly of smoke.

* * *

The man from the crown came on a Tuesday, which I have always considered an insult. Important catastrophes should arrive on Fridays, when there is wine already open and one has stopped pretending to be useful. He came instead at midmorning, while I was arguing with a delivery boy about whether a crate marked FRAGILE: CONTAINS REGRET could legally be left in the rain.

"Guildmaster Sefu." He was small and neat and gray as ledger dust, the kind of man who has been overlooked so long he has made an art of it. "Dele Adio. Office of Proclamations."

"There's no such office," I said.

"There is now." He smiled the way a knife smiles, which is to say not at all, but with great precision. "May we speak somewhere private? Somewhere without ears?"

"In this house?" I gestured at the shelves, the jars, the ladder sulking against the wall. "Everything here has ears. Some of them have opinions. But come up to my office. It only has mine."

He climbed the stairs behind me, and I noted — because noting things is the whole of my profession — that his boots made no sound on the treads. Not quiet. *No sound.* Someone had paid good money for that, or worse, done it himself.

My office is the smallest room in the guildhouse, which is deliberate. Small rooms discourage long conversations. I sat behind my desk, which is oak older than the city charter, and he sat across from me without being asked, which told me more than anything he would say all morning.

"You have a guest," he said.

"I have many guests. The guild takes lodgers."

"A woman. Burned. Arrived eleven days ago carried through your back door by your archivist."

The rain argument had left my hands cold; now they went colder still. "We take in the injured. It's charity. The Guild of Impossible Things does not turn away the impossible, and a woman who survived what she survived is very much impossible."

"What she survived," Adio said, "was a lawful action pursuant to a royal proclamation."

"Ah." I folded my hands. "You're going to use words like *pursuant* all day, aren't you. Should I send for tea, or will that slow us down too much?"

He ignored that, which was answer enough. "The woman's name is Ishola Adeyemi. She is wanted for questioning regarding sedition, unlicensed gift-bearing, and theft of crown property."

"Theft of—"

"A seal." He produced a sketch from his coat and laid it flat on my desk, face up, like a card he knew I'd already seen. A dragon-scale, worked into a medallion, old as sin and twice as heavy. "Recovered property. The crown has been searching for it for some years."

"And you believe it's here."

"I believe," he said, "that your archivist burned a page of his own ledger the day after the proclamation was read. I believe that page described a woman arriving at your door. I believe your night watchman saw her carried in. And I believe, Guildmaster Sefu, that you are a man who loves this house more than he loves any single person in it."

There it was. No preamble, no courtesy, no pretense that this was anything but what it was. I found, to my own disgust, that I respected him for it.

"Those are a great many beliefs," I said. "Belief is not evidence."

"No. Evidence is what happens next if belief proves correct." He leaned forward. "Let me tell you what happens next either way, and you can decide which version of next you'd prefer."

* * *

Here is what I did not tell him, sitting there with my spine straight and my voice level: that Ishola Adeyemi had been in my house for eleven days and I had not once gone down to look at her.

Mara tended her. The cook fed her. The healer with the borrowed name changed her dressings. I stayed in my office and did arithmetic, and the arithmetic went like this: one woman, weighed against four hundred and six items, nine residents, three employees, and a building that has stood since before the last king's grandfather learned to walk. One woman, weighed against the only home I have ever loved.

I am not proud of the arithmetic. I want that recorded somewhere, even if nowhere safe exists to record it. I ran the numbers every night for eleven nights and every night they came out the same, and every night I hated them a little more, and every morning I got up and kept them anyway, because that is what keeping something alive means. You feed it things. Sometimes the things you feed it are your better self.

Adio laid out the terms with the smoothness of a man who had rehearsed them in front of a mirror.

"The guild continues. Charter intact, inventory untouched, residents unmolested. Your archivist — yes, we know what he is, we've known for some time, we simply lacked cause — receives a general amnesty, contingent on continued discretion. In exchange, the woman and the seal are delivered to the Office of Proclamations within the fortnight. Quietly. No fuss. She'll be questioned and released."

"She won't be released."

"She'll be questioned," he repeated, "and released."

"I've been keeping ledgers for thirty-one years, Master Adio. I know what a false entry looks like. That one's written in disappearing ink."

For the first time, something moved behind his gray little face. Not sympathy. Recognition, maybe — the look of a man finding another professional in the wild.

"The alternative," he said, "is that in nine days, a warrant arrives. Royal watch, full search, every room, every vault, every memory in your cellar catalogued and confiscated. Your charter suspended pending investigation. Your residents detained for questioning — and Guildmaster, the questions asked of gift-bearers these days are not gentle ones. Your archivist goes to the Iron House. Your cook, your watchman, your healer-with-the-false-name, all of them, held until the investigation concludes. Investigations of this kind conclude when the crown concludes they conclude. There is a woman in Kesh who waited six years."

The rain had stopped outside. I could hear the delivery boy giving up and leaving the crate of regret on the step, which struck me then as the most honest act anyone had performed in my presence all week.

"And if I refuse outright?" I asked. "If I throw you out now and burn this conversation out of my memory myself?"

"Then the warrant comes faster, and I'm sorry to say we also stop pretending about the archivist." He rose, smoothed his coat. "You have twelve days. I'll call again on the ninth. You needn't decide today."

"I decided nothing today," I agreed.

"Of course." At the door he paused, and for a moment the gray professionalism slipped and I saw the tired man underneath, the one who had been overlooked so long he'd become a tool of the people doing the overlooking. "For what it's worth, Guildmaster — I don't enjoy this work. I'm told someone has to do it. I've never met the person who told them."

Then he was gone, silent as his boots, and I sat alone in my small office with the arithmetic sprawled across my desk like a corpse I couldn't bury.

* * *

That evening I went down to the common room, which I almost never do, because a guildmaster who haunts his own common room becomes furniture, and furniture gets moved without consultation. But I wanted to see them. All of them. The way a man reads a letter twice before burning it.

Mara was at the corner table doing the inventory, quill scratching, that careful invisible stillness wrapped around him like the shawl our late seamstress knitted for the frost-jar. He has made an art of taking up no space, our Mara. I used to think it was humility. Lately I've understood it's armor, and armor means somebody shot at you first.

Ishola sat near the fire, wrapped in blankets, her ruined skin catching the light like old bronze. She was teaching the cook's daughter a counting song in a language the girl had never heard, and the girl was mangling it joyfully, and Ishola was laughing — actually laughing, this woman whose village had been put to the torch, laughing at a child singing wrong.

I stood in the doorway and watched, and I thought: *this is what I would be selling.* Not a stranger. Not a problem. This. The laugh. The song sung wrong. The firelight on a face that should never have been asked to carry what it carries.

And then I thought: *and if I don't sell it, I lose all of it anyway, plus her.*

Love curdles slowly, or so the poets say. The poets are wrong. It curdles exactly as fast as fear allows, and fear in my house was moving at a remarkable pace.

"Guildmaster." Mara had looked up. He always knows when I'm in a room; I've never determined whether it's the gift or just good sense. "There's tea."

"There's always tea," I said. "It's the one impossible thing we produce ourselves."

I took the cup he poured and drank it standing, and I said nothing about gray men from offices that don't exist, and Mara watched me say nothing about it with eyes that missed nothing, and I thought: *he knows. He's known since the man walked in. And he's waiting to see what I am.*

So was I, frankly.

* * *

On the third night I went down to the memory-vault.

This requires explanation, or would require explanation if anyone were ever to read this, which no one will, because I intend to be the last person who knows certain things and then die tidily. Beneath the guildhouse there is a cellar that isn't on any plan of the building, including the plans I keep locked in my head. The founders built it to hold artifacts too dangerous to sell and too true to destroy. Objects that remember. Objects that insist. The crown has raided three guildhouses in my lifetime and never found ours, because the door opens only for someone who genuinely intends to preserve rather than possess, and no raider in history has managed to mean it.

I went down because I needed to think, and the vault is the only place I think honestly. The artifacts keep you honest. Stand next to a jar of thunder long enough and your own small lies start rattling in their corks.

I stood among the shelves and admitted it to the dark: I was going to do it. Or I was going to consider doing it, which in practice is the same thing wearing a nicer coat. Twelve days. Nine days, then eight. I would hand her over, and the guild would live, and Mara would keep his careful invisible life, and the four hundred and six items would stay on their shelves, and somewhere in the Iron House a woman who taught a child a counting song would be asked questions that are not gentle ones.

Unless.

Unless the questions broke her, and she gave them the seal, and the seal gave them whatever it gives, and the false history the crown is writing got one more stone in its wall. Unless the arithmetic I kept running was wrong not in its sums but in its categories — unless a guild is not its inventory, not its charter, not its walls. Unless a guild is the people in it, in which case I was preparing to spend the people to save the thing that only existed to hold the people, which is the kind of accounting error that ends civilizations.

I picked up the frost-jar. It hummed against my palms, remembering winter.

"I know," I told it. "I know what I am."

The jar, to its credit, did not disagree. Jars of thunder are more merciful than mirrors.

* * *

On the sixth day, Adio sent a note. No messenger I could see; it was simply on my desk when I came up from breakfast, which meant someone had been in my office, which meant the watching had begun in earnest, which meant my time had quietly halved itself.

*The offer stands,* the note said. *The offer improves daily. So does the alternative. — D.A.*

Beneath it, in different ink, smaller, almost apologetic: *They know about the second ledger.*

I read that line four times. Then I sat down, because my legs had filed a formal complaint.

Mara's secret ledger. The one he'd started after Ishola's testimony — I hadn't known about it, but of course I should have guessed; a man who burns one page starts writing another somewhere else, that's the nature of our kind. And the crown knew. Which meant the crown wasn't watching Ishola anymore. They were watching *him*. Which meant the bargain I was negotiating wasn't a bargain at all. It was a stall, a leash, a way of keeping everyone in one convenient building until the crown decided to collect the whole collection — the woman, the seal, the swordspeaker, the ledger, and the guildmaster who'd been fool enough to think he was trading pieces when he was the piece.

I laughed. I'll admit it. Alone in my office, I laughed until it turned into something else, and I let that happen too, briefly, with the door locked.

Then I unlocked the door and began to work, because that is the other thing keeping something alive means: when the arithmetic fails, you change the equation.

I could not save the guild by surrendering her — the surrender bought nothing but time, and the time was already spent. I could not save her by defying the crown openly — I am seventy-one years old and my weapons are stationery. But there is a third column in every ledger, the one bookkeepers pretend doesn't exist: the column where you move the assets before the auditors arrive.

Ishola could leave. Tonight, tomorrow, through the salt gate with the wool merchants, out of the city before the ninth day. The seal with her. Mara too, if he'd go, and he wouldn't, not cleanly, not without the guild — but that was his arithmetic, and I had spent my life respecting other people's books.

And the guild? The guild would remain, hollowed, watched, eventually raided, its charter suspended, its inventory confiscated, its guildmaster detained for questioning. Everything I had built. Everything I loved. Spent — not sold, not traded, *spent*, the way you spend a coin you know is counterfeit anyway, because the crown had already decided the guild was guilty and my cooperation was only ever going to determine the manner of the execution, never prevent it.

They had offered me a trade: her life for the guild's. And I had nearly taken it, God forgive me, I had sat in the dark with the frost-jar and *nearly taken it*, because love curdles fast and fear moves faster.

But a trade requires two parties who intend to honor it. And I keep ledgers. I know a false entry when I read one.

* * *

On the seventh evening I climbed down to the common room and found Mara alone, closing the inventory for the night. He looked up, and this time I didn't pretend to want tea.

"We need to talk," I said, "about a gray man, a false entry, and how much of this house we can carry out through the salt gate before the ninth day."

He set down his quill. And I swear to you, before I said another word, before I explained any of it, he reached beneath the table and drew out a satchel already packed — as if he had been waiting seven days for me to stop doing arithmetic and start being a guildmaster.

Which is, I suppose, the final entry in tonight's ledger: I betrayed this house in my heart for six days. My archivist trusted it for all seven.

I intend to spend what remains of my honesty making that difference matter.
