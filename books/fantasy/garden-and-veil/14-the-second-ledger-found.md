## 14. The Second Ledger Found

There is a particular sound a floorboard makes when it is stepped on by someone who does not want to be heard. Most people never learn to distinguish it from ordinary creaking. I have had eleven years of practice, and I can tell you the difference is in the timing — an honest footfall lands on the beat of a person's walk, but a careful one hesitates, testing, like a tongue probing a cracked tooth.

I was in the counting room at half past midnight, reconciling the week's receipts against Sefu's forged ledgers, when I heard that hesitation in the corridor outside. Three floors below us slept forty guild members who trusted this house to keep them invisible. Above me, behind a loose panel in my own sleeping cell, lay a ledger that could hang every one of them.

The second ledger. My ledger.

I had started it the night after Ishola told me about Ashenmere — no, she hadn't named her village yet then, she'd only said *they came at dusk and left at dawn and by dawn there was no dusk to remember*. I'd sat down with a fresh quire and written, in the small cramped hand I use for figures I don't want anyone auditing: *Village unnamed. Forty-one households. One survivor.* And beneath it, because a ledger is nothing without entries: *Witness: Ishola. Corroborating evidence: none. Status: erased.*

That was three weeks ago. The book had grown fat since. Every name she could dredge out of the ash of her memory, every date, every detail of the purge — the king's men wearing no colors, the fire that burned green at its heart, the historian's clerk who rode through afterward asking cheerful questions about harvest yields. I had added what the seal showed me, transcribed as carefully as I could render visions in ink. I had even begun keeping a column for things I remembered myself: my mentor's face, the oath he made me swear to silence, the exact words he used when he said *never speak it, not to save your life, especially not to save your life.*

It was, I will admit, a stupid book to keep in a house under royal watch. But grief wants a ledger the way hunger wants bread, and I had stopped pretending I was wise sometime around the moment I realized the informant's offer to buy the seal meant the crown already knew it was here.

The floorboard hesitated again. Closer now. The counting room door has a hinge I oil monthly, so when it opened without a whisper I knew whoever held it had oiled hinges too, or had been given a key.

I put out my lamp.

In the dark, the counting room is a country of shapes: shelves of receipt-boxes, the great table scarred by two centuries of knives used as letter-openers, the iron strongbox Sefu believes nobody knows weighs four hundred pounds and contains nothing but his mother's letters. I slid behind the shelf of tax assessments — the one corner of the room where the moonlight from the high window doesn't reach — and did what I do best.

I became furniture.

The door opened fully. A lantern kindled, hooded, its light aimed at the floor like a man ashamed of looking. And there stood Master Quill — no, that's unkind, though not inaccurate — there stood Osen Vael, the crown's buyer of curiosities, the man who paid in names instead of coin, standing in our counting room at midnight with a lantern and a long thin knife that was probably for opening correspondence and probably not.

He moved through the room the way I imagine a magistrate moves through a confession: thorough, unhurried, faintly disappointed. He opened receipt-boxes. He ran his fingers along shelf-edges feeling for dust interruptions. He knelt at the hearth and studied the ash there with the attention of a man reading scripture.

And then he went to the wall panel.

Not my panel — mine is upstairs, behind my bed, disguised as water damage. He went to the *other* one, the one in the counting room's northeast corner, the one I had chosen for the second ledger before I chose better, back when I thought hiding a book inside a wall cavity that already smelled faintly of old paper was clever rather than obvious. I had moved the ledger weeks ago. The cavity was empty.

He reached into it anyway, and came out with his hand full of dust and one sheet of paper that had snagged on a nail.

I watched him hold it up to the lantern. I watched him read it. And I understood, with the slow cold certainty of a man watching floodwater reach the third stair, exactly which page it was.

The page I had practiced on. The first entry, the trial run, where I had written *Ashenmere* in full — the name Ishola finally gave me — along with the number of households, the color of the fire, the clerk's cheerful questions. A complete record of a massacre that officially had never happened, sitting in the hand of the crown's own informant, in a guildhouse full of sleeping people.

Vael smiled. It was not a nice smile, but it was an honest one, in the way a trap is honest about being a trap. He folded the page into his coat with the tenderness of a man pocketing a love letter, took one last look around the counting room — his gaze passing across my shelf of tax assessments, pausing, moving on, God forgive me, moving *on* — and let himself out through the window onto the tiles, closing it behind him with the same courtesy he'd shown coming in.

I counted to two hundred. Then I went upstairs and woke Ishola.

* * *

"We burn it," she said.

"It's the only copy."

"Then we burn it twice as carefully." She was already dressed, which told me she hadn't been sleeping either. Burned survivors, I've learned, sleep the way cats do — lightly, and ready to run. "Mara. He has one page. Pages are testimony. You taught me that. But a whole ledger in a hidden wall of a guildhouse that's already watched? That's not testimony anymore. That's a confession with all our names in the margin."

She wasn't wrong, which annoyed me, because I had been hoping she'd be wrong so I could feel noble about keeping it. "Sefu—"

"Sefu will say whatever keeps the guild alive. That's not a criticism. It's just what he is." She said it gently, the way you tell someone their dog is old. "You've seen it yourself. He loves this house more than any truth in it. So we don't give him the choice."

I looked at the ledger in my hands. Eleven years of careful invisibility, and I had spent three weeks writing the most visible document in the kingdom. There's a lesson there about grief and arithmetic, but I haven't learned it yet.

"The vault," I said. "There are records in the vault older than the guild charter. If the watch ever comes with a writ, they'll take everything anyway. If Vael comes back with friends, they'll take everything *and* find this." I weighed the book. "We can't save it and we can't keep it. So we choose what they're allowed to find."

Ishola looked at me for a long moment. "You're talking about burning the guild's history."

"I'm talking about deciding which history survives. There's a difference. It's a thin difference, but I'm going to need it to be a real one, or I won't be able to live in the morning."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about burning a library: it takes forever.

Paper resists. It curls and blackens at the edges and then, if you look away for a moment, lies there smoldering and intact in its heart. Ink holds on like a drowning man. We worked in the memory-vault's antechamber, feeding the great hearth page by page, and I will not pretend I was dignified about it.

The guild's records go back two hundred and six years. Receipts for impossible things: a sword that remembers its kills, sold and re-sold and finally retired to the vault; a mirror that shows the viewer's grandmother; seventeen jars of weather, various vintages; the deed to a house that exists only on Tuesdays. Acquisition ledgers. Sales ledgers. The membership rolls, generation upon generation of names — some real, some invented, because a guild of impossible things has always kept a few impossible members on its books for cover, and because Sefu's predecessors understood that a list of names is both a treasure and a target.

I saved certain pages first. I'll be honest about my cowardice: I pulled the membership roll for the year I arrived, and the year my mentor died, and the handful of pages recording the guild's quiet tradition of sheltering oath-bound members — the ones Sefu showed me once and swore me to secrecy over, the ones proving I was not the first swordspeaker this house had hidden and fed and lied for. Those I tucked into my shirt, against my skin, like contraband warmth.

Everything else went into the fire.

"This one," Ishola said, holding up a sales record from sixty years ago. "A remembering sword, bought by a provincial magistrate. Look at the note in the margin."

I looked. In faded brown ink, someone had written: *Buyer intends to use it to try cases fairly. May it shame him daily.* Someone, sixty years dead, had believed a sword's memory could serve justice inside the law. I had tested that belief myself just days ago, selling another such blade to Magistrate Corren, and it had half-worked, which is more than most ideas manage. I took the page from her, read it once more, and put it in my shirt with the others.

"You're building a new ledger," she said quietly.

"I'm building a *seed*. A ledger you can replant." I fed another armful to the flames. "A true record isn't the paper. It's what somebody remembers well enough to write again. That's the whole point of everything, isn't it? That's why they burn villages and rewrite wars. Because memory is the only court that can't be bribed — until someone finds a way to bribe it."

The seal, hanging at my throat under my shirt, was warm. It had been warm for days, warmer each day, and I had stopped telling Ishola because every time I mentioned it she got the look she gets — the one that says *speak the oath, Mara, burn the lie out of the world* — and I wasn't ready, and I am still not ready, and the fire in front of me was eating two hundred years of my life one page at a time while I lectured her about memory.

Hypocrisy, I find, is much easier to carry than honesty. It's lighter. It doesn't catch.

* * *

Near dawn, Sefu found us.

I heard him on the stairs — honest footfalls, heavy, on the beat — and by the time he filled the doorway I had arranged myself and Ishola and the fire into something almost explainable. Almost. His eyes went from the hearth to the empty shelves to the ash drifting up in gray moths, and something in his face closed like a shop shutting early.

"How much?" he asked.

"Everything except what matters," I said.

"That's not an answer, boy."

"It's the only kind I have left." I stood up. My knees cracked; I'm thirty-four and the vault stairs are unkind. "Vael found the second ledger. One page of it. He knows what I am, or he'll know by tomorrow's breakfast. When he comes back, he won't come alone, and everything in this house becomes evidence. So I decided what evidence exists."

Sefu came into the room slowly. He picked a scrap of paper out of the ash — a corner of a receipt, I think, for a jar of fog, 2019 guild reckoning — and held it like a man holding a dead bird. For a moment I thought he would strike me. I want to record, in whatever ledger survives this night, that part of me wanted him to. It would have been simpler.

"You should have come to me," he said.

"You'd have said no."

"Yes." He set the scrap down on the mantel with terrible gentleness. "I'd have said no, and negotiated, and bargained, and found a price, because that is what I do, Mara. I keep this house alive. It is the only thing I have ever done well." He looked at the fire a long time. "How many pages did you keep?"

"A few. Names. The oath-bound tradition. One margin note about a fair magistrate."

"Then you kept the guild," he said, "and burned the guildhouse. Which is either wisdom or betrayal, and I genuinely cannot tell you which, and I suspect neither can you."

"No," I agreed. "But ask me again in ten years, if either of us is alive to answer."

Something moved behind his face then — I saw it clearly, and I have spent the days since trying to name it, and I will spend years more failing, because what crossed Sefu's features was not anger or grief but *calculation*, the merchant's reflex, the weighing of a loss against a price. At the time I read it as an old man mourning. I am recording it now, honestly, because I have begun to suspect I misread it, and because a ledger that flatters its subjects is worthless.

"Go," he said finally. "Both of you. Before first bell. Take the seal and take whatever pages you stole and get out of my city. If Vael returns and finds you gone and the records ash, maybe — *maybe* — I can sell him a story about a thief who robbed us and fled. Maybe the house survives. It won't survive with you in it."

"And if it doesn't survive anyway?"

His jaw worked. "Then it dies clean," he said, "which is more than most houses get."

Ishola touched my arm. We went upstairs together, silently, past the sleeping cells of forty people who had fed us and lied for us and never asked what we carried. I did not wake any of them. I have regretted that every day since, and I expect to regret it longer.

At my cell, I pried loose the panel and took out the last thing hidden there besides the ledger's ashes-of-absence: my mentor's sword, wrapped in oilcloth, the one object in the world that remembers its kills and therefore remembers him. I strapped it on. The weight was familiar and wrong, like wearing a dead man's coat.

"Mara," Ishola said from the doorway. "The east gate. The caravans leave at first light for the salt road. If we're on one—"

The bell began to ring.

Not the market bell. Not the guild's own bell. The watch-bell, from the citadel hill, ringing the pattern I had heard only once before in my life, on the night they came for the neighbor with the faint gift: three strokes, a pause, three strokes. *Search authorized. Doors to be opened.*

Below us, boots were already in the street. Many boots. And among the ordinary clatter of the watch I heard something else, something that turned my blood to well-water: a voice giving orders, smooth and courteous and carrying, the voice of a man who pays in names.

Osen Vael had not waited for breakfast.

Ishola looked at me, and I looked at the window, and beyond the window the rooftops fell away toward the east wall and the salt flats beyond, silver-gray under the last of the stars, wide and flat and merciless and — for the next hour, at least — unwatched.

"Nowhere left to hide it," she said.

"No," I said, and drew my mentor's sword, and felt the seal at my throat heat like a coal. "Now we carry it."
