## 15. Flight Across the Salt Flats

*Mara*

The guildhouse burned behind us for exactly as long as it took to walk out of sight of it, and then it was just a smudge on the horizon, and then it wasn't anything at all.

I want to tell you I felt noble about it. That I stood in the road and watched my life's work go up in smoke and thought, *this is the price of truth, and I pay it gladly.* What I actually thought was: *I left my good boots.* And then: *I left eleven years of ledgers.* And then, somewhere around the third milestone: *Sefu's face when he finds the ash in the strongroom.*

That last one kept me warm for a while, which was useful, because the salt flats do not keep anyone warm. They don't keep anyone cool either. They just sit there under the sun like a plate that's been scoured too many times, all white glare and cracked clay, going on forever in every direction as if the world had run out of ideas.

"You're limping," Ishola said.

"I'm walking with character."

"Your character is bleeding through your boot."

She had me there. The controlled burn had not been entirely controlled — fire is a colleague of mine, not an employee, and it takes liberties — and a shelf bracket had come down across my ankle on the way out. Nothing broken. Probably. I have a professional relationship with probably.

We walked. The seal rode against my chest in its oilcloth wrapping, and every few hundred paces it did its little trick of getting warmer, the way a cat gets heavier when it doesn't want to be carried. I'd stopped asking it what it wanted. It only answered in pictures anyway, and the pictures were all teeth and wings and kings with wet eyes, and I had enough trouble keeping my own nightmares fed without importing more.

Behind us — I didn't look, but you always know — behind us were two things. One was Master Quillon, the informant, who paid in names and collected in flesh, and who now knew about my second ledger because he'd found it, because I'd been clever in exactly the way clever people are stupid, hiding a thing so well that finding it became his whole personality. The other was whoever the crown sent when they stopped sending clerks. Veyra, the courier's description had said. A swordspeaker-hunter, which is a phrase that should not exist, like "firefighter-fire" or "honest tax."

"They'll have horses," Ishola said.

"They'll have horses," I agreed.

"We have your limp and my everything."

"That's called asymmetric warfare. Very fashionable."

She didn't laugh. She hadn't laughed since the guildhouse, and I understood why, and understanding didn't help. When a woman has watched her village emptied and her name struck from the record and her skin turned into a map of somebody else's history, she develops a certain economy of expression. Everything she still says means something. It makes conversation exhausting, honestly. I missed people who could waste words.

The sun climbed. The salt got brighter. My ankle got louder.

Somewhere around midday we found the thing that saved our lives, though neither of us knew it yet: a dead caravan. Or rather, the bones of one — a wagon frame, four ox skulls arranged by scavengers with more patience than taste, and, wedged under the axle where some driver had kicked it in frustration years ago, a water skin. Half full. Tasting of leather and despair.

Ishola drank first. I noticed that I didn't mind, and then I noticed that I noticed, and decided not to examine it. There's a kind of trust that grows in terrible places, and it never looks like trust. It looks like handing someone the water first without doing arithmetic about it.

* * *

*Ishola*

He talked. All day, he talked. About the guild, about the inventory, about a sword that remembered its kills and a mirror that showed you the face you'd have if you'd made different choices and a jar — a *jar* — labeled POSSIBLY WEATHER, DO NOT OPEN ON TUESDAYS.

I think he thought he was distracting me. He was not distracting me. But he was doing something else, something he maybe didn't know he was doing: he was teaching me what a place sounds like when nobody in it is afraid of being recorded wrong. In my village, before, we spoke carefully. After the soldiers came, we didn't speak at all. But this man had spent eleven years writing down impossible things in a house full of impossible things, and his voice had the looseness of someone who never once checked over his shoulder out of habit.

Until recently. Now he checked. I watched him check. Every hour or so his head would turn toward the horizon behind us, quick, like a bird drinking, and come back. He thought I didn't see. I let him think it. Letting people keep their small dignities is most of what kindness is, out here.

"The informant," I said, when the sun had gone low enough to make the salt go pink instead of white. "Quillon. Tell me how he found the ledger."

Mara's jaw worked. "He didn't find it. I showed it to him. Not on purpose. He asked me what I was writing, at the market, the day of the proclamation, and I lied, and I'm a bad liar when I'm frightened, and he watched my hands." He held them up. Ink-stained, burn-scarred, shaking slightly, though that might have been the walking. "A man who pays in names knows what frightened hands look like."

"And the hunter?"

"Veyra." He said the name carefully, tasting it. "I've never met her. But the courier said she carries a sword that doesn't speak anymore. Broken oath-bound, they call them. Giftless. Which means she hunts us for the crime of having what she lost."

"Do you pity her?"

"I haven't decided. Pity's expensive. I'm budgeting."

I almost laughed. Almost. It came out as a breath through my nose, which for me these days is a standing ovation.

We made camp in the wagon frame, such as it was, backs together, because heat leaves a body slower when there's another body leaking back into it. The seal between us did its slow pulse, warm-warm-cool, like something breathing in its sleep. I put my hand on it sometimes. It had shown me things in the vault — the dragons' last night, the kings' faces, the moment the oaths broke — and I think it liked me better than it liked him, or maybe it simply recognized me. Burned things know each other.

"Mara." His name in the dark. "When we reach the dragon-road. If the fire asks something of you that costs more than you have—"

"It won't ask politely, is my point," I finished for him, because he'd gone quiet. "Nothing crown-touched ever does."

In the morning my feet had hardened overnight into something like resolve, or possibly just blisters with ambition. We walked east, where the salt gave way to scrub, where the scrub gave way, eventually, the maps promised, to the dragon-road.

Behind us, small as a beetle, a rider crested the rise we'd crossed at dawn.

* * *

*Mara*

Here is what nobody tells you about being pursued across a salt flat: the pursuer doesn't need to catch you. They just need to exist, at a distance, moving at their pace while you move at yours. It's a mathematics problem, and the answer is always them.

By the second day the rider had resolved into a rider plus remounts, which meant supply lines, which meant the crown, which meant Veyra or someone wearing her job description. By the second evening they were close enough that I could see the sword on their hip catch the sunset, and I confess I spent a shameful amount of time trying to determine whether the blade had the particular dead shine of a broken oath-sword or just ordinary polish.

"She's not gaining," Ishola said.

"She's not losing either."

"Why hasn't she closed? Two days. She could have ridden us down on the first night."

I had been avoiding this thought the way you avoid a loose tooth. But she was right. A hunter with remounts and open ground does not trail her quarry at a courteous distance unless she wants something other than capture. Information, maybe. To see where we were going. Or—

"Or she's not sure," I said slowly. "About the hunt. About what she's hunting."

"You're guessing."

"I'm a ledger-keeper. Guessing is just bookkeeping for the future."

Ishola looked at me for a long moment, and in that look I saw her decide something, and I braced, because decisions made by burned women on salt flats tend to arrive with consequences attached.

"There's a well," she said. "Two days east, at the edge of the flats, where the dragon-road begins. My grandmother drew water from it when she was a girl, before. The purge didn't take the well. Wells are beneath the crown's dignity." Her mouth did something that was almost a smile and mostly wasn't. "If we can reach it, we can leave the flats. If we stay on the flats, we die tired."

"And if she follows us to the well?"

"Then we learn whether she's hunting us or herding us." She stood, and the last light caught the scar tissue along her arm, silver-on-brown, like a map of a country that no longer appeared in any atlas. "Come on, ledger-keeper. Your future needs balancing."

* * *

*Ishola*

He fell on the second night. Not dramatically — no cry, no collapse, just a slowing, and then a stumble, and then Mara Oyelaran sitting down in the middle of the salt flat with the finality of furniture.

"Ankle's worse than I said," he admitted, from the ground. "It's been worse than I said since roughly the guildhouse."

"You said 'walking with character.'"

"I contain multitudes of liars."

I crouched beside him. The moon was up, fat and indifferent, and the salt threw it back up at the sky so that we sat inside a bowl of grey light, and somewhere behind us the rider's campfire was a red pinprick, patient as debt.

I should tell you what I was thinking, since apparently that's part of this. I was thinking about my village, and specifically about old Bintou, who broke her hip the winter before the soldiers came, and how three neighbors carried her to the temple every festival day rather than let her miss it, complaining the entire way, arguing about whose turn it was, and how when the purge came those same three neighbors were the ones who went back into the burning houses looking for children. I was thinking: carrying is a habit. Communities are just people who have practiced.

And I was thinking: this man burned his own history to protect mine. Whatever else he is, whatever cleverness or cowardice lives in him, he put a match to eleven years of himself and walked away from the smoke.

So I took his arm over my shoulders, which was like taking aboard a bundle of wet rope that talks, and I stood us both up.

"Left foot," I said. "Then right foot. Then repeat until rude awakening or well water."

"This is undignified."

"Yes."

"I want it noted in whatever ledger survives us that I objected."

"Noted. Witnessed. Filed." We moved. Step, step. The salt crunched like old snow. "You know what I remember from the vault?" I said, after a while, because the dark makes talk easier and because he needed talking-to more than resting. "The dragons' last night. Before the betrayal. There was a young dragon — a juvenile, scales like green glass — and it kept trying to sleep in the king's tent, and the king kept pretending to mind, and everyone pretended not to see the king not-minding." My throat did something. I pushed through it. "They loved them, Mara. The kings loved the dragons, and then they wrote the loving out, and then they wrote the dragons out, and then they taught their grandchildren that the empty sky had always been empty."

Step, step. His weight shifted. He was listening the way hurt people listen — with the whole body.

"So when I say hurry," I said, "it isn't fear hurrying you. It's that I have seen what forgetting looks like from the inside, and I would rather die tired on a salt flat than arrive at that fire one hour too late to matter."

He was quiet for ten steps. Twenty. Then: "Bintou," he said.

"What?"

"You said her name. Old Bintou. You've never told me her name before." His voice had gone strange and careful. "Say another one."

So I did. I said the names of my village into the dark, all of them, the ones I could still reach, walking him east step by step, and he repeated each one back like a man taking dictation, and I understood suddenly that this was what he was for. Not the gift. Not the oath. This: a mouth that keeps what it's given. The crown could burn every page in the world and it would still have to deal with people like him, people who hear a name and cannot rest until it's written somewhere true.

* * *

*Mara*

We reached the well at dusk on the fourth day, and it was real, and it was wet, and I have never in my life been moved nearly to tears by municipal infrastructure before, but there's a first time for everything.

Ishola hauled the bucket like she was punishing it. I sat on the well's stone lip with my leg extended like a compass needle pointing at my failures, and drank, and the water tasted of iron and depth and absolutely nothing like leather, which alone made it the finest vintage of my career.

That was when the rider came over the last rise.

No pretense now. No courteous distance. Just a horse at a canter, and a figure in travel-grey, and a sword whose shine I could finally read at fifty paces: dead. Dull as lead. A broken oath-blade, sure enough, and its bearer reining in at the edge of the well-light with the easy balance of someone who has done this a thousand times and been paid for most of them.

"Don't," Ishola breathed, her hand finding the seal through my shirt, as if she could hold it down.

"I wasn't going to do anything."

"You were going to say something wry. I could feel it starting."

The rider dismounted. Younger than I expected, or worn younger; a face built for orders, currently fighting them. Eyes that went to the seal-shaped bulge at my chest, then to Ishola's scars, then to my ruined ankle, cataloguing, and — here was the strange part — flinching, slightly, at the scars. As if they were familiar.

"Veyra," I said. "I presume. You've been very patient with us. It's unnerving."

"Give me one reason," she said, and her voice cracked in the middle of it, which I suspect surprised her more than it surprised me, "why I shouldn't take that seal and you both back to the capital. Give me one reason, and it had better be better than the reasons I was given."

Behind her, far off across the darkening salt, a second light had appeared. Smaller. Moving fast. Not a campfire.

Quillon traveled light, and he traveled angry, and he had clearly stopped trusting his hunter to do his collecting for him.

Ishola's hand tightened on my shoulder. Veyra's hand went to her dead sword. And I opened my mouth — because she was right, I was going to say something wry, God help me, it's how I pray —
