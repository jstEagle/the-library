## 16. The Village That Forgot

The salt flats ended the way most good things end in my experience: abruptly, and with someone pretending it was always going to happen that way.

One morning there was nothing but white crust cracking under our boots, and the next there was a line of green — actual green, trees, a river smell — and Mara stopped walking so suddenly I nearly went into his back.

"Emberlow," he said.

"You know it?"

"I know *of* it. It was on the guild's trade routes. A weaving village. Wool from the high pastures, dyed with madder root." He said it the way he said everything from the ledgers, flat and precise, like reciting inventory. "Population, last record, four hundred and eleven souls."

"And now?"

He didn't answer, which was its own kind of answer. We walked down off the flats' edge into the green, and I understood why he'd gone quiet.

Emberlow was not empty. That was the horror of it. If it had been empty — burned, salted, given over to crows — I would have known what to do with that. I had practice. But Emberlow was *occupied*. Smoke rose from chimneys. Laundry hung between houses. A dog trotted out to bark at us with tremendous professional commitment and no conviction whatsoever.

And every field we passed stood half-harvested. Grain gone to seed in one strip while the next was cut clean. An orchard with fruit rotting under trees nobody had picked. A loom house with its door open and shutters banging, and inside, when I looked, a half-finished cloth still on the frame, warped and mildewing.

"It's like they all left mid-sentence," I said.

"Some of them did," Mara said quietly. "Look at the doors."

I looked. Red paint, a handprint-width smear on each lintel. I knew that mark. Every survivor of Ashfall knew that mark. The crown's assessors marked households for seizure before the soldiers came through. In my village they'd painted the marks at dusk and by dawn the houses were full of smoke and screaming.

But here the houses weren't burned. Here people were living in them. Living behind the marks.

An old woman was drawing water at the well. She watched us come up the lane with the mild, unbothered attention you give strangers who are clearly not worth interrupting your morning for.

"Good day," Mara called, doing his polite-clerk voice, the one that made him sound exactly as harmless as he pretended to be. "We're travelers. Might we buy bread? And perhaps ask a few questions?"

"Bread's at Ferren's, two doors past the shrine." She hauled up her bucket. "Questions cost nothing but everyone's got too many answers these days."

It should have been funny. It wasn't, because she said it without any irony at all, the way people say things that have been sanded smooth by repetition until the edges don't catch anymore.

*Everyone's got too many answers.* As if answers were weather. As if they fell on Emberlow from outside.

We bought bread from Ferren, a broad man with flour to his elbows and a smile that came easily and went just as easily. He was happy to talk. That was the strangest part. In Ashfall, after the purge, the survivors couldn't stop talking — names, dates, the number of soldiers, the color of the fire. We talked because silence felt like letting them win twice.

Ferren talked about wool prices.

"The madder crop failed this year," he told us, wrapping a loaf in cloth. "Shame. Best dye-root in three valleys, my grandfather planted those beds. Or —" he paused, and something flickered across his face, quick as a fish under ice, "— someone did. Someone planted them. Long time back."

"Your grandfather?" Mara asked gently.

"I suppose." Ferren frowned at his own hands. "Funny. I can see the beds clear as anything. Red roots, red stains on everything, my mother's hands were red to the wrist every autumn. But I can't tell you who taught me to harvest them. There's a shape where that should be, you know? Like a chair that's been moved out of a room. You can see the mark on the floorboards where it stood."

Mara's hand moved — I saw it, a small involuntary motion toward his coat, toward the seal hidden against his ribs — and then stilled.

"How long has the village been like this?" I asked. "Half-harvested?"

"Oh, years," Ferren said cheerfully. "Since the sickness."

"What sickness?"

And here is the thing I will remember until I die, however long the crown lets me keep a memory: Ferren smiled. He smiled warmly, readily, the smile of a man being helpful, and he said, "What sickness?"

I hadn't mentioned a sickness. Neither had Mara.

* * *

We took a room above the shrine — the only roof willing to take coin from strangers, which told us something about how often strangers came through, which told us something worse. From the little window I could see the whole village laid out: the rotting orchard, the uncut grain, the loom house, and beyond them, on the low hill at the valley's throat, a walled compound with a slate roof and a chimney that smoked day and night.

"That's new construction," Mara said. He'd been at the window an hour, watching. "The stone's unweathered. Five years old at most. Everything else here is older than the guild."

"A garrison?"

"Garrisons don't need chimneys that size. That's a furnace." He turned. His face in the fading light had the look I was learning to dread — the ledger-look, when the numbers refused to balance. "Ishola. The assessors' marks. They're fresh. Repainted. Yearly, I'd guess."

"Why would they repaint marks on houses they never burned?"

"Because the burning isn't the point anymore." He sat down across from me, and lowered his voice even though there was no one to hear. "Think about what the seal showed me. The war. The kings who fought beside dragons and oath-bound, and then wrote them out. I thought that was history — old sin, old ink. But what if it isn't finished? What if the rewriting is a *practice*? Something they do, and got better at, and kept doing?"

"They erased Ashfall," I said. My voice came out strange. "They erased my village. I thought — I've spent three years thinking I escaped because I ran fast enough. Because I hid in a culvert while they burned the houses and I was smoke and luck."

"You did escape."

"No." I was standing now, though I didn't remember getting up. "Don't you see? Ashfall was *recorded*. There were survivors. Me. Dozens of us scattered. The crown couldn't erase what happened because witnesses kept existing. So they did the next best thing — they killed us slowly, hunted us, made sure our testimony died with us. But this—" I pointed at the window, at the mildewed loom house, at Ferren's missing grandfather-shaped hole. "This is different. This is cheaper. Why burn a village when you can leave it standing and take the memory out of it? No refugees. No survivors telling stories. No hunt needed. Just a furnace on a hill and a village that smiles at you and can't say who taught them their own trade."

Mara was very quiet. When he finally spoke, it was barely above breath. "The purge isn't the crime. The purge is the *cleanup*. For anyone they miss."

* * *

We stayed three days. I'll tell you why, though it shames me: I wanted to find one person in Emberlow who remembered. One witness. If even one mind held the truth, then erasure wasn't total, and if it wasn't total, it could be fought, and if it could be fought—

Her name was Sela. Nine years old. She sat on the shrine steps every evening sorting dyed wool scraps into piles, and she sorted them wrong, or rather she sorted them into colors that didn't exist — she kept making a pile of a deep red she called "grandmother-red," and there was no such color in any pile, because the madder beds had failed, because no one harvested them, because no one remembered how.

On the third evening I sat down beside her. "That's a fine red," I said.

"It's grandmother-red." She didn't look up. "Nobody else makes it right."

"Who taught you?"

She shrugged. "Nobody. It's just —" Her small hands stilled over the wool. I watched a child's face do what Ferren's face had done, the fish-under-ice flicker, except on her it lasted longer, because children haven't learned to paper over the holes. "There was a lady. On the hill. With red hands." Her brow furrowed. "No. That's not — there's no hill. There's a hill but there's no *lady*."

"Sela." I kept my voice soft. "Did soldiers ever come here?"

"No."

"Did anything bad ever happen here?"

"No." Then, smaller, looking at her hands: "Why do I keep almost-crying?"

I have been burned. I have walked out of my own village's ashes with my skin coming off in sheets. I did not cry then, or I don't count the crying I did, and I am telling you honestly that nothing in the purges broke me the way that child asking *why do I keep almost-crying* broke me.

Because that's what erasure is. Not forgetting. The wound stays. The grief stays, orphaned, with no name attached, no face, no reason — a grief that eats itself forever because they cut away the thing it was for. They left her sorrow and stole its object. Somewhere under her skin a nine-year-old was mourning a woman with red hands, and she would mourn her her whole life without ever once knowing why, and the crown would call that mercy, because no one screamed, no one fled, no one testified.

"Mara," I said that night, and my voice shook, and I let it. "We have to burn it down."

"The furnace?"

"The whole system. The seal, the flame, all of it. I understand now. I thought we were running *from* them. We're running *to* the only thing that can undo this. Dragon-fire doesn't just burn wood, does it? Your vision — the oath-bound — what did the fire do?"

Mara stared at the floor for a long moment. "In the vision," he said slowly, "the false histories were written in ordinary ink. But the true ones — the oath-ledgers — were written in fire. Spoken aloud, bound to flame. The dragons' fire was the archive. It doesn't destroy memory, Ishola. It *anchors* it. Fire spoken with a true oath can't be unwritten."

"Then that's the answer. That's the whole answer."

"It's also a death sentence for whoever speaks it." He looked up at me, and there was no fear in his face, which frightened me more than fear would have. There was arithmetic. "Every swordspeaker who ever spoke a full oath paid for it. My mentor—"

"Your mentor is dead. Sela's grandmother is deader than dead — she's been *unmade*, do you understand? They didn't kill her, they killed her twice, and the second killing never ends. Every year they repaint the marks. Every year the furnace smokes. How long before they come for whatever's left in that child? Before they sand her smooth like Ferren?"

He didn't answer. Outside, the evening bell rang, and the village of Emberlow rang back with all the warmth of a place with nothing to hide, because it genuinely had nothing to hide, because its hiding had been taken from it.

* * *

We left on the fourth morning, early, in grey light. I want to record — even if no one ever reads this, even if I'm ash before the ink dries — that as we passed the well, the old woman from the first day nodded to us and said, "Safe roads," and then, pausing, frowning at nothing, added:

"Strange. I had a dream about fire. Whole street of it. Can't think why I'd dream that. Nothing ever happened here."

Nothing ever happened here.

Mara walked faster after that. I matched him step for step, and neither of us spoke until the village dropped behind the hills, and then he stopped in the middle of the road and took out the seal and held it in both hands like something that weighed far more than scale and metal.

"She almost remembered," he said. "The old woman. The dream — that's residue. Whatever they use, furnace or working or artifact, it doesn't cut clean. It leaves scar-tissue in the mind. Which means it can be *read*." His thumb moved over the seal's carved whorls. "And if it can be read, it can be reversed. Ishola — the keeper legends, the dragon-road, the flame — I used to think restoring the fire was about justice for the dead. Ancient wrongs. Old kings."

"And now?"

"Now I think the dead can wait." He put the seal away, and his eyes when they lifted to mine were lit with something I hadn't seen in him before — not the careful clerk, not the frightened fugitive. Purpose, banked and burning. "There's a nine-year-old on those shrine steps who will spend her whole life grieving a woman whose name was stolen before she could learn it. That's not history. That's tomorrow. We move faster. We reach the dragon-road before the crown finishes whatever it started here — because if they can hollow out a village of four hundred and eleven souls and call it peace, then nowhere is safe, and no one remembers anything, and eventually—"

He stopped. I watched him arrive at the end of his own sentence and stand there in the wreckage of it.

"Eventually what?" I asked, though I already knew.

"Eventually there's no one left who almost-cries," he said. "Come on. The road turns north here. And Ishola—" he glanced back once, down the long green valley, at the smoke rising thin and innocent from the furnace on the hill, "—we're being followed. Have been since the flats. Don't look. Keep walking."

So I kept walking, and did not look, and behind us Emberlow sank back into its warm, smiling, murdered peace — and somewhere on the road north, footsteps that were not ours kept pace with ours, patient as ink drying.
