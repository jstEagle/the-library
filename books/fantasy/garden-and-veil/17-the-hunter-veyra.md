## 17. The Hunter Veyra

The thing about hunting your own kind is that you already know all the hiding places.

I knew the loose floorboard under the third stair because I'd hidden letters there once, in another life, in a house that no longer existed. I knew the smell of a suppressed gift — like banked coals, like a room where someone burned something sweet and then regretted it. I knew how oath-bound people stood when they were trying not to stand like oath-bound people: weight on the back foot, hands loose, always half-turned toward a door.

So when I walked into the Salt Flats waystation and saw the innkeeper's daughter stacking wood with her weight on her back foot, I didn't need the crown's divination rods or the informant's little book of names. I needed a cup of tea and an hour of patience.

"Tea," I told the innkeeper, laying down a coin that was worth ten cups. "And whatever she's having."

He blinked at me. "My daughter doesn't—"

"She does now." I sat by the window where I could watch the road both ways. Old habit. When you've spent six years doing the crown's dirty arithmetic, you learn to count exits before you count coins.

* * *

Let me be honest about what I am, since no one else will be. I am Veyra Solm-Kessarin — though the surname is struck from the rolls now, so perhaps just Veyra — and for eleven years I was a swordspeaker of the old school, trained at my mother's knee in the speaking of edges. Then the Edict came, and I did what sensible people do: I knelt, I recanted, I let the suppression-rites close over my gift like scar tissue over a wound, and I offered the crown the only thing more valuable than loyalty.

Expertise.

They made me a hunter. Of course they did. Who better to find swordspeakers than one who knows exactly how we breathe?

You want to know if it eats at me. Everyone wants to know if it eats at me. The chaplain who travels with the garrisons asked once, gently, over bad wine, and I told him the truth: it doesn't eat at me, because I feed it. You keep a thing fed, it doesn't gnaw. Every oath-bound I bring in, I tell myself the same story, and the story goes like this:

The oath-bound started the ancient war.

That's the history. That's what's written in the crown's histories, in ink older than my grandmother, in the great illuminated folio they parade through the capital every Founding Day. Dragons rose against the kingdoms. Oath-bound speakers gave the dragons their voices, taught fire to answer human words, and the world burned for a generation until the loyal kings put them down. Every child learns it. I learned it at my mother's knee too, right alongside the edge-speaking, which should have told me something even then — that the two lessons lived in the same mouth, in the same house, warm as bread.

But here is what I know that the children don't: my mother never spoke of the war except to say *we lost*, and she said it the way you say *we lost* about a family argument, not a righteous defeat. And when the Edict came, she didn't fight. She knelt faster than I did. Righteous rebels don't kneel that fast. Guilty people do — or frightened ones, which is its own kind of testimony, if you're willing to read it.

I wasn't willing, back then. Reading testimony gets you hanged alongside the guilty. So I took the story I was given, and I fed it to the thing inside me every morning, and it has kept me fed in return: a salary, a rank, a horse that doesn't spook, and the particular clean sleep of a person who believes herself on the right side of a very old wrong.

Mostly clean. There are nights. But everyone has nights.

* * *

The girl's name was Tessa, according to the innkeeper, who talked the way lonely men talk when someone finally buys them tea — everything pouring out, nothing held back. Tessa, seventeen, quiet, good with animals, had "fits" as a child during which the kitchen knives would hum.

Humming knives. Saints preserve us. She wasn't even oath-bound; she was barely kindled. A spark with no training, no oath, no ledger. Under the old law — my mother's law — she'd have been apprenticed by now, learning to still her own blades before she ever touched another's.

Under the new law, she was cargo.

I sat with my tea and did the arithmetic I always do. Report her, and she goes to the Iron House, where they do things I sign the paperwork for but have never watched, because I arrange my assignments the way I arrange my sleeping: carefully, facing the door. Don't report her, and some other hunter finds her eventually, because the informant's network has grown teeth this season, and the crown wants swordspeakers found the way a man dying of thirst wants water.

There was a third option once. There used to be a third option. You warned them, and they ran, and you wrote in your report that the trail went cold, and the cold trails stacked up behind you like snowdrifts until someone noticed the shape of your shoveling.

I stopped leaving cold trails two years ago, after the Aldermoor business. I don't talk about the Aldermoor business. Let's simply say that a village can burn very thoroughly when the soldiers are frightened, and that frightened soldiers are the natural consequence of officers telling them the people in the village can turn rivers to blood, and that the officer who tells them that is usually me, because I'm very good at my job, and being good at your job means making sure other people do the parts you couldn't stomach doing yourself.

Feed the thing. Keep it fed.

"Tessa," I said, when she came near with the wood. "Come sit. Your father won't mind. I'm buying."

She sat the way oath-blood sits across from a hunter: spine straight, eyes down, every muscle pre-decided about running. I pushed the second cup toward her.

"I'm going to ask you a question," I said, "and I want you to think very hard before you answer, because your whole life is on the other side of it." I let the silence stretch. "Have you ever spoken to a blade?"

Her eyes flicked up. Brown, ordinary, terrified. "No, m'lady."

"Mm." I turned my cup a quarter-turn on the saucer. "Because if you had — if you'd ever, say, asked a knife to stop humming, and it had listened — then there are things I would need to do, and places you would need to go, and none of them would be pleasant. But if you hadn't, then you're just a girl who stacks wood badly, and I'll finish my tea and ride on, and this conversation never happened."

"I stack wood fine," she said, with the small stubbornness of the young, and something in my chest that I keep locked for professional reasons rattled its door.

"Do you," I said. "Show me."

She showed me. It was bad. It was genuinely terrible — logs crossed at angles that would collapse in a strong wind, and I found myself saying, "No, heel to heel, flat side down, you're building a wall, not a prayer," before I remembered that I was supposed to be terrifying, and that my mother had said those exact words to me thirty years ago beside our own woodpile, in a voice gone twenty years into the ground.

I finished my tea. I rode on. In my report that evening I wrote: *Waystation at the Salt Flats crossing. No trace of gift-work. Innkeeper's daughter questioned and cleared.*

Then I sat by my fire for a long time, feeding the thing, and for the first time in years it wouldn't take the food.

* * *

Here is what changed, if you want the exact hour of it. Hunters get orders, and most orders are routine — check this town, question that smith, follow the informant's marks. But three weeks ago the orders changed flavor. Suddenly it wasn't *find the swordspeakers*. It was *find the seal*. A dragon-scale seal, carried by a burned woman out of the eastern purges, last seen moving south with a guild clerk of all absurd things — a *clerk*, a man who keeps ledgers for the Guild of Impossible Things, which even by guild standards is a name that announces itself like a drunk at a funeral.

And with the new orders came new briefing materials. A historian from the capital, traveling with the column, giving evening lectures to the officers on the ancient war. I attended the first one out of duty and stayed for the rest out of something I refused at the time to name.

The historian was very good. Smooth. He had charts. He had the great illuminated folio copied page by page onto canvas screens, and he walked us through the betrayal of the kings, the dragon-pacts, the burning of the loyal provinces. And it was all correct. It matched everything I'd been taught. Every detail sang the same note as the history in my bones.

Except one.

He said the oath-bound struck first. He said it twice, in fact, in different lectures, with slightly different phrasing, and the second time he added a detail — the name of the border town where the first blow fell — and the name was wrong. Not disputed. Wrong. I know the geography of the old war the way farmers know weather; my mother drilled it into me with the edge-speaking, because a speaker who doesn't know where the war happened doesn't know why the oaths were shaped the way they were. The town he named wasn't on any front. It was a market town, inland, nowhere near anything, famous for cheese.

A small error. Historians make them. But he made it twice, confidently, to rooms full of officers who'd repeat it in taverns from here to the coast, and by midsummer every soldier in the column would *know* the oath-bound struck first at a cheese town, and knowing is a thing that doesn't un-happen.

I went to him after the second lecture. I was polite. I said, "Master Historian, the town you named — I believe the sources place the first engagement further east."

He smiled at me the way a man smiles at a dog that has learned one trick. "The sources have been revised," he said. "Recently revised. The crown's scholarship is ongoing."

"Revised to what?"

"To the truth," he said, still smiling, and something in the smile made me understand that he was not a scholar at all, that scholarship was his uniform the way my rank was mine, and that somewhere above us both sat men who had decided what the past was going to say and were simply employing people to say it loudly enough.

I walked out of that tent and stood in the dark between the fires, and I felt the thing inside me shift its weight, and for the first time I wondered — actually wondered, not in the safe 3-a.m. way but in the dangerous daylight way — what exactly I had been feeding all these years.

Because here is the arithmetic I could not avoid, standing there smelling woodsmoke and horse: if the history could be revised *now*, while I watched, then it could have been revised *before*. Before my mother. Before the Edict. Before the great illuminated folio. The whole edifice I'd built my clean sleep on might be a renovation, and I might be living in a house whose foundations were poured by liars.

Hunters are taught to look for forged documents. We're taught the signs: fresh ink on old paper, margins trimmed to hide earlier text, seals reapplied. I had inspected a hundred false relics in my career and condemned them without hesitation.

It had simply never occurred to me to hold up the lamp to my own history and check the age of the ink.

* * *

The trail led south, as trails do. The clerk and the burned woman moved like people who had never fled anything before — stopping too long, paying in coin where a fugitive pays in barter, leaving witnesses alive and chatty. I gained on them at the rate of half a day per week, which meant I had time, and time is the enemy of certainty. Give a hunter time to think and she starts thinking like the hunted.

At the ford country I picked up their tracks properly, and also picked up company: the informant, a soft-footed man called Master Quill by everyone who disliked him, traveling with two of the Iron House's quieter employees. Quill and I had worked together before. I trusted him the way you trust a knife you've checked for defects — completely, right up until the moment you shouldn't.

We compared notes at a crossroads shrine, standing among offerings left by farmers who still believed the old gods bothered with crops.

"They were at the guildhouse," Quill said. "Left in the night after a search. The guildmaster cooperated beautifully — gave us his ledgers, all in order, nothing incriminating." His smile was thin as a razor's edge, and I mean that literally; I've spoken to enough edges to know one when I see it. "Almost too beautiful. Forged, I'd wager. Which means the clerk is more than a clerk."

"You don't say."

"There's a second ledger," Quill said. "Records of the purges. Names, dates, villages. My people nearly had it." He watched my face as he said it, the way a card player watches. "The burned woman is Ishola Adeyemi. Her village was Aldermoor."

The world went very quiet around me, the way it does before a blade speaks.

Aldermoor. The business I don't talk about. The village that burned very thoroughly, where the soldiers were frightened because I had told the officers to frighten them, and where afterward the reports recorded — I signed them myself — a nest of oath-bound agitators plotting sedition, armed, dangerous, justly destroyed.

Quill watched me not react, and I watched him watch me, and we each filed the other's non-reaction away like careful archivists.

"Adeyemi," I said. "Any relation to the swordwrights?"

"Distant, I believe. Why?"

"No reason." I adjusted my saddle girth, which didn't need adjusting. "The seal. What does the crown intend for it?"

"That's above my station." Everything was above Quill's station except other people's secrets. "Though the historians are very eager. And the army is marching south behind us — you'll have seen the dust. They mean to be at the dragon-road within the fortnight."

"The dragon-road," I repeated. "For a seal."

"For everything," Quill said pleasantly. "The flame, the road, the memory of it all. The crown prefers its history consolidated. Scattered things are so easily misread." He tipped an imaginary hat. "Ride safely, Hunter. Do try to take them alive. The woman especially. The Iron House has questions, and she has such interesting answers."

I rode north along the ford road instead of south, because I needed an hour without his voice in my ear, and because the tracks of two fugitives weren't going anywhere.

An hour. That's all I gave myself. I told myself it was tactics — letting the quarry tire, letting Quill's people commit to a route I could cut off later. Very sound. Very professional.

What I actually did was sit on a hill above the ford with my mother's old practice-blade across my knees — yes, I still carry it; yes, that's illegal; no, it has never once spoken, because I paid the suppression-rites too well — and I tried to remember the war.

Not the history. The *war*. The stories underneath the story. My mother's voice saying *we lost* like a family argument. The way she flinched, once, at a Founding Day procession, watching the folio paraded past — a flinch I'd taken for grief and now, on this hill, with the ink-revision still fresh in my mind, took for something else entirely.

Grief looks like grief. But shame looks like grief, too, if you're not looking closely. And hunters are trained to look closely.

*They started the war.* I said it to myself the way I said it every morning, the feeding-words, the clean-sleep words. *The oath-bound started the war, and the kings put them down, and everything since is justice.*

The words went in and hit nothing. No purchase. Like driving a nail into a wall and finding hollow brick behind the plaster.

I sat with that hollowness for a long time. Below me, the ford glittered, ordinary and blue, the kind of river that has never once run red despite what the histories say about it. Somewhere south of it, a burned woman was carrying a piece of scale that the entire apparatus of the crown — armies, historians, informants, and me — was converging to bury.

And I thought: *if the history needs this many guards, what is it hiding?*

I thought: *a lie doesn't need an army. Only a truth does.*

I thought, finally, the thought I'd been not-thinking since the cheese town: *what if we lost because we were wrong?*

The sun went down. I got up, sheathed the silent blade, and turned my horse south, because orders are orders and I am, whatever else is true, still very good at my job.

But that night, making cold camp in the scrub, I took out my report-book and looked at the entry I'd written about the innkeeper's daughter — *questioned and cleared* — and beneath it, in the space reserved for observations, I wrote four words in my smallest hand:

*Sources may require verification.*

It isn't treason. It isn't doubt, even, officially. It's diligence. Any auditor reading my book would nod approvingly.

But I know what I meant. And the book knows. And books, as a certain guild clerk could tell you, are patient things. They wait. They keep what's written in them. And someday, somewhere, someone opens them and reads the truth that the writer couldn't yet say aloud.

Two days later I found the ford churned and steaming, mist rising off water that should have been cold in the early spring, and the grass on the far bank scorched in a perfect arc, and I knew — before Quill's riders caught up, before anyone told me anything — that a swordspeaker had spoken there. Half-spoken. Strained. Desperate.

I dismounted and knelt and put my palm flat against the warm mud, the way my mother taught me to read a blade's residue thirty years ago, and the earth told me what the histories never had:

Whoever had done this hadn't wanted to. Whoever had done this had been *forced* — hunted to a river's edge with no exit and made to spend a piece of himself he'd never get back.

I had spent six years hunting monsters out of the old war's shadow.

Kneeling in that warm mud, I began, very quietly and very late, to wonder whether I'd been hunting in the wrong direction the whole time.
