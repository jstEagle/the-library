## 19. What Half an Oath Costs

There is a particular quality to pain that comes from speaking half of something sacred. I want to describe it for you precisely, because precision is what I have left, and because if you ever find yourself tempted — as I was at the ford, with the water rising and the hunter's crossbow bolt singing past my ear — I would like you to know exactly what you are buying.

It begins in the bones.

Not the skin. The skin is nothing; the skin is the ledger's cover, pretty and replaceable. It starts in the bones of the hand, as though someone has reached inside your wrist and lit a small, patient fire among the knuckles, and then it climbs, joint by joint, up through the carpals and the radius, looking for something. That is the horror of it. Ordinary pain wants only to be felt. This pain *wants*. It moves through you like a clerk moving through a warehouse with a list, opening crates, checking contents against inventory, deciding what stays and what goes.

I learned this lying on my back in the reeds on the far side of the ford, while Ishola pressed river mud against my sword-hand and told me, in the flat voice she uses when she is most frightened, not to sleep.

"I'm not sleeping," I said. "I'm taking inventory."

"Of what?"

"My hand." I flexed the fingers. They moved. That was the good news. The bad news was that they moved wrong — stiffly, like hinges on a door no one had oiled in years, like fingers belonging to a man I had read about rather than been. "Everything appears to be present," I reported. "The thumb is sulking but accounted for."

"Mara."

"Ishola."

"You turned a river to mist."

"A very small river. A stream, really. A generous ditch."

She didn't laugh. Ishola almost never laughs, and when she does it sounds like an apology, so I have stopped trying to earn it and started simply talking, which she says is worse. She sat back on her heels and looked at me with those ash-grey eyes, and I understood that she was doing arithmetic. I have watched guildmasters do it over ledgers, watched Sefu do it over a shipment of bottled thunder that arrived two jars short. You count what came in, you count what went out, and the difference is either profit or theft.

She was counting me.

* * *

We made camp that night in the hollow beneath a fallen watchtower, one of the old border towers from before the Unification, back when this land had too many kings and each king had his own dragons and his own oath-bound to keep them honest. The stones were warm even after dark. Dragon-road stone does that; it holds heat the way a good ledger holds truth — stubbornly, long after everyone wishes it wouldn't.

I built a fire one-handed, which took three times as long and looked, according to Ishola, "like a drunk man fighting a chicken." Gratitude has never been her strongest currency. I fed the fire with my left hand and held my right over the flames, and studied it by the light.

The burn was not a burn. That is the thing I could not stop looking at. There was no blister, no char, none of the ugly wet ruin fire leaves behind. Instead the skin of my palm and fingers had gone smooth and pale, like wax, like paper that has been pressed too long under a heavy book. And across the palm, faint as watermark, there was a mark now where no mark had been: a line, curving, interrupted. Half a sigil. The first half of the oath-shape, drawn there by the words themselves, the way ink bleeds through cheap vellum.

Half a word, written in half a hand.

"It's the price," Ishola said. She had gone quiet for a long time, and when she spoke again it was with the careful diction of someone reciting something learned young. "My grandmother told me. The oath isn't magic you spend. It's magic you *trade* with. The fire doesn't come from nowhere. It comes out of you."

"Out of my hand, apparently."

"Out of your *memory*, Mara." She said it the way you'd say *out of your chest* or *out of your spine*. "The gift lives in what you remember. Every swordspeaker knows the shape of every oath because their teachers carved it into them — into their remembering, not their muscles. When you speak a piece of it without paying the whole price, the fire takes its payment anyway. It just takes it wherever it can reach."

I looked at my pale, smooth, watermarked hand and felt the small patient fire still ticking away in the bones, unhurried, thorough, a clerk who knows exactly how many pages remain in the ledger.

"And where can it reach?" I asked, though I already knew. Of course I already knew. I am a keeper of records; I know that everything in a house is connected, and I know that when the flood comes it enters through whatever door is openest.

"What do you remember of your teacher?" Ishola asked softly.

And here is where I must be honest, because honesty is the only discipline I have ever been any good at.

I remembered that I had had one. I remembered the fact of him the way you remember a room you slept in as a child — you know it existed, you could draw its floor plan, but the smell of it is gone and the sound of the floorboards is gone and you cannot, no matter how you strain, put yourself back inside it. His name should have been right there. Names are the first thing we learn about people and the last thing we lose, I have always said so in my ledgers, I have written it in the front matter of every inventory I have ever kept: *record names first, for all else may be replaced.*

His name was gone.

Not hidden. Not misplaced, the way a thing is misplaced, waiting in some pocket of the mind to be found again. Gone the way a page is gone once it has burned — and I know the difference, gods help me, I have burned enough pages to know the difference between lost and *consumed*.

"He taught me the grip," I said slowly, feeling along the edges of the absence like a tongue probing a missing tooth. "He taught me — there was a drill. With the wooden sword. You stood in the rain. He said — he said—" Nothing. Rain, yes. A wooden sword, yes. But the words he'd said in the rain had gone with his name, and the color of his eyes had gone, and whether he was tall or short, kind or severe, whether he died well or badly, whether I loved him or feared him or both —

Both, I thought desperately. Surely both. One is always both.

But *surely* is not memory. *Surely* is inference. And inference, in my trade, is worth less than nothing, because it looks like knowledge and behaves like guesswork.

"Mara." Ishola's hand — cool, real, five-fingered, unwatermarked — closed over my ruined one. "Breathe."

"I'm breathing. Breathing is one of my remaining skills. Along with cataloguing and sarcasm."

"Good. Keep all three."

* * *

I did not sleep, despite her orders, or perhaps because of them. I sat up through the small hours with my back against the warm tower-stone and conducted the most terrible audit of my life.

You should understand what an audit is, for a man like me. It is not merely counting. It is *reconciliation*: you take the record and you take the world and you lay them side by side, and every place they disagree is a wound you must account for. All my life I have done this with other people's things — the Guild of Impossible Things keeps inventory of objects that ought not exist, and my gift, my secret, criminal, beautiful gift, was that I could hold a sword and hear the names of everyone it had killed, could reconcile the object against its history without a single page of writing. Perfect recall. Perfect reconciliation. The crown outlawed it because a man who remembers perfectly cannot be lied to, and a nation built on a lie cannot survive such a man.

Now the fire was auditing *me*.

I laid out what remained. I will set it down here as I set it down that night, in order, because order is the last comfort of clerks:

Item: I knew I had been born in the river districts of Ilorin-Under-the-Wall. The streets, yes. The smell of the tanneries, yes. My mother's face — present, thank every merciful power, present and clear, though when I reached for her voice it came back thin, as if heard through a wall.

Item: I knew I had been given to the swordspeaker order at nine years old. I knew the gate of the training compound. I knew the taste of the morning porridge, which was terrible, and the evening porridge, which was the same terrible porridge reheated, and I knew that we complained about it in a ritual way, the same joke every night, and the joke itself was gone but the shape of laughing was there, a laughter-shaped hole.

Item: my mentor. Nameless. Faceless. A presence defined entirely by absences, a silhouette cut from black paper. I knew he had died in the purge — the first purge, the quiet one, eleven years ago, before the crown grew bold enough to do it loudly. I knew I had run. I knew I had sworn, over his body or near it, that I would never speak the oath aloud, never draw the fire, never give them a reason to hunt me the way they had hunted him. The oath of silence I remembered perfectly. Every word of it. Isn't that a fine joke? The vow I made to stay silent survives intact, and the man I made it to has been eaten.

Item: my hand hurt.

I sat with the list until the fire burned down to coals, and I did the arithmetic that Ishola had done hours earlier, and the total was this: the fire had taken perhaps a tenth of me. A single chapter torn from a long book. And it had taken the chapter about him — deliberately, I think, or as close to deliberate as a force of nature gets. Because that was the open door. That was the room where I had left the window unlatched. For eleven years I had kept my mentor folded away in the sealed part of myself, the grief-room, the room I entered as rarely as possible, and grief unvisited is memory unmaintained, and the fire is a clerk too, and a clerk takes first from what is least attended.

Half an oath costs a tenth of a self. I lay awake doing that division over and over. A full oath, then — the true oath, spoken whole, with the whole price paid —

Would cost everything.

I did not know this the way you know a sum at the bottom of a column. I knew it the way you know weather coming, in the ache of old scars and the behavior of birds. But I knew it. And somewhere south of us, guttering in a mountain shrine, waited the last dragon-fire, which answers only a full oath, and somewhere behind us came Veyra, and somewhere ahead of us lay a choice shaped exactly like the one I'd already made at the ford, except larger, except final.

* * *

Morning came grey and cold. Ishola had taken first watch and second watch both, refusing to wake me, and when I surfaced from my non-sleep she was sitting at the lip of the hollow, looking north the way she always looks north, toward the Iron House where her village's survivors are kept, toward everything the crown has erased and everything she refuses to let be erased.

"We move within the hour," she said without turning. "The dragon-road is four days east. Maybe five, with your hand."

"With my hand it's four," I said. "I refuse to be slowed down by my own extremities. It sets a precedent."

She turned then, and looked at my hand where it lay in my lap, pale and marked with its half-sigil, and her face did something complicated. Pity and fury and guilt, all sharing a small space, none willing to yield the room to the others.

"Say it," I said.

"Say what?"

"Whatever you've been not saying since the ford. You've been composing it all night. I can hear the drafting."

Her jaw tightened. "You shouldn't have done it. At the ford. You swore — you told me you'd sworn never to speak it. And then one bad moment and you spent a piece of yourself, a piece of *him*, the man who made you, and for what? To avoid getting wet?" Her voice cracked on the last word, and I realized the anger was fear wearing armor, the way it usually is. "If you can spend him for a river, what won't you spend for the flame? What's left of you by the time we reach the shrine? Who walks into that fire, Mara? A stranger wearing your coat?"

I had no answer that wasn't cruel or false, so I gave her the true one instead.

"A man with fewer memories and better aim," I said. "Ishola — I heard the bolt leave the string. Do you understand? I *heard* it, before it flew. The half-oath opened something, and now it doesn't fully close. Last night I could hear the tower-stone cooling. I could hear your heartbeat through twenty feet of dark. The gift isn't waiting politely for me to decide anymore. It's leaning on the door." I raised my ruined hand between us, the watermark catching the grey light. "This isn't a wound. Wounds heal. This is a *lien*. The fire has claimed collateral, and it intends to collect the rest."

"Then we don't pay the rest."

"The rest is already owed. That's what an oath is. I spoke half the words; the whole debt exists now, whether I finish the sentence or not. The only question is whether I pay it into a ditch at the ford or into the last flame on earth." I tried to flex the fingers again. They obeyed, stiffly, and the small patient fire ticked in the bones, and far away in the sealed room of myself, in the grief-room, something shifted — I felt it distinctly, a settling, like a shelf taking weight. Another item being carried to the counter. Another entry pending.

And I thought, with the strange clarity of the half-robbed: *his hands.* I could no longer see my mentor's face, but I suddenly, urgently needed to know if I still had his hands — the memory of watching them correct my grip, the first kindness anyone had shown my nine-year-old self. I reached for it.

Gone. Or — no. Not gone. *Going.* I could feel it going, feel the memory thinning like ice in spring sun, and I gripped it the way you grip anything drowning, uselessly, with love, with no skill at all.

"Mara?" Ishola said. "Your face."

"Nothing." I closed my fist. The watermark disappeared inside it. "Let's walk. Four days to the shrine, and I intend to arrive as myself — or as much of myself as the accounting allows."

We climbed out of the hollow into the grey morning, onto the old dragon-road, whose stones held the warmth of dead fires the way I now held the warmth of dead men: imperfectly, gratefully, and not for long.

We had made perhaps a mile, the tower sinking behind us, when Ishola stopped so abruptly I walked into her shoulder.

On the road ahead, pressed into the soft dust at the edge of the stones: bootprints. Fresh. A woman's stride, long and even, the prints of someone trained to walk quietly walking quickly. And beside them, planted upright in the dust like a signature, a single bolt from a royal crossbow — the same make, I noted with a clerk's hopeless precision, as the one that had missed me at the ford.

Veyra had found our trail. Veyra had stood on this road, not an hour ahead of us.

And she had walked on past without setting an ambush, without raising the alarm, without leaving so much as a marker for her handlers — as if, I thought, watching the light glint off that abandoned bolt, she had stood where we now stood, and looked down the road toward the mountain, and found that she could no longer quite reconcile her orders against her ledger.

Even hunters, it seems, keep accounts.

The question — the question that would follow us all four days east, ticking in my bones alongside the fire — was what hers would total.
