## 20. Veyra's Doubt

I have been a hunter for eleven years, and in eleven years I have learned that the crown's paperwork is always three days ahead of the truth and never once behind it. Orders arrive before the crime is committed. Warrants name men who haven't yet been accused. The bureaucracy of the realm moves like prophecy, which should have told me something long ago about what kind of god it serves.

But I believed. Let's be honest about that, since honesty is apparently the theme of the week I'm having. I believed the way soldiers believe — not with the head but with the boots, one march at a time, until the marching itself becomes the argument. The oath-bound had started the Dragon War. My grandmother had said so, and her mother before her, and every schoolbook bound in royal blue said so in letters tall enough to read from horseback. Swordspeakers spoke words that unmade rivers and turned men to mist, and words like that don't keep company with loyalty. Words like that are a knife you point somewhere.

So when Captain Dossan handed me the campaign file on the purge of Ashenmere — my own commission, sealed with the Lord Historian's mark — I sat down on a stump outside his tent and read it the way I'd read a hundred files before: looking for the parts that confirmed what I already knew.

The file was thorough. That was the first thing wrong with it.

* * *

Here is what I mean by thorough. The report on Ashenmere ran forty pages. It named the village, its population, its guild affiliations, the suspected swordspeaker at its center — a woman called Ishola Adeyemi, though the file spelled her name three different ways, which I noticed and did not notice, the way you can see a crack in a wall for years and call it shadow. It described the raid. It described the fire. It described, in language so clean it could have been written by a man washing his hands, the "regrettable but necessary" loss of civilian life.

And then, appended to the back like an afterthought, it included the evidence. Witness depositions. A scout's map showing oath-sigils burned into the village wellhead. A captured letter, allegedly written by Ishola Adeyemi, pledging the village's swords to the "false memory" faction.

I read the captured letter twice.

The second time was because the first time, something had snagged in me like a fishhook, and I wanted to find out what it was. What it was, was this: the letter was dated the ninth of Harvestmonth. And the scout's map — the one showing the sigils, the one justifying the whole operation — was dated the eleventh.

You cannot pledge allegiance to a rebellion two days before the evidence of your rebellion exists. Unless you are a prophet. And if Ishola Adeyemi had been a prophet, she might have mentioned the fire coming.

I sat on that stump for a long while. Somewhere behind me Dossan's tent flapped in the wind like a thing trying to escape. A crow was working at something dead in the grass with the patience of a tax collector. And I held the file in my hands and felt the first hairline fracture go through eleven years of marching.

Because here's the other thing. Here's the thing I'd been carrying without knowing its weight. I am a swordspeaker.

Not a real one. Not anymore. When I was nine years old I spoke a word over a broken millstone and it sang, actually sang, a note like a struck bell, and my father went white as flour and made me swear on his life never to do it again, and I never did, and he died anyway four winters later of a fever, so much for oaths protecting anyone. But the word stayed in me. It sits behind my teeth like a coin I've never spent. Every year of my service, I have been hunting people who carry the same coin, and the crown has assured me — gently, repeatedly, in the voice you use with a dog that might bite — that I am different. That my gift is small. Dormant. Registered and harmless. That I would know, better than anyone, how dangerous the full-grown version is.

I had accepted this. I want that on the record. I had accepted being the leash that hunts the wolves, and I had even been proud of it, because pride is what they give you instead of answers.

* * *

I did what any good soldier does with a doubt too large to hold alone. I took it to my superior.

Dossan received me in his tent with his boots off, which he only did when he wanted you to feel like a guest, and feeling like a guest in Dossan's presence was a warning sign the way sweet smells near a swamp are a warning sign. He poured wine without asking. He always poured without asking; it made refusal awkward and acceptance complicit, and I think he enjoyed watching which one you chose.

"The Adeyemi file," I said. "The dates don't reconcile."

He didn't look up from pouring. "Which dates?"

"The deposition letter predates the scout's evidence by two days. Either the letter's forged or the map is."

"Mm." He handed me the cup. "Or both were prepared together, by clerks working from the operational schedule rather than the field record. Happens all the time. Paperwork's a river, Veyra. It flows downhill toward whatever conclusion the dam wants."

"That's not paperwork," I said. "That's evidence. In a capital case. Against a woman we burned alive."

"We burned a village," Dossan corrected mildly, "in which she was unfortunately present. There's a difference, legally speaking." He smiled at me with the bottom half of his face. "You've been in the field a long time. Fieldwork makes everything look like conspiracy. You get sun-addled. You start reading dates like omens." He leaned back. "Is there anything else? Any actual problem with the operation?"

"No, Captain."

"Good. Because the Lord Historian has taken personal interest in the Ashenmere matter, and personal interest, in my experience, is best met with personal discretion." He raised his cup to me. "To discretion."

I drank. Of course I drank. Eleven years of marching teaches you to drink.

But walking back through the camp, past the picket lines and the cook fires and the boys sharpening knives they'd never need against the enemy they'd actually meet, I turned his sentence over. *The Lord Historian has taken personal interest.* Not the Lord Marshal. Not the Crown Prosecutor. The historian. The keeper of records. Since when does the man who writes history take personal interest in making it?

Since, said a voice in me that sounded unpleasantly like my father going white as flour, since history became the weapon instead of the account of the weapons.

* * *

I want to be clear about the shape of the next few hours, because I've heard converts describe their revelations as lightning, and mine was nothing like that. Mine was arithmetic. It was sitting in my own tent with a candle and the campaign file and doing sums a child could do, and each sum came out the same way, and each time it came out the same way a little more of the floor gave way beneath me.

Sum the first: the hunt for swordspeakers, the great righteous sweep I'd been running for two years, had begun six weeks *before* the incident that supposedly provoked it. The proclamation cited the Ashenmere conspiracy. The orders for the hunt predated Ashenmere. I checked. Three times. The ink doesn't lie about when it dried; that's the one honest thing about ink.

Sum the second: every village on our route contained exactly one suspected swordspeaker. Not two, not zero. One apiece, distributed with the tidy regularity of a tax assessment. Real conspiracies cluster. Real fear lumps up in some places and misses others entirely. This was a ledger. Someone had drawn up a list, and someone else had dressed the list as intelligence, and I had been the hound sent down the list, kennel to kennel, barking at names.

Sum the third, and this was the one that made me put the candle out because I couldn't bear to see my own face while I thought it: the survivors. We had taken survivors, in the early sweeps, before the protocol changed. They'd all been questioned by Historian's men, not ours. And the ones who remembered too much — who remembered details that contradicted the official account — those ones had been transferred to the Iron House for "extended testimony." None had returned. The ones who came back to us remembered less. Much less. One woman, in Kellin's Ford, had looked at the ruins of her own house and asked me, with genuine puzzlement, whether anyone had ever lived there at all.

I had written in my report: *civilian disorientation consistent with trauma.*

I had written that. With my hand. My registered, dormant, harmless hand.

* * *

Near midnight I did the thing you're not supposed to do. I opened the restricted satchel.

Every hunter carries one. Sealed with the Historian's wax, to be opened only on direct order, containing — we were told — contingency protocols. I'd carried mine for two years the way you carry a stone in your pack that you've stopped noticing. Now I broke the wax with my thumb and found out what the stone was made of.

It wasn't contingency protocols. It was a printing kit. Plates, ink, presses small enough to travel — and stacks of blank documents bearing every seal in the realm. And beneath them, wrapped in oilcloth, the master plates themselves: the templates from which the "evidence" was struck. The oath-sigil marks. The forged correspondence. A standard-form deposition, with blanks where the name of the village went, blanks where the name of the swordspeaker went, blanks where the date went.

A form. The purge evidence was a form. Fill in four fields, run the press, and any hamlet in the realm could be guilty of ancient treason by morning.

I sat there holding a template with a blank space where a woman's life was supposed to go, and I understood — finally, fully, in the place where understanding stops being information and starts being damage — what the hunt was. Not a hunt. An authorship. The crown wasn't finding swordspeakers. It was writing them, village by village, into existence retroactively, building a war that had never happened so that the war that *had* happened — the one where kings betrayed the dragons and their speakers and burned the witnesses — could be quietly replaced. You don't need dragon-fire to rewrite memory, I realized. You just need paper, patience, and enough people willing to carry the paper without reading it.

People like me.

The coin behind my teeth felt suddenly very heavy. Eleven years I'd kept it unspent because they told me spending it made me the monster. And all the while the true monsters had been at desks, pressing monsters out of blank sheets, and calling the press a sword.

* * *

There was a moment — I'll admit it, because a confession that stops short of the shameful part isn't a confession — when I considered simply burning the satchel and riding west until the realm ran out. Desertion. A clean break. Let the historians write whatever they liked about Veyra the vanished; at least the plates would melt.

I even had the satchel over the brazier before I stopped.

Stopped because of the woman in Kellin's Ford. The one who couldn't remember her own house. If I rode west, the presses kept rolling. If I burned one satchel, there were forty more, and the Lord Historian's personal interest would simply acquire new hands. And somewhere ahead of me — I knew this now with the certainty of the converted, which is the most annoying certainty there is — there were people who had figured all of this out before I had, and were running, and were carrying something worth more than plates. A seal. Survivors talked, even diminished survivors; the name Oyelaran had crossed three reports already, always misspelled, always attached to a guildhouse the crown was circling like a cat around a hole.

If the last true record still existed anywhere, it was moving toward the old dragon-road, and the crown was moving to intercept it, and the only person positioned to matter was a cracked believer with a coin behind her teeth and a hunter's warrant in her pocket.

I took the satchel off the brazier. I packed the template and one forged deposition into my coat, against my ribs, where the wax seals pressed cold little circles into my skin like eyes.

Then I wrote my evening report — dutiful, dull, confirming the next village on the list — and signed it, and left it where Dossan would find it in the morning, and bought myself the hours between midnight and dawn.

In those hours I saddled my horse, and rode out past the pickets under a sky the color of a bruise healing, and turned west. Toward the salt flats. Toward the fugitives. Toward whatever was left of the truth, guttering somewhere out there like a flame nobody had tended in a hundred years.

My grandmother would have called it treason. My father, white as flour, would have called it the oath keeping itself at last.

I called it arithmetic. The sums had finally come out right, and I could not un-add them, and God forgive me, I found I didn't want to.

Behind me the camp slept on, dreaming crown-blue dreams. Ahead of me the flats began, pale and endless, and somewhere out on all that whiteness were two people who had every reason to put a crossbow bolt in the first hunter they saw.

I'd worry about that at the ford.
