## 21. The Dragon-Road

The road announced itself before we saw it.

Ishola felt it first — she stopped mid-stride on the salt flats' edge, where the crust gave way to scrub and stone, and said, "Do you hear that?"

"Hear what?" I heard nothing. I had been hearing nothing for three days, which was its own kind of music: wind, our footsteps, the small wet sounds of Ishola's bandages being changed at dusk. Silence is a luxury I no longer trust.

"The road," she said. "It's humming."

And then I felt it too — not a sound exactly, but a pressure behind the sternum, like standing near a struck bell after the ringing has faded. The old books in the guildhouse called it *resonance*, the memory of stone that has carried weight. Every road remembers its travelers, the archivists wrote. Most roads forget quickly. This one had not forgotten in a thousand years.

We crested the last rise at dawn, and there it was: the dragon-road, running north to south across the world's spine like a scar that someone had decided to be proud of. It was not paved so much as *grown* — plates of dark stone the size of doors, fitted without mortar, each one faintly warm underfoot even in the cold mountain morning. And along its length, at intervals of perhaps a hundred paces, stood pillars. Not carved. *Grown* too, I think, though I have no better word for it — columns of the same dark stone rising twenty feet, their surfaces rippled like cooled wax, like something enormous had once curled around them and left its shape behind.

Scale-stone. Everyone knows the word; nobody believes it until they stand on it.

"It's real," Ishola whispered. She had been whispering for three days, ever since the ford, ever since I turned a river into warm mist and paid for it with a piece of my mentor's face that I can no longer summon. "Mara. It's *real*. They told us it was a story."

"They tell us a lot of things are stories," I said. My voice came out rougher than I intended. Wryness is armor, and mine had developed a crack somewhere around the salt flats, when I woke in the night and could not remember whether Master Ekon's eyes had been brown or black, only that he had laughed at me once for mislabeling a jar of pickled newts, and that the laugh had sounded like — like —

Like nothing. Like a laugh-shaped hole.

"Your hand," Ishola said.

I looked down. My sword-hand — the one that had burned when I spoke the half-oath, the skin seamed with pale scarring from wrist to knuckle — was *warm*. Not burning. Warm, the way a hearthstone stays warm after the fire dies. The same warmth as the road beneath my boots.

"It knows me," I said, before I could stop myself. Then, because honesty between fugitives is cheaper than lies and lasts longer: "Or it remembers what I'm supposed to be."

* * *

We walked the dragon-road for two days.

I should describe it properly, because if this ledger — the one I keep now in my head, since paper burns and informants read — survives me, someone will want to know what it was like. The road ran straight as a plumb line through country that had no business permitting straightness: over ridges, through valleys, across rivers on bridges of the same scale-stone that arched without support, elegant as a heron's neck. No crown engineer could build it. No crown engineer could even explain it, which is why the histories call the dragon-road a natural formation, a geological curiosity, a trick of volcanic basalt.

A trick of volcanic basalt that hums when you walk on it. That warms itself against winter. That shows you things, sometimes, out of the corner of your eye — I saw them twice, shapes moving at the road's far edges, vast and slow, present the way a smell is present in an empty room. Ishola saw them too. We agreed not to discuss it, which is the traditional pilgrim's covenant, I suppose: awe shared, terror divided.

On the second day we passed the first village.

Or rather, the place where a village had been. The dragon-road does not detour around human habitation; it goes through it, and once, clearly, towns had flourished along its length like beads on a wire. This one was stone foundations and chimney stubs, a well with its bucket-rope still knotted, a shrine niche by the roadside holding a figure whose face had weathered to a smooth blank. But someone had been here recently. Fresh flowers — mountain crocus, out of season, carried up from the lowlands — lay in the niche. Someone remembered this place enough to bring it flowers.

"Somebody still comes," Ishola said.

"Somebody who remembers what?" I asked, and neither of us answered, because we both knew the answer might be *nothing*, might be habit worn smooth as the shrine's face, devotion outliving its object the way a dog waits at a gate for an owner ten years dead.

But that night, sheltering in the shell of a roadside waystation — roofless, but the scale-stone walls held the day's warmth like a banked oven — Ishola took out the seal.

She had carried it against her skin since the guildhouse, wrapped in linen, next to the worst of her burns. She unwrapped it now in the waystation's dimness, and it did what it always did: it made the air go thick and attentive, the way a room goes when a dangerous man enters it.

Dragon-scale seal. The size of my palm, iridescent black-green, and warm — warmer than the road, warmer than my hand, warm as a living thing. On its face, the sigil of the oath-bound: a sword crossed with a tongue of flame, the flame rendered not as decoration but as script, tiny characters flowing along the fire's outline. Characters only swordspeakers can read. Characters I could read, God help me, and which said, in the old conjugation:

*What is spoken cannot be unspoken. What is written cannot be trusted alone.*

"I've been thinking," Ishola said, "about what happens when we get there."

"There being?"

"The fire." She turned the seal in her fingers. In its light — it gave off a faint green-gold luminescence, like deep water holding sun — her burn-scarred face looked carved, monumental, a saint on a cathedral nobody built yet. "The keeper. Whatever's left of it. Do you know what you're going to say?"

"I know what I swore never to say."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only answer I have." I pulled my blanket tighter. The waystation was warm but the warmth had a strange quality, almost maternal, and it made me want to confess things, which I resisted on principle. "Ishola. When I spoke the half-oath at the ford, it cost me—" I stopped. Started again. "There's a price. A full oath, spoken whole, with the seal and whatever else the fire requires — I don't know what that costs. I don't know if there's anything left of me on the other side of it."

She was quiet a while. The road hummed beneath us, patient as breathing.

"You know what they did to us," she said finally. "In Ashfall Vale. You've seen it in the seal. You've heard me say the names every night — forty-one names, Mara, I say them every night so they stay somewhere other than my mouth. If the crown finishes what it started, those names won't just be dead. They'll be *unhappened*. Some clerk in the capital will write a page saying Ashfall Vale never existed, and the page will be true, because truth is just what everyone agrees happened, and they'll make everyone agree." She looked at me. Her eyes were the only part of her face the fire hadn't touched, and they were very bright. "You're worried about what it costs *you*. I buried what it cost *me*. I'll pay again. The question is whether you will."

"That's not fair."

"No," she agreed. "It isn't. Nothing about this is fair. Fairness is for people with ledgers that balance." She rewrapped the seal, and the light went out of the waystation, and we slept in the dark on a road that remembered dragons, and I dreamed of Master Ekon teaching me the first conjugation, his mouth forming words I could no longer quite see.

* * *

The shrine appeared on the third morning the way mountains appear: all at once, impossibly, as if it had been waiting for us to be worthy of the view.

The dragon-road climbed through a pass between two peaks, and at the pass's summit the road widened into a plaza — a hundred paces across, ringed with the tallest pillars we'd seen yet, their wax-rippled surfaces catching the early sun. And at the plaza's center, built into the living rock of the peak itself, stood the shrine.

It was smaller than I expected. That's the thing nobody tells you about the sacred: it's rarely large. The shrine was a single chamber, maybe thirty feet square, its walls of the same dark scale-stone, its doorway a high narrow arch with no door — open to the wind for a thousand years, and yet when we stepped inside, the wind stopped at the threshold like a servant at a great house.

Inside, the air smelled of hot iron and, absurdly, of my grandmother's kitchen — some spice I couldn't name, something roasted and resinous. Memory does that, I've learned. It dresses itself in domesticity.

And there, in a basin of black stone at the chamber's heart, burned the last dragon-fire.

I want to describe it honestly, which means describing how unimpressive it first seemed. A flame the size of a cupped hand. Guttering. Pale gold shot through with that deep green, flickering like a candle in a drafty church, and I thought — I am ashamed to record this — *that's it? That's the fire that burned the sky? That's what the crown is hunting children through the provinces for?*

Then it guttered low, nearly out, a blue thread of flame — and the chamber *noticed* us.

There's no other word. The shadows leaned. The warmth in my sword-hand flared from hearth-warm to fever-hot, and the scars lit faintly, tracing the half-oath's path through my flesh like veins of ember. Beside me, Ishola gasped, and the seal at her breast glowed through her clothing, and for one vertiginous moment I felt the chamber the way the chamber presumably felt us: two small warm bodies carrying an old debt up a long road.

The flame steadied. Grew. Not large — it never grew larger than two cupped hands — but *denser*, somehow, its light acquiring weight, throwing our shadows sharp and black against walls that suddenly seemed less like architecture and more like the inside of something's ribs.

"We're here," Ishola said. Her voice shook. "Mara. We're actually here."

"Yes."

"What do we do?"

What do we do. The question of my entire life, asked at last in a room that might actually contain the answer. I approached the basin slowly, the way you approach a sleeping animal or a grieving person, and looked down into the flame.

And the flame looked back.

Not with eyes. With *recognition*. It read me the way I read ledgers — entry by entry, line by line, the remembering sword I'd catalogued, the page I'd burned, the river I'd misted, the name I'd already lost. I felt it find the gaps in me, the Ekon-shaped hole, the surname I'd traded at the Iron House — wait. No. That comes later. I'm confusing my own chronology; grief does that, and so does fire. Let me set it straight: at the shrine's threshold I had lost only my mentor's face, and my hands were scarred, and my oath was still folded inside me like a letter never sent.

The flame read all of it. And then it did the most frightening thing it could have done.

It waited.

"You're supposed to speak," I told it. My voice echoed strangely in the rib-chamber. "Aren't you? You're the last dragon-fire. You're supposed to — I don't know. Judge. Instruct. Something dramatic."

The flame flickered. Patient. Terrible in its patience, the way a creditor is terrible.

From the shadowed corner of the chamber, where I would swear on any ledger you name that no one had been standing a moment before, a voice said:

"It doesn't speak first. It never speaks first. That was the first lesson, and it seems the world has forgotten even that."

I spun, hand going to a sword I'd sold weeks ago, and found myself facing an old woman seated on a bench of scale-stone that I now realized had been there all along, the way the truly important things in a room hide in plain sight. She was small, brown as walnut, wrapped in layers of undyed wool, with eyes the exact green-gold of the flame — and she looked at me with an expression I recognized from mirrors: the careful invisibility of someone who has survived by being overlooked.

"You're the keeper," Ishola breathed.

"I'm the keeper," the old woman agreed. "Such as the position amounts to, these days. Staff of one. No pension." She rose, joints cracking like green wood, and hobbled toward the basin, and the flame leaned toward her the way a plant leans toward sun. "Three hundred years I've kept it. My mother kept it before me, and hers before her, back to the Burning, back to when there were enough fires that keeping one was a chore instead of a vocation." She looked up at me, and her gaze went directly to my scarred hand. "And in three hundred years, no one has come up the dragon-road. Pilgrims stopped coming when the histories stopped mentioning us. So." She spread her hands. "You'll forgive me if I ask the obvious question, swordspeaker. Why are you here?"

"To light it," Ishola said, stepping forward, the seal bare in her burned hands now, held out like an offering. "To wake it. To burn the lie out of—"

"No," the keeper said.

The word fell into the chamber like a stone into a well. The flame guttered. Ishola froze, the seal extended, her face — her ruined, magnificent face — going carefully blank, the blankness of a woman who has been refused too many times to show the wound.

"No?" I said.

"No." The keeper's voice was gentle, which made it worse. "Child, the fire isn't a lamp. You don't light it. It lights *you*, or nothing happens at all." She turned those green-gold eyes on me. "It answers oaths. Full oaths. Spoken whole, by an oath-bound tongue, with a true ledger open before it — the speaker's own ledger, sworn and witnessed. That is the rite. That is the only rite. There is no shortcut, no substitute, no clever archivist's workaround. Believe me. I've had three hundred years to look for one."

"A true ledger," I repeated. My mouth had gone dry. "Sworn and witnessed. My own."

"Yours," the keeper said. "Open. Complete." She smiled then, and it was the saddest smile I have ever seen on a living face, because it held the particular pity of someone who already knew what I did not yet know — what I would learn three chapters from now, though I didn't know it then, standing in the warm ribs of the world with my gift intact and my history whole.

"And tell me, swordspeaker," she said softly, as the flame guttered low between us, patient as a creditor, terrible as a truth deferred, "when you burned your guildhouse records and fled — did you happen to keep *everything*?"
