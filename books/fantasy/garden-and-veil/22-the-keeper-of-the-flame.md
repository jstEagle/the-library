## 22. The Keeper of the Flame

I have kept this shrine for one hundred and forty years, and in all that time the fire has spoken to me exactly twice. Once to complain about the damp. Once, more recently, to tell me that someone was coming up the dragon-road who smelled of oaths half-swallowed and ledgers badly balanced.

The fire is a gossip. It always has been.

My name is Tenu. I was named for a river that no longer exists, which I have always felt was an appropriate beginning for a man whose entire life consists of tending something that everyone else insists is finished. The crown's maps mark this mountain as barren. The crown's histories mark the dragons as traitors. The crown's historians, when they bother to climb this far — they rarely do; the road eats boots and certainty in equal measure — mark me as a hermit, a madman, or a myth.

I am none of those things. I am a custodian with bad knees and a very good memory, which in this age is the most dangerous combination a person can possess.

* * *

They arrived at dusk on the third day of the month the farmers call Small Rain, though it had not rained here in eleven years. I watched them come up the switchbacks from my window: two figures, one tall and moving like a man carrying a sack of stones he refuses to set down, one slighter and moving like a woman who has already died once and found it inconvenient.

Behind them, far below at the mouth of the valley, dust hung in the air where dust had no business hanging. Riders. Not many. Enough.

"Fire," I said to the hearth, "you could have mentioned the escort."

The flame guttered in what I have chosen to interpret as apology. It is perhaps four feet high and burns without fuel, which makes it either a miracle or a very old habit that forgot how to stop. Its color is wrong for ordinary fire — gold shot through with green, like sunlight passing through bottle glass — and its heat is strange as well. It does not warm the room so much as it warms *remembering*. Sit by it long enough and you will recall your mother's voice with perfect fidelity, including the things she said that you had spent decades pretending not to hear.

This is why I live alone.

I put water on to boil and opened the door before they knocked, because a keeper who waits to be knocked for is a keeper who has forgotten his function. Hospitality is the first duty. Instruction is the second. Grief, unfortunately, is the third, and there is no order of operations that lets you skip ahead.

"You're the keeper," said the tall one. He had a swordspeaker's hands — burn-scarred across the knuckles, the right hand worse than the left, recent damage, self-inflicted by gift rather than accident. His eyes went past me into the shrine and fixed on the fire with an expression I recognized because I see it in every mirror: hunger and terror wearing each other's clothes.

"I'm the keeper," I agreed. "You're the oath-bound. And she—" I looked at the woman, whose skin carried the topography of old burning, "—is the reason he hasn't turned around yet."

"Mara Oyelaran," he said. "This is Ishola Adeyemi. We were told—"

"You were told the fire answers oaths. You were told correctly, and you were told incompletely, and whoever told you should be ashamed, unless it was the seal, in which case it should be forgiven, since seals are honest the way knives are honest." I stepped aside. "Come in. Take off your boots. The floor remembers every rude thing done to it and I would prefer it remember nothing new about either of you."

* * *

They sat. I poured tea — bitterroot and honey, the traditional welcome of this place, designed to shock the tongue awake after the long climb. Ishola drank hers like a woman who understood ceremony. Mara held his cup and did not drink, which told me more about him than anything he might have said.

"The fire," he began.

"Is listening," I said. "It has been listening since before your guildhouse was built, since before your guild existed, since before the first fool swore the first oath over a dying dragon's heartblood. It is very patient and very tired, and if you say something stupid in front of it, it will remember that too."

His jaw tightened. Good. A man who bristles at rudeness still has fight in him. A man who accepts it meekly is already halfway to ash.

"Then let me not be stupid," he said. "How does it work? The oath. What does it need?"

I set down my own cup. This was the part I had rehearsed for a century and never once performed, because no one had ever climbed this road ready to hear the answer. Pilgrims came wanting miracles. Scholars came wanting footnotes. Soldiers came wanting weapons. None of them came wanting *terms*, and the terms are the whole of it.

"It needs three things," I said. "First, a full oath — not the fragments your kind has been whispering for generations to light candles and warm rivers and frighten tax collectors. The whole architecture of it, beginning to end, every clause, every name. Do you know the full oath?"

He was quiet long enough that the silence itself answered.

"I know pieces," he said finally. "My mentor taught me fragments. She said the rest was lost."

"The rest was *buried*," I corrected. "There is a difference, and the difference matters. Lost things cannot be recovered. Buried things can be dug up, if you are willing to break your nails on the truth. But we will return to that. Second requirement: a true ledger."

"A ledger," Ishola repeated. Her voice was flat with disbelief. "A book of accounts."

"A book of *witness*," I said. "Yes. You find it absurd. Everyone finds it absurd until they understand what the fire actually is." I leaned toward the hearth. The flame leaned back, which never stops being unsettling, even after a hundred and forty years. "Your historians will tell you the dragons were beasts. Your older historians, the ones the crown burned, would have told you the dragons were allies. Both are lies of scale. The dragons were *witnesses*. They lived eight hundred years. They saw everything — every treaty signed, every promise made, every promise broken. When the kings of the old alliance turned on them, they were not destroying dangerous animals. They were destroying the memory of the world. Dragon-fire does not merely burn wood and flesh. It burns falsehood out of living memory. That is why it had to be extinguished. Not because it was powerful. Because it was *accurate*."

Mara's scarred hand had gone white around his teacup. "And the oath?"

"The oath is the mechanism by which a mortal lends their voice to that accuracy. But a voice alone is wind. The ledger is the proof. The fire will not act on assertion. It acts on *evidence*. Speak the full oath holding nothing, and nothing happens but a sore throat. Speak it holding a ledger that contains true testimony — names, dates, accounts, witnessed and bound — and the fire reads it the way a magistrate reads a confession, and then it judges, and then it burns accordingly." I looked at him steadily. "It burned the false histories once before, young man. Three hundred years ago, the last time it was fed properly. Your crown's founding myth — the noble war of liberation? That story exists precisely because the fire was put out afterward and the record rewritten while the embers cooled. Feed it again, and every mind in range of its light will remember what actually happened. Every soldier. Every clerk. Every king."

"That's the point," Ishola said quietly. "That's exactly the point."

"It is also the price," I said, "which brings me to the third requirement, and it is the one that breaks people." I turned to Mara. "The ledger must be *true*, and it must be *yours*. Not testimony gathered secondhand. Not a book of other people's grief, however righteous. The fire reads the speaker through the ledger. If the book contains evasions, omissions, convenient forgettings — yours — the oath fails, or worse, it succeeds partially, and you have seen what partial oaths cost."

His hand moved involuntarily to his scarred fingers. Ah. There it was. He had already paid the tuition for the introductory lesson.

"How much of it fails?" he asked. His voice was carefully level, the levelness of a man standing on ice and asking how thick it is.

"All of it, if the book is false enough. Some of it, if the book is mostly true. And always — always — the parts of yourself you have hidden inside the gaps." I let that sit. "The fire is not cruel. It is precise. Precision feels like cruelty to anyone with something to hide."

* * *

Ishola asked the practical questions while Mara sat with his tea going cold — where the ledger must be bound, in what ink, witnessed by whom. Good questions. Survivor's questions. She had been erased once, professionally and thoroughly, and she understood better than he did that records are not paperwork. Records are resurrection.

But I watched him, and I did not like what I saw in his face, and I have been reading faces longer than most families have existed.

"There is something else," I said, when the practicalities were exhausted. "Something I am obligated to tell you, though I confess I hope it changes nothing, because if it changes nothing you may yet succeed, and if it changes everything you may yet turn back, and I genuinely do not know which outcome I want."

"Tell us anyway," Ishola said.

"The fire spoke to me nine days ago. As I said — it speaks twice a century, if I'm lucky. It said: *the speaker comes, and the speaker comes empty.*" I folded my hands. "Empty, Mara Oyelaran. Not incomplete. Empty. Whatever ledger you carry — and I can see from the way your shoulders changed just now that you carry one — the fire has already read it from a hundred miles away, the way it reads everything, and it has judged it insufficient. Not false. Insufficient. There is a page missing. A page that belongs in it. A page that was true, and witnessed, and binding, and is now smoke."

I saw him go through it — the rapid inventory, the desperate audit of everything he had ever written and burned. I gave him the time. A keeper learns that some truths need a corridor to walk down before they can enter a room.

"The page I burned," he said at last, very quietly. "In the guildhouse. My own history. My own guild's record. I burned it to protect them."

"Yes."

"And the fire needs it."

"The fire needs *truth*, and that page was true, and you destroyed it, and unlike the crown's historians you destroyed it for love, which the fire will weigh in your favor, but weighing is not the same as waiving." I rose, knees cracking like green branches, and went to the window. Down in the valley dark, the riders' fires had begun to bloom — small orange points, ordinary fire, the dishonest kind. "They will reach the shrine in two days. Perhaps three, if their commander is cautious, and crown commanders are always cautious, because caution is what gets promoted."

"So we've brought the army its target," Mara said bitterly. "Led the procession ourselves."

"You've brought the fire its last chance," I corrected. "Those are different processions, however much they travel the same road." I turned back to him. "Here is what I know, oath-bound, and I will say it plainly because plain speech is the last luxury of old men. The full oath can be reconstructed — buried, not lost, as I told you. The ledger can be completed — testimony is a living thing; it grows wherever the erased gather, and word has reached even this mountain that the erased are gathering. What cannot be manufactured, improvised, or replaced is the missing page. Only you know what it contained. Only you can restore it — from memory, from witness, from whatever the fire left in you when it took your payment at the ford."

"And if I can't?" he asked. "If it's gone? If I burned it so thoroughly that not even I remember what was written?"

I looked at the hearth. The flame had gone very still and very bright, the way it does when it is paying attention, and I thought — not for the first time, but with new weight — that it was not looking at him at all.

It was looking at the space behind his eyes, where a page used to be.

"Then," I said, "we will discover together whether the fire accepts a debt repaid in something other than paper. But I warn you, swordspeaker: it has never once accepted an apology."

Outside, the wind came down off the peak carrying the smell of the riders' cookfires, and beneath it — faint, unmistakable, impossible — the smell of dragon-fire waking.

The flame in my hearth stood up straight, all four feet of it, and for the third time in a hundred and forty years, it spoke.

It said one word.

It said *soon*.
