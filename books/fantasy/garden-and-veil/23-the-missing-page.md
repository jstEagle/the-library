## 23. The Missing Page

The Keeper of the Flame had a way of looking at you that made you feel like an entry in a ledger she'd already audited and found wanting.

"Say it again," she said. "Slowly. The part where you burned it."

I said it again. Slowly. It did not improve with repetition.

We sat in the antechamber of the mountain shrine, on stone benches worn smooth by centuries of pilgrims who had presumably come here with better news than mine. Through the archway I could see the last dragon-fire guttering in its iron cradle — a flame no bigger than a candle's worth of stubbornness, blue at the heart, gold at the edges, breathing in a rhythm that had nothing to do with air. Ishola stood by the arch with her arms crossed, ash-skinned and unimpressed, which was her resting state and, increasingly, mine.

"So let me make sure I have this right," the Keeper said. She was small, ancient, and dry as a page left too long in the sun, with eyes the color of banked coals. "The fire answers only a full oath, spoken aloud, witnessed by a true ledger."

"Yes."

"And a true ledger must contain every name the oath binds together. Every hand that fed the guildhouse. Every debt forgiven. Every record kept."

"That's what you said, yes."

"And your guild's ledger — the true one, the original, the one written in the founder's hand —"

"I burned it," I said. "Page by page. In a brazier. On the night I fled the guildhouse. Fourteen chapters ago, if we're keeping count, and I am, because apparently the universe keeps count too and has decided to invoice me for it."

The Keeper did not laugh. Keepers of ancient flames, I was learning, are not a profession that rewards wit.

Ishola laughed, though. One short bark, like a door slamming. "You burned your own history."

"I burned it so the informant couldn't use it." I heard my voice go flat and defensive, the tone of a man reading his own indictment. "The second ledger — the one with the purges, the erased villages, the names the crown wanted gone — that was hidden inside the guild ledger. Interleaved. My handwriting between Sefu's entries like smuggled goods between bolts of cloth. If Veyra or the informant had found it, every name in it would have died twice. Once in the flesh, once in the record. So I burned the whole thing. Guild history and purge testimony alike. A controlled burn, I told myself. Sacrifice of my own past to protect everyone else's future."

"And now," the Keeper said, "your own past is precisely what the fire requires."

There it was. Laid out on the bench between us like a corpse at an inquest.

I looked at the flame through the archway. It guttered. Somewhere in its small blue heart, I could have sworn it guttered *expectantly*, the way a dog looks up when it hears its name.

"How much does it need?" I asked. "Exactly. Be precise. I'm an accountant; precision is the one prayer I know."

The Keeper rose, joints crackling like green wood, and beckoned us toward the fire. "Come. I'll show you what a true ledger looks like when it's whole."

* * *

The shrine's vault lay behind the flame chamber, down a stair cut into the living rock, and it was less a library than a wound in the world that had learned to organize itself.

Shelves of slate and dragon-bone climbed into darkness. And on them, ledgers — hundreds of them, thousands, each one humming faintly with the particular resonance of a thing that remembers being believed in. The Keeper ran her hand along a spine as we passed, and the ledger shivered like a sleeping animal acknowledging a familiar scent.

"The first oath-bound kept their records here," she said. "Before the kings. Before the betrayal. Before anyone thought to make a weapon out of forgetting." She drew down a volume and opened it across her palms. The pages were not paper. They were something thinner and more alive, and the writing on them moved — slowly, the way clouds move, so that you only noticed the change when you looked away and back. "This is what a true ledger is. Not ink on vellum. Ink on vellum is a *copy* of a memory. This is the memory itself, given a spine."

Ishola leaned close. "And the fire reads it?"

"The fire doesn't read it. The fire *is* it. Dragon-fire is memory that chose to burn instead of fade. When an oath-speaker speaks before a true ledger, the fire checks the account." Her coal-dark eyes found mine. "It balances the books, Master Oyelaran. Every name spoken in the oath must appear in the ledger. Every name in the ledger must be spoken. If the accounts do not balance, the flame goes out entirely — and this time, it will not relight. There is no second ember. What gutters here tonight is the last of it."

"Wonderful," I said. "No pressure."

"You asked for precision."

"I'm regretting it already. So: my guild ledger. The original. If I had it here — the whole thing, unburned — the oath would bind to it?"

"To it, and through it. Your guildhouse stood on the old dragon-road, boy. You didn't know that?" She watched my face do whatever it did. "No. Of course you didn't. Sefu never told you. The Guild of Impossible Things was founded by an oath-bound swordspeaker three hundred years ago, on the exact spot where the last dragons made their stand. Your guildhouse isn't near the fire's road. It *is* the road. Its ledger is a limb of this flame. That's why the crown wants the seal, the ledgers, the survivors — they're not hunting memories. They're hunting the fire's roots, one root at a time, pulling them up and burning them in their historians' kilns."

I sat down on the stairs. My legs made the decision without consulting me.

Three hundred years. I had dusted those shelves. I had catalogued a sword that remembered its kills and a mirror that showed you the day you were kindest and a jar of last words, all of it filed under *Miscellaneous, Potentially Dangerous, Do Not Sell Without Sefu's Signature*. I had spent six years believing I was hiding in a house of oddities, and the whole time I'd been sleeping in the vestibule of a cathedral.

"Sefu knew," I said.

"Sefu knew," the Keeper agreed.

"He forbade me to use the gift. He hid the guild's founding. He negotiated with the crown behind our backs. And he never once — not once — told me that the dusty inventory I kept was the spinal column of the last free memory in the world."

"Men keep secrets to protect what they love," Ishola said quietly. "And then the secrets eat what they love from the inside, and all that's left is the secret, wearing the loved thing's clothes." She touched the seal at her throat — the dragon-scale, warm even now, patient even now. "I've seen it done to villages. Turns out it works on guilds too."

I wanted to argue. I wanted to defend him, because gratitude is a debt and debts are my native language. But the arithmetic wouldn't come out. Six years of shelter against three hundred years of concealment, and the shelter had been priced in exactly the coin that mattered.

"The page," I said. "The burned pages. Can the fire work with — fragments? I remember some of it. The founding entry, bits of it. Sefu's early years. My own induction, he wrote it out longhand, I copied it once for practice —"

"Memory is not a ledger," the Keeper said, not unkindly, which was somehow worse. "Memory is a rumor about a ledger. The fire will smell the difference. No, Master Oyelaran. The oath requires the true text. The founder's hand. The guild's whole account, from first line to last." She paused, and I watched her decide whether to say the next thing, and then say it anyway, because Keepers are constitutionally incapable of mercy when accuracy is available. "Including the pages you burned."

The stairwell was very cold. Or I was.

"All of them?"

"All of them. The ledger must be whole. A missing page is not an absence to the fire — it is a *lie*. A hole shaped like a truth. The flame cannot balance an account with holes in it. It would rather die."

"Then it will die," I said, "because I burned them. With my own hands. I fed them to a brazier page by page while the informant's spies walked the street outside, and I remember every single page going in, because I am cursed with a gift for memory in a story where the moral is apparently that memory costs too much." I heard my voice climb and did nothing to stop it. "Do you understand what I destroyed? Not just the guild's history. The purges. Ishola's village. The names of the erased — I burned those too, to hide them, and hiding them was *erasing them again*, and I knew it, I knew it while I did it, and I did it anyway because the alternative was letting the crown take them —"

"Mara." Ishola's hand on my shoulder. Ash-warm. "Breathe. You're doing the thing where you audit yourself at three in the morning."

"It's always three in the morning somewhere in my head."

"I know. I've shared a campfire with you for two weeks." She crouched to my eye level. "Listen to me. You burned those pages to keep names alive. That was the right arithmetic then. It may still be the right arithmetic. Ledgers get rebuilt. That's the entire point of ledgers."

"Not from ash," the Keeper said. "Not the true text. The fire doesn't accept reconstructions. It accepts *witnesses* — but the witness must hold the true text whole, in mind or in hand, and speak it without error while the oath is spoken. A reconstruction with gaps is worse than nothing. It's a forgery wearing a dead man's coat."

I put my head in my hands.

Somewhere above us, the flame guttered. I swear — I swear on every ledger I have ever balanced — it sounded like a sigh.

* * *

We climbed back up into the firelight, and I did what I always do when the world ends, which is make lists.

Item: the guild ledger, burned, fourteen chapters ago, irrecoverable. Except.

Except.

I closed my eyes and went back to that night. The brazier. The informant's spies on the street, their lanterns swinging like slow pendulums. Sefu's forged ledgers stacked on his desk, beautiful lies in a tidy hand. And the true ledger open under my fingers, and me tearing the pages out one at a time, and —

and reading each one before I burned it.

Because I couldn't help it. Because I was raised by a guild of record-keepers and a mentor who used to say *you don't destroy a document until you've read it twice, boy, destruction is also a form of citation.* Because some stubborn clerk-ghost in my chest refused to let a single page pass into flame unread.

I had read every page. Twice, some of them. The founding entry, in Adaeze's hand — the founder's hand, the oath-bound founder, the woman whose spine of a building I'd been dusting. The year of the first hidden swordspeaker. The year of the flood. The year Sefu took his oath as Guildmaster, and the terms of it, which I now understood far better than I wished to. My own induction, witnessed and countersigned. Sixty years of impossible things, faithfully recorded, and I had held every page of it in my hands and read it into myself before I gave it to the fire.

"I might have it," I said.

They both turned.

"In here." I tapped my temple. "Not perfectly. Memory is a rumor about a ledger, you said, and you're right, and half of these pages I read once, at night, by bad light, while terrified. But the founding entry I copied out as practice, years ago — I've read it dozens of times since. My induction page I know by heart because it embarrassed me. Some of Sefu's early entries I re-copied during a slow winter. It won't be whole. It'll be full of holes. Gaps shaped like truths." I laughed, and it came out wrong. "A forgery wearing a dead man's coat. That's what I am."

"No," the Keeper said slowly. Her coal eyes had changed — brightened, the way coals brighten when breathed on. "What you are is a *copyist*. And there is one kind of copy the fire accepts, boy, one kind, and it is rare enough that I have seen it twice in three hundred years." She stepped closer. "A copy made by someone the ledger itself was written *about*. The founder's hand recorded the guild. But the guild was also recorded in the bodies of everyone it sheltered. If you speak the pages you carry — not recite, *speak*, with the oath in your mouth and the scale-seal as witness — the fire can weigh your memory against the flame's own root-memory. Where they agree, the page stands. Where they disagree, the page burns away and takes a piece of you with it."

Ishola went very still. "Define 'a piece.'"

"Memories," the Keeper said. "The ones bound to the disputed lines. The fire takes its payment where the account is thin." She looked at me with something that in a younger face would have been pity. "You've already paid once, haven't you? Half an oath at the ford. Your sword-hand. Your mentor's face, or pieces of it. I can see the scarring from here. It looks like a hesitation whenever you reach for him."

My hand had drifted to my sword-hilt without my noticing. The burn scars there had faded to pale lace, but they ached in the cold, and when I reached for Olu — my mentor, my Olu — I found him mostly intact but wrong at the edges, like a portrait hung slightly crooked. His voice was perfect. His hands were perfect. His *face* was a hallway with a door at the end that I had stopped opening because opening it cost more than I had.

"Yes," I said. "I've paid."

"Then you know the exchange rate."

"I know the exchange rate," I said. "I also know I don't have much left in the account. My surname went at the Iron House. Pieces of Olu at the ford. Whatever the full oath takes —" I looked at the flame, small and stubborn and waiting. "Whatever it takes, it takes everything. You said so. The gift, whole. And the gift is where I keep myself."

The Keeper said nothing, because there was nothing accurate to say.

Ishola sat down beside me on the bench. For a while neither of us spoke, and the fire breathed its alien rhythm, and the mountain held its silence around us like a cupped hand.

"When I was in the Iron House," she said finally, "they asked me where the seal was. I recited names instead. Four hundred and eleven names, over nine days. By the end I wasn't doing it to defy them. I was doing it because the names were the only thing in the cell that was real, including me." She turned the dragon-scale over in her fingers. "You're going to stand up there and speak pages you barely remember, and the fire is going to take whatever it wants, and you're asking yourself whether what's left of you will be enough to be a person afterward. I know, because I did the same arithmetic in that cell. And here's what I concluded, accountant: a person is not an inventory. You don't lose yourself by losing items. You lose yourself by refusing the count."

"That's very moving," I said, "and completely useless for balancing purposes."

"I learned it from a swordspeaker," she said. "Terrible with numbers. Good with fires."

Despite everything — despite the cold stairwell and the crooked portrait of my mentor and the hole in the world shaped like sixty years of guild history — I laughed. It hurt, the way laughter does when it has to climb over something heavy to get out.

* * *

I sat up most of that night in the antechamber with a blank book and a stylus, doing what I could not stop doing: reconstructing. The founding entry, from my practice copies — good, nearly word-perfect, I'd wager. My induction — verbatim, embarrassingly verbatim. Then the long middle years, coming back in shards. The flood year, mostly whole. A dispute over a haunted harpsichord, vividly present for no reason I could determine. Sefu's oath as Guildmaster — the terms of it, which I read three times and then set down my stylus and stared at the wall for a long while, because the terms included a clause I had never understood until now:

*...to keep the house standing upon the road, though the road forget itself, until such time as the last speaker speaks.*

He'd known. All those years of forbidding me, protecting me, hiding me — and underneath it, in the founding law he'd sworn himself to, a standing instruction to wait for exactly the thing he'd spent my whole tenure preventing.

Or trying to prevent. Or terrified of. Perhaps all three. Sefu was always capable of holding three contradictory positions at once; it was the only magic he ever practiced.

Toward dawn, footsteps on the stair — not the Keeper's shuffle, not Ishola's deliberate tread. Someone climbing fast and badly, stumbling, catching themselves on the walls.

I was on my feet with my hand on my hilt before my mind caught up.

A figure came through the archway into the firelight: a young man, guildhouse livery torn to ribbons, one eye swollen shut, blood dried black down the side of his face. I knew him. Teslim. Sefu's youngest clerk. The one who watered the memory-moss and complained about it constantly.

He saw me and nearly collapsed with relief, and then straightened, remembering why he'd come, and pulled something from inside his ruined coat wrapped in oilcloth, and held it out with both hands the way you hand someone a live coal or a newborn.

"From the Guildmaster," he gasped. "He said — he said you'd need it, and he said—" Teslim coughed, wet and bad. "He said to tell you he was sorry he taught you to count. And that the ledger — the true one — he kept the ashes. All of them. Every page you burned. He sifted them out of the brazier and kept them, ordered, in a box, for two years, hoping —"

My hands had gone numb around the oilcloth package. Inside, I could feel the shape of a box, and within the box, the soft whisper of things that had been pages.

"— hoping you'd find a fire that could read ashes," Teslim finished, and swayed, and I caught him, and above us the last dragon-fire flared — once, sharply, like a struck bell — and settled, and waited.
