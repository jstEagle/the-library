## 24. Sefu's Confession

There is a particular kind of silence that lives in an empty guildhouse, and I had spent sixty years learning to fill it with other people's noise. Now the noise was gone and the silence had teeth.

I stood in the great hall of the Guild of Impossible Things on the morning after I had sold a woman to the crown, and I counted the things that were wrong with the room. The ledger stand by the east window: empty. The shelf where the singing compass used to hum itself hoarse every dusk: bare wood, pale rectangle of un-faded paint where it had leaned for thirty years. The memory-vault door, which should have been locked, was open, and inside it the shelves gaped like pulled teeth. The watch had taken what they wanted. What they hadn't taken, my own signature had surrendered — because I had signed a bargain in ink that said the guild's records would be spared if Ishola went quietly.

The guild's records had not been spared. Of course they hadn't. I had been a merchant of impossible things for four decades and I had still, at the crucial hour, believed a promise made by men whose entire profession was making promises to people they intended to bury.

I want it noted — by whom, I no longer know; there is no one left to note anything — that I did not lie to myself about what I'd done. That is the one coin I have left. Most men who betray a house tell themselves stories until the betrayal wears the costume of necessity. I did not bother. I knew exactly what I had done when I did it, and I did it anyway, and I would like to report that the reasoning was magnificent. It was. I built it brick by brick over three sleepless nights: the guild survives, the children in the dormers survive, the old ones with nowhere else survive, and one burned woman — who was dying anyway, who was a lit fuse walking around my hall — goes to the Iron House instead of all of us. A fair trade. Arithmetic. Any guildmaster worth his salt makes that trade.

And then the watch came anyway, two days later, with a writ I could have written better myself, and inventoried my house down to the copper hinges, and told me with the flat courtesy of men reading from a list that the Guild of Impossible Things was dissolved by order of the Crown, its charter revoked retroactive to the spring, its assets forfeit, its members dispersed under penalty of the new statutes.

Retroactive. They had dissolved us before I made the bargain, and let me make it anyway. Or perhaps the bargain had been the point — perhaps they wanted my signature on a surrender so they could show the other houses what cooperation bought. It bought this. It bought me standing alone in a hall full of pale rectangles where things used to be, holding a broom I had picked up out of some atavistic instinct toward tidiness, sweeping nothing.

I laughed. I will admit that. I laughed until it turned into the other thing, and then I sat down on the floor of my own guildhouse like an old man, which is what I am, and let the floor hold me for a while, since no one else was available.

* * *

Let me tell you how it was done, because if this confession is going to exist at all it should exist honestly, and honesty begins with procedure. I have always trusted procedure. Procedure was my religion before I ever heard of gods.

Ishola came to us half-dead, smelling of smoke and carrying a seal that hummed against the vault wards like a struck bell. Mara wanted to shelter her. Of course he did. He was always the best of us and the worst liar, which are the same quality seen from different angles. And I told him — I remember the exact words, I have been replaying them like a man picking at a scab — I told him, "We do not light candles in a house full of paper."

Which was true. Which has been our rule for forty years and has kept forty years of impossible things from burning. And she *was* a candle. The informant had already been through my ledgers twice with his soft questions and his soft purse. The watch had walked the street twice that week. Every day she stayed, the probability of the house burning rose, and I am a man who has spent his life pricing probabilities.

So I priced her.

I did it at night, alone, the way you price anything shameful. I sent a message through the chandler on Cordwainer Street — a neutral channel, deniable, the kind of arrangement you build hoping never to use it — and I offered the crown its burned survivor in exchange for the guild's charter and the safety of its members. The man who came to finalize terms wore a clerk's grey and smelled of ink and had hands soft as dough, and he smiled at everything I said as though I were a child reciting sums correctly, and I hated him, and I shook his hand.

Ishola knew. That is the part I keep circling back to. She was not fooled, not for a moment. When the watch cart came, she looked at me across the hall — past the two guards, past Mara being held back by three grown men who were weeping while they held him, which tells you something about the caliber of person I sold — and she did not curse me. She said, "Guildmaster. Keep the record." As if I were the one being entrusted with something. As if I had not just proven myself the last man alive to trust with a record.

Then she went up into the cart, straight-backed, ash-skinned, and the door closed, and the cart rolled away, and I turned to my guild and told them the house was safe now.

The house was not safe now. The house was already dead; I simply hadn't seen the body yet.

* * *

For three days I performed normalcy the way I once performed prosperity in lean years — loudly, and with inventory. I re-shelved. I re-inked. I gave the apprentices tasks designed to occupy hands and quiet minds. On the fourth day the dissolution writ arrived, and on the fifth the members began to leave, and I stood at the door and shook each hand and told each one the guild would rise again, and I watched them carry that lie away like a parcel I had wrapped myself.

Tomaso took the road south with the singing compass hidden in a flour sack, though God knows what a compass that sings will do for him now except remind him. The Ferro twins went to their aunt in the tannery quarter and swore — actually swore, on their mother's grave — that they had never seen anything impossible in their lives. Old Wren, who has been deaf since the fire of '61 and hears only what the artifacts tell her, packed a single bag and paused at the threshold and looked at me with those clouded eyes and said, clear as a bell, the way she says things that aren't sounds:

"You traded the flame for the lamp."

I have thought about that sentence every hour since. You traded the flame for the lamp. Meaning: I gave up the thing that generates light to protect the thing that merely holds it. A guildhouse is a lamp. A guildhouse without its people, its records, its impossible inventory — a lamp with no oil, a lamp you can polish forever in the dark.

Or meaning something crueler: that I mistook the container for the contents. That forty years ago, when I inherited this house from Guildmaster Adaeze — who hid oath-bound members through two purges and died in her bed, defiant and uncaught, and whose portrait watches me from the stairwell with an expression I can no longer bear — I began, slowly, imperceptibly, year by year, prudent compromise by prudent compromise, to love the *building* more than the *work*. Adaeze risked the building constantly. That was the whole point of her. The work was dangerous and the building existed to make danger survivable, not to make danger avoidable. Somewhere along the way I inverted it. I kept the roof dry and let the fire go out.

So when Mara came to me with a burned woman at the door, I did not see Adaeze's legacy. I saw a candle in a house full of paper. And I put it out.

* * *

On the sixth day I went looking for what remained, because a man who has lost everything will still count the change.

The watch had missed things. They always miss things; that is the small mercy of large institutions, that they are too big to be thorough. Behind the false back of the appraisal cabinet — Adaeze's hiding place, older than me, known to three guildmasters and now, apparently, to a fourth — I found the surviving guild ledger. Not the display ledger, the beautiful one with the tooled cover that the informant had paged through twice with his soft fingers. The true one. The working one. Forty years of appraisals in my own cramped hand, and beneath mine, in faded iron-gall, Adaeze's entries, and beneath hers, entries in a hand I did not recognize at all, going back further than the guild's own founding story admits.

And here is the thing that undid me, the thing I keep returning to like a tongue to a broken tooth: the ledger was full of names. Not objects. Names. Because that is what the guild really was, underneath the singing compasses and the swords that remember their kills. We were a house of record for people the world wanted forgotten. Every entry I had ever made — every "one (1) compass, sings, condition fair" — was a coded line in a longer book, and the longer book was a list of the erased, and I had been its keeper without ever letting myself read it whole.

I sat on the floor of the vault with that ledger in my lap and read it from the first page to the last, and somewhere around Adaeze's entry for a girl named Bintou — "one (1) voice, remembers songs, condition endangered, sheltered" — I understood exactly what I had done when I handed Ishola to the cart.

Ishola was not a candle. Ishola was a page. She was testimony with a pulse, the last living copy of a village the crown had burned off the world's memory, and I had sold the original manuscript to save the binding.

* * *

Now. The courier.

His name was Pelu, and he was not a courier by trade; he was a tanner's factor who owed me money from '68 and had a mule and no questions. I chose him because he was nobody, and because the roads east were crawling with the crown's riders, and because a dying man attracts less attention than a live messenger. He was dying — the wet cough, the grey lips, six weeks maybe, and he knew it, and when I offered him the job plus forgiveness of his debt plus enough coin to buy his daughter an apprenticeship, he looked at me with the frankness of the terminally ill and said, "You're sending your sins east, guildmaster. Mule can carry those. Mule doesn't care."

Everyone sees through me now. It is remarkable how clearly a man becomes legible the moment he has nothing left to hide behind.

I wrote the letter first. Four drafts. The first was defensive — I burned it. The second was explanatory — I burned that too; explanation is just apology wearing armor. The third I got as far as "Mara, you must understand the position I was—" before I stopped, because he didn't have to understand anything, and understanding was not what I was asking for, and dressing the request up as comprehension insulted us both.

The fourth draft said:

*Mara. The guild is dissolved. The house is stripped. I sold Ishola and it bought nothing, which means I sold her for less than nothing, which is the arithmetic of damnation and I did it anyway. Enclosed is the true ledger — Adaeze's and mine and older than both of us. It wants a keeper who understands that a record is not paper. It wants a keeper who is willing to burn, which I discovered, too late and at everyone else's expense, that I am not. Do not forgive me. Use me. If there is ever an accounting — and there will be, because the erased keep better books than the crown does — put my name in it on the wrong side, plainly, where it belongs. I am done hiding my entries. — S.*

I sealed it with the guild's stamp, which felt obscene, and then I did it anyway, because the stamp was the one authority I had left and I meant the letter to carry weight.

Pelu took the ledger wrapped in oilcloth inside a saddlebag of dried fish, because no rider searching for a dragon-scale seal or a fugitive archivist opens a bag of fish that ripe. He rode east on the salt road. He coughed blood at the second milestone, according to the carter who passed him, and kept riding.

Whether he reaches Mara, I do not know. Whether Mara reads it, whether he throws it in a river, whether he comes back here to put my name in a ledger on the wrong side — none of that is mine to know anymore. That is what a confession is, I think, finally, at my age: the act of handing the truth to someone else and losing all control of it. I kept control of the truth for forty years, filed and coded and shelved behind false cabinet backs, and look what my careful custody purchased.

* * *

Tonight I sit in the guildmaster's office with Adaeze's portrait watching me, and I have done the last piece of housekeeping, the one I have been avoiding because even now, even hollowed, some cowardly guildmaster reflex in me wants to keep a reserve.

But a reserve is a hedge, and hedges are how I got here.

So: the remaining seals of the guild — the charter seal, the vault key, the little brass stamp that says *Impossible, Verified* — I have wrapped in the guild banner and buried beneath the third flagstone of the kitchen hearth, where Adaeze hid oath-bound members through the purge of her own era, and where, if any member ever returns, they will find them. The house is theirs. It was always theirs. I was only the steward, and stewards who begin to believe they are owners are the ones who sell the household to save the walls.

And tomorrow — this is decided, I have the ink and the paper ready — I am going to do the one thing left in my power that is neither flight nor flattery nor arithmetic.

I am going to walk to the Iron House, present myself at the gate, and confess. Not to a crime they care about — they will barely look up; I am a dissolved guildmaster, a rounding error in their campaign. But Ishola is in there, in the interrogation rooms, being asked questions by men with soft hands, and she believes — she said it herself, straight-backed, going up into the cart — that the record survived. She believes someone kept it. And the crown's historians will have told her otherwise, will be telling her every day, the way they tell everyone, that nothing survives, that memory is a rumor, that resistance is a filing error.

I cannot free her. I have neither the force nor the cunning; my talents ran entirely to inventory and compromise. But I can stand in whatever passes for a visiting cell and say to her face: the ledger lives, it went east with a dying man on a mule that smells of fish, and your village is written in it, every name, in my hand and Adaeze's and hands older than ours, and it will outlast the men asking you questions. I can give her that. It is almost nothing. It is the only thing I have.

And then — and this is the part that frightens me, the part that feels like standing at the edge of the memory-vault in the dark — I am going to ask her to add my testimony to hers. Whatever tribunal the erased convene someday, outside the law, in whatever language of accounts they keep — I want my name entered. Voluntarily. On the wrong side. With the whole transaction described, in detail, in my own words, so that no future guildmaster can dress it up as necessity the way I dressed it up for myself.

Remorse is worthless as a feeling. Everyone feels it; it costs nothing; it is the cheapest coin in circulation. Remorse is only worth what it purchases, and the only thing it can purchase is the truth, delivered while it can still be delivered, to whoever can still use it.

The letter is gone east. The seals are under the hearthstone. Tomorrow, the gate of the Iron House.

Adaeze is watching from the stairwell. For the first time in forty years, I find I can meet her painted eyes — not because I am forgiven, but because I have finally, finally stopped negotiating with the truth and started paying what I owe.

Somewhere on the salt road, a dying man is coughing blood onto a mule's flank, and my whole life's ledger is wrapped in oilcloth beside the dried fish, riding east toward the only person young enough and stubborn enough to know what to do with it.

Ride, Pelu. Ride.
