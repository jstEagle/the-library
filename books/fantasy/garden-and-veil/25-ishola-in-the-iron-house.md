## 25. Ishola in the Iron House

The Iron House has three rules, and I learned all of them before breakfast on my first morning.

Rule one: you do not speak unless spoken to. Rule two: you eat what is given. Rule three: you tell the interrogator what he wants to hear, and the interrogator decides what he wants to hear, which means rule three is really just rule one wearing a nicer coat.

I broke all three before noon.

Not because I'm brave — let's be honest about that, since honesty is apparently the only currency they can't confiscate. I broke them because I had spent four days in a cell with nothing but a straw pallet, a bucket, and my own thoughts, and my own thoughts are terrible company when they're the only ones allowed at the table. So when the door opened and a man in a grey coat asked me where the seal was, I said, "Good morning to you too," and we were off.

His name was Adewale. I know this because he told me, which was either a courtesy or a mistake, and I have decided it was a mistake. He had kind eyes. That's the worst part of these places — they don't send monsters anymore. Monsters are inefficient. They send patient men with kind eyes who genuinely believe that if they just explain things clearly enough, you'll see reason, and reason will look exactly like surrender.

"Ishola Adeyemi," he read from a folder. "Of Ashfall village."

"There's no such place," I said pleasantly.

He looked up. "Pardon?"

"Ashfall village. You won't find it on any map. Your historians burned it out of the records eleven years ago. So either I'm lying about where I'm from, or your entire archive is lying, and only one of us is being paid for it."

Something flickered behind the kind eyes. Not anger. Something more dangerous: recognition. He knew. Of course he knew. Everyone in this building knows; that's the whole architecture of the thing. The Iron House isn't built to extract secrets from people who have them. It's built to make people doubt the secrets they carry until they hand them over just to be rid of the weight.

"Let's begin again," he said, with the serenity of a man who has done this many times and been thanked for it afterward. "Where is the dragon-scale seal?"

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about interrogation: it's boring. Genuinely, profoundly boring. There's no dramatic music, no sweating under a single lamp. There's a table, two chairs, a pitcher of water that never gets poured, and a man asking you the same question in forty different grammatical arrangements, hoping one of them catches you off guard like a fishhook in a lazy mouth.

*Where did you receive the seal?*

*Who gave it to you?*

*You received it. Who gave it?*

*Did someone give it to you?*

*From whom did you receive the seal?*

By the third day I started answering in kind, rearranging my refusals the same way. *No. No one. No one gave me anything. I found it. I made it. It grew out of my chest like a second heart.* That last one made him write something down, which delighted me more than it should have. Let them chase a seal grown out of a woman's ribs. Let their surgeons open me looking for it and find nothing but an old burn scar and a stubborn streak a mile wide.

But here's the thing about boredom: it's a weapon pointed both ways. On the fourth day, alone in my cell, I realized I was starting to count the cracks in the ceiling. On the fifth day I named them. On the sixth day I caught myself wondering what Adewale's children looked like, because he'd mentioned them once, casually, the way jailers do — *my daughter turns seven next week* — planting himself in my sympathy like a seed in wet ground.

That was the moment I understood I needed armor, and the only armor I had ever owned was names.

So I began to recite.

* * *

It started quietly, under my breath, the way you'd hum. The names of Ashfall. My mother first — Oyinkan, who could coax a stew out of nothing and once talked a tax collector into refunding the tax. Then my father, Tunde, who mended nets and believed, sincerely and fatally, that the crown's soldiers would never burn a fishing village because fishing villages fed the crown's soldiers.

Then the neighbors. Mama Efunroye with her goats. The twins, Kehinde and Taiwo, who were nine. Old Sanya who claimed he'd fought in the border wars and lied about every detail except the limp. The miller. The miller's wife. The miller's apprentice, whose name I never learned because by the time he arrived in our village he was already smoke, and I have carried that namelessness for eleven years like a stone behind my teeth.

I recited them in the dark of the cell. I recited them while the bucket filled and the straw flattened and the crack I'd named Bertha continued its slow work across the ceiling. And here is what happened, and why I tell you this not as sentiment but as strategy:

They couldn't stop me.

Oh, they tried. When the listening-hole in the door picked it up, they came in and told me silence was in my best interest. I recited louder. They took my supper. I recited through the hunger, and hunger, I discovered, improves diction. They moved me to a cell at the end of the row where the walls were thicker, and I recited there, and a boy who mopped the corridor — a boy with quick eyes and a face I memorized on principle — slowed his mop outside my door one evening, and I swear on my mother's stew-coaxing hands that his lips moved along with mine for three names before he hurried away.

That's when I understood what I was doing. I wasn't keeping myself sane. Sanity was a side effect. I was doing what Mara does with his ledgers, what the guild does with its impossible inventory, what the seal itself does when it presses against your palm and shows you dragons dying for kings who signed their deaths into law.

I was keeping records.

The crown burns pages. Fine. But a page is only paper, and paper is only memory wearing a coat, and memory lives in mouths, and mouths live in bodies, and they would need to burn rather a lot of bodies to get mine quiet. They had tried already, back in Ashfall. Eleven years ago. The smoke reached the river before the screaming did.

* * *

Adewale came back on the ninth day. I could tell it was scheduled as a fresh start because he brought a new folder and wore a slightly better coat, the way men do when they're losing and want the loss to look like a change of tactics.

"You've become difficult," he said.

"I've become consistent," I said. "There's a difference. Difficult people change. I say the same thing every day. Ask anyone."

"The seal, Ishola."

"The names, Adewale." I leaned forward. "You say yours, I'll say mine. Fair trade. You go first — you have fewer."

Something crossed his face then, fast, like a bird past a window. "I don't know what you mean."

"Sure you do. You mentioned a daughter. Seven next week. What's her name?" I let the silence sit. "You see how it works? A name is a hook. You put it in me hoping I'd soften toward you. Now it's in me, and I keep things. That's my gift — not swordspeech, not fire, nothing glamorous. I keep things. Your historians erase a village, and I keep it. You burn a ledger, and I keep it. You put me in a room with a kind-eyed man and a folder, and I keep him too."

He closed the folder. For a long moment he just looked at me, and I want to be fair to him here, because fairness is another thing they can't confiscate: he looked tired. Not cruel-tired. The other kind. The kind that comes from carrying out orders you've stopped examining because examination leads to places with no pension.

"My daughter's name is Folake," he said quietly.

And then he left, and I sat with that name the way you sit with a coal you've decided not to drop.

* * *

They changed tactics after that, which meant I was winning, which meant it was going to get worse. I want to be clear-eyed about the arithmetic of defiance: it doesn't come free. Every day I didn't talk, someone recalculated, and the recalculations were trending somewhere with less water in the pitcher and colder floors. I could feel it coming the way you feel weather in an old scar.

On the twelfth day they took away the bucket privilege and gave me a companion instead — a new interrogator, younger, with none of Adewale's courtesies and all of his certainty. He didn't ask about the seal. He asked about Mara Oyelaran. Where was he heading. Who else knew. Did the guildhouse keep other oath-bound.

I recited the names of Ashfall at him until he left, and then I kept going, because the cell felt emptier with the echo gone.

But that night, lying on the flattening straw, I did something I hadn't let myself do yet. I thought about Mara properly. His careful hands. The way he wrote numbers like each one might sue him later. The half-oath at the ford that Veyra must have reported by now — a river turned to warm mist, witnessed, documented, filed. They would have shown me the report if they'd had it. They were saving it. Or worse: they didn't need it, because they already knew where he was going. Everyone goes to the dragon-road eventually. It's the only direction left when every other road is watched.

Which meant the seal's location mattered less every day, and my usefulness was a candle burning down, and candles in the Iron House get snuffed, not honored.

Good, I thought, and surprised myself. Good. Because a candle's job isn't to last. It's to be lit when it matters.

* * *

On the fifteenth day, Adewale came back without a folder.

He sat down across from me and didn't open with the seal. He opened with: "Folake lost her first tooth yesterday."

I waited.

"She wanted to know why I work late." He turned his hands over on the table, palms up, like a man showing he carried nothing. "I told her I keep records. She asked whose."

The listening-hole in the door was right there. We both knew it. We both spoke anyway, in the particular grammar of people who have decided the risk is the point.

"And what did you tell her?" I asked.

"That I keep records of people who are difficult." He met my eyes. "She asked if difficult people are bad people."

"What did you tell her?"

He stood, smoothed his coat, and walked to the door. At it, he paused — not dramatically, just the pause of a man arranging his face before a corridor full of colleagues.

"I told her," he said, "that the worst thing a person can be in this building is forgotten. And then I told her she should always remember her grandmother's village. She's never seen it. It isn't on any map." A beat. "It was called Ashfall."

Then he knocked twice on the door — the guard's signal — and was gone, and I sat alone with the loudest silence the Iron House had ever produced.

I should have felt triumphant. Instead I felt the way you feel when a wall you've been leaning on shifts: grateful and terrified in the same breath, because a wall that moves for you can move against you, and kindness inside these walls is either a crack in the foundation or bait on a hook, and I have been wrong about which is which before.

But that night, I added a name to the recitation. Not one of Ashfall's. A new one.

"Folake," I said to the ceiling, to Bertha the crack, to whoever listened through the little hole. "Daughter of Adewale. Age seven. One tooth fewer than last week. Grandmother's village: Ashfall. Which existed. Which exists. Which will exist."

And far below the floor, far below the foundations, I imagined — no, I *knew*, the way you know weather in a scar — a guttering flame on a mountain shrine leaning toward my voice like a plant toward a window.

Somewhere out there, Mara was walking toward it with a hole in his memory shaped like everything he'd traded away. Somewhere out there, a hunter was reading manufactured reports and feeling her certainty crack along lines suspiciously like conscience. And somewhere in this building, a kind-eyed man had just committed treason in front of his daughter's name.

They think the seal is a thing you hide.

It's not. It's a thing you *say*. And I had fifteen days of saying in me, and room for years more, and every name I spoke was a coal passed hand to hand down a corridor they couldn't search.

The lock turned. Boots in the corridor. New interrogator, by the stride — the young one, the certain one, coming back with whatever they'd decided my silence was worth.

I sat up straight on the straw, cleared my throat, and began at the beginning.

"Mama Efunroye," I said, as the key found the lock, "kept goats. Forty-one of them. I will now tell you about every single one."
