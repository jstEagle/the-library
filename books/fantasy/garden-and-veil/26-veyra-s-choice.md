## 26. Veyra's Choice

Treason, I had always assumed, would feel like fire. Like the moment a swordspeaker's oath catches and the world tilts sideways. What it actually felt like, on the third night of my deliberation, was an itch behind my left ear that no amount of scratching could reach.

I want it noted — by whom, I couldn't say; there is no ledger for this kind of thing, which is rather the point — that I did not come to my decision in a single dramatic instant. There was no thunderclap. No vision of dragons. Just a woman sitting at a camp desk outside the Iron House, reading campaign orders she was never meant to question, with an itch she couldn't scratch.

The orders were spread out before me in Captain-General Ossory's own hand, which was elegant in the way that expensive things are elegant. *Operation Veilfire*, he'd titled it, as though naming it something pretty would keep anyone from noticing what it actually was.

What it actually was: three companies marching on the dragon-road within the fortnight. A sapper detail with enough blackpowder to bring down the shrine mountain's eastern face. And a supply requisition for something called a *null-iron lattice* — forty feet of it, forged to specification, to be erected around the last dragon-fire like a cage around a candle.

Not to seize the flame. To smother it. To make certain that no oath, spoken by any voice, ever answered it again.

I read it four times. On the fourth reading, my hand was steady, which surprised me, because everything else in me was not.

* * *

You should understand what I was before this night, because I have spent thirty years being it, and a person doesn't shed thirty years the way a snake sheds skin. More like the way a tree sheds a limb. Slowly, painfully, and leaving a scar shaped exactly like the missing thing.

I was the crown's hunter. The Sword-Catcher, some of the younger watchmen called me, half in awe and half in the way you name a thing you hope never turns to look at you. I had taken eleven swordspeakers alive and put down two who wouldn't come quietly, and every one of them I had delivered to the Iron House with a clear conscience and a written report in a fair hand.

A clear conscience. I want to laugh at that phrase now. It's like saying *a dry drowning*.

Here is the arithmetic I had lived by: the oath-bound started the ancient war. That was history. That was in the histories, inked and official, taught to every child in every schoolroom from the capital to the salt flats. Dragons and their sworn speakers turned on the kings who sheltered them, burned the old cities, and were only put down at terrible cost. Every swordspeaker alive was a lit fuse walking around in human shape. Catch them, cage them, and the realm slept safe.

I believed it the way you believe the floor will hold you. Not as a thought. As a fact of standing upright.

And then, three weeks ago, in a burned-out village with no name on any map I owned, I found a ledger.

* * *

It was buried under the floor of the shrine-house, wrapped in oilcloth, and I only found it because I was hunting a survivor whose trail ended there, and because I have spent thirty years learning how people hide things. The village itself was strange — empty, but not ruined. Doors hanging open. Meals long since rotted on tables. As if everyone had simply walked out one morning and forgotten to exist.

I opened the ledger expecting names of oath-bound sympathizers. What I found instead was a census. Every soul in the village, recorded in a clerk's careful hand: births, marriages, trades, the little debts and kindnesses that make up a place. And then, in a different hand, pressed hard enough to tear the page, a single line:

*They came at dusk. They wore no colors. They asked for those who dreamed of wings. When we said we knew of none, they took every third household anyway, so that the rest would forget faster.*

Below that, dates. Below the dates, tally marks. Four hundred and six.

Four hundred and six people who had never existed, according to the crown's own maps. The village I stood in wasn't on them. I checked. I stood in its square and checked my survey charts twice, and the space where it should have been held nothing but the printed notation: *unimproved grassland.*

Unimproved grassland. Four hundred and six souls, unimproved.

I sat down in the shrine-house doorway and I did not move for a long time. A soldier learns to be still. It is one of the few gifts the trade gives.

And here is the thing I want recorded, if anything I write survives what's coming: I did not weep. I wanted to. I felt the tears sitting right there behind my eyes like guests who know they're unwelcome. But I didn't weep, because some cold practical voice in me said, *not yet, not until you know what this means, not until you're sure,* and that voice has kept me alive through eleven captures and two killings, and I obeyed it even then.

The voice was right to wait. Because the next morning, my own orders arrived, and they explained everything.

* * *

The orders confirmed my patrol schedule through the season and appended a new directive: upon locating any surviving settlement records from the northern purge zone, such records were to be seized and destroyed, and the discovering officer was to file a report confirming the zone contained no permanent habitation.

No permanent habitation. I was standing in one. My boots were muddy from its streets.

That was the moment the floor gave way beneath me. Not the ledger — the ledger was grief, and grief a soldier can carry. But the orders. The orders were instructions to lie, issued in advance, to me specifically, which meant they expected me to find things, which meant they knew what was in the ground here, which meant —

Which meant the hunt was never a hunt. A hunt implies quarry that might escape. This was a harvest. We were not looking for dangerous survivors. We were looking for evidence, and burning it, and burning the people attached to it, and calling the whole operation a service to the realm.

I thought about the eleven I had taken alive. Where were they? The Iron House did not release prisoners. It did not execute them either — executions leave bodies, and bodies leave questions. So where?

I thought about the word the ash-woman had shouted at me through her ruined throat when I cornered her outside the salt flats, before she slipped away into the dark. I'd assumed it was a curse. Now I wondered if it had been a warning. Or a greeting. She had looked at me — burned past recognition, half her face gone to scarring — and she had looked at me with something worse than hatred.

She had looked at me with recognition. As if she knew what I was before I knew it myself.

My mother sang to me once, when I was very small, a song about wings and fire, and my father made her stop, and neither of them ever spoke of it again. I had filed that memory away as a child files things: without understanding, permanently. Sitting in that dead village holding a dead village's book, I took the memory back out and looked at it properly for the first time in thirty years.

Oh, I thought. Oh, no.

* * *

I am not going to pretend the next three weeks were noble. They were miserable, and cowardly in places, and I drank more than I have since my cadet days, and twice I nearly burned the ledger and reported the zone clean and went back to being the Sword-Catcher, because being the Sword-Catcher was warm and certain and mine.

But the itch behind my ear wouldn't stop. That's what conscience feels like, in case you were wondering. Not fire. An itch. The sense that something is wrong with your own skin, and the growing certainty that the only cure is to take the skin off.

I did my duty in those weeks. I filed my reports. I wrote *no permanent habitation* in my fair hand and hated myself with a thoroughness that would have impressed my old drill instructor. And all the while I copied. Ossory's campaign plans, the sapper schedules, the guard rotations at the Iron House — I have a memory trained for exactly this, the one gift the crown gave me gladly — and I copied it all onto thin paper in small letters, and sewed the pages into the lining of my riding coat.

And I stole a key.

Not the grand iron key to the prison gates; those are chained to wardens' belts and missed within the hour. I took something better. In Ossory's field office there hangs a board of keys for the interior doors — the interrogation wing, the record rooms, the cells where prisoners are kept who are not officially prisoners. One key was tagged *Ward B — null-iron cells.* Null-iron. The same metal as the lattice they meant to wrap around the dragon-fire. The metal that keeps a swordspeaker's gift from answering.

They weren't interrogating the prisoners in Ward B. You can't interrogate someone through null-iron; the wards scramble thought like weather scrambles birdsong. Ward B was storage. Living storage. People kept alive and giftless and forgotten, because killing them was untidy and releasing them was unthinkable.

Eleven captures. Two killings. Thirteen minus two is eleven.

I counted the keys on that board three times before I understood what my hands were doing. Then I palmed the Ward B key, walked out of Ossory's office, saluted a lieutenant in the corridor, and went and ate a full supper in the mess tent while my whole life rearranged itself behind my ribs.

* * *

The last piece fell into place two nights ago, and it was the smallest piece, and it nearly broke me.

I was reviewing the prisoner manifest — the official one, the fiction — and cross-referencing it against my own capture reports, because I am thorough, thoroughness being the virtue they built me out of. Eleven names I had delivered. Eleven cells unaccounted for in the official record. And against one of my own reports, in someone else's annotation, a note in red ink:

*Subject claims knowledge of the seal. Transfer pending. Do not allow contact with other subjects.*

The seal. The dragon-scale seal. The thing the entire northern operation was secretly bent around, the thing the burned woman had carried, the thing Ossory's spies had been hunting through every market and guildhouse in three provinces while the official hunt for swordspeakers provided cover.

And the subject who claimed knowledge of it — the annotation named her cell. Ward B. Cell nine.

I knew that handwriting. I have known that handwriting for twenty years. It belonged to Guildmaster Sefu of the Guild of Impossible Things, who had sold us information twice before, small things, harmless things, or so I'd been told, and whose latest communication had apparently been large enough to put a living person into a box made of forgetting.

I don't know why that was the thing. The purges hadn't done it. The orders hadn't done it. But an old man trading a woman's freedom for his guild's safety — that went through me like a blade finding the seam in armor. Perhaps because I recognized it. Perhaps because I understood, in the marrow-deep way you understand your own reflection, that Sefu and I had been making the same bargain our whole lives: keep the institution safe, keep yourself useful, and tell yourself the arithmetic works.

His arithmetic had just cost a woman her name. Mine had cost four hundred and six people their existence.

Time to balance the books.

* * *

So. Here I sit, one hour before dawn, in the dark of my quarters at the Iron House garrison, wearing a coat with a country's worth of treason sewn into its lining, and a stolen key warm in my fist, and I find that I am calm. Genuinely calm. The itch has stopped. Whatever comes next — the rope, the null-iron cell of my own, the long oblivion they give to hunters who turn — the itch has stopped, and I am grateful to it for thirty years of service and glad to see it go.

The plan, such as it is, fits in a sentence: ride north ahead of the column, find the swordspeaker Mara Oyelaran and the ash-woman Ishola, give them the campaign plans and the key, and pray to whatever gods remain unedited that they can do something with both before Ossory's sappers reach the mountain.

It is a bad plan. It relies on finding two fugitives who do not wish to be found, on my defection going unnoticed for at least four days, and on the possibility that the people I hunted might accept help from the hand that hunted them. I have considered each flaw carefully and concluded that I do not care. A soldier spends her whole life executing plans she didn't make. Let this one be mine, flaws and all.

There is one more thing, and then I ride.

In the ledger from the dead village, beneath the tally marks, the clerk had written one final line, in a hand gone shaky:

*If any find this: we were real. Say that we were real.*

I have said it every night since, alone, like a prayer in reverse — not asking anything of heaven, just informing it. Tonight I'll say it one last time in this room, and then I intend to spend the rest of whatever life I have left saying it louder, in places that don't want to hear it, to people who would prefer I didn't exist either.

Let them send a hunter after the hunter. Let them learn what I learned in a village of unimproved grassland: that a person who has finally stopped lying is very difficult to catch.

The east is graying. Somewhere north of here, a guttering flame is waiting to be remembered, and a man with burned hands is waiting to be told that the war is coming to him whether he likes it or not.

I saddle my horse in the dark. The key to Ward B rides in my breast pocket, over my heart, where treason belongs — close enough to feel it beat.
