## 28. The Informant's End

*Mara*

There is a particular quality to being hunted by a man who has read your handwriting.

I don't mean it as poetry. I mean it literally. Duro Adeyemi — the Buyer Who Pays in Names, the informant whose ledgers I had stolen and copied and burned my own guildhouse to protect — had spent three years cataloguing the small dishonesties of people like me. He knew I pressed too hard when I wrote the letter *r*. He knew I crossed my sevens. He knew, because he'd once bought a page of my ledger from a fence in Kesh, that when I lied in writing I made my letters *smaller*, as if I could hide the lie by shrinking it.

So when we found the first marker on the road out of the Iron House district — a chalked seven, crossed exactly the way I cross mine, drawn on a wall where no child would bother — I understood that he wasn't chasing us anymore.

He was *corresponding* with us.

"He wants us to know he's reading," Veyra said. She crouched by the wall, hood up, one hand resting on the hilt of a sword she hadn't drawn in eleven years. "It's a courtesy. He's telling us the hunt is over and the collection is about to begin."

"Charming man."

"He's not charming. He's *thorough*. There's a difference, and the difference is that charming men let you go." She stood. Under the hood her face was still marked from the Iron House — a burn along her jaw where a ward had kissed her when the key turned wrong the first time. She wore it without complaint, the way she wore everything, like a debt she intended to pay personally. "He'll have the eastern gate. The river gate. The salt road. He's had three days."

"Then we take none of them."

"We take the one he's *paid* to ignore." She almost smiled. It was the closest thing to humor I'd seen from her since she'd put her oath on the table beside her treason. "Every hunter has one road they leave open because someone above them told them to leave it open, and every someone above them has been paid by someone below them to leave it open. Corruption is a map, Mara. You just have to know how to bribe your way along its margins."

"And you know this how?"

"I was the crown's sword for nine years." She said it flatly, without shame, which is either the mark of a healed wound or a very well-dressed one. "I know exactly which roads are for sale."

The road she chose ran through the tannery district, which in Ashkell means it runs through smell. The vats steamed in the cold morning and the whole quarter reeked of lime and rendered fat, and I confess that some part of me — the archivist part, the part that had spent years cataloguing impossible things in careful, invisible script — found a grim comfort in it. A place that stank this thoroughly was a place that hid things honestly. Nothing in the tanneries pretended to be anything other than what it was.

Which is why I should have known better than to trust it.

* * *

*Veyra*

I want it recorded — somewhere, anywhere, even if only in the memory of a guttering flame — that I did not walk us into the trap out of arrogance.

I walked us into it out of arithmetic.

Duro Adeyemi had forty men at his disposal, or the coin to rent them. We had two swords, one of them mine and rusted with disuse, one of them Mara's and burning him alive from the inside every time he used it. He had the city gates, the watch rolls, and the particular patience of a man who collects names the way other men collect debts. We had Ishola free but hidden, a seal worth more than the city, and a head start measured in hours.

Arithmetic said we would be caught. The only question was where, and whether we could make the *where* expensive.

I chose the tannery district because it was the only quarter in Ashkell where forty men cannot surround you. The vats and drying yards break the streets into corridors; the steam breaks sightlines; the lime burns eyes that aren't used to it. If Duro wanted us, he would have to come narrow, and narrow is where a swordspeaker stops being an outlaw and starts being a catastrophe.

I did not plan for him to come alone.

That was the mistake, and I have replayed it enough times since to know its shape: I assumed a collector would hide behind his collection. I forgot that some men collect *because* they believe no one will ever collect *them*.

He stepped out of the steam between vat three and vat four, and he was, infuriatingly, exactly what he had always seemed: a soft-bellied clerk in a good coat, ink-stained fingers folded over his stomach, smiling the smile of a man who has already entered today's events into a ledger and found the totals satisfactory.

"Mistress Veyra," he said. "And the archivist. How lovely. I had us down for a chase ending at the river gate, but I do enjoy being surprised. It happens so rarely in my line of work."

Behind him, out of the steam, came his men after all — six of them, fanning wide, crossbows low. Not forty. Six. Because Duro Adeyemi did not need forty men to kill two fugitives. He needed six men to kill two fugitives and thirty-four to make sure no one asked questions afterward.

"Stand down," I said, and put my hand on my hilt, and heard Mara behind me do the same, and felt the old heat wake in my palm like a dog hearing its name after years.

"Oh, please," Duro said mildly. "Do. I've written three reports on what a swordspeaker's oath does at close range. It would be lovely to correct them with primary observation." His smile widened a fraction. "Though I should mention — purely as professional courtesy — that the moment your gift lights up, every ward-net in this district will sing, and the crown's garrison will arrive in sufficient numbers to arrest whatever remains. You're not trapped, Mistress Veyra. You're merely *priced*. Everything has a price. That's rather the whole of my philosophy."

"You're wrong about one thing," I said.

"Do tell. I do love corrections. In triplicate."

"It's not a gift." I drew the sword. Eleven years of dust came off the blade in one long whisper. "It's a debt. And I'm done paying it to the crown."

I looked back at Mara, once. He understood. Of course he understood — the man catalogs everything, including the faces of people deciding to burn their lives down. He nodded, once, and moved left, away from me, giving me the corridor clean.

I spoke the oath.

Not half of it. Not the mumbled fragment they teach suppressed speakers so the gift can be strangled quiet. The whole of it, every word, in the old cadence, the words rising out of me like water finding a crack in a dam that had held for eleven years —

— and the world *answered*.

* * *

*Mara*

I have seen dragon-fire. I have seen the shrine-flame gutter and roar. I have seen what a full oath does to a valley, though I saw most of it through tears and the wrong end of forgetting.

But I had never seen it *small*. Contained. One woman, one corridor of steam and lime, one corrupt little clerk and his six hired crossbows — and Veyra's oath filling all of it like sunrise poured into a bottle.

Her sword lit white-gold. The steam didn't boil; it *bowed*, bending away from her in a perfect sphere of clear air. And the sound — the sound was the thing no report ever captured, I'd stake my archive on it. It wasn't thunder. It was a voice saying a name. Her name. The fire knew her. It had been waiting eleven years in her scarred palms like a letter unopened, and now it read itself aloud.

The crossbowmen broke first. Men can stand against a sword. Very few can stand against being *recognized* by something older than their kingdom. Two dropped their weapons and ran; one knelt, which I suspect was instinct rather than conversion; the rest raised their bows anyway, because Duro paid well and fear of him was, apparently, more immediate than fear of heaven.

Veyra moved through them like a sentence being struck through. I will not describe the details. I am an archivist; I record what matters, and what mattered was this: she killed no one who ran, and everyone who stayed died quickly, and she wept while she did it, silently, the way soldiers do, grief and duty running down the same face.

Duro Adeyemi did not run.

That is the part I keep returning to. The wards were singing — I could feel them, a high thin shriek in my teeth, the whole district's net lighting up around us like a netted fish. The garrison was coming; we had perhaps four minutes. Any sensible collector would have fled into the steam and lived to file a report.

Instead he opened his coat, took out a small black book, and held it up between himself and the fire.

"The campaign plans," he called to me, over the roar of Veyra's oath dying down to embers along her blade. "The full rewrite schedule. Every village, every date, every historian on the crown's payroll. It's all here, archivist. Your second ledger made flesh." He smiled through the heat haze, and I saw — this is the detail I hate — I saw that he was *enjoying* this. "You want it. I know you want it. You're a record-keeper. This is the record of records. So here is my last offer, and note that I am making it while your pet soldier holds a burning sword, which shows remarkable confidence in my own value: the book, for the seal. You give me the dragon-scale, I give you the truth, and we both walk away having traded fairly. Isn't that what your guild does? Impossible things, fair prices?"

And the worst part — the part I will carry into whatever forgetting waits for me — is that for one full second, I considered it.

Because he was right. I *did* want it. Every erased village, every forged history, every name on the crown's list of things to unwish — bound in calfskin, complete, *provable*. An archivist looks at a book like that the way a starving man looks at bread. The seal was testimony. The book was *everything*.

Then I thought of Ishola in the Iron House, reciting names to men with pliers, refusing to trade the one thing she had left. I thought of Sefu, trading a woman for a guildhouse and getting neither. I thought of the page I had burned in my own ledger, my own history gone up in smoke because I thought sacrifice was the same thing as wisdom.

"No," I said.

Duro's smile flickered — genuinely flickered, the first honest expression I ever saw on him. "No?"

"The seal isn't mine to sell. It never was. It belongs to everyone your masters erased." I drew my own sword, and my burned hand screamed, and I let it scream, because some prices you pay standing up. "And you've misread your ledger, Adeyemi. You think you're the buyer here. But you walked into a corridor with a swordspeaker and an oath-bound archivist, and you offered to trade the truth for a trinket. That's not a purchase. That's a *confession*, witnessed." I glanced at Veyra. "Isn't that right?"

She was looking at Duro with an expression I had not seen on her before. Not hatred. Something colder and sadder. Recognition.

"You wrote the order," she said quietly. "For the purge of Sable Ford. I executed it. Nine years ago. I always wondered whose handwriting was on it." She raised her sword. "It was yours."

"Orders originate above me," Duro said, and for the first time his voice lost its clerk's smoothness. "I merely — "

"Recorded them." Veyra's fire flared. "You collected them. You filed them. You sold them upward and sold us downward. You're not a clerk, Adeyemi. You're the *memory* of everything rotten in this kingdom, walking around in a good coat." Her voice cracked, just slightly, on the next words. "And I have spent nine years being its hands. So no. No trade. No price. Some entries get closed."

What happened next happened fast, and I will set it down plainly because plainness is all I have left to offer anyone.

Duro threw the black book — *threw it*, into the lime-pit slurry between the vats, spite's last arithmetic, better destroyed than surrendered — and went for a knife I hadn't seen, going not for me but for Veyra, going for the throat, a collector's final entry.

Veyra's oath answered before her mind did.

The fire took him mid-lunge. Not a blast — I need to be precise about this, because precision is the only penance I know — a *touch*. A hand's width of white-gold flame, reflexive, the gift defending its speaker the way a body flinches. It caught his coat, his chest, and Duro Adeyemi, the man who paid in names, made a sound I will not record, and fell, and the lime-slurry steamed around him, and the man who had catalogued the erasure of hundreds of villages became, in the space of four seconds, smoke.

The black book sank slowly into the pit. I dove for it. I got my arm in to the shoulder. The slurry ate the leather, ate the binding, ate the first signatures — and then my fingers closed on the inner pages, the ones rolled tight in the center, and I hauled them out dripping and half-dissolved, and clutched them to my chest like a rescued child.

Half a ledger. Half the rewrite schedule. The villages of the southern march dissolved to pulp; the mountain provinces legible; the historian payroll, miraculously, intact.

"Bitter," Veyra said behind me. She was on her knees beside what remained of Duro, and her sword-fire had gone out, and her hands — the hands that had held an oath for eleven years and finally spoken it — were shaking. "This is what victory tastes like. Nobody warns you. It tastes like lime and smoke and a man's last smile."

"He gave you no choice."

"He gave me every choice. That's what makes it bitter." She looked up at me, and her face was wet, and the burn on her jaw had gone white at the edges. "The wards sang, Mara. The garrison is coming. And I spoke a full oath in the middle of the capital's largest city. Whatever hiding we had left — his death just spent it. Both of ours. We're loud now. We're *known*."

Above the tannery roofs, bells began to ring — not the market bell, not the alarm bell, but the garrison bell, three strokes and a pause, the signal for a manifested gift inside the walls. I had heard it once before, years ago, and had told myself it meant nothing, that they'd never come for *him*, for quiet Mara with his careful ledgers.

The bells rang again. Closer.

Veyra rose. She wiped her face with the back of her hand, and something settled in her expression — not peace, nothing so kind, but a kind of terrible clarity, like a ledger finally balanced at a loss.

"They'll call it murder," she said. "An unregistered speaker killing a crown officer. They'll write it up tonight. By morning every garrison from here to the salt flats will have our descriptions." She picked up her sword, and sheathed it, and the sheathing sounded like a door closing. "We can't go to the dragon-road quietly anymore. So we go loudly. We go the way Duro never expected anyone to go."

"Which is?"

She looked south, toward the walls, toward the mountains beyond them where a guttering flame waited for a true ledger and a spoken oath.

"We stop running ahead of the army," Veyra said, "and start running *toward* it. Every survivor, every erased village, every witness between here and the shrine — we gather them as we go. Let the crown chase a *procession*. Let them try to arrest a testimony with a thousand mouths." She almost laughed, and it was the bitterest sound I have ever heard, and also the most hopeful. "He paid in names. Fine. Then we spend every name he ever touched. All at once. On his grave."

The garrison bells rang a third time, and boots were echoing in the tannery lanes, and I stood there holding half a dissolving ledger against my chest, and I realized I was smiling.

Bitter victory, yes. But victory. And for the first time since a burned woman collapsed at the guildhouse door carrying a dragon-scale seal, the next move was ours.

"Come on, archivist," Veyra said, already moving toward the south wall. "History's waiting to be rewritten properly — and this time, we hold the pen."

I followed her into the steam, and behind us the lime-pit closed over the last of Duro Adeyemi, and ahead of us the bells rang on, counting down the hours until an army marched on a guttering flame — and until, God willing, it met not two fugitives, but a nation of the unrecorded, standing on the old dragon-road with a true ledger open and an oath ready on every tongue.
