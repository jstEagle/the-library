## 29. Assembly of the Erased

I have counted my dead so many times that they have worn grooves in me, like water wearing stone. My mother. My father. The twins, Ade and Aduke, who were nine. Old Basir who taught me letters. The miller's daughter whose name I never learned because she was new to the village and I was eleven and self-absorbed in the way of children who do not yet know that names are perishable things.

Two hundred and six. That is the number. It has not changed since the night the soldiers came to Ilorin-under-the-Hill with their torches and their writs and their careful, bureaucratic cruelty. Two hundred and six people, erased from every record the crown keeps, unmade by ink as thoroughly as by fire.

Or so I believed, standing on the dragon-road at dawn, watching figures emerge from the mist on both sides of the ancient stones.

They came alone and in families. They came limping and carried. They came wrapped in the grey cloaks of the road-poor and the fine coats of merchants who had bought their safety one lie at a time. They came from the salt flats and the river country, from the iron valleys and the coastal towns where the crown's historians had never bothered to look because no one had ever told them there was anything worth erasing.

They came because a burned woman and a swordspeaker had walked the length of the dragon-road telling anyone who would listen: *You are not the only one.*

* * *

The first to arrive were the Ogunlesi family from the river country — a grandmother, two grown sons, and a girl of perhaps twelve who kept touching her own wrist as if checking it was still there. Their village had been purged fourteen years ago for harboring a woman who could make bread rise without yeast. Not swordspeaking. Bread.

"They took her on a Tuesday," the grandmother told me, in the flat voice of someone reciting something rehearsed against grief. "Market day. She'd sold out early. They said she'd spoken an oath over the flour." Her hands worked at each other. "We never learned what happened to her. We weren't allowed to ask. Asking became... dangerous."

"Her name," I said, and opened my ledger.

"Ayoola. Ayoola Ogunlesi."

I wrote it down. The ink seemed louder than it should have been.

By midmorning there were forty of us. By noon, ninety. Mara stood at the edge of the assembly with his sword-hand bandaged and his face doing something complicated every time another survivor spoke a name he wrote into the growing ledger. He had been quiet since the ford — quieter than even his usual careful silence — and I had caught him more than once testing his own memory the way a man tests a loose tooth, pressing his tongue against the absence of things.

He did not remember his surname anymore. He had told me this on the second night of our flight, in the matter-of-fact tone of a man reporting weather. *I traded it,* he'd said. *It bought us past the Iron House wards.* And then he had looked at me with an expression I could not read, and I had understood that he was waiting to see if I would grieve for him, and that my grieving or not-grieving mattered to him more than he wanted it to.

I had not grieved. I had written *Mara* in my ledger under the heading of *living witnesses*, and I had thought: *there is a man who pays for other people's memories with his own, and calls it fair.*

* * *

A one-eyed ferryman named Tunde arrived at midday with a fragment of a royal proclamation nailed to a board, torn from a market post. He had pried it off himself, he said, the night after his brother was taken.

"It says here," Tunde announced, holding the board up before the assembly like a priest elevating a relic, "that swordspeaking is a corruption of the natural order, first recorded in the reign of King Adewale the Pious, and that its practitioners brought the dragons' fire upon innocent kingdoms in the ancient war."

Murmurs ran through the crowd. An old woman near me spat.

"I knew a woman who remembered her grandfather speaking oaths," she said. "Peacefully. For harvests. For healing fevers. Nobody's kingdom burned."

"The proclamation is wrong," I said, "and we are going to prove it is wrong, publicly, in front of the army that is coming to enforce it."

That got their attention. Ninety-odd faces turned toward me, and I felt the old terror rise in my throat — the terror of being looked at, of being *counted*, which for people like us had always been the same as being hunted. But I made myself stand straighter, and I made my voice carry.

"You have all been told you are alone," I said. "You have all been told your testimony is worthless, or worse, that it never happened. The crown has spent three hundred years teaching this country to forget itself. Well." I held up the seal, and the dragon-scale caught the noon light and threw it back in a green shimmer across the assembled faces. "Something remembers. And now, so do we."

* * *

They came forward one at a time after that, to the folding table Mara had set up beneath the largest of the dragon-road stones. Each gave a name, a date, a place, a fragment. A cooper from the eastern hills whose father had whispered oaths to keep the cellar dry. A pair of sisters whose mother had been taken for singing songs that made wounds close. A former soldier, missing two fingers, who had deserted rather than burn a village of gift-holders and now could not enter a town without shaking.

Mara wrote until his bandaged hand bled through the linen. I watched him flex it between entries, watched him wince, and understood: the half-oath at the ford had started the consumption, and every word he wrote in service of others' memories was a word spent against his own. When I touched his shoulder he flinched, then covered the flinch with a smile that fooled neither of us.

"How much left?" I asked quietly.

"Enough," he said.

"That's not an answer."

"No," he agreed. "It isn't." He turned back to the table. "Next."

* * *

Toward evening, a cart arrived bearing an old man so bent he seemed folded in half, and when they lifted him down he stood swaying in the last light and looked at the dragon-road stones with an expression I recognized: the look of a person seeing a thing they had only known as story.

"This is real," he said. "My grandmother used to say the road went all the way to the mountain. I thought she was making it grander than it was. People do that. Make the lost things grander so the losing hurts less."

"What was her name?" I asked.

"Folake." His eyes shone. "Folake Adeyemi. She could calm storms with a word. Small ones. Just enough to save the boats." He gripped my arm with surprising strength. "They came for her when I was six. I hid in the flour barrel. I heard everything."

And then he told us. All of it. The boots, the door breaking, his grandmother's voice saying one small word — not a battle-word, a *gentle* word, a word like a hand laid on a frightened animal — and the soldiers stumbling, confused, giving her the four seconds she needed to push the barrel with the boy in it out the back and into the reeds. Four seconds. One gentle word. A life.

I wrote it all down. Folake Adeyemi. Storm-calmer. Died protecting a grandson who spent sixty years thinking he had imagined her.

When I finished, the old man looked at the entry for a long time. Then he said, wonderingly: "She existed."

"She existed," I confirmed.

"Someone will know that? After?"

"After," I promised, "everyone will know."

* * *

Night fell, and we built fires along the road — small ones, disciplined, the fires of people who had learned what unwatched flame can cost. Survivors sat in clusters, trading fragments the way traders trade grain, and I moved among them with my ledger open, and the pages filled, and filled, and filled.

It was near midnight when Veyra came to me.

She stood at the edge of the firelight in her watch-leathers, stripped of insignia, looking like a woman who had cut herself out of her own life and was still deciding whether the wound was fatal. In her arms she carried a satchel of documents — the crown's campaign plans, the Iron House key, and, she told me later, a stack of interrogation transcripts she had stolen from her own desk.

"I brought you something else," she said, and handed me a slim volume bound in grey.

I opened it. Names. Hundreds of them. Dates, villages, methods. Written in a clerk's precise hand — the informant's hand, the one Mara had found evidence of, but these were not the informant's records of *finding* gift-holders. These were the informant's private tally of everyone he had destroyed, kept, I think, the way some men keep trophies and some men keep sins: because he could not stop counting.

"Why give me this?" I asked.

Veyra stared into the fire. "Because I helped build the machine that made those lists necessary," she said. "And because when the tribunal comes — and it will come, Ishola, whatever else happens tomorrow — I want it on record that I chose. Nobody made me choose. I want that written down somewhere that isn't the crown's ink."

I looked at her for a long moment. Then I opened my ledger to a fresh page and wrote: *Veyra, hunter turned witness. Chose freely.*

She read it upside down, and something in her face loosened, just slightly, like a knot worked almost free.

* * *

Dawn came grey and cold, and with it came the sound.

Not drums. Not horns. Something worse: the creak of many wheels, the jingle of harness, the vast slow respiration of an army waking. From the ridge above the dragon-road we could see them — banners in the crown's colors, ranks glittering, supply wagons stretching back toward the horizon like a serpent deciding whether to swallow.

The survivors gathered at the road's edge and looked, and I watched fear move through them like wind through wheat. The cooper began to shake. The sisters held each other. The old man who had hidden in the flour barrel sixty years ago stood very straight and said, to no one in particular:

"Well. She didn't hide me in that barrel so I could run from kings."

Mara appeared beside me, pale, his bandage dark again. He looked at the army, then at the growing stack of ledgers and testimonies bound together on the table behind us — the true ledger, nearly whole, waiting only for Sefu's guild-book and whatever fragments remained locked in Mara's own consumed memory.

"It's going to work," I said. It was half question.

"It's going to *cost*," he said. Which was, I was learning, how he said yes.

Behind us, the keeper of the flame emerged from the shrine path, ancient and unhurried, carrying a clay lamp in which the last dragon-fire guttered like a candle in a draft. The keeper looked at the army, and at us, and at the ledger, and said only:

"The fire is listening. It has been listening for three hundred years. What remains is for someone to say the words — and for the book to be true."

I put my hand flat on the bound testimonies. Two hundred and six names from Ilorin-under-the-Hill. Ayoola Ogunlesi, who made bread rise. Folake Adeyemi, who calmed a storm for four seconds. Veyra, who chose. Hundreds more, arriving still, stragglers coming up the road even now with their scraps of paper and their memorized griefs.

The army crested the ridge.

And down the far side of the valley, dust rising, a single rider came fast — carrying a package wrapped in oilcloth, and riding like a man pursued by his own conscience.

"Sefu's courier," Mara breathed beside me. "The guild ledger. It's come."

The rider was still a mile out. The army was closer than that.
