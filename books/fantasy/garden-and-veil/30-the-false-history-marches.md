## 30. The False History Marches

I had marched behind a lot of banners in my life, but I had never before marched behind a banner that was lying.

That's not quite true. All banners lie, in the way that all uniforms lie — they say *we are one thing*, when in fact we are four thousand separate hungers wrapped in the same dye. But this banner lied in a new and specific way. It bore the crowned flame of the Reclamation, stitched in gold thread so fresh it still smelled of the workshop, and beneath it the motto the historians' office had issued that spring: *THE FIRE RETURNS TO ITS RIGHTFUL KEEPERS.*

Rightful keepers. I turned the phrase over as the column wound up through the foothills, and it turned over like a coin with two tails.

I rode at the head of the vanguard because that is where you put a hunter — out front, where she can smell trouble before it smells her — and because General Ossory liked having me visible. A swordspeaker-hunter riding openly under the crown's colors was itself a piece of propaganda. *See,* the sight of me said to every village we passed, *even their own kind serve us now.* I had been that sentence for eleven years. I had mostly stopped hearing it, the way you stop hearing your own name called in a market square.

I heard it now. The mountain did that. The dragon-road ahead of us had a way of making a person listen to things she'd trained herself not to.

"Veyra." Ossory reined up beside me. He was a broad, patient man with the face of someone who had once been kind and had filed it down for practical reasons. "Report."

"The road narrows at the shrine approach," I said. "Two hundred men abreast becomes forty. If they've fortified it—"

"They haven't." He said it comfortably, the way he said everything, and I understood that his comfort was policy. Doubt was inefficient. "A handful of peasants, a burned woman, and a disgraced archivist do not fortify a mountain."

"A handful of peasants held the Salt Ford for three days during the Border War."

"And were slaughtered for it," Ossory said pleasantly. "History remembers who wins, Captain. That is rather the point of this entire expedition."

There it was again. *Rather the point.* I had spent weeks now carrying a splinter of knowledge under my skin — the manufactured evidence, the forged reports, the hunt for swordspeakers that had never really been a hunt for swordspeakers but a census, an inventory, a rounding-up of anything that remembered wrongly — and every time I tried to look at it straight, Ossory's voice was there, smooth as oiled leather: *history remembers who wins.*

The problem, which I had not yet said aloud to anyone, was that I no longer believed we would win. Not because of odds. Because of the flame.

* * *

We made camp that night in the ruins of a village that appeared on no map older than twenty years.

I want to be precise about that, because precision was all I had left. The maps showed a quarry and a woodcutter's track. The ground showed foundations. Forty of them, maybe fifty, stone footings softened by moss, a well with a good rope still coiled on its lip, an orchard gone wild but unmistakably planted in rows. Somebody had lived here for generations. Somebody had *named* this place, and the name was gone — not changed, not forgotten the way names fade, but gone the way a page is gone when it has been cut out and the binding smoothed over.

My lieutenant, a boy named Doss with a farmer's shoulders and a scholar's squint, stood beside me looking at the orchard.

"Odd," he said. "Fruit trees don't volunteer in rows."

"No," I said. "They don't."

"You think there was a fire?"

"I think there was whatever leaves no ash." I said it quietly, and Doss looked at me with the particular wariness soldiers develop toward officers who start saying strange things, and I smiled the smile I'd been wearing for eleven years, and he went back to his tents.

But I walked the village until full dark. I put my hand on the well-rope. I stood in what had been a doorway — you could tell by the threshold stone, worn concave by feet — and I tried to remember my orders, the official ones, the ones read to us at muster:

*The dragon-road was seized by oath-bound rebels in the last year of the Border War. They perverted the sacred flame to treasonous ends. The crown reclaims its inheritance.*

And underneath the words, faint as watermark, another memory stirred — one I hadn't asked for, one that had been growing since the ford, since I watched Mara Oyelaran turn a river to warm mist with half an oath and a face full of grief. The memory said: *you knew a woman from a village like this one. You knew her name. You sat in her kitchen.*

I couldn't find her. I reached for her the way you reach for a word on the tip of your tongue, and there was nothing — just the shape of the reaching, the certainty that something had been there. That frightened me more than the army's numbers ever could. Not because I feared what I'd lost, but because I understood, standing in a dead man's doorway in a village with no name, exactly how it had been lost. Not torn out. *Smoothed over.* And if it could be done to a village, and done to me—

Then the histories were not records. They were wounds being kept open on purpose, or closed on purpose, whichever served.

* * *

Ossory summoned me to his pavilion at midnight. I knew why before I entered. The scouts had come back.

Inside, the map table glowed under lamplight, and around it stood the men who mattered: Ossory; Magister Corvel of the historians' office, a dry stick of a man whose ink-stained fingers looked like they'd been dipped to the knuckle in old blood; and Captain Renholt of the Iron House detachment, who had brought thirty wardens and a wheeled cage that I pretended not to have looked at too long.

"The shrine is occupied," the scout reported. "Perhaps sixty. Survivors of the purges, by their dress — salt-flat clans, river folk, hill people. They're not fortifying. They're—" he hesitated, embarrassed by his own report, "—they're writing, sir. Copying papers. Binding them."

"Binding them," Corvel repeated, with the distaste of a man watching amateurs handle surgical tools.

"There's a ledger on a stand at the head of the road," the scout went on. "Big one. People keep coming up to it and speaking, and scribes write down what they say."

Silence around the table. Then Renholt laughed, short and ugly. "They're building a book to stop an army."

"They're building a *record*," Corvel said softly, and something in his voice made me look at him properly for the first time. There was fear in it. Real fear, the kind that doesn't care who sees. "Captain, do not mistake paper for weakness. Paper is the only thing that outlives a victory. We are here to make sure that what outlives us is *ours*."

He caught himself then, smoothed his voice back into bureaucratic gravel, and turned to the map. "The flame answers an oath spoken with a true ledger present. That is the old doctrine; our researches confirm it. Therefore the operation has two objectives, in order: the ledger, and the woman Ishola, who carries the seal. The flame itself cannot be moved while guttering. Once the ledger is destroyed and the seal secured, the historians' office will install a corrected account at the shrine, and the flame will answer *us*. Gentlemen — we are not burning a book tomorrow. We are writing one."

Everyone nodded. Everyone always nodded. I nodded too, and inside my chest something lay down flat and cold, like a body.

Because I finally saw the whole design, end to end, the way you see a pattern in a carpet only when you're standing on the wrong side of the room. The hunt for swordspeakers. The purges. The erased villages. The cage on wheels. It had never been about suppressing the gift. It was about *monopolizing* it. You don't kill the thing that can rewrite memory — you gather up everyone who might compete, burn the true ledgers, and then hold the only pen left in the world.

And I was riding at the front of it, wearing its colors, being its sentence in the market squares.

"Veyra." Ossory's eyes found mine across the lamp. "You know the road. You know their swordspeaker — Oyelaran. Assessment."

"He'll speak the oath," I said. My voice came out steady, which surprised me. "If the ledger is real, he'll speak it. He's already paid half his hand trying. Men who pay that much don't stop at the door."

"Then we arrive before he finds the courage," Ossory said. "We march at dawn. Vanguard takes the switchbacks. Veyra — you have leave to engage him if he begins any working. Kill him cleanly. The crown prefers its miracles interrupted."

*Cleanly.* As if there were a clean way to kill a man who was only trying to say a true thing out loud.

"Yes, General," I said.

* * *

I didn't sleep. I told myself it was the campaign habit, the old soldier's trick of resting with one ear open, but the truth was simpler and worse: I kept seeing the cage.

It sat at the rear of the column, iron-barred, padded inside — the padding was the detail that undid me, the *consideration* of it, the way cruelty had learned manners. It had been built for Ishola. Renholt had described its workings to me over supper with the pride of a craftsman: wards keyed to suppress speech, so that a prisoner inside it could not form the words of an oath even in her own skull. *She can think,* he'd said, *but she can't say.* And I had eaten my stew and nodded, because that is what I did, that is what I had been doing for eleven years, nodding while the world was smoothed over.

Somewhere past midnight I gave up on the tent and walked the picket line. The army sprawled below the last ridge — cookfires like fallen stars, four thousand men breathing in the dark, farriers working late, a chaplain murmuring over a nervous boy. From the ridge you could see the dragon-road climbing ahead of us, a pale seam of ancient paving winding up toward the shrine, and above it, faint as breath on glass, a reddish glow where the last dragon-fire guttered in its mountain cradle.

Four thousand against sixty copyists. It should have been a joke. Instead it felt like watching a tide prepare to erase something written in sand, and knowing — *knowing*, the way I had known at the ford — that the writing mattered more than the tide.

Doss found me at the ridge. He did that sometimes; he had a farm boy's instinct for when an animal was unhappy.

"Can't sleep either, Captain?"

"Something like that."

He looked up at the glow. "Is it true what the priests say? That the fire used to talk? That dragons answered oaths?"

"It's true."

"My grandmother swore her village was spared in the Border War because an oath-bound spoke for them. She used to say the word — the first word, the opening one." He said it then, quietly, half-embarrassed, a child's word kept alive in a kitchen for forty years: "*Ashen-veil.*"

The world tilted. Just slightly. Just enough.

Because I knew that word. Not from training, not from the hunters' manuals that catalogued forbidden phrases like botanical specimens. I knew it the way you know a lullaby — from somewhere warm, from a kitchen, from a woman whose name had been smoothed over, a woman who had said that word to a frightened girl with a gift she didn't understand and told her: *if you ever must use it, use it to protect, never to punish, and never let them make you ashamed.*

"Your grandmother," I managed. "What was her name?"

Doss frowned. The frown deepened. He opened his mouth, closed it, and I watched the exact moment the smoothing took hold of him — watched him reach for a name and find the reaching empty, watched the confusion settle into the comfortable blankness they bred into us. "Funny," he said slowly. "I call her Gran. I always call her Gran. Why can't I—"

"Doss." I gripped his shoulder. "Listen to me. Tomorrow, when we go up that road — whatever you see, whatever you're ordered to do — hold onto that word. *Ashen-veil.* Say it to yourself. Don't let go of it the way you let go of her name. Do you understand me?"

He stared at me, frightened now, a man feeling the edges of a hole he couldn't see. "Captain, what's happening to us?"

"That's what tomorrow is about," I said. "That's exactly what tomorrow is about."

* * *

We broke camp before light.

The column formed in the grey dark with the discipline of a machine built over centuries for exactly this purpose: the advance companies, the pike blocks, the wagons, the historians' traveling press with its crates of *corrected accounts* ready to be nailed to every post between here and the capital, and at the rear, creaking on its axles, the cage.

I rode at the point, as always. Ahead of us the dragon-road climbed into the dawn mist, and somewhere up there a burned woman was reciting names, and a man with a ruined sword-hand was deciding whether to say the most dangerous word in the world, and sixty people with nothing but paper were preparing to meet four thousand spears.

Ossory drew beside me and raised his arm for the advance.

"One more thing, Captain," he said, almost gently. "If you see anything unusual up there — anything that troubles your certainties — remember your oath to the crown. It was the first oath you ever spoke. It will hold."

It was meant as reassurance. It landed like a diagnosis.

The arm came down. The horns sounded. Four thousand men began to climb, and I rode in front of them with a word in my mouth — *ashen-veil*, *ashen-veil* — holding it the way you hold a coal in cupped hands, walking into the smoke of a fire that was about to wake up and remember everything.

Above us, thin and clear across the morning air, carried down the whole length of the dragon-road, we heard a bell begin to ring.

Not the market bell. Not any bell of the crown.

It rang like a ledger opening.
