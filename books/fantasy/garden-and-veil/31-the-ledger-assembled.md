## 31. The Ledger Assembled

There is a particular smell to a room where people are trying to make something true. I know it well — the guildhouse had it, under the dust and the tallow and Sefu's pipe smoke. It's ink and fear and the faint sweetness of paper that has been handled too much by hands that were afraid of it.

The shrine chamber smelled of all three that night, plus dragon-fire, which smells like a forge having an argument with a thunderstorm.

We had cleared the long table beneath the Keeper's guttering flame and covered it with everything we owned in the way of truth. It did not look like much. Truth rarely does, laid out flat. It looks like scraps. It looks like a beggar's coat cut into patches.

"Right," I said, because someone had to say something, and I have always been the sort of coward who fills silence with administration. "Let's see what we've got."

What we'd got:

Forty-one testimonies, written or spoken, from survivors of purges most of them could only half remember. That was the cruelty of it — the crown's historians hadn't just burned villages, they'd burned the remembering of them, so half our witnesses kept apologizing for their own memories. *I think there was a river,* one woman said. *I think my brother's name started with an O.* Ishola sat beside each of them and wrote down what they had, exactly as they had it, including the *I thinks*. She refused, violently, to smooth anything over. "A ledger that flatters is just a lie with good posture," she told me, and I wrote that down too, because it deserved preserving even if it never made the final text.

Sefu's guild ledger, delivered by a dying courier who had ridden three days on a horse that died under him at the door. The courier lived. The horse got a burial. I'm not sure which of those facts Sefu would have found more fitting.

And my fragments. My poor, moth-eaten memory of my own life: the mentor whose face I had traded away at the Iron House, his name — Adewale, I still had the name, I clung to it like a man clinging to a rail in a flood — the oath he made me swear, the words of it, half-eaten now, the shape of his voice without the voice itself.

I spread it all out and looked at it the way you look at a wound before you clean it.

* * *

Binding a true ledger is not like binding any other book. I should say that plainly, because the histories — the real ones, the ones we were about to feed to a fire older than kingdoms — will be read by people who assume a ledger is a neutral thing, a list, a clerk's chore. It isn't. A true ledger is an argument you make against forgetting, and every argument has a structure, and the structure is where you can fail.

I learned binding from Adewale, back when I still had the memories of learning it. He used to say: *the page remembers what the hand meant when it touched it.* At the time I thought this was mysticism dressed up as stationery advice. Now I had watched a dragon-scale seal show me a war that never officially happened, and I had watched a flame answer to testimony, and I no longer thought anything was merely stationery.

The problem was order. Where do you begin a history the crown has spent two hundred years unwriting? With the war? With the first purge? With Adewale's death in a ditch outside a town whose name I'd also sold?

"With the sword," said Ishola.

She was sitting across the table from me, her burn-scarred hands flat on the wood, and she had that look she gets — the one that means she has been thinking about something for hours and has decided, out of mercy, to let me catch up.

"The remembering sword," she said. "Chapter one. The guild recorded it. You told me — a sword that remembers its kills. If your guild could record impossible things honestly, then the argument isn't that remembering is possible. The argument is that it was always possible, and they made it criminal."

"That's not how ledgers work," I said. "You start with accounts. Dates."

"You're not writing an account," she said. "You're writing a case. And a case starts with proof that the truth existed before anyone tried to kill it."

I hate being corrected. I especially hate being correctly corrected. I made a note of the feeling too, because the ledger was going to contain everything tonight, apparently, including my wounded professional pride.

* * *

We worked until the candles drowned in their own wax, and then we worked by dragon-fire light, which turns everyone the color of old bronze and makes even the frightened look like statues of heroes. There were perhaps thirty of us in the chamber at various points — survivors, the Keeper drifting between us like a rumor, Veyra standing guard at the door with her sword drawn and her face doing something complicated whenever anyone thanked her.

Veyra brought the crown's campaign plans, and we nearly didn't include them.

"It's their document," she said, setting it down. "Their orders. Their lists of names to erase." She tapped a column. "This is a schedule. Villages, dates, which memories go first. They do the soft ones early — festivals, songs, small griefs. Then the hard ones. Then the people."

"Should it go in?" asked a miller named Tunde, whose village had been emptied so thoroughly he was its only surviving witness and couldn't remember his wife's face. "It's their truth, not ours."

"It's evidence," Veyra said.

"It's poison," Tunde said.

They looked at me. This is the occupational hazard of having been a ledger-keeper: people assume you arbitrate. I picked up the campaign plans and held them a while.

"We put it in," I said finally, "at the end. In an appendix. Unbound from the main text — loose pages, sewn in separately, so anyone reading can choose whether to touch it." I looked at Tunde. "Your wife's name goes in the main body. First page after the sword. Their machine goes in the back, where it belongs — documented, contained, and not allowed to organize our story for us."

Tunde thought about it. Then he nodded, once, the way men nod when they've been given back a small piece of control over something enormous.

Later, sewing the appendix in with a separate thread — red thread, the Keeper's suggestion, "so the stitching itself testifies" — I caught Veyra watching me.

"You're enjoying this," she said. Not accusingly. Wonderingly.

"I am having the worst week of my life," I said, "and yes. A little. Is that monstrous?"

"No," she said. "I think it might be the point. They took the joy out of records. Made them instruments. You're making one that's — " she searched for the word — "generous."

Nobody had ever described my work as generous. I turned back to the sewing before she could see what the word did to my face.

* * *

The hard part came at midnight, and it wasn't the sewing or the ordering or the forty-one testimonies. It was Sefu's ledger.

I had avoided opening it since the courier arrived. There is a difference between knowing a thing and reading it in a dead man's careful handwriting, and Sefu is not dead, which somehow made it worse — he was alive, somewhere south of here, walking toward us to stand trial before a tribunal of the people he'd tried to sell, and his ledger was in my hands, and every page of it was a confession he hadn't yet had the courage to speak aloud.

He had recorded everything. Of course he had. He was a guildmaster; recording was the whole of his religion. He had recorded the bargain with the crown's informant, the price agreed for Ishola's surrender — *one guildhouse, continued, in perpetuity* — and he had recorded, in the margin, in smaller writing, as if the ink itself were ashamed: *I believed they would keep their word. I knew better. I chose to believe.*

And then, further in, the thing that stopped my hands for the better part of an hour:

*Mara's page. The boy burned his own history rather than let the informant find it. I have the original. I have always had the originals. I copy everything before I let anyone destroy anything. It is the one dishonest honesty of my office.*

He had my page. The page I burned in chapter fourteen of my life — the guild history, my induction, Adewale's signature, the oath witnessed and dated. The page the Keeper said the true ledger required. The page I had grieved like a limb.

I sat there holding Sefu's ledger and felt something I didn't have a clean name for. Fury, partly. He had watched me sacrifice my own past and said nothing, let me believe it gone forever, because — why? Because his guilt needed company? Because some clerk's instinct toward completeness outweighed any duty to tell a frightened boy he hadn't actually lost what he thought he'd lost?

And underneath the fury, worse: relief. Enormous, shameful relief. We had the page. The ledger could be whole.

"Mara." Ishola's voice, quiet. "You've stopped breathing."

"Sefu kept copies," I said. "Of everything. Including the page I burned."

She was silent a moment. "That's either a miracle or an obscenity."

"Both," I said. "It's usually both, with him."

I copied the page into the new ledger myself. My hand shook through Adewale's signature, so that his name came out looking like it was written during an earthquake, which, in fairness, it might as well have been. When I finished, I sat back and looked at the assembled thing for the first time.

* * *

Here is what a true ledger looks like when it's done, in case you ever bind one:

It looks unfinished. That's the first thing. Every honest record does, because honesty is a snapshot of a wound that's still bleeding. Our ledger had testimonies that trailed off mid-sentence. It had Tunde's *I think her name was Bisi, I think, I think* repeated four times like a man knocking on a door nobody answers. It had gaps where entire villages should have been, marked only with the crown's own erasure-orders as tombstones: *record destroyed by royal decree, nothing recoverable.* It had my fragments, stitched between stronger cloth, Adewale's voice present only as an outline, a negative space shaped like a mentor.

It had Ishola's testimony at the center, because the others had insisted, over her loud objection. Her village. The purge. The word only swordspeakers know, spoken through burned lips at the guildhouse door. She had fought being central the way she fought everything — quietly, stubbornly, with the full weight of a person who believes she survived by accident and doesn't deserve the podium.

"The ledger isn't about you," I told her, when she objected the fourth time.

"Good," she said.

"It's about what was done to you, which is different, and which you don't get to refuse on behalf of the other forty."

She glared at me. I have been glared at by guildmasters, magistrates, and one actual dragon-cultist, and Ishola's glare ranks in the top three. But she let it stand.

The last thing we added was a blank page at the very front. Blank, except for a single line in the Keeper's ancient hand: *For whatever is still missing, and whoever is still coming.*

"A ledger with a blank page is an invitation to doubt," I said.

"A ledger without one is a lie," the Keeper said. "No record is finished. Only closed by force." The Keeper looked at me with eyes that had watched centuries and found most of them wanting. "Leave the door open, swordspeaker. Even after the fire. Especially after the fire."

* * *

Near dawn, we bound it.

Not with glue — glue is for books that want to stay shut. We bound it with cord soaked in the dragon-fire's light, or as close as the Keeper could manage, which turned out to be cord left overnight on the shrine's altar until it seemed to hold a warmth that had nothing to do with temperature. Each survivor who wished to touched the cover before it was sewn shut. Some wept. Some laughed, horribly, the laugh of people who have carried something alone too long and felt the weight leave. Tunde pressed his whole palm flat and left it there until Ishola gently moved it.

When it was done, it weighed more than it should have. I don't mean mystically — well, I do, but also literally. Everyone who lifted it remarked on it. Forty-one testimonies, one guildmaster's confession, one burned-and-restored page, one crown appendix in red thread, and a blank page at the front, and the whole thing heavier than the sum of its paper, as though truth has mass after all, as though every erased name re-added its weight when you wrote it back.

"So," said Veyra, arms crossed, looking at it the way soldiers look at maps of unwinnable battles. "Now what? You have a book. They have an army."

"Now," I said, and my voice did something I didn't authorize — it steadied, the way it used to when I spoke near the old gift, before the gift was mostly ash in me, "we open it in front of the flame, and I say the words."

"You can't," Veyra said. Not cruelly. Factually. "The half-oath ate your memories. The Keeper said the fire answers a full oath spoken with a true ledger — but it also needs a speaker who can hold the whole of it. Can you?"

I wanted to lie. Lying to Veyra would have been easy; she expects soldiers to manage morale with falsehoods, it's practically doctrine. Instead I looked at the ledger, at the blank first page waiting behind the Keeper's line, at the space where my certainty should have been and where instead there was a hole shaped like everything I'd paid.

"No," I said. "I don't know if I can. I've lost the mentor's face, my surname, half the oath's cadence. What I have is the ledger, and forty-one people who remembered when remembering was illegal, and a fire that supposedly honors exactly that kind of stubbornness." I picked up the book. It pulled at my arms like a living thing. "If the fire wants a perfect speaker, it will be disappointed. If it wants a true record and someone willing to stand in front of an army and read it aloud — "

Outside, far off across the salt flats, horns. Not one horn. Many, layered, the sound of a host coordinating, and under them, fainter, the ground-tremor of ten thousand boots keeping time.

Everyone in the chamber went still. The dragon-fire guttered, then stood up straighter, as if it too had heard.

"They'll reach the valley by midmorning," Veyra said, already moving to the door. "Maybe sooner. They march fast when they think history's on their side."

Ishola came and stood beside me, and put her scarred hand on the ledger's cover, next to mine.

"Then we'd best finish the last page," she said.

I frowned. "The last page is sewn. Everything's bound."

"Not that kind of page." She nodded toward the blank sheet at the front, the Keeper's invitation. "Whatever happens out there this morning — that goes in the book too. Win or lose. That's what a true ledger is, Mara. It doesn't stop at the parts you planned."

The horns sounded again, closer, and I gathered the heavy, warm, unfinished truth of forty-one lives into my arms, and followed her out to meet the morning.
