## 32. Sefu's Atonement

I have spent sixty years learning how to lie with a ledger, and it turns out the hardest thing I ever wrote was the truth.

The road up the dragon-road is longer than I remembered, or perhaps my legs are shorter than they used to be. Both are probably true. The mountain does not care either way. It sits there under its veil of cloud like a creditor who knows you cannot pay, patient in the way that only very old and very certain things are patient.

I brought a mule. Her name is Bitterleaf, because she bit me the day I bought her and has not bitten me since, which is more courtesy than most of my associates have shown me lately. She carries everything I own that matters: two blankets, a kettle, a change of clothes, and a ledger bound in grey goatskin that weighs more than any book has a right to weigh.

It weighs what guilt weighs. I checked. Guilt, as it happens, is roughly four hundred pages at standard guildhouse paper stock, plus whatever the ink adds when you have written names in it that should never have been written at all.

The courier reached me eleven days ago. He was dying when he arrived — a knife wound gone to rot, the kind of wound that comes from being on the wrong side of someone's desperation — and he pressed the ledger into my hands with both of his, which were shaking, and said, "The boy said you'd know what to do with it."

Mara. He still calls me Guildmaster in his head, I'd wager. Or worse — he calls me *Sefu*, plain, the way you say the name of someone who disappointed you and whom you have decided to stop hating because hatred takes up room you need for other things.

I knew what to do with the ledger. That was the terrible part. There was no mystery in it, no agonized deliberation by candlelight. I looked at the grey goatskin and I knew, the way you know a debt has come due, the way you know rain before it falls. What to *do* was never the question.

The question was whether I would be the man who did it.

* * *

Let me tell you about bargains, since I have made more of them than anyone alive ought to be permitted.

A bargain is a beautiful thing when you are making it. Everything is clean. You hold your piece of the world in one hand, the other party holds theirs, and for one shining moment the future looks like arithmetic. Two things exchanged, both parties walking away whole. I built the Guild of Impossible Things on bargains. Every impossible thing we kept safe was kept safe because someone, somewhere, had decided that sheltering it was worth the price, and I was very good at making sure the price got paid by people who could afford it.

That is the lie I told myself for thirty years: *the price gets paid by those who can afford it.*

Here is what actually happens. The price gets paid by whoever is standing closest when the bill arrives. And I made very sure, over the years, that I was never standing closest.

When the crown came for the swordspeakers, I stood far away. When they came for the memory-keepers, I stood farther. When they came for Ishola — a burned woman who had walked out of a purge with nothing but her skin half-gone and a seal she could barely carry — I stood close enough to smell the ash on her, and I picked up a quill, and I negotiated.

I told myself it was mercy. I have told myself so many mercies over the years that I keep them in a mental inventory, catalogued and cross-referenced, and this one is filed under *Sefu, self-deceptions, catastrophic*. Surrender the woman, save the guild. One life against forty. Any merchant would make that trade. Any sensible man.

The guild was hollowed anyway. Did I mention that? Of course I didn't; I haven't mentioned it to anyone, because there was no one left to mention it to. I handed Ishola to the crown's men on a wet Tuesday, and I watched them put her in an iron cart, and I went back inside and stood in my own hall among my own shelves, and I felt it — the emptiness. Not the absence of her. The absence of everything the bargain was supposed to protect. The guild had already been gutted, quietly, page by page, informant by informant, and I had been so busy guarding the walls that I never noticed the house inside them was gone. I had traded a woman for a shell and called it survival.

Bitterleaf stops at a bend in the road and I let her, because my knees have opinions and they are all hostile. Below us the valley opens up, and there they are.

The camp of the erased.

I had imagined — I don't know what I had imagined. Tents, maybe. A scattering of fugitives, huddled, hungry, the way the dispossessed are supposed to look in the stories. Instead there is something almost orderly about it. Cookfires in rows. A rope-line where people are hanging washing. Children — gods, children — chasing each other between the shelters with the particular shrieking joy that belongs exclusively to children who do not yet know what was taken from them.

And everywhere, everywhere, people writing. Sitting on stones, on cart-tails, on their own heels, scratching testimony into whatever paper they have. Some of them write with the careful slowness of the unpracticed. Some write fast, as if the words might escape if they don't pin them down. An old man near the path dictates to a young woman who takes it down word for word, nodding, not softening anything.

They are building a ledger. I can see it from here. They are building the thing I spent thirty years pretending to build.

"Well," I say to Bitterleaf. "This will go splendidly."

She flicks an ear at me. She has no talent for reassurance, which I respect.

* * *

They see me coming long before I reach the camp, of course. A lone man on a mule, leading with a ledger, is either a fool or a threat, and the erased have learned to assume both until proven otherwise. By the time I pass the first cookfire I am being watched by perhaps thirty people, and none of them are subtle about it, and I find — this surprises me — that I don't mind. Let them look. Looking is honest. I have been looked *past* for years, smiled at and deferred to and quietly discounted, and there is something restful about being seen plainly as a problem.

A woman steps into my path. Broad-shouldered, grey-streaked hair braided tight, hands like someone who has done real work with them. She looks at me, then at the ledger, then at me again.

"You're the guildmaster," she says. "Of the Impossible Things."

"I'm the former guildmaster," I say. "Of the former guild."

"Is it true you gave Ishola to the crown?"

There it is. No preamble, no courtesies. I decide immediately that I like her.

"It's true," I say. "I negotiated the terms myself. I was thorough. I got good terms." My voice does something strange on the last words, cracks along a seam I didn't know was there. "I always get good terms. That's the problem. I've been getting good terms my entire life, and every one of them was paid for by someone standing closer to the fire than me."

She studies me for a long moment. Behind her, the watching faces do not soften and do not harden. They simply wait, the way people wait who have learned that the next thing said matters more than the last hundred things said to them.

"What's in the book?" she asks.

"The truth," I say. "All of it. The purges I knew about and stayed quiet about. The names I sold. The bargains. Including the one for Ishola — dates, terms, the name of the officer I dealt with. And the guild's own records going back to its founding, which include things the crown would burn the mountain to destroy." I pat the goatskin cover. It is warm from the sun and heavier than ever. "I'm told the oath requires a true ledger. This one damns me on nearly every page. I'm hoping that makes it more useful, not less."

"And why," she says, and her voice is not cruel, which somehow makes it worse, "should we believe a man who admits to thirty years of lying?"

I had prepared an answer for this. I had prepared several, actually, because old habits die hard and I had rehearsed this whole journey in front of my kettle like a man auditioning for his own redemption. But standing here, with the smoke of their cookfires in my nose and the children shouting somewhere behind her, every prepared answer curdles in my mouth.

"I don't know that you should," I say. "Believe me, I mean. Belief is yours to give and I've forfeited the claim to it. But here is what I can offer instead." I swing down off Bitterleaf, which takes longer than it used to and hurts more than I'll admit, and I kneel — actually kneel, in the dirt of their road, with my bad knees screaming — and I set the ledger on the ground between us. "You can read every page. All of you. Before anyone uses it for anything, you read it, and you check it against what you know, and you find my lies if there are any left to find. I surrender the book to the assembly, not to any one person, and certainly not to myself. That's the only kind of honesty I have left. The auditable kind."

Silence. Somewhere a pot lid clatters. The old man who had been dictating has stopped dictating.

The broad-shouldered woman crouches, takes the ledger, and weighs it in her hands like a fishmonger judging a catch. Then she looks at me with something that is not forgiveness — I want to be clear about that, it is absolutely not forgiveness — but might be the raw material forgiveness gets made from, given time and heat.

"The tribunal sits tomorrow," she says. "You'll stand witness against yourself. You'll answer every question they ask, and there will be questions, Sefu of the Guild, and some of them will be asked by people whose names appear in your book."

"I know," I say. "I wrote them there."

"Yes," she says. "You did." She stands. "There's soup. It's bad. You'll eat it anyway."

* * *

The soup is, as promised, bad. It is also the best meal I have eaten in a year, which tells you something about my year.

I sit at the edge of the firelight with my bowl and I listen. That is the discipline I have assigned myself for the evening: listen, speak only when spoken to, and take no notes. The last one is the hardest. Sixty years of habit wants my quill in my hand, wants me recording, ordering, controlling the shape of what happened by deciding what gets written down. I fold my hands instead and let the evening happen without me.

An old woman nearby is telling a girl about a village called Tessen Ford, which does not exist anymore, though the crown's maps still show it. She describes the well. She describes the bell. She describes the morning the soldiers came, in the flat voice of someone who has told it enough times that the telling no longer cuts, and the girl writes it all down, and when the old woman falters on a date the girl says gently, "Take your time. It goes in true or it doesn't go in."

*It goes in true or it doesn't go in.*

I think about my ledgers. All of them. The beautiful ones, the forged ones, the ones with double entries and hidden pages and the elegant little systems of misdirection I invented so that a raiding inspector would find exactly the falsity I wanted him to find. I was proud of those systems. I taught them. Somewhere in the ashes of my guildhouse there may still be a student of mine running a double ledger, believing, as I believed, that the forgery was the shield that kept the truth safe.

But the truth was never safe in my keeping. That is the whole of it. That is the confession underneath the confession. I told myself I lied to protect the truth, the way a man tells himself he drinks to steady his hand. The truth sat in my vaults for thirty years while I traded pieces of it away for the guild's survival, and each trade seemed reasonable, and the sum of all the reasonable trades was a lie the size of a kingdom.

The girl catches me looking and narrows her eyes. "Who are you?" she asks. Not hostile. Just thorough. They're all thorough here. Erasure teaches thoroughness.

"A bad archivist," I say, "come to learn the trade properly."

She considers this, decides it is insufficient, and returns to her writing. Fair enough. I wouldn't believe me either.

Later — much later, when the fires have burned low and the camp has gone quiet except for the sentries' slow rounds — I take out the small notebook I permit myself, the one with no secrets in it, and I write one line:

*Day one. They fed me anyway.*

Then I sit with my back against Bitterleaf's warm flank and I do not sleep, because tomorrow I stand before a tribunal of the people I helped erase, and I open my own book, and I read aloud the worst of what I am, and there is no version of the arithmetic where I come out whole.

And here is the thing I discover, somewhere in the cold hours before dawn, staring at the ember-light on the mountain's veiled shoulder: I am not afraid.

I expected terror. I expected the animal panic of a cornered merchant. Instead there is something else, something I have no ledger-category for. Relief, maybe. Or the specific lightness of a man setting down a weight he forgot he was carrying because he had carried it so long it became part of his spine. Thirty years of bargains, and the only one that ever felt clean was the one where I surrendered everything and asked for nothing.

Redemption through surrender. Who knew. Not me. I had it backwards my whole life. I thought you held on and thereby saved. You let go and thereby become worth saving. Perhaps.

Dawn comes grey and honest. The tribunal convenes under an awning of patched canvas, and the broad-shouldered woman — her name is Adaeze, I learn, and she lost three brothers to a purge I have an entry about — strikes a stone with a bell-hammer, once, and the sound goes through the camp like a pulse.

"Sefu of the Guild of Impossible Things," she reads from a scrap of paper. "You stand as witness against yourself. Do you consent?"

"I consent," I say, and my voice holds, which surprises us both.

I carry the grey ledger to the table. I untie the cord. Four hundred pages of guilt, and every eye in the camp upon it, and somewhere up the mountain a guttering flame waiting for exactly this — for a book with nothing false in it, even if the nothing-false includes me.

I open to the first page. And before I can speak a single word of it, a runner bursts into the camp from the eastern road, mud to the knees, chest heaving, and shouts the words that turn every face toward the valley below:

"The army's crossed the ford. Crown banners. Three days out — maybe less."
