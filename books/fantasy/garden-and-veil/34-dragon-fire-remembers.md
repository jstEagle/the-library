## 34. Dragon-Fire Remembers

*Mara*

I have spoken the oath.

I want to write that down first, before anything else happens, because I suspect that in a few minutes I will not be a reliable narrator of my own life. The ledger is open on the stone before me — the true ledger, bound from forty voices and one confession and whatever shreds of my own memory survived the road — and the words are out of me, all of them, every syllable I swore at nineteen never to say.

The mountain shrine does not applaud. That was my first disappointment. You spend your whole life believing a thing will arrive with trumpets, and then it arrives the way weather arrives: quietly, then all at once.

The guttering flame in the shrine's heart — the flame the Keeper had tended for two hundred years like a widow tending one candle — stood up.

That is the only honest way to describe it. It stood up. It went from something you could cup in two hands to something with a spine, with shoulders, with an opinion. The heat did not push us away; it pulled us in, the way a hearth pulls in a cold child. Ishola's hand found mine. Her palm is ridged with burn-scar, and it is the warmest thing I have ever held.

Then the fire remembered.

I do not know how else to say it. Fire is not supposed to remember. Fire is famously the opposite of remembering; ask any archivist whose archive burned. But this fire had been waiting two centuries inside a scale of dragon-hide for someone to read to it, and now that someone had, it began reciting.

The light came off it in rings. Not heat-rings — memory-rings. Each one passed through me like a wave through shallow water, and each one carried something true: a name, a face, a betrayal, a debt. I felt the ancient war pour back into the valley. Not the war the histories tell — the noble war, the grateful kings, the dragons who died nobly for a crown that loved them. The real one. Kings who signed oaths in dragon-blood and wiped their pens on the treaty. Soldiers ordered to turn on the wings that had carried them. A midnight of knives and a morning of official gratitude.

And beneath all of it, under everything, one sentence repeated like a heartbeat:

*They were ours. We were theirs. They were ours.*

The rings rolled down the mountain, down the dragon-road, into the valley where thirty thousand crown soldiers stood in formation to seize a flame they had been told was a weapon.

I watched them from the shrine steps and I confess — wry narrator, keeper of impossible things — I expected them to burn.

They did not burn.

They *remembered*.

*Veyra*

I was three ranks deep when it hit me, which means I had thirty seconds more than the front line to pretend I was still in command of myself.

I want to be precise about what happened, because afterward no one will believe me, including me. The light came up the valley like a tide of gold, and where it touched, things fell out of my head that I had not known were there.

The first thing was small. My mother's voice, singing a lullaby in a dialect I had been told my family never spoke. A swordspeaker's dialect. My grandmother had been oath-bound. Someone had paid good money to have that scraped out of our household records, and the scraping had taken the song with it, and here it came back, note by note, riding a wave of dragon-light like flotsam returning on a tide.

The second thing was larger. The purge at Ashfall. I had been told — I had *believed*, God help me, I had written reports based on the belief — that the village of Ashfall had risen in rebellion and been justly suppressed. I had trained recruits on the lesson of Ashfall. *This is what oath-treachery earns.*

What came back was a village of farmers who had sheltered a wounded dragon for one winter. What came back was the order, signed and sealed, that preceded any rebellion by four months. What came back was the smell.

I dropped my sword. I am told officers do not do that. I dropped it the way you drop a snake you have suddenly realized has been sleeping in your bed for years.

Around me, thirty thousand soldiers were having their own private apocalypses. Some wept. Some screamed. One man near me sat down in the mud and began reciting names — village names, purge names — like a man counting coins he'd forgotten he owned. Our banners looked suddenly obscene, wet paper in the golden light. Somewhere behind me a colonel was shouting orders, and his voice had the desperate brightness of a man shouting at a flood.

Here is the thing about certainty: it feels exactly like knowledge until the moment it doesn't. For eleven years I had been certain the oath-bound started the old war. The certainty had a weight, a warmth, a shape. I could hold it. And now the fire had reached into my chest and lifted it out like a coal from a grate, and underneath was nothing but ash and a question:

*If they lied to me about this, what else?*

I turned to look up the mountain. At the shrine steps stood a thin man with a burned sword-hand and an open book, holding the whole valley in a sentence.

I had hunted him for six weeks. I had wanted, in my coldest hours, to be the one to put him down.

I have never been so glad to fail at anything in my life.

* * *

*Tomas, a soldier of the Third Rank*

You don't know me. Nobody knows me; that's rather the point of being third rank. But somebody should write down what it was like down in the mud where the history was happening, because the officers will get it wrong. Officers always get it wrong. They were looking at the battle. We were looking at each other's faces.

The light came and I thought, well, this is how I die, and I thought of my sister's wedding, which I'd missed, and I thought, at least it's warm.

Then it wasn't dying. It was — you know when you walk into your childhood house after twenty years and the smell knocks you sideways? It was that, except the house was my own head, and everything in it had been rearranged while I was away.

I remembered my grandfather. Everyone in the company remembered their grandfathers, apparently, or their grandmothers, or some old aunt with a gift she hid like a sickness. Mine used to hold his hand flat over the coals and make them dance. I'd forgotten. No — not forgotten. *Been relieved of.* There was a hole in me shaped like him and the fire poured in and filled it, and I sat down in the mud and cried like a recruit.

Sergeant Adeyemi grabbed my shoulder and said, "On your feet," and then the light got her too, and she let go of my shoulder and said, "Never mind," and sat down next to me.

Thirty thousand of us. Sitting in the mud. In formation, technically — the most honest formation the crown army ever held, which is to say, on our backsides facing the truth.

The colonel kept shouting. Somebody's lieutenant drew steel, and a hundred veterans looked at him — not angry, just *looking*, the way you look at a child waving a stick at a storm — and he put it away.

We didn't mutiny. I want that recorded. Mutiny is against orders. This was the opposite of orders. This was thirty thousand men and women simultaneously remembering why they'd enlisted in the first place, and discovering it wasn't for the crown at all. It was for a grandfather making coals dance. It was for a village that never needed burning. It was for the person standing next to you in the mud.

By full dark, half the companies had turned for home. Not deserting — *turning*. Walking in step, colors furled, drums silent, going home to ask their families what else had been taken out of them.

I stayed one night longer, to help carry the wounded. Then I turned too.

But that's later. Right then, in the light, I only knew one thing, and I said it out loud to nobody, and the sergeant heard it and nodded:

"They lied to us about everything."

*Ishola*

I stood beside Mara on the shrine steps with my scarred hand in his unburned one, and I waited for it to reach me too.

It took longest for me. I think the fire was gentle with those of us who had already paid. Or perhaps I simply had less false history to burn — when they took my village, they took the record, but they could not take what I carried in my body. Ash remembers. Ask anyone who has survived a fire: the truth gets into the bone.

But when the wave finally broke over me, it brought the one thing I had stopped praying for.

Names.

Not my dead — I never lost my dead; I have recited them so many times they are carved into the inside of my skull, and I will recite them again tomorrow and the day after, because a name spoken aloud is a grave marker the crown cannot confiscate. No. The fire gave me *other people's* dead. Every village. Every purge. Every erased ledger entry in the kingdom came home on that golden tide, and I stood on the steps and received them like a midwife receiving children, one after another after another, weeping without shame.

Somewhere below, the army was learning what I learned alone in the Iron House: that you cannot interrogate a memory out of thirty thousand minds at once.

Mara's grip tightened on my hand. He was watching the flame with an expression I recognized because I had worn it at the Iron House when they asked me where the seal was and I recited names instead. It was the expression of a man counting a cost.

"Mara," I said. "What is it paying with?"

He didn't answer. His lips were moving — not speaking, *rehearsing*. Trying to remember something and finding the shelf empty.

I should have understood then. Perhaps part of me did. When you bargain with a fire, you do not get to keep the receipt.

But the valley was singing — actually singing, thirty thousand cracked soldier-voices stumbling through a lullaby none of them had heard since childhood — and I let the moment have me. Catharsis is not a word I trust. Catharsis is what poets call it when other people's suffering resolves nicely. But I will admit this: for one hour on that mountainside, the world was true, top to bottom, king to mud, and I had helped make it true, and my dead stood around me in the golden light like an honor guard.

I said their names into the fire, all of them, one last time as a complaint.

After tonight, I would say them as a memorial instead. The difference matters. Complaints are for when no one is listening.

*Sefu*

I arrived at the foot of the dragon-road as the light was dying into ordinary dusk, leading a mule loaded with the guild ledger I had sworn never to surrender, and I found the valley transformed and my own name already being spoken in it.

Not kindly. I had expected that. I had sent my confession ahead of me by courier precisely so that it would arrive before I did — let them hate the document first and the man second, if they must hate both.

What I had not expected was to stand among the assembled survivors and watch the last ring of firelight pass over me and find... nothing false to burn.

That is the cruelty I had not prepared for. The fire burned lies out of minds. And the lie I lived under — the lie that the guild could survive anything if it stayed quiet enough, bent enough, useful enough — that lie had already been burned out of me, alone, in my empty guildhouse, weeks ago, when I handed over a burned woman and received in exchange a permit stamped by men who hollowed us anyway. There was nothing left in me for the fire to take. I was already clean. Clean the way a field is clean after it has been salted.

An old woman with burn-scars looked at me across the assembly fires. Ishola. She had every right to demand my death, and the tribunal would convene in the morning, and I would plead guilty to every count, and I would accept whatever they gave.

But tonight she only looked at me, and then at the mule, and then at the ledger, and she said — and I will carry these words into whatever comes after — "You brought the books."

"I brought the books," I said.

"Then sit by the fire," she said, "and start writing down what you remember. All of it. Especially the parts that damn you."

So I sat. And I wrote. And the fire, which had burned a false history out of thirty thousand minds, left me mine intact — every cowardly, calculating, loving piece of it — as if to say: *you are your own punishment, and also your own evidence.*

Some nights I think that was mercy. Some nights I think otherwise. Both thoughts are probably true. The guild taught me that much, at least: most true things come in pairs.

* * *

*Mara*

The valley is quiet now, in the way a valley is quiet after a storm that rearranged the furniture of heaven.

The army is breaking apart into columns of walking memory, heading home to ask questions. The survivors' fires dot the dark like a constellation someone spilled. The Keeper has climbed down from the shrine and is walking among the people with the unhurried gait of a woman who has waited two hundred years and can therefore wait until morning for congratulations.

And I am sitting on the shrine steps with the true ledger open on my knees, doing the one thing I am still good at.

Recording.

Except.

Except that when I try to write the account of the oath — my oath, spoken an hour ago, the hinge of the age — I find the page staying blank in my mind. I remember deciding to speak it. I remember opening the ledger. I remember the first word leaving my mouth.

I do not remember the rest. It is not foggy. It is *absent*, the way a pulled tooth is absent — a clean socket where something load-bearing used to be.

And just now, Ishola came and sat beside me and said, "Rest now. You've earned it." And I looked up at her — this extraordinary woman, ash-made, name-carver, the bravest person in the valley — and I heard myself say, politely, warmly, terribly:

"Thank you. Have we met?"

She went very still. Then she smiled at me, a smile with a crack running through it like ice on a pond, and she said, "Yes. We've met. We'll meet again tomorrow, and the day after, if you'll have me."

"Of course," I said, because she seemed kind, and because I have always trusted people who look at me as though I matter.

She walked away toward the fires. I picked up my pen.

I am writing this last entry quickly, while I still understand what is happening to me. The oath is not finished taking its price — I can feel it working through me like frost through soil, patient, thorough. Something else is missing already; I checked for my mentor's face and found the shelf swept bare. Whatever remains, it seems to be taking the things I love in the order I love them.

So, for whoever reads this ledger — and someone must, because a record only exists if it is read — know this: the man who spoke the oath paid gladly, and would pay again, and asks only one thing.

If he forgets why the garden behind the ruined guildhouse is planted the way it is planted — plant it again.

If he forgets the names — the ledger has them. Read them to him anyway. He will not know what they mean. Say them regardless. That is the whole art. That was always the whole art.

The fire is settling now, folding itself back down toward an ember, satisfied, its great work done. Tomorrow the survivors will convene a court, and Sefu will stand trial, and the world will begin the slow unglamorous labor of writing itself down honestly.

Tonight, I close the true ledger, and I find that my hand — the burned one, the oath-hand — is steady.

And I cannot remember, no matter how I search, what my surname is.
