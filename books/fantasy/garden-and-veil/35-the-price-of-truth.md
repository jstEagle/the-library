## 35. The Price of Truth

I have kept ledgers long enough to know that every entry has a cost. Ink costs. Paper costs. The truth, most of all, costs. But I had always believed the ledger paid its debts to other people. I never imagined it would come for me.

The fire went through the valley like a bell being rung inside the skull of the world.

I remember that much. I remember standing before the guttering flame with the true ledger open in my hands — Sefu's guild record bound against Ishola's testimony and the fragments forty survivors had carried across half a kingdom — and I remember speaking the oath. All of it. Every word my mentor made me swear into silence twenty years ago, spoken now at full voice with dragon-fire listening.

I remember the fire rising.

After that, the remembering stops.

* * *

They tell me I was on my knees when it ended. They tell me the flame settled down afterward, small and steady as a hearth-coal, and that the army in the valley stood like people waking from a dream, some of them weeping, some of them simply staring at their own hands as if they'd never seen them before. A soldier — a boy, really, from the front rank — is supposed to have dropped his spear and said, loudly, in the silence: *We were told they started the war.* And then, quieter: *We were lied to.*

I don't remember any of this. I was there, apparently. I am told I was there.

What I remember instead is sitting in the ash-warm grass above the shrine with an empty feeling behind my eyes, like a room that has been swept so thoroughly even the dust has been carried out. Veyra crouched beside me, saying my name. Just "Mara." Over and over, the way you call someone up from deep water.

"Mara Oyelaran," she said.

And I looked at her and thought: *that's a strange thing to call me,* because *Oyelaran* meant nothing. It rang no bell. It was a word in a language I did not speak.

"I'm Mara," I agreed, because that part still worked. "Who's Oyelaran?"

Her face did something complicated. I have seen that face since, on many people, many times — the face of someone deciding how much truth a person can hold without spilling. She chose to pour carefully. "You are," she said. "That's your name. All of it."

"All of what?"

She didn't answer. Behind her, Ishola was coming up the shrine steps, and I remember — this is the strange part, the part I keep turning over like a coin I can't spend — I remember being glad to see her. Glad the way you're glad to see a kind stranger at a well. I knew she mattered. I knew it the way you know a load-bearing wall matters, by the way everything sagged toward her. But her face —

Her face was a country I had never visited.

She knelt in front of me and took my hands, and there were burn scars on them I somehow knew the story of without knowing her, the way you can know a road leads somewhere without having walked it. "Mara," she said. "Do you know who I am?"

"You're the one from the seal," I said. "The ash woman. You came to the guildhouse door." And then, because something in her expression cracked: "That's all I have. There was more. There was clearly more. It's just — gone."

"Gone where?" she asked, and her voice was steady, which must have cost her everything.

"I don't know. That's rather the problem."

* * *

Here is what the keeper explained to me later, sitting beside the ember that had been a conflagration, while I held a cup of tea I couldn't taste:

Dragon-fire does not burn nothing. Nothing burns clean; that's why liars love it. Dragon-fire burns *falsehood*, and it has to take the fuel from somewhere near the flame. An oath spoken with a true ledger gives the fire a great deal to work with — but the speaker is the nearest fuel of all. The gift itself, the swordspeaker's ear for memory, the thing that let me hear a sword remember its kills: consumed. And whatever else the fire judged closest to the oath. My name. My mentor's face — I reached for it and found a shape where a face should be, warm and stern and utterly featureless, like a portrait painted over with whitewash. My years at the guildhouse, most of them. And Ishola. Not the facts of Ishola — those survived, filed away like entries in a ledger I could still read. But her. Her laugh, which I am told I once said sounded like water finding its way downhill. The way she said my name when she was angry versus when she was afraid. Whatever it was that had made two burned and hunted people into — whatever we had been.

The fire took the true things because the true things were what fed it. Falsehood burns fast and cheap. Truth burns slow, and it burns *you*.

"Was it worth it?" the keeper asked me, not unkindly.

I thought about the soldier dropping his spear. About the valley full of people who suddenly remembered a war correctly. About the false history — the crown's neat, poisoned version, the one that made dragons monsters and oath-bound traitors — burned out of ten thousand minds at once, leaving only the raw, unlovely, undeniable truth.

"Yes," I said. And then, because I am apparently incapable of not keeping accounts, even now: "But I'd like it noted that the invoice was steep."

The keeper almost smiled. "The fire doesn't take requests."

"No," I said. "It takes payment."

* * *

The days after were a strange kind of poverty. I had spent my whole life invisible on purpose — the careful clerk, the man nobody looks at twice — and now I was invisible in a way I hadn't chosen, a stranger even to myself. I would reach for a memory and find the shelf empty, or worse, find the shelf *reorganized*, truths still present but stripped of the warmth that had made them mine.

I sat with the ledger that night — the true ledger, our ledger, the one assembled out of forty testimonies and a traitor's confession and the fragments of my own burned history — and I read my own name in it like a stranger's. *Mara Oyelaran, last oath-bound swordspeaker.* Someone had written a line beneath it in Ishola's hand: *He gave everything he remembered to make the world remember.*

"That's dramatic," I told her. She was mending a cloak by the fire, because apparently some things about a person survive even when your memory of them doesn't; her hands still moved like that, patient and exact.

"It's accurate," she said.

"It's flattering. Those aren't the same thing."

"No," she agreed. "But you were both."

I turned pages. Here was the guildhouse inventory I had spent fifteen years keeping — the sword that remembers its kills, the mirror that shows tomorrow's regret, the door that only opens for the lost. Here was Sefu's confession, entered in full, damning him in his own ink. Here was the proclamation that started everything, copied out in my own handwriting, and beneath it a note: *burned this page once already. Some things you have to burn twice.*

"I wrote that?" I asked.

"You did. You laughed when you wrote it. It wasn't a kind laugh."

"It sounds like me."

"It *is* you," she said, sharper than she meant to, and then softer: "That's what nobody understands yet. The fire took your memories, Mara. It didn't take you. I watched you choose, every step of the way. I watched you burn your own history to protect ours. I watched you trade pieces of yourself like a man paying off a debt he never owed. Whatever is left sitting there reading that ledger — that's the part that chose. The memories were just the price."

I wanted to believe her. I want to believe her still. But there is a difference between being told you are brave and remembering it, and I am learning to live in that difference the way you learn to live in a house with a missing wall — carefully, and mostly at night.

* * *

On the third morning, Veyra found me trying to write.

I had taken a blank page — the first blank page I had owned in years that didn't frighten me — and I was attempting the exercise my mentor once set: *write the oath from memory.* A test, he used to say, of whether the words lived in you or merely on you.

My pen hovered. The page stayed white.

"I can't," I said, when Veyra stopped beside me. "I know I spoke it. Everyone says I spoke it. I can feel the shape of it, the way you feel a staircase in the dark after the candle's gone out. But the words themselves—"

"Maybe that's the point," Veyra said. She is not a comforting woman; she comforts the way a whetstone comforts, by being hard against the edge. "The oath was never supposed to live in one person. That's how they killed it last time — one bearer, one throat, one purge away from silence. Now it lives in a ledger. In forty witnesses. In a valley full of soldiers who can't unknow what they know." She tapped the page under my pen. "You didn't lose the oath, Mara. You *distributed* it."

"That's a very accountant-like way to describe the worst day of my life."

"I learned from a very good accountant."

I looked at the blank page a long time. Then, instead of the oath, I wrote something else. Something small and true and entirely mine, the first entry in whatever comes next:

*Item: one oath, spoken, whereabouts distributed. Item: one gift, consumed, receipt retained by the fire. Item: one man, remaindered. Condition: honest.*

I showed it to Veyra. She read it twice.

"Honest," she said. "Is that what you're calling it?"

"It's what I've got left. Everything false got burned. What's remaining is either true or nothing."

"Then you're richer than the crown," she said, and for the first time since I'd known her — however long that was; I genuinely could not say — she smiled.

* * *

Ishola found me at dusk, down by the ember-shrine, where I had gone to do the thing I now did every evening: sit with the fire and try, methodically, patiently, like a clerk reconciling accounts, to remember her.

Not the facts. The facts were safe in the ledger. I mean *her*. I would close my eyes and reach for her face and get — weather. A warm blur where a person should be. I knew she had a scar at her hairline. I knew she hummed when she worked. I knew these things the way I knew the inventory of a guildhouse I could no longer picture: accurately, uselessly, from the outside.

"You're doing it again," she said, sitting down beside me.

"Doing what?"

"Auditing me."

"I'm not—" I started, and stopped, because I was. "It's the only skill I have left."

"It's not the only one." She pulled her knees up, watching the ember breathe. "You know what I keep thinking about? The night I collapsed at your guildhouse door. Burned half to death, carrying a seal that could get everyone in that building killed. And you — this clerk I'd never met — you looked at me and I could *see* you deciding. Weighing it. Your survival against mine. And then you carried me inside anyway." She glanced at me sideways. "You don't remember that. But I do. And I've decided somebody has to, so it might as well be me."

"That seems like a heavy thing to carry for a stranger."

"You're not a stranger." She said it flatly, like correcting an error in a column. "You're just a stranger to yourself. There's a difference, and I intend to keep pointing at it until you see it."

We sat awhile. The ember breathed. Somewhere below us in the valley, the army that had come to seize the flame was breaking camp, company by company, men walking home along roads they now remembered differently than they had marched in on. Justice, of a kind. The fragile, unwitnessed kind no court will ever record — except that now, for the first time, it *was* recorded. In our ledger. In forty heads. In a boy's dropped spear.

"Ishola," I said finally. "I don't remember your face. I want you to know that I know that. I'm not pretending. When I look at you, I see a brave woman I'm told I trusted with my life, and I believe the telling, but I can't feel it, and I won't insult you by faking it."

She nodded slowly. "I know."

"But," I said, "I notice that I keep choosing you anyway. Every morning, I wake up not knowing you, and every morning I find myself sitting next to you anyway. Whatever the fire burned, it apparently missed the part of me that decides. And that part keeps deciding *you*."

For a moment she said nothing. Then she laughed — short and startled, water finding its way downhill, though I only know that because she told me — and pressed her hand briefly over mine, scarred palm to scarred palm.

"Then we'll start there," she said. "A stranger who keeps choosing me. I've had worse beginnings. I had ashes, once."

* * *

Later that night, alone with the ledger, I made my final entries for the day. Old habit. Perhaps the only habit left entire.

*Item: one valley, undeceived. Item: one army, disbanded by memory. Item: one false history, burned, no recoverable remains. Item: one swordspeaker's gift, expended in full — see attached testimony of forty witnesses, none of whom will ever be erased again.*

I paused, pen above the page, and added one more line, because a ledger should be honest even when its keeper cannot be whole:

*Item: one archivist, former. Remembers nothing of why any of this mattered. Records that it mattered anyway.*

I blotted the page. And that was when I heard the footsteps on the shrine path — unhurried, deliberate, familiar in a way that snagged at something too deep for the fire to reach — and looked up to see Guildmaster Sefu standing at the edge of the ember-light, travel-worn and gray-faced, holding out a battered book in both hands like a man surrendering a weapon.

"Mara," he said, and my own name in his mouth felt like a key turning in a lock whose door I'd forgotten existed. "I've brought you the rest of the ledger. And a confession that isn't mine to keep anymore."

I looked at the book in his hands. I looked at his face — a stranger's face, worn hollow by something I had no memory of — and I felt the old clerk's instinct rise in me, undimmed, undestroyed, the one thing the fire had left untouched:

the need to know what was written, and to write down what happened next.

"Sit down, Guildmaster," I said. "Start at the beginning. I'll keep the record."
