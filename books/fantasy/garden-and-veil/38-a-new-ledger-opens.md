## 38. A New Ledger Opens

The guildhouse had burned twice in its long life before I set fire to it myself, and both previous times it had been rebuilt by people who believed the Guild of Impossible Things was worth the timber. I had always assumed that belief was a kind of madness. Now I was one of the mad ones, standing in the ash of my own history with a hammer in my hand and no gift left to speak of, and I found — to my considerable irritation — that I believed too.

The morning after the trial of Sefu Okonkwo, Ishola came to me with a blank ledger.

"You'll want to start it properly," she said. "First page matters. Everything after is just argument."

I turned the book over in my hands. Good paper, sewn binding, the kind the old guild used for records that were meant to outlive their keepers. "And what do I write on the first page?"

"The truth," she said, "in whatever order you can manage it."

"That's not an order. That's a dare."

"Most true things are." She sat down on an upturned crate — we had very little furniture and most of it was crates — and watched me with those patient eyes that had once stared down an Iron House interrogator without giving up a single name. "You're hesitating."

"I'm thinking. There's a difference. Hesitating is when you don't know what to say. Thinking is when you know exactly what to say and are trying to find a way to say it that won't get anyone killed." I uncapped the ink. "The old guild solved this problem by never writing anything true at all. Forged ledgers, invented provenance, a sword listed as 'ceremonial' that had eaten eleven men. It worked for two hundred years."

"And then?"

"And then it didn't." I looked at the first page for a long moment. Then I wrote: *Guild of Impossible Things, re-founded in the spring after the burning of the false history. This ledger will contain only what actually happened.* My hand shook slightly on the last word. Old habits. "There. Either the bravest thing I've ever done or the stupidest, and given my record, the smart money's on stupidest."

"It can be both," Ishola said. "Most things worth doing are."

* * *

Rebuilding a guild is mostly carpentry punctuated by arguments.

We had survivors now — the Assembly of the Erased had not dispersed after the oath was spoken and the army had turned home. Some had nowhere to go back to; their villages were empty shells where people smiled politely and could not tell you why the fields stood fallow. Others had somewhere to go but chose not to go there, because going home meant returning to neighbors who still half-remembered nothing, and memory restored is a heavier burden than memory lost. So they stayed, and they built, and they argued.

Veyra argued about defenses. "An open house of record is a target," she said, pacing the half-raised frame of the new common room. She still moved like a hunter even in peacetime, weight forward, hands loose. "You've hung a sign on the door that says *all true things recorded here*. Every crown, every guild, every petty lordling who ever falsified a tax roll is going to want that sign taken down."

"Good," said Ishola.

"Good?"

"If they come here to argue, they have to argue against witnesses. Against record. Against testimony bound and preserved." Ishola folded her hands. "For years the truth lived in cellars and hidden vaults and the backs of people's throats. Let it live in the open for once. If someone burns this place down again, the ledger survives, because the ledger has copies, and the copies have copies, and every copy has a name attached who will swear to it. You cannot burn a thing that lives in a hundred mouths."

Veyra stopped pacing. "That's either very brave or very naive."

"You sound like Mara."

"I resent that deeply," I said from the rafters, where I was pretending to inspect a joint. "I'm far more cynical than either of you. I simply lack the energy to act on it anymore. The oath took the gift. Apparently it took the pessimism too. I keep waiting for it to grow back."

It hadn't. That was the strange mercy of it. When dragon-fire burned the false history out of the world, it also burned through me — took the swordspeaking entire, took memories I still sometimes reached for and found smooth walls where doors used to be. I did not remember my mentor's face. I did not remember my own surname; Oyelaran was a name the archivists gave me afterward, reconstructed from a shipping manifest, and I wore it like a borrowed coat. But the fear had gone out with the fire. Or most of it. What remained was a smaller, more manageable dread, the kind a man feels carrying something breakable across ice — careful, watchful, but no longer drowning.

Sefu argued about nothing, because Sefu was not permitted to argue. His sentence was to rebuild the records he had betrayed: every forged entry in the old guild ledgers corrected, every name he had sold or suppressed restored, every lie he'd told in two hundred years of institutional cowardice written out in his own hand with the truth beside it. He worked in a corner of the ruined scriptorium from dawn until his eyes failed him each evening, and he did not speak unless spoken to, and when spoken to he answered precisely and briefly, like a man counting out a debt he knew he could never finish paying.

I hated him for a long time. I want that on the record, since we're keeping one now. I hated him in the mountains while the flame guttered, and I hated him when the courier died delivering his ledger, and I hated him through the whole strange tribunal where the survivors declined to kill him and sentenced him instead to the one punishment that fit: to become the archive of his own crimes. But hatred is heavy luggage, and I had already paid enough in carried things. Somewhere around the third month of watching him labor, hunched and gray, correcting entries he had made before I was born, the hate thinned into something else. Not forgiveness. I'm not sure I believe in forgiveness as a single event; I think it's more like weather. But the pressure lifted, some days, and on those days I could hand him ink without my jaw tightening.

He noticed. Of course he noticed. He'd spent a lifetime reading rooms.

"You don't have to be kind to me," he said one evening, not looking up from his work.

"No," I agreed. "But the ledger says I have to be accurate, and 'Mara was cruel to the penitent' is accurate, and I'd rather not be accurately cruel. It's exhausting."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth. It might have been the ghost of a smile. It might have been pain. With Sefu the two had always been difficult to distinguish.

* * *

They made me archivist in the autumn, which surprised no one except me.

The Assembly met in the finished half of the common room — forty-odd souls, give or take, the unrecorded and their kin — and Veyra stood up and said, in her flat soldier's voice, "The house needs a keeper of records. Someone who understands what documents cost. Someone who has burned them and been burned by them. I nominate Mara."

"I object," I said.

"Noted," said Veyra. "All in favor?"

Every hand went up. Even Sefu's, raised slowly, like a man saluting a verdict.

"You're all insane," I told them. "I lost the gift. I can't verify testimony against the memory of things anymore. Half of what an archivist does is *know*, in the bones, whether a record is true. I'm hollowed out. I'm a sieve. You might as well appoint the teapot."

Ishola rose beside me. "Say what you lost," she said quietly. "All of it. They should hear."

So I said it. I stood in front of the Assembly of the Erased and enumerated my losses like a merchant taking inventory: the swordspeaking, gone. My mentor's face, gone — I had loved her twenty years and could not now have picked her from a crowd. My surname, traded ward by ward to walk into the Iron House. And at the end of it, the largest loss, the one I almost couldn't say: the oath itself. I had spoken the words that remade the world's memory, and now I could not remember speaking them. I knew the story of that night the way you know a story — beginning, middle, fire — but the living center of it, the moment the words left my mouth, was a smooth wall.

"When I wrote the first page of the new ledger," I finished, "my hand shook. Not from fear. From habit. The gift isn't in me anymore, but its shape is, the way a scar remembers a wound. Is that really who you want keeping your records? A scar?"

Silence. Then an old woman near the back — Adaeze, who had walked out of a village that forgot her husband's name and carried his memory for both of them — said, "Yes. Exactly that one."

"Why?"

"Because everyone else who kept records thought remembering was easy," she said. "You know better than any of us what remembering costs. You'll guard it accordingly."

I looked around the room. Forty faces, none of them fooled, all of them certain. And I understood, finally, what the gift had been for — not the speaking, not the fire, but this: making memory matter so much that ordinary people would build houses to protect it. The gift had been scaffolding. The building was always going to be made of us.

"All right," I said. "But I'm keeping the teapot as deputy."

* * *

Winter came early that year, soft and gray, and the new guildhouse grew a skin of frost and smoke. We opened formally on the first day of the thaw — no ceremony, because ceremonies invite crowds and crowds invite the crown, though the crown had been strangely quiet since the valley. A new proclamation had gone out in the capital, we heard: the hunt for swordspeakers was "concluded." No apology. No acknowledgment. Just a hunt that had simply stopped being mentioned, the way inconvenient things stop being mentioned, and I confess that frightened me more than the hunt itself had. Silence is how erasure begins. We had burned the great false history out of the world's mind, but small falsehoods regrow like weeds, and nobody was tending the garden.

That was the phrase Ishola used, actually, standing beside me at the door as the first petitioners came in — a farmer with a deed dispute, a widow whose village record had listed her son as "deceased in service" when he had in fact deserted, a young man who remembered a massacre nobody else in his town could recall and wanted it written down before doubt ate it.

"A garden," she said. "That's what this is. You plant testimony like seed. Most of it grows slow. Some of it takes seasons to come up. But if you tend it—"

"—someone comes along and calls it weeds and burns it," I said. "I've read this book. I was in it."

"You were the gardener," she said, "at the end."

"At the end I was the compost."

She laughed — a real laugh, unguarded, rare as sun in that season — and I felt the absence of my memories of her like a tooth missing. I knew what she had been to me. The ledger said so, in my own hand: *Ishola Adeyemi, survivor of the purge at Ilé-Òrìṣà, bearer of the seal, my friend, my witness, the reason for everything.* But knowing is not remembering. I knew she had held my hand at the ford. I could not feel it. I knew she had sung to me on the salt flats when I wanted to lie down and stop. I could not hear it. She was, in the truest sense, a stranger whose story I had memorized, and she knew it, and she stayed anyway, and I have never decided whether that was kindness or cruelty or simply the price we had both agreed to pay without discussing it.

"Tea?" I asked her, because tea was the ritual we had instead of the past.

"Tea," she said.

* * *

The ledger grew. That was the miracle of it, the quiet daily miracle: it grew.

By midwinter we had filled three volumes. Testimony came in from farther and farther out — people who had walked weeks to reach us, carrying fragments. A woman who remembered the true name of a river the crown had renamed. Two brothers with matching halves of a song their grandmother sang about the dragons, each brother convinced the other's half was wrong, both halves correct. A former royal scribe, gray and terrified, who brought a box of drafts — discarded versions of the official histories — and laid them on my desk like a man surrendering weapons. "These show the edits," he whispered. "You can see where they changed the war. Line by line. I kept them because I couldn't bear to destroy them and couldn't bear to obey them, and I've been carrying that contradiction for thirty years."

"We can hold it for you," I said. "Both halves. The obedience and the refusal. That's what a record is — it doesn't make you choose. It just refuses to let either choice disappear."

He wept onto my desk. I pushed the inkwell out of range and let him. First honest tears in the new guildhouse; I noted it later, in the margin, in pencil, because some things belong in margins.

Sefu's corrections came in too, volume by volume, his cramped penitent's hand marching through the old lies. He worked faster now, as if speed were a form of payment. Sometimes I checked his work against testimony and found it flawless. Once I found an error — he had transposed two dates — and I brought it to him and he went white as salt and re-did the entire page and thanked me, thanked me, for catching it, and I walked away feeling like I'd kicked a dog that was already bleeding, and wrote in my private journal: *Note to self: accuracy without mercy is just cruelty with good handwriting.*

Veyra drilled the household in defense anyway, over Ishola's objections, because Veyra was Veyra. "Peace is a supply line," she said. "It runs out." But even her drills softened; she taught the children of the Assembly to read terrain and ended up teaching them letters instead, because they kept asking what the words on the boundary stones meant, and she discovered she liked answering. The hunter became a schoolmistress by accident. She would deny this under torture. I have it in the ledger.

And the ember — well. The ember is another chapter, and belongs to the keeper to tell, not me. I will say only this: once a month, a rider goes up the dragon-road with a copy of whatever we've recorded, and once a month the rider comes back with the keeper's blessing scratched on bark paper, and the last time, the bark paper said something different. Something addressed to me by name.

I was reading it when Ishola came in with the evening tea.

"What is it?" she asked. "You've gone strange."

I handed it to her. The keeper's characters were old-fashioned but clear, and Ishola read aloud, slowly, the way you read something you don't yet believe:

*"The ember asks for its witness. Come before the thaw ends. Bring nothing but yourself — the ledger stays here, where it is needed. The fire remembers what you cannot, swordspeaker, and it has been keeping something of yours."*

Ishola lowered the paper. Her hands were steady. Mine were not.

"Keeping something of mine," I repeated. "What could it possibly be keeping of mine? I have nothing left. That was rather the point."

"Maybe," Ishola said, setting down the tea, reaching for her traveling cloak with the calm of a woman who had been waiting months for exactly this, "that's why it wants you to come."

Outside, the thaw was starting. Water ticked off the eaves like a slow clock, counting toward spring, and I stood in the doorway of the house I had helped raise from ashes, holding a message from a fire that remembered me better than I remembered myself, and understood that the garden was about to ask something of its gardener again.

"Pack light," Ishola said. "The road's long."

"It always is," I said, and went to find my boots.
