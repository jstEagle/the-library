## 39. The Last Ember

I have kept the flame for sixty-one years, and in all that time it has never once done what I asked of it.

This is not a complaint. A keeper who expects obedience from dragon-fire is like a fisherman who expects the sea to apologize. But I had grown comfortable with its moods. It guttered when liars approached. It flared at true grief. It once turned a shade of blue I have no name for when a child wept over a dead sparrow on the shrine steps, and I wrote that down in my book because a keeper's whole duty is to write down what cannot be explained.

What it did after the oath was spoken was none of these things.

It went out.

Not all at once. Not dramatically, though there was drama enough in the valley that day to spare. The fire stood up — I will call it standing, because that is what it looked like, a column of white-gold light rising out of the shrine bowl like something that had been kneeling for three hundred years and finally remembered its legs — and then it sat back down, and then it was small. Small as a candle. Small as the coal at the heart of a banked hearth. It has remained exactly that size since, through wind and rain and one regrettable incident involving a pigeon, and I have come to understand that this is not diminishment.

This is a decision.

* * *

The morning after the army turned home, I swept the shrine steps as I always do. Sixty-one years of sweeping. My knees have opinions about it now, and they voice them around dawn.

The young man was already there.

He sat on the third step with his hands open on his knees, palms up, the way oath-bound used to sit when they were listening for a word. Except he wasn't listening. I could see that from the top of the steps. He was waiting the way a man waits in an empty house for someone who isn't coming home — patiently, hopelessly, out of habit rather than faith.

"You're the swordspeaker," I said.

"I was." He didn't look up. "Mara Oyelaran."

"I know your name. It's in my book." I came down the steps slowly, letting him hear me, because men who have lost things startle easily. "Written down the night before the battle. Along with the name of every person who walked up these steps carrying testimony. Four hundred and eleven names. Yours is two hundred and ninety-six."

That made him look up. His eyes were the eyes of a man doing arithmetic he doesn't like: *if I can't remember her face, but my name is written in a stranger's book, which of us holds me?*

"Can I see it?" he asked.

"No." I sat down beside him, one step above, which is where a keeper belongs relative to everyone else. "But I'll read it to you, if you like. That's what books are for. Not looking. Telling."

He thought about it. I watched him think about it, and I will tell you what I saw, because I have spent six decades watching people stand where he was standing, and I have earned the right to describe it. He looked like a man reading a letter addressed to someone with his handwriting.

"Later," he said. "Maybe later."

"Later is fine. Later is mostly what I deal in."

We sat while the sun cleared the eastern ridge and laid itself across the valley floor. Below us, the survivors' camp was waking — cook-smoke, a hammer somewhere, the particular argumentative murmur of people building something together, which is the most hopeful sound humans make and also the most irritating. Ishola moved among the tents like a woman counting them. She counted everything now. I understood why. Counting is how you hold a thing when you're afraid the world will take it back.

"She comes every morning," Mara said. "To see me."

"Yes."

"And I don't know her."

"No."

"But she knew me. Before."

"Before," I agreed.

He nodded slowly, and then he did something I did not expect. He laughed. Just a breath of it, more air than sound, but real.

"That's the joke, isn't it," he said. "The fire burned the false history out of everyone's memory. And took mine with it as payment. So now I'm the only man in the valley with a blank page where his life should be, sitting next to four hundred people who got theirs back."

"The fire is not known for its sense of proportion," I said. "It is known for its thoroughness."

* * *

Let me tell you about thoroughness, since I am apparently in a telling mood, and old keepers get few chances before the young ones take the broom from their hands.

Dragon-fire does not burn wood. Anyone can burn wood; I've seen children do it with a glass bead and a sunny afternoon. Dragon-fire burns meaning. It eats the difference between what happened and what was said to have happened, and it leaves only what happened. That is its nature and its cruelty and, if you ask me — and nobody has, in sixty-one years, which tells you something about the value of my opinions — its mercy.

When Mara Oyelaran spoke the full oath with the true ledger open on the shrine stone, the fire read that ledger the way I read mine: completely, without skipping. And the ledger contained everything. The purges. The betrayals. Sefu's bargain, confessed aloud on those same steps with his hat in his hands. Veyra's treason. The soldier who turned his company around. And woven through all of it, holding it together like thread through cloth, was Mara himself — his mentor, his guild, his burned page, his surname traded away in an iron house, his gift poured out to the last drop.

The fire finished reading. And then it did what fire does to whatever it finishes with.

It consumed him. Not the flesh. The record. Every memory that had been touched by the gift — and his whole life had been touched by the gift, because a man who hides a thing shapes his entire self around the hiding — went into the flame as fuel. The false history burned out of ten thousand minds, and the true history burned out of one.

Balance. The fire is very fond of balance. It has never once cared whose.

* * *

"I've been thinking about becoming useful," Mara said, some days later. He had taken to coming up the steps in the mornings whether I invited him or not, which I respected. In my experience the ones who wait to be invited are the ones who leave.

"We're all useful here," I said. "Half the camp is digging latrines and calling it civic renewal."

"I mean at the shrine. With you." He gestured at the ember, which sat in its bowl doing its impression of patience. "You're old."

"Thank you."

"You know what I mean. Someone should learn this. What you do. The writing-down part." He hesitated. "I can't be a witness anymore. I don't remember anything worth witnessing. But I can hold a pen. And apparently I'm good at ledgers. Everyone keeps telling me that like it's supposed to mean something to me."

"It should," I said. "It's the only thing left that does."

He frowned at me.

"Sit down properly," I said, "and I'll explain what I mean, and then you'll spend the rest of your life explaining it to other people, which is how knowledge works. It's a bucket brigade against a fire that never goes out. Or — well." I glanced at the ember. "A fire that almost never goes out."

He sat.

"Here is what I know," I said. "I know that memory is not a treasure. Treasures get stolen. Memory is infrastructure. It's the road the truth travels on, and roads need mending, and mending is dull work done by unglamorous people with bad knees. The crown understood this better than we did, God rot them. They didn't send soldiers first. They sent historians. They rewrote the road, and the armies just marched along it afterward, certain they were going somewhere good." I tapped my book. "This is a road-mender's tool. Nothing more. But nothing less, either."

"And the ember?" he asked. "What's it for, now that it's small?"

"Ah." I looked at it for a while. It flickered, the way it does when it knows it's being discussed. Vain thing. Even at the size of a thumbnail it has vanity. "Come here. Closer. No — closer than that. Hold your hand near it. Not in it. Near."

He did. I watched his face.

"Warm," he said, surprised. "Just warm. Like — " He searched. "Like a kitchen. Like bread."

"Like a hearth," I said. "Yes. That's what it is now. Do you know what it was before? Before the oath, when it answered to the old keepers? It was a weapon. It burned cities. It burned the false history out of the world, yes, but only because someone finally aimed it at the right target. For three hundred years before that, kings tried to aim it at each other. That's why the oath-bound were made. Not to wield it. To *guard the aiming*." I shook my head. "And now it's done. It has burned the last great lie, and it has settled, and it will not grow again. I have fed it testimony for weeks. Grief. Anger. Sefu's confession, which was fuel enough to level a mountain range, if fuel were what it wanted. It takes all of it and stays the size of a coin."

"What does it want, then?"

"To be carried," I said. "That's all. It wants to go home with people. Into kitchens. Into nurseries. It wants to be the light you read by and the warmth you argue across and the small fire you gather around when the night gets long. It is done burning the world. Now it only wants to light it. There is a difference, and the difference is the whole rest of history."

Mara held his hand over it a moment longer. Then he drew it back and looked at his palm — the scarred one, the sword-hand, the one the half-oath had marked at the ford. I don't know what he saw in it. He couldn't have seen the ford. But he looked a long time.

"All right," he said. "Teach me the writing-down part."

* * *

I taught him the way I was taught, which is badly, by immersion.

He learned the inventory of the shrine: the artifacts in the vault below, each one labeled in my crabbed hand, each one still humming faintly with what it remembers. He learned the cataloguing of testimony — how to write down what a survivor says without sanding off the edges, how to resist the archivist's great temptation, which is to make other people's suffering tidy. He learned that a ledger is a promise made to strangers not yet born, and that you break it the moment you let yourself improve the story.

He was good at it. Annoyingly good. Within a month he had reorganized my shelves according to a system he invented and could not entirely explain, and when I protested that I'd had a system, he said gently, "Alphabetical by the keeper's mood on the day of entry is not a system, it's a diary," and I had no defense, because it was true.

But that is not what I want to set down here. What I want to set down is the morning I named him.

* * *

There is a rite. There is always a rite; old institutions are mostly rites wearing coats. When a keeper's strength begins to fail — and mine began failing honestly around year fifty-eight, though I lied about it in my own ledger, which I mention only to demonstrate that even road-menders dig potholes — he names a successor. Not a keeper. Keepers tend the flame. A *witness* is something else, and rarer, and until that season I had never named one, because a witness requires a particular kind of hollow.

Here is the requirement, and it is terrible: a witness must be someone who has given everything to the truth and received nothing back. Not a scholar — scholars love knowing too much to be trusted with it. Not a believer — believers decorate. A witness must be a person who carries the record precisely because the record is all they have left of themselves. The empty handed the task of holding fullness. I have only met two such people in my life. One was me, and I was busy.

The other sat on my steps every morning, learning my crabbed alphabet, laughing at my jokes a half-beat late because he was reconstructing them from context, and going down at dusk to drink tea with a woman whose face he could not remember and whose tent he nonetheless always found without asking directions.

I watched that last thing for weeks before I understood it. He could not remember Ishola. But his feet remembered the way to her. Some part of him — deeper than memory, older than the gift, down where the oath had lived — still knew whom it belonged to. The fire had taken his ledger of himself. It had not taken the shelf the ledger stood on.

So on a grey morning in the turning of the season, with the camp's new archive half-built down the slope and the smell of fresh-cut timber riding the wind, I brought out the keeper's ring.

It is not much to look at. Iron, unadorned, sized for a finger somewhat thicker than mine ever was. It has been passed hand to hand for longer than any kingdom currently claiming to matter, and every keeper who wore it wore it loose, because the point was never possession.

"Mara Oyelaran," I said, using his full name, both parts of it, because a name spoken whole is a small oath in itself. "I can't make you a keeper. The keeping needs a memory, and yours is spent, and the fire chose that price, not me, and I have stopped arguing with the fire — you'll stop too, give it a decade. But a witness needs no past. A witness needs only this: to write what is true, to read it aloud to whoever asks, and to never, ever improve the story." I held out the ring. "Will you carry the record?"

He looked at the ring for a long time. Then at the ember. Then, briefly, down the slope, toward the tents, toward the tea, toward the woman counting things.

"I won't remember why it matters," he said quietly. "Tomorrow, or next year. Whatever today means to me will be gone by evening. You're handing the record to a man with holes in him."

"I'm handing it to a man who *is* a hole," I said, "and who fills it every single day anyway, with other people's truths, and never once with comforting lies. I've watched you do it for months. You are the only honest archivist I have ever known, and I have known several, and the others all stole spoons." I waggled the ring. "Take it before I develop dignity."

He laughed — the real laugh this time, the whole one — and took it.

It fit. Of course it fit. The ring has always fit the hand that stops reaching for things.

* * *

That evening I wrote the final entry of my own ledger, and I will copy it here because copying is what witnesses do, and I find at the end that I want to be witnessed myself:

*Today the fire is the size of a coal, and it is enough. Today the camp below raised the roof-beam on a house of record open to anyone with a story and no place safe to say it. Today I gave away an iron ring and felt lighter than I have in sixty-one years. Today Mara Oyelaran became the witness of the last ember, and he will not remember this entry, and so I have asked him to read it back to me tomorrow, and he has promised, and his promises are the kind that hold.*

*The fire will not burn again. Let no one grieve this. It will light. It will light and light and light, in kitchens and sickrooms and the small hours when a frightened person needs to see a page clearly enough to copy it. That is not a diminished thing. That is the only thing that was ever actually asked of it.*

*I am done. The broom hangs on the left peg. The ink is in the third drawer, and alphabetical by mood is a diary, boy, and someday you will admit it.*

*— Keeper Yewande Adisa, who swept these steps, and saw what she saw.*

* * *

He reads it to me every morning now. All of it. He does the voices — mine is apparently a crow with a head cold — and he never skips the part about the spoons, because I told him the entry must be read whole or not at all, and he has taken the instruction the way he takes everything: seriously, gently, and slightly too literally.

Yesterday, halfway through, he paused at the line about the ring fitting the hand that stops reaching for things, and he looked at his own hand for a long moment, and something crossed his face like weather.

"I feel like I'm missing something," he said. "When I read that part. Like a word on the tip of the tongue."

"Everyone is missing something," I said. "That's why we built the house of records. Bring your missing things there. We'll write them down."

He nodded and kept reading. Outside, the ember sat in its bowl, small and steady and utterly content, lighting the page.

And down the slope, at the edge of the new-built archive, a traveler had appeared on the road — dusty, footsore, leading a mule loaded with what even at that distance I recognized as ledgers, dozens of them, strapped and wrapped against the weather — and Ishola had gone out to meet her, already counting.
