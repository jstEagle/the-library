## 40. Garden and Veil

The first thing I planted was a mistake.

I had meant to put the beans along the south wall, where the old guildhouse kitchen used to stand and where the soil is richest — all that rendered fat and spilled wine and forty years of Sefu's cooking soaked down into the earth like a confession. But my hands have their own opinions now, and they put the beans by the well instead, and the tomatoes where the ledger-room once was, and I decided that a man who has traded away most of his memory should not argue with whatever sense of order remains to him. The garden knows what it's doing. I'm mostly here to carry water.

It's been three years since the fire came down off the mountain and burned through every mind in the valley, mine included. Three years of learning what it means to be a man with an honest face and no history behind it. People assume I'm a widower. There's something in the way I look at the shrine at the edge of the garden — veil-draped, always clean, never opened — that makes them draw their own conclusions, and I've stopped correcting them. It's easier than explaining. And anyway, they're not entirely wrong. Something did die here. I just can't tell you its name, because neither can I.

Here's what I know, and I keep it written down now, in a plain hand, in a plain book, because writing things down is the only craft I have left:

I know that there was a guild here, the Guild of Impossible Things, and that it kept ledgers of objects too strange or too dangerous for anyone else to hold. I know this because the foundation stones are still here, and because the neighbors remember it even if they remember nothing else about me. I know that I was its last archivist. I know that there was a war, long ago, and that the histories everyone learned were wrong, and that something happened on a mountain road involving a flame and an oath and a great many people whose names I helped gather — though when I read the list of names in my own handwriting, I recognize none of them, and each one lands on me like a stone dropped into deep water: a ripple, then nothing.

I know that I paid for the truth. That's the part I understand best, because the bill keeps arriving. The gift is gone — the swordspeaker's gift, the speaking-to-things gift, whatever it was; I've read my own descriptions of it and they read like letters from a man I once lent money to. And the memories went with it, or most of them. The oath took its price in installments. A river here. A mentor there. My surname, apparently, which is why the sign over my gate says only MARA'S GARDEN, and why children call me Old Mara, and why I answer to it as if it were the whole truth, which perhaps by now it is.

What I resent most — and I want to be honest in this account, because honesty is the only thing I have left in quantity — is not the big losses. It's not forgetting my teacher's face, though some mornings I wake up reaching for a name that isn't there and lie in the grey light feeling like a house with the furniture sold off. No. What I resent is small. I resent that I don't know whether I liked honey in my tea before, or whether I decided it afterward and mistook the decision for a memory. I resent that I can't remember if I was brave or merely cornered. Everyone who visits tells me I must have been brave. They say it the way you'd compliment a man's garden, easily, and I nod, and inside me there is a locked room, and behind the door of that room is either a hero or a coward or, most likely, a tired clerk who did one difficult thing on one impossible day, and I will never know which. The oath didn't just take my past. It took my right to an opinion about it.

But I'm getting ahead of myself, and a garden doesn't care about your grievances. Gardens only care whether you showed up.

* * *

Mornings begin with the well. The rope is fraying again — third time this season — and I've taken to talking to it while I haul, which is either madness or habit, depending on how charitable you are and how much you know about what I used to be. Things used to talk back, I'm told. Now it's just me and the rope and the creak of the windlass, and honestly? The rope's silence has a certain dignity to it. It never asks me questions I can't answer.

The beans by the well are thriving, which vindicates my hands' judgment entirely. The tomatoes over the old ledger-room are another matter. They grow lush and strange, fruit heavy and dark, and the neighbors won't eat them. "Grief-tomatoes," the baker's wife calls them, only half teasing. She thinks anything grown over ruins must be steeped in sorrow, and she may be right, but sorrow makes excellent compost and the fruit is sweet as anything, so I eat them and think: somewhere under your roots is every record I ever burned, and you're welcome to it.

That's the arrangement I made with the ground, you see. When I came back here — after the mountain, after everything, after the tribunal and the trials and the long slow dispersal of people who had been briefly, impossibly gathered — I found the guildhouse a black shell, and I could have rebuilt it. There were offers. The new Assembly of the Erased wanted a hall, a monument, a place with its name carved over the door. Ishola argued for it, I'm told. I'm told I argued against it, and then forgot the argument, which strikes me as a suspiciously convenient way to win a dispute.

Instead I built a wall around the ruin and put soil over the ashes and planted. Because here is the thing I understood, standing in that burnt doorway with my hat in my hands: monuments ask you to remember from the outside. You walk past them, you read the stone, you feel solemn for a moment, and then you go buy bread. But a garden makes you participate. You kneel in it. You pull weeds out of history. You learn which plants come back every year on their own — those are the truths, the ones that reseed themselves no matter what gets burned — and which ones you have to replant every spring, coaxing and worrying, because they're fragile, because they're testimony, because testimony dies without tending. I don't remember making this philosophy up. Possibly someone taught it to me. Possibly it grew in me like the beans, from seed somebody else planted. Either way, it holds.

And at the west edge, where the sun sets straight down the rows, stands the shrine.

* * *

I built the shrine in the first autumn, though I couldn't tell you why, which should tell you something about how much of me survived the fire intact. It's a simple thing: a stone post, a frame of blackened guildhouse timber, and a veil — good linen, dyed indigo, replaced every year on a date I don't understand but never fail to observe. Under the veil there is a shelf. On the shelf there are objects. I have never looked beneath the veil. Not once in three years.

This requires some explanation, and I'll give you the explanation I gave the priest who came out from town last spring, all curiosity and cassock, asking was it a saint's relic, was it a family altar, would I consider a consecration. I told him: it's a debt. He frowned at that, so I elaborated, because I'm a generous man with other people's confusion.

"Everything I lost," I said, "is under that veil. Everything I paid. And I have a rule, which is that you don't open a ledger you can't balance. If I lift that cloth, I'll see objects that mattered to someone I used to be, and I won't know why, and I'll spend the rest of my life trying to reconstruct a man out of his belongings like a scholar reading tea leaves. The veil stays down until I can pay for what's under it. Since I can't ever pay, it stays down forever. It's very tidy."

The priest said that was the most heretical act of devotion he'd ever heard, and asked if he could write it down, and I said the last person who wrote down my words caused a considerable amount of trouble, so no.

But I'll confess what I don't confess at the gate: I talk to the shrine. Every evening, after the watering, I stand in front of it and give it the day's report, the way a clerk reports to a superior. Rain today; beans coming along; the well-rope needs replacing; a stranger passed on the road and I offered water. And sometimes — this is between us — sometimes I say things like *I hope it was worth it*, and *I hope whoever you were would approve of the tomatoes*, and once, memorably, during a hailstorm, *if you planned any of this, you have a lot of nerve*. The veil never answers. But the wind moves it sometimes, a slow breathing swell of indigo, and I choose to take that as acknowledgment. A man in my position takes his acknowledgments where he can find them.

* * *

She came on a Tuesday, near the end of the bean harvest.

I knew she was coming before I saw her, the way you do — the dog stood up first, then the geese complained, and then there was a figure on the road, walking with the particular gait of someone who has traveled far and is determined not to limp. Burn-scarred hands, I noticed when she got close. Old burns, silvered with years, the kind that come from fire or from holding something that burns. She carried a staff and a satchel and nothing else worth mentioning, and she stopped at my gate and looked at the sign — MARA'S GARDEN — for a long time.

"You're the archivist," she said. Not a question.

"I'm a gardener," I said. "The archiving is more of a hobby these days." I keep the true ledger, you see — the assembled one, the one bound from a hundred testimonies and a traitor's confession and the fragments left in my skull. It lives in the vault under the well-house, and once a week I check its seals and oil its hinges and read a page at random, the way you'd visit a grave. Word of it has gotten out, despite my best efforts. Pilgrims come, sometimes. Scholars. One memorable tax inspector.

"I've come a long way," the woman said.

"They usually have." I unlatched the gate. "There's tea. There's always tea. It's the one thing I'm never short of, possibly because I can't remember ever running out."

She followed me up the path between the bean rows, and I caught her touching the leaves as she passed — not picking, just touching, the way you'd touch a wall to confirm it's real. At the shrine she stopped dead. I watched her look at the veil, and I watched something move across her face that I couldn't read, some weather passing over country I didn't know. Her hand came up. Hovered near the hem of the indigo cloth. Stopped.

"Don't," I said, gently. "Everyone wants to. It's the rule that keeps it standing."

Her hand withdrew slowly, like something being talked down from a ledge. "Whose is it?" she asked. "Under there. Whose?"

"That's the interesting part," I said. "I genuinely don't know. I built it, I dress it, I report to it every evening like a junior clerk to a magistrate who never speaks. Whatever's under there belonged to me once. Beyond that—" I spread my hands. "You'd be amazed how much of a life you can run on the outside of a locked room."

She looked at me for a long moment with an expression I've seen on pilgrims before — that terrible tenderness people aim at you when they know your story better than you do. I find it unbearable, frankly. I busied myself with the kettle.

We sat under the lean-to by the well-house, and I poured, and she wrapped both scarred hands around the cup like it was something rescued from a fire. Which, given her hands, struck me as either a habit or a joke she was telling herself.

"Honey?" I asked, holding up the jar.

"Yes," she said, and then, oddly, "you always—" and stopped herself, and drank.

We talked about ordinary things. The road, the weather, the state of the bridges east of the salt flats. She had a dry way of speaking that kept snagging on something in me, like a burr in wool — familiar without being identifiable, a tune heard through a wall. Twice she laughed, and both times I felt the laugh land somewhere below memory, in the body, where old knowledge lives. I didn't ask. People who arrive at my gate carrying grief get to decide how much of it to unpack, and this woman carried hers the way soldiers carry kit: compact, distributed, ready to move at speed.

But near the end, setting down her cup, she said: "Do you ever regret it? What you spent?"

I considered lying, out of hospitality. Then I considered the truth, out of respect. Truth won, narrowly.

"Every day," I said. "And no. Both, simultaneously, permanently. It's like being rich and robbed at the same time. I bought something enormous — I know I did, because the whole valley remembers differently now, because there are names in that ledger downstairs that appear in no crown record, because children in three provinces learn a version of the war that includes dragons on the *right* side of the story. I did that. Or a man wearing my hands did. And the same transaction emptied me out, and I live in the emptying like a tenant who can't meet the rent." I turned my cup. "Would I do it again? I honestly can't say. I don't remember doing it the first time. That's the joke, if you want it. The one story I can't verify is my own."

She was quiet a while. The geese muttered. Down the rows, the late beans rattled their dry pods, reseeding themselves, needing nobody.

"She'd be proud of you," the woman said at last.

"Who would?"

Something crossed her face — that weather again, that country. "Someone," she said. "Someone who knew you. Someone who owes you more than she can say, and came a long way to say part of it, and finds that the man she owes doesn't—" Her voice did something complicated and recovered. "Doesn't need the debt explained. That's all. Someone would be proud."

"Well," I said, because the alternative was watching her cry into my second-best cup, "tell them the tomatoes are excellent. That's the important intelligence. Everything else is commentary."

She smiled. It transformed her face entirely, scars and all, and for one unaccountable second I thought: *I know that smile. I have filed that smile.* And then it was gone, the way a fish-shadow goes, and the water closes, and you doubt yourself.

* * *

She stayed two days. She helped with the harvest — knew her way around bean rows suspiciously well — slept in the guest room, and on the second morning stood a long time at the shrine with her hand flat on the post, not lifting the veil, just resting it there, like a hand on a shoulder. I pretended to be busy with the well-rope and watched her sideways and said nothing, because some conversations happen fine without me.

When she left, at the gate, she pressed something into my hand. A scale. Dragon-scale, dark as a dried plum, warm past reason, with a faint pulse in it like a sleeping heart. I knew what it was instantly — the way you know a word in a language you thought you'd forgotten — and I also knew absolutely nothing about it, and both certainties arrived together and shook hands.

"What's this for?" I asked.

"For the ledger," she said. "It's testimony. The oldest kind. Seal of the last covenant — proof that the fire answered, once, to people who kept their word. It belongs in the true ledger, and the true ledger belongs to you." She hesitated. "It belonged to you before, too. You'll have to take my word for that."

"I'm afraid I take very little on anyone's word these days," I said. "Including mine."

"I know," she said softly. "That's rather the point of you."

Then she went down the road, south, toward the coast towns where the erased are building their records out of whatever survived, and I stood at the gate holding a piece of a dragon and feeling like a man handed a letter addressed in his own handwriting that he cannot read.

* * *

That evening I did my rounds. Watered the late beds. Fixed the well-rope properly, with a splice that would have earned approval from someone whose name I've lost. Gave the geese their grain and the dog his bone and the beans their silence, which is what they prefer.

Then I went to the vault, and opened the true ledger, and set the dragon-scale on the page beside the oldest testimony, and watched it sit there glowing faintly like a coal remembering heat. And I took up my pen — plain ink, plain hand — and wrote, because that is the whole of my remaining religion:

*A woman came today. Scarred hands. Dry humor. Knew the beans. Knew me, I think, though she had the grace not to say so. She brought a scale of the old fire and asked me to keep it, and I will, because keeping is what I do now — not speaking, not remembering, just keeping. I do not know her name. I suspect that once I knew it very well indeed. If she comes again, I will offer her tea, and honey, because I am told — I am almost told — that she takes it that way.*

I closed the ledger. I climbed the stairs into the dusk.

At the shrine I gave my report — rain coming, harvest in, a visitor, a gift — and the veil breathed in the evening wind, indigo going grey, and I stood there a while longer than usual, listening to the garden settle into itself. Somewhere under the tomato rows lay everything I'd burned. Somewhere under the veil lay everything I'd paid. And above both, in the cooling air, the swifts were doing their last circuits, screaming thin joy, and the first star came out over the mountain where the ember still burns, small and steady, enough to light.

Peace without wholeness. That's the crop this garden grows, and it comes in every year, and I have learned to call it enough.

I blew out the lamp and went in, and behind me the veil moved once, like a nod, like a wave goodbye, like a promise — and held.
