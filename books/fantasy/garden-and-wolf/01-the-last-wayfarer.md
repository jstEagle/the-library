## 1. The Last Wayfarer

The rain came sideways off the Thornfells, the way it always did in the last week of Harvestmonth, and Sebastian Montrose stood at the head of six mules and one nervous merchant and read the mountain like a page.

She had been reading it since she was seven years old, when her grandmother first put a staff in her hands and said, *the road tells you what it wants, girl, if you stop talking long enough to listen.* Fourteen years later, Sebastian had mostly stopped talking. The road still told her things.

Ahead, the trail bent north around a shoulder of black granite, and the wind funneled through the gap with a sound like a woman weeping into her sleeves. Below them, three hundred feet down, the Whitemere river ran gray with silt, swollen and fast. Above, the cloud ceiling hung low enough to touch if she'd been fool enough to climb for it.

"Is it safe?" Master Aldous Fenwick asked, for the eleventh time that day. He was a wool merchant out of Carrowgate, soft in the middle and softer in the nerve, and he had paid the Wayfarers' Guild its full fee—which was to say, a fee calculated for a guild that no longer existed at anything like its former size—and now he kept looking at the clouds as though they might owe him money too.

"It's safe," Sebastian said. "It's not comfortable. Those are different words."

"I paid for—"

"You paid for passage through the Thornfells before the passes close. You'll have it." She adjusted the strap of her pack and did not look at him while she said it, because looking at people while promising things made the promises feel heavier than they needed to be. "Stay behind me. Keep your mule's head turned from the drop. When I raise my hand, you stop walking, and you don't ask why until we're past it."

Fenwick's mouth opened, closed, opened again. "And if I need to ask why?"

"Then ask the mule. It understands the mountains better than you do."

One of his hired drovers snorted. Sebastian allowed herself the ghost of something that was not quite a smile, and turned up the trail.

* * *

The waystation at Greyfall Hollow had burned two winters ago, and nobody had rebuilt it.

Sebastian knew the exact night it went—Harvestmonth, the seventeenth, a clear cold evening with stars sharp as nailheads—because she had been eleven miles south at the Hollow Bridge station when the glow lit the underside of the clouds, and old Master Coram had stood in the doorway with his pipe unlit in his hand and said nothing at all for a long time. The fire had started, the story went, from a carelessly banked hearth. The story was almost certainly true. That was the worst part. Nobody had even bothered to burn it down on purpose. The guild was dying of neglect, not murder, which meant there was no one to hate and no one to fight, only the slow embarrassment of watching a thing fade because everyone who could have saved it had somewhere better to be.

She smelled the station before she saw it—that particular char-and-rain smell that old burns never quite lost, wood ash gone sweet and sour in turns with the seasons. Then the trees broke open and there it was: the long low roofline sagging in the middle where the beams had cooked, the stone chimney standing alone and absurd like a grave marker, the stable wall blackened to lace. Weeds came up through the flagstones of the yard. Someone had left a wagon wheel leaning against the well, rusted orange, furred with moss.

Behind her, Fenwick pulled up short. "Gods' teeth. What happened here?"

"Fire," Sebastian said.

"I can see that. Was anyone—"

"No." She had checked the records herself, afterward, because someone had to and no one else would. All four residents had been out on runs that night. The station had simply died empty, the way a body dies when the soul has already gone elsewhere. "Waystations are spaced a day apart along the high roads. The next shelter is Harrow Stone, six hours on. If the weather breaks worse, we make for it tonight instead of camping here."

"Camping? In *that?*"

"In the lee of it, yes. The chimney wall blocks the north wind, and the stones hold heat better than you'd think." She was already moving through the yard as she spoke, reading without seeming to read—the scuff patterns in the mud, a fox's prints, older hoofprints from some other caravan weeks gone, the way the weeds bent. Habit. Her grandmother's voice in her skull: *a guide who stops noticing is just a walker with opinions.*

Halfway across the yard she stopped.

There were fresh tracks after all. Boot prints, two sets, coming up from the southern switchbacks sometime in the last day—the edges still sharp, not yet softened by rain—and then leaving again toward the ridge path east. Not guild boots. Guild boots were resoled with the double-stitch pattern every member learned to recognize before their first solo run; hers had it, her grandmother's had had it, half the graves in the guildyard below Hollow Bridge had it. These were military-issue, broad-heeled, and heavy, the kind of tread the Crown issued to garrison soldiers and, more recently, to men who wore no colors at all.

Inquisitors traveled light and moved quiet. These had not been either.

Sebastian crouched, touched the print, felt the dampness still holding in the deepest part of the heel. This morning, maybe. Yesterday evening at the latest. They had come up the south road, looked over the ruin, and gone east toward the ridge—toward nothing, as far as any map showed, except old paths the guild had stopped maintaining a generation ago.

She straightened slowly and found Fenwick watching her with the expression of a man who has realized his guide is doing something he cannot see.

"Trouble?" he asked.

"Two travelers passed through," she said. "Headed east."

"That's all?"

"That's all." She smiled at him, the small efficient smile she kept for clients, the one that closed conversations like a door. "Water your mules. We eat cold. We move in an hour, and we move quiet—I don't like the feel of this hollow today."

It wasn't entirely a lie. She didn't like the feel of it. But the truth underneath the lie—the part about the boots, the part about the eastward tracks leading nowhere any honest traveler needed to go—she folded away into the same place she kept everything else that didn't fit neatly into a client's day. There would be time to think about it on the road. There was always time to think on the road. Thinking was the one thing the road gave freely, and the one thing walls never seemed to leave room for.

* * *

They made Harrow Stone by full dark, and it was there, drying her socks by the great hearth while Fenwick snored like a landslide in the bunkroom, that Sebastian let herself do the arithmetic she had been avoiding since Greyfall Hollow.

Harrow Stone was the last waystation on the western run still fully maintained. Its roof was whole, its stores were stocked, its shutters hung square in their frames. A hundred years ago there had been forty-one stations like it threaded through the Thornfells, each one staffed, each one a promise: *no traveler on a guild road will ever sleep under the open sky against their will.* The Wayfarers had built them with toll-coin and dragon-hoarded pride—or so the old songs said, though the songs also said the founders had ridden beasts out of legend, and sensible people didn't put much stock in that part anymore.

Now there were nine stations left standing. Three were staffed year-round. And the guild that had once mustered four hundred sworn guides—the guild whose sigil, the seven-pointed star over a winding road, had once been respected from the coast to the high passes—now consisted of exactly three living members.

Master Coram, seventy-one years old, whose knees had finally retired before he had, and who spent his days tending the records hall at Hollow Bridge and pretending the dust on the ledgers was someone else's fault.

Young Tam, nineteen, who had joined two years ago because his father had owed the guild money and settling debts with labor was cheaper than coin, and who guided the lowland milk-run twice a month and dreamed, loudly and constantly, of the capital.

And Sebastian. Twenty-one years old, fourteen of them spent on these roads, sworn at fifteen in a ceremony attended by four people, one of whom had been the cook.

Three members. Nine stations. Forty-one promises, thirty-two of them already broken.

She turned her sock in the firelight and thought about the boot prints going east.

East of Greyfall Hollow lay the Old Warden Paths. Every guide learned their names early—Wren's Ladder, the Sighing Stair, the Coldmother traverse—not because anyone used them but because the maps still carried them, faint ink ghosts beneath the newer roads, and the guild had never quite brought itself to strike them out. They had been closed before her grandmother's time. Closed by decree, sealed by order of the Crown, with a formality so total and so sudden that even the guildmasters of the era had recorded no reason in their books. Just a line in a ledger, in a clerk's careful hand: *By command, the eastern ways are shut. Let no charter pass beyond the boundary stones.*

Her grandmother had never spoken of why. Sebastian had asked, once, aged twelve, and received a look she had never forgotten—not anger, not fear, but something older and sadder, the look of a woman deciding how much of a wound to show a child. *"Some roads are closed because they're dangerous,"* her grandmother had said. *"And some roads are closed because someone powerful wanted them forgotten. Learn the difference. It matters."*

Sebastian had spent nine years learning the difference, and she still couldn't say which kind the eastern roads were. But soldiers had walked out of a burned waystation and onto them yesterday, and soldiers did not go sightseeing in the Thornfells in Harvestmonth.

She fed another split of pine to the fire and watched the sparks climb.

Here was the thing she did not say aloud, to Coram, to Tam, to the dark, because saying it would make it real in a way that hurt worse than keeping it: she loved the guild the way you love a parent who is dying slowly and refuses to discuss it. She loved the ledgers and the smell of the records hall, the double-stitch on her boots, the way a stranger's face changed at a waystation door when they understood that warmth and safety had been waiting for them specifically. She loved the covenant—the actual words, sworn on the star and the road, *to carry the traveler, to keep the way, to ask no reward but passage given forward.* She loved it all, and she was twenty-one, and she was fairly certain she would spend her life as the last person on earth who did, keeping empty buildings warm for travelers who no longer came, and being thanked by merchants like Aldous Fenwick in the tone one uses for a competent horse.

That was fine. That was—she searched for the word and settled on it with the grim precision she applied to everything—*sustainable*. Roads were easier than people. Roads made sense. A road told you exactly what it was: where it went, where it broke, where the rockfall had come down in the spring. People lied, and left, and died, and burned down waystations by accident, and there was no guidebook anywhere for the route a human heart took. Give her a whiteout on the Sighing Stair over a dinner party, any day of her life. Give her a flooded ford and a frightened mule over a conversation about feelings.

The fire settled. Rain worked at the shutters. Somewhere out in the black, an owl called twice and got no answer, and Sebastian thought, with a pang she refused to examine, that she knew exactly how it felt.

* * *

They came down off the high pass on the third morning, into the long green throat of the Vale Road, and by late afternoon the guildhall at Hollow Bridge rose out of the mist ahead of them—stone-built, slate-roofed, older than the bridge it was named for, with the seven-pointed star carved above the door and worn nearly smooth by weather and indifference.

Fenwick paid at the door, counting coins with the reluctance of a man parting from friends, and tipped her an extra silver, which she accepted because refusing tips confused clients more than accepting them. "You're the best guide I've had on these roads," he said, gruffly, meaning it, and then ruined it: "Shame about the guild, though. Really. A shame. You ought to have been born fifty years earlier, eh?"

"Yes," Sebastian said. "Good fortune on your road home, Master Fenwick."

He went. The yard emptied. The mist thickened. And Sebastian stood alone in the gateway of the Wayfarers' Guildhall of Hollow Bridge, listening to the building breathe.

It was a big building for three people. That was the thing no one warned you about—a dying institution doesn't shrink to fit. It just grows quieter, room by room, the way a house goes quiet after a funeral. The great common room with its forty-foot hearth, built for two hundred guides roaring drunk on midwinter night, now held one old man, one boy, and a chess set missing four pieces. The dormitories slept ghosts. The map room—the map room was the worst, forty feet of shelving holding charts of every pass and ford and goat-track in the Thornfells, lovingly maintained, meticulously updated, consulted by no one, because no one came to consult them. Sebastian still cleaned it every rest-day. She couldn't have said why. Some promises you keep even when the person you made them to is dead and the thing promised to is fading.

Coram was in the common room, in his chair by the hearth, a ledger open on his knees he hadn't turned a page of in an hour. He looked up when she came in, and his face did the thing it always did—brightened, then caught itself, the way you'd catch yourself smiling at a memory.

"The Fenwick wool run," he said. "Back already. Any trouble?"

"Rain, a merchant, and two sets of soldier's boots at Greyfall Hollow heading for the eastern paths."

The ledger did not get turned. Coram's eyes, pale and shrewd under white brows, held hers for a long moment. "Soldier's boots."

"Broad heel, Crown issue. No colors. They looked over the burn and went east, up toward the old warden ways, like they had business there."

For a moment the old man was silent, and in the silence Sebastian heard the fire tick and, far off, the bridge stream running high with rain. When Coram spoke again his voice had the careful flatness of a man stepping over something in the dark. "Eastern paths are closed. Have been for sixty years. Probably surveyors. The Crown does like its surveys."

"On the Sighing Stair? In Harvestmonth?"

"Perhaps they're thorough." He closed the ledger at last, gently, as if it might bruise. "You're wet, you're tired, and you've earned a rest. Tam's making stew. Eat, sleep, and on the morrow take your rest-day. The roads will still be here next week."

"And the boots?"

"Will be somebody else's problem, as they always are." But he said it looking at the fire, not at her, and his hand rested on the closed ledger the way a man rests a hand on a wound.

Sebastian unbuckled her pack in the doorway, hung her dripping cloak on the peg that had been her grandmother's peg, and did not push. That was the discipline of the guild: you read the road, you noted the hazard, and you chose your moment. Coram knew something. Or suspected something. Or feared something. Sixty years of closed roads sat behind his eyes like weather waiting to break.

She went down the corridor to the small room that had been hers since she was seven, set her boots by the door with the double-stitch facing out, and lay down on the narrow bed to listen to the rain.

Tomorrow she would check the records hall for the old surveys of the eastern paths. Tomorrow she would ask Tam, casually, whether he'd seen riders on the lowland roads. Tomorrow she would do what she always did—notice, note, and wait, invisible and competent, the last wayfarer keeping watch over a guild of three and a mountain full of closed doors.

She was asleep almost before she finished the thought, and she did not hear, through the rain and the dark and the thick stone walls of the guildhall, the knock that would come at the gate just past midnight—nor the sound of a body falling against it, nor old Coram's candle flaring alight in the window, nor the storm opening its throat over the Thornfells as if the mountain itself had begun, at last, to speak.
