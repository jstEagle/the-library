## 2. A Stranger at the Door

The storm came down the Thornfells like something with a grudge.

Sebastian Montrose heard it before she saw it—a low grinding in the west, the sound the old-timers called the mountain clearing its throat—and she had just enough time to bring the last of the caravan's mules under the overhang at Miller's Bend before the sky split open. Rain fell in ropes. The kind of rain that didn't fall so much as arrive, all at once, as if it had been waiting higher up for permission.

"Good timing," called Aldous Vetch from beneath his wagon, where he'd wedged himself with the particular misery of a man who owned wool and watched it get wet. "Another hour and we'd have lost the whole load."

"Another hour and you'd have lost more than the load." Sebastian shook water from her hood and crouched beside him, counting mules by habit—seven, eight, nine, all accounted for, ears flat, eyes rolling white. "This kind of storm takes carts off the switchbacks. Takes people too, if they're slow about getting under stone."

"You always this cheerful?"

"I'm always right. Cheerfulness is extra."

Vetch laughed, which was what she wanted. A laughing merchant was a merchant who didn't ask why a Wayfarer guide traveled alone these days, or why her guild badge—the little tin wolf that every member wore at the collar—was polished so carefully and yet never explained. He paid her half up front, as agreed, and told her she'd have the rest when they reached Dunmarrow in three days' time, and Sebastian settled against the wagon wheel to wait out the weather and did not think about anything at all.

That was the trick of it, the thing no one taught you because no one who knew it could bear to say it aloud: the road was easier than rooms. On the road you had tasks. Watch the footing, read the clouds, count the mules, count them again. A body occupied with tasks had no space left over for noticing that the guildhall in Harrowgate had held forty members once and now held three, or that the waystation at Miller's Bend—which they'd passed that morning, its roof burned to black ribs, its well choked with ash—had been the busiest stop on the eastern run when she was a girl, back when her grandmother still walked the roads and everyone knew the Wayfarers' green-and-grey.

She thought about the burned waystation anyway. She couldn't help it. It had been Torvald's station, once. Torvald who kept bees and told terrible jokes and had died two winters back with no one but Sebastian to bury him, out past the tree line where the ground was soft enough to dig.

Rain hammered the overhang. The mules dozed standing up. Somewhere above them, thunder rolled along the ridge like a barrel going down stairs.

*Three days to Dunmarrow,* she told herself. *Then home, then the next contract, then the next.* It was a good life, if you didn't look at it directly. Like an eclipse.

* * *

The storm broke sometime after midnight, and Sebastian took first watch because sleep had gone sour on her. She sat under the overhang with her cloak around her shoulders and the tin wolf in her hand, turning it over, rubbing her thumb across the worn engraving until she could feel the shape of it in her skin rather than see it.

That was how she heard the horse.

Or what had been a horse. It came down the old guild road at a stumbling canter, foam flying, and even in the dark Sebastian could tell the animal was running on nothing—running the way a log rolls downhill after the fire has eaten its heart. There was a rider bent low over its neck.

"Mules," she said quietly, and one of the hired hands woke with a grunt. "Stay here. All of you. If anyone comes down that road that isn't me, you keep the wagons closed and your voices down."

"Trouble?" Vetch asked from the dark.

"Maybe. Maybe just a fool riding a dead horse through a storm." She was already moving. "Either way, it's mine before it's yours. That's what you're paying for."

The guild road dropped toward Harrowgate in a long series of switchbacks, and Sebastian took them at a jog, boots finding the wet stone without needing light. Twenty years on these mountains. Her feet knew the road better than her eyes did.

She met the rider at the third bend, where the path widened beside a shrine to some road-god whose name had worn off the stone a century ago.

The woman made it three more steps past Sebastian before she fell.

Not slid, not stumbled—fell, straight down, like a puppet with cut strings, and Sebastian caught her mostly by accident, arms full of sodden wool and blood. There was so much blood. It had soaked through the rider's coat entirely, and it was warm, which meant it was still coming out of her somewhere important, and the smell of it hit Sebastian's hindbrain with a message older than language: *bad, bad, get away.*

"Hold on." She lowered the woman against the shrine. "Hey. Hold on. I've got you."

The woman's eyes opened. They were grey, startlingly light in the lightning-flash, and they fixed on Sebastian with a focus that frightened her more than the blood did—because dying people looked at the middle distance, at the sky, at nothing. This woman looked at *her*. Looked at her face, and then lower, at the tin wolf at her collar, and something moved across the ruined landscape of her expression that might have been relief.

"You're—" The voice was a ruin. "Guild. You're guild."

"Wayfarers," Sebastian said. "Last one, near enough. Who did this to you? Talk fast, I can't—"

The woman's hand came up. It was shaking badly, fingers black with cold and crusted red, and it clutched something flat against her chest the way a drowning woman clutches driftwood. For a moment Sebastian thought it was a shield, some scrap of armor, and then the lightning came again and showed her otherwise.

It was a map. Or it had been a map, once. It was made of overlapping pieces that clicked faintly against each other as the woman's hand trembled—pieces shaped like scales, broad at the base and tapering to a point, each one etched with lines fine as hair. They weren't metal exactly, and they weren't shell exactly, and in the strobing light they shifted color the way oil shifts on water: bronze, then green, then a deep ember-orange that seemed to hold light rather than reflect it.

Dragon-scale. The word arrived in Sebastian's mind uninvited, from some story her grandmother had told her by a fire twenty years dead, and she dismissed it just as quickly because dragon-scale was a story, and stories didn't bleed out on the Miller's Bend road.

"Take it." The woman pressed it into Sebastian's hands. Her grip had surprising strength left, the strength of the very last thing a body does. "They'll burn it. They burn everything. You have to—"

"I don't know what this is."

"You do." The grey eyes held hers. "Look at it. *Look* at it."

And Sebastian looked, because there was something in the woman's voice that was less a request than a key turning, and the map lay in her palms and the rain ran off it without wetting it—not a drop touched those scales, she would swear to that later, the water beaded and rolled as if from wax—and the ember-orange lines seemed to move, very slightly, the way coals breathe.

"The Ember," the woman whispered.

"What?"

"It goes to the Ember. Carry it—" A cough took her, a terrible wet sound, and when it passed she was smaller somehow, folded inward, spending herself. "Carry it to the Ember. Don't let the ash-men have it. Promise. Promise me."

"I'm not—" Sebastian heard her own voice crack and hated it. "I'm nobody. I guide merchants. I count mules. Whatever this is, you've got the wrong—"

The woman smiled. It was the worst thing yet, that smile—gentle, and certain, and utterly without doubt, aimed at Sebastian like sunlight through a crack.

"There you are," she said.

Then her hand slipped from the map, and her head tipped back against the shrine-stone, and the grey eyes went fixed on the rain instead of on Sebastian, and she was gone between one lightning-flash and the next, quiet as a candle pinched out.

Sebastian knelt in the flooded road holding a map that weighed almost nothing and felt like it weighed everything, and the storm went on falling, indifferent, and she found she was saying "no, no, no" under her breath in the voice of a much younger person, someone she hadn't been in years.

She checked anyway. Pressed two fingers to the woman's throat, waited through ten of her own hammering heartbeats, waited through ten more. Nothing. The blood had stopped moving; the body was already beginning to cool toward the temperature of the stone.

Lightning lit the shrine again, and Sebastian saw, for the first time, the woman's collar. Underneath the ruined coat, at the throat: a badge. Not a tin wolf. A small disc of black iron stamped with a stylized blade, crossed by a bar of ash-grey.

Sebastian had never seen one in her life. And some cold, wordless part of her, the part that had kept her alive on twenty years of bad roads, understood perfectly well that she was looking at the reason the woman was dead.

* * *

She carried the body the rest of the way to Harrowgate. There wasn't really a decision in it; you didn't leave people on the mountain, whatever they'd been carrying, whoever had chased them. That rule was older than the guild and would outlast it. She wrapped the woman in her own cloak, slung her across her shoulders—it was a hard carry, four miles of switchbacks with the rain trying to pull them both off the path—and reached the guildhall door an hour before dawn, soaked through, arms trembling, the scale-map sealed inside her shirt where it lay against her sternum like a second heartbeat.

Harrowgate's guildhall had been built to house sixty. It stood at the crook of the river where the valley narrowed, three storeys of good grey stone with the Wayfarers' sigil carved over the door—a wolf and a road, walking together—and these days only the ground floor was lived in, because heating the rest cost more than they had. The great hall's hearth was lit. Master Edran Corvane sat beside it with a ledger open on his knee, doing accounts by firelight, and he looked up when the door banged open and did not stand, because Edran did not stand for things anymore unless they were on fire.

He was seventy if he was a day, dry as old rope, and he had been guildmaster since before Sebastian could walk. He had taught her knots. He had taught her stars. He had taught her, without ever meaning to, that a man could love a thing and still watch it die without lifting a finger, because lifting fingers cost strength and Edran had decided decades ago to spend his on survival.

"Storm turned you back?" he asked.

"No." Sebastian laid the body down on the hall's long table, gently, arranging the cloak over the face. "Found her on the Miller's Bend road. Dead by the time I reached her. Bled out."

Edran set down the ledger. He came to the table slowly, joints cracking, and pulled back the cloak, and looked for a long moment at the stranger's face—at the grey eyes now shut, at the blood-blackened coat, at the iron badge Sebastian had turned face-up so there'd be no pretending otherwise.

Edran's breath went out of him slowly, the way air leaves a room when a door closes somewhere far away.

"Ashen Blade," he said.

"You know them."

"I know the stories." He covered the face again. His hands were steady, but it was the steadiness of a man holding himself still on purpose. "Everyone knows the stories. An order of knight-inquisitors, burned out of the southern kingdoms a hundred years ago for burning everything else. Everyone also knows they're dust. Extinct. A cautionary tale grandmothers tell."

"This grandmother isn't telling anyone anything anymore."

"How did she die, precisely?"

"Bled out, I said. Wounds under the coat—I didn't strip her, she deserved better than being butchered twice, but I saw enough. Sword cuts. More than one. Someone wanted her stopped, not killed; they cut her legs and her sword arm first and let the mountain finish it." Sebastian heard her own voice going flat and clipped, the guide's voice, the voice that reported facts, and was grateful for it. Facts were a kind of floor. "She was riding for Harrowgate. She knew the guild road. She saw my badge and she gave me something and she died."

"Gave you something." Edran's eyes came up. They were pale, watery, and sharper than they had any right to be. "What did she give you, Sebastian?"

And here was the moment. Sebastian knew it was the moment even as she stood inside it, the way you know the exact plank on a bridge that's going to give. She could take the map out and put it on the table and let Edran decide, because Edran was the master and she was the youngest of three and that was the shape of her whole life: other people deciding, while she watched the road.

Instead she said, "Her last words. That's all. She said 'carry it to the Ember,' and then she was gone."

Something flickered behind Edran's eyes. It was there and gone faster than the lightning, but Sebastian had spent a lifetime reading faces on bad roads, and she caught it: recognition. Brief and buried, but real.

"The Ember," he repeated.

"You know the word."

"I know a lot of words." He turned away, back toward the fire, and his voice flattened into the tone he used for closing discussions. "Here is what happens now. We dig a grave in the morning, east of the wall, no marker—we don't mark graves for strangers, it invites questions. You say nothing of this to anyone, including Tam and Ilse. As far as the world knows, no one came to this door tonight. And if anyone comes asking—riders, officials, anyone with questions about a woman on the road—you smile, you shrug, you know nothing. Can you do that?"

"She was murdered, Edran."

"People are murdered every day in the lowlands. It's why we live in mountains." He poked the fire harder than it needed. "We are three people in a hall built for sixty, girl. Our charter is a hundred years stale, our funds wouldn't cover a winter, and the Crown's men already look at us the way you look at a rotting fence post—wondering whether to bother knocking it down. Whatever that woman was carrying, whatever she was running from, it is bigger than us. Bigger than the guild has been in fifty years. We cannot fight it. We can only fail to be interesting to it. That is the whole art of surviving, Sebastian. Be uninteresting."

"I've spent my whole life being uninteresting," Sebastian said. "It's not an art. It's just disappearing slowly."

Edran looked at her then, really looked, and for a heartbeat she thought she saw something almost like grief in the old man's face—grief for her, maybe, or for the guild, or for the fact that he had taught her to survive and never taught her what surviving was for.

"Go dry off," he said. "Sleep. In the morning we bury her, and this night ends."

* * *

It did not end.

Sebastian climbed to her room in the corner of the ground floor—the smallest room, the cheapest, hers since she was sixteen—and barred the door and sat on the edge of her cot with the map in her lap, and told herself she was only keeping it safe until morning, when she would give it to Edran, or bury it with the woman, or throw it in the river. Any of those. All reasonable. All tomorrow's problem.

The map lay in the dark and breathed.

That was the only word for it. The scale-pieces rose and fell, very slightly, against each other, with a rhythm that was not quite the rhythm of her own pulse but close enough to make her skin crawl. And the ember-orange lines were brighter now, here in the dark, tracing their hair-fine ways across the scales—roads, she realized. They were roads. Mountain roads, switchbacks and passes, drawn in a style older than any chart she'd ever guided by, converging somewhere near the top of the piece, toward a mark like a flame lying on its side.

She should burn it. Edran would say burn it, and Edran was right, Edran was always right in the way that kept people alive and nothing else—

A fist hammered the hall's front door.

Sebastian was on her feet before she knew she'd moved, map stuffed into her shirt, heart slamming. Through her door's gap she heard Edran's tread cross the hall, unhurried, and the bar scrape, and then the night air came in wet and cold around the figures standing in the doorway: five riders in oiled cloaks, rain streaming off them, and at their head a tall woman whose bearing needed no introduction. Behind her shoulder, glimpsed in the lantern-light, the same black-iron badge the dead courier had worn.

"Master of this house?" the tall woman said. Her voice was pleasant. That was the worst part, later, remembering how pleasant it was. "Forgive the hour. We're searching for a missing courier. She would have come through these mountains tonight."

There was a silence in which Sebastian, pressed to the crack of her own door, discovered that her whole life had been practice for exactly one skill: not being seen.

"She'd have frozen on those roads," Edran said mildly. "Anyone out tonight would have. Bad storm."

"So she might have sheltered somewhere."

"If she did, it wasn't here. We're a poor house. We turn away more than we take in, these days."

The tall woman's gaze drifted past Edran into the hall—over the hearth, over the ledger, over the long table where a cloak still lay folded at one end, covering what it covered. Sebastian watched the woman's eyes touch that cloak and pause there, a pause no longer than a blink.

"Well," the inquisitor said pleasantly. "If she passes this way, send word to the garrison at Dunmarrow. We pay for information. We pay very well."

"We don't need paying," Edran said. "Good night to you."

The door closed. The bar scraped home. Boots faded into the rain.

Sebastian stood in the dark of her little room with her heart going like a drum and the map warm against her skin, warmer than it should be, and understood with total clarity that the night had not ended—that it had, in fact, only just begun, and that the invisible life she had built, plank by careful plank, had just been set alight.

In the morning they would bury the stranger east of the wall.

Sebastian looked down at the map glowing softly through the weave of her shirt, and knew—with dread, and beneath the dread, something worse, something that felt disgracefully like relief—that she would not be putting it in the grave.
