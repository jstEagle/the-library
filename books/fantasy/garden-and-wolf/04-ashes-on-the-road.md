## 4. Ashes on the Road

Sebastian left the guildhall before dawn, while the frost still held the puddles like small grey mirrors and the town had not yet decided to be awake. She went out through the smoke-door in the cellar, the one the guild had used for a hundred years when cargo needed moving without conversation, and she did not look back at the building that had raised her. Looking back was a habit for people who expected something behind them.

She carried what she always carried: rope, flint, dried meat wrapped in waxed cloth, a skin of watered wine, and the map.

The map rode against her ribs, sewn into the lining of her coat where her grandmother's journal had once ridden, and it was warm. It should not have been warm. Leather and scale and old ink did not hold heat like a living thing, but this one did, faintly, the way a stone holds the memory of sun long after sunset. When she pressed her palm flat against her own chest she could feel it answering, a slow pulse just beneath the fabric, patient as a second heartbeat.

*Burn it,* Aldric had said, standing over the dead woman with his jaw set like a man bracing against a blow. *Whatever she gave you, burn it, and we speak of this to no one.* But Sebastian had tried, alone in her room with the shutter closed, and the fire had slid off the thing like rain off oiled wool, and then it had glowed—glowed, gold-green, alive—and shown her roads she had walked all her life and never seen marked on any chart. Roads under roads. A thread running east into the Thornfells, toward a word the dying courier had spent her last breath on.

*The Ember.*

So now she ran. Not because she was certain the inquisitors would come for her—the grey-eyed one had asked his questions politely enough, had smiled even—but because she had watched that smile the way she watched weather, and weather like that meant a storm coming from a direction no one expected. The Wayfarers' first rule was *read the road before you read the rider*, and every instinct she owned told her the road out of Thornfell was about to be very badly ridden indeed.

She took the old ways. Not the King's Road, wide and honest and crawling with eyes, but the guild roads—the smugglers' veins of the mountains, cut by her grandmother's generation and maintained since by fewer and fewer hands until only hers remained. Paths that threaded scree fields and slipped beneath hanging forests of pine, crossed streams three times to kill pursuit, ducked through hollows so narrow she turned sideways to pass. Her feet knew them the way other people knew prayers. For two days she moved north and east, sleeping in the open with her back to stone, eating cold, speaking to no one, and it was—she admitted it only in the dark honesty before sleep—the closest thing to happiness she had felt in years.

Alone on a road, she was not invisible. Alone on a road, she was exactly as large as she chose to be, which was large enough to move a mountain if the mountain cooperated.

The trouble began on the third morning, when she found the waystone at Miller's Bend defaced.

It was an old marker, chest-high, carved with the guild's sigil—a stylized road splitting around a flame—and generations of Wayfarers had touched it for luck. Someone had chiseled the flame out. Not weathered, not broken: *chiseled*, deliberately, recently, the fresh scars pale against a century of lichen. And below it, burned into the stone itself with a heat that had cracked the granite, was a mark she did not know: a sword drawn through a ring of ash.

She stood before it a long time, breath steaming, reading the mountain the way she'd been taught. The chisel-work was days old. The burn was fresher. And there were boot-prints in the frost-melt mud—not one pair. Six, maybe seven, moving in formation, spaced like soldiers rather than travelers, heading the same direction she was going.

Someone was walking the old guild roads ahead of her. Someone who hated the flame on the stones.

Sebastian did not like coincidences. Coincidences were how the mountain killed careless guides; she had buried two of them. So she left the main vein at Miller's Bend and dropped onto a secondary path along the ravine country—a route so old it wasn't on any chart but the map's, though when she checked, the scale-patterned thing against her ribs pulsed warmer, almost approving, and showed her the way in lines of soft gold light that faded when she blinked.

That was how she came to hear the crows.

Ravens, properly. A dozen of them working the air above the next ravine in that particular circling, dipping way that meant carrion, and meaning it persistently—they were not leaving, which meant whatever they had found was too large to finish or too fresh to trust. Sebastian went forward low and quiet, bow unstrung across her back but arrows loose in her belt, and edged along the lip of the gorge until she could look down into it.

The ravine was narrow, choked with frost-blasted alder and the bones of a rockslide, and at its bottom, half-buried in scree, was a man.

He was armored—or had been. The plate was blackened as if it had been near a fire, and dented in the way armor gets when someone has taken it off a body and put it on a living man who then declined to die politely. One arm ended above the elbow in a crude tourniquet gone brown with old blood. He lay on his side in the shadow of a boulder, and he was not moving, and the ravens were patient.

Sebastian should have passed him by. That was the smart play, the guild play: a dying armed man in a ravine is a trap wearing a person's shape, and she had a map worth killing for sewn into her coat and no time to spend on charity. She even took three steps along the rim, putting him behind her.

Then she saw the mark on his pauldron, half-scraped away but legible in the slanting light: a sword drawn through a ring of ash.

The same sigil that was burned into the waystone. The same men whose boots pointed east, ahead of her, toward the Ember.

She stood on the rim of the ravine with the wind pulling at her hood and understood that she was looking at either her death or her answer, and that the difference between those things might be a few questions asked quickly.

* * *

Getting down was easy; getting back up with a grown man would not be. She descended the slide-loose slope in a controlled rush, scattering the ravens, and knelt beside him with her knife already drawn and resting against the underside of his jaw before she touched him anywhere else.

His eyes opened. They were grey, bloodshot, and utterly unsurprised, which frightened her more than fear would have.

"Took you long enough," he rasped.

"Say another word and I'll open your throat," Sebastian said. "Nod or shake your head. Are there others nearby?"

A pause. Then, slowly, he shook his head.

"Are they coming back?"

This time the pause was longer, and something moved behind his eyes that was not quite a lie and not quite the truth. He shook his head again, but she caught the flicker, the half-second hesitation of a man deciding whether hope was worth the breath.

"You're Ashen Blade," she said. "I've seen your mark on my stones."

"Yes." His voice was gravel ground fine. "And they left me here to die, so you may draw your own conclusions about my current standing in the Order."

"Conclusions are cheap. I want facts. Why did they leave you?"

"Because I wouldn't do what I was ordered to do." He said it flatly, the way a man says a thing he has said to himself many times in the dark until it has worn smooth. "There was a farmstead in the foothills. The Order believed a courier had hidden documents there. The family—" His jaw worked. "The family was in the way of the questioning. I refused to help question them. Ser Halvard broke my arm for it and left me for the ravens. He said a knight who has lost his stomach has lost his use."

"And your stomach," Sebastian said, "came back?"

"No." He looked at her then, fully, and up close his face was younger than his voice—younger than she'd guessed, thirty perhaps, with a week of beard and exhaustion carved deep. "My stomach is still gone. What's left is spite."

She kept the knife where it was. "You know what I have in my coat."

"I know what the Order thinks you have in your coat." Something kindled in his grey eyes, tired but steady. "They've been hunting a courier's cargo for a month. Vane wants it personally. Whatever it is, it matters enough that seven blades walked these hills in the cold, and enough that they'll walk them again. You're carrying it, and you're heading east, and I'm the only man in these mountains who can tell you where they'll be before they're there."

"Or you're bait, planted in a ravine, waiting for a fool to fish you out."

"If I were bait," he said, with the ghost of something that was almost humor, "wouldn't I be in better condition?"

It was, absurdly, a fair point. Sebastian looked at him—at the ruined arm, the blackened armor, the scraped-off sigil he hadn't bothered to finish removing—and made the mistake of thinking about it instead of simply walking away. Thinking was how the mountain got you. Feeling was worse.

But she remembered the waystone at Miller's Bend, the flame chiseled out, and something hot and unfamiliar moved in her chest. These people had put their mark over her grandmother's flame. They had walked her roads with soldier's boots. They had made a farmstead family into *questioning*.

"What's your name?" she asked.

"Corwin. Corwin of Halemark, formerly of the Order of the Ashen Blade, currently of nowhere at all."

"Well, Corwin of nowhere at all." She sheathed the knife and pulled the good rope off her shoulder. "You're going to tell me everything the Order knows, everything they plan, and everywhere they'll be. And if you lie to me once—if once—I leave you for the ravens myself, and I won't lose any sleep."

"I would expect nothing less," he said solemnly, and then spoiled it entirely by fainting.

* * *

Getting him up the ravine took the better part of two hours and cost her most of her rope, half her water, and nearly all her patience. Twice the scree shifted and nearly took them both down; the second time he woke enough to wedge himself against the slope and take weight off his ruined arm without being told, which told her something about him that words hadn't. Soldiers who thought under pain were rare. She'd known whole caravans that couldn't manage it.

By late afternoon she had him propped against a waystone in a hollow above the ravine, out of the wind, and she fed him snowmelt and a strip of dried meat soaked soft. He ate like a man relearning the habit.

"The arm needs proper setting," she said, probing it carefully above the tourniquet. He didn't cry out, which she noted. "You'll lose some of it if we don't find a healer."

"There's a village. Highmoor, two days east. They don't love the Order there."

"They don't love anyone there. Fine. Two days." She sat back on her heels and studied him. "Start talking. Why does Vane want my cargo?"

"I don't know what it is." He said it simply, and she watched his eyes and found no flicker. "I know what Vane believes. I served under her for six years. She believes dragons are abominations—that the fire in them is a corruption older than the Crown, and that every war ever fought in the Thornfells traces back to it. She doesn't want your map for power, guide. She wants it so she can burn whatever it leads to and salt the ashes."

"And you believed that? Once?"

The question landed harder than she'd intended, and she watched it land. He was quiet a long moment, turning the meat-strip over in his remaining hand without eating it.

"I believed in the Order," he said finally. "Not in Vane. There's a difference, or there was, when I swore. We were supposed to be shield-brothers against the dark things. Somewhere along the way the dark things became anything Vane pointed at." He looked down at his mangled arm. "I told myself the orders were lawful. Lawful is a comfortable word. It lets you do terrible things and call it duty." His gaze came up and fixed on her, and there was shame in it, raw and unhidden, and it was that—not the wound, not the information—that made her believe him. "I executed a village headman last spring because he sheltered a traveler the Order suspected of carrying dragon-relics. The traveler was a peddler. The relics were painted toys. I did it anyway, because refusing meant becoming what I am now: hunted, crippled, and lying in a ravine waiting for the birds."

Sebastian said nothing. The wind moved through the alders, and somewhere far off a raven complained.

"Why are you telling me this?" she asked at last.

"Because you looked at me the way everyone looks at the Order, and you weren't wrong to." He swallowed. "And because if I'm going to ask you to trust me, you should know precisely what kind of thing you're trusting. I'm not a good man, guide. I'm a man who was very good at being bad for a very long time, and who has recently run out of excuses. If that's useful to you, use me. If it isn't, leave me here, and I'll understand."

It was, she thought, the most honest speech anyone had given her in ten years of guiding liars and merchants and smugglers through these hills. It disgusted her a little, how much it moved her.

She should send him away. Every rule of survival said so: he was wounded, he was hunted, he was a former officer of the enemy, and the map against her ribs was warm and alive and worth more than both their lives. Taking him meant slower travel, split food, a trail a blind man could follow.

Taking him also meant eyes inside the Order. Meant knowing where seven sets of boots would be before they arrived. Meant, though she hated the word, company.

"I don't trust you," she said.

"I haven't earned it."

"But you're going to earn it, starting now, because I need what you know and you need what I have, which is a road out of these mountains that the Order doesn't." She stood, shouldering her pack, and looked down at him with what she hoped was iron and suspected was something softer showing through. "Can you walk?"

He tested his legs against the stone, grimaced, and nodded. "If we go slowly."

"We go slowly." She turned east, toward Highmoor and the darkening peaks, and then paused. "Corwin. The mark on the waystones. The flame with the road around it. Do you know what it means?"

He frowned. "The Order calls it the Warden's Sigil. Old thing. Vane has lists of where they appear—she burns them when she finds them." He paused, and she saw him arrive at the conclusion a beat after she did. "Your guild uses it."

"My guild *is* it," Sebastian said quietly, and the map against her ribs pulsed warm, like something listening. "And I think that's why Vane wants what I'm carrying. Walk. We've lost enough light."

They went east together into the blue dusk, a disgraced knight limping after the last Wayfarer, neither of them trusting the other, both of them knowing—with the particular certainty of people who have been abandoned—that being left behind again was the one thing they could not survive twice.

Behind them, far down the ravine, the ravens rose all at once, wheeling, silent, as if something had startled them out of the trees.

Something was coming up the valley.
