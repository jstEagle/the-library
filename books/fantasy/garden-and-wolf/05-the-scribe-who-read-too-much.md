## 5. The Scribe Who Read Too Much

Wren had learned to read by candle-ends, the stubs other scribes threw away, and she had learned, in the same guttering light, that knowledge was the only currency that never devalued. Ink could be scraped. Parchment could be reused. But a thing once known stayed known, even in a girl with no family name, no patron, and no coin worth the weighing.

She was thinking about currency now because she had exactly none of it, and because the man across the tavern table thought he was buying her supper.

"You said you could copy anything," he said. He was a wool-factor with ink-stained fingers of his own, which meant he'd tried to forge his own ledgers and failed at it. "Hand like a printing press, they say."

"They say correctly." Wren dipped her bread in the pottage without eating it. The tavern in Millbrook sat low under the eaves of the Thornfell foothills, all smoke and tallow-smell, and she had been here nine days, long enough for the innkeeper to start looking at her sideways. Nine days was too long anywhere. "Ledgers, letters, charters. I can age paper. I can match any secretary hand you bring me."

"And you'll not ask what it's for."

"I never ask." She smiled the smile that had gotten her fed from Harrowgate to the border: pleasant, empty, a door closed politely in the face. "Asking is how scribes end up in rivers."

The wool-factor laughed, paid, and left her his sample pages. Wren carried them up to her rented loft — a straw pallet, a broken shutter, a view of nothing — and copied them twice for practice, then sat a long while in the dark listening to the rain come off the mountains.

Nine days. In the Order's scriptorium at Candlemas House, nine days of absence would have earned her a notice pinned to every chapter-house door from here to the sea: *Wren of the Undercroft, fled with documents, to be taken alive.* Taken alive, always. Dead scribes told no tales, but dead scribes also couldn't be questioned about who else had read what.

She hadn't meant to take anything. That was the truth she clung to on bad nights, the truth she turned over like a coin worn smooth. She had only meant to *read*.

The Order of the Ashen Blade kept its archives the way misers kept gold: locked, catalogued, and counted nightly. Wren had spent four years in the Undercroft copying decrees — beautiful work, terrible work, the kind of work where you transcribed a sentence ordering a village burned and then went to supper as though your hands were still clean. She had told herself for four years that copying was not doing. Then, one winter evening, shelving a folio that didn't belong on its shelf, she had found the margin note.

*Subject recalls too much. Recommend transfer to the Questioners.*

It was written beside a deposition from a shepherd in the Thornfells, a man who claimed — in careful, terrified script — that he had seen fire fall from the sky over the high valleys, and that it had not fallen the way lightning falls. It had fallen the way something *lands*.

Wren should have shelved it. She had shelved a thousand such things. Instead she had read the whole file, and then the file cross-referenced in its margins, and then the file after that, night after night, a thief in her own house, until she understood two things beyond any possibility of unknowing them.

First: the Order believed dragons still lived.

Second: the Order intended to find the last of them and burn them out of the sky, and it had spent thirty years and uncounted lives preparing to do it — and it called this preparation *peacekeeping*, and wrote the word in gold leaf on the dedication page of its founding charter, and made girls like Wren copy that page eleven hundred times until the lie looked like calligraphy.

She had run with nothing but the clothes she wore and the memory of everything she'd read, which was, she reminded herself now, huddled under her one blanket in the Millbrook loft, worth more than any purse. Knowledge was currency. She only had to find someone willing to trade.

The problem was that everyone who might pay for what she knew wanted her dead for knowing it.

Rain hammered the broken shutter. Somewhere below, the innkeeper's dog barked at the storm, and Wren lay awake cataloguing her assets like a merchant: she could forge anything, cipher anything, speak three tongues and read five. She knew the Order's internal routes, its safehouse signs, the rotation of its questioners. She knew that Inquisitor Vane had left Candlemas House in the spring with a warrant sealed in black wax, and that black wax meant the target was a person rather than a place, and that Vane did not come back from warrants empty-handed.

And she knew — this was the newest entry in her ledger, added just that morning when a mud-spattered courier had thundered through Millbrook without stopping, heading up into the Thornfells — that something was happening on the mountain roads. Something that made the Order move.

Her asset list needed one more line, and she wrote it in her head in her neatest secretary hand: *Find out what.*

* * *

She found out, three days later, from a dying woman's map.

Not directly. Nothing in Wren's life came directly anymore; everything arrived sideways, wrapped in coincidence, the way contraband arrived. What happened was this: on the morning of the third day, hungover with sleeplessness, she went down to the millstream to wash her one spare shift, and found the road above the mill crowded with riders in grey-and-cinder livery, and understood with a cold drop through her stomach that the Ashen Blade had come to Millbrook, population forty, for no reason that had anything to do with wool.

She did the sensible thing. She picked up her bundle of wet linen and walked, unhurried, in the opposite direction, along the stream, out past the tannery, into the alder woods where the ground went soft and the noise of the village died. Only when the mill was well behind her did she let herself think about what grey-and-cinder livery meant, and by then her hands were shaking so badly she dropped the shift in the mud and left it there.

They were asking questions. She heard it later, secondhand, from a drover she fell in walking with on the high track: riders going door to door, showing a sketch of a woman, dead these ten days or more, and asking after the woman's *effects*. A courier, the sketch said. A courier carrying documents.

Wren stood in the drover's yard with her heart going like a hammer and asked, in the flat curious tone of a person with nothing to hide, what documents, and the drover shrugged and said nobody knew, but the Blade men had torn apart the tollhouse at the ford and burned the tollkeeper's shed besides, and were asking after a guildhall too. Some wayfarer place up the valley. The last one, they said. Run by a woman named Montrose.

Sebastian Montrose. Wren had copied the name before. It surfaced in her memory with the particular clarity of things read in dangerous places: *Montrose, S., licensed guide, Thornfell circuits* — a marginal entry in a routing ledger, one of hundreds, except that Wren had noticed, because noticing was her disease and her gift, that Montrose's circuit licenses had been renewed every year for fifteen years by the same clerk, and that clerk's signature matched the signature on a requisition order for wyvern-oil stores dated the year of the Night of Cinders.

A small thing. A thread. Wren had followed threads like that for four years in the archives, and the threads had taught her that in the Order's vast woven record, nothing was truly separate. Everything connected, if you pulled gently enough.

So: an Ashen Blade courier, dead. Documents missing. An old wayfarer guide named Montrose, whose paperwork touched the Order's oldest secrets. And the Blade tearing up the foothills like men hunting a spark in a haybarn.

Wren stood in the drover's yard and did the arithmetic of her life. Option one: keep running west, alone, forever, waiting for some checkpoint clerk to look too closely at her papers — forged beautifully, but forged. Option two: go toward the danger, toward the guildhall and the guide, and offer the only thing she had.

Knowledge was currency. It was time to spend it.

* * *

She caught up with Sebastian Montrose on the fourth day, on the old guild road above the Greywater falls, and she knew it was her before she saw her face, because the woman walked like nobody Wren had ever met: not hurrying, not hiding, simply *moving*, with the easy economy of someone for whom distance was a language and she was fluent in it. Tall-ish, weathered, hooded, leading a mule that looked better cared-for than most children. A knight limped half a step behind her — Wren clocked the sword, the ruined surcoat, the Ashen Blade training in every line of him, and felt her stomach tighten — and beside the knight trotted a small figure in an oversized cloak whose gait was wrong in a way Wren couldn't name until it turned its head and she saw the fine bronze scales along its jaw.

Well, she thought, standing in the bracken with her bundle on her shoulder. There goes option two being simple.

"Hail the road," she called, using the old wayfarer greeting, because if you were going to gamble, gamble loudly.

All three stopped. The knight's hand went to his sword. The scaled child made a sound like a kettle starting to boil. And the guide — Sebastian Montrose — turned and looked at Wren with eyes the color of wet slate, and Wren watched those eyes do in two seconds what an inquisitor's did in an hour: weigh her, price her, decide whether she was worth the trouble of killing.

"Nobody uses that greeting anymore," Montrose said. Her voice was low and roughened, a road-worn voice. "Where'd you learn it?"

"In an archive." Wren came down the slope slowly, palms visible, the universal grammar of harmlessness. "I've learned a great many things in archives. For instance, I know why the Ashen Blade is hunting you, and I know what your courier was carrying, and I know—" she paused at the edge of their little circle, close enough to talk, far enough to bolt— "that the child with you shouldn't be on an open road, because Vane's outriders crossed the Greywater ford yesterday morning, six of them, moving fast."

That landed. She saw it land — saw the knight and the guide exchange a look over the child's head, quick and practiced, two people who had already learned each other's signals.

"Who are you?" the knight demanded.

"Wren. No surname worth the saying." She shifted her bundle. "Formerly of Candlemas House, Undercroft division, Order of the Ashen Blade. I copied their decrees for four years. Then I read too much, and now I'm here, offering you a trade."

"A trade," Montrose repeated. Flat. Unimpressed. But she hadn't told Wren to leave, and Wren had survived enough interrogations-by-conversation to know that not-being-told-to-leave was the whole game.

"You want to know what the map is," Wren said. "You've looked at it. You've seen the paths glow, maybe — blood-marked scale-work does that, it's old craft, older than the Order. But glowing paths aren't the same as *reading* them. The map isn't drawn in wayfarer notation. It's drawn in warden notation, and there are maybe four people alive who can parse warden notation, and I'm one of them, because I spent a winter transcribing the fragmentary journals of a woman named Maren Coalharrow, who was a Wayfarer before the covenant broke." She let that sit. "In exchange, I want passage. Out of the Order's reach, east over the Thornfells, wherever you're going. Food, road-cover, and your word you don't sell me to anyone. That's the whole price."

The child made the kettle-sound again, softer, and tugged Montrose's sleeve, and whispered something Wren couldn't hear. Montrose listened the way she walked — completely, without hurry — and whatever the child said made her look back at Wren with something new in the slate-grey eyes. Not trust. Interest, maybe. The interest of a gambler seeing an unfamiliar card.

"Pip says you're telling the truth," Montrose said. "Pip says you smell like old paper and fear, and that the fear is mostly for somebody else." A pause. "Is that right? Is the fear for somebody else?"

Wren opened her mouth to give the polished answer, the deflection, the pleasant closed door — and found, to her considerable annoyance, that she didn't want to use it. Something about this woman's stillness made lying feel like wasting both their time.

"My mother was a Thornfell shepherd," Wren said instead. "Before she died. There was a deposition in the archives, from a shepherd in the high valleys, a man who saw fire land on the mountains like a living thing. The Order flagged him. *Recalls too much.* I used to tell myself copying wasn't doing. I don't tell myself that anymore." She hugged the bundle tighter. "So yes. The fear is for everybody. It's for the whole world, if Vane finds what she's looking for. Does that pass your test?"

Montrose studied her a moment longer. Then, unexpectedly, almost against her will, the corner of her mouth moved — not quite a smile, but the ghost of where a smile might live on a face that had forgotten the address.

"Old paper and fear," she said. "I know the smell. I wear it most days." She jerked her chin up the road, north, into the darkening shoulders of the Thornfells. "Walk with us, then, scribe. You get tonight to prove the notation claim. If you can't read the map by morning, you walk back down the way you came."

"That's fair."

"And Wren." Montrose had already turned, but she spoke without turning, the words tossed back over her shoulder like a coin to a beggar — except they weren't charity, Wren realized; they were payment, honestly rendered. "The child hears lies. So does the map, according to some accounts. So whatever you're not telling me — and everyone's not telling me something — hold onto it loosely. Roads have a way of shaking things loose anyway."

They walked. The knight — Corwin, the child called him, in a voice like wind over a bottle — fell in beside Wren and watched her sidelong with the wary arithmetic of a man deciding whether she was a hostage or a bomb. The child, Pip, drifted close instead, unafraid, nostrils flaring, and announced to the road at large: "She smells like the archive under the mountain. Like dust and candle-ends and locked doors."

"Guilty," Wren said.

Above them the first stars were coming out over the Thornfells, sharp and cold, and somewhere in that darkness a map rode folded against a guide's chest, humming its slow burnt-gold hum, drawing them all toward a word Wren had seen exactly once, in a margin, in a dead woman's handwriting: *Ember*.

That night, by firelight, with three strangers watching, Wren unrolled her memory of Maren Coalharrow's journal and began, stroke by stroke, to teach the last Wayfarer how to read her inheritance — and if her hands shook a little as she drew the first sigil, well. Fear and hope, she had learned, felt identical in the fingers.

It was only afterward that you could tell them apart.
