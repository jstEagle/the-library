## 6. Ghosts of the Guild

The waystation at Hollowmere had been dead for eleven years, and the mountains had begun to take it back.

Sebastian saw it first as a shadow against the gathering dusk—a squat stone shape crouched where the old guild road bent around the shoulder of the fell, its slate roof half-collapsed, its chimney standing like a broken tooth. Ivy had climbed the eastern wall in a green tide. The door hung on one hinge, swaying in a wind that smelled of coming snow.

"Shelter," she said, and it wasn't a suggestion.

Corwin looked up from the road, gray-faced. Three days of hard walking since the ravine, and the wound along his ribs had gone from angry red to a sullen purple that Sebastian didn't like at all. He'd stopped complaining about it somewhere past the second ridge, which worried her more than any groaning would have. Men who stopped complaining were either healing or dying, and Corwin did not look like a man who was healing.

Wren came up beside him, hugging the map-case to her chest as though it might bolt. The girl had barely spoken since they'd left the crossroads shrine—had barely slept, either, if the shadows under her eyes meant anything—but she read the road the way she read everything, with that hungry, cataloguing attention, and when she saw the waystation her shoulders dropped half an inch.

"That's a guild mark," she said quietly, nodding at the lintel above the door. "The crossed staff and the seven stars."

"It is." Sebastian pushed the door open with her boot. It gave a long, mournful groan, like something waking unwillingly.

Inside, the dark was thick with dust and the smell of cold ash. A common room, once: a hearth big enough to roast a goat, trestle tables shoved against the walls, bunks along the far side behind a partition. Someone had left in haste or in grief—the tables still bore the pale rings where mugs had sat, and a wooden cup lay on its side in the corner, rolled there by some draft years ago and never touched again.

Sebastian stood in the doorway a moment longer than she needed to. She knew this place. Not this waystation specifically—Hollowmere was two hundred miles from anywhere she'd walked as a girl—but she knew *this place*, the species of it. She had grown up in rooms like this one, in a dozen stations strung along the Thornfells like beads on a fraying cord, each one warm with firelight and voices and the particular music of travelers arguing about routes. Her grandmother's voice lived in these rooms. *Mind the third step, Sebby, it lies.* *Never trust a bridge you haven't cursed yourself.*

She hadn't thought of that voice in years. It hurt more than she expected to hear it now.

"Fire," she said aloud, to break the spell, and started hauling boards from the broken partition.

* * *

They got a blaze going in the great hearth, and the room changed. Firelight found the shapes of things and made them almost friendly: the scarred table, the iron hooks where packs once hung, the soot-blackened beam overhead carved with names and dates going back further than anyone could read. Wren discovered the carving within an hour and spent another hour tracing letters with her fingertip, murmuring, building a census of the dead out of initials and years.

Corwin slept—or tried to. He lay on one of the bunks with his sword across his knees even now, even here, and his breathing had a wet edge to it that Sebastian catalogued and refused, for the moment, to examine too closely.

The fever would come tonight or tomorrow. She'd seen enough wounds go bad to know the signs. Which meant they needed to stay put regardless of what her instincts screamed about staying ahead of pursuit, and if they were staying put anyway—

Her eye kept drifting to the far end of the common room, where a low archway led to what had been the station-keeper's office. The door there had held, mostly. And guild stations kept records. Ledgers, route-books, correspondence. Eleven years abandoned, but paper was patient.

She told herself she was looking for anything useful—old maps, caches, word of safe roads. That was the professional justification, and it was even true. But underneath it, honest underneath, was something else. Something that had been growing in her since the night a dying woman had pressed a dragon-scale map into her hands and changed the shape of Sebastian's whole life with four whispered syllables.

She wanted to know what the Wayfarers had been. Before they were three people in a falling-down hall, before they were smugglers and guides for hire, before the guild became a name that made merchants shrug instead of bow. Somebody had built these stations. Somebody had carved stars over every door between the coast and the high passes. Somebody had believed in something worth walking two hundred miles of wilderness to maintain.

Sebastian had inherited the name and never asked what came with it.

She took a candle and went through the archway.

* * *

The office was smaller than she remembered such rooms being, though of course she'd never stood in *this* one. A desk under the window, its top furred with dust so deep it held her fingerprints like snow. Shelves along one wall, most of their contents rotted to pulp or eaten to lace by mice. A chair. A cold stove. On the desk, a ledger lay open where some last keeper had left it mid-entry, the ink faded to the brown of dried blood.

*Received of Aldous Vey, one consignment, wool, twelve bales,* it read. *Roads good to the Stair. Rain below.*

Sebastian read it twice. Twelve bales of wool, and a note about weather, written by a hand that had been dust for a decade, in a room that no one had entered since. There was something unbearable in it. The smallness. The ordinary faith of it—that someone would come after, that the record mattered, that the guild went on.

She set the ledger down carefully, the way you set down something sleeping.

The shelves were worse than the desk. Damp had gotten into the lower ones and turned the ledgers there into bricks of mold. But the top shelf, dry beneath the roof's one intact slope, had fared better, and she worked through it bundle by bundle: route surveys, toll agreements, a sheaf of complaints from a wool merchant whose name matched the open ledger, a thin book of station accounts. Guild business. Smuggling business. The life she knew.

Near the end of the shelf, wrapped in oilcloth tied with cord gone brittle as biscuit, she found a packet that was different.

She knew it was different before she unwrapped it. The oilcloth was finer, older, stitched rather than glued at the seams, and the cord was waxed silk—the kind of thing a careful person used for things they meant to survive. Her fingers were steady. She noticed that. Whatever was coming, some part of her had already decided to meet it without flinching.

Inside were papers. Letters, mostly, tied in a second wrapping. And beneath the letters, a book. A journal, bound in gray leather gone soft as cloth, its corners worn round, its spine cracked in the particular way of a book that has been opened ten thousand times by hands that loved it.

Sebastian turned it over. On the back cover, stamped into the leather in gilt that had dulled to a ghost of gold, was a sigil: a circle crossed by a winding line, like a road passing through a ring.

She had seen that mark before. Once, as a child, burned into the underside of her grandmother's traveling trunk, hidden beneath the false bottom where the real cargo rode. She had asked about it and been told it was nothing, an old maker's stamp, mind your own nose, Sebby.

She sat down in the keeper's chair—it held, creaking—and opened the journal.

* * *

The early pages were route-notes. Water sources, pass conditions, which village headmen could be trusted and which would sell you to the toll-men. Her grandmother's handwriting, unmistakable—the same sharp downstrokes Sebastian saw in her own hand every time she signed a waybill. Reading it was like hearing a voice across a grave.

Then, a third of the way in, the entries changed.

*Met with the others at Highmoor station. All nine wardens present, first time in six years. Maren says the signs are worsening. The stones are cooling.*

*Wardens.* Sebastian frowned at the word. The page before had been about a shipment of salt.

*Took the oath renewal at the Ember-stone with Tamsin and the Vane boy looking on. Grandmother Ysolde's flame held true. She is weaker every year, but she let us warm our hands at it, and Pip—*

The entry stopped there, ink blotting where the pen had rested too long. Sebastian turned pages faster.

*Betrayal. I hardly can write it. Harrow sold the outer eyries' approaches to the Order of the Ashen Blade. Sold the roads he himself swore upon. They came at moonset with fire-pots and the new wyvern-oil, and by dawn the eyries were slag and the clans are calling it the Night of Cinders. We lost forty wardens holding the passes so the young ones could be gotten away. Tamsin among them.*

*The covenant is broken. The Order declares dragons abomination, and the Crown backs them, and the clans are frightened enough to look away. What can nine wardens do? What can three?*

*We will not carry the name "warden" anymore. Too many died wearing it openly. We become what we must: guides, carriers, quiet folk on quiet roads. But we keep the stations. We keep the roads. And we keep watch. If ever the Ember needs lighting again, the roads must be there to walk it by.*

*Let the guild forget, if forgetting keeps them alive. I will not forget. I am writing this so that someone, someday, does not have to.*

Sebastian became aware, distantly, that her cheeks were wet.

She wiped them with the heel of her hand and kept reading, because stopping was unthinkable now. The journal ran backward through years—her grandmother young, then younger, learning the covenant oaths, describing the roost in words that made the map in Sebastian's pack seem suddenly less like a curiosity and more like a key:

*The Ember Roost is not a place you find. It is a place that permits you. The scale-maps are living things; they know warden blood and open for it. Strangers see only blank vellum. This is why the Order wants them—not to find the roost, but to deny it to us forever.*

And then, near the end, in a shakier hand, an entry dated the year Sebastian was seven:

*My granddaughter shows the blood. She traced the old sigil freehand today, perfect, without ever having been taught. I said nothing. She loves the roads; let her love them simply, while she can. The world is not kind to those who keep dying fires. But if the day comes—if the map finds her—she will know what she is, because I wrote it here.*

*I only wish I had been braver. I only wish I had told her herself.*

Sebastian closed the journal.

For a long time she sat in the ruined keeper's chair in the ruined station on the dead guild road, and she let the grief come—not the sharp grief of the last weeks, hunted and bleeding, but an older, quieter grief, the grief of a girl who had spent thirty years thinking herself unloved in a specific way. Unremarked. Unchosen. Her grandmother had known. Had watched her trace the sigil with a child's finger and had said *nothing*, had loved her in silence and locked the truth in a book and left it on a shelf two hundred miles from home, waiting.

*So that someone, someday, does not have to forget.*

She hadn't been invisible. She had been *entrusted*. There was a difference, and the difference was the width of her whole life.

Outside, the wind rose, worrying the loose slate on the roof. In the common room she could hear Wren still murmuring over the carved names, and Corwin's wet, stubborn breathing, and beneath both—so faint she might have imagined it—the map humming in its case, patient as a banked coal.

Sebastian opened the journal again, to the last written page, and read the final lines by candlelight.

*If you are reading this, then the day has come. Take up the roads. Take up the watch. You are not a smuggler who found a map. You are a warden who forgot, and the forgetting is over.*

*Walk well, child. The Ember remembers you.*

She sat until the candle guttered, and then she rose, tucked the journal inside her coat over her heart, and went back into the firelight to tell the others what she had learned—and to decide, before morning, whether they could afford to believe it.

Because in the margin of that last page, in fresh ink that had not been there when she began reading, a single new line had appeared in the journal's familiar sharp hand:

*They are coming. Burn nothing. Run.*
