## 7. The Child in the Hollow

The rain had followed them for two days, and by the third it had found the mountains and settled in to stay.

Corwin Ashvale felt it in the wound along his ribs first—the old one, the one he'd earned at Redford Bridge carrying a boy out of a burning granary, back when he'd still believed the Order's fires only ever burned what deserved burning. The rain came down through the pines in gray sheets, turning the trail to porridge, and Sebastian Montrose walked ahead of it all as though weather were a personal acquaintance she'd long since stopped trying to impress.

"Storm shelter's half a mile," she said over her shoulder, not slowing. "Old guild cache. My grandmother used it."

"You say that about everything," Wren muttered from behind Corwin. The scribe had her satchel clutched to her chest like an infant, its oilcloth wrapping triple-checked against the wet. "The bridge. The shrine. The *goat track* we nearly died on Tuesday."

"The goat track was my grandmother's too."

"She sounds like she hated you."

"She loved me," Sebastian said, and there was something in her voice that closed the subject like a door.

Corwin said nothing. He was getting better at saying nothing. It was a skill the Order had taught him first as obedience—speak when spoken to, brother—and then as command—give the order, brother, they're only heretics—and now, stripped of both uses, it had become something else entirely: the silence of a man listening for whether the people around him had yet realized what he was.

Sebastian knew. She'd seen his face when he'd told her, three nights ago, kneeling in the snow with his sword laid flat between them like a confession on an altar. She hadn't forgiven him. She hadn't condemned him either. She'd simply looked at him the way one looks at a burned house—assessing what remained, deciding whether anything inside was worth saving.

Wren didn't know yet. Corwin could feel the not-knowing between them like a held breath.

The storm shelter announced itself as little more than a shadow in the cliffside, a cleft in the rock behind a curtain of hanging moss, but when Sebastian pulled the moss aside Corwin saw the waystones set into the threshold—worn smooth, their sigils nearly erased, but unmistakable to a man who had spent six years hunting the people who carved them. The Wayfarers' mark. The open road, the sheltering hand.

"In here," Sebastian said. "We'll wait out the worst of it."

* * *

The cave went deeper than a storm shelter needed to.

That was the first thing Corwin noticed, once Wren had coaxed a small fire from damp kindling and the light reached past the entrance chamber. The passage continued inward, sloping down, its walls worked in places—not built, exactly, but *shaped*, the stone smoothed into flowing lines like something liquid had once poured through and hardened. Waystones studded the walls at intervals, and these were not worn at all. They glowed faintly, a green-gold shimmer, pulsing with a rhythm Corwin might have mistaken for his own heartbeat if he hadn't been so certain his heart had no business beating that steadily.

Sebastian stood before the nearest stone with the dragon-scale map half-unrolled in her hands. The map had been doing strange things since the highlands—it warmed against her palm, and its paths shifted like light through water—but here, in this hollow throat of the mountain, it had begun to do something new.

"It's humming," she said quietly.

"Humming." Wren scrambled up from the fire. "Maps don't hum."

"This one does." Sebastian pressed the scale to the waystone, and the sound that filled the cave was not a sound so much as a feeling—a low, resonant thrum that Corwin felt in his teeth, in the scar tissue of his ribs, in the soles of his boots against the living stone. The waystones flared. Down the passage, deeper in, more lights answered, one after another, like windows being lit down a long dark street.

And then, from somewhere far below, something answered back.

It was not a voice. It was barely even a sound. It was a single high note, thin and wavering, like a child humming to keep the dark company—and it rose up through the stone and out of the glowing waystones and hung in the air of the cave, trembling.

Nobody moved. The fire crackled. Rain hissed somewhere far above.

"That," Wren whispered, "is not an echo."

"No," Sebastian said. Her face had gone very pale, and very still, the stillness of a woman recalculating every road she'd ever walked. "No, it isn't."

Corwin's hand had gone to his sword hilt without his permission—an old reflex, the Order's reflex, hand to steel whenever the world produced something that did not fit its categories. He made himself let go. Made himself uncurl his fingers from the grip one at a time.

"We should go down," Sebastian said.

"We should absolutely not go down," Wren said.

"It's a child." Sebastian was already rolling the map, already moving. "You heard it. That's a child singing to itself in the dark under a mountain, and it's been down there long enough that it doesn't stop when strangers arrive." She paused at the mouth of the passage, firelight catching the sharp line of her jaw. "Or it stopped being afraid a long time ago, which is worse. Come on."

Corwin went. Of course he went. That was the whole ruinous architecture of him now—he no longer believed in the Order's certainties, but he had discovered he believed in following Sebastian Montrose into dark places, and he did not yet know what to do with a faith that small and that stubborn.

* * *

The passage descended in slow spirals, and the waystones brightened as they went, until they were walking through a corridor of soft green-gold light and the darkness beyond it seemed almost courteous, waiting its turn. The humming grew clearer. It resolved, gradually, into something with shape and intention—a melody, simple and looping, the kind of tune a child invents because no one has taught them another.

Corwin counted turns. Forty paces, spiral left. Sixty paces, spiral right. A chamber where the ceiling opened into a natural chimney dripping with cold condensation. He counted because counting kept the other thing quiet—the other thing being the memory of the last time he had descended into a place where innocent things hid from men with swords. Harrow Fen. The village of Harrow Fen, where the Order had found "dragon-taint" in a well and burned four families for the taste of the water. Corwin had been twenty-three. He had held the torch himself, because Commander Vael said a brother must never ask another to carry a weight he would not carry, and because Corwin had wanted, desperately, to be the kind of man who could carry anything.

He had learned, later, that the well was clean. That the taint had been invented by a land-hungry magistrate with a donation pending to the Order's coffers. He had learned it the way one learns such things—all at once, at three in the morning, years too late, staring at his hands in the dark of a barracks room while everyone else slept.

The passage opened.

The chamber below was vast—a cathedral of stone ribbed with veins of the same luminous crystal that fed the waystones, light pooling in its hollows like water in cupped hands. And at its center, beside a small heap of belongings arranged with heartbreaking neatness—a tin cup, a rag doll stitched from sacking with button eyes, three smooth stones lined up in a row—sat a child.

She was perhaps eight years old. Small for eight, Corwin thought, and then he saw why, and everything in him went quiet.

Scales. Fine ones, opalescent, dusting her forearms like freckles cast in mother-of-pearl, catching the crystal-light and scattering it in tiny rainbows. They traced her cheekbones, climbed the curve of her ears—which were subtly, unmistakably pointed—and ran in a ridge down her spine beneath a patched coat two sizes too large. Her hair was dark and uncombed and full of cave dust. She sat cross-legged with her palms flat on the floor, and as they watched, frozen at the chamber's edge, she hummed her note again—and the waystones around the chamber pulsed in answer, and the crystal veins flushed brighter, and the whole cavern breathed light like a sleeping animal breathing warmth.

The child was singing the mountain awake. Or the mountain was singing the child. Corwin genuinely could not tell which.

"Don't move," Sebastian breathed.

"I wasn't going to—" Wren started.

The child's head snapped up.

Her eyes were gold. Not brown-gold, not amber—gold like the heart of a forge, gold with a vertical pupil that contracted to a slit as she stared at them, and Corwin watched the exact moment she registered what they were: three adults, armed, muddy, one of them wearing a sword with the Order's sun-and-ash stamped on the pommel because he had never thought to grind it off.

The child screamed.

Not a child's scream. Something underneath it—a resonance, a subsonic shudder that rattled the crystal veins and sent shards of loose stone raining from the ceiling. She scrambled backward, snatching up her doll, putting the little heap of her possessions between herself and them as if a tin cup and three stones could hold a line.

"Stay back!" Her voice cracked between registers, girl and something older. "I'll—I'll burn you! I mean it!"

"Wren," Sebastian said softly, "the sword."

"What?"

"His sword. Get it away from her sightline. Slowly."

Corwin understood a half-second after she said it, and the understanding landed in his chest like a dropped stone. He drew the blade—slowly, smoothly, the way one moves around a frightened horse—and reversed it, offering it hilt-first toward Wren, who took it with wide eyes and backed away holding it like a dead snake.

"There," Sebastian said to the child. Gentle. Conversational, as if they'd met on a road instead of at the bottom of the world. "No swords. See? We're just wet and tired and we heard someone singing."

"You're liars," the child said. "Adults are always liars. The last ones wore white and ash-gray and they had dogs." Her chin trembled, and her fists tightened on the doll hard enough that Corwin heard the stitching strain. "They killed Marta. They killed Marta and they would have killed me but I ran and ran and the stones helped me, the stones showed me the way down, they *sing*—" She stopped herself, jaw setting with a ferocity that was almost funny on a face that small, except nothing about this was funny. "I'm not going back."

"No one's taking you anywhere," Sebastian said. She sank down slowly, cross-legged, mirroring the child's posture, making herself small. Making herself, Corwin realized, *unthreatening*—a skill he had never possessed and was watching with the awe of a man watching someone speak a language he'd never learned. "My name is Sebastian. This is Wren. The big one is Corwin, and he's sorry about the sword, aren't you, Corwin."

"Yes," Corwin said. His voice came out rougher than he intended. He cleared his throat. "I am. I should have taken it off days ago. I'll break the pommel-stamp off tonight, if it would help."

The child's slit-pupiled eyes fixed on him with unnerving intensity. "You smell like smoke," she said. "Old smoke. Bad smoke. Like the men with the dogs." Her nostrils flared—actually flared, Corwin saw, the pupils widening as she read the air. "But not the same. You smell like smoke and then like snow. Like after."

"After," Corwin repeated.

"When the fire's done and the ground is cold and things can grow again." She frowned, puzzled by her own words, by whatever sense had produced them. "You smell like *after*."

Corwin knelt. Slowly, deliberately, the way he'd once been taught to kneel before altars, back when kneeling meant something different. The stone was cold through his trousers. The child watched him the entire way down.

"My name is Corwin," he said. "I did bad things for bad men, a long time ago. Before you were born, probably. Smoke and worse than smoke." He held out his empty hands, palms up, the gesture stripped bare—he had no gift to offer, no coin, no cleverness, only the plain fact of himself. "I can't undo any of it. But I give you my word, here, in front of these stones that sing—that's the most binding oath I know of—that no one takes you anywhere you don't choose to go. Not while I'm breathing."

The silence stretched. Somewhere above, impossibly far, the rain went on falling.

The child looked at Sebastian. Sebastian looked back at her and shrugged slightly, as if to say: *he's heavy baggage, but he carries it honestly.*

"Pip," the child said at last. "My name's Pip. Marta called me Pip." Her lower lip wobbled and she mastered it, the way children master such things, with a violence that hurt to watch. "Marta said if anyone ever asked, I was to tell them my mother was a lizard from the southern swamps. She said it would make them laugh and forget to look at my arms." She pushed back her sleeve, and the opal scales caught the light, beautiful and damning. "But you already looked."

"We already looked," Sebastian agreed gently. "And we're still here."

"Why?" Pip's voice rose, cracking, the question tearing out of her with weeks of fear behind it. "Everyone always—when they see, they either run or they fetch the white-cloaks, those are the only two things that ever happen, so *why are you still here?*"

And Corwin, kneeling on the cold stone of a hidden cathedral at the bottom of a mountain, found that he knew the answer—not the safe answer, not the strategic one, but the true one, the one that had been growing in him since a ravine and a snowbank and a disgraced knight's confession given to a woman who owed him nothing.

"Because I spent my life being the reason children hid in caves," he said. "And I'm done. Whatever it costs. I'm done."

Pip stared at him. The vertical pupils widened, rounder, and for a moment she looked like nothing so much as an ordinary child—tired, filthy, orphaned, eight years old and carrying a weight no eight-year-old should know the shape of.

Then her stomach growled, enormously, echoing off the crystal vaults, and the solemnity of the moment collapsed like a tent in wind.

"Do you have food?" Pip demanded. "Real food? I've been eating cave moss and it tastes like *regret*."

"Cave moss tastes like regret?" Wren asked weakly, from the doorway.

"Very old regret. Marta said I'd learn to like it. Marta lied about lots of things." But she said it without bitterness now, clutching her doll, edging forward across the stone one cautious inch at a time. "She said someone would come eventually. She said the stones would send someone. She said—" Pip stopped, right at the edge of arm's reach, looking up at Sebastian with those forge-gold eyes. "She said the map would find me. Did it? Is that why you're here? Because I've been hearing it for *days*, humming through the stones, getting louder, and I thought—I hoped—"

Sebastian drew out the dragon-scale map, and the moment it cleared her coat the whole chamber responded—the waystones blazing, the crystal veins flushing rose-gold, and the map itself glowing like a coal cupped in living hands. Pip made a sound that was half laugh, half sob.

"It's been calling *me*," she whispered. "All this time, I thought I was hiding. I think it was leading."

* * *

They made camp in the crystal chamber, because Sebastian ruled that no one climbed back up into that rain until the storm broke, and because Pip, once fed, fell asleep mid-sentence against Wren's shoulder with the trusting totality of a creature who had decided, finally and completely, to stop running.

Corwin sat apart, at the chamber's edge, working at his sword pommel with his knife. The Order's sigil came away in flakes of gilded brass—sun and ash, the emblem of purification. He let the pieces fall into the dust between his feet. It changed nothing. It changed everything. He couldn't tell the difference anymore, and suspected the difference didn't matter; what mattered was the weight leaving his hand.

Sebastian settled beside him, silent as ever, and for a while they simply watched the child sleep and the crystals breathe their slow light.

"She heard the map from three miles underground," Sebastian said at last. "Vane will have felt that too, if the Order has anyone left who knows what to listen for. And they took Marta. Which means they know she exists. Which means—"

"They're coming," Corwin said. "Not chasing us anymore. Coming *here*."

Below them, deep in the mountain's roots, the waystones pulsed on—steady, patient, singing their low green-gold song to a sleeping child, like a lighthouse that had waited thirty years for ships.

And far above, muffled by a hundred fathoms of stone, Corwin heard—or dreamed he heard—the first baying of dogs.
