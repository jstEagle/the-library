## 8. Inquisitor Vane

The rain had followed Sister Vane from the coast for nine days, and she had come to think of it as a kind of accompaniment—a gray percussion beneath which the world confessed more readily. People spoke softer in rain. They hunched. They wanted it over with. In her experience, a man soaked to the bone and offered a dry chair would tell you anything you liked just to keep the chair.

The guildhall at Harrow's Ford was not what she had expected. She had imagined something grander for the once-famous Wayfarers' Guild: banners, maps, the faded gilt of better decades. Instead she found a squat stone building with a sagging porch, one shutter hanging by a single hinge, and a sign whose letters had been repainted so many times they stood up from the wood like scar tissue. The smell of wet wool and old smoke seeped out through the door seams.

Inside, three people. That was all. She counted them as she entered, the way she counted everything—doors, exits, hands, lies. An old man by the hearth who might have been handsome forty years ago and had decided not to renew the lease on his own dignity. A woman mending a saddle blanket with the mechanical patience of someone who no longer expected anything to improve. And behind the master's desk, a broad-shouldered man in his fifties with a guildmaster's chain around his neck that looked too heavy for him, or perhaps he was simply too light for it.

"Inquisitor Maren Vane," she said, and let the title sit in the room like a stone dropped into still water. "Of the Order of the Ashen Blade. You received my letter."

"We did." The guildmaster rose. His name, according to the letter of introduction the local magistrate had provided, was Aldous Crane. He had the voice of a man accustomed to being obeyed on roads, where obedience was a matter of survival rather than respect. "We've done nothing wrong, Inquisitor. The Wayfarers are licensed carriers. We pay our tariffs. We ask no favors."

"No one has accused you of anything." She smiled—the small, courteous smile she used for opening doors—and removed her hood. Rainwater ran off the oiled canvas in threads. She was aware of how she looked: a narrow woman of fifty-one years, gray-streaked hair bound back without ornament, dressed in the traveling grays of her order with the ash-blade sigil worked small at the collar. Unremarkable. That was useful. Remarkable women were watched; unremarkable ones were underestimated, and underestimation was the sharpest tool she owned. "I'm here about a courier. A woman. She passed through this region eleven days ago carrying correspondence of interest to the Crown."

Crane's face did an admirable job of nothing at all. Admirable, but not perfect. His eyes went—not to the door, not to the window, but briefly, involuntarily, to the floor near the eastern wall. To the yard beyond it, perhaps. To a grave.

Vane had interrogated smugglers in Kettering, pirates in the Salt Straits, a hedge-witch who had genuinely believed herself innocent until the third hour. She had learned that guilt lived in the body before it reached the tongue, and that the body always pointed. *There*, said his eyes. *We put something there.*

"You may sit," Crane said. "Marta, bring the inquisitor tea."

"Don't trouble yourself." She took the chair opposite the desk uninvited, arranging her sodden cloak across its back with care. "I'll be brief. I know you value brevity on the roads."

"The roads value it. I can't speak for the guildhall."

"A courier left Emberhold on the ninth of the month bearing sealed dispatches. She never arrived at her destination. Her horse was found at the crossing below the Thornfells, saddled, riderless. Witnesses place her heading north along the old way-road—the same road your caravans use." Vane folded her hands. "I am told the Wayfarers' Guild knows every path through those mountains better than the Crown's own surveyors. Better, some say, than the Crown is comfortable with."

"That's slander," said the old man by the fire, stung into speech. "We're guides, not traitors."

"Hush, Tam." Crane didn't look at him. "Inquisitor, if a courier died on our road, that grieves us, but mountains kill travelers every season. Exposure. Rockfall. Wolves. If you're asking whether we found a body—"

"I am asking precisely that."

"Then no. We found nothing."

It was beautifully done. Calm, regretful, final. Vane almost applauded. But there had been that flicker of his eyes, and there was this: the woman with the saddle blanket had stopped sewing. Her needle rested against the cloth, motionless, and she was listening with the whole of her body, the way people listen when a lie concerns someone they love.

*Interesting.*

"Then perhaps you'll permit my associate to look around." She gestured to Brother Cassel, who waited by the door with the patient immensity of a well-fed mastiff. Cassel was not subtle and was not meant to be. His presence changed the arithmetic of a room. "A formality. The Order thanks the guild for its cooperation."

Crane hesitated—one heartbeat, no more. "Our records are private. Guild charter, signed by King Aldric's grandfather."

"Charters burn, Master Crane. It's remarkable how many things burn." She said it mildly, conversationally, the way one remarks on weather. Then she leaned forward slightly. "But let us set aside threats, since we both find them vulgar. Let me tell you instead why I've come so far north in the rain, and you may judge for yourself whether your charter matters."

She let the silence stretch. Silence was her native language; she spoke it more fluently than any living tongue.

"Three hundred years ago," she began, "the Thornfells burned. Not wildfire—dragonfire. The great roosts of the northern eyries, the nests of creatures older than every kingdom in this land, were put to the torch in a single night by men who understood what those creatures were. Your histories call it the Night of Cinders and treat it as tragedy. Our histories call it the First Cleansing and treat it as duty only half-completed." She watched his face. Nothing moved in it, but his breathing had gone shallow, and shallow breathing was a confession in itself. "Because it was half-completed, Master Crane. The dragons were broken, scattered, driven into hiding—but not destroyed. And everything that hides, waits. Everything that waits, grows. Fire sleeps in the deep places of the world, and men grow comfortable, and then one morning the mountain opens its mouth."

"You believe there are still dragons," Crane said flatly. "After three centuries."

"I believe in evidence. Wyvern-fire recovered from a border raid two months past, burning at temperatures no alchemist can reproduce. Livestock taken from high valleys without a drop of blood spilled—lifted, not killed. A shepherd in the Graystones who swears he saw wings against the moon, and whose testimony I would dismiss, except that his flock's bones were fused." She recited it evenly, without drama, because the facts needed no decoration. Drama was for people who lacked facts. "The Order does not chase ghosts, Master Crane. We chase embers. And an ember, given time and shelter, becomes a conflagration. My life's work is ensuring that the last war between men and dragons, should it ever come, is fought on our terms—or better, never fought at all, because there will be nothing left on their side of it to fight."

The old man by the fire made a sound that might have been disgust. The woman with the needle had begun sewing again, fast and hard, as though she could stitch the conversation shut.

"And the courier?" Crane asked. "What has a dead courier to do with dragons?"

"Her dispatches concerned sightings in the Thornfells. Sightings, Master Crane, of a living dragon." Vane let that word stand alone in the smoky air of the guildhall, three hundred years heavy. "Which means that somewhere between Emberhold and this room, someone intercepted her. Someone took what she carried. And someone—" she paused, delicately, "—may have helped her hide."

*There.* His eyes again, to the eastern wall, and this time he caught himself doing it and looked down at his desk, and the looking-down was worse than the glance, because it confirmed the glance.

Brother Cassel shifted by the door. Outside, the rain thickened against the shutters.

* * *

They walked the grounds in the gray afternoon while Crane trailed them protesting charters and privacy and the rights of licensed carriers, and Vane let him protest, because protest was a kind of music that covered other sounds. The yard behind the guildhall was mud and gravel, a stable, a woodshed, a low wall. And near the eastern wall, where the ground was newer than the ground around it, a patch of turned earth already greening over with the haste of recent rain.

"No marker?" Vane asked.

"Strangers don't get markers," Crane said. "Pauper's burial. We're not a charity."

"Mm." She stood over the grave for a long moment, rain dripping from her nose, and felt the familiar cold clarity settle over her, the state her novices called *the stillness* and she thought of simply as seeing. Somewhere under that mud was a woman who had carried knowledge of a living dragon, and someone in this hall knew what that knowledge was, and someone in this hall had touched it. "What was her name?"

"How would I know? We never saw her alive."

"But you buried her. With your own hands, presumably, or your people's. And no one asked her name? No one searched her effects for identification? No one reported the death to the magistrate, as the law requires?" She turned and looked at him fully for the first time, and let him see what was in her face—not anger, nothing so warm as anger, only attention, total and pitiless. "You are lying to me, Master Crane. You have been lying since I walked through your door, and you lie badly, which tells me you are not a liar by trade. Which tells me you are protecting something. Or someone."

"I'm protecting my guild." For the first time, heat came into his voice, and Vane welcomed it the way a smith welcomes a draft. "Three members left, Inquisitor. Three. You want to know what happened to the Wayfarers? Kings taxed our roads, wars closed our passes, and now your Order burns waystations and calls it cleansing. We are the last of a thing that was once great, and I will not hand you the throat of the last of us because you've got a feeling about a grave."

"Your guildmaster's loyalty does him credit," Vane said to Cassel, pleasantly, as though Crane weren't standing there. "Loyalty is the raw material of everything I do. It can be shaped. Stretched. Turned." Then, to Crane: "How many?"

He frowned. "How many what?"

"Members. You said three. I count four." She held up her fingers, ticking them off with slow ceremony. "Yourself. The old man. The woman. And a fourth bed upstairs—I saw the straw pressed, the blanket turned back, a cup on the sill with the water not yet evaporated. Someone left in a hurry recently, or someone is hiding very quietly. Which is it, Master Crane?"

The silence that followed lasted four heartbeats. She counted them.

"There was a girl," Crane said finally, and his voice had gone strange, hollowed out, the voice of a man stepping off a ledge he'd been circling for days. "Sebastian Montrose. Our guide. She... left. Before your courier would have passed through. Days before. She's not here, and whatever you think she—"

"*Montrose.*" The name landed in Vane's chest like a coin dropped down a well, and she heard the depth of the water by the sound it made. She kept her face still, but inside, everything aligned with the soft click of a lock turning. Montrose. She knew that name from archives older than this guildhall, from a ledger of the old covenant, from a list of bloodlines the Order had watched for three hundred years and nearly forgotten why. "Sebastian Montrose," she repeated. "And where did she go?"

"I don't know. She's a guide. Guides go everywhere."

"When?"

"Nine days ago."

Nine days. The courier had died eleven days ago, within a day's ride of this hall. The arithmetic arranged itself in Vane's mind with the elegance of a proof: courier flees north, wounded or pursued; dies at the guildhall door; is buried in secret; and within a day, the guild's last guide abandons her post for the first time in her careful, invisible life and vanishes into the Thornfells. Taking, no doubt, whatever the dying woman had pressed into her hands.

"She took something," Vane said. It was not a question, and she watched him understand that it could not be answered safely either way, and watched him choose silence, and marked the choice. "Thank you, Master Crane. You've been more helpful than you intended. That's usually how it goes."

"What will you do?" The woman with the needle had come to the doorway, and there was iron in her voice now, fear transmuted. "She's done nothing. Whatever you think she's carrying—she's nobody. She's been nobody her whole life. Nobody even sees her."

Vane considered her for a moment with genuine, almost tender interest. "You'd be surprised," she said, "how often nobody turns out to be the most important person in the story."

* * *

That night, camped in the guildhall's best guest room with Cassel keeping watch below, Vane sat by candlelight with the old ledger open across her knees and read the entry she had memorized twenty years ago and never once needed until tonight.

*Of the Warden Lines,* the faded script ran, in a hand three centuries dead. *When the covenant was sworn, seven families took the oath of keeping. Six lines failed or fled or were extinguished in the Cleansing. The seventh, the Montroses, were believed ended with the betrayal at Cinder Pass. Believed. The Order's founders wrote that belief into our founding law: watch the name. Watch the blood.*

Watch the name. Watch the blood. And here was a Montrose, alive and walking north into the Thornfells with a dead courier's secret in her keeping, nine days ahead and gaining.

Vane closed the ledger and sat for a while listening to the rain, and examined herself, as she did each night, for the cracks that pride opens in even the most careful vessel. She found none. She never doubted. Doubt was a luxury of people who could afford to be wrong; the sister of a burned border town, the mother of a soldier taken at the Weeping Stair thirty years ago, the child she had been who had watched a wyvern's stray fire take her village granary and half her street—none of them could afford her doubt, and so she carried none for them. What she felt was not hatred. Hatred was hot, and heat distorted judgment. What she felt was colder and cleaner: the certainty of a surgeon who has seen the infection and knows the knife is mercy. Dragons were fire given will, and willful fire had only ever ended in ash. Every age that had tolerated them had paid in cinders. She would be the last inquisitor, if need be—the one who finished the work, who made the world safe from the sky, and history could call her monster or savior as it pleased. Both, probably. It hardly mattered. Righteousness was not a popularity.

One thing troubled her, and she turned it over honestly, because self-deception was the first rot. The girl. Sebastian Montrose. By every account a small life, a gray life, a guide who kept to herself and wanted nothing. Such people did not steal dragon-secrets and run. Unless they had been chosen. Unless the blood in them had answered something. Vane had read enough of the old covenant lore to know the wardens' oath was said to live in the veins, waiting, and that it did not ask permission.

So the girl was not a criminal. She was a symptom. The disease itself lay deeper, in the mountains, guttering and ancient and nearly finished—and yet, apparently, not finished enough to stop choosing heirs.

Vane snuffed the candle and stood at the dark window, watching lightning walk the ridgelines of the Thornfells far to the north, silent thunder rolling in a minute later like an argument arriving late.

"Cassel," she said to the darkness behind her. "Send riders at first light. To the garrison at Emberhold, to the chapterhouse at Grey Ford, and to the Commander of the Ashen Blade himself. Tell them the Montrose line lives. Tell them the heir is in the mountains. And tell them I want the Thornfell passes sealed—every road, every pass, every shepherd's track. Nothing comes out of those mountains until I have seen what sleeps inside them."

She watched the lightning again, and this time she did not flinch at the thunder.

Somewhere under that storm, a guttering ember was trying to become a flame. Maren Vane had spent her whole life making certain the world stayed dark, and she meant to see the work completed—whatever it cost, whoever it burned.

The rain fell harder. North, the mountains waited, and she smiled at them in the dark like a promise.
