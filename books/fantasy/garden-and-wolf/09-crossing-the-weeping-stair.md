## 9. Crossing the Weeping Stair

The Weeping Stair announced itself long before they saw it, first as a sound—a thin, ceaseless dripping that echoed up the gorge like the mountain counting its own heartbeats—and then as a smell, wet stone and cold iron, and finally as a wall of black rock rising out of the mist so sheer that Pip stopped walking and simply stared at it with her mouth open.

"That's not a road," she said. "That's a cliff that somebody had a bad idea about."

"It's a road," Sebastian said. "The worst one the guild ever kept. Which is why we're the only ones who still know it."

She stood at the base of the gorge with her hands on her hips, letting herself feel the old familiar dread and the old unfamiliar warmth of having three people standing behind her waiting for her judgment. A year ago—two months ago—the decision would have been hers alone to make and hers alone to suffer. Now there were four sets of lungs breathing in this narrow place, and every one of them was her responsibility.

The Stair itself was a seam of switchbacks cut into the gorge wall by hands long vanished, ascending perhaps eight hundred feet through a constant curtain of meltwater that seeped from the rock above and fell in threads of silver down the face of the climb. In summer it was merely treacherous. In autumn, when the seepage froze at night and thawed by day, it became a lottery played with ice and gravity. The guild records called it *the Ladder of Sighs*. The wayfarers who worked it called it nothing at all, because wayfarers were a superstitious lot and did not name the things that might name them back.

Above it, the high pass to the Ember Road waited. Below it, three days' detour through the flooded lowlands of the Mirefen, where Vane's riders could move fast and they could not move at all.

"We have lead time," Wren said, reading Sebastian's silence correctly, as she always did. "Four days, maybe five, since we slipped the patrol at Thornhollow. The Stair costs us half a day if we go straight up. The Mirefen costs us three days but keeps us invisible."

"Invisible," Corwin repeated. He said the word carefully, the way he said most words now, as though testing each one for hidden blades before he let it out of his mouth. His wound from the snow-pass had closed, mostly, but it pulled at him when he moved, and Sebastian had watched him favoring his left side all morning without saying anything, because he was a knight and would rather die than be told to rest.

"Vane isn't chasing us anymore," Wren pressed on. "You heard the refugees at the ford. She's burning towns now—or pretending dragons are burning them. She doesn't need us. She needs an army and a story. Every day we spend on this mountain is a day she spends building both."

Sebastian looked up at the Stair. The water fell and fell and fell, patient as grief.

"And if she finds the valley," Sebastian said quietly, "none of the rest matters."

Wren exhaled through her nose. "I didn't say don't take the Stair. I said know what it costs."

"I always know what it costs." Sebastian shouldered her pack. "It's the only thing my grandmother ever taught me properly."

* * *

The first hundred feet were almost kind.

The steps were wide enough for boots, cut with a drainage channel that some dead mason had angled with real genius, and the water fell beside them more than on them. Sebastian went first, testing each hold with her heel before committing her weight, calling back the rhythm: *left foot high, right hand low, never trust the seam, trust the stone under the seam.* Pip climbed behind her with the unthinking ease of the young and the winged-adjacent, her small claws finding purchase where fingers struggled, her amber eyes bright with something that was not fear. Behind her came Wren, pale and grim and competent in the way of people who have decided competence is cheaper than panic. Corwin took the rear, because he refused any other position, and his breathing was a metronome Sebastian counted without meaning to.

*One-two-three-four. One-two-three-four.* Steady. Good.

At two hundred feet the kindness ended.

The drainage channels had cracked sometime in the last century, and the water no longer ran beside the path but across it, sheeting over the stone in a skin of moving glass. The temperature dropped as they climbed into the cloud layer, and the spray began to freeze where it landed—not on the rock, which breathed its ancient slow warmth, but on everything else. On their cloaks. On the iron buckles of their packs. On the rungs of the old guide-chains, which hung from rusted staples driven into the cliff face by guild hands two hundred years gone, and which Sebastian had promised herself she would not need.

At three hundred feet, she needed them.

"Chains," she called back. "Left side. Move together—one moves, one holds. Wren, you clip your sling through the links. Corwin—" she glanced back, down the white-and-black ladder of the cliff, at the knight clinging forty feet below her, "—how is the side?"

"Quiet."

"Liar."

"A quiet liar," Corwin agreed, and hauled himself up another step, and Sebastian let it go because there was nothing else to do with it here, on the face of the world, with the drop yawning beneath them like a held breath.

Pip laughed. It was a small sound, startled out of her by the absurdity, and it rang off the stone and was swallowed by water, and for one moment the whole terrible climb felt almost like joy.

Then the cloud swallowed them, and the joy went with it.

* * *

Above five hundred feet the Stair changed character entirely. The cut steps gave out—collapsed, probably, in some long-forgotten winter—and what remained was the raw seam itself, a diagonal crack in the cliff face that the old wayfarers had crossed by leaning into the mountain and shuffling, foot over foot, along ledges no wider than a handspan. The chains continued, but sparsely now, ten feet between anchors, twenty, thirty, and the water fell here in earnest, not in threads but in ropes, cold enough to stop the breath, striking the ledge and exploding upward in freezing bloom.

Sebastian had done this crossing eleven times. She knew it the way she knew her own hands. She also knew that she had done it eleven times alone, carrying goods, carrying guilt, carrying nothing that could fall.

Now she carried a child.

"Pip." She got the girl against the rock at the last wide stance, crouched to her eye level, and made her voice the flat, calm voice of the guild road—the voice that had talked merchants across collapsing bridges and talked herself across worse. "Listen to me. From here to the notch above, we cross in pairs. You stay behind me. When I say *press*, you press your whole body to the stone and you do not look down and you do not look at the water. You look at my shoulders. Only my shoulders. Can you do that?"

Pip's throat moved. Her scales had gone dull with cold, the copper of them dimmed to old bronze, and her pupils were wide and black in the grey light. But she nodded once, sharp, and lifted her chin the way she did when she was frightened and refusing to be.

"I'm not scared," she said.

"I know," Sebastian lied gently. "Neither am I. That's how you'll know we're family—we lie about the same things."

Behind them, Wren made a sound that was almost a laugh and almost a sob.

They crossed.

It took an hour to cover sixty feet. Sebastian moved with the water hitting her back like thrown gravel, counting holds, calling the rhythm, feeling Pip's small clawed grip on her belt like a second heartbeat. Twice the ledge narrowed to nothing and they shuffled blind, cheek to wet stone, the drop singing below them. Once a chain anchor groaned—a soft, rotten-metal sound that Sebastian felt in her teeth rather than heard—and she moved them past it faster, and did not tell anyone until later, much later, when it could become a story instead of a prayer.

And then Pip slipped.

There was no drama to it. No cry, no lurch. One moment her weight was on the ledge; the next, the ledge was a smear of ice where a rope of falling water had frozen in the night, and her foot skated, and her claw-hold crumbled, and seventy pounds of half-dragon child swung out over eight hundred feet of air with nothing under her but weather.

Sebastian caught her by the wrist.

The jolt nearly took them both. Sebastian's knees slammed the ledge, her shoulder wrenched in its socket with a hot white flare, and for one endless second she lay with her body over the void and Pip's full weight hanging from her arm, and the water roared, and the drop sang, and somewhere very far away Wren was screaming her name.

"Press!" Sebastian roared, to herself as much as anyone. "PRESS—"

She got her other hand into a crack. Got her boot toe wedged. Began, inch by screaming inch, to haul. Pip's free hand found the chain—found Sebastian's collar—found the stone. And then, with a scrabble of claws that tore Sebastian's cloak open from shoulder to hem, the child was up, was on the ledge, was pressed against the rock and shaking, and Sebastian lay beside her with her arm throbbing like a struck bell and her heart trying to climb out through her teeth.

Below, Corwin was climbing toward them as fast as the mountain allowed, his face grey. Above, Wren clung to a chain and shouted things that were mostly swearwords in three languages.

"I'm sorry," Pip gasped. "I'm sorry, I'm sorry—"

"Look at me." Sebastian rolled onto her side, ignoring the shoulder, and took the girl's chin in her good hand. "Did you fall?"

"Yes—"

"No. The mountain dropped you. There's a difference, and the difference is that one of those things is your fault and neither of them are." She pressed her forehead briefly to the child's scaled brow. "We don't leave people on mountains, Pip. Not for falls. Not for anything. That's the whole guild. That's the entire secret. Everything else is just walking."

* * *

They reached the notch above the crossing as the light failed, and there Sebastian made the decision that would cost them.

The safe route—the route she had planned, the route in her head for two days—continued along the eastern traverse: longer, lower, sheltered from the wind, a gentle contour path that would bring them to the high pass by tomorrow evening with everyone intact. It was the sensible route. It was the route a guide owed her clients.

But the traverse skirted the base of the Serpent's Tooth, a tower of rotten granite that shed rock whenever the temperature swung, and the temperature was swinging now, hard, the warm front that had thawed the Stair colliding with the coming night's frost. By morning the traverse would be a killing floor of falling stone. By afternoon, maybe sooner.

The other option was the chimney—a vertical gash in the ridge directly above them, six hundred feet of it, climbing through the heart of the storm front. It would put them on the summit saddle within hours, ahead of the rockfall, ahead of the weather. It would also put them in the open during the worst of the wind, with a wounded man, a scribe who had never climbed anything taller than a library ladder, and a child who had already fallen once today.

Sebastian looked at the traverse. Looked at the chimney. Felt the arithmetic of survival grinding in her skull like millstones—days of lead time, Corwin's wound, Wren's terror, Pip's exhaustion—and then felt Pip's hand slip into hers, small and clawed and trusting, and the arithmetic went silent.

"The chimney," she said.

Wren stared at her. "Absolutely not."

"The traverse runs under the Serpent's Tooth. The thaw's brought half of it down already—you can hear it." And she could: faint, intermittent, the deep knocking of stone on stone drifting up from the dark. "By dawn it'll be raining rock. We'd be crossing its shadow for four hours."

"So we wait for dawn, we cross fast—"

"—under a tower that's actively shedding, with a child, in the dark, on ice. Or we climb six hundred feet of chimney tonight and sleep on the saddle." Sebastian flexed her bad arm and made herself not flinch. "I know what it costs, Wren. I'm telling you what the other thing costs."

"What it costs is our lead! Two days, maybe three, if the weather closes behind us—which it will, look at that sky—Vane gets her crusade launched while we're sitting in a snowdrift, and everything we—" Wren stopped. Pressed her lips together. Started again, quieter, and the quietness was worse. "This is sentiment. You nearly died on that ledge holding her wrist, and now you're going to gamble all of us on a chimney because you can't stand the thought of her being afraid again. That's not guiding. That's guilt wearing a compass."

The words landed exactly where they were aimed. Sebastian felt them sink in, felt the old loneliness rise up to meet them—that familiar cold certainty that she was, in the end, a smuggler playing at shepherd, that competence was all she had and even it was sentiment in disguise.

Corwin spoke from the shadows by the notch wall. "The chimney," he said.

"You don't get a vote, you can barely stand—"

"I've marched retreats," Corwin said. "Three of them. Each time, the commander did the arithmetic. Left the wounded. Saved the column. Won the day." He looked at Sebastian, and in the failing light his scarred face was unreadable and absolutely clear. "Every one of those columns died anyway. Later. Somewhere else. Because an army that leaves its people learns it's disposable, and disposable men fight like it." He touched his bandaged side, almost absently. "I was the arithmetic once. I know how it ends. Take the chimney."

Silence, except the water, and the far-off knocking of the Serpent's Tooth shedding its skin.

Wren looked from him to Sebastian, and something in her face broke and reformed—anger collapsing into something rawer, younger. "If she dies," she whispered, "it won't matter what the guild serves. There won't be any of us left to serve it."

"Then we don't let her die," Sebastian said. "That's the plan. That's the whole plan. It's been the plan since the door of the guildhall, and every road since." She picked up her pack. "The guild serves travelers, Wren. Not schedules. Not maps. Not even the Ember. Travelers. You're travelers. All of you. So we climb."

* * *

The chimney took the night.

It was not climbing so much as imprisonment inside the mountain's own wound, a slot of darkness six feet wide and six hundred deep, choked with loose stone and running melt, where the wind funneled down from the saddle above and screamed like something being unmade. They climbed by touch and by the fitful green-gold shimmer of Pip's scales, which had begun, in the deep cold, to glow—faintly, unevenly, like embers remembering fire—and which lit the walls around them with a light that made Sebastian think, wildly, of the map, of the ember-road, of everything warm that was waiting for them or hunting them or both.

Corwin climbed badly. He climbed anyway. Three times Sebastian anchored him with the rope, braced her ruined shoulder against a flake of stone, and hauled while he swore softly and steadily in the manner of a man reciting penance. Wren climbed better than anyone expected, small and stubborn, muttering catalogued curses, refusing every offered hand until the one moment near the top when the wind hit them all at once and she grabbed Sebastian's arm and held on and did not apologize after, and Sebastian did not ask her to.

Pip glowed and climbed and glowed, and near the end, wedged in a hollow while the worst gusts tore past above them, she leaned against Sebastian's good side and said, drowsily, into the roar:

"The map's humming louder up here. Did you know? It's been humming since the notch. Like it knows we're close."

Sebastian looked down at the pack where the dragon-scale map slept against her spine. She could not hear it. But she believed it, the way she had come to believe in chains that hold and children that catch and liars that turn into family mid-climb, without proof, on faith alone.

"Then it can hum us the rest of the way," she said.

They came out of the chimney onto the summit saddle two hours before dawn, into a sky scoured clean and violent with stars, and the wind up there was a living thing that tried to strip them off the ridge, and none of them cared. They crawled to the lee of the old covenant cairn—the first warden-stone Sebastian had ever seen outside her grandmother's journal, humming faintly under her palm with the same note, she realized, that Pip had described—and they dug in behind it, and Corwin built a fire out of wet heather and pure spite, and they ate cold food and drank hot tea and shook, all four of them, in the same rhythm, like one creature that had climbed out of the mountain instead of four people who had climbed up it.

Wren sat with her knees drawn up, staring into the flames. For a long time she said nothing. Then, without looking at anyone:

"I copied decrees for the Order for two years. I knew what they were. I told myself ink wasn't blood." Her jaw tightened. "You spent one night choosing a harder road because of a child, and I called it weakness. I'm—" She stopped. Started again. "I'm sorry. That's all. I'm sorry."

Sebastian passed her the tea. "The guild serves travelers," she said again, gentler now. "Including the ones traveling away from themselves. I'd know. I did it for twenty years."

Wren took the cup. Their fingers brushed. Neither of them mentioned it.

Corwin was asleep sitting up, sword across his knees, one hand still unconsciously guarding the space where Pip curled against his uninjured side. The child was asleep too, truly asleep, her scales banked to a soft dull copper, her breath fogging in the starlight. Above them the saddle stretched north toward the high pass, and beyond it, unseen, the hidden valley, the ember-road, the last dragon guttering in the dark like a candle someone had forgotten to guard.

Sebastian sat the middle watch alone, as she always had, and found—for the first time in her life—that the watch did not feel like loneliness. It felt like standing guard. There was a difference, and the difference was everything.

Near dawn, the map woke against her back.

Not humming now. *Burning*—a heat like a coal through canvas, and when she dragged it out into the starlight the scale-paths were glowing gold, all of them, converging on the pass ahead, and beneath the glow, faint as breath on a mirror, new lines were surfacing that had never shown before. Roads no chart showed. And at the edge of the light, at the northern lip of the saddle, where the pass dropped toward the valley—

Smoke.

A thin thread of it, rising in the still morning air from the lowlands beyond the pass. Too steady for a campfire. Too wide for a chimney.

Wren saw it too. She had come awake without a sound, the way she always did, and she stood at Sebastian's shoulder with the firelight in her eyes and the smoke in them both.

"That's not a village," Wren said quietly. "Villages burn and go out. That's been burning all night."

Sebastian folded the glowing map against her chest and looked north, at the road they had bought with two days and a shoulder and whatever it was the four of them had become on the face of the mountain.

"Then we'd better find out what has," she said.
