## 10. The Village That Remembers

The village announced itself by smell before sight: woodsmoke and peat and, beneath it, something sweeter—honey, Wren thought, or heather burned low. Then the road bent around a shoulder of grey stone, and there it was, clinging to the mountainside like a lichen that had decided to become architecture. Thatched roofs the color of old bone. Dry-stone walls stitching terraces up the slope. A single bell-tower with no bell, only an empty iron yoke where one had once hung.

"Thornhollow," Corwin said, reining in beside her. His wound had gone from angry red to a bruised purple in the three days since the pass, and he held his left arm close to his body the way a man holds a secret. "Or what's left of it."

"It's on none of my maps," Wren said.

"No," Sebastian said quietly, from the front. "It wouldn't be."

Wren had learned to read Sebastian's silences over the weeks of their flight—the way she went still and watchful when the past pressed close, like a guide reading weather in a cloudless sky. This silence said: *the guild knew this place, and chose to forget it, or was made to.*

Pip stirred against Sebastian's back where she rode pillion, her small clawed hands loose around Sebastian's waist. She had been quiet since the Weeping Stair, quieter even than grief allowed, and her scales had taken on a faint pearlescence in the highland light that Wren tried not to stare at and failed.

"They're watching us," Pip said suddenly. Her voice was thin but clear. "Everyone. Behind the walls. They've been watching since the bend in the road."

Corwin's hand drifted toward his sword, then stopped. "How many?"

"All of them," Pip said, and almost smiled. "They smell like peat smoke and fear. But not *our* kind of fear. Old fear. Like they keep it in a box under the floor."

The first door opened as they drew level with the lowest terrace—an old woman, small and bent as a question mark, standing in her doorway with a wooden bowl in her hands as if she'd been waiting years for someone to fill it. She looked at them for a long moment. At Sebastian. At Pip. At the map-case slung across Sebastian's shoulder.

Then she turned back inside and called out, in a voice cracked with age but carrying remarkably well: "Brida! The kettle! And tell your father the road has finally sent them back!"

*Them back.* Wren felt the words land somewhere between her shoulder blades. Not *travelers*. Not *strangers*. *Back.*

* * *

They were given the longhouse at the top of the village—the largest, oldest building, its roof-beams black with centuries of smoke, its central hearth wide enough to roast a sheep whole. The elders came to them there, six of them, moving slowly, settling onto benches along the walls with the unhurried gravity of people who had outlived everyone who ever hurried them.

The eldest was a man called Tammas Reeve, though Wren suspected the title was older than any office the Crown recognized. He was blind in one eye, milk-pale, and he looked at Sebastian with the good one for a long time before speaking.

"You have her look," he said. "The Montrose look. Your grandmother came through here the winter I was nine. Sat where you're sitting. Fed me honey-cakes because I cried at her wolf-dog." He paused. "You'll take tea first. Then we'll talk about what you're carrying."

Sebastian's hand moved to the map-case, stopped. "You know what it is."

"We know what it is." Tammas's voice didn't waver. "We've known for sixty years. We've been waiting for someone to come carry it properly." He leaned forward, and the firelight caught the deep lines of his face like furrows in plowed ground. "Show us, girl. Show us whether it still lives."

Sebastian glanced at Wren—a question, a habit now—and Wren nodded. Together they unrolled the map on the long trestle table, weighting its corners with cups and a whetstone and, after a moment's hesitation, one of Pip's small hands.

In the warm dimness of the longhouse, the map glowed.

Not the fierce pulse Wren remembered from the ravine or the stair, but a slow, deep light, like embers breathing under ash. The paths shimmered. And the villagers—all of them, not just the elders, for word had traveled and the longhouse had filled with quiet bodies pressing at the doorways—made a sound Wren had never heard from a crowd before. Not gasping. Not cheering. A low, collective exhale, like a congregation at the moment of absolution.

Tammas reached out one gnarled finger—not touching, never quite touching—and traced the air above the valley marked at the map's heart.

"The Ember Roost," he said. "My grandmother saw it burn. The outer eyries, that is. The Night of Cinders."

* * *

They told it slowly, the way old stories are told—passed between them like a cup, each elder adding what the others forgot, correcting, supplementing, until the tale stood complete in the smoky air.

"There were nine eyries on the outer crags," said a woman named Sorrel, whose hands were stained blue-black with dye. "Nesting-sites, watch-posts, places where the dragons' friends could rest between flights. The Wayfarers kept them. Fed the fires. Carried messages no king could intercept."

"And then the Order came back," said another, a stonemason with forearms like tree roots. "The Ashen Blade. They'd been quiet for a generation—everyone thought them dead, scattered. But they came up the Thornfells in the autumn, wearing pilgrim gray, asking sweet questions in the villages. Where are the flying stones? Which families trade with the high passes? My grandfather answered them. He was proud to be asked."

Sorrel took up the thread, her voice flattening. "On the third night of the frost-moon, they burned all nine eyries in a single night. Came down the mountain with fire-oil and faith. The wardens who weren't killed were given a choice: renounce the covenant or hang from their own waystones. Most hung. Some ran. One—" she looked at Sebastian, and her eyes were not unkind, but they were not gentle either, "—one betrayed the roost's approaches to save her own children. The Order let her live. They always let the traitors live. It's how they grow."

Silence. The fire popped. Wren watched Sebastian's face and saw nothing move in it at all, which was worse than anything.

"The dragons withdrew into the deep valley," Tammas said. "Folded their wings over the last of the eggs and waited for the world to forget them. And we—" he gestured at the walls, the village, the empty bell-yoke visible through the doorway, "—we melted our bell for the fire-oil, so no sound would call anyone up the mountain again. Sixty years of silence. Sixty years of keeping the old marks off our doors and the old songs unsung. Because the Order doesn't forgive, and it doesn't forget, and neither do we."

He turned his milk eye to the fire and his good eye to Sebastian.

"But we remember. Every child in Thornhollow learns the Night of Cinders before they learn their letters. We remember because forgetting is how they win twice."

Pip had gone very still, her hand still resting on the edge of the map. When she spoke, her voice was barely above the crackle of the hearth. "Did any of the little ones get out? From the eyries? Before?"

The elders exchanged looks—long, weighted looks, the kind that carry whole conversations.

"Some," Sorrel said finally. "A few. Carried down by wardens who broke their own hearts doing it. Scattered. Hidden." She did not say more, and Wren understood that the not-saying was itself a kind of shelter, extended even now, even to strangers, even to a scaled child sitting in their midst.

* * *

They stayed three nights. It was meant to be two, but the weather closed in—a proper highland storm, rain turning to sleet, the mountain roads dissolving into rivers—and Tammas declared with some satisfaction that the road had decided for them.

Wren spent those days in a state she couldn't immediately name. It took her until the second evening, sitting in the kitchen of Brida—the old woman with the bowl—helping to roll oatcakes while children darted around her legs, to identify it.

She felt *known*.

Not the way the Order had known her—as a name on a ledger, a function, a girl who copied well and asked too few questions until the day she asked one too many. Not even the way Sebastian and Corwin knew her, which was real but new, built in flight, still soft at the edges. This was different. Here she was simply *the scribe traveling with the Montrose girl*, and that was enough, and no one watched her hands to see if they trembled.

Brida caught her staring at nothing and pressed another lump of dough into her hands. "You're far away, scribbling-girl."

"I'm trying to decide if I trust this," Wren admitted. It was easier to say to a stranger. "Warmth. It usually has a bill attached."

Brida laughed, a dry rustling sound. "Everything has a bill. The question is who pays it, and when, and whether the paying breaks you." She shaped her dough with quick, practiced movements. "Our bill came due sixty years ago. We paid it in fire and silence. What's left is ours, free and clear. So we can spend it how we like." She nodded toward the main room, where Sebastian sat talking with Tammas, heads bent together over the glowing map. "And we like spending it on that one. Her grandmother carried our dead down off the crags, you know. Forty-one of them. Walked them down through the snow herself, one journey at a time, so their mothers could bury them proper. The Order called it treason. We called it the only holy thing done in these mountains in a hundred years."

Wren's throat tightened. She looked at Sebastian—at the woman who preferred roads to people, who wore competence like armor over loneliness—and tried to reconcile her with the figure in Brida's story. She couldn't, quite. But she found she wanted to. She found that wanting had weight, and direction, and a name she wasn't ready to say yet.

That night, in the loft above the longhouse, she copied. It was what she did, what she was; the Order had taught her that much and she'd repurposed the skill the way you repurpose a knife found at a murder scene. By candlelight, while the others slept, she transcribed everything the elders had told them—the names of the nine eyries, the route of the Order's ascent, the description of the traitor-warden whose descendants might still live under some changed name. Evidence. Pattern. The raw material of the case she was slowly, secretly building in her head: the case that would need to be made someday, before someone with power, if any of this was to mean anything beyond survival.

She was three pages in when she heard the floorboard creak below.

* * *

The boy's name was Callum, and he was eleven, and Wren had noticed him the first day—noticed him the way she'd learned to notice things in the Order's scriptorium: not because he was remarkable, but because he was *positioned*. Always at the edge of rooms. Always listening. A thin, sharp-faced child with his father's red hair and his mother's watchful silence, and eyes that followed Pip everywhere she went with an expression Wren had initially misread as wonder.

On the third night, she woke to the absence of sound—that particular quality of quiet that means something has recently left it—and went to the window, and saw him.

He was crossing the lower terrace in the rain, moving fast, hunched, heading for the drover's track that wound down toward the lowlands. Toward the garrison road. Toward the nearest place a boy could sell information for enough coin to matter.

Wren didn't think. Later, examining the memory, she would find the gap where thinking should have been, filled instead with something older and faster—the instinct of a girl who had once run from exactly the kind of house that buys children like Callum.

She went down the ladder, out into the freezing rain, barefoot on stone, and caught him at the wall.

He fought like a trapped animal, all elbows and panic, and she took a cut across her cheekbone for her trouble before she got both his arms pinned and turned him to face her. Up close, in the lightning-flash, she saw what she should have seen days ago: not greed. Terror. The specific terror of a child who has decided that the adults around him are too slow, too kind, too *faith-bound* to do what must be done, and that if no one else will act, he must.

"They came to our house in spring," he gasped, rain and tears indistinguishable on his face. "Gray cloaks. Asking about the high passes. They gave my da a medal and my ma a blessing and they wrote down every name in the village, and Da says they'll come back, they always come back, and next time it'll be fire, it was fire last time, my gran *told* me, and you're going to bring it down on us, you and your dragon-child and your burning map, and nobody will stop them, so I'm going to make sure they owe *us* when they do—"

"Callum." Wren shook him once, gently. "Look at me. Look at my face. I was the Order's scribe. I copied their decrees. I know exactly what they write down, and I know exactly what they do with it, and I am telling you—" her voice cracked, and she let it, "—I am telling you that selling us won't save this village. It will only buy you a seat closer to the fire when it starts."

He stared at her. Rain fell. Somewhere behind them, a lamp flared in a doorway—Brida, awake, watching, saying nothing.

"They already know I'm here," Wren said, softer now. "Don't you understand? The moment we crossed your ridge, the moment the map lit up in your longhouse, the clock started. You can't sell what's already sold. All you can choose is what side of it you stand on when it arrives."

His face worked. Eleven years old, carrying sixty years of inherited dread, and no one had ever told him the truth plainly enough to hold.

"I don't want to burn," he whispered.

"No," Wren said. "Neither do I. Neither does she." She tilted her head toward the longhouse, toward the sleeping child with the brightening scales. "So here's what happens instead. You wake your father. You wake Tammas. And you tell them the garrison road runs both ways, and this village has one night to decide whether it's a village that remembers—or a village that gets remembered *about*."

* * *

They left before dawn, in the grey wet hush after the storm, and the whole village turned out to see them go—not cheering, not waving, but standing at their doors in the early dark, silent, present, the way people stand at gravesides and weddings alike. Tammas pressed a bundle into Sebastian's hands: dried meat, honey-cakes wrapped in oilcloth, and a small flat stone carved with a spiral sigil that made Pip inhale sharply when she saw it.

"A waystone," Tammas said. "First cut in sixty years. My grandson made it. Tell the mountain the village remembers."

Sebastian bowed to him—actually bowed, low, formal, in the old style Wren had seen only in guild records—and said words in a language Wren didn't know but understood anyway, the way you understand a mother's tone without translation.

They were a mile down the drover's track, the village lost behind them in the mist, when Corwin, riding rear-guard, said quietly: "Riders. Three. Coming up fast from the garrison road. Gray cloaks."

Wren touched the scratch on her cheekbone and thought of a boy standing in the rain, choosing, at the very last possible moment, to choose.

"Not scouts," she said. "Scouts travel light and stay distant. Those are messengers." She met Sebastian's eyes across the space between their horses. "Which means Vane already knows we passed through Thornhollow. Which means she's not following the map anymore."

Sebastian's face, in the grey light, was unreadable. Then she turned her horse up the track, toward the high passes, toward the hidden valley, and said the words that would govern everything that came after:

"Then we ride like the map is already burning."
