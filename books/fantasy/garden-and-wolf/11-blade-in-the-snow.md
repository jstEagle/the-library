## 11. Blade in the Snow

The snow began an hour before the ambush did.

Corwin felt it first in the old break in his left arm, the one he'd earned at Caelmoor Bridge breaking a farmer's door so the Order's men could drag him out by the hair. The bone ached the way guilty things ache—quietly, constantly, and worst at night. Now it ached in daylight, under a sky the color of dirty wool, and he knew before Wren said anything that the weather was turning against them.

"We should make the tree line," she said, hugging her satchel of stolen documents as if it were a child. "There's shelter past it. An old shrine, maybe."

"Shrines have doors," Sebastian said. She was walking point, as she always did, her stride easy on the frozen track even with Pip riding her shoulders like some strange, heavy scarf. The child had gone quiet in the last hour—not sleeping, but listening, her small scaled hands pressed flat against Sebastian's hood. "Doors mean someone can come through them."

Corwin said nothing. He was watching the slope to their right, where the pines stood too evenly spaced, like men drawn up in formation. Twenty years of soldiering had ruined him for landscapes; everywhere he looked now, he saw ground that could be held, ground that could be lost, ground where bodies would lie well or badly.

He was looking at the trees when he saw the snow fall wrong.

It was a small thing—a puff of white sliding off a branch with no wind to move it. Then another, thirty feet north. Then a third, and between the second and third, a glint of steel that might have been ice except that ice doesn't breathe.

"Ambush," he said, and his voice came out flat and calm, the voice of a man who had done this many times, and that calm frightened him more than the soldiers did. "Right side. At least six. They've been tracking us since the village."

Sebastian didn't waste breath asking how he knew. That was one of the thousand small mercies she extended to him without ever naming it. She simply turned, set Pip down behind a boulder, and drew the short blade she carried with a smuggler's economy—no flourish, no wasted motion.

"How long?" she asked.

"They'll want us in the open a little longer." He unslung his sword, the plain arming sword he'd kept when they took everything else—the Order hadn't wanted it back, because a sword is only holy in the hand of a believer. "They're waiting for the commander's signal."

"You know who commands them."

It wasn't a question. Corwin closed his eyes for half a heartbeat and let himself see the face he'd been seeing in dreams for three days, ever since Wren had spotted the first sign of pursuit: boot prints in a pattern he recognized, a campfire banked the Ashen way, ash raked into a circle.

"Seren Halvard," he said. "Knight-Commander of the Third Vigil. My commander. For eleven years."

* * *

They came out of the trees the way the Ashen Blade always came—without shouting, without challenge, as though the world already belonged to them and they were merely collecting what was owed. Six figures in grey-and-white, snow-cloaks over mail, visors down. And at their center, taller than Corwin remembered, a woman whose armor bore the black sunburst of the Order's high command.

Seren Halvard pushed back her visor. Her face was older than his memory of it, lined at the eyes, but the eyes themselves hadn't changed at all. They were the eyes of a woman who had never once doubted, and Corwin had spent eleven years mistaking that for virtue.

"Brother Corwin." Her voice carried across the snow, warm as a hearth, terrible as one. "We held a vigil for you. Did you know? Three nights, candles lit. Ser Maren wept."

"I doubt it," Corwin said. "Maren doesn't weep. She reports."

A ripple went through the six—laughter strangled at birth, indignation suppressed. Halvard smiled faintly, acknowledging the hit the way one acknowledges a child's cleverness.

"You're wounded," she said, nodding at his side, where the wyvern-scar from the pass still pulled at him. "You're limping. You're traveling with a smuggler, a runaway clerk, and—" her gaze found Pip behind the boulder, and something moved behind her certainty, something hungry and afraid, "—*that*. Look at yourself, Corwin. Look at what you've become."

"I have," he said. "That's rather the point."

"The Order forgives." She spread her hands, gauntlets gleaming. "You know this. Kneel, give up the map and the abomination, and your penance will be light. Service. Cleansing work. You remember how good it feels, afterward, when the fire is out and the town is quiet and the ledger is balanced."

*The ledger.* Yes. He remembered the ledgers. He had kept one himself, once, in a fair hand, recording the names of everyone the Third Vigil burned in the border towns, because the Order believed every death should be accounted for, that accounting was mercy. He had written *Hesta, miller's wife, unrepentant* in his neat clerk's script, and slept soundly that night, and woken feeling clean.

"No," he said.

Halvard sighed, and the warmth went out of her voice like a snuffed candle. "Then I'm sorry, brother. Truly. Draw, if you must. But understand—I am not here to duel you. I am here to end you, quickly, and take the child gently. Those are the only mercies on offer."

"Then we understand each other." Corwin stepped forward, putting himself between the six blades and his people, and heard Sebastian move up on his left without being asked. "One thing, Commander. Send your five away. What happens next is between you and me."

"You were never my equal."

"No," he agreed. "I was your instrument. Instruments break. Let's find out which of us does today."

For a moment he thought she'd refuse, order all six onto him at once—and he saw Sebastian shift, ready to spend herself against impossible odds, and hated that he'd brought her to this. But pride was pride, and Halvard had fed hers on him for a decade. She lifted two fingers, and the five fell back to the tree line, and the snow fell into the silence between them like ash into a brazier.

She came at him fast.

* * *

Fighting Seren Halvard was like fighting a memory that had learned new tricks. She fought the way she'd taught him—economy, pressure, the patient accumulation of small advantages until the enemy found himself fighting uphill without knowing when the hill had been built. She drove him back along the track, boot over boot, and he gave ground gladly because giving ground was the only gift he had left to give.

His wound tore early. He felt it in the fourth exchange, a hot wet line across his ribs where the half-healed flesh split, and after that every parry cost him something he couldn't afford. She saw it too—he watched her see it—and her next attacks came slower, almost gentle, offering him chances to quit.

"Kneel," she said again, low, between strokes. "Corwin. *Kneel.* I don't want your name in the ledger."

"My name's been in ledgers for years," he gasped. "I wrote it there myself."

Her blade opened his cheek. His opened her pauldron strap. Around them the five at the tree line shifted, restless, and beyond them Sebastian circled wide with her knife, and Wren shouted something about the shrine, and Pip—

Pip screamed.

Not a child's scream. Something older, resonant, a sound that came up through the snow itself, and every horse the Order had brought screamed back, and two of the five broke and ran before anyone gave the word. Halvard faltered—one heartbeat, her eyes going to the boulder, to the small scaled figure standing on top of it with her arms flung wide and her eyes glowing like coals—and in that heartbeat Corwin stopped fighting fairly.

He swept her legs, put her down in the snow, and set his point at the joint of her gorget.

They stared at each other, breathing hard. Snow settled on her upturned face. Ten feet away, Sebastian had one of the remaining soldiers disarmed and kneeling, and the other two were backing toward the trees, and none of it mattered, none of it existed, because Corwin was looking down at the woman who had made him and understanding, finally, what she was.

"Do it," Halvard said. "Finish it. Be the monster they've made you."

"That's the difference between us," Corwin said. "I don't think you're a monster either. I think you're a believer, and belief without doubt is just a blade nobody's ever sharpened against truth." He stepped back. Lowered the sword. "Go home, Seren. Tell them I'm dead. Tell them whatever keeps the rest of them out of Thornfell. Because if the Order comes into these mountains after that child, I will not be the only brother who turns."

She rose slowly, snow sliding from her cloak, and for one moment—the first moment in eleven years—she looked at him and truly saw him, and what she saw made her step back as if from a fire.

"You'll die out here," she said quietly. "All of you. And worse than dying, you'll be *wrong*, and no one will ever correct the record."

"Then write it however you like," Corwin said. "I'm done being written."

She whistled up her broken remnant and withdrew into the trees, and the snow closed behind her like water over a stone.

* * *

The wound was bad.

Wren said so first, in the clinical tone she used when she was frightened, peeling back his shirt where the old scar had torn open along its whole length. The flesh around it was angry and dark, and blood welled slow and stubborn, refusing to clot the way wounds refuse when the body has decided, somewhere below thought, that it has bled enough for one life.

"It won't close cleanly," Wren said. "I've seen this in the Order infirmary. Wounds taken in shame fester."

"That's not medicine, that's theology."

"It's both, with them." She packed it with moss and bound it tight, efficient and gentle at once, and Corwin sat against the shrine wall—it had turned out to be a real shrine, a waystone chapel of the old guilds, its carved lintel worn nearly smooth—and let himself be tended, because refusing care was a pride he'd finally outgrown.

Sebastian sent Pip to gather wood and then came and sat beside him, close enough that their shoulders touched, saying nothing. That was her way. She could wait out a storm, a siege, a man's silence. He lasted perhaps ten minutes.

"You should know," he said, to the snow. "What I did. Before you decide whether I walk with you any further."

"I've seen your ledger face," she said. "I've known since the ravine."

"Not all of it." His hands wouldn't stay still; he folded them, unfolded them. "You know I executed people for the Order. You don't know how many. I do. I kept count. Forty-one. No—forty-three. Two I try to forget and forgetting them is its own crime." The numbers came out of him like stones from a wound. "Mostly dragon-blooded. Mostly innocent. A family in Marrowdale—a man, his sister, a boy of six who had scales only on his shoulder blades, hidden under his shirt, who never hurt anyone, who *hid*, because his parents taught him to hide, and we found him anyway because I was good at finding."

The snow fell. Somewhere Pip was stacking wood with careful, humming concentration, and the ordinary sound of it was unbearable.

"Halvard called me brother," Corwin said. "And I let her. For eleven years I let her, because brotherhood is warm and guilt is cold, and I chose warm. Every night. I chose it every single night until a knight I'd trained cut my rope in a ravine and left me to die, and lying there at the bottom I finally understood that the Order doesn't forgive, it *archives*. It files you away. And I lay there thinking about the boy from Marrowdale, and I swore that if I climbed out of that ravine, I would spend whatever was left of me paying down the debt." He laughed, and it hurt his ribs. "I didn't expect the debt to have a face. Or to hum when it's happy."

Sebastian was quiet a long time. Long enough that he began composing his goodbye—where he'd go, how he'd draw pursuit away from them, whether the wound would let him climb.

"When I was nine," she said at last, "my grandmother took me aside and told me our family had a duty. A great, old, sacred duty. And then she died, and the guild fell apart, and I decided she'd been a mad old woman telling stories to make a lonely girl feel important." She turned and looked at him, and her eyes in the grey light were steady as waystones. "I spent twenty years believing a lie because the truth was heavier. So no, Corwin. I'm not going to cast you out for doing the same thing with worse company."

"It's not the same."

"No. Yours is worse." She said it plainly, without cruelty, and he flinched, and she let him flinch. "But you climbed out of the ravine. That's the whole test, as far as I can tell. Everyone else is still down there."

He looked at her—at this woman he'd known barely two weeks, who trusted with her eyes open, who had weighed his sins and found him worth the risk anyway—and something in his chest cracked along a different line than his ribs, and healed differently too.

"Pip shouldn't hear the number," he said hoarsely. "Forty-three. When she's older, if she asks—"

"She'll ask," Sebastian said. "And you'll tell her. And she'll decide what you are, same as I did." She stood, brushing snow from her knees, and offered him her arm. "Come on, arms-master. Wren says there's fever coming. We move tonight or not at all."

He took her arm and rose, and the world tilted, and far to the south—so far it might have been imagination—there was a glow on the horizon that wasn't dawn.

Wren saw it too. She had gone very still at the edge of the shrine, her satchel clutched to her chest, her face draining white.

"That's not a forest fire," she whispered. "Forest fires don't burn green."
