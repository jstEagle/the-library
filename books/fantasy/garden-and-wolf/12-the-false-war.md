## 12. The False War

Inquisitor Vane had always believed that fire told the truth.

It was one of the first lessons her mother taught her, back in the ash-country before the Order had a name again—before the banners were sewn and the oaths re-sworn. Fire did not lie. It consumed what it touched with perfect impartiality: the guilty and the innocent, the beautiful and the worthless, all of it rendered down to the same gray honesty. A lie could survive a courtroom. A lie could survive a century of songs. But no lie survived flame.

So when she stood on the ridge above Harrowmere at dusk, watching the first plumes of smoke rise from the town's eastern quarter, she permitted herself to feel what she always felt at such moments: not triumph, but clarity. The world was becoming honest at last.

"Timing is good," said Brother Cassel beside her, squinting through the lens of his spyglass. "The lamplighters are out. Half the town will see it."

"The whole town will see it," Vane corrected. "That is rather the point."

Below them, Harrowmere sat in its bowl of hills like a pat of butter left too near a hearth—a prosperous border town, fat with trade from the Thornfell passes, its slate roofs still holding the day's warmth. Three days ago it had been an ordinary place where ordinary people complained about grain prices and the weather. By tomorrow morning it would be something else entirely. It would be a wound. And wounds, Vane knew, were the most persuasive documents ever written.

She turned to the men waiting behind her in the bracken—six of them, hooded, their armor wrapped in sacking to dull any gleam. At their feet lay the iron cask, banded shut, sweating cold despite the summer evening. Even through the metal she could feel it: a low, wrong heat, like a fever dreaming beneath the skin.

"Open it," she said.

The cask's lid came away with a sound like a sigh, and the smell rolled out—sulfur and char and something older underneath, something that made the horses stamp and the youngest brother take an involuntary step backward. Wyvern-fire. Harvested from the last captured beast of the old wars, hoarded in the Order's vaults for thirty years in wax-sealed jars, waiting. It did not burn like mortal fire. It clung. It remembered being alive. Water would not kill it, nor sand, nor prayer; only dragon's blood or a full season of starvation would quench it once it took hold.

Vane lifted one of the jars—a clay vessel no larger than a wine flask, its contents glowing a sullen orange through the glaze—and held it up against the darkening sky.

"Do you know why we do this?" she asked the six hooded men. She did not wait for an answer; she never did. "Because the mountain clans have forgotten. Because the Crown has grown comfortable. Because for a generation the lords of this realm have looked at the Thornfells and seen nothing but sheep pasture and smugglers' trails." Her voice stayed level, almost gentle; she had learned long ago that conviction spoke best in a whisper. "But I have read the old records. I have stood in the burned eyries. I have seen what sleeps in those mountains, brothers. And sleep is not the same as death. Not for *them*."

She let the jar catch the last light. Inside it, the wyvern-fire stirred, slow as honey, like something turning over in its sleep.

"One spark," Vane said, "and every soul in that valley will know exactly what I have known my whole life. Fear is a teacher. Tonight, Harrowmere goes to school."

* * *

The attack itself took less time than the sermon afterward would.

They went down in three pairs, moving along the streambed where the water muffled their steps, and Vane watched from the ridge with the patience of a woman who had done this arithmetic many times. Two jars into the tannery district, where the hides would feed the flames for hours. One jar into the granary row—not enough to destroy the harvest, only enough to blacken it, to make hunger a rumor in every belly. One jar into the temple of the Nine, because faith that had never been tested was merely habit, and Vane intended to test Harrowmere's faith until it broke into whatever shape she needed.

The fourth jar—the largest—was reserved for the churchyard wall, where the town's oldest stones stood. Old stone burned strangely under wyvern-fire. It cracked and wept and slumped like melting candlewax, and in the morning the survivors would find their ancestors' monuments fused into grotesques, and they would say, in voices gone small and childish: *nothing human did this.*

Which was true. Nothing human had. That was the elegance of it. Vane had never once lied in the prosecution of her great work. Every report she filed, every testimony she extracted, every tearful account she coaxed out of a soot-blackened survivor—all of it would be accurate. A dragon had attacked Harrowmere. Dragon-fire had burned it. The distinction between *dragon-fire* and *a dragon* was a lawyer's distinction, and lawyers had never stopped her yet.

From the ridge she saw the first jar burst against the tannery roof, and even at this distance the light was wrong—not the gold of honest flame but a deeper color, a bruised amber, the light of something ancient and furious. The screaming started within moments. Small, at first, scattered cries, then the great rolling bell of collective terror as windows lit up across the town and people poured into the streets in their nightclothes, pointing eastward, pointing at the sky, finding shapes in the smoke because terror always finds shapes.

"There," whispered Brother Cassel, gripping the ridge-stone. "Look—they're saying it. They're saying *dragon*."

Vane did not need the spyglass to know it was true. She could hear it on the wind, thin and ragged: the word passing mouth to mouth down the burning streets. *Dragon. Dragon. A dragon.* Thirty years since the last verified sighting, and still the word lived in the marrow of these people, planted there by grandmothers' tales and chapel frescoes and the Order's own careful catechisms. You did not have to teach a child to fear dragons. You only had to refrain from teaching him not to.

"Note the hour," she said. "Note which districts burned. We'll want the details consistent when the survivors testify."

Cassel produced his ledger without being told. He was a good man, Cassel—dull, obedient, incapable of imagining that his inquisitor might be doing anything other than the world's most necessary work. Vane sometimes envied him that. Her own certainty required maintenance. It required her to stand, night after night, watching towns burn by her own hand, and to hold two truths in her chest at once: *I am saving them*, and *I am the reason they are screaming*. Most minds would collapse trying to carry both. Hers had learned to let them share the weight, the way a mule carries panniers balanced on either side.

Because that was the truth no one else would speak: the dragons *were* coming back. She had proof—fragments of a courier's dispatches, rumors of a scale-map moving through the underworld of the guild roads, reports of a half-blood child glimpsed in the high passes. The creatures were stirring in their hidden roost, gathering strength, and the realms below had spent a generation disarming themselves, sentimentalizing the old wars, telling stories where dragons were noble and men who hunted them were villains. When the fire came—and it would come—it would fall on a world that had chosen to forget how to fight it.

Unless someone made the world remember. Unless someone gave the fear a shape and the fear a purpose and the fear an army.

Vane watched Harrowmere burn and felt the balance of her panniers hold steady.

* * *

By dawn the story had grown legs.

She walked through the ruined eastern quarter while the ashes were still warm, hooded, anonymous among the relief columns already arriving from the garrison at Cael Ford. This was the part of the work she never delegated: listening. A crusade built on manufactured grief was still a crusade that needed real grief to stand on, and grief, properly harvested, was the richest crop in the world.

In the smoking ruin of the tannery district she found a woman sitting in the road, holding a child's shoe. Just the one. The woman did not weep; she had passed through weeping into some country beyond it, and her eyes tracked Vane with the flat wonder of the newly bereaved.

"It came out of the north," the woman said, unprompted, to anyone who would listen. "Out of the mountains. My boy saw it first—he said its wings blotted out the stars."

There had been no wings. There had been four clay jars and six hooded men. But Vane knelt in the ash beside her, took the woman's ink-stained hand in both of hers, and said, "Tell me everything. The Crown must hear it all."

And the woman told her everything—the wings, the roar, the eyes like furnace doors, each detail growing more vivid as she spoke, because memory under terror is a forgiving medium and grief paints in bold strokes. By the time Vane rose to move on, the woman's account had become scripture. By noon, when the garrison captain took her formal deposition, it had acquired a second witness, then a third, each corroborating the other because each had heard the same tale told in the square before swearing to their own version of it. Terror, Vane reflected, was the only lie that assembled itself. You struck the spark; the crowd wrote the confession.

By evening of the second day, riders were galloping south with dispatches sealed in black wax. By the third day, the first companies of volunteers were forming at Cael Ford—farmers' sons with scythes and hunting bows, smiths with hammers they meant to melt into spearheads, gray-bearded veterans of the old wars coming out of retirement with tears in their eyes, saying they had prayed all their lives for the chance to finish what was started at the Night of Cinders.

Vane reviewed them from a scaffold in the market square, and the sight of them filled her with a feeling she was careful never to name aloud, because naming it would make it vulnerable. It was not pride. Pride was a sin of self. This was closer to awe—the awe of watching a machine assemble itself out of raw human material, gears of grief meshing with wheels of faith, the whole vast engine turning toward the mountains with a momentum no council, no crown, no court of law could now stop.

Three thousand spears at Cael Ford alone. The mountain clans would add more once the summons went out—clansmen who owed the Order old debts, clansmen whose grandmothers remembered the eyrie-burnings and sang about them still. And the Crown, which had dithered for years over petitions to garrison the Thornfell passes, would sign the crusade writ within the fortnight, because no monarch alive could refuse three thousand grieving spears and survive the politics of it.

"They're calling it the Rekindling," Cassel reported, walking beside her along the review line. "The preachers, I mean. The fire of Harrowmere rekindling the old vigil. It's spreading through the villages faster than our own riders can carry it."

"Good," Vane said. "Let them preach. Faith does our recruiting better than coin ever did."

"And the map, Inquisitor? The courier's cargo? Our agents in the Thornfells report the girl Montrose has gathered companions—a knight, a scribe. They were seen crossing toward the Weeping Stair."

Vane paused. Somewhere behind her, a company of volunteers was singing—one of the old hymns, the ones about watchfires on the heights, their voices cracking with earnestness. She found, to her mild surprise, that the question of Sebastian Montrose and her stolen map had begun to shrink in her attention, the way a lamp shrinks when the sun comes up.

"Let her run," Vane said. "A month ago, the map mattered—yes. If she reaches the roost before us, if the beasts are warned..." She shook her head slowly. "But look at what stands behind you, Cassel. Look at what stands behind me. Three thousand men who watched a border town burn. Do you understand what I have purchased with one cask of wyvern-fire? I no longer need to find the roost by following a map. I need only drive the whole crusade into the Thornfells and let three thousand torches search where a single courier failed. The mountains will give up their dead. And if the girl reaches the dragons first—" the ghost of something crossed her face, not quite a smile, "—then she will be standing in the roost when we arrive. She has saved us the trouble of a siege."

Cassel laughed, a short bark of surprise. "You've stopped hunting her."

"I've stopped needing to hunt her," Vane corrected. "There is a difference. The hare may outrun the hound, brother, but nothing outruns the fire that burns the whole wood." She turned to face the ranks one last time—three thousand faces upturned, lit by morning sun and by the reflection of their own certainty. "Besides. Every road she takes now leads her deeper into the range. Every companion she gathers is another witness to her treason. She thinks she is carrying the map to safety." Vane folded her hands behind her back, and her voice dropped to the register she saved for truths spoken over open graves. "She is carrying it to the pyre."

The hymn swelled behind her. Down the review line, the volunteers began to march, spear-butts striking earth in rhythm, a sound like a slow enormous heartbeat beginning to quicken. Vane stood on the scaffold and let it pass beneath her, wave after wave of it, and felt the future tilt beneath her feet toward the mountains.

One thing remained. That evening, alone in her command tent, she unrolled the Order's oldest relic across her field desk: a scrap of dragon-scale, cured and mounted, taken from the corpse of the last wyvern. On its surface, in blood-ink that never faded, an old prophecy copied from the burned eyries:

*When the ember gutters, a warden's blood shall wake it.*

Warden's blood. The Wayfarer line. Which meant the girl Montrose was not merely a thief fleeing with stolen goods. She was, by the dragons' own ancient reckoning, the key that fit the lock—and keys, Vane knew, were only useful to whoever held them last.

She weighted the scale-flat with her dagger and studied it by lamplight for a long time.

Then she called for her horse. The crusade would march at dawn, and Inquisitor Vane intended to ride at its head—not behind three thousand spears, but ahead of them, into the Thornfells, to meet the last warden of the Wayfarers' Guild face to face.

Some fires, after all, were best set personally.
