## 13. Ember Road

The map woke at dawn.

Sebastian had grown used to its moods over the weeks of travel—the way it lay dull and cold in her pack when they walked true, and how it warmed against her hip like a sleeping animal when they strayed. But this morning it was not warm. It was *lit*. She pulled it out where she crouched by the dead fire and the scale-patterning across its surface shimmered with a light that came from nowhere else in the grey world, a light like coals seen through closed eyelids.

"Pip," she said quietly. "Come look at this."

The child was already awake—Pip was always awake before anyone, curled at the edge of camp like a cat who trusted no one enough to sleep deeply—and came padding over on bare feet that left faint scorch-prints in the frost. That was new. A month ago her feet had been only feet. Now the frost hissed where she stepped, and Sebastian pretended not to notice because Pip hated being noticed.

The child looked down at the map and went very still.

"It's singing," Pip said.

"It's glowing."

"It's *singing*." Pip's voice had an odd, strained quality, as though she were hearing something through water. "It's been humming for days but now it's—it's like someone calling my name from very far away. Except it's not my name. It's older than names."

Corwin rose from where he'd been checking the straps on their gear, one hand going to the sword he still carried even though the wound in his side made every movement a negotiation. The bandage beneath his shirt had stopped seeping two days ago, which Wren declared a miracle and Corwin called "the wound deciding to be patient," and neither of them laughed. "Which way?" he asked.

Sebastian turned the map in her hands. The glow pooled toward the northeast, up a slope of grey scree where no path showed—no path on the ground, and none on any chart she'd memorized in thirty years of walking the Thornfells. She knew these mountains the way other people knew their own hands. There was nothing northeast of here but the Weeping Stair's back wall and the empty crown of Mount Cairnloss.

Except the map disagreed. The map insisted there was a road.

"The Ember Road," Wren murmured. She'd come up behind them without sound, as always, her scribe's eyes already narrowed at the light. "I copied fragments about it once, in the Order's archive. Deeds of land transfer, mostly, four hundred years old. They mentioned a road that 'opens only to warden blood.' I assumed it was poetry." Her mouth twisted. "I assumed a great deal of things was poetry."

Sebastian stood, shouldering her pack, and felt the map settle against her spine like a hand between her shoulder blades. "Then we walk northeast."

"There's nothing northeast," Corwin said.

"There's a road," Sebastian said. "We just haven't been invited yet."

She started up the scree, and after a moment she heard them follow—Wren's quick light steps, Corwin's heavier tread favoring his left side, Pip's whispering bare feet. Behind them the frost-bound camp dissolved into ordinary morning mist, and ahead the mountain rose grey and indifferent, and between those two greys Sebastian walked toward a glow only she and a half-dragon child could see, thinking: *this is either the last mistake of the Wayfarers' Guild or the first right thing we've done in three hundred years.*

* * *

They climbed for most of the day, and the strangeness began slowly, the way all true strangeness does.

First it was the warmth. Sebastian noticed it as a wrongness before she noticed it as a comfort—a patch of air against her cheek that should have been cold and wasn't, like walking past an open oven door in a frozen kitchen. Then another patch. Then more, until the patches braided together into a current flowing downhill past them, warm air rising from somewhere above, which was impossible, because warm air rises and therefore must be born below, and above them there was nothing but stone.

"Feel that?" Corwin asked, and for once there was no guardedness in his voice, only wonder, and it made him look ten years younger and ten years more lost.

"I feel it," Sebastian said.

"It smells like—" Pip stopped, nostrils flaring, and whatever she smelled made her eyes go wide and wet. "It smells like the dream. The one I have when I sleep near the map. I never told anyone about the dream."

"You can tell us now," Wren said gently.

But Pip shook her head and pressed her lips shut, and Sebastian understood. Some things broke if you said them too soon. She'd felt the same about the guildhall, once, about the smell of tallow and old leather that meant *home*, and she'd learned to keep it behind her teeth where it couldn't be taken from her.

Then it was the stones.

They appeared first as a glint on the slope ahead—a curve of weathered granite thrusting from the scree like a tooth. Sebastian would have passed it without a glance; the Thornfells were full of standing stones, most of them natural, the rest raised by clans whose names predated the Crown. But as she drew level with it, the stone *hummed*. Not a sound in the ears—a sound in the chest, in the fillings of her teeth, in the soles of her boots. A low, patient note, like a bell that had been ringing since before the world finished cooling and intended to continue.

Pip pressed both palms flat against it and laughed—a startled, unguarded laugh, the first Sebastian had ever heard from her. "It knows me! It says—I don't know how I know what it says—but it says *welcome home, little ember*."

Sebastian laid her own hand on the stone. Nothing. Cold granite, a faint vibration, perhaps imagination. But when she looked at her palm afterward, the calluses left by thirty years of rope and road seemed suddenly like something other than a smuggler's marks. Like a grip worn into a tool by long use. Like a handle shaped by hands that expected it to be held again.

More stones followed, each one humming, spaced with a regularity no glacier could explain—a rhythm, Sebastian realized as the afternoon wore on, a *cadence*, like milestones. Like markers along a road.

Because it was a road. She saw it then, or rather she saw the absence that was a road: a line up the mountainside where the scree lay differently, where the lichen grew in a seam too straight, where the snowmelt ran in channels cut by no water. A road built by hands that had wanted it invisible, that had folded it into the mountain the way the Wayfarers folded contraband into legitimate cargo. Her guild's oldest trick, written in stone across an entire mountainside.

"We were never smugglers," she said aloud, without meaning to.

"No," Wren said softly. "You were keepers. The smuggling was the disguise. Or maybe—" she hesitated, scribe's precision wrestling with kindness, "—maybe the keeping became the disguise, after. When there was nothing left to keep."

Sebastian didn't answer. Ahead, the road bent around a shoulder of rock, and the warmth pouring down it had thickened into something almost liquid, and the light of the low sun had turned the color of forge-coal, and Pip was running—actually running, bare feet striking sparks off the stone—toward the bend in the road like a child running toward a door she'd been locked outside of all her life.

"Pip!" Corwin lunged after her, wound and all, and Sebastian followed, and the three of them came around the shoulder of the mountain together and stopped as one.

* * *

The valley opened below them like a held breath finally released.

It was small—as valleys went, hardly a dent in the Thornfells' massif—and it should have been invisible from any distance, tucked between two ridges that leaned together like conspirators. The walls rose steep and dark, but the floor of it glowed. Not with fire. With *warmth made visible*: a soft amber suffusion that rose from the ground itself, from seams in the rock and veins in the soil, so that the whole valley looked like a bed of embers raked over with thin ash, breathing light.

Waystones ringed the entire bowl of it—hundreds of them, Sebastian guessed, some tall as men, some mere knee-high nubs, each one humming, and the hums braided together into a chord so deep she felt it in her back teeth and her ribs and the roots of her hair. Old terraces cut the lower slopes, long abandoned, orchard-rows gone to scrub. And at the far end, high up where the two ridges met, a darkness in the cliff-face that might have been a cave mouth, or a doorway, or an eye.

The air was warm as late summer. In the middle of the Thornfells. In the dying of the year.

"Four hundred years," Wren whispered. She had her journal out and her hands were shaking too badly to write. "Four hundred years the Order has hunted dragons to extinction, and this was here the whole time. Hidden under a covenant. Under *your* covenant, Sebastian. Your grandmother's people didn't fail. They hid it so well that even their descendants forgot."

"Not forgot," Sebastian heard herself say. "Were made to forget. There's a difference." But she didn't know yet what the difference cost, or who had paid it, and the valley's warmth lay against her face like a question she couldn't translate.

Pip walked past them all.

Down the road, onto the valley floor, through the amber light, and as she went she *changed*. Sebastian watched it happen and would remember it all her life. The dull grey-green scales scattered across the child's skin—across her forearms, her throat, the ridge of her cheekbones—began to brighten, kindling from within like coals breathed upon. By ones and twos they shifted color: bronze, then copper, then a deep burning orange-gold, until the child walking ahead of them was flecked with living ember-light, each scale a tiny lamp. Her gait changed too, the wary hunch straightening, her chin lifting, her nostrils working the air like a creature reading a language written in scent. At the valley's center she stopped beside a waystone taller than Corwin, laid both hands on it, and *sang*—a single clear note that matched the chord of the stones exactly, the way a key matches a lock.

Every stone in the valley flared.

Light rolled outward from Pip in a slow wave, stone to stone, and the hum swelled into something that was almost speech, almost music, almost a word spoken so slowly that a human lifetime was too short to hear it end. Warmth washed over Sebastian like surf. Somewhere in the cliff-face darkness at the valley's head, something vast exhaled—a sound like wind through a cathedral, like a bellows the size of a hill drawing breath—and the amber light of the whole valley flickered, dimmed, and steadied, brighter than before.

Corwin had gone to his knees. Whether from reverence or his wound, Sebastian couldn't tell, and suspected he couldn't either. His sword-hand gripped the hilt—not, she thought, to draw it, but because holding steel was the only prayer he knew. "Gods forgive me," he said, to no god in particular. "All those years I served liars, and the truth was sitting in a valley the whole time, humming."

"Don't kneel," Sebastian said. "Get up. Whatever comes next, it'll need us standing."

Wren was writing now, furiously, tears cutting tracks through the trail-dust on her face, narrating aloud in the flat voice she used when feeling threatened her composure: "*The fourteenth day of the descent. The Ember Roost is real. The covenant is real. Pip is—*" She stopped. Looked up. "Sebastian. What *is* she?"

Sebastian watched the child turn in the flaring light, scales blazing gold, face lifted toward the dark doorway in the cliffs with an expression Sebastian had no name for—recognition, maybe, or homesickness for a home she'd never seen.

"She's the reason," Sebastian said. "Whatever else this place is, it's the reason the map picked us. Come on. Before she walks into that cave alone."

They crossed the valley floor together, three travelers and one blazing child, and the waystones sang them past, and the warm air carried a smell that Sebastian finally, finally placed—the smell of her grandmother's hall on baking day, of banked hearths and cedar and something older underneath, something mineral and vast and patient. The destination felt less like arrival than like *returning*. Like a memory she'd been carrying all her life without knowing it, and the valley was simply where the memory lived.

At the base of the cliff, before the dark mouth of the roost, the amber light gathered itself into a pool on the stone, and in the pool of light lay a single object: a waystone shard, small as a knife, its surface carved with a spiral sigil Sebastian had seen exactly once before—burned into the flyleaf of her grandmother's journal.

As she knelt to pick it up, the deep exhale sounded again from the darkness above, closer now, and with it came a voice—not heard so much as remembered, arriving fully formed inside her skull, old as the chord of the stones:

*"Three hundred years, little warden. You are late."*

Sebastian rose slowly, the shard in her fist, her heart hammering against her ribs like a trapped bird, and looked up into the dark where the last dragon waited.
