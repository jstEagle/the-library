## 14. The Last Dragon

The valley did not open so much as admit them.

Sebastian had followed the map's glow for three days—through a pass so narrow the sky was a ribbon of slate, down a slope where the pines grew silvered and strange, their needles whispering in a wind that seemed to come from beneath the earth rather than above it. And then, at dawn on the fourth morning, the walls of the gorge simply fell away, and there it was: a bowl of green cradled between peaks that wore their snow like crowns, warm air rising from vents in the stone, carrying the smell of hot iron and something older, something like rain on sun-baked rock.

Waystones lined the path into the valley's heart. Each one hummed as they passed—a low, resonant note that Sebastian felt in her back teeth, in the bones of her wrists, in the scar tissue of her palms where twenty years of rope and road had written their history. The stones were taller than she was, carved with spirals worn nearly smooth by time, and each one flickered faintly as she drew near, like coals breathed upon.

"They know you," Pip said softly, walking at her elbow. The child's scales had brightened over the last days from dull bronze to something approaching gold, catching the morning light along her jaw and forearms. Her eyes—those unsettling amber eyes with their vertical pupils—were wide and wet. "The stones. They're singing to you."

"Stones don't sing."

"These do." Pip pressed her small hand against one as they passed, and the hum deepened, sweetened, resolved into something almost melodic. "They've been waiting a long time for someone who could hear them properly."

Sebastian said nothing. She had spent the whole climb refusing to think about what waited at the valley's end, and she refused to start now. Ahead, Wren walked with her satchel clutched to her chest like a shield, muttering fragments of translation under her breath. Behind them, Corwin limped—the wound in his side had closed but not healed, a dark seam of flesh that refused to knit clean—and he kept his hand near his sword out of habit rather than hope. What sword would matter here?

At the valley's center stood a hill, and atop the hill, a ring of standing stones larger than any Sebastian had ever seen, each one blackened and cracked as if by ancient fire. Beyond the ring, half-hidden in the heat-shimmer rising from the ground, something vast shifted.

Sebastian stopped walking.

Wren nearly collided with her back. "What—oh." The scribe's voice went thin. "Oh, that's real. That's actually—I thought perhaps the map was symbolic, I thought—"

"It's real," Corwin said quietly. He had gone very pale beneath his weathering. "I saw one once. From a distance. At the burning of the eyries." His hand found the hilt of his sword anyway, and Sebastian watched him force his fingers loose again, one by one. "It doesn't help. Seeing one before. Nothing helps."

The thing beyond the stones unfolded itself, slowly, the way a mountain might unfold if mountains could be tired. Scales the color of old ash shot through with veins of dying ember—red-gold light guttering beneath grey like a fire sinking into its own coals. Wings folded against a body long as a river barge, wings that must once have shadowed whole villages and now lay slack, their membranes scarred and patched. A neck curved down, down, until a head the size of a haywain hovered above the standing stones, regarding them through eyes like deep wells of amber lit from below.

When Ysolde spoke, her voice did not come through the air so much as through the ground, up through Sebastian's boots and shins and spine, resonating in the same register as the waystones.

*Three travelers and a spark.* The great head tilted. *And one of them carries my map.*

"We didn't come to steal anything," Wren blurted, then winced, as if surprised by her own courage. "I mean—we came because we were asked. Carried. The courier said—to the Ember. She died getting it this far. Well, not this far, but—"

*Hush, little archivist.* There was no menace in it. If anything, the dragon sounded amused, in the way of someone who has heard every possible opening speech across ten centuries. *You will all have your turn. But first—*

The amber eyes fixed on Sebastian, and the weight of that gaze was physical, a hand pressing down upon her shoulders.

*First, the one whose blood sings.*

---

Ysolde did not ask them to approach. She simply lowered her head further, until her chin rested among the standing stones, and waited, and there was nothing to do but wait with her. Up close, Sebastian could see the truth the distance had softened: the dragon was dying. Not wounded—dying, the way a fire dies when the last log has burned to ash and only the shape of the heat remains. The ember-light beneath her scales flickered irregularly now, guttering, and when she exhaled, the breath carried the scent of cooling metal.

*I have slept a hundred years,* Ysolde said, *and woken for an hour at a time, keeping my vigil over what remains. The map was my last act of faith—that somewhere in the world of men, one still existed who could carry it home. The courier was meant to find such a one. Instead she found you.*

"I'm nobody," Sebastian said. The words came out flat, automatic, the reflex of a lifetime. "A smuggler. A guide. The Wayfarers' Guild is three people and a leaking roof."

*Is it.* The amber eyes did not blink. Dragons, Sebastian realized, probably did not blink often enough for the habit to be comfortable. *Then let us discover what you are. All of you. Truth is the toll of this valley; no one crosses my stones without paying it.*

She turned her head toward Wren first, and Sebastian felt the group contract around the scribe like hands closing.

*Little thief of other people's words. You copied decrees that sent families to the pyres, and you told yourself ink is not fire. You ran when your conscience woke—but tell me, archivist: did you run to make amends, or merely to stop feeling?*

Wren's face went white, then blotchy red. She stood her ground, barely, arms wrapped around herself. "Both," she said finally, and her voice cracked on it. "I'd love to tell you it was principle. It wasn't, not at first. I ran because I couldn't sleep. Because I kept seeing names I'd copied and wondering if they were dead yet." She swallowed. "But I stayed with these people. I decoded the map. I stole Vane's documents knowing what they'd do to me if I was caught. So maybe it started as cowardice, but it isn't cowardice anymore. That has to count."

*A beginning counts,* Ysolde said. *Beginnings are all any of us are given.* The great eye moved on, and Corwin straightened as though bracing for a blow.

*Knight. You wore the Ashen Blade's colors while its fires burned children in their beds. You executed the innocent on orders and called it duty. Why should my valley open to you?*

Corwin did not flinch away, and Sebastian, watching him, understood that he had rehearsed this confession his whole ride across the Thornfells—not for the dragon, but for himself. "I shouldn't be forgiven," he said. "There's no version of arithmetic that balances those books. I can't give back what I took." His hand went to his wounded side, unconsciously. "All I can do is stand on the other side of the line now. Every time. Until I fall. It isn't penance. Penance implies I expect to come out clean. It's just... the only honest thing left."

For a long moment the dragon regarded him, and the ember-light beneath her scales pulsed once, slow, like a heartbeat deciding.

*You understand cost,* she said at last. *That is rarer than virtue. Enter, knight. Your debt travels with you, but it will walk behind you, not ahead.*

Then the amber gaze returned to Sebastian, and everything else in the world went quiet—the wind, the humming stones, even Pip's small breathing beside her.

*And you. Guide of the fading guild. You have spent your whole life making yourself small. Invisible. Useful and unseen. Tell me why.*

"That's not a question anyone needs a dragon for," Sebastian said. Her voice came out rougher than she intended. "People leave. People die. The guild taught me roads are safer than faces. You learn the terrain instead of the person, because terrain doesn't die on you in winter."

*Liar,* Ysolde said, gently, terribly. *You learned that lesson before the guild ever taught it. Your grandmother carried you on her hip along these very paths, and when the covenant broke and the wardens scattered, she hid what she was so well that she hid it from you. You are not invisible because the world failed to see you, daughter of Montrose. You are invisible because you inherited the hiding.*

The words landed like a blade sliding between ribs. Sebastian's mouth opened and produced nothing.

*The map did not choose you by accident,* Ysolde continued, and now her voice dropped lower, deeper, until Sebastian felt it more than heard it, felt it in the marrow the way she felt the waystones' song. *The courier was drawn to you as a needle is drawn north. Your blood carries the warden covenant—sworn generations ago, sworn in fire, passed mother to daughter down the years like a coal wrapped in ash. Your grandmother kept it. Her mother kept it. They kept it so well that the keeping became silence, and the silence became a life of small roads and small cargoes, and everyone forgot—including you—what the cargo was ever for.*

"I guide people," Sebastian whispered. "That's all. That's all I've ever been."

*No.* The dragon's head lowered further, until one vast amber eye filled Sebastian's entire sky, and in it she saw reflected a woman she did not recognize: small, weathered, standing straight despite everything, with light from the waystones caught in her hair. *You are the last warden of the Ember Roost. You always have been. The only question left is whether you will do it kneeling or standing.*

---

Afterward, none of them spoke for a long time.

Ysolde had withdrawn to the far side of the stone ring and settled herself with a groan of shifting stone and scale, folding her ruined wings close, and the travelers stood in the valley's warmth like pilgrims at the edge of a shrine, unsure whether they had been blessed or sentenced.

Pip broke the silence first, tugging at Sebastian's sleeve. "She said daughter of Montrose. Is that true? About your grandmother?"

"My grandmother taught me to read trail-sign before she taught me letters." Sebastian's voice sounded distant to her own ears. "She used to sing songs about fire that weren't lullabies. I thought they were just... old things. Guild things." She looked down at her hands, at the rope-scarred palms that had suddenly become relics of something she'd never agreed to. "She never said a word."

"Maybe saying a word was the dangerous part," Wren offered quietly. "Everything I copied from the Order's archives says the wardens were hunted to extinction. If your grandmother kept the covenant alive in secret, silence wasn't betrayal. It was armor."

"Easy to say when you're not the one wearing it."

"No," Wren admitted. "Not easy. Necessary, maybe."

Corwin said nothing at all. He had settled onto a fallen stone at the ring's edge, one hand pressed to his wound, watching the dragon with an expression Sebastian couldn't read—something between awe and grief, as if he were looking at everything he'd helped destroy and everything he'd never let himself believe survived it.

Sebastian walked to the ring's edge alone.

Below, the valley spread green and steaming, threaded with streams that ran warm and mineral-bright. Waystones dotted the slopes in patterns that, from this height, resolved into something deliberate—a sigil, vast and ancient, written across the land itself. The same sigil, she realized with a jolt, that was etched into the guildhall's oldest foundation stone back home, the one everyone assumed was decorative. The one her grandmother used to touch, every single morning, on her way out to the road.

Every road she had ever walked rose up in her memory at once: the frozen passes, the smugglers' trails, the burned waystations, the long gray years of decline. She had thought she was fleeing something all that time. Running from smallness, from obscurity, from the slow death of the only family she'd known. But the paths hadn't been flight. They had been practice. Twenty years of learning every stone and storm-track of the Thornfells—not so she could survive, but so she could arrive. Here. Now. With the map in her blood and the last dragon breathing behind her.

She hated it. That was the honest truth, the toll the valley had exacted. She hated being chosen, hated the weight settling onto her shoulders like a yoke fitted by generations of dead women, hated that the invisibility she'd built her whole self around had never once actually protected her—it had just delayed this moment, this standing-stone moment, when the world would look at her and see exactly what she was.

And underneath the hatred, quiet as an ember under ash, something else.

Relief.

"You're staring at the sigil," Pip said, appearing at her side. The child's golden scales caught the afternoon light. "Do you hear it yet? The stones. They're louder when you're close."

"I don't hear anything."

"You do." Pip took her hand, small fingers lacing through scarred ones, and pressed their joined hands against the nearest waystone. The hum surged up through Sebastian's arm, through her chest, and this time—this time she couldn't pretend. It wasn't sound. It was recognition. The stone knew her the way an old dog knows footsteps on a lane, and the song it sang was patient and immense and had been waiting, apparently, for her whole life and a hundred years besides.

"There," Pip said softly. "See?"

Sebastian withdrew her hand. Her eyes were burning, and she turned her face away so the child wouldn't see, which was, she realized, exactly the old habit exactly as described. Hiding. Inherited hiding.

She made herself turn back. Made herself let Pip see.

"What happens now?" she asked, and it wasn't really addressed to the child.

*Now,* said Ysolde, from across the ring, in a voice like the settling of continents, *you decide whether you are the last warden—or the first of the new ones. And you should decide quickly, little Montrose.*

The dragon's ember-veined scales flickered, and her head lifted toward the southern sky, nostrils flaring at some scent on the wind that made the ancient eyes narrow to slits.

*Because the smoke I smell on this wind is not mine.*
