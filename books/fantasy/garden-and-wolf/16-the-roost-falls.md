## 16. The Roost Falls

Wren had copied a thousand decrees in her life, and she had learned to read the silences between their lines—the way an order for "relocation of assets" meant burnings, and "pacification of the eastern holdings" meant graves dug before dawn. So when she saw the boy Tam standing at the village well that morning, too clean, too still, watching the road north with the particular patience of someone waiting for something to arrive, her stomach turned over like a page in a book she did not want to finish.

She had said nothing at first. That was the thing she would carry afterward, the thing that would sit on her chest at night like a stone: *she had seen it and said nothing*, because accusing a child felt monstrous, because Sebastian was exhausted from the Weeping Stair, because Corwin's wound had gone bad and needed tending, because there were a hundred small mercies that added up to one large failure.

By the time she worked up the courage to speak—standing in the doorway of the elder Maren's house, where they'd been given bread and a fire and one night's shelter—Tam was gone.

The boy was gone, and the road north was empty, and the wind coming down off the high passes smelled wrong.

Wren stood in that doorway with her satchel of stolen Order documents against her hip and understood, with the terrible clarity of someone who has spent her life reading other people's intentions, exactly what she was looking at.

"He went down the shepherd's track," Maren said behind her. The old woman's voice was flat. She had seen it too. She had perhaps seen it all along. "Toward the Serpent's Gate."

"How long?"

"An hour. Maybe less." Maren's hands were knotted together in her apron. Her knuckles were white as river stones. "My granddaughter played with him. I taught him his letters." She swallowed. "I thought—I thought if we were kind enough—"

Kindness was not a wall. Wren knew that better than anyone. She had been kind to the Order for six years, had copied their decrees in a fair hand and kept her head down and told herself that survival was its own virtue, and the Order had burned eyries full of sleeping dragons anyway.

She found Sebastian at the corral, checking the girth on the one mule they'd bought from the village, moving carefully because Corwin was leaning against the fence with his bandaged arm cradled like something broken off him. Pip sat on the rail above them, legs swinging, and even from across the yard Wren could see the change in the child—the scales along her jaw catching light that wasn't there, the way she kept tilting her head toward the valley as though listening to music no one else could hear.

"The boy," Wren said. No preamble. There wasn't time for preamble anymore; preamble was a luxury of people who still believed tomorrow would resemble today. "Tam. He's gone down the shepherd's track toward the Serpent's Gate. He sold us out."

Sebastian went very still. It was a stillness Wren had learned to read in the weeks since the guildhall—a guide's stillness, the stillness of someone measuring a collapsing slope. "How long ago?"

"An hour. Maybe more."

Sebastian looked north, where the pass called the Serpent's Gate cut through the mountains like a wound, the only practical approach to the hidden valley short of flying. Then she looked at Corwin, whose face had gone gray around the mouth.

"One man can't hold the Serpent's Gate," Corwin said quietly.

"No," Sebastian agreed. "But one man can make them pay for it slowly enough that the roost hears them coming." She turned to him, and whatever passed between them was old and wordless, forged somewhere on the road between the ravine and here. "You don't have to—"

"I know what I have to do." Corwin pushed off the fence. He moved stiffly, favoring his left side, and when he reached for his sword belt his fingers fumbled the buckle once, twice, before closing on it. The wound in his shoulder had been sewn in the village by a woman who kept goats and had delivered half the valley's babies, and it had held for two days, and everyone knew it would not hold for three. "Vane will send veterans first. Men I trained. They'll come up the switchbacks in column because the rock walls won't let them do anything else, and a column is just a queue of people waiting to be fought." He managed something that was almost a smile. "I've queued in enough of them to know how to break one."

"You're wounded," Wren said, and heard her own voice crack on the word, and hated how small it made everything sound. *You're wounded*—as if that were the shape of what was happening, as if a bandage were the problem.

Corwin looked at her then, really looked, and his eyes were calm in a way that frightened her more than panic would have. "Wren. You're the one who reads everything. Tell me what happens if Vane reaches the valley unopposed."

Wren's throat closed. She made herself answer anyway, because he had asked her in the language she spoke best: the language of consequences, laid out plain. "Ysolde is dying. She can barely lift her head. Pip is—" She glanced up at the child on the rail, who was watching them with those too-bright eyes, listening to everything. "Pip is nine years old. And there are eggs in the deep vaults that haven't woken yet. If the Order gets into the roost—" She stopped. Finished it anyway, because he'd asked. "There is no after. Not for any of it."

"Then I hold the gate," Corwin said, "and you run."

* * *

They ran.

Not all of them—Corwin went up alone, refusing company with a finality that closed the subject like a door—and not gently. The path from the village into the hidden valley was the map's own road now, the waystones humming underfoot with a warmth that had once awed Wren and now only terrified her, because every hum was a countdown, every glowing sigil a marker saying *this far, this fast, this is how much time you have left.*

Sebastian ran ahead, because Sebastian had been running these roads since before Wren could write her own name properly, and even exhausted, even grieving, she moved like water finding its way downhill—reading the ground, choosing the line, never hesitating. Wren followed in her wake with her satchel banging against her ribs and her lungs full of fire, and Pip rode the mule because Sebastian had put her there with both hands and said, *you ride, you don't argue, you are carrying something none of the rest of us are.*

Pip, for once, didn't argue. The child sat the mule with her chin tucked and her eyes fixed on the peaks ahead, and every so often her whole body shivered, feathers-and-scales, like a struck bell.

"They're scared," she said at one point, apropos of nothing, her voice thin with altitude and something else. "Down in the deep place. The quiet ones. They can feel it. Like when you're asleep and someone opens the door."

Wren's blood went cold. "Can Ysolde feel it? Does she know they're coming?"

"She's so tired." Pip's voice cracked. "She's using everything she has left just to stay awake, and she told me—she told me if the fire ever came down the mountain, I shouldn't try to fight it. I should take the eggs and go deeper. But I don't know how to move them, they're so heavy, and I'm only—" Her breath hitched. "I'm only little."

"You're not going to have to move anything," Sebastian said, without turning, without slowing. Her voice was iron wrapped in cloth. "Because they're not getting in."

Wren wanted to believe her the way you believe in a story you loved as a child—completely, desperately, knowing all the while that stories were the one thing the Order had taught her not to trust. She had read Vane's early decrees. She had copied the requisitions for wyvern-fire, the manifests, the cold arithmetic of barrels and chains. She knew precisely what was marching up the Serpent's Gate behind a child's borrowed knowledge, and it was not a raiding party. It was a crusade with supply trains.

Behind them, faint as a heartbeat, the first sounds of battle came down off the mountain.

Everyone stopped. Even the mule stopped, ears swiveling. The sound was distant, thinned by miles of rock and wind—steel on steel, a shout, another shout—but Wren had spent six years in the Order's scriptorium listening to reports read aloud, and she knew the grammar of a fight. One voice. One blade. A rhythm too steady for panic.

"He's doing it," Pip whispered. "He's holding them."

"For now," Sebastian said. "Run."

* * *

The valley opened before them like a held breath finally released.

Wren had seen it first through the map's glow, days ago, and had thought then that nothing in her life had prepared her for it. She had been wrong. Nothing had prepared her for seeing it *afraid*. The warm air still rose from the deep vents, still carried the smell of sun-baked stone and something older, mineral and alive, but the ancient waystones along the valley floor were flickering now, their hum stuttering like a lamp in wind. High overhead, the roost clung to the cliff face in tiers of carved galleries and hanging gardens gone wild, and the great doors of the inner vaults stood open, breathing slow pulses of heat.

And in the forecourt, beneath the first tier, Ysolde lay coiled in the last of the afternoon light.

Wren had met dragons the way most people meet gods: once, and insufficiently. Ysolde was vast beyond sense, her body a landscape of ash-gray scale shot through with veins of ember-light that pulsed slower now than they had five days ago. When they came pounding up the valley trail she raised her head, and the movement took visible effort, like a mountain deciding to be a hill, and her eyes—old gold, filmed at the edges—found Sebastian first, then Wren, then settled on Pip with an expression Wren could only call tenderness.

"You felt it," Ysolde said. Her voice was thunder heard through many walls. "Little flame. Yes. They come."

"How many?" Sebastian demanded, chest heaving. "Ysolde, how many, and how long do we have?"

"A thousand feet below your friend's blade. Perhaps four hundred souls. Siege engines disassembled for the climb, being reassembled even now." The dragon's gaze drifted upward, toward the peaks, and something moved through the ember-veins of her throat that might have been a laugh, if laughter could be made of grief. "Your knight holds the Serpent's Gate still. He fights like a man settling accounts."

Wren's eyes stung. She blinked it away. Crying was for people with time.

"We have the documents," she said, and pulled the satchel around, and her hands were shaking as she unbuckled it—not from fear, or not only fear, but from the fury of a lifetime spent making copies while the originals did their work. "Vane's requisitions. The wyvern-fire manifests. The staged attack on Harrowmere—it's all here, sealed orders, her own cipher, I copied half of these myself before I ran. If the clans see them—if anyone sees them—"

"Then we show them," Sebastian said. "That was always the plan. Justice before the clans, in the open, before her army gets here." She looked up at the flickering waystones, at the stuttering light on the cliffs, and Wren watched the plan die in her face in real time. "But the clans are three days out. Vane is hours. She changed the timetable on us."

"Then we keep the roost until they arrive," Wren said. "We hold. Like Corwin."

"Wren." Sebastian turned to her fully, and there was something in her expression Wren had never seen before—something that looked, absurdly, like apology. "There are four hundred of them and four of us, and one of those four is dying and one is nine."

"Five," said Pip.

They all looked up. The child had slid off the mule and was standing very straight, and the scales along her arms and throat had begun, softly, impossibly, to glow—the same ember-gold as the dragon's veins, as the waystones, as the map that had started everything. Her shadow on the stones had wings it should not have had.

"The eggs woke up," Pip said. "When the army crossed the threshold stones, they woke up. All of them. And they're singing, and I'm the only one who knows the song, because Ysolde taught me, and—" Her voice wavered, steadied. Nine years old, and steadying. "You said I was carrying something. This is it. I'm not going deeper. I'm going to stand in front of the doors, and I'm going to sing, and the roost is going to help us."

"Pip, no—" Wren started forward.

Ysolde's tail moved—not fast, but inexorable, a wall of ash-gray scale settling between Wren and the child with the gentleness of a door being closed by someone who loves you. The dragon's great head lowered until one ancient gold eye was level with Wren's face, close enough that Wren could feel the furnace-heat of her breath, could see her own reflection trembling in it.

"Scribe," Ysolde said. "You of all of them should understand. Some things are written that cannot be unwritten. She was hatched for this hour. I have known it since the day I found her in the snow, and I have grieved it every day since, and I would not trade it away if I could, because it is *hers*." The eye blinked, slow as a season. "Go to the archive vaults. Everything the Wayfarers ever recorded of the covenant is there—every oath, every name, every proof. If justice is to be spoken before the clans, it must be spoken from evidence, not from hope. That is your road now. Run it well."

For one moment Wren stood frozen, caught between the child and the calling, between the instinct to grab Pip and run and the terrible understanding that Ysolde was right—that Wren's whole life had been training for exactly this, the finding and carrying and speaking of true words at the moment they mattered most.

Then the mountain shook.

It was not thunder. Thunder does not come with a smell, and this came with a smell Wren knew from the Order's manifests, from the inventory lists she had copied with her neat, complicit hand: *wyvern-fire, rendered, forty barrels.* The northern rim of the valley lit orange. A section of the high trail simply ceased to exist, rock and waystone and ancient carvings lofted skyward in a fist of black-red flame, and the shockwave rolled down the valley and set every flickering waystone blazing at once.

Through the ringing in her ears, Wren heard the sound she had been dreading since the village: the rhythm of battle from the Serpent's Gate, steady for so long, faltering.

Faltering, and then stopping.

Somewhere above them, in the smoke and falling rock, Corwin Amberdale's account came due—or did not; she couldn't know, couldn't let herself know, not yet, not with words to carry—and the gate was open, and the Order was coming down into the valley with fire at their backs.

"GO," Sebastian roared, already shoving Wren toward the archive vaults, already drawing the old guild knife from her belt, already turning to face the burning north with the stance of a woman planting herself like a waystone. "Wren—the records—RUN!"

Wren ran.

Behind her, small and clear and rising over the crackle of flame, a child began to sing.
