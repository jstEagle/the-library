## 17. Fire and Judgment

The valley smelled of smoke and old stone, and beneath both, faintly, of something sweeter—warm resin, sun-baked pine, the breath of a creature older than the kingdoms that wanted her dead.

Sebastian Montrose stood at the mouth of the Ember Roost with ash on her boots and blood drying at her temple, and she watched the Order of the Ashen Blade come down the mountain like a gray tide.

They filled the pass in ranks, three hundred strong or more, their white-and-ash banners snapping in the high wind. Behind them came the camp followers, the supply wagons, the smiths and the surgeons—the apparatus of a war that had been built in secret and unveiled this morning on the doorstep of the world's last dragon. And behind *them*, riding up the switchbacks at a pace that made even hardened soldiers look nervous, came the mountain clans: the Redwater, the Stonekin, the Hollowmere folk, hundreds of armed men and women who had answered Vane's summons because she had promised them proof of a dragon menace at their borders.

*Proof.* Sebastian touched the leather satchel at her hip where Wren's stolen documents lay wrapped in oilcloth. Proof was exactly what they would get. Just not the kind Vane intended.

"They're forming up," Corwin said beside her. His voice was rough. The wound in his side had been bound twice since dawn and had soaked through both bandages, and he stood anyway, sword drawn, as if standing were simply what his body did now regardless of what it cost. "Left flank's climbing the scree. They mean to take the high ground before they parley."

"Vane doesn't want to parley." Sebastian's eyes tracked the gray ranks below. "She wants witnesses. That's why she brought the clans. She wants them to watch her burn a monster and call it justice."

"Then we give them a different thing to watch."

Sebastian almost smiled. Almost. Below them, at the roost's threshold, Ysolde lay with her head raised just enough to see over the valley rim, her scales gone dull as old coals, her breathing slow enough that between one exhale and the next you could count your own heartbeat twice. She had not flown in a century. She might never fly again. But her eyes—her eyes were still furnace-gold, and when they turned to Sebastian there was no plea in them at all. Only a question, patient as stone:

*What are you?*

The same question the map had asked her in the dark of the guildhall. The same question every road since had been asking, in one form or another.

Sebastian had spent thirty years making sure no one ever asked it.

"I'm going down there," she said.

Corwin turned. "Alone?"

"With Wren. With the truth." She looked at him—at the knight who had executed innocents for a lie and had chosen, bleeding, to stand against it—and found that her voice did not shake. "You hold the pass with Pip. If Vane sends soldiers up the scree while I'm talking, buy me the time to finish."

"And if she sends them while you're *still* talking?"

"Then I'll talk faster."

Pip was waiting at the waystone, small hands pressed flat against its humming surface, scales along her jaw catching the light like flecks of gold leaf. When Sebastian knelt to her level, the child's amber eyes were wide and wet.

"You're scared," Pip said. Half-dragon children, Wren had learned, could smell fear the way other children smelled bread baking.

"Yes."

"But you're going anyway."

"Yes."

Pip considered this with the terrible seriousness of the very young, then reached out and pressed one small scaled hand against Sebastian's chest, right over the heart. Warmth bloomed there—not fire, nothing so dramatic, just heat, like a stone kept by a hearth—and Sebastian felt something settle in her, some final loose piece sliding into place.

"Warden," Pip said. It wasn't a title. It was a naming.

* * *

The walk down into the valley took eleven minutes. Sebastian counted them, because counting was what guides did when the road turned bad—you counted your steps, your breaths, the distance to the next safe ground—and these were the worst steps of her life.

Wren walked beside her, pale, clutching the satchel like a shield. The scribe had argued for ten full minutes back at the waystone—*they'll kill us, they'll kill us before we say six words, this is madness*—and then had stopped arguing, squared her shoulders, and said only, "If I'm going to die, I'd rather die having read everything I ever stole." Which was, Sebastian suspected, the most honest prayer Wren had ever offered.

The clans had gathered in a crescent at the valley floor, held back from the Order's lines by a thin strip of trampled grass and by the sheer authority of the woman on the gray horse at the center of it all.

Inquisitor Vane sat her mount like a blade set upright in a scabbard. Her armor was unadorned white, her cloak the color of cold ash, and her face—Sebastian had imagined it gaunt, fanatical, cruel, but it was none of those things. It was calm. Kind, even, in the way of someone who has already forgiven you for what she is about to do to you.

"Sebastian Montrose." Vane's voice carried across the valley without effort, pitched for the crowd. "Last of the Wayfarers. Smuggler. Thief of Crown secrets. Harborer of an abomination." She let each word fall like a stone into still water. "I offer you mercy. Kneel, surrender the child and the creature, and walk away. This is the last kindness I will extend."

Every eye in the valley fixed on Sebastian. Three hundred soldiers. Five hundred clansfolk. One dying dragon. One half-dragon child hidden somewhere above, whose whole future hung on the next sixty seconds.

Her whole life, Sebastian had been good at being unseen. She could cross a border at night, slip past a toll, vanish into a crowd. Invisibility had been her craft, her comfort, her armor. And now—

Now she stepped forward, alone, into the widest-open space she had ever stood in, and made herself visible.

"I'm not kneeling," she said, and her voice, trained by years of shouting over wind in high passes, carried farther than Vane's. "But I'll answer your charges. All of them. Because unlike you, Inquisitor, I brought evidence."

Murmurs moved through the clan crescent like wind through wheat. Vane's calm flickered—one degree, no more—but Sebastian had spent her life reading weather, and she saw the storm front shift.

"Evidence," Vane repeated. "From a smuggler."

"From a warden." Sebastian pulled off her glove and held up her bare hand so the clans could see. "Do any of you know what the Wayfarers' Guild was, before it fell? Not what the Crown told you. What it *was*." She turned slowly, addressing the crescent, the same way she'd address a frightened caravan before a storm crossing. "Three hundred years ago, your ancestors signed a covenant with the dragons of Thornfell. The waystones on your passes—the ones that keep your roads open through winter, the ones your grandmothers still leave offerings at—they aren't Crown works. They're dragon-fire, banked in stone. And the guild my grandmother served existed for one purpose: to tend that fire, and to guard the creatures who gave it. We weren't smugglers. We were the ones who stood between the mountains and the dark. Until the Order of the Ashen Blade murdered the last covenant and called it holy work."

"Lies," Vane said, but she said it too quickly.

"Is it?" Sebastian reached into the satchel. "Then you won't mind explaining *this*."

She drew out the first document and held it high—a page bearing the Order's own seal, the ash-blade sigil stamped deep in black wax. "A requisition order, dated six weeks ago. Signed by your own quartermaster. For forty measures of wyvern-fire, drawn from the Order's vaults at Karst Hold. Wyvern-fire, Inquisitor. The substance that burns green and leaves no ash. Now—" she turned to the clans again— "who among you has seen the burn-marks at Harrow Ford? Who walked the ruins of Millbrook after the 'dragon attack' three weeks past?" Hands rose in the crescent. Voices stirred. "Green flame. No ash. Dragons don't burn that way. Wyvern-fire does. Someone bought forty measures of it and fed your town to it."

"That document is forged!"

"It bears your seal, your cipher, and your quartermaster's hand—I have three scribes here who can swear to the handwriting, and one of them copied decrees in your own chancery for four years." Wren stepped forward, chin up, terrified and immovable. "I know that hand. I traced it a hundred times. It's Master Ellery Vosk's, and everyone in the Karst chancery knows it."

Vane's horse shifted beneath her. Down the line, Sebastian saw a clan chieftain—the Redwater woman, broad as a door, arms crossed—lean over to whisper to the Stonekin elder beside her. Whispering is how armies start to die, Sebastian thought. Not with swords. With whispers.

"There is more," Sebastian said. She drew the second page. "A field report, marked *sealed*, describing the 'dragon attack' on Millbrook in detail—written *two days before* Millbrook burned." She let that land. Let the silence stretch until it creaked. "You cannot report a disaster before it happens, Inquisitor. Unless you wrote the disaster first."

The crescent broke into sound—not roaring yet, not violence, but the low grinding murmur of five hundred people rethinking everything at once. A Stonekin man spat. Somewhere a child asked her mother what "before" meant, and the mother didn't answer.

Vane dismounted.

That was the moment Sebastian understood how dangerous the woman truly was. A lesser enemy would have raged, denied, ordered arrests. Vane did none of it. She walked forward through her own lines, unhurried, unarmed, and stopped ten feet from Sebastian, and when she spoke it was quietly, for Sebastian alone—or so she thought, but the wind carried it, and the front rank of clansfolk heard every word.

"You think this changes anything," Vane said. "You've shown them paper. Paper is nothing. I have seen what fire does to a village, Montrose. I stood in the ashes at Cael Sorrow when I was nine years old, after the last great drake burned it, and I pulled my sister out from under a beam, and what was left of her fit in my two hands." Her voice never rose. Her eyes never left Sebastian's. "Everything I have done—all of it, the forged reports, the bought fires, the dead—I did it so that no other child ever holds what I held. You call it murder. I call it the only mercy arithmetic allows. One dragon dies tonight, or ten thousand people die over a century. Choose."

And there it was. The terrible, honest heart of her. Sebastian had expected a fanatic; she found instead a wound that had learned to command armies.

For one long breath, Sebastian almost pitied her.

Almost.

"You're right," Sebastian said, and heard the clans go quiet, heard even Corwin above on the scree go still. "One death is cheaper than ten thousand. Any guide knows that arithmetic—we do it on every hard crossing. Leave the slow one behind. Save the many." She stepped closer. "But you've got the sum wrong, Inquisitor. Because the dragon you're here to kill isn't the one that burned Cael Sorrow. Ysolde hasn't left this valley in a hundred years. She was *here*, guarding the passes, keeping your roads warm, the night your village burned. The thing that burned Cael Sorrow was a rogue drake—one the covenant wardens were sworn to hunt, and would have hunted, if your Order hadn't slaughtered the wardens first. You didn't avenge your sister tonight. You're finishing her murderers' work."

Vane went very still.

"You want arithmetic?" Sebastian lifted the third document—the oldest, the one Wren had wept over translating. "Here's a ledger from your own archive. Karst Hold, ninety years ago. Payments made to a mercenary company to drive a rogue drake *toward* populated lands—because a dragon threat, manufactured, justified the Order's charter expansion. Your Order has been building its own monsters for a century, Inquisitor. Your sister died for a budget line."

The silence that followed was absolute. Even the wind seemed to hold its breath.

Vane's hand drifted toward her sword—not fast, not angry, just inevitable, like a door closing—and Sebastian saw the exact moment the woman decided that words had failed and only fire remained.

"The clans will believe what I tell them to believe," Vane said softly. "They always have."

"No," said a new voice—deep, female, worn smooth as river stone. The Redwater chieftain stepped out of the crescent, flanked by the Stonekin elder, and she was looking not at Sebastian but at the ridge above the roost, where Ysolde had raised her head higher than anyone had seen it rise in living memory. "We'll believe what the mountain tells us. And the mountain says the waystones have sung for three days straight." She turned to Vane, and her hand rested on her axe with terrible gentleness. "Inquisitor, you summoned us under a false pretense. The clans remember debts. Both kinds."

Above, on the scree, a horn sounded—the Order's left flank, moving early, disobeying or obeying, no one would ever know which—and the whole fragile architecture of the morning tilted toward ruin.

Vane drew her sword.

Sebastian did not step back. Thirty years of roads, every hard crossing, every lonely mile, every nameless night—it had all been practice, all of it, for this single square yard of grass where she now stood between a drawn blade and a dying dragon, and she found that she was not afraid anymore. She was *seen*. By the clans. By the mountain. By herself.

"Inquisitor Vane," she said, loud enough for the whole valley, "in the name of the warden covenant, older than your Order and older than your grief, I name your war false, and I forbid it."

Vane smiled. It was the saddest thing Sebastian had ever seen.

"Covenants," she said, leveling the blade, "are made to be broken."

And she came at Sebastian with fire kindling in her free hand—wyvern-green, wrong, hungry—and somewhere above them a child's voice cried out in a language no human tongue had spoken in a hundred years, and the sky began to change.
