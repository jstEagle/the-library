## 18. Vane's Last Argument

Inquisitor Seraphine Vane had never once, in forty years of service to the Order of the Ashen Blade, doubted the rightness of her cause. Doubt was a disease. She had watched it rot men from the inside—watched it soften a brother-in-arms until he wept over a burning village, watched it hollow out a commander until she could no longer sign the writs that kept the realm safe. Vane had burned her own doubt at nineteen, in a brazier lit with the hair of the first wyrm-cultist she had ever condemned, and she had kept the ash in a locket against her heart ever since.

She stood now on the frozen shelf of rock above the hidden valley, and for the first time since that brazier, the ash felt cold.

Below her, the mountain clans had come. Not as allies—she could see that already, even from this height, even through the smoke of her own staged war. They had come armed and grim, drawn by her forged terror, by the wyvern-fire she had loosed on Harrowgate's border town, by the lie she had spent three months and eleven lives constructing. But they were not looking at the valley with fear anymore. They were looking at *her*.

The girl—Montrose, the last Wayfarer, the courier-thief who had stolen what should have been delivered to the Order's pyres—stood between the clans and the great dragon, and she was speaking. Vane could not hear the words at this distance, but she did not need to. She knew the shape of them. She had heard that shape before, in courtrooms and confessionals: the voice of someone laying truth down like a blade across a table.

Behind the girl, a scribe held up documents. Vane recognized them even at fifty paces. Her own seal. Her own hand. The requisitions for the wyvern-fire, the orders for the false attack, the correspondence naming the boy who had sold the valley's location for a copper promise and a warm meal. Wren. The little runaway who had copied decrees in the Order's scriptorium for six years before she grew a conscience. Of course it was her. It was always the quiet ones.

"Archivist," Vane said, without turning.

Her remaining retainer stepped up beside her. Brother Halvard, gray-bearded, faithful, one of only four who had survived the pass where Corwin Ashvale—the traitor, the disgrace, the man she had once called friend—had held the narrows like a man possessed by every sin he was trying to outrun.

"Yes, Inquisitor."

"How many of the clans below can read my seal?"

Halvard was silent a moment. "The hillfolk keep their own law. But the lowland delegates—the ones from Harrowgate itself—" He hesitated. "They will know it, my lady."

"They will know it," Vane agreed softly. Below, a murmur was moving through the assembled clans like wind through wheat. She watched a broad-shouldered chieftain turn the stolen papers over in his hands, watched him look from the seal to the smoke still rising from his own distant town, and she saw the exact moment the arithmetic finished in his eyes.

Vane had built her life on arithmetic. Every fire balanced against every future. Every innocent condemned weighed against every generation saved. That was the argument she had made to herself in ten thousand dark hours: *I am the wall between the world and the flame.* Dragons were not creatures; they were catastrophes wearing scales. She had seen the old records—she had *burned* the old records—and she knew what the wyrms of the first age had done when their hunger woke. Kingdoms ended in an afternoon. The sky itself had been a wound.

That the last dragon below her was ancient, guttering, dying—that meant nothing. Fire did not stop being fire because it flickered. A dying dragon was merely a catastrophe with less time left, and catastrophes, in Vane's experience, were most dangerous precisely when cornered.

But the arithmetic had failed her today. That was the truth she would not let herself name. The clans were not afraid. They were *convinced*—and not by her.

"Inquisitor," Halvard said carefully, "perhaps we should withdraw. Regroup at Thornhold. The Order's authority—"

"The Order's authority is in that scribe's hands," Vane said. "Along with my seal and my signature." She looked down at the valley, at the waystones humming their soft amber light along its rim, at the enormous scarred shape of the dragon coiled around something deep in the roost's heart, wings folded like a cathedral closing. "No. We do not withdraw. Withdrawal is how lies win."

"And what does my lady intend?"

Vane drew the long knife from her belt. Not the ceremonial ashen blade she carried for judgments—that was for crowds, for theater, for the clean grammar of law. This was the other knife, the one no writ authorized, the one she had carried since Harrowgate. Its edge was quenched in consecrated salt and cold iron, and it had been forged for exactly one purpose: to open a dragon's throat.

"I intend," she said, "to finish the argument myself."

* * *

She went down alone. She ordered Halvard and the others to hold the rim—not because she feared they would interfere, but because she feared they would follow. What came next was hers. It had always been hers. Forty years ago, as a novice, she had stood in the ashes of the outer eyries on the Night of Cinders and sworn that no dragon would ever again darken a living sky while she drew breath. The Order had grown soft since then. Politicians in armor. Men who talked of containment instead of extinction. But Vane remembered the oath in its original language, the one written in cinders, and she intended to keep it.

The clans parted before her. She felt their eyes, hundreds of them, and she did not lower her gaze from a single one. Let them look. Let them see what conviction looked like when it stopped apologizing.

The girl Montrose turned as Vane approached, planting herself squarely in the path, and Vane gave her the courtesy of stopping—one sword-length away, close enough to speak without shouting, far enough that neither could pretend this was anything but what it was.

"You should have stayed invisible," Montrose said. There was blood on her sleeve—someone else's, Vane guessed—and soot on her face, and she stood like a woman who had finally learned her own weight. "You could have run. You still could."

"Run where?" Vane asked. "There is no road left that leads away from this." She lifted her voice so the clans could hear. "Hear me, all of you! I have seen what dragons do. I have walked through the ashes of towns whose names you've forgotten because there was no one left to remember them. Everything I have done—the fire at Harrowgate, the deaths, the war I tried to build—I did it so that none of you would ever have to bury your children under a burned roof. Judge me if you must. But judge me knowing that I am the only one here who has never lied about what I am willing to do."

"She didn't lie about it either," said the knight, Corwin, limping forward with his bandaged side and his ruined honor. "She just never told the truth. There's a difference, Seraphine. You taught me that yourself, the day you had me execute those farmers at Dellhollow. You said: *the truth is whatever keeps the most people alive.* Well. Look around you. Count them. Then tell me which of us kept more alive today."

Vane looked at him—at the man she had broken and remade and lost—and felt nothing but a vast, tired pity. "You were the best of us once, Corwin. That is why your fall grieves me more than all the rest."

"Then fall with some grace," he said.

"No," Vane said quietly. "Falling is for people who believe they were standing on something."

And she moved past him. Past the girl, past the scribe clutching her stolen papers, past the half-dragon child whose eyes had gone wide and gold and terrible—past all of them, straight toward the dragon.

They did not stop her. Later, many of them would say they should have. But there are moments when conviction moves like weather, and no hand raised against it seems worth the raising.

Ysolde lay coiled in the heart of the roost, and up close she was vaster than Vane had imagined and smaller too—vast in the way mountains are vast, small in the way that a guttering candle is small. Her scales had gone the color of old ash shot through with fading embers, and her breath came slow and deep, each exhalation stirring the warm air of the valley like a bellows too tired to work. One eye opened as Vane approached. Amber. Ancient. Unafraid.

"So," the dragon said, and her voice was the sound a hearth makes when it settles. "The last of the ash-priests comes to finish her scripture."

"I am no priest," Vane said, raising the knife. "Priests serve gods. I serve the living."

"As do I," Ysolde said. "We differ only in our arithmetic."

"Then let us settle the sum."

Vane lunged. She was sixty years old and she moved like a woman of thirty, because hatred had kept her body the way duty kept her mind—hardened, honed, unforgiving. The salt-iron knife went for the throat, for the soft seam beneath the jaw where the old texts swore the scale gave way, and for one suspended heartbeat the whole valley seemed to hold its breath.

The heartbeat ended.

Between the knife and the dragon, the child appeared.

Not walked—*appeared*, the way dawn appears, sudden and total. Pip stood with her arms spread wide, her small body planted before Ysolde's throat, and the change that had been working in her since the valley gates had completed itself in that instant. Scales ran up her arms like spilled mercury catching firelight, gold-shot and living, and her eyes were twin furnaces. When she spoke, her voice had harmonics in it, two voices braided into one.

"You taught everyone here what hate looks like," Pip said. "So we'll show you what it feeds."

The knife stopped a finger's width from her chest. Vane had killed children before—she told herself she hadn't, she had signed the writs and let others do the lifting, but she knew the ledger's true columns—and she found, in that frozen moment, that her arm would not complete the stroke. Not from mercy. From something worse. From recognition.

Because the child was not afraid of her. And everything Vane had ever done, every fire she had fed, had been built on the fuel of fear—others' fear, and the fear she carried banked in her own chest like a coal that never cooled. This child looked at the knife and the sixty years and the forty-year oath, and saw none of it as power. Saw it, Vane realized with a clarity like falling, as *sadness*.

"You're dying already," Pip said gently. "I can hear it. Your fire's been eating you for years. All that hate—it doesn't keep people warm, lady. It just burns the house down around them."

"Silence," Vane whispered.

"It's hungry," the child said. "It wants more. Give it me, then. Give it the dragon. Give it the whole valley. And when it's eaten everything—" her golden eyes held Vane's, merciless with compassion— "what's left for it to eat?"

"*Silence!*"

Vane struck. Not at the child—her arm refused that—but downward, at the ground, at the covenant stone beside her, driving the salt-iron knife into the humming amber rock to break the valley's old warding, to give her retreating faith one last act of vandalism. And the stone answered.

The knife had been quenched in consecrated salt and cold iron, yes—but it had also spent forty years in the presence of a woman whose hatred had never once been allowed to cool, and things soaked that long in such fires take on their nature. The covenant stone drank the blow and returned it. Light erupted up the blade, up Vane's arm, white-gold and absolute, and in that light Vane saw—as the dying see, all at once and truly—every fire she had ever lit. Harrowgate burning with its staged terror. The eyries of the Night of Cinders. The brazier at nineteen, and the locket of ash against her heart, and the doubt she had burned before it could teach her anything.

The locket. She felt it then, against her breastbone, going hot—her own kept ash, her own hoarded hate, forty years of it, igniting at last in the presence of true fire. It did not burn her the way a pyre burns. It burned her the way her own sermons had always promised the wicked would burn: from the inside out, thoroughly, and with perfect justice.

She heard Halvard shouting her name from the rim. She heard the clans murmuring—not in triumph, she noted distantly, but in something like grief, which was somehow worse. She heard the dragon sigh, long and sorrowful, a sound like a hearth going cold after a lifetime of warmth.

And she heard the child again, closest of all, kneeling beside her as she fell, saying words Vane could not have imagined hearing at the end: "I'm sorry. I'm sorry it ate you too."

Seraphine Vane had prepared for death her entire life. She had imagined the scaffold, the pyre, the assassin's blade, the dignified collapse at her desk with an unfinished writ beneath her hand. She had composed her final words a hundred times, and they had always been the same: *I was right.*

What came out instead, as the light took her, as her own consumed fire rose off her shoulders in a column of gray and gold and scattered on the valley wind like the ash it had always been, was quieter, and truer, and arrived too late to save anyone:

"Oh. So *that's* what it was for."

The locket split. The ash inside it—her doubt, her youth, the last unburned piece of the girl she had been before the brazier—rose free on the heat and drifted, briefly bright, over the heads of the clans, over the waystones, over the wounded knight and the weeping scribe and the girl who had chosen to be seen, before settling at last, fine and light as forgiveness, on the warm stones of the Ember Roost.

The knife clattered to the rock. The column of fire went out. And in the silence that followed, deeper than any silence the valley had ever known, the ancient dragon lowered her great head and breathed one small, careful flame onto the place where the inquisitor had fallen—a funeral rite older than any human kingdom, a warding for the dead, an ending.

When the flame faded, Sebastian Montrose was already moving toward the covenant stone where the knife still stood quivering, and the dragon's amber eye turned to follow her, and Ysolde spoke words that only the girl could hear, words that would send her walking into the deep places of the roost before another day was out:

"Now, warden. Come and see what I have guarded all these lonely years. Come and see what hope looks like when it sleeps."
