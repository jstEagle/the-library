## 19. What the Fire Costs

The wound in Corwin's side had stopped bleeding sometime during the night, which should have been a mercy and was instead a quiet terror. Blood that would not flow was blood deciding where to go instead—into the flesh, into the fever, into the slow drowning of a man from the inside. He had seen it before, on campaign, on men tougher than he was. They always said they felt fine. They always said it right up until they didn't.

He sat propped against the eastern wall of the Ember Roost's great hollow, where the mountain opened like a cupped hand around warmth and old stone, and he watched Ysolde die.

There was no other honest word for it. The dragon lay along the valley floor of the roost like a felled hill, her scales gone from ember-orange to the grey of banked coals, her breath so shallow that the warm air of the cavern barely stirred with it. Her eyes were open. They had been open for hours. Ancient gold, filmed now at the edges, fixed on the covenant stones that ringed the hollow—a circle of standing monoliths, each carved with the sigil Sebastian carried on her map, each dark and cold as any grave marker.

They had been cold for two hundred years. By morning, if Ysolde had her way, they would not be.

"You should be lying down," Wren said, settling beside him with an armful of bandages she had clearly stolen from somewhere and repurposed badly. She was twenty years old and had the bedside manner of a scolding schoolmistress. "You should be lying down, and you should be drinking this, and you should stop looking at the dragon like that."

"Like what?"

"Like you're memorizing her." Wren pressed a clay cup into his hands. It smelled of willow bark and honey and something sharper underneath. "In case she doesn't last the day."

Corwin looked into the cup. His hands were steady, which surprised him. He had thought everything in him would shake, after. After the pass. After Ser Aldric's sword finally slipping from his grip and the man himself going down into the snow with Corwin's blade through his chest and a look on his face that was not hatred but recognition—the look of one brother acknowledging another across an unbridgeable distance.

"She won't," Corwin said. "Last the day. You can see it. She's spending herself."

"Then don't watch her do it."

"Yes I will." He drank. The draught was bitter and warm and did nothing at all for the fire in his side. "I spent eleven years burning things for the Order of the Ashen Blade, Wren. Villages. Granaries. A library once—I still don't know what books were in it. I can spend one morning watching the opposite of that."

Wren was quiet a moment. Then, because she was Wren, she said: "You're not going to die of this wound. I've decided. I've read four treatises on field surgery and none of them say you get to die today. So don't."

"Aye," Corwin said, and found, absurdly, that he meant to try.

* * *

Sebastian came to him an hour past dawn, moving through the hollow with the particular gait she used when she was trying not to disturb anyone and fooling no one. She had not slept either. None of them had. Pip had not slept at all—the child had spent the whole night at Ysolde's flank with one small scaled hand pressed flat against the dragon's hide, and when Corwin looked over now, he saw the scales on the back of that hand had brightened overnight, from dull bronze to a living copper that caught the light of the dying fire below the mountain.

"How is she?" Sebastian asked, crouching by him. Up close he could see what the victory had cost written all over her—the raw voice, the shadowed eyes, the way she held her left arm close to her body where Vane's soldiers had worked her over before the clans came.

"Dying," Corwin said. "But on her own terms. There are worse ways."

"There are better ones."

"Not many." He shifted, winced, kept talking before she could fuss. "She told me something. Last night, while you were with the clan mothers. I asked her why she'd waited—two hundred years alone up here, guarding eggs nobody knew about, letting the world forget dragons entirely. Why not come down, why not fight, why not make them remember her the hard way."

Sebastian went very still. "What did she say?"

"She said—" He had to stop, because the words still sat strangely in his chest, too large for it. "She said that fire is only ever borrowed. That nothing that burns is its own flame; it takes from what fed it, wood or coal or the hearts of the ones who tend it. And that the Order burned the world because they believed fire belonged to whoever was strongest. And she would rather gutter out alone than prove them right."

Sebastian looked away, toward the dragon, toward the child at her flank. When she spoke again her voice was rough. "And you? What do you believe, Sir Corwin?"

"I believe I'm not a sir anymore, and haven't been since the day they stripped me." He smiled without much humor in it. "And I believe she's right, and it's the most damnable thing I've ever heard, because if fire is borrowed then everything I burned was taken from someone. Not destroyed. Taken. Carried now in everyone who remembers it." He looked down at his hands. They were a swordsman's hands, scarred and steady, and he hated them and could not imagine being without them. "I keep thinking about the library. Whatever books those were. Somebody carries them now. In their heads, maybe, half-remembered. Or in the shape of the hole they left."

"Corwin." Sebastian's hand closed over his wrist—not gentle, exactly. Firm. The grip of someone who guided people for a living and knew that comfort was sometimes just another kind of direction. "You held a pass against forty men with a hole in your side. You bought us the hour that let Wren reach the clans with the documents. Whatever debt you think you're carrying—you paid more than the principal yesterday."

"The interest," Corwin said, "is the rest of my life. Which is fine. I'd signed it over already."

Before she could answer, a sound moved through the hollow—a low, vast exhalation, like wind through a canyon, and every head in the roost turned. Ysolde was rising.

It took her a long time. That was the worst part, watching it—an ancient creature hauling herself up joint by joint, each movement costing something visible, the grey scales shifting like storm clouds. Pip scrambled back to give her room, and Corwin saw the child's face and understood that Pip knew too. Children always knew. They hadn't yet learned the polite lies that let adults pretend otherwise.

Ysolde turned her great head toward the circle of covenant stones, and her voice, when it came, filled the hollow the way water fills a bowl.

*Little wardens.* The words arrived not as sound so much as pressure, meaning pressed directly into the mind. *Come. All of you. This is the last labor, and it is not mine alone.*

* * *

They gathered at the circle as the sun cleared the eastern rim of the valley and struck the monoliths full-on. Up close Corwin could see the carvings properly for the first time: the covenant sigil repeated on every stone, but beneath it, worn nearly smooth by centuries of weather, lines of script in a language older than any kingdom—older, Wren breathed, than any alphabet still in use, though she recognized three characters from fragments in the Order's oldest archives, the ones she had been forbidden to read and had read anyway.

The elders of the mountain clans stood in a wide ring beyond the stones. They had come at dawn, forty of them, wrapped in wool and fur, faces seamed and unreadable. These were the people Vane had needed as allies, the people whose grandmothers remembered the Night of Cinders, the people who had watched Sebastian stand between an army and a dying dragon and speak justice aloud with no law behind her but her own spine. They had sworn nothing yet. They were here to see.

Ysolde lowered herself before the first stone. Her breath came ragged now, and with each exhale a faint glow pulsed deep in her throat, like light through closing doors.

*Two hundred years,* she said, *I have kept the last of my fire hoarded. A dragon's flame is not mere heat, little wardens. It is memory. It is oath made substance. When the covenant was broken—when the wardens betrayed us and the Order burned the eyries—I quenched the stones myself, so that the false order could never use them. And I have regretted it every year since, because quenching is not the same as ending, and a thing quenched may yet be woken.*

Her head swung slowly, taking them in. Sebastian. Wren. Corwin, upright through sheer refusal to be otherwise. Pip, standing closest of all, copper-bright, trembling.

*I have fire enough left for one work. One. I can carry myself to my sleep, or I can wake these stones, and the covenant with them—but not both. Understand what I choose. Understand that I choose it gladly.*

"No," Pip said. It came out cracked, childish, furious. "No, there has to be—it doesn't have to be—"

*Little flame.* Ysolde's voice gentled into something that made Corwin's throat close. *You are the answer to a prayer I did not dare finish praying. Do not begrudge me the last line of it.*

She turned back to the stones. And then, impossibly, she looked at Corwin—at him specifically, out of all of them—and he felt her attention settle on him like a physical weight, like snow.

*You. The one who burned and regrets. Come here.*

Every instinct he owned told him to refuse. He walked forward anyway, one step, two, the pain in his side flaring white with each, until he stood within the reach of her breath—which was warm, warmer than it had any right to be, warm as a hearth in a house he half-remembered from childhood.

*Why do you weep, knight?*

"I'm not—weeping, I'm not—" But he was; he could feel it running hot down his face, and he was too tired and too hurt to be ashamed of it. "Because I know what I am. I know what I did. And you're going to ask me to help you, and I don't understand why anything would trust me again."

*Because,* Ysolde said, *fire is only ever borrowed—and repayment must begin somewhere. Let it begin with the hands that took the most.*

She opened her jaws.

What came out was not the roaring torrent of the battle stories, not the annihilating flood that had met Vane's army at the valley mouth. It was a single thread of flame, thin as a ribbon, white-hot at the core, and it moved with deliberate slowness from stone to stone as Ysolde walked the circle on legs that shook beneath her. Where the flame touched, the sigils woke—first sullen red, then orange, then a deep steady gold, and Corwin felt the change in the air itself, a hum rising through the soles of his boots, through his bones, through the fever in his blood.

At the fourth stone Ysolde faltered. Her foreleg buckled. Before Corwin had decided to move, he had moved—was under her neck, shoulder braced against that impossible weight, holding her up the way you hold up a comrade on a long march, without thinking, without question, because that was what arms were for.

*Good,* she rumbled, and the word vibrated through his whole body. *Hold. Now the rest.*

Stone by stone. Seven, eight, nine. By the eleventh Corwin's vision had gone grey at the edges and his braced arm screamed and he held anyway. At the twelfth and final stone—the one bearing the sigil that matched the map, that matched the mark now faintly visible on Sebastian's palm where the scale had burned her—Ysolde paused, gathered whatever remained, and gave it.

The flame leapt. The ring ignited—not with destruction but with illumination, twelve pillars of quiet golden light, and the hum became a chord, and the chord became, unmistakably, a song. Old. Wordless. The sound, Corwin thought, that a road makes when it has been waiting a long time for travelers.

Around the circle, the clan elders had begun, one by one, to kneel.

* * *

The swearing took the rest of the morning.

It was not elaborate. That was what struck Corwin most, watching from the wall where Wren had finally, physically forced him to sit. No oaths on relics, no blood, no grand liturgy. Each elder simply walked the circle, laid a hand on a waking stone, and spoke their name and their line and the words that the stones themselves seemed to supply—Corwin saw lips moving in unison across the ring, heard fragments drift to him on the warm air: *keeper of the road, warden of the winged, servant of the fire that serves.* Forty elders. Then their grown children. Then, hesitantly, some of the younger ones, the ones who had grown up on Order tales of abominations and were kneeling now with wonder on their faces like people reading by a new kind of light.

Sebastian stood at the circle's center and received each of them. She had refused a throne when the clan mothers offered one—Corwin had heard the argument through the tent canvas, brief and brutal, ending with Sebastian saying, *I've spent my whole life invisible; I'm not starting my second life sitting above people*—so she stood instead, plain and tired and bandaged, and gripped every hand offered, and Corwin watched the strangest transformation he had ever witnessed: the moment a person becomes an institution while insisting she is still a person.

Pip stayed with Ysolde. Through all of it, the child never left the dragon's side, and Ysolde's breathing grew slower and slower, and the glow beneath her scales dimmed by degrees, like a sunset measured in hours instead of minutes.

Near midday, Corwin could no longer bear the wall. He crossed the hollow—Wren trailing him, muttering about treatises—and knelt, carefully, agonizingly, at the dragon's head.

Ysolde's eye found him. The gold in it had faded almost to amber glass, but it was still, even now, seeing him completely.

*You held me up, knight.*

"You held up the whole world longer," Corwin said. "A few tons of dragon seemed fair exchange."

A sound moved through her, deep and rusty, and Corwin realized with a jolt that it was laughter.

*The eggs sleep in the deep vaults. Twelve. Perhaps thirteen; the smallest keeps its secrets. They will need wardens who understand fire—what it costs, what it borrows, whom it burns and whom it warms.* The great eye blinked, slow as a season turning. *You will do.*

"I'll fail at it. Repeatedly."

*Yes,* Ysolde agreed placidly. *That is what wardens are for. Failing, and remaining.* Her gaze drifted past him, to Pip, to the singing stones, to the mountains beyond the valley mouth where spring was coming on whether anyone swore covenants or not. *It is enough. It was always going to be enough, if only someone stayed long enough to see it. Stay, little knight. See it.*

"I will," Corwin said. "I'm not going anywhere."

*I know,* said Ysolde, and there was, unmistakably, amusement in it. *Your wound sees to that. Stubborn and perforated—the guild could build a school on you.*

Her eyes closed. Not all at once—by degrees, the way evening comes, the way a fire settles. Her breathing lengthened. Slowed. Became something deeper than breathing, something that moved the whole hollow gently in and out, the mountain itself inhaling with her, until Corwin could no longer tell where the dragon ended and the sleeping valley began.

She did not die that day. Or the next. She simply dimmed, and settled, and sank by imperceptible degrees into the stone of her own roost, and the warm air of the valley continued, and the stones sang, and after a while the children of the clans began leaving offerings at her flank the way one leaves flowers at the door of a sleeping grandmother—not because she is gone, but because she is resting, and deserves to know she was seen.

* * *

Three days later, Corwin stood at the roost's entrance in the early light, dressed—for the first time in eleven years—in colors that were not ash-grey. The clans' weavers had made him a coat, deep green shot with copper thread, and Wren had sewn onto the shoulder a badge of her own design: the covenant sigil, rendered in stitchwork slightly crooked and entirely unmistakable.

His wound had turned in the night—not worse, the healer said, but changing, the fever breaking at last into sweat and then into the profound exhausted weakness of a body that had decided, against all reasonable expectation, to live. He could feel it in himself, the strange lightness of a man walking around inside a life he had not expected to keep.

Below him, the road down through the valley was busy. Clan folk coming and going. Supplies moving. And at the head of it, mounted on a borrowed pony and arguing cheerfully with Wren about the proper organization of a chronicle, rode Sebastian Montrose, last guide of the Wayfarers' Guild and first master of whatever it was becoming—heading home to reopen a guildhall that had three members and, as of this morning, forty-one new applicants.

She reined in beneath him and looked up.

"You're supposed to be lying down," she called.

"I'm supposed to be lots of things." He tested his weight on the steps. Held. "Master Montrose. Strange words on you."

"They're heavy ones. I keep waiting for someone to take them back." She glanced down the road, then back up at him, and her expression shifted into something quieter. "Ysolde?"

"Sleeping. Singing, if you listen with your boots off." He came down the last steps, slowly, and stood beside her pony, and for a moment neither of them spoke, because some things did not need it.

Then Sebastian reached into her saddlebag and drew out a folded square of oilcloth, and passed it down to him, and inside it Corwin found a strip of pale bark-paper bearing a single line in Wren's careful hand:

*First lesson of the new guild, arms-master: the road home is also a road. Teach them to walk it both ways.*

Corwin laughed—a real laugh, surprising him with the sound of it—and tucked the paper inside his coat, over his heart, where the borrowed fire was learning to burn.

"Tell Wren," he said, "that the first lesson's accepted. And tell her she'd better hurry home."

"Why's that?"

"Because," Corwin said, looking east where the road ran down out of the mountains toward a guildhall with its lamps being lit, "she's about to find out what's sleeping under the floor of the deep vaults—and I don't think twelve eggs intend to stay asleep forever."
