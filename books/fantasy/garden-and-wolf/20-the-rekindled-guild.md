## 20. The Rekindled Guild

The guildhall door had not been opened in a year and a half, and it announced the fact with a groan that Sebastian felt in her teeth.

She stood on the threshold with her hand still on the iron ring, letting the smell of the place wash over her—cold ash, old tallow, dust gone soft as fur, and beneath it all the deep stone-and-pine scent that was simply *home*, the way a road smells like a road no matter how long you've been away from it. Morning light fell through the high eastern windows in dusty columns, striping the great hall where once three people had eaten their meals at a table built for forty.

Three people. That had been the whole of the Wayfarers' Guild when the courier died at their door: Old Maren, who could no longer walk past the first bend of the river; Tam, who drank; and Sebastian herself, who preferred roads to people because roads never asked her to stay.

Now she counted heads out of habit—Wren behind her with two satchels and a crate of books balanced against her hip, Corwin leaning on his new-made staff because his wound still pulled in cold weather, Pip darting between their legs to be first inside—and the count came up wrong. Too many. And that was before the wagons arrived.

"Mind the lintel," Sebastian said, uselessly, because Pip had already cleared it in a leap no human child could make, landing in the hall's center with wings half-unfurled from under her cloak, scattering a year and a half of dust into the light.

"It's *smaller* than I remembered," Pip announced.

"You were seven," Wren said, setting her crate down with the care of a woman handling relics. "Everything was bigger."

"I was eight."

"You were seven and lying about it, which is worse." But Wren was smiling as she said it, and she turned slowly in the middle of the hall, taking inventory the way only Wren took inventory—not of chairs and tables but of *records*. Her eyes went straight to the archive room's closed door, and Sebastian saw her fingers twitch with the hunger of a chronicler facing eighteen months of unattended paper.

Corwin came through last. He always did now; he'd learned in the Thornfells that haste cost more than patience. He stood beside Sebastian in the doorway for a moment, looking down the length of the hall, and said nothing, which from Corwin was its own kind of speech. His hand found her shoulder—brief, heavy, certain—and then he went to work, because Corwin Ashvale did not know how to enter a room without finding something in it that needed mending.

* * *

They worked for three days before anyone else arrived.

Sebastian had expected the clans to send someone—a delegation, an observer, a polite letter declining. What she had not expected was the sound of cart wheels on the mountain road on the fourth morning, and then a fifth cart, and then voices, many voices, climbing the switchbacks below the guildhall like water rising in a well.

By noon there were eleven of them standing in the yard: clan-folk in wool and leather, two of them gray-bearded elders she recognized from the judgment at Ember Road, and among them—young ones. A girl of perhaps fourteen with a shepherd's crook and a stare like flint. Twin brothers who kept finishing each other's sentences. A boy so thin he looked woven from rope, clutching a bedroll as though someone might take it back.

"We heard the guild was open," the elder Brannoc said, as though that explained everything, as though a rumor traveling down a valley were a summons sealed in wax.

"The guild isn't—" Sebastian began, and stopped, because what was the truth? *The guild is one smuggler, one disgraced knight, one runaway scribe, and a dragon-child sleeping in the loft.* She looked at the young faces. She thought of her grandmother's journal, of the oath written in a hand steadier than any she'd ever managed: *We keep the roads safe for those who walk them, and we keep watch over the fire that warms the world.* Not smuggling. Never just smuggling. The guild had been dying for sixty years because everyone had forgotten what it was for, and here stood eleven people who had somehow, impossibly, not forgotten—or who wanted to learn.

"Can any of you read a trail?" she asked instead.

The shepherd girl raised her crook like a spear. "I can read weather, tracks, and liars."

"Then you'll do. All of you will do." Sebastian heard her own voice come out steady and was quietly astonished by it. "First lesson starts at dawn. It starts cold, wet, and early, and if any of you expected otherwise, the road back down is right there."

Nobody left. The rope-thin boy actually smiled.

* * *

By midsummer the hall had forty names on its roll, and Sebastian had stopped counting out of habit and started counting out of wonder.

It changed everything and nothing. The great table filled again—she'd had Corwin split it into three smaller ones, because forty people made conversation impossible and community, she was learning, lived in conversation. The old dormitory bunks creaked under students instead of silence. The kitchen, which had produced three joyless meals a day for three joyless people, now roared from before dawn till well past dark, and the smell of bread became as much a part of the guildhall as the smell of stone.

Her days acquired a shape she didn't recognize at first, and then did: mornings on the practice slopes teaching mapwork and star-reading, afternoons in the yard while Corwin drilled them in staff and blade and, more often, in when *not* to draw either, evenings around the fire where Wren taught the covenant's history from the journals they'd carried out of the ruined waystations—one by one, recovered, copied, returned to their proper home.

It was strange, being looked at. Being *addressed*. Students knocked on her door with questions about routes and rights-of-passage, about the old warden law, about whether the stories of the Ember Roost were true (she gave the same answer every time: *come to your second year and see*). Strangers in the valley towns knew her name now. Knew her *face*. Once, in the market at Dunmarrow, a child pointed and whispered *that's the Warden-Master*, and Sebastian had stood frozen over a basket of apples until the fruit-seller laughed at her.

"I spent my whole life trying not to be seen," she told Wren that night, on the wall above the yard, watching the last students come in from evening drill.

"And how does it sit?"

"Heavy. Like a pack I can't set down." She turned an apple in her hands—she'd bought it without thinking, some old reflex of solitary travel. "But it's a good weight. The kind that tells you you're carrying something."

Wren nodded, making one of her small notes—Sebastian had long since given up objecting to being chronicled—and then said, carefully, "There's a matter for the morning council. The roost sent word."

Sebastian went very still. Word from the roost meant word from Ysolde, or from those who tended the guttering flame in the hidden valley, and word from Ysolde was never casual. "What kind of word?"

"Pip's teachers say she's ready to begin the deep lessons. And—" Wren hesitated, and Sebastian realized the scribe was afraid, which almost never happened. "And Ysolde asks for you. By name. She says there is something beneath the roost she has guarded alone for longer than the guild has existed, and she says the time has come for wardens to see it. She says—" Wren glanced down at her own notes, as if needing the exact words. "She says the fire wants to be shared, and she cannot share it alone anymore."

Below them, the yard lamps were being lit one by one, small flames answering each other across the stones. Sebastian watched them and thought about fires: how one alone gutters, how many together make a hearth, how the oldest lesson of the road was that no traveler survives the winter by themselves.

"Then we'll go," she said. "All of us. The guild goes where it's needed—that's the whole point of it."

* * *

They left on the first hard frost, a party of nine: Sebastian, Wren, Corwin, and six second-year students chosen for steadiness rather than talent, because Sebastian had learned that talent was common and steadiness was not. Pip flew ahead and circled back and flew ahead again, bright as a struck match against the gray sky, too excited to hold formation even though she'd promised, even though Corwin had made her promise twice.

The old guild roads took them north and up, through passes that remembered their feet. At the burned-out waystation where this whole road had begun—the one from the first run, the one where she'd mourned the guild's decline without knowing the guild was only sleeping—Sebastian called a halt and walked its ruins alone.

The walls still stood to shoulder height. Someone had already been here: a new waystone rose at the center of the broken floor, uncut granite bearing the covenant sigil, fresh-chiseled and unmistakable. One of the clans' masons, working from the lists Wren had compiled. Standing stones along the old roads, marking them as what they had always been—not smugglers' paths but warden roads, the veins of an old promise running warm again.

Sebastian laid her palm flat on the stone. It held the day's sun like a living thing.

"Grandmother," she said quietly, to nobody, to the mountains, "you'd have hated how long it took us. But we got there."

From the doorway behind her, Corwin said nothing, which was his way of agreeing completely.

* * *

The hidden valley opened before them on the third evening exactly as memory insisted it must: air turning warm as bathwater, ancient waystones humming underfoot, the light going gold and strange. The students stopped talking all at once. Even Pip went quiet, dropping out of the sky to walk the last mile at Sebastian's side, her scales catching the amber glow and throwing it back doubled.

Ysolde waited for them at the roost's mouth.

The last dragon was dimmer than Sebastian remembered—her scales like coals at dusk rather than noon, her breathing slow and vast, each exhale stirring the grass in waves. But her eyes were the same: old gold, patient, seeing everything and judging little.

*Little warden,* Ysolde said, and her voice resonated in Sebastian's chest bones rather than her ears. *You built your house.*

"We reopened one that was already yours."

*Mine. Yours. The distinction mattered less each century.* The great head lowered until one ancient eye was level with Sebastian's face, close enough that she could feel the faint warmth radiating off the scaled hide, like standing near a hearth. *I asked for you because what lies beneath this roost was entrusted to the first wardens, and it has slept through betrayal and burning and forgetting. It should not sleep through the waking. Come. Bring your family. There is something I have guarded alone for longer than your guild has existed, and I am tired, and the time has come.*

Ysolde turned, slow as a season changing, and moved toward the roost's deepest dark. Pip fell into step beside the dragon as naturally as breathing. Wren already had her journal out. Corwin checked the students' line with a glance and a lifted hand.

Sebastian lingered one breath at the threshold, looking back the way they'd come—at the road unwinding south toward a lit guildhall, toward forty names on a roll and bread in the ovens and a life she had never dared imagine for the invisible girl of the Wayfarers.

Then she followed the fire down.
