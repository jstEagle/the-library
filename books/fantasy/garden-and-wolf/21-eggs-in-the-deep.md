## 21. Eggs in the Deep

The vaults beneath the Ember Roost had no business being warm.

Wren noticed it first, because Wren noticed everything—had spent six years in the Order's scriptorium learning that the world announced itself to anyone patient enough to read it. The deeper they descended past Ysolde's sleeping chamber, past the covenant stones with their banked and breathing fire, the warmer the air grew, until her ink-stained fingers stopped aching in the mountain cold and her breath came easy, like walking into a kitchen where bread had just been pulled from an oven.

"Nobody said anything about this," Corwin muttered behind her. His wound had closed at last into a pale seam across his ribs, though he still favored his left side on stairs, and he carried his sword now less like a weapon than like a walking stick he was embarrassed to need. "Three weeks we've lived here. Three weeks, and nobody thought to mention there's a whole second roost under the first one."

"Ysolde didn't mention it," Sebastian said. She walked ahead of them both, torch held high, and Wren watched the light slide along walls carved with spirals older than any script she could read—which, for Wren, was saying something. "Pip did."

"Pip is four."

"Pip is also right." Sebastian glanced back, and in the torchlight her face had changed over these weeks—not softer exactly, but *unhidden*, as if the woman who'd arrived at the Ember Road carrying nothing but a dead courier's map had finally set down some weight she hadn't known she was holding. "She's been hearing it for days. The humming. I thought it was the stones settling."

Wren had heard it too, if she was honest. A sound below sound, felt in the teeth more than the ears, like the memory of a bell. She'd told herself it was the mountain's blood moving through old channels. She'd told herself a great many convenient things in her life; lying to herself was the first skill the Order had ever taught her, though they'd called it discipline.

The passage opened without warning.

One moment the corridor was narrow enough that Wren's shoulders brushed stone on both sides, and the next the torchlight simply ran out of walls, spilling into a cavern so vast that the flame seemed to shrink, embarrassed. Wren stepped forward and felt the floor change underfoot—from rough-cut passage to something smoother, worn, deliberate. Ancient waystones studded the ground in great concentric rings, each one glowing faintly gold, each one humming that subterranean note, and beyond them—

Beyond them, arranged in a spiral that followed the rings inward like a written sentence, were the eggs.

There were dozens of them. Perhaps a hundred. Each one large as a rain barrel, shell the color of storm-cloud and coal veined through with lines of dim ember-light that pulsed—slowly, so slowly Wren might have imagined it—in rhythm with the hum. They sat in shallow basins of black glass, and the warmth of the room rose from them, all of it, years and years of it, a hearth tended by no one.

No one living.

"Oh," Wren said. It was not a word she chose. It simply fell out of her, the way truth sometimes does when the body stops consulting the mind. "Oh, she never said."

"She couldn't have." Sebastian had gone very still, the way she went still before choosing a route down a cliff face. "If Vane had known—if the Order had known there were eggs—" She didn't finish it. She didn't have to. All of them had seen what the Order did with things that burned.

Corwin lowered himself carefully onto one knee beside the nearest basin, wincing, and laid his palm flat against the stone rim. "Warm," he said quietly. "Not warm like rock. Warm like—" He looked up at Wren, and there was something naked in his face, something she'd only seen there since the snow, since his confession, since she'd chosen to keep walking beside him anyway. "Like something waiting."

"They're dormant," Wren said, because someone had to say the true thing out loud or they would all stand here drowning in awe until they starved. She was already circling the outer ring, cataloguing, counting—the scribe in her refusing to be silenced even by wonder. "Deep dormancy. Look at the veins—the light cycles are months apart, maybe seasons. These aren't dying, they're *waiting*. Waiting for conditions." She frowned, crouched, pulled the little stub of charcoal from her pocket and a scrap of vellum, began sketching the spiral arrangement before she'd consciously decided to. "Waiting for what, though? Heat, clearly. But heat alone—"

"It isn't heat alone."

The voice came from behind them, small and clear, and none of them jumped anymore—they'd stopped jumping at Pip's arrivals within a week of knowing her—but Wren still turned quickly, because Pip had been forbidden the deep vaults. Forbidden by Ysolde herself, in words the dragon had spoken while looking directly at Sebastian: *Not yet. Some doors open once.*

Pip stood at the edge of the torchlight, barefoot as always, her scales catching the flame in ripples of bronze and green. In the three weeks since Vane's fire had broken against her, since she'd stood between an army and a dying dragon and *shone*, the child had changed. She was taller—or seemed taller. Her eyes had gone from amber to something molten, and when she moved, the air moved with her, faintly, like heat-shimmer given a personality.

"You weren't supposed to come down here," Sebastian said gently.

"I know." Pip came forward anyway, unrepentant, the way children are, the way dragons perhaps always were. She stopped at the first ring of waystones and looked out over the spiral of eggs, and her expression was one Wren had never seen on a child's face: recognition. Grief and joy in equal measure, tangled together like roots. "I heard them singing. Grandmother said not yet, but they've been getting louder every night, and last night—" Pip's voice caught. "Last night one of them said my name."

The silence that followed had weight. Wren felt it press on her chest.

*Grandmother.* Pip had started calling Ysolde that two weeks ago, and the dragon had neither corrected nor encouraged it, which from Ysolde was practically a christening.

"One of them spoke?" Corwin asked carefully.

"Not spoke." Pip shook her head, impatient with the poverty of human words, a frustration Wren recognized intimately—a scribe's frustration, a translator's frustration, the eternal gap between the thing known and the thing writable. "It's like... you know how you can feel someone looking at you? Even if you can't see them? It's like that, but inside. Like being *looked at from inside an egg*." She padded forward, past the waystones, and the hum deepened around her, the vein-light in the nearest eggs brightening almost imperceptibly, and Wren's charcoal froze mid-stroke because she was watching the pattern respond. Watching the spiral *answer* the child.

"Pip," Wren said slowly, "what happens if you touch one?"

"I don't know." And then, with the terrifying honesty of the young: "That's why I want to."

Sebastian looked at Wren. Wren looked at Sebastian. It had become, over these weeks, a whole conversation conducted in glances—the guide asking the scholar, the scholar answering the guide—and Wren found, not for the first time, that she trusted this wordless council more than any decree she'd ever copied in triplicate.

"The Order's records mentioned clutches," Wren said. "Old ones. Pre-crusade documents I copied before I understood what I was copying." She tapped her temple. "It's all still up here. Every page. They burned the archives, but they made me *memorize* them first, because paper burns and obedience doesn't." Her mouth twisted. "The texts said dragon eggs wake to three things: sustained warmth, a warden's oath spoken over them, and—" she hesitated.

"And?" Sebastian prompted.

"A dragon's voice. A living dragon, calling them toward hatching. Preferably kin." Wren looked at Pip—at the molten eyes, the shimmering skin, the half of her that was something the world hadn't seen in centuries. "Pip is the closest thing to kin those eggs have ever going to get."

*

They brought Ysolde down themselves, which was its own quiet ordeal—the great dragon's descent through the lower passages took the better part of a day, her body too diminished now for the open sky routes she'd flown for a thousand years, and Wren walked beside her the whole way with a lantern, listening to the old stories the dragon told in that voice like wind through a canyon. Stories about the eggs' mothers. About the Night of Cinders, told finally from the other side of it. About how, in the last desperate years of the covenant, the wardens had carried the clutches down into the deep vaults and sung them to sleep, because a sleeping egg cannot be murdered, only forgotten—and forgetting, Ysolde said, was the cruelest mercy the world had ever offered her.

Now the dragon lay coiled in the center of the innermost ring, her scales dull as old pewter, her breath slow and vast, and the eggs around her seemed to lean toward her the way seedlings lean toward a window.

"Wake them," Ysolde said, and her ancient eyes found Pip. "Or try. I have kept them alive a hundred years with warmth and stubbornness alone. Warmth and stubbornness will not hatch them. That requires something I no longer have." A plume of smoke, thin as thread. "It requires a future. I am not a future, little one. I am a very long past."

"What do I do?" Pip asked. No fear in it. Only hunger.

"Sing what you heard."

"That's all?"

Ysolde's laugh was a low rumble that made the waystones flicker. "Child. All the great workings are 'that's all.' The difficulty is not the doing. The difficulty is meaning it."

So Pip sang.

Wren had expected melody. What she got instead was something older than melody—a rising and falling tone that seemed to come not from Pip's throat but from her sternum, from her blood, from whatever mingled inheritance slept in her half-dragon veins. The sound filled the cavern the way water fills a vessel, and the hum of the waystones shifted to meet it, harmonized with it, and Wren—scribing frantically now, charcoal flying, because if this was ever to be recorded then let it be recorded *properly*—watched the vein-light in the nearest egg begin to pulse faster.

Faster.

Faster.

The egg rocked.

It was a small motion, a shiver, easily denied—but Corwin saw it too, and Sebastian saw it, and Ysolde raised her great head with a creaking groan of old bone and old hope, and the egg rocked again, harder, tapping against the black-glass basin with a sound like a knuckle on a door.

"Keep singing," Sebastian breathed.

"I *am* singing," Pip said, laughing and crying at once, and the sound of it broke something loose in Wren's chest that she suspected would never quite reseal.

The egg's veins blazed. For one heartbeat the whole cavern lit gold, every waystone flaring, every shadow fleeing to the far walls—and inside the shell, silhouetted against its own inner fire, Wren saw it: a shape. Curled. Small. A wing folded like a closed hand. A heartbeat visible as a pulse of light, quick and unmistakable and *alive*.

Then the light faded. The egg settled. The pulse slowed back toward its long seasonal patience.

But it had answered. It had *answered*.

Pip sank down beside the basin, exhausted, glowing faintly, grinning through her tears. "It said my name again," she whispered. "It says I sound like its mother."

Ysolde made a sound Wren had never heard from her—something between a sob and a landslide—and lowered her head until her brow nearly touched the eggshell, and stayed that way for a long time.

*

Later—much later, when Corwin had carried a sleeping Pip up to the warm chambers and Sebastian had gone to check the watch rotations out of a habit she claimed she'd quit—Wren remained in the vault alone with her notes and the eggs.

This was her element, though she'd never had an element before. Lamplight, parchment, patient observation. She sketched the spiral again, corrected her counts—one hundred and fourteen eggs, thirty-one showing renewed vein-activity since Pip's song—and began drafting the first page of what she privately intended to become the new guild chronicle. Not the Order's kind of record, where truth was trimmed to fit decree. A real one. The kind her grandmother's generation had supposedly kept, before everything burned.

*Day one,* she wrote. *The vault is warm. There are one hundred and fourteen reasons to believe the war was never necessary.*

Her charcoal paused over the page.

Because that was the thought underneath all the wonder, wasn't it? The thought that had been circling her for weeks like a moth around a lamp. Vane had built her crusade on the claim that dragons meant war—that fire, left unguarded, must inevitably turn on the world. And here, in the deepest dark of the world, sat a hundred and fourteen sleeping proofs that the opposite had always been true: that the fire had been guarded, patiently, at terrible cost, by people like Sebastian's grandmother, waiting not for conquest but for company. The Order hadn't feared a threat. The Order had feared an *accounting*.

Wren blew a strand of hair from her ink-stained face and kept writing.

She was three pages in when she noticed the wall.

It was behind the innermost ring, half-hidden by a flow of ancient calcite, and she'd taken it for natural stone until her lamp angle shifted and the light caught regularity where there should have been none. Chisel marks. Deliberate ones. And beneath them, cut deep and filled with something that still gleamed faintly gold after centuries:

Script.

Not the spiral-carvings of the outer cavern. Actual script—old High Wayfarer cant, the ceremonial hand used only for oaths, the hand Wren had spent two miserable years of her apprenticeship transcribing and therefore knew better than her own name. She crossed the ring of waystones, heart ticking oddly, and lifted her lamp.

The inscription read:

*HERE SLEEPS THE SECOND CLUTCH. WE WARDENS DO NOT BURY OUR HOPE; WE PLANT IT. LET THE ONE WHO READS THIS KNOW: THE COVENANT WAS NEVER ENDED. ONLY PAUSED.*

And beneath it, a list of names. Dozens of names. Wardens' names, signed in the oath-hand, and near the bottom, in a script Wren knew from a journal in a ruined waystation, from a woman long dead whose granddaughter now walked these halls wearing her inheritance—

*Elara Montrose.*

Wren stood very still for a long moment, lamp trembling slightly, listening to the hum of a hundred and fourteen sleeping hearts.

Then she rolled up her notes, tucked the charcoal behind her ear, and turned for the stairs at a pace just short of running. Sebastian needed to see this. Sebastian needed to see it tonight, because Wren had transcribed enough oaths in her life to know what that inscription was, formally, legally, in the old law of the guild:

It was a founding document. And it named a Montrose among its signatories—which meant the covenant hadn't merely survived.

Somewhere in the deep, in a vault no one had thought to search, it might still hold provisions. Instructions. Perhaps even authority—written authority, sealed and sworn, that no clan-moot and no crown court could overturn.

And Wren, who had once read too much for the Order's comfort, intended to find out exactly what Elara Montrose had left behind—because the third line of the inscription, the one half-eaten by mineral bloom, had ended with a phrase she couldn't stop turning over in her mind:

*...and the key sleeps with the youngest warden.*

Wren climbed toward the lamplight of the upper halls, already composing the message in her head, and wondered—with a scribe's cold thrill and a friend's warm dread—whether Pip knew she was humming in her sleep.
