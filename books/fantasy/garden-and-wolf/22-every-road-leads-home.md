## 22. Every Road Leads Home

The Thornfells had learned to be kind again, or perhaps Sebastian had simply learned to see the kindness that had always been there, folded into the stony folds of the passes like letters tucked into books no one opened. A year ago these mountains had been a place she moved through—efficiently, invisibly, a shadow among shadows—and now they were a place she belonged to, and that belonged to her, in the way old debts and older promises belong to people.

She walked at the head of six students, and the strangeness of it still caught in her throat some mornings like cold air.

They were an odd company, as all good companies were. Tam, the miller's son with a surveyor's eye and a fear of heights he was slowly, stubbornly outgrowing. The twins, Isolde and Bryn, who had come to the guildhall from a border town Vane's false war had emptied, and who finished each other's sentences and each other's mistakes. Old Marta's granddaughter, a solemn girl named Effie who could read weather in the behavior of birds better than anyone Sebastian had ever taught. And at the back, quiet as falling snow, a boy called Dannel who spoke rarely but noticed everything, which was, Sebastian had told them on their first morning, the entire secret of wayfaring: *the road tells you everything, if you are the sort of person who listens.*

"The cairn's wrong," Effie said, pointing ahead with her chin. "Third stone from the left is turned."

Sebastian smiled without turning around. "And what does a turned stone tell us?"

"That someone came through recently. Someone who knew enough to mark their passage but not enough to know the stones mark themselves." Effie frowned at the cairn where it hunched against the wind below the pass. "Or someone who wanted us to think someone came through."

"Better," Sebastian said. "Both answers are true more often than you'd think. Remember them both."

The wind off the high snowfields carried the smell of pine resin and, faintly, of warm stone—the smell of the valley they were walking toward, though it was still two days away. The students didn't know yet what waited there. They knew only that their first true road led over the Weeping Stair and down into the deep Thornfells, and that their master had promised them they would meet something at the end of it that would change how they understood every map ever drawn.

Pip was waiting for them at the top of the pass.

She sat cross-legged on the summit cairn as if she'd grown there, her scales catching the afternoon light in bands of copper and green, her wings—still growing, still slightly too large for her shoulders, the way a puppy's paws are too large—folded with elaborate casualness. She had insisted on flying ahead to "scout," which everyone understood to mean she had wanted to be the first one up the mountain so she could watch them struggle.

"You're slow," Pip announced.

"We're thorough," Sebastian said.

"You're slow *thoroughly*." But Pip slid off the cairn and fell into step beside Sebastian, and her tail curled once, briefly, around the guide's ankle—a gesture that had started as accident and become ritual, the half-dragon child's wordless way of saying *you're here, I'm here, we found each other.* "Ysolde says the eggs are singing again. Two of them. Wren thinks it might mean something. Corwin says Wren thinks everything means something."

"And what do you think?"

Pip considered this with the gravity she reserved for questions about eggs and dragons and other matters of blood. "I think," she said, "that you should hurry."

* * *

They made camp that night in the ruins of a waystation—not just any waystation, though the students didn't know that either. It was the one where Sebastian had found her grandmother's journal, where the guild's oldest lie had come apart in her hands and its oldest truth had taken its place. The walls still stood to shoulder height. Someone—wardens, probably, or villagers from the highland settlements—had partially roofed the great hall with new timber, and a fire pit had been swept clean, and someone had carved small marks into the doorframe: tallies, names, dates. Travelers keeping faith with travelers across the years the guild had been dead.

Sebastian ran her thumb over the carvings while the students saw to the fire. *Marta, 3rd thaw. The Corrin brothers, autumn. E.A., alone, well.* Alone, well. She remembered being alone and well. It had seemed like enough, then.

"Master Montrose?" Dannel had appeared beside her, silent as ever. He nodded at the doorframe. "You've been here before."

It wasn't a question. "Yes."

"It matters to you."

"Yes," Sebastian said again, and found she didn't mind saying it. That was the year's great lesson, still settling into her like a bone knitting: that being seen did not kill her. That she could hand people pieces of herself and remain whole. "This is where I learned what the guild actually was. Come here—I'll show you something."

She led him to the corner where the records had lain, and showed him the loose stone beneath which she had found the journal, and told him—briefly, plainly, because the truth deserved plainness—about the Wayfarers' founding covenant. Dragon-wardens. Guardians of the Ember Roost. Sworn not to move goods through the mountains but to keep safe the oldest living things in them.

Dannel listened the way he did everything, completely. When she finished he was quiet a long moment, looking at the loose stone.

"My father was a soldier," he said at last. "He told me stories were what weak men used to hide from strong truths. I believed him for a long time." He looked up at her. "But some stories are the strong truths. Aren't they. The others are the hiding."

Sebastian put her hand briefly on his shoulder. "That's the first exam," she said. "You've passed."

* * *

They crossed the Weeping Stair the next day, and Sebastian watched her students do carefully, competently, exactly what she had taught them, and felt something in her chest that she suspected was pride but experienced as almost physical ache—as if her ribs had been built too small for what was growing inside them.

At the worst of the crossing, where the meltwater sheeted the stone and the drop yawned away into blue mist, Tam froze. Sebastian had known he would; she had chosen this route partly *because* he would, because fear met on a controlled road was fear mastered, and fear avoided was fear fed. She talked him across it, voice level, hand steady on the rope, telling him about footholds and weight distribution and nothing else, giving his mind honest work to do. And when he reached the far side and stood shaking and grinning and crying a little all at once, she let the twins hug him and pretended to check the anchors.

A year ago, she thought, watching them, I would have crossed this stretch alone in half the time and never known any of their names.

A year ago, I didn't know that was the poorer bargain.

* * *

On the third day, she took them somewhere she had never taken anyone.

The grave lay in a fold of hillside above the old guild road, where the courier had fallen—where a storm had driven a dying woman to a guildhall door, and a map made of dragon-scale had passed from her hands into Sebastian's, along with two words that had unmade and remade a life: *the Ember.*

Wren had found the site, in those early frantic weeks, using records and deduction and that terrifying archival instinct of hers. There hadn't been much to mark. The Order's purge had seen to that. But the mountain clans had their own rites, and after the judgment at the roost, the elders had come down and raised a cairn of their own fashioning—dark stones fitted without mortar, and set atop it a single slab bearing no name, because no one had ever learned the courier's name, only a sigil: the interlaced flame-and-road of the warden covenant, cut deep so the weather would need centuries to erase it.

Sebastian stopped the students fifty paces off.

"Wait here," she said. "This part is mine."

They obeyed—good students, learning—but she felt their eyes on her back as she climbed the last slope alone, and she found she didn't mind that either. Being watched and being seen were different things. She had spent thirty years confusing them.

The cairn stood against the sky, patient as all stone. Snowmelt had seeded the hillside around it with tiny white flowers that hadn't been there a year ago, or perhaps she simply hadn't been the sort of person who noticed flowers a year ago.

"I don't know your name," she said aloud. Her voice sounded strange in the open air, speaking to no one living. "I've tried to find it. Wren searched every courier roll in three provinces. You were careful, whoever you were. Careful the way I used to be careful—invisible on purpose, carrying things worth killing for, trusting no one until the very end, and then—" her breath caught, and she let it catch, "—and then trusting a stranger at a door in a storm, because there was no one else, and somehow you knew that would be enough."

She knelt and took from her pack the thing she had carried four hundred miles and a full turn of the seasons: a waystone, cut by her own hands in the guildhall yard through the winter, shaped in the old style her grandmother's journal described. It was the size of a loaf of bread, river-smoothed granite, and into its face she had carved the covenant sigil—the flame threaded through the road—and beneath it, in the old guild script, the words every Wayfarer waystone had borne since the founding:

*All who walk are welcome. All who guard are kin.*

"This is how we say thank you, where I come from," she said. "We say it in stone, so it outlasts the saying." She set the waystone at the cairn's base, fitting it into the hillside as if it had grown there, and pressed her palm flat against the carved sigil. The granite held the sun's warmth. Somewhere far below and away, in a hidden valley, ancient stones were humming with dragon-fire, and she liked to think this one heard them—that the covenant ran through all of them like water through connected ground.

"You gave me a map," she said quietly. "I've tried to give back a road. There are children learning the old signs now. There will be wardens on the passes within five years—young ones, sworn properly, taught what my grandmother taught in secret. The Order is broken. The clans remember. And there's a girl with scales who can hear eggs singing, and she calls me—" Sebastian laughed, wet-eyed, surprised by it, "—she calls me family. Not master. Family. I fought it for months. I've stopped fighting it."

Wind moved through the white flowers. High overhead, a shape wheeled against the clouds—too large for an eagle, too graceful for anything else—and Sebastian shaded her eyes and watched Pip circle twice above the grave, wings bright, before banking away toward the valley, toward home, leaving a single long cry that echoed off the peaks like a bell rung once.

"She watches over the roads now," Sebastian told the cairn. "Your roads. Ours."

She stayed a while longer, saying nothing, needing to say nothing. Then she rose, brushed the dirt from her knees, and climbed back down to where her students waited with the particular patience of people who understand that some parts of a journey belong to the traveler alone.

Effie looked at the cairn, and at the new waystone gleaming at its foot, and at Sebastian's face. "Someone who mattered," she said.

"Someone who changed everything," Sebastian corrected gently. "Most of those never get stones. So when you find one that does—" she touched the covenant sigil as she passed it, leading them on, "—you stop. You stop, and you remember, and you carry it forward. That's the whole craft. Everything else is footwork."

They walked on together, down the long green shoulder of the mountain, toward the warm stone smell rising from the valley, toward the humming waystones and the singing eggs and the family gathered there—Wren with her chronicles, Corwin teaching sword-work to clan youths with his scarred hands, Ysolde guttering but not yet gone, tended and honored and loved. Toward the school that had been a smuggling hall, the covenant that had been a rumor, the name that had been no one's.

Sebastian Montrose walked in the middle of her students, not at the front, letting them set the pace, correcting nothing, because they didn't need correcting. The road unspooled ahead of them, bright with afternoon, marked with stones that said *all who walk are welcome,* and for the first time in her life she was not invisible on it, not passing through it, not alone on it.

She was seen. She was home. And every road, she knew now, had always been leading here.
