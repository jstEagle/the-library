## 1. Charms for a Godless City

The ash came down on Ashfall the way it always did in autumn: gently, almost tenderly, as if the mountain were trying to apologize for something it had done long ago and could not remember. It settled on the awnings of the Tallowmarket, on the shoulders of the fishwives, on the tin roofs of the charm-sellers' row where it hissed faintly against still-warm metal. By midmorning everything wore a fine grey skin, and by evening the street sweepers would push it into drifts that smelled of cinders and old rain.

Jude Yarrow had learned to love the ash, or at least to be grateful for it. Ash meant the mountain was quiet. Ash meant no one was looking up.

He set out his wares the way he did every morning, with the practiced indifference of a man arranging things he did not believe in. A strip of oiled canvas across two crates. Luck-knots braided from red thread and river-grass. Salt-ward medallions stamped with a spiral that was supposed to mean *protection* but mostly meant *I paid three coppers for this*. Dried feverfew in paper twists. Bone buttons polished smooth, sold as *ancestor-touchstones* to anyone whose grandmother had died recently enough to make them generous. And at the end of the row, because every stall needed a centerpiece, a single iron nail bent into a rough circle — a *hearth-ring*, he called it, guaranteed to keep a home's fire honest.

None of it worked. He knew this the way a counterfeiter knows his own coin. The knots were knots; the salt was salt; the bone buttons had come off a pig. Fifteen years ago he could have told you exactly what lived inside a true charm, could have felt it the way you feel a second heartbeat under your palm — the warm attention of something vast leaning close to listen. Now there was nothing. There had been nothing for fifteen years, and Jude Yarrow had made his peace with nothing the way a man makes peace with a limp: by never speaking of it and walking anyway.

Except.

He flexed his left hand under the canvas, out of habit rather than hope. Between the third and fourth knuckle, half-hidden in the whorls of an old burn scar, the mark sat like a coal banked under ash. It had been white and dead for years — a scar among scars, the last receipt of a faith that had bankrupted him. But lately, these past few weeks, when the wind came down off the mountain in a certain way, or when the temple bells of Saint Vessa's rang their flat, tuneless noon, the scar warmed. Not burned. *Warmed.* The way a stone warms long after the sun has gone, holding a heat it should have surrendered hours ago.

Jude told himself it was weather. He told himself it was age, blood, imagination, indigestion. He had become, over fifteen years, very good at telling himself things.

"Charm-man." A voice like gravel in a sack. Old Bess from the tannery yard, her forearms stained the color of strong tea, her breath carrying the sweet rot of cheap plum wine. "You got any of them fever-twists left? My boy's coughing again."

"Feverfew's good this batch," Jude said, already wrapping three twists in brown paper. "Steep it hot, honey if you've got it, and put the knot under his pillow." He tied a red thread around the paper because Bess believed in the thread, and belief, he had found, was the only active ingredient he stocked.

"You're a good man, Yarrow."

"I'm a cheap man," Jude said. "Two coppers."

She paid four and told him to keep the change, which was how Bess's faith worked too. She shuffled off through the ash-fall, and Jude watched her go and felt the familiar small shame settle over him like the ash itself — soft, grey, everywhere. *A good man.* He sold pig-bone to widows and called it ancestor-work. He tied knots with no more power in them than a shoelace and let mothers believe they were guarding their children. It wasn't a lie, exactly. It was worse than a lie. It was a performance of a truth that had died, kept alive by a man who was too much a coward to bury it and too hungry to stop performing.

*You swore,* said a voice in him that sounded like his own but older, younger, the voice of a man in white robes standing in a circle of singers with his hand pressed to a burning altar. *You swore your voice to the Choir, and the Choir's silence to the world.*

And then the other voice, the one he'd built brick by brick over fifteen years: *The Choir is dead. Dead gods don't mind.*

The morning went on. He sold a hearth-ring to a nervous newlywed. He sold a salt-ward to a carter who was making the mountain road run and wanted, he admitted, "something against whatever's up there," though he couldn't say what. Everyone wanted something against what they couldn't name. That was the whole business of charms in one sentence, and Jude had built a life inside that sentence like a snail in its shell.

Around noon, the light changed.

It didn't brighten or dim so much as *thin*, the way broth thins when you add water, and Jude looked up from counting coppers and saw the ash falling slower, each flake hanging in the air a half-beat longer than it should. The crowd in the market went on haggling and spitting and laughing, oblivious. But a dog three stalls down lifted its head and stared at nothing. A baby started crying in a way that had no hunger in it, only alarm. And between Jude's knuckles, under the scar-tissue and the ash-grey skin, something *ticked*.

Warmth. Faint as a memory of warmth. Then gone.

Jude closed his fist and held it against his stomach and breathed through his nose until the shaking stopped. Fifteen years. Fifteen years since the night the sky over the Vael Marches had gone silent — not dark, not loud, *silent*, the way a room goes silent when everyone in it stops breathing at once. Fifteen years since the Choir's song had cut off mid-note, since the wards had guttered like candles in a draft, since the thing he'd given his whole self to had simply... stopped answering. They'd said the god died. The priests who survived long enough to say anything had said it in whispers: *the Choir is dead, the Voice is lost, the covenant is broken, run.*

So he had run. And he had landed here, in a city named for its own funeral, selling knots to people who would never know how badly they needed the real thing.

"Excuse me."

Jude looked down. A child stood at the edge of his canvas, a girl of maybe eight, thin as a reed, with ash in her black hair and enormous serious eyes the color of river water. She held a single copper in both hands, cupped, the way you hold a wounded bird.

"We're closed—" he began automatically, though he was not, in fact, closed.

"Mama says you sell luck." Her chin came up, defiant, daring him to contradict her. "Real luck. Not the kind that's just string."

Jude looked at the luck-knots laid out in their neat rows. Red thread and river-grass. Three coppers apiece, or two for a mother with a sick boy and wine on her breath. String. Exactly, precisely, only string.

"They're all just string," he said, and heard his own voice come out gentler than he intended. "But sometimes string remembers being something else."

That was the kind of thing he said when the shame got too big to swallow whole and had to be broken into smaller, prettier pieces. The girl considered it with the gravity of a magistrate.

"It's for my brother," she said. "He's going down the mines tomorrow. First day. Papa says the deep galleries eat boys." Her hands tightened around the copper. "Mama prayed at Saint Vessa's but Vessa doesn't listen anymore. Everybody says the gods are all dead or gone. So." She thrust the copper at him. "String that remembers."

Something moved in Jude's chest, slow and painful, like a door opening on a room that had been locked so long the hinges had rusted into the frame. He took the copper. He took up a length of red thread and river-grass, and — because it was easier than thinking, because his hands remembered even if nothing else did — he began to braid.

Three strands. The old pattern. *One for the road behind, one for the road ahead, one for the walker.* His fingers moved without him, and somewhere in the middle of the third crossing his left hand began, very softly, to warm.

He told himself it was the sun coming through the ash-cloud. He told himself it was his own blood. He braided, and the warmth grew, and the ghost of a sound moved through him — not a sound, really, not in his ears; more in the hollow behind his sternum, the place where a second heartbeat had once lived. A hum. A single held note, low and patient, like a choir warming up in a cathedral so far away the sound had to cross a desert to reach him.

Fifteen years of silence, and now a hum.

Jude's hands did not stop. That was the terrible part. His hands knew this work the way a drowning man knows swimming, and they finished the knot, drew it tight, sealed it with the old closing word — *sela*, *it is held* — spoken so quietly it was barely breath, in a language that had been dead for fifteen years and apparently hadn't noticed.

The knot flared.

Not visibly. Anyone watching would have seen only a charm-seller handing a trinket to a child. But Jude felt it leave his fingers the way you feel a spark jump from a doorknob — a tiny discharge, a completion, a circuit closing after years of open air. The girl took the luck-knot and the warmth of it must have reached her too, because she blinked, and frowned at it, and then smiled with her whole face, sudden as sunrise.

"It's warm," she whispered. "You said they were just string."

"Just string," Jude agreed. His mouth was dry. "Remembering."

She ran off through the ash-fall clutching it, and Jude sat down slowly on his crate and put his face in his hands.

*It shouldn't have worked.* That was the thought, over and over, turning like a wheel. *It shouldn't have worked. Nothing works. Nothing has worked for fifteen years. I am a fraud in a city of frauds and my god is dead and dead gods do not answer children's prayers through pig-string and river-grass.*

But the scar between his knuckles was warm. Warmer than it had ever been in all these years of careful, deliberate nothing. And underneath the warmth, fainter than the hum, thinner than smoke, he could almost — *almost* — hear something that was not quite a voice saying something that was not quite his name.

*Jude.*

No. Not that. Never that. His name was a door he had nailed shut, and he was not going to stand here in the Tallowmarket and pry the nails out because a child's luck-knot had gotten warm.

He raised his head. The market flowed on around him, grey and ordinary. The dog had put its head back down. The baby had stopped crying. Whatever had thinned the light had thickened again, and Ashfall went about its business of ash and haggling and forgetting.

Jude packed up early. He told himself it was the cold coming on, the ache in his bad knee, the headache building behind his eyes. He wrapped the unsold charms in oilcloth with hands that were almost steady, and only at the end, folding the canvas, did he allow himself one look at his left hand.

Between the third and fourth knuckle, the scar glowed. Faintly. Softly. The exact grey-gold color of embers an hour after the fire has been declared dead.

He pulled his glove on over it and walked home through the falling ash, and behind him, in the empty space where his stall had been, the last flakes settled on the boards like snow on a grave — or like snow on a bed where someone is sleeping, and has only just begun, after a very long time, to stir.

That night, in his rented room above the chandler's shop, Jude lay awake listening to the mountain breathe. And just before sleep took him, in the gap between one heartbeat and the next, the hum came back — closer now, clearer, a note held by a throat that had been silent for fifteen years and was remembering how.

Every charm in his oilcloth bundle answered it, all at once, in the dark.

They glowed through the cloth like a basket of coals, and outside his window, high above the ash-cloud, where no star should have been visible through the fall of grey —

— one star went out.
