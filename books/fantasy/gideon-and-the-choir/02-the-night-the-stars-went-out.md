## 2. The Night the Stars Went Out

The first star went out while Jude was haggling with a widow over the price of a fever-charm.

He didn't see it happen. He heard it—a sound like a wet finger drawn around the rim of a glass, thin and high and wrong, coming from everywhere at once. The widow heard it too. She stopped mid-sentence, her knuckles whitening around her coin purse, and looked up through the smoke-haze of Ashfall's night market as if she expected to find the sky's throat cut.

"Odd," she said.

"Wind in the flues," Jude said, because that was what he always said. "Old city. Old pipes."

She bought the charm—three coppers, down from five, because fear made people stingy before it made them generous—and shuffled off into the crowd clutching it like a talisman, which was precisely what it was not. It was a knot of braided horsehair and river-clay, blessed by nothing, warmed by nothing except the heat of his own hands. He had made forty of them that afternoon by candlelight, humming to himself the way a man hums when he is trying not to think, and every one of them was hollow as an eggshell.

Or so he believed until the second star went out.

This one he saw. He was standing beneath the broken awning of his stall, counting coins into a chipped clay pot, when the sky above the eastern rooftops simply... blinked. Not clouded over—Ashfall's perpetual coal-smoke did that often enough—but *extinguished*, the way a lamp goes out when someone pinches the wick. One moment there was the Salt-Star burning low and orange over the border hills; the next there was a hole in heaven where it had been, a perfect circle of perfect black, and the darkness of it was somehow worse than any darkness he had ever seen, because it had edges.

Around him, the market faltered. A fishmonger set down his knife. Two dockhands stopped arguing about wages. Somewhere a dog began to howl, then another, then a dozen, a ragged chorus rising from every yard and alley in the district, and beneath the howling Jude felt something stir against his chest—the brand over his heart, the mark he had carried for fifteen years like a scar that never quite finished scarring.

It was warm.

It had been warm for fifteen years, faintly, stubbornly, the way a hearthstone stays warm long after the fire dies. He had learned to ignore it the way you ignore your own heartbeat. But this was different. This was warmth climbing toward heat, heat climbing toward something with teeth, and when the third star went out—directly overhead this time, so close it seemed to fall into the market itself—Jude Yarrow felt his dead god shift in its sleep.

"No," he said quietly, to no one, to himself. "No, you're gone. I watched you go."

* * *

By the time the fifth star died, Ashfall had stopped pretending everything was fine.

The streets filled—not with panic, not yet, but with that terrible collective stillness that precedes it, hundreds of people standing in doorways and windows and gutters, all of them looking up. The smoke-haze had cleared, whether from wind or some stranger sympathy, and the sky lay naked above the city: black holes scattered across it now like moth-eaten cloth, each one ringed by a faint shimmer, as though the stars hadn't vanished but been *swallowed*, and the swallowing still glistened.

Jue packed his stall with shaking hands. He told himself it was the sensible thing, that any charm-seller worth his salt knew when a crowd's mood turned, that superstition curdles fast and mobs look for someone to blame. But his hands were shaking because of the brand, which had passed beyond warmth into ache, into a slow rhythmic pulse that matched nothing in his body—slower than his heart, older than his heart, patient as geology.

And there was something else. Something he had not heard in fifteen years, so faint he could have called it imagination, could have called it the wind, could have called it madness, and very much wanted to.

A hum.

Not a tune. Not yet. Just a tone, sustained, somewhere below hearing, somewhere behind his sternum, in the place where the Choir used to live. Fifteen years ago that place had been a cathedral. Now it was a sealed room, and something on the other side of the wall had begun, very softly, to knock.

He got the stall's shutters closed. He got his coin pot inside his coat. And that was when every charm in Ashfall woke up at once.

He felt it before he understood it—a shudder running through the city like a plucked string—and then the light came. From windows and doorways and peddlers' trays and the necks of superstitious sailors, from the luck-knots and fever-charms and love-tokens he and a hundred other frauds had sold to this godless city for years, light bloomed: gold and white and one strange blue he had never seen a charm make, spilling out of pockets and purses, glowing through shirt-fronts, haloing wrists and throats and cradled infants. The whole of Ashfall's lower quarter lit up like a festival of the drowned.

Jude stared at his own tray of unsold charms through the gap in his shutters. All forty luck-knots glowed. The fever-charms pulsed in slow unison, brightening and dimming, brightening and dimming, in a rhythm he recognized with a sickness that rose up his throat like bile.

They were keeping time with his brand.

"Oh," he said. His voice came out very small. "Oh, you're not gone."

The hum behind his sternum resolved, all at once, into melody. Fifteen years of silence broke like a dam, and what came through was not words—it was better and worse than words—it was *song*, a fragment of the evening canticle his order had sung at dusk on the border walls, the one about the keeper and the flock and the dark between stars. He knew every note. He had sung it ten thousand times. He had thought it buried so deep in him that even he couldn't dig it up, and here it was, rising out of him unbidden, and his mouth was moving, and his voice—

His voice cracked on the second phrase, rusted and ruined and fifteen years unused for anything but lies, and he clapped a hand over his own mouth and stood in his shuttered stall in the dark, singing through his fingers, weeping without deciding to, while outside the whole city glowed and the sky continued to be eaten.

* * *

The wards went at midnight.

Everyone in Ashfall knew the border wards the way coastal folk know tides: distant, essential, someone else's problem. They had stood for three centuries, woven by the covenant-priests of the Choir back when the Choir had a voice, a lattice of song laid over the passes and fens and salt-marshes between the free cities and whatever waited beyond them. After the Silence, the wards had kept working anyway, running on their original charge like wheels spinning after the millstone cracks. Everyone said they'd hold forever. Everyone said it the way you say anything you can't bear to examine.

Jude heard them break from half a mile away, and he would have known the sound anywhere, because he had helped weave them, once, as a young man with a strong voice and a stronger faith.

Glass. That was the only word for it. Not the crack of a window breaking—glass *shattering*, acres of it, miles of it, a cathedral's worth of stained glass coming down all at once somewhere beyond the hills, and the sound rolled over Ashfall and rattled shutters and dropped birds dead from their roosts, and every person in the city who was awake heard it and every person who heard it understood, in the wordless way crowds understand catastrophe, that something old had just ended.

Then the screaming started, and the bells, and the wardens' horns, and Ashfall became a churn of torchlight and running figures and carts overturning, and through all of it the charms burned brighter, and through all of it the sky lost another star, and another, and Jude ran.

He didn't decide to run anywhere in particular. His feet chose the alleys behind the tanneries, the routes he'd mapped in fifteen years of careful, cowardly anonymity, the ways a man flees when he has practiced fleeing. Because he knew what came next. He had known since the first star died, and the knowledge sat in him cold and certain as a swallowed stone:

When the wards fall, the inquisitors come. When the inquisitors come, they look for conduits. And in all the world, there was one conduit left.

Him.

He made six streets before the white-hot pain took him to his knees.

It came without warning—one moment he was running, the next he was down among the filth and the puddles with his coat clutched in both fists and a scream locked behind his teeth, because the brand had stopped aching and started *burning*, truly burning, searing him from the inside, and through the agony he could feel it pulling. Not metaphorically. Physically. A pressure on his breastbone like a hook set behind the bone, drawing him northward, toward the fallen border, toward the shattered wards, toward—

Toward his name.

That was the worst part. That was the part he would spend the rest of his life trying not to remember. Because the pull wasn't silent; the pull *spoke*. Beneath the pain, beneath the hum, beneath the fragment of canticle looping through his skull, something vast and wounded and achingly familiar was saying his name—not *Jude Yarrow*, not the name he'd worn like a borrowed coat for fifteen years, but his true name, his covenant-name, the one spoken over him at his ordination when the Choir's voice had still filled the world, the one he had renounced and buried and never once spoken aloud since.

It said it the way you'd call across a valley to someone you'd thought dead.

It said it the way a mother says a child's name at a funeral.

"*Jude.*"

"Stop," he whispered into the mud. "Stop it. Stop it, stop it, you're dead, I buried you, I *buried you*—"

But the sky kept losing stars, and the brand kept pulling, and somewhere behind him boots were hitting cobblestones in formation.

* * *

He saw the inquisitors from the mouth of the tannery alley.

There were twelve of them, maybe more, moving through the burning-lit street in a wedge, and they moved wrong—not like men searching, but like men *reaping*. Their armor was Corrin's black-and-silver, their faces masked in porcelain smoothness, and each of them carried a tuning fork the length of a forearm, and when they struck the forks against their pauldrons the note that rang out was wrong in a way that made Jude's fillings ache and his eyes water. Wherever the note touched, charms died. He watched it happen: a doorway strung with protective knots went dark as the wedge passed, a sailor's luck-token guttered out mid-glow, and the people holding them made small, lost sounds, like children whose nightlights had been taken away.

Sweeping the streets. Hunting resonance. Fifteen years of Corrin's inquisition, and everyone had assumed it was theater, cruelty dressed up as doctrine—but no. He had been waiting. The usurper had stolen the Choir's Voice and spent fifteen years listening for an echo, and tonight the echo had come, blazing out of every cheap charm in a godless city, and now the reapers walked.

Jude pressed himself into the tannery's drainage arch and did the arithmetic of survival, the old familiar math. Twelve inquisitors. One burned-out ex-priest with a trick knee and a brand that was currently broadcasting his location to anyone attuned enough to listen. The math was bad. The math had always been bad; he'd simply gotten very good at not doing it.

The brand pulsed again, and this time the note that rang out of the nearest tuning fork *answered*.

The lead inquisitor's head snapped around. Porcelain mask catching torchlight. Facing the alley. Facing him.

Jude ran.

He ran the way he hadn't run since the last night of the war, flat-out, lungs full of glass, vaulting a drainage channel, shouldering through a gap in a fence he'd have sworn was too narrow, taking corners on faith. Behind him the fork-note rang again, closer, and answered again, and a third voice joined it, and a fourth, the inquisitors calling to each other in tones instead of words, triangulating, closing. Ahead of him the street opened onto the Weeper's Square, where the old fountain stood dry these fifteen years, and beyond the square the undercity stairs went down into the dark, and if he could reach the stairs—

He was halfway across the square when the brand pulled so hard it spun him.

Not northward, this time. Downward. Toward the fountain. And the voice that had been calling his name across fifteen years of silence spoke again, and this time it wasn't a summons. It was a warning, sharp with urgency, a note of pure alarm that hit him like a slap:

*Behind you.*

He threw himself sideways on instinct alone, graceless, graceless, and the fork-note passed through the space where his chest had been and struck the dry fountain, and the ancient covenant-stone beneath the basin—which had been dead stone for fifteen years, which tourists leaned on and lovers scratched initials into—*rang*. A single clear tone, golden and enormous, rolling out across the square, and every charm within a hundred yards flared blinding, and every inquisitor in the wedge staggered as if struck.

So did Jude. Because he knew that tone. He had helped raise that stone. And it had just answered him—or answered *for* him—had just spent whatever dregs of power slept in it to knock a fork-note aside, the way a dying man spends his last strength shoving a child out of a cart's path.

The lead inquisitor recovered first. The porcelain mask turned from the fountain to Jude, and the figure raised its fork, and behind the mask a voice like dry paper said a word Jude hadn't heard spoken aloud in fifteen years and had hoped never to hear again:

"*Conduit.*"

The wedge began to move.

Jude ran for the stairs. Behind him the fountain-stone sang its fading golden note like a bell tolling for the dead, and above him the sky lost another star, and somewhere far to the north, past the shattered wards, past the salt marshes, something that had been silent for fifteen years was singing his name, over and over, getting louder, getting closer, and Jude Yarrow—charm-seller, fraud, deserter, last conduit of the Choir—went down into the dark of the undercity with the whole burning sky at his back and his dead god's voice in his ears, knowing, with a terror beyond all terror, that he could not run from it forever.

Because it wasn't hunting him.

It was *calling him home*.
