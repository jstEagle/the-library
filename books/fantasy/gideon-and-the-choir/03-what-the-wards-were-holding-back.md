## 3. What the Wards Were Holding Back

The border had been a wall of light once. Sera Vane remembered it from her childhood—her mother lifting her onto her shoulders so she could see the shimmer where the free cities met the Empire, a curtain of pale gold rising from the earth into the sky like the aurora made obedient. The wardens had called it the Covenant's Hem, stitched by conduits in the old days, renewed every generation, humming so faintly you felt it in your teeth before you heard it.

Now she stood on a ridge of black rock and looked at the place where it had been.

There was nothing there. That was the horror of it—not rubble, not ruin, but absence. The air where the Hem had hung was simply air, and through that air she could see the Imperial watchtowers on the far side, their signal-fires already lit, already talking to each other in the old tongue of smoke. The border was not broken. It was *gone*, the way a voice is gone when a throat is cut.

The shard against her sternum pulsed once, warm as a swallowed coal.

*Not now,* she told it. *Not here.*

She had been running for eleven days. Her boots had been good leather when she started; now they were a memory of leather held together with wire and prayer, and her feet inside them were worse. She had crossed the Saltmarsh by night, wading chest-deep through water that smelled of iron and rot, because the shard could not be submerged—it screamed, silently, in a register only she could feel—and she had learned on the second day that if she let it touch open water, fish floated up around her, dead, their small bodies arched as though they had been singing when they died.

Behind her, somewhere in the darkening east, Inquisitor Maren was looking for her.

Sera did not let herself think about Maren. Thinking about Maren was a door she had nailed shut, and she had nailed it shut with the part of herself that still wept at night, and if she opened it even a crack the whole wall would come down, and she could not afford walls coming down. Not yet. Maybe not ever.

She adjusted the strap of her pack—the false bottom, the papers identifying her as a wool merchant named Cora Tessel of Meridian, the vial of ash she carried for reasons she no longer fully remembered—and began to descend toward the lowlands, toward the road, toward Ashfall.

* * *

Ashfall announced itself an hour before she reached it: a smudge of woodsmoke on a sky the color of dirty pewter, then the smell—coal and tanneries and the particular sourness of ten thousand people living too close together and liking it no better than they had to. It was the largest city this side of the fallen wards, which meant it was the largest city that was about to learn what the wards had been holding back, and its people did not know it yet. They were buying bread. Arguing about grain prices. Hanging lanterns for the evening market.

Sera walked through the south gate with her merchant's papers and her merchant's slouch and her heart going like a trapped bird, and no one looked at her twice. That was the thing about grief and terror done properly: they made you invisible. She had spent fifteen years learning how to be nobody, first in the Choir's cloisters, where ambition was punished by silence, and then after, in the Empire, where being somebody got people killed.

The shard shifted under her ribs. It did that when she lied, even to herself.

*I'm not lying,* she thought. *I'm resting.*

It pulsed again, patient, unconvinced. Fifteen years of carrying it and she had learned its moods the way sailors learn weather. It wanted to go home. It wanted, with a longing so vast it made her dizzy, to be given back—or given forward, she supposed, to whoever came next. It was a fragment of the Voice of the Choir, torn out of a god's throat by a man who had decided gods were inefficient, and everything it wanted was impossible, and it never stopped wanting, and some nights Sera lay awake in whatever ditch or stable or rented room sheltered her and understood that she and the shard were the same kind of creature: something stolen, still singing, with nowhere left to sing.

She found a room above a dye-shop in the Tanners' Quarter, paid too much for it, and slept four hours with the shard wrapped in wool and pressed between two floorboards, because sleeping with it against her skin gave her dreams of the Spire, and the dreams were worse than the running.

* * *

In the morning she went hunting rumors.

This was the part of the work she hated most, and she was good at it, which she hated more. You did not ask questions in Ashfall; asking questions was how inquisitors found you. Instead you listened. You sat in the right taverns—the ones near the old temple district, half-abandoned now, where the godless and the nostalgic drank side by side—and you bought rounds slowly and said almost nothing, and eventually someone always talked.

By midday she had three rumors, and one of them made her hands shake so badly she had to set down her cup.

The first rumor was ordinary: the wardens had doubled the night patrols since the stars went strange, and there was talk of a curfew. The second was stranger: charms were working again. All over the city, luck-knots and hearth-blessings and the little tin amulets sold by street-corner charm-sellers—junk, fraud, the dried husks of a dead faith—had begun to *do things*. A fisherman's storm-charm had turned back a squall. A widow's mourning-cord had gone warm in her hand. The wardens were confiscating them, which meant the wardens were frightened, which meant the wardens knew something.

But the third rumor—

The third rumor came from a one-eyed carter who had drunk enough honey-wine to mistake Sera's silence for sympathy. He leaned across the table, breath like a fermenting orchard, and said:

"They took a charm-man in last night. Off Tanner's Row. Young fellow, sells those knots and blessings, everybody knows him, harmless as bread." He tapped the table with a knuckle like a hammer. "Except his brand lit up. White-hot, they say. Right through his shirt. And he didn't scream, mind—he *sang*. One note. Just one, like a struck bell, and every lamp on the row went out at once, and the wardens had to carry him away because he couldn't stand."

Sera kept her face still. She had been trained, once, in the keeping of faces; the Choir taught its singers that the mouth was a gate and everything behind it was treasure, and treasure unguarded invited thieves. But beneath the table, her hand closed around the edge of her bench hard enough to bruise her palm.

"Charm-man," she repeated, mild as milk.

"Jude Yarrow," the carter said, pleased to be the center of something. "Used to be a battle-priest, they say, back before the Silence. Deserted, some say. Survived, others say. Nobody says anything to his face."

*Fifteen years,* Sera thought. *Fifteen years, and I find him in a cell.*

Because it had to be him. She had buried too many conduits to hope easily—that was the truth she carried like a stone in her boot, worn smooth by repetition—but the signs were unmistakable, stacked one atop another until they formed a shape she recognized from her worst nightmares and her one wild dream. A brand burning white after fifteen years of cold. A voice that put out lamps. A man who had survived the purges by becoming small, becoming nothing, selling hollow charms in a godless city—and whose hollow charms had started working the moment the stars went out.

The conduit was alive. The last conduit of the Choir was alive, and he was in a warden's cell in Ashfall, and every hour he spent there was an hour closer to Corrin's inquisitors finding him, and if they found him—

If they found him, they would not kill him. That was the thing people didn't understand about Vael Corrin. Killing a conduit was waste. Corrin would take him apart the way you take apart a lock, patiently, piece by piece, to learn how the mechanism worked, and then he would rebuild him into a key.

Sera finished her wine without tasting it, thanked the carter for his company, and went out into the gray afternoon to plan a rescue she had no right to believe would succeed.

* * *

She had buried nine conduits.

She counted them that evening, sitting in the dark of her rented room with the shard unwrapped and glowing faintly against her palm like a chip of captive dawn, because despair was a country she knew the map of, and she preferred to know exactly where she stood in it.

The first had been Brother Aldous, in the second year, a fat gentle man who had hidden in a monastery and been betrayed by his own abbot. They had burned him in the square at Vail, and the fire had sung, and everyone who heard it had wept without knowing why, and Sera had been in the crowd in a borrowed coat, and she had not looked away, because looking away was how the forgetting started.

Then Marta of the Fenlands, drowned in her own well. Then the twins, Ilse and Roderic, who had lasted four days past the fall of Highmoor. Then old Tam Broadfoot, who had fought—gods, how he had fought, taking six inquisitors with him, and Sera had arrived too late and buried what was left and taken the shard from his ashes, the third shard, the one she carried now. Then a girl of nineteen whose name Sera had made herself forget because remembering her was a wound that never scabbed. Then two more whose faces blurred together in the long gray years. Then Callen, last winter, in the snow outside Corrin's own capital, who had died whispering that he was sorry, sorry, sorry, though for what, he never said, and Sera had knelt beside him in the drifts and held his hand until it cooled and thought: *I will be next, and it will be a mercy.*

Nine conduits. Nine funerals conducted in ditches and ruins and one memorable drainage culvert, each grave dug by her own hands because no one else could be trusted, each marked with nothing, because marks were how inquisitors found the next one. She had gotten good at digging. She had gotten good at the words you said over a body when the god they served was silent and the heaven they'd earned was sealed. She had gotten so good at burying hope that she sometimes mistook the habit for peace.

And now the tenth was alive, in a cell, singing one note that put out lamps.

*Don't,* she told herself, the way she always told herself. *Don't build the house before you've checked the ground.* Hope was a structure, and structures collapsed, and she had been under enough of them. The carter might have garbled it. The wardens might have already shipped him east. It might be a trap—Corrin's inquisitors used living bait the way fishermen used worms, and Sera Vane, defector, thief of shards, was a fish worth catching.

But the shard was pulsing against her palm, faster now, brighter, and it had never done that before, not in fifteen years, not over graves or rivers or the smoking ruins of safehouses. It was pulling. South and slightly west, toward the warden's keep on Tanner's Row, steady as a compass needle finding north.

Toward him.

"Oh," Sera said aloud, to the empty room, to the shard, to the silenced god whose voice she carried in a scrap of wool. "Oh, you *do* remember your own."

The shard flared, and for one impossible moment she heard it—not felt it, *heard* it—a single thread of harmony, high and clear, threading through the dark of the room like light through a keyhole. It sounded like the cloisters at morning. It sounded like the first rehearsal of her life, when she was seven and terrified and the choirmaster had touched her shoulder and said, *you have a voice, child; the question is what it will be for.*

It sounded like the beginning of an answer.

Sera closed her fist around the shard until the light went out, and her eyes were wet, and she was furious about it, furious at the shard and the god and the dead man in the cell and most of all at herself, because she had promised—she had sworn on nine graves—that she would not do this again, would not let the wanting precede the knowing, would not run toward a heartbeat before confirming it wasn't a snare.

She wiped her face with the heel of her hand. She wrapped the shard. She took out her merchant's papers and studied the seal of Cora Tessel of Meridian, wool merchant, licensed to travel, and beneath it, in the false bottom, the other tools: the picks, the thin blade, the vial of ash, the ring of warden's keys she had lifted off a drunk sergeant in Vail three years ago and never needed until now.

The wardens' keep on Tanner's Row was not Vail. It was a squat granite block built to hold smugglers and debtors, garrisoned by men who had signed on to collect taxes and break up brawls, not to guard the last ember of a murdered god. There would be maybe forty of them. There would be records, and records meant paperwork, and paperwork meant time, and time was the one currency Sera had never had enough of—but tonight, tonight the stars were wrong and the charms were waking and somewhere in the Empire's east, Inquisitor Maren was reading reports of a border that had simply ceased to exist, and doing arithmetic.

Sera did the same arithmetic, and it came out the same way it always did: *not enough days.* So it would have to be tonight.

She dressed as Cora Tessel, plain and forgettable, and then she dressed over that as nobody, in the grays and browns that made watchers' eyes slide off, and she stood a moment in the doorway of the little room with the shard warm against her breastbone and the city breathing below her window, ten thousand people who did not know that the thing holding back the Empire's armies had just died, that the sky itself had been pickpocketed, that salvation was currently locked in a cell on Tanner's Row wearing a fraud's cheap charms and probably hating himself for surviving, again, the way survivors do.

"Hold on," she said quietly—to him, to it, to whichever of them was listening. "I've buried nine of you. I don't have another grave in me."

The shard pulsed once, soft, like a hand squeezing hers.

She went out into the dark, and the door clicked shut behind her, and above the rooftops of Ashfall the starless sky waited, vast and wounded, for what its last singer would decide to do.
