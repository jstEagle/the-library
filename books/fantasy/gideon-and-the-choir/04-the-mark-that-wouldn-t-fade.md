## 4. The Mark That Wouldn't Fade

They took his boots first.

Jude never understood that—the ritual of it. Two wardens with iron-shod staves and the flat gray faces of men paid by the hour, and before they asked a single question they crouched at his feet like cobblers and pulled off his boots with careful, almost tender hands. Then the belt. Then the little knife he kept in his sleeve for cutting twine, which was all it had ever been used for, gods honest. They laid everything out on a tray as if cataloguing a dead man's estate.

"Stand," said the taller one.

He stood. The cell they put him in was under the Warden's Hall, three floors of stone between him and the burning sky, and even down here he could feel it—that pressure behind his ribs, like a second heartbeat that wasn't his. The brand on his chest had not stopped singing since the stars went out. It wasn't pain, exactly. Pain would have been simpler. It was *attention*, the sensation of being looked at from inside his own skin.

The lamp in the corner guttered once when they shut the door, and Jude could have sworn the flame leaned toward him, like a dog toward its master's hand.

*Stop,* he told it silently. *Lie down.*

It didn't. Nothing had obeyed him in fifteen years, and now, now, when obedience meant an inquisitor's pyre—

*Now* it wanted to listen.

He sat on the bench bolted to the wall and did what he'd done through every bad night of his life since the Choir fell silent: he made himself small. He thought about his stall in the Tanners' Row market, the faded awning, the tray of luck-knots and hearth-blessings and traveler's wards that were, he had told himself so long he almost believed it, nothing but string and bone and a good sales patter. He thought about the boy with the chipped tooth who'd bought the knot yesterday. He thought about how the knot had worked, and how that single fact sat in his stomach like a swallowed coal.

Because if the charms worked—if the old covenant still ran underneath the world like water under ice—then it hadn't died. And if it hadn't died, then everything Jude had done to survive its death was built on a lie, and he had no idea yet whether that was mercy or damnation.

The door opened after what might have been an hour or a day. Down here, time moved like syrup.

The warden who entered was different from the ones who'd arrested him. Older. A woman with gray shot through her black braid and a scar bisecting one eyebrow, wearing the deep blue sash of a senior interrogator rather than the street-wardens' dun. She carried a chair under one arm, set it down across from him without ceremony, and sat with her hands folded on her knee.

"You're the charm-seller," she said. "Yarrow."

"That's me." He kept his voice easy, market-day warm. "Though if this is about the tax stamp, I can assure you—"

"It isn't about the tax stamp." She studied him the way a butcher studies a carcass, deciding where to begin. "Last night every ward-stone in Ashfall flared at once. Every one. Stones that haven't held a charge in fifteen years lit up like festival lanterns and burned themselves to slag. Do you know what that means?"

"I imagine it means someone sold you very old stones," Jude said. "I could recommend—"

"Eleven people died when the border wards came down." Her voice didn't rise. That was worse. "A caravan out of Hollowmere. The things that had been pressing against those wards for fifteen years came through in the space of a heartbeat, and eleven people learned exactly what we've been paying to keep out. So you'll forgive me if I'm not in the mood for your patter."

Something cold walked down Jude's spine. He'd seen the sky go dark. He'd felt the brand ignite. But he'd spent the whole night telling himself it was distant, abstract, a storm on the horizon. Eleven people. A name attached to the horror now. Hollowmere.

"I'm sorry," he said, and meant it, which surprised him.

"So am I." She leaned forward. "Here's my problem, Yarrow. When the wards went down, my officers swept the streets, as is procedure. And in the crowd outside your shuttered stall, they found something interesting. A man on his knees in the gutter, holding his chest, weeping. When they helped him up, he begged them—not for help, mind—for forgiveness. Said he was sorry, over and over, sorry to whom he wouldn't say. And when they brought him here, every lamp he passed flickered. Every ward-stone in this building hummed. My senior stone-reader says the resonance pattern matches nothing in our records except—" she paused, delicately, the way one names a disease, "—except the old texts. Pre-Silence texts."

The brand pulsed against Jude's sternum, warm as a hand pressed flat. *Be still,* he begged it. *Please.*

"That sounds like a sick man," he said. "Grief does strange things. Half this city saw the stars die; I'd wager more than one of them wept in a gutter."

"Mm." She reached into her coat and produced a small object wrapped in cloth, unwrapped it on her palm: a shard of ward-stone, blackened, cracked—and at its heart, a thread of light that pulsed. In rhythm. Jude realized with a lurch that it was pulsing in rhythm *with him.*

"This was recovered from the Warden's Gate," she said. "Watch."

She held it up. The light swelled. Jude's brand answered, heat blooming across his chest like spilled wine, and he clenched his jaw against the gasp that wanted out.

"There it is," the interrogator said softly. "Every time. Like calling to like. You want to tell me what you are, Yarrow?"

"A seller of trinkets," he said, and his voice only shook a little. "String and bone. Ask anyone on Tanners' Row."

"String and bone." She turned the shard in her fingers. "You know what happens next, don't you? This goes to the capital. There are people there—Corrin's people, inquisitors—who have been waiting fifteen years for a resonance like yours. They have instruments for it. Instruments I've heard described by men who came back gray-faced and wouldn't say more. If I send you east, you will wish the things beyond the border had found you instead."

"Then don't send me east," Jude said. "Send me home. Confiscate my stock. Fine me. I'll close the stall, move to Meridian, take up fishing—"

"Why?" she cut in. Lean forward, eyes sharp. "Why did you hide it? Fifteen years. Whatever you are, whatever mark you carry—you hid it through the purges, through the outlawry, through all of it. A man doesn't hold his breath that long unless he's terrified. So tell me what frightens you more than my chair, Yarrow. Tell me what's coming."

And there it was, the question he'd been avoiding since the first star winked out, since the brand woke and began dragging him by the ribs toward something calling his true name in a voice like a thousand throats underwater. Because he knew what frightened him. What frightened him was that the silence was breaking, and he didn't know if that meant salvation or the resumption of a war the world had barely survived ending.

"I don't know what you mean," he said.

She sighed, and for a moment she just looked tired—a woman with a scar and a braid and too many dead caravans. "I believe you," she said, standing. "That's the worst part. You genuinely don't know. Get some rest. We'll continue tomorrow."

At the door she paused. "For what it's worth—I've read the old texts too. All of them we could save. And if there's even a chance the Choir isn't gone..." She didn't finish it. The door closed. The lock turned.

Jude sat alone in the humming dark, his brand burning like a swallowed star, and laughed until it turned into something else entirely.

* * *

Sleep came in fragments, each one punctured by the same dream: a battlefield, mud the color of rust, and a song cut off mid-note. Fifteen years and the dream never changed. Fifteen years and last night it had changed completely—the silence in it had *cracked*, and through the crack came light, and he'd woken with tears on his face and the taste of hymns in his mouth.

He was still tasting them when the wall opened.

There was no other way to describe it. The stones simply grew soft, the mortar going dark and pliant as wet clay, and a section of the wall swung inward like a door that had always been there, and a woman stepped through carrying a hooded lamp.

She was younger than the interrogator, mid-thirties maybe, with the lean, road-worn look of someone who'd walked a long way on short rations. Dust grayed her traveling cloak to the color of ashfall itself. Her hair was cropped short, practical, and her eyes—her eyes were doing something Jude had never seen eyes do. They were moving across him the way a musician reads a score.

"Oh," she breathed. "Oh, it's *true.*"

"Guards," Jude said, at conversational volume. "GUARDS."

"They won't come." She set the lamp on the floor and knelt, pulling a stoppered flask and a coil of thin copper wire from her pack. "I dosed the watch rotation's tea an hour ago. We have perhaps forty minutes, and I intend to use all of them, so please—stop shouting and let me look at you."

"Look at me?" Jude pressed himself into the corner. "Who in the hells are you?"

"Sera." She said it like a key turning. "Sera Venn. Late of the Corrinite Conservatory, First Chair, Voice-Bearers' Order." Her mouth twisted on the title. "Currently: deserter, heretic, and the only person alive who knows what's happening to you."

"Nothing is happening to me."

"Your brand is visible from across the room," she said. "In the dark. Did you know that? I watched it through the peephole for two hours before I started digging. It glows, conduit. Faintly, like coals under ash—but it glows. Fifteen years of hiding and the moment the Silence cracks, you light up like a beacon. Which means the inquisitors' instruments will find you too, and unlike me, they won't bother digging through a wall. They'll dig through the city."

The word landed like a dropped stone. *Conduit.* Jude hadn't heard it spoken aloud since—since the last time, since a dying battle-priest had gripped his wrist in the mud and pressed two fingers to his sternum and whispered *you carry it now, boy, carry it far, carry it quiet.* He'd been twenty-two. He'd run. He'd been running ever since.

"I don't know what you're talking about," he said, but it came out hoarse, and they both heard it.

Sera didn't gloat. That was the strange thing. She just nodded slowly, as if he'd confirmed a diagnosis she'd already made, and uncorked the flask. "Drink this. It'll dampen the resonance for six hours, maybe eight. Enough to get us out of the city."

"*Us?*"

"I'm not leaving without you." She held out the flask. "You're the last one. Do you understand what I'm saying? I've spent three years following rumors. A hedge-priest in the Salt Marches—dead before I got there, inquisitors took him. Twin girls in Callow's Ford—dead, their own neighbors gave them up for a pardon. An old woman in the Drowned Quarter of Meridian who turned out to be faking it for alms." Her voice stayed level, but her hands weren't quite steady around the flask. "I have buried every conduit I ever found, Jude Yarrow. Most of them in pieces. You are the last living thread of the Choir, and I am *done* watching threads get cut."

The name again. His name, spoken plainly, in a cellar, by a stranger. It should have felt like a blade at his throat. Instead it felt like rain after a drought—terrifying, and desperately wanted.

"How do you know my name?"

"The Choir told me." She said it without embarrassment, without drama, the way one reports the weather. "Or what's left of it. A fragment. I carried a shard of the stolen Voice out of the Conservatory three years ago, and sometimes it... sings to me. Fragments. Names. Yours was the loudest thing it's sung since I left." She looked at him then, really looked, and her eyes were wet and furious. "Do you know what it's like, hearing a god through a keyhole? Hearing one voice of ten thousand, stolen, chained, forced to sing war-marches for that *man*—and knowing somewhere out there the rest of the choir is waiting? Silent? Waiting for a throat to sing through?"

Jude's back hit the stone wall. The brand was blazing now, past concealment, past denial, and the ghost-light of it limned Sera's face as she knelt there with her offered flask, and he hated her for it, suddenly and completely, because she had said the thing out loud that he had spent fifteen years not saying.

"There is no Choir," he said. "It died. It died at Redford Field and everyone who sang for it died with it and I—" His voice cracked. He mastered it. "I survived. That's all I am. A survivor selling string and bone."

"String and bone that worked." Sera's head tilted. "The luck-knot. The boy bought it yesterday morning. It shouldn't have worked—it was cheap twine, no rites, no blessing. But it worked, didn't it? Because the covenant is waking up, and it's reaching for the nearest hand that ever held it. That's *you.* That has only ever been you."

"Then it can reach for someone else."

"There is no one else!" For the first time her composure fractured, and what was underneath was raw and exhausted and grieving. "Don't you understand? That's why the stars went out. That's why the wards failed. The covenant isn't waking because it's strong—it's waking because it's *dying*, truly dying, and what's left of it is spending its last strength trying to reach its conduit before Corrin's machines find you first. You aren't being hunted by accident, Jude. You're being hunted because you're the only door left, and both sides know it."

Silence. The lamp flame stood perfectly straight, leaning toward him.

Jude looked at his hands. Charm-seller's hands, twine-callused, ink-stained from price tags. Once they had traced sigils in consecrated oil and the air had rung like a struck bell. He had told himself the story for fifteen years: *the god is dead, the war is over, survival is enough.* And beneath the story, the brand had waited, patient as a seed under snow.

"What do you want from me?" he asked finally. Quietly.

"Escape. Tonight. There's a drainage culvert under the east foundation that lets out past the tanneries; I've already loosened the grate." She ticked it off practically, a soldier's list. "Then north, to Meridian. The free cities have an archive—hidden, pre-Silence. Rites. Training. And a council that might, *might*, be persuaded to shelter you if I can convince them you're worth more breathing than bartered."

"And if they decide otherwise?"

"Then I'll break you out of that too," she said, "and we'll keep running. But Jude—" she leaned forward, and the ghost-light of his own brand haloed them both, conspirators in a crime against the age, "—there are answers in Meridian. Why the Choir fell silent. What Corrin actually stole. What the covenant asks of the one who reforges it. Everything you've spent fifteen years afraid to ask. I can give you those answers."

"And if I don't want them?"

Her expression gentled into something almost pitying. "Then you're lying to yourself worse than you lied to your customers. You wept in a gutter last night begging forgiveness. Men who want the past to stay dead don't do that."

The words went in like a splinter, deep, working toward the heart. Jude closed his eyes. Behind them: mud the color of rust, a song cut off mid-note, a dying man's fingers on his sternum. *Carry it far. Carry it quiet.* He had carried it quiet. He had carried it so quiet he'd nearly convinced himself it wasn't there at all.

And last night it had called his true name, and some traitorous part of him had *turned toward the sound.*

"If I go with you," he said slowly, opening his eyes, "it's not allegiance. It's not faith. I'm not your conduit or your chosen anything. I'm a man who doesn't want to burn, and you're the exit."

"Fine," Sera said.

"And if it comes to it—if the inquisitors corner us, if it's you or me—I won't hesitate. I've done worse to survive."

Something flickered across her face at that, old and private, a grief with its own history. "So have I," she said. "Drink the draft. We have thirty minutes."

He took the flask. The draught tasted of iron and chamomile, and as it went down, the brand's blaze dimmed—not extinguished, never that, but banked, like a fire covered for the night. For the first time in a day and a half, Jude could breathe.

Sera was already at the softened wall, feeding the copper wire along its seams, murmuring under her breath—and Jude froze, because he recognized the melody. Four notes, rising, falling. A fragment of evensong. The one the novices used to sing while trimming the altar lamps.

She caught him staring. "Habit," she said, a little sheepishly. "It helps the working. Old songs do."

"Don't," Jude said. His voice came out rougher than he intended. "Just—don't sing that. Not yet."

Sera held his gaze for a long moment. Then she nodded, once, and switched to a counting rhyme, profane and tuneless, and the wall finished opening with a grinding sigh.

Cold air moved through from somewhere below, smelling of river and tannery rot and freedom. Jude Yarrow, charm-seller, deserter, last conduit of a silenced god, pulled on his confiscated boots—the wardens had left them on a shelf by the door, and Sera had thoughtfully retrieved them—and followed her into the dark.

Behind them, faint through the closing stone, the lamp flame leaned after him like something waving goodbye.

Above ground, far to the east, the first instruments of Inquisitor Maren were already humming.
