## 5. Fifteen Years of Small Lies

The undercity of Ashfall smelled of wet stone and old smoke, the way it always had—the way it had smelled the night he first came down here with a stolen name and a brand he'd tried to burn out of his own arm. Jude moved through the dark with Sera's hand locked around his wrist, her grip iron despite her exhaustion, and told himself that the shaking in his legs was only the cold.

It was not the cold.

* * *

Fifteen years ago there had been a field called Vessany's Ford, though no map still carried the name, because maps were drawn by the living and the living had agreed to forget what happened there.

Jude remembered it in pieces, the way a man remembers a wound: not whole, but by its edges. The dawn mist lying low over the river like a shroud someone had laid down and forgotten to finish. The Choir standing on the ridge behind them—two hundred voices in grey robes, singing the covenant-song that turned fear into iron and iron into purpose. He had been twenty-three years old and newly sworn, the mark still tender on his forearm, and when the choir sang he could feel every note in his teeth, in the roots of his hair, in the small bones of his hands. That was what being a conduit meant. You were the door the music walked through.

The enemy came out of the mist at second bell.

And the Choir went silent.

Not faded. Not faltered. *Silent*—mid-phrase, mid-breath, the way a throat closes around a scream. Two hundred voices cut off at once, and the silence that followed was worse than any sound Jude had ever heard, because it wasn't empty. It was full. It pressed against his eardrums like deep water, like something enormous holding its breath, and every conduit on that field felt it at the same moment and knew, without words, without doctrine, that their god had stopped speaking.

He remembered Brother Callum turning toward the ridge, mouth open, no sound coming out. He remembered Sister Aveline falling to her knees in the mud, clawing at her own ears as if she could dig the silence out. He remembered the enemy lines surging forward because they felt it too—they felt the covenant break—and the battle going from a battle to a slaughter in the space of four heartbeats.

He remembered running.

That was the piece he kept wrapped in cloth at the bottom of himself, the piece he took out only at night, alone, the way other men took out a knife to reopen an old scar. The Choir had gone silent and the conduits had died where they stood—not all at once, but quickly, the enemy's blades finding them unguarded, their god's protection withdrawn—and Jude Yarrow, who had sworn his voice and his life to the god of the covenant, had turned his back on his kneeling brothers and sisters and run into the mist with his sword still sheathed.

He had not looked back. That was the detail that would not wash out. Not once. He could have looked back. He chose the mist instead.

* * *

"Left here." Sera's whisper came sharp against his ear. "There's a cistern channel ahead. It runs under the tannery district."

"You've been down here before," he managed.

"I've been everywhere before." She pulled him left. "Keep moving. The wardens will have found your cell empty by now, and Ashfall's wardens are slow but they're thorough, and thorough men with torches are worse than fast ones."

They waded into the channel. The water came up to their knees and moved like something reluctant, greasy with tannin, and the dark above them was stitched with the occasional grate through which fell thin bars of lamplight and the sounds of a city pretending nothing had happened. But everything had happened. The stars were still gone—he could feel their absence like a missing tooth, his tongue worrying the gap of the sky—and somewhere above them every charm in Ashfall was burning or dead or screaming, and somewhere beyond the fallen border the thing that had silenced his god was learning that its silence had cracked.

His brand ached under his sleeve. Not burned now—ached, the way an old break aches before rain. As if it were listening too.

* * *

For the first year after Vessany's Ford, he had told himself he'd run because he was ordered to. There had been a moment—he clung to this, built a small house on it and lived in it—when Sister Aveline, bleeding from the mouth, had grabbed his ankle and gasped something. *Go*, perhaps. Or maybe just his name. In the years after, the memory softened and reshaped itself until it became a command, until he could almost believe that dying woman had spent her last breath releasing him from his vows rather than reaching for him the way drowning people reach for anything.

But lies need maintenance. That was the thing no one told you about them. They were not stones you set down once; they were fires you fed, daily, or they guttered and showed you what was underneath.

So he fed them. He told innkeepers he was a carter. He told magistrates he'd been born in a village whose records had conveniently burned. He told the woman in Corvane—he had loved her, or something adjacent to love, for one winter—that he had no family, no history, no god, and watched her decide he was mysterious rather than hollow, which was the kindest lie anyone ever believed about him.

And he told himself, most often and most carefully, that the Choir was dead. That gods died like men did, that Vessany's Ford was a grave and not a wound, that the silence was simply the absence of a voice and not the presence of a gag. Because if the Choir was dead, then his desertion hadn't mattered. If the god was gone, then there had been nothing left to protect, nothing left to betray, and a man who ran from a finished thing was only a survivor, and survivors deserved to eat.

Then came the night the stars went out, and every charm in Ashfall flared like struck matches, and the brand on his arm burned white-hot and began, unmistakably, to pull him toward something calling his true name—a name he had buried so deep he'd nearly convinced himself it belonged to another man.

Dead gods did not call names.

* * *

The channel opened into a vaulted junction where three tunnels met, and Sera finally let him stop. She sagged against the wet wall, breathing hard, and in the grey seep-light Jude got his first real look at her since the cell: the shadows under her eyes like bruises, the fine tremor in her hands that she kept trying to still by pressing them flat against her thighs. She carried a satchel across her chest the way soldiers carried ammunition, and when she caught him looking at it, her arm curled around it, protective.

"What's in the bag?" he asked.

"A shard." She said it like a confession. "Of the Voice. What Corrin stole—or a piece of it, broken off when he took it. I was a singer in his choir-house for six years before I understood what we were really singing." Her jaw tightened. "I've been carrying this across two borders and four hundred miles, looking for rumors of a living conduit. Every rumor was a corpse. Do you understand? I dug seven of them out of graves and ditches and one very dignified tomb in Meridian, and every single time I thought: *this is the last one, this has to be the last one,* and every single time it wasn't."

"And now?"

"Now there's you." She looked at him with those exhausted eyes, and there was no warmth in the look—only assessment, the flat arithmetic of a woman deciding whether a thing was worth more than the cost of carrying it. "A charm-seller in Ashfall with a working luck-knot and a brand that burns when the wards fall. The last conduit of the Choir, hiding in a cellar selling trinkets to grandmothers."

"Don't." The word came out harder than he intended. "Don't call me that."

"It's what you are."

"I'm a man who sells hollow charms to frightened people." His voice was climbing and he couldn't stop it; fifteen years of careful quiet cracking along seams he'd plastered over so many times. "I take copper from widows for knots that don't work. I tell them the old words over them because the old words make them feel safe, and I don't believe a syllable of them, and I sleep fine. That's what I am. Whatever I was—" he pulled back his sleeve, let the brand show, the mark of the covenant glowing faintly in the dark like a coal refusing to die— "that man died at Vessany's Ford with everyone else. I'm what crawled away from the body."

Sera didn't flinch. She looked at the brand for a long moment, and then at him, and something in her face shifted—not softer, exactly. More tired, in a way that went deeper than the road.

"I know what happened at the Ford," she said quietly.

"Everyone knows what happened at the Ford. The Choir broke. The line broke. The free cities wrote songs about it."

"I know what happened to *you*. I read the inquisitors' rolls, Jude. All of them, every purge record Corrin's people kept on the order of conduits. Your name is in eleven of them. Listed among the dead." She paused. "You're listed among the dead because everyone else was dead. Two hundred and six singers and forty-one conduits, and the rolls close the ledger with forty-one names, and yours isn't among them because you weren't there to be killed."

The water moved around his legs. Somewhere far off, through stone and earth, the city muttered in its sleep.

"So say it," he said. His mouth tasted like the channel water, like copper and rot. "Whatever you dragged me out of that cell to say. Say it and be done."

"You want me to call it betrayal."

"Don't you?"

"No." She said it simply, without cruelty, which somehow made it worse. "I call it a price. You paid it. You've been paying it for fifteen years—I watched you flinch when I said the word *conduit* back there. Men who betray cleanly don't flinch. Men who betrayed and got away with it don't spend their lives selling luck-knots to widows because some starved scrap of the covenant in them can't quite stop doing small mercies." She stepped closer, boots splashing. "You ran. Yes. And you've been running ever since, and the running has been its own punishment, and it hasn't bought you a day's peace. That's not absolution. I'm not offering absolution. I'm telling you the ledger isn't closed. The god you ran from is calling your name, and you're going to answer, and answering is going to cost more than running ever did."

"You don't know that."

"I buried seven conduits." Her voice didn't rise at all. "I know exactly how much it costs."

* * *

He thought about Aveline again, as he always did, in the dark places between one breath and the next. Not the version he'd polished into an order—*go, go, save yourself*—but the real one, the one he kept locked below even the memory of running. The real one was this: her hand on his ankle, her eyes finding his through the smoke, and in them not command but *pity*. Pity, and something else. Forgiveness, maybe, already given, already extended toward him like a hand across a table while the blades came down. As if she had known, in the last seconds, what he would do—as if the conduits always knew each other that well—and had forgiven it in advance, which was the cruelest gift anyone had ever handed him, because it meant he'd carried a debt no one was collecting.

He had built fifteen years of small lies on top of that look. He had stacked them like masonry: the false names, the false histories, the dead god, the harmless charms. And tonight, in a flooded tunnel beneath a city whose sky had gone blind, every one of those stones had come loose at once, and underneath them was just a young man in the mist with his sword sheathed, running, and a dying woman's pity on his ankle like a shackle.

"Sera." His voice came out low. "When we get wherever we're going—if the Choir wakes, if any of this works—will it bring them back? The ones at the Ford?"

She was quiet for a long moment. Honest, at least, in her silence.

"No," she said finally.

"Then what am I answering *for*?"

"For everyone who comes after." She shouldered her satchel. "For the villages Corrin's song-taken soldiers leave humming. For the stars. For the next person who'd have to run, if you run again." She turned down the left-hand tunnel, where the dark ran deeper and colder, toward the river roads and the long way to Meridian. "Come on. The wardens' hounds will pick up our scent at the junction, and I'd rather not explain to Ashfall why their prisoner grew a second set of footprints."

Jude stood one moment longer in the black water, in the dark, in the wreckage of his own careful lies. Then he pulled his sleeve down over the burning brand and followed her.

Behind them, faint as breath, the ghost-choir hummed the first three notes of the covenant-song—the same three notes the Choir had been singing when the silence fell—and did not stop, and did not come closer, and waited, with the patience of the dead, for him to learn the rest.
