## 6. The Free Cities Decide

Meridian announced itself by smell before sight: salt and tar and the green bite of the sea, carried on a wind that had crossed three hundred miles of open water to find them. Sera smelled it from the ridge road at dawn, when the city was still only a smudge of gray against the gray horizon, and she stopped walking without meaning to.

Jude walked on four paces before he noticed, then turned back, his hood dark with the night's drizzle. "What is it?"

"Nothing." She made herself start moving again. "I've just never come to Meridian as anything but a prisoner or a courier."

"And now?"

"Now I'm bringing them the one thing they've spent fifteen years pretending doesn't exist." She glanced at him. He looked terrible in the honest way of men who hadn't slept properly in weeks—hollow-cheeked, rain-flushed, the brand beneath his collarbone giving off that faint heat she could feel now whenever she stood close, like standing near a banked oven. "They're going to look at you the way farmers look at a bull they're deciding whether to slaughter."

"Comforting."

"I'm not here to comfort you. I'm here to keep you alive long enough for them to make the right mistake."

The road down was lined with refugees—not many yet, not the flood that would come later, but enough: families with carts, a woman carrying a caged hen like an infant, two old men arguing about whether the stars would come back before winter. Sera counted them out of habit. Forty-one souls on the road, all walking toward walls instead of away from them. That was new. For fifteen years the roads of the borderlands had flowed only one direction, away from Corrin's silence, and now something had changed the current. The wards were broken. The stars were gone. And somewhere behind them, in the dark between extinguished constellations, the usurper's armies were learning what it meant to march without fear.

She tried not to think about Maren. She failed, the way she always did, somewhere around the second mile.

* * *

Meridian's Salt Gate still stood, which told Sera the council hadn't finished arguing. Gates that were going to be surrendered got quietly unbolted; gates that were going to be defended grew teeth. This one had grown teeth overnight—iron caltrops scattered in the approach, archers on the towers with the nervous posture of men who'd been handed weapons three days ago, and a line of wardens at the gatehouse checking papers with the grim thoroughness of people who had finally been given permission to be afraid.

Sera used her old credentials. They were forged, but they were good ones—she'd paid a forger in Hollowmere half her savings for documents identifying her as a courier in the service of the Meridian Trade Compact, and the seal was close enough that the gate-sergeant squinted at it, then at her face, then waved them through with the weary indifference of a man who had checked nine hundred papers that morning and would check nine hundred more before dark.

Inside, the city was holding its breath. She felt it in the streets—the way conversation dropped when strangers passed, the way shopkeepers stood in their doorways watching the sky where stars should have been, the way children had been kept indoors even though it was market day. Bells rang the hour, and nobody flinched, and everybody listened a beat too long after the last note faded, as if checking that the bells still worked, that sound itself still worked.

"They know," Jude said quietly. "About Corrin."

"Everyone knows. The question is what they intend to do about it, and that question is being answered upstairs right now, badly." She steered him off the main avenue into the tanner's quarter, where the smell covered everything and no one asked questions, to a safehouse above a chandler's shop that she'd used twice in her defection year and trusted once. The chandler, a broad woman named Odile with forearms like hams and a face that suggested she'd forgiven nothing in her life, took one look at Sera and said, "You're supposed to be dead."

"I get that a lot."

"The Choir's little runaway bird. Odile shook her head, but she stepped aside to let them in, and that was its own kind of welcome. "There's bread. There's watered wine. There's a council session at midday that every spy in this city is trying to get into, and I'm guessing you two are why."

Sera set down her pack. Her shoulders ached in a way that had become permanent. "We need to speak to General Vess. Before the session. She'll listen."

"Vess is dying," Odile said flatly. "Old wound, old age, old stubbornness. She attends sessions propped up like a scarecrow and everyone pretends not to notice. If you want her ear, you'd better climb fast, because the vultures are already circling."

* * *

General Amara Vess received them in a room that smelled of camphor and old leather, high in the war college, with a window that looked out over the harbor and the sea beyond—open water, open sky, and above it the blank black hole where the stars had been. Sera saw the general see Jude, and saw the recognition hit her like a physical blow.

Vess had been beautiful once, in the hard way of soldiers; now she was a ruin of herself, thin as kindling, one hand curled uselessly in her lap, her eyes still sharp as awls. She stared at Jude Yarrow across twenty years and a broken covenant, and her ruined hand trembled against the blanket.

"You're dead," she said. "You've been dead since Ashfall. I signed the memorial notice myself."

"I'm sorry about that," Jude said. "It seemed safer."

"It seemed—" Vess laughed, and the laugh turned into coughing, and Sera waited it out with her fists clenched at her sides. When the general recovered, there were tears in the creases of her eyes, unshed and furious about it. "Fifteen years. Fifteen years I told myself the order died clean. That you all went down singing. And you—" she gestured with her good hand, taking him in, the charm-seller's coat, the hollow cheeks, the whole shabby survival of him, "—you've been selling luck-knots to fishwives."

"Yes," Jude said. He didn't dress it up. Sera gave him credit for that; she'd expected excuses, and he offered none. "Yes, I have."

Vess closed her eyes. When she opened them again, the general was back, the grief packed away somewhere load-bearing. "Why are you here? Don't tell me sentiment. Sentiment doesn't cross a fallen border."

"Because the wards didn't fail," Sera said. "They were fed. Corrin has been using the stolen Voice to power them, and now he's pulled the power home for his war. The silence is ending, General, and when it ends fully, either the Choir wakes or Corrin becomes the only god left standing. This man is the last living conduit of the old covenant. He's the difference between those two futures."

Vess looked at her for a long moment. "You were Maren's student. Third chair, choir of the Eastern Spire. You sang the litany of severance at your own defection trial—you sang it so beautifully they couldn't bring themselves to execute you." A dry pause. "Maren wrote to me about you for years. Looking for you."

"I know." Sera's throat was tight. "Is she—"

"She leads Corrin's Hand now. His first inquisitor." Vess let that land. "So understand what you're asking, girl. You're asking me to shelter the last conduit while the woman who trained you hunts him. And you're asking me to do it in front of a council where three cities have already sent secret envoys to Corrin offering terms." She turned back to Jude. "And you. Do you even know what you are anymore? Or are you just the shape the god left behind?"

Jude was quiet for a moment. Through the window, the starless sky pressed down on the harbor like a lid.

"I don't know," he said finally. "I know what I was. I know what I ran from. I don't know if any of it's still true. But I know the mark burns, and I know the silence is breaking, and I know that whatever happens next, it happens through me whether I stand in front of your council or hide under a bridge. I'd rather stand."

Vess studied him the way she must once have studied battlefields. Then she said, "Midday. The Hall of Voices. You'll speak, if I can buy you the floor. And boy—if you humiliate me in there, I will personally feed you to the harbor."

* * *

The Hall of Voices had been built in prouder times, when the free cities believed their arguments deserved architecture: a great amphitheater of white stone, ring upon rising ring of seats, the ceiling painted with constellations that some zealous clerk had draped in mourning cloth after the stars went out. Sera sat in the third tier with Odile beside her and watched the council convene below, forty-one delegates from eleven cities, and felt the old claustrophobia of institutions close around her like a fist.

She had grown up in rooms like this. Choirs had their councils too—rows of ranked seats, voices weighed and counted, the strong argument and the louder voice winning equally often. She had believed, once, that a room full of reasonable people could reason its way to the truth. Then she had watched such a room vote unanimously to erase a village that had heard too much, and she had stopped believing it, and here she sat anyway, because there was nowhere else to put hope except inside broken machines.

The debate had been running for two hours when Vess spent the last of her political capital.

Sera heard it in fragments, the way you hear weather coming: the delegate from Cael Port arguing for negotiation—"Corrin asks only for the conduit, only for the man, and his armies turn east"—and the delegate from Vhal answering in a shaking voice that Corrin asked for the conduit the way a fire asks for the nearest door. The word *surrender* was used six times and dressed in five different euphemisms. *Appeasement. Accommodation. Pragmatic disengagement. Terms.* Somewhere in the middle of it, a young delegate from the river towns stood and said, "We outlawed the conduits ourselves. We burned their sanctuaries. Are we surprised the god went quiet? We are being billed for our own crime," and was shouted down by three delegations at once.

Then Vess rose.

She rose badly, leaning on a cane, and the hall quieted not out of respect but out of the particular silence crowds give to the visibly dying. She stood in the well of the chamber, small and bent, and her voice came out rusted but level.

"I fought at Redford Ford," she said. "Some of you were there. Most of you were children. At Redford Ford we held a crossing for six days against an army three times our number, and we held it because the Choir's conduits stood in the water and sang, and while they sang, no man among us knew fear. Not fearlessness—we knew fear fine, we drowned in it—but it couldn't *move* us. It couldn't make us run. Six days. On the seventh, the song stopped." She paused. In the hush, Sera could hear the harbor gulls outside, crying over water that reflected no stars. "On the seventh day the conduits fell silent, mid-verse, all together, and within an hour we broke, and I lost my hand, and two thousand of ours are buried in the mud of that ford. Fifteen years I wondered what silenced them. Now I know. One man cut out his own god's tongue because the god asked him to stop feeding it war—and we called the resulting peace a blessing."

Murmurs. Someone called out, "To your point, General—"

"My point," Vess said, "is that I have spent fifteen years listening to this council explain why surrendering our protectors was wisdom. We outlawed them. We hunted them. We sold them to Corrin's inquisitors one by one and called it diplomacy. And now the bill has come due, and this body's answer is to pay it again—to hand over the last conduit, the last voice of the only power that ever made us brave, and pray the tyrant keeps his word." She turned, slowly, painfully, until she faced the whole rising ring of seats. "I am old. I am dying. I have very little left to lose and less time to lose it in. So I will spend both. I move that Meridian grant sanctuary to Jude Yarrow, last conduit of the Choir, and that the free cities commit to the reforging of the covenant—or, failing that, that Meridian commits alone, and the rest of you can explain to your grandchildren why you chose the comfortable grave."

The hall erupted. Sera sat with her nails biting her palms and watched democracy do what democracy does: grind. Delegates on their feet, points of order, the Cael Port delegation demanding a recess, the river towns' young delegate shouting *seconded, seconded* over the noise. Odile leaned close. "Your general just lit the fuse on her own funeral."

"Yes," Sera said. "Watch what it costs her."

It cost her everything, and she paid it gladly, and the vote, when it finally came near dusk, came down to seven delegates who had not committed—the undecided, the frightened, the men and women doing arithmetic with other people's lives. Six broke for surrender. One broke for sanctuary. A wool merchant from the hill cities named Perrin Doss, who had said nothing all day, stood, adjusted his spectacles, and said, "My grandmother sang the evening litany. She wasn't a conduit; she was a cook. But she sang it every night of her life, and when they outlawed the songs she sang it in her kitchen with the shutters closed, and she told me: *Perrin, a city that gives away its voice deserves the silence it gets.*" He sat down. "Sanctuary."

One vote. The hall went silent, and then it didn't, and Sera found she was standing without remembering having risen, and Jude was being escorted to the well of the chamber to be seen—displayed, examined, catalogued, the living relic passed from hand to hand of history.

* * *

They made him stand on the floor of the Hall of Voices while the delegates filed past.

That was the compromise, in the end: sanctuary granted, but the council wanted to *see* him, the way you examine a coin you suspect is counterfeit. So Jude Yarrow stood in the amber lamplight in his charm-seller's coat while forty-one delegates and their clerks and their spies-in-residence walked by him in a slow procession, some nodding, some sneering, some reaching out—actually reaching out—to touch his sleeve, his hands, as if the covenant might be contagious, or purchasable.

Sera watched from the tier and hated every minute of it, and understood, with a clarity that felt like cold water, why he had run for fifteen years. This was what the order had been. Not the songs, not the grace—this. Being a thing. Being *evidence*. Being the object in everyone else's argument.

Jude bore it the way he seemed to bear everything: with that flat, tired steadiness, his eyes fixed on the middle distance, answering questions in short, honest sentences. Yes, the brand still burned. No, he could not demonstrate the covenant—he hadn't sung in fifteen years. Yes, he had deserted at Redford Ford. No, he was not sorry he had lived.

A delegate from Cael Port—a silver-haired woman with rings on every finger—stopped in front of him last of all and looked him up and down like livestock. "You understand," she said, loud enough for the chamber, "that if the council's gamble fails, we will reconsider. Sanctuary is a loan, conduit, not a gift. And loans are called in."

"I understand," Jude said. "I've been called in my whole life."

Someone laughed. It broke something in the room—the tension thinned, the procession ended, the delegates dispersed into the evening to argue in smaller rooms. And Jude stood alone in the well of the emptying hall, looking smaller than Sera had ever seen him, and then he looked up and found her in the tiers, and something passed between them that was not quite hope but was its raw material.

She came down to him. Up close, in the lamplight, she could see how much the day had cost him—the sweat at his temples, the faint glow of the brand through his shirt, the way his hands wouldn't quite stay still.

"Well," he said. "That was humiliating."

"Yes."

"I'd forgotten how much I hated it. Being looked at like that." He flexed his fingers, opened and closed them. "When I was nineteen I thought it was an honor. Standing in front of councils. Being the proof of something."

"And now?"

He was quiet a moment. Outside, through the high windows, the first lamps of the city were being lit against a sky with nothing in it, and somewhere down by the harbor a ship's bell rang the evening watch, one clear note that hung in the vast stone air.

"Now I think," he said slowly, "that if they want to look at me—if all of them, the whole council, the whole rotten compromise of the free cities wants to stare at the last conduit of the Choir—then let them look. Let them see exactly what their fifteen years of surrender bought them. And then let them watch what I do next."

It was the first time she'd heard fire in his voice. It was banked low, the way his brand was banked, but it was there, and Sera recognized it because she'd carried its twin since the day she'd defected: humiliation, aged fifteen years in the dark, decanted at last into purpose.

"Don't make promises in front of empty rooms," she said. "Rooms like this remember."

"It wasn't a promise." He turned toward the doors, toward the waiting city, and the lamplight caught the edge of the brand's glow at his collar. "It was a warning. And not to them."

"To whom, then?"

Jude looked at her, and for a moment Sera could have sworn she heard it too—faint, beneath the bell and the wind and the murmur of the dispersing council, a hum with no source, threading through the stones of the Hall of Voices like water through a cracked wall. His expression said he heard it clearly.

"To whatever's been listening to me since the stars went out," he said. "I think it's getting impatient."
