## 7. Lessons in a Dead Tongue

The archive beneath Meridian smelled of dust and old grief.

Jude felt it before he saw it—that particular weight of a place where many things had been hidden and few things had been found. The stair down from the tanner's cellar was narrow and spiraled the wrong way, as if built by someone who expected to be followed and wanted the following to be difficult. Sera went first with a lamp whose flame burned green instead of gold, some ward-chemistry he didn't recognize, and the light slid along walls of fitted stone that had no seams, no mortar, no beginning or end that his eye could find.

"Older than the cities," she said, catching him looking. "Older than the Choir's first temples, if you believe the archivist. He says the archive wasn't built around the books. The books grew here."

"Grew."

"You'll see."

The stair opened into a vaulted dark so large that the lamp seemed to shrink in Sera's hand, its green glow swallowed after twenty feet like a coin dropped into deep water. And then Jude heard it—the sound he would carry for the rest of his life, though he did not yet know it: a rustle, vast and slow, like wind through a forest of paper, like ten thousand pages turning at once in a room where no one stood.

*There,* hummed the ghost-choir, faint and pleased, somewhere behind his sternum. *There, there, there.*

He had learned in the weeks since Ashfall to keep his face still when it sang. It was harder than it sounded. The choir had no body, but it had habits—it leaned toward certain sounds the way a cat leans toward a window, and when it leaned, Jude felt himself lean with it, a tilt behind his ribs that threatened to become a stumble.

"The archivist is called Brother Quillon," Sera said. "He was a conduit's scribe, before. When the order fell, he carried what he could down here and—" She hesitated. "He stayed. Fifteen years alone with dead texts and a living library. He's not what he was. Be patient with him."

"I've been patient with worse."

"No," she said quietly, "you haven't. That's rather the problem."

* * *

Brother Quillon turned out to be a thin man folded into a chair made of stacked books, which creaked when he moved like a nest of small animals. His robe had once been choir-grey; now it was the color of nothing in particular, stained at the cuffs with ink of at least four shades. His eyes were the shock—wide, wet, and quick as a bird's, fixing on Jude the moment the lamplight touched him with an attention so total it felt like being read aloud.

"Oh," Quillon said. "Oh, *oh.* You're warm."

"I'm sorry?"

"You're *warm.*" He unfolded from the chair in stages, joints cracking like green wood in a fire, and came close enough that Jude could smell camphor and old vellum. "Fifteen years I've kept them. Fifteen years, and every page has gone cold as river stone, and I thought—I told myself it was the damp, it was my hands, it was age—but it was never the books. It was the voice in them dying. And you—" His trembling hand hovered an inch from Jude's chest, over the brand beneath his shirt. "You're carrying a coal in a rainstorm, boy. Who told you how to keep it lit?"

"No one," Jude said. "It never went out. That was rather the whole of my misfortune."

Quillon laughed then—a dry, startled bark, like a hinge remembering motion—and something in the sound made the ghost-choir stir and lean, and Jude swayed on his feet without meaning to.

Sera caught his elbow. "He hears them," she said. "The fragments. He won't say much about it, but he hears them."

"I hear humming," Jude corrected. "Mostly off-key."

"That's the dead conduits," Quillon said, matter-of-fact, already turning away, already pulling books. "They sing what they remember. Memory frays at the edges, so the edges go flat. Come. Sit. We have perhaps a season before Corrin's hounds sniff you out of this city, and there are forty-one rites in the covenant and you will need all of them, and I am old, and you are late."

Jude looked at Sera. Sera looked at the ceiling, which was lost in darkness, and offered him nothing but a shrug that said, quite clearly: *I warned you.*

He sat.

* * *

The first lesson was not a lesson. It was a listening.

Quillon set a single page before him—actual paper, soft as cloth with age, covered in notation Jude recognized the way one recognizes a childhood street: not the details, but the *feel* of it, the slope of the lines, the way the marks clustered and breathed. Covenant-script. The Choir had never written in words. Words were too small, too straight-edged, too much the property of mouths and throats. The covenant was written in intervals—in the space between two tones, which is where, the priests used to say, God actually lived.

"Read it," Quillon said.

"It's been fifteen years."

"Fifteen years is nothing. Read it."

Jude looked at the page. The marks sat there, patient and cold. He tried to remember the seminary drills, the rote shapes, Master Ellery rapping his knuckles with a tuning fork for singing the third interval flat. What came instead was not memory but sensation: a warmth in his fingertips, a faint pressure behind his eyes, and the ghost-choir rising slowly inside him like water finding its level, humming toward the page, *that one, that one, begin there—*

"The second mark," he said slowly. "It's not a note. It's a... gap. A held breath between two notes. You don't sing it. You *leave room* for it."

Quillon went very still.

"Fifteen years," he repeated softly, "and you leave room for it." He pressed both palms flat to the table, and Jude saw that his hands were shaking—not with age, he realized, but with something closer to fear. "Boy. Do you understand what that means? Every scribe since the Silence has read that mark as a rest. A pause. Nothing. We transcribed four hundred pages treating it as *nothing.* If it is a gap—if the covenant requires absence, requires *room left open*—then half our scholarship is inverted, and the rites we reconstructed are not broken versions of the true ones. They are their opposites."

"Then teach me the true ones."

"They're gone." Quillon's voice cracked on it. "The true ones lived in voices, and the voices are ash, and all I have are four hundred pages of beautiful mistakes and one warm boy who leaves room where scholars wrote silence." He looked up, and his bird-quick eyes were wet. "So we start over. From the beginning. Like children. Can you bear that? Can a soldier bear being a child again?"

Jude thought of Ashfall, of luck-knots sold to people who needed real luck, of fifteen years of pretending the warmth under his skin was scar tissue. He thought: *I have been a child in every way except the one that matters.*

"Yes," he said.

* * *

They worked in the deep stacks, because Quillon claimed the books listened better there, and Jude privately suspected the archivist simply liked the company of things that could not contradict him.

The method was strange. There were no drills, no rote. Quillon would set a page before him and ask questions that had no answers in any text: *What does this interval weigh? Is this phrase asking permission or granting it? If you sang this to a dying man, would it comfort him or hurry him?* And Jude would close his eyes and reach for the ghost-choir, and the choir would lean, and fragments would rise through him like bubbles through dark water—half-phrases of harmony, a single perfect chord remembered by someone two hundred years dead, the ghost of a rhythm that made his heart try to match it.

He learned to leave room. That was the whole art, it began to seem. Not power—room. The covenant did not flow *through* a conduit the way water flows through a pipe. It gathered in the spaces he made, the way silence gathers in a cathedral, and what he had spent fifteen years thinking of as his dead god's warmth was, he slowly understood, the god itself, waiting patiently in the gaps of him for permission to be sung.

Some nights the fragments were almost unbearable. A lullaby in a minor mode that made him weep without knowing why. A marching cadence that made his hands curl into fists. Once, unbearably, a fragment of a blessing for new initiates—*come in, come in, the door was always yours*—and he had to leave the stacks and sit in the dark of the stairwell until his breathing steadied, while Sera waited at the top without comment and without leaving.

"She does that," Quillon observed, when he returned. "Waits. It's her whole strategy, I think. Wait near the thing until it stops being frightening." He turned a page with a fingertip. "She buried eleven conduits, you know. Before you. She finds them, she trains them, she carries them toward the Spire, and Corrin's hounds take them, one by one, and she buries what's left and goes looking again. You should ask her sometime why she keeps the ledger. Eleven names in a little book she reads at night. Ask her why the twelfth line is blank."

"Why is it blank?"

"How should I know?" said Quillon, who knew perfectly well. "Sing me the fourth rite."

* * *

The fourth rite was called, in the old tongue, *Vessia*—the Opening. It was the simplest working in the covenant and the foundation of all others: the act of making room deliberately, of opening a gap in oneself wide enough and shaped rightly enough that the Choir's presence could pool there and be *used*—a candle's worth of it, a cupful, never more. Every healing, every warding, every word of power began with Vessia. It was the door. Everything else was what you carried through it.

For six days, Jude failed at it.

Not dramatically. That was the maddening part. He would breathe, center, find the interval, leave the room—and feel the presence gather at the edge of the gap like light at a doorway, warm and eager and *right there*, and then his own fear would snap the space shut like a trap, and the warmth would recede, polite and unhurt, waiting to be asked again. Fifteen years of hiding a coal under his skin had taught him, above all else, how to close. How to clamp down, how to go gray and charm-seller dull, how to make himself unremarkable to inquisitors' senses. He had survived by shutting doors. Now every instinct he owned was a lock, and the god was standing outside all of them, knocking gently, forever.

"You flinch," Quillon said on the sixth evening, watching him fail for the hundredth time. "Every time. Right at the threshold. Why?"

"Because the last time I opened fully," Jude said, "everyone I loved died."

The old man was quiet a long moment. Around them the deep stacks rustled their paper-forest rustle, and the ghost-choir hummed low and sorrowful, and did not disagree.

"Well," Quillon said at last. "Yes. That would do it."

* * *

It happened on the ninth night, and it happened because of a child.

Not a real child—a memory in the margins. Quillon had given him a psalter from the last years of the order, and in its margins some novice, long dead, had drawn a small figure with a tuning fork and written, in a looping hand: *Master Ellery says I sing the third flat but Gideon says flat thirds are just honest.* Jude stared at the name—his own name, in a dead child's handwriting, in a book that had outlived everyone who ever touched it—and something in his chest came loose.

He didn't decide to open. That was the terrible, wonderful truth of it. The gap simply opened, the way a fist opens in sleep, and the Choir poured in.

It was a candle's worth. It was an ocean.

Warmth flooded every hollow place he had ever dug in himself—all the clamped doors, all the gray years, all the careful nothing—and it was not gentle, because he had made so much room and kept it empty so long that the presence came through him like a river through a breached dam. Light stood up out of his skin. The nearest shelves bowed outward with a groan of stressed wood, pages lifting, the whole archive inhaling. He heard himself singing—he was not choosing the notes; the notes were choosing him—and the sound was the third interval, flat, honest, enormous, and the ghost-choir rose around him in full voice for the first time, dozens of harmonies braiding through his single thread, and for three seconds—three eternal seconds—Jude Yarrow was not a man sitting in a basement. He was a bell. He was the moment after the bell.

Then the room came back, and it came back *owed*.

The warmth reversed. It drained out of him through channels he hadn't known he had, taking its toll in the only currency conduits carried: himself. His vision went white, then black at the edges. His heartbeat stuttered like a bird against glass. He felt his own name begin to slip—felt it loosen in his mouth like a tooth—and he clawed it back with the last of his strength, jaw clenched, hands fisted in his own hair, *my name, my name, Jude, Jude Yarrow, Ashfall, the luck-knots, the child with the knot that worked*—

And then Sera's hands were on his face, and her voice was in his ear, sharp and ordinary and human: "*Breathe. Breathe, damn you, you're not done, you don't get to spend yourself on a psalter, breathe—*" and she said his name, over and over, plain and unadorned, giving it back to him syllable by syllable like someone returning dropped coins.

When his vision cleared, he was on the floor, and Quillon was kneeling beside him holding the psalter, and the old man's face was wet.

"A candle's worth," Quillon whispered. "That was a *candle's worth.*"

Jude laughed. It hurt to laugh. He laughed anyway—cracked, gasping, helpless laughter, the laughter of a man who has just remembered he owns something priceless and nearly destroyed himself showing it off. "Fifteen years," he managed, "and I'm still doing everything wrong."

"No." Quillon pressed the psalter into his hands. "You did it *right*. That's what frightens me. Boy—you opened a door tonight that the greatest conduits of the old order opened once in a lifetime. And you paid a candle's price for it." He looked up, and behind the bird-quick eyes was something older and graver. "Now imagine the Spire. Imagine what the covenant asks at the end of the road. And imagine what it will cost a man who can open *that* wide."

The ghost-choir hummed, low and sweet, somewhere behind his sternum. It sounded, for the first time, less like longing and more like patience.

Like something waiting for him to be ready to pay.

Above them, far up through the stone, a bell began to ring—not the hour, not the alarm, but the council summons, urgent and triple-struck, and Sera was already on her feet, and her face in the lamplight told him the world outside the archive had stopped waiting.
