## 8. Corrin's Beautiful Machine

The hall had been a cathedral once.

Vael Corrin kept the bones of it—the ribbed vaults, the pillars worn smooth by centuries of kneeling pilgrims—but he had stripped out everything that knelt. The altars were gone. The icons were gone. In their place rose the tiers: nine concentric rings of polished stone ascending toward the broken oculus, and on each tier, the singers.

They sat in rows like instruments in their cases, robed in grey, their throats bared to the cold air. None of them moved unless he moved them. That was the whole beauty of it. A choir, at last, that did not choose what to sing.

Corrin stood on the conductor's dais where the high altar had been, baton of white ashwood in his hand—not because he needed it, but because the shape of conducting pleased him. Above and behind him, suspended in a cradle of silver chain and old iron, hung the Voice.

It did not look like much. That was the first thing every visitor noticed, and the last thing most of them ever thought, because looking at it too long was like looking into a well and finding no bottom. It was a shard of something—glass, or crystal, or frozen sound—no larger than a forearm, veined with light that moved when music moved near it. Fifteen years ago it had been torn from the throat of a god, and it still remembered how to sing.

He raised the baton.

The tiers woke in order, ring by ring, as they always did. First the outermost, two hundred voices humming a single low tone, the note that lived underneath all other notes, the one that sounded like the earth deciding to be patient. Then the next ring joined, and the next, each entering precisely when his wrist turned, until the ninth and innermost tier lifted its voices into the high register and the whole hall became a single breathing instrument.

And the Voice above him began to shine.

This was the part Corrin never tired of. Light poured off the shard in slow tides, and where the light fell, the music changed—it thickened, deepened, took on harmonies no human throat could have found alone. The stolen Voice wove itself through his singers like gold thread through grey cloth. He was not commanding it. He was *collaborating* with it. There was a difference, and he clung to that difference the way drowning men cling to driftwood, though he would have cut out his own tongue before admitting the clinging.

Below the tiers, behind a screen of iron lattice, the recruits waited.

There were three hundred of them this season, taken from the border provinces, from villages whose names Corrin had already forgotten because names were a luxury of peacetime. They stood in ranks in the dark beneath the gallery, and they were afraid. He could smell it from the dais—fear has a smell, sour and animal, sweat and bile and the faint copper tang of clenched jaws. For fifteen years he had been told by soft men in soft cities that fear was necessary. That fear was what made soldiers hold. That a man who did not fear death was either a saint or a corpse.

Corrin had found a third option.

He turned his wrist, and the music changed key.

The shift was subtle—a rising fourth, a brightening, the harmonic structure opening like a hand unclenching—and the Voice followed it eagerly, hungrily, pouring its borrowed radiance down through the lattice screen into the dark where the recruits stood. And the fear went out of them.

Not suppressed. Not drugged numb. *Removed*, the way a surgeon removes an arrowhead: cleanly, completely, leaving only the wound-shaped space where it had been. Corrin watched it happen from the dais, watched three hundred faces go slack and then calm and then—this was the miracle, this was the thing he had built—*serene*. Shoulders dropped. Breathing slowed. One young man near the front, who had been weeping silently since they marched him in, stopped weeping mid-tear, as if someone had simply informed his body that there was nothing left to cry about.

They would fight now. They would march through fire and stand in the shield wall while arrows fell like weather, and they would do it without the screaming madness that fear breeds, without the rout, without the atrocities committed by terrified men trying to feel brave. His song-taken soldiers did not rape. Did not pillage for pleasure. Did not break and run. They did exactly what they were asked, with the perfect tranquility of people who had been relieved of the worst thing about being alive.

The priests had called it soul-theft. The free cities called it the Hollowing, and mothers in Meridian frightened children with stories of the humming men. Corrin called it what it was: mercy, industrialized.

* * *

After the forging, he walked the long gallery alone, as he always did, past the windows of colored glass he had not removed.

That was a private indulgence, and he knew it. The glass depicted the Choir in its full glory—nine ranks of singer-saints ascending toward a radiant throat, the god rendered as pure voice, mouths open, eyes closed, every face caught in the rapture of being sung through. He had ordered the cathedral stripped of everything except these windows, and when his masons had asked why, he had told them the truth: *So I remember what I am saving the world from.*

Because that was the heresy at the heart of him, the truth he carried like a stone behind his sternum: the Choir had been real. The god had been real. He had not risen to power by denying the divine; he had risen by capturing it.

He stopped before the largest window, the one showing the Conduit—that figure at the center of the old faith, the mortal throat through which the god spoke. The artist had given the conduit a face of unbearable gentleness, hands open, head bowed under the weight of glory. Corrin studied it the way a general studies a map of enemy country.

Fifteen years ago, he had stood in a trench at the Salt Marches with forty thousand men dying around him, and the Choir had been singing through its conduits overhead, and the singing had been *beautiful*, and it had accomplished nothing. That was the thing no one understood, the thing the histories would never record because he made sure they didn't: the god had not failed to stop the war. The god had *declined* to stop it. Corrin had heard it himself, in the last hour, when the conduits' song turned strange and sorrowful—he had heard the god pleading through their mouths. *Enough. Enough. Lay it down. I will not sing for slaughter anymore.*

And the world's answer had been to keep killing, and to ask the god to bless it.

So Corrin had done the arithmetic that no priest had the courage to do. If the god would not act, and men would not stop, then the god's power should be taken from the god and given to men who would use it properly. He had cut the Voice from the Choir's throat with rites older than the faith itself—rites the priests had locked away because they feared them, which was precisely why Corrin had learned them. The god had gone silent. The conduits had died or scattered. The war had ground on for another two years, and then Corrin had ended it, with an army that felt no fear, against armies that felt nothing else.

Three million dead in the Godswar. Eleven dead in the Taking of the capital. He had done that sum a thousand times, and it always came out the same way, and the window's gentle conduit-face always looked at him with the same mild reproach, and he always met its gaze.

*I saved you,* he told the painted face silently, as he did every morning. *You were going to let the world burn rather than dirty your hands, so I dirtied mine. Every hollow soldier is a man who didn't die screaming. Every silenced god is a prayer that finally got answered.*

The window said nothing. Windows were good that way. Gods, he had learned, were not.

* * *

The interruption came at dusk, and it came wrong.

Corrin knew the rhythm of his machine the way a husband knows a wife's breathing in the night. So when the outermost tier faltered—one voice, half a beat late, a single sour grace-note hanging in the vaulted air—he set down his cup and was moving before the echo died.

In the antechamber off the gallery, a courier knelt in the posture of absolute submission, mud to the knees, horse killed under him somewhere on the road by the look of him. He held out a dispatch case sealed with black wax, and his hands were shaking, and Corrin noted the shaking with clinical interest. Fear, in his capital, was rare enough to be almost charming.

The dispatch was from the border garrison at Vhal's Crossing—the crossing where the old wards had stood for fifteen years, the wards Corrin himself had raised from the Voice's resonance, the fence he had built around the god's grave.

He read it twice.

The stars over the northern marches had gone out. All of them, in a line, like snuffed candles. The wards had shattered—not faded, not frayed, *shattered*, every stone of them ringing at once like struck glass. And in the hours after, the garrison's own charms had flared alive: luck-knots braided by grandmothers, hearth-blessings chalked on doorposts, cheap tin amulets sold by traveling peddlers. Warming. Glowing. Working.

Corrin read the report a third time, more slowly, and felt something he had not felt in fifteen years.

It was not fear. He had excised his own fear long ago, personally, in this very hall, in a session the singers still did not speak of. It was something adjacent to fear but colder and more useful: the recognition of a variable he had not accounted for.

The god was not awake. He would have known—the Voice hung above his dais even now, and if its source had stirred, the shard would blaze like a captured star. No. This was smaller and stranger. This was the silence *leaking*. As if somewhere in the north, a door he had nailed shut had come off its hinges, and the old covenant's residue was spilling back into the world, waking charms, darkening stars, calling to—

Calling to.

Corrin closed the report. "The second page," he said. His voice was quiet; it was always quiet; loud men were men who hadn't yet learned what quiet could do.

The courier swallowed. "There is no second page, Radiance."

"There is always a second page." He let the silence stretch until the man broke, as men did. "The garrison commander included a personal addendum. Unofficial. You memorized it, because you are not a fool. Say it."

"The—the wardens pulled a charm-seller out of Ashfall," the courier recited, sweating. "A hedge-charlatan. Luck-knots and love-powders. But his brand—" The man hesitated, and Corrin simply looked at him, and the hesitation died. "His brand was warm, Radiance. Fifteen years cold, and it was warm. The garrison's resident reader says it's a conduit's mark. She says it's the deepest mark she's ever seen on a living man."

The hall was very quiet. Somewhere above, the Voice turned slowly in its silver cradle, and its light, Corrin noticed, had shifted—a fraction of a degree warmer, a fraction brighter, the way a sleeper's breathing changes when a familiar footstep crosses the floor.

*A conduit lives.*

He walked back up the gallery to the great window, to the gentle painted face with its open hands, and he stood before it for a long time while the courier knelt forgotten behind him.

One conduit. Alive. Marked deeper than any the reader had seen—which meant marked *before* the silencing, which meant this man had sung with the god's actual voice in his throat, had been one of the throats. And the silence breaking over the north, the waking charms, the darkened stars—all of it bending toward Ashfall like water finding a crack. Toward *him*.

If the covenant could be reforged—if a living conduit reached the Spire and gave what the old rites demanded—then everything Corrin had built would come apart. Not defeated. *Undone.* The song-taken would wake screaming, three hundred thousand men drowning in fifteen years of stored terror all at once. The wards would fall. The war would return, with interest, and this time the god would be awake to watch it, and the god, as always, would decline to stop it.

No. Corrin had not climbed out of the world's grave to be buried in it again by a hedge-charlatan with a warm brand.

But there was a subtlety here, and he honored subtlety. He could not simply kill the man. Kill a conduit near a breaking silence and the death itself might ring through the covenant like a bell—martyrs were fuel, and the old faith ran on martyrs the way his machine ran on the Voice. No. The conduit had to be taken whole. Taken quietly. Brought here, to the hall, to the tiers—and *studied*. Because if a living conduit could reforge the covenant, then a living conduit, properly persuaded, might forge something else entirely. A new covenant. With a new throat. His throat, or one he owned.

He turned from the window. "Send for Maren."

The chamberlain, hovering at the door, went pale. "Radiance—inquisitors are already—"

"Send. For. Maren." He crossed back to the dais and took up the white ashwood baton, weighing it in his palm. "I don't want her to capture him. Anyone can capture a man. I want her to understand him—what he was, what he remembers, what he'll trade. She was choir-trained herself, before she came to me. She knows the old songs from the inside." He smiled, and it reached his eyes, which was the most frightening thing about him. "Besides. I'm told she had a student who defected. Sera, her name was. Last seen crossing the fallen border, heading for Ashfall, asking after a living conduit." He let that sit. "Maren will want to be the one. Give her whatever she asks for."

"And if the conduit resists, Radiance?"

"Then she brings me his brand in a lead box and his name in her memory, and we discover together whether the covenant can be sung by force." Corrin ascended the dais steps, unhurried, and faced his tiers—two hundred grey-robed throats waiting in perfect stillness, the loveliest and most terrible instrument ever built. "But it won't come to that. Nothing living resists forever. That's not cynicism. That's engineering."

He raised the baton.

Far to the north, in a city of ash and peddlers, a man with a burning mark was running through streets full of waking charms, hunted by everyone, called by something he had spent fifteen years pretending was dead. Corrin could almost hear it from here—the silence cracking, the old music leaking back into the world, one warm brand at a time.

Let it crack, he thought, and brought the baton down, and two hundred voices rose together into the dark.

Every song ends. The art is in choosing who holds the final note.

Above the dais, the stolen Voice shone, and shone, and did not answer him—and Corrin, conducting his beautiful machine, chose—as he always chose—to believe that the silence meant consent.
