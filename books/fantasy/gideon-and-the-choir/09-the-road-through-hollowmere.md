## 9. The Road Through Hollowmere

They smelled the village before they saw it.

Bread, Sera said later—warm bread, and woodsmoke, and the green smell of cut hay. A village going about its morning. But Jude caught something else beneath it, something that made his brand prickle under his sleeve like a splinter working toward the heart: a sound too low to be called singing and too patterned to be called anything else. It rose from behind the hills in a long, unbroken thread, the way smoke rises from a chimney when no one is watching the fire.

"Listen," he said.

The company stopped on the ridge road. There were nine of them now—Sera; Brannoc the general's man with his scarred jaw and his spear that had never once left his hand since Meridian; two archers out of the Salt Gate who had followed Jude out of something between gratitude and awe; a drover named Pell whose cart carried their food and their wounded; and three others whose names Jude knew and whose stories he was still learning, because that was what you did now, that was what an army of nine required of you: you learned names.

They listened. The wind came up the valley carrying the hum, and the horses put their ears back, and one of them—the gray mare, the gentle one—shivered all along her neck as if a hand had been drawn down her spine.

"That's not bees," Pell said. His voice had gone thin.

"No," Jude said. "It isn't."

He knew the tune. That was the thing he could not say yet, standing there in the cold morning light with frost still silvering the fence posts below. He knew it the way you know your mother's voice through a closed door—not the words, not even the melody entire, but the shape of it, the way it leaned at the end of each phrase like a sentence waiting for its answer. Fifteen years since he had heard it sung by living throats, and here it came drifting up out of a valley where no living throat should know it, worn smooth as river stone, passed from mouth to mouth to mouth until it had lost everything but its bones.

*The Lament of the Ninth Hour.* The evening song. The one the choir sang when the lamps were lit and the god turned His attention homeward over His people, the one meant to say: *you are seen, you are kept, you may sleep.*

Someone was using it as a leash.

* * *

Hollowmere sat in the crook of the valley like a thing set down carefully and then abandoned mid-thought. The gates stood open. The well rope hung slack. Laundry moved on its lines in the wind—shirts, a child's smock, sheets gone gray at the folds—and no one came to take it in. Chickens scratched in the street, fat and fearless, masters of the town.

And the people were everywhere. In doorways. At fences. Kneeling in gardens with their hands in the cold dirt, planting nothing. Standing in the square in loose rings, facing one another, close enough to touch, never touching. An old man sat on a mounting block with a half-shod horse standing forgotten beside him, the hoof pick still in his fingers, his arm rising and falling in a slow rhythm that matched nothing at all.

Every one of them was humming.

It was not the same note. That was the horror of it, once Jude's ear untangled the threads—the whole village hummed together, but each voice held its own small piece of the song, and the pieces wove, and the weaving was perfect. A woman by the bakehouse held the low drone. Two children on a doorstep, six years old perhaps, held a third above her, their small faces tilted toward a sky they did not seem to see. The old farrier carried the phrase itself, over and over, patient as water wearing stone.

"They're alive," one of the archers whispered. "Aldric—they're all alive."

"I can see that."

"But they're not *in* there."

No one answered him, because there was nothing to answer with. Jude dismounted slowly, and the moment his boots touched the ground the brand flared—not burning this time, not the white-hot dragging pain of the night the stars went out, but a deep resonant ache, like a tuning fork struck against his spine. The mark of the covenant answering music. Answering *His* music, bent to another purpose.

"Jude." Sera had come around beside him. She always knew. He had stopped wondering how. "What is it?"

"It's the Ninth Hour." His voice came out flat, which was the only way he could get it out at all. "The evening canticle. Third setting—the old one, the one we weren't supposed to sing outside the cloister because—" He stopped. Swallowed. "Because it was written to open the listener. To make the heart receptive. You sing it to someone and for a little while they can't hold a thought against it. It was for blessing. For peace at the end of the day."

"And Corrin found it."

"Corrin didn't find anything." The anger surprised him with its heat. "You don't *find* the Third Setting. It's not written down anywhere it could be found—it lived in the choir, in the singers, in the—" He pressed the heel of his hand against his sternum, against the brand. "It lived in *us*. Someone gave it to him. Or he took it out of someone who knew it, the way you'd take a coin out of a dead man's purse."

Sera said nothing. She was looking at the children on the doorstep, and her face had gone very still, the stillness she wore like armor, the one he had learned meant that underneath it something was being carefully, quietly broken.

Brannoc came up on Jude's other side, spear leveled at nothing, at everything. "Orders?"

"We go through. Carefully. We don't touch anyone." Jude looked down the street, at the rings of humming people, at the laundry turning on the line. "We take what food we need and we pay for it—we'll leave coin—and we don't stop moving. If any of them wake while we're here, I want to be gone before they understand where they are."

"You think they'll wake?"

"I think the song has to end sometime." He heard his own voice shake on it. "And I think when it does, they're going to look down at their hands and find dirt in them from planting in November, and they're going to start screaming, and I would rather be over the next hill before that happens."

* * *

They moved through Hollowmere the way you move through a sickroom. Quietly. Reverently. Not meeting the eyes of the afflicted, though the affliction here was that there were no eyes to meet—or rather there were, open and clear and brown and blue and gray, looking straight through the world as though it were a window fogged from the inside.

Jude walked past the baker's and the bread smell nearly undid him. The ovens were cold, but the loaves of yesterday sat cooling on racks no one had covered, and beside them a woman stood with flour to her elbows, humming her fragment, her hands moving in the ghost-shape of kneading. Working dough that wasn't there. Jude took four loaves and left a silver mark on the rack, and the woman's hands never paused, and her fragment of the Ninth Hour slid past him like water past a post, and he had to stand a moment with his eyes shut because for one terrible half-second it was beautiful. It was *beautiful*. Fifteen voices braided into one seamless cloth, the way it was meant to be, the way it had been every evening of his first life, and some starving part of him wanted to stand in the square and open his own throat and give them the answering line—

*Don't.* The thought arrived with the taste of ash. *That's exactly what He wants. What Corrin wants. One more voice in the weave.*

Because that was the thing he understood, walking those streets, and wished with everything in him that he didn't: the song was not finished. It was missing a part. The villagers held the drone and the inner voices and the cantus line, but the Ninth Hour was written for a lead—a single voice to carry the melody home, to gather the others and resolve them. Whoever had built this had left the lead empty. A chair at the head of the table, waiting.

Waiting for a conduit.

"He's fishing," Jude said aloud. They had regrouped at the far edge of the square, by the shrine—by what had been a shrine. Someone had taken down whatever figure or flame the village had kept there and left only an empty alcove, swept clean, and in front of it the largest ring of villagers stood, forty of them maybe, humming directly at the hollow space as though the song itself were the occupant now. "This whole valley is a net. He doesn't know where I am, so he seeds the roads with choirs. If a conduit passes—if *I* pass—the brand answers the music, and either I can't help joining the song, or I run, and running tells him which road I took."

Sera's head came up sharply. "Then we don't run. We walk."

"We walk," Jude agreed. But his legs felt like someone else's legs, and the hum was in his teeth now, in the roots of his molars, and the empty alcove of the shrine seemed less empty every time he glanced at it.

Pell found the children's mother. Or a mother—one of the women kneeling in a garden, dirt black under her nails, a scrap of embroidery fallen from her lap into the mud. Pell crouched beside her for a long moment, and when he stood his big weathered face was wet, and he said nothing to anyone, just walked back to the cart and took up the reins and waited with his jaw working.

They were nearly to the north gate when the farrier stopped humming.

Just him. Just for a moment. The old man on the mounting block went silent in the middle of his phrase, and his arm stopped its useless rhythm, and his eyes—his eyes came back. Jude watched it happen from thirty feet away, watched the fog lift the way frost lifts off glass in a warm room, watched the man look down at the hoof pick in his hand, at the horse, at the village, at the humming neighbors, and begin, very softly, to scream.

The song caught him again before the second breath. The drone swelled—the woman by the bakehouse, the children, the ring at the shrine, all of them leaning their fragments toward the gap he'd torn—and the old man's face smoothed over like water closing above a drowned thing, and his arm resumed its rise and fall, and his voice rejoined the weave on precisely the note it had left.

But the scream had done its work. Horses screamed too, and the archers had arrows nocked and shaking, and Aldric was shouting for order, and Sera had Jude's arm in a grip that would bruise.

"You felt it." Her eyes were fierce and wet. "When he woke—you felt it. Tell me you felt it."

"Yes."

"It let him go. For a second, it *let him go*—why would it—"

"Because it's not a wall." Jude's mouth was dry. "Walls don't have gaps. Songs do. Rests. Breathing places. Whatever's holding them lets them surface every so often—long enough to remember, not long enough to break free." He looked back at the square, at the ring of faces aimed at the empty shrine. "Long enough to suffer. That's not mercy in the design, Sera. That's seasoning. Fear feeds the Voice. Corrin's soldiers don't feel fear anymore because it's been *sung out of them*—and it has to go somewhere."

No one spoke. Down the street, the laundry turned on its lines. Somewhere a rooster crowed, absurd and defiant, the only creature in Hollowmere with an unsilenced opinion.

"Move," Brannoc said quietly. "Now. While the old man's quiet."

They moved.

* * *

The north road climbed away from the valley through bare birches, and for a mile no one talked. The hum faded behind them by degrees, note by note dropping away, until it was a memory of a sound, and then not even that—except that Jude could still hear it. He suspected he would hear it for the rest of his life, however long that proved to be. Some songs you don't hear with your ears.

At the top of the ridge they stopped to rest the horses, and Jude sat apart on a fallen trunk with his hands laced over his knees, and Sera came and sat beside him without asking, which was her way.

"The lead part," she said finally. "The empty chair. You're sure?"

"I'm sure."

"So when he finds a conduit—when he finds *you*—"

"He doesn't put me in chains. He puts me in the choir." Jude watched a birch leaf come loose and spiral down through the cold air. "All of this—the armies, the song-taken, the villages—it's scaffolding. It's him building toward the day he sets me at the head of the table and lets the Ninth Hour finish. And I keep thinking—" His laugh came out wrong. "I keep thinking about how good it sounded. How much I wanted to join. Fifteen years I've told myself the faith was dead in me, burned out, buried with everyone else, and today I stood in a graveyard of a whole town and my first feeling was *homesick*."

Sera was quiet for a while. Below them the valley lay blue in the afternoon haze, harmless-looking, its chimneys smoking, its laundry turning.

"When I was in the choir," she said at last, "before I ran—we used to argue about whether the Voice was still in the songs Corrin stole. Maren said no. Maren said a stolen thing loses its soul, that what Corrin conducts is just shaped noise, power without presence." She pulled her cloak tighter. "I think she needed to believe that. I think I did too. Because the other thing to believe is that He's in there. In every village. In every soldier. Hearing Himself sung wrong, by mouths that were never taught, for purposes He'd weep over—and unable to stop it. Unable to say a single word."

Jude looked at her.

"The dead conduits chose silence," she went on, very evenly, the way you carry something hot across a room. "Fifteen years ago they broke the covenant rather than let the theft be complete. So He's been alone in there ever since. Mute. In His own music. In *our* music." Her voice cracked on the last word and she let it, and did not apologize for it, and Jude loved her a little for that. "So yes. Be homesick. Be horrified. Both. That's not weakness, Jude. That's the only honest prayer anybody's offered Him in fifteen years."

He didn't answer. There was nothing in him equal to it. He just sat with her on the cold log while the light went long and gold, and somewhere below in the darkening valley the Ninth Hour rose again for the evening, patient as water, faithful as a dog at a grave, singing to an empty shrine in a town full of sleeping souls.

It was Brannoc who broke the silence—came up the slope at a jog, spear in hand, and the look on his scarred face emptied the day of all its gold.

"Riders," he said. "Behind us on the south road. Six. Moving fast." He paused, and his jaw tightened. "And the one leading them is flying white. Inquisitor's colors."

Sera stood. Jude saw her recognize what was coming before he did—saw it land in her like a stone dropped down a well, falling a long way before it hit.

"Sera?" he said. "Who?"

She didn't take her eyes off the south road.

"Maren," she said. "My mentor. The woman who taught me to sing."
