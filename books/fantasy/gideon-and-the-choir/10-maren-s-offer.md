## 10. Maren's Offer

They made camp in the ribs of a dead watchtower, three days out from Hollowmere, where the road bent north toward the high passes and the Silent Spire beyond them. Sera chose the spot because the walls still stood to shoulder height and because the wind through the broken arrow-slits made a sound like breathing—low, rhythmic, almost human. She had learned, on the long road, that Jude slept better when the world sounded like a body at rest.

She did not sleep much herself.

The shard of stolen Voice she carried against her sternum had grown warmer since Hollowmere, warmer since the humming village, warmer with every mile they put between themselves and Corrin's border. It was not a comfortable warmth. It was the warmth of a hand closing around something it meant to keep. She lay on her bedroll with her palm flat over her breastbone and listened to it pulse, faint as a second heart, and told herself—as she had told herself for two years—that she would know what to do with it when the time came.

She was lying awake cataloguing the lies she'd stopped believing when she heard the horses.

Not one horse. Not the scattered percussion of travelers or the careful quiet of bandits. One horse, walking slowly, deliberately, its hooves striking the frozen mud like a metronome. Someone who wanted to be heard coming.

Sera was on her feet before the thought finished forming, knife already in her hand, and she saw by the starless dark that Jude had woken too—he sat up with his blanket pooled around his waist, his brand glowing faintly through his shirt like an ember under ash, and his face wore the expression she had come to dread: recognition.

"You know who it is," she whispered.

"I know the song," he said. His voice was rough with sleep and something else. "There's a song walking up the road. It's wearing armor."

* * *

Maren came alone, dismounted at fifty paces, and walked the last stretch with her hands open at her sides and her sword still sheathed—the oldest courtesy of the Inquisition, the gesture that meant *I come to talk before I come to kill*. She was smaller than Sera remembered, or perhaps Sera had simply spent two years making her larger, the way you do with every wall you have to climb. Gray had taken her temples. The black-and-silver of Corrin's Inquisition fit her like it had been tailored by someone who hated her, which, knowing the Inquisition's quartermasters, it probably had.

"Sera Vane," Maren said, and stopped at the edge of the firelight.

"Inquisitor Maren Kestrel," Sera answered, because if this was going to be a ceremony of names, she would not be the only one stripped bare by it.

"Formerly," Maren said. "Of both."

Behind Sera, she heard Jude rise, heard the whisper of him moving to stand where the tower wall could cover his back. Good. He was learning. Fifteen years of hiding had taught him everything about vanishing and nothing about standing, but he was learning.

"You tracked us across the Salt Flats," Sera said. "Through Hollowmere. You let us see the village first." She didn't ask it as a question. She wanted it said aloud, wanted it hanging in the cold air between them, because she had done the arithmetic herself on the road and the answer had kept her up three nights running: Maren had reached Hollowmere a day ahead of them and waited. A day. An Inquisitor did not wait unless waiting served a purpose, and the only purpose Sera could find was that Maren had wanted her to see what Corrin's song did to people before she heard whatever offer was coming.

"I wanted you to understand the stakes," Maren said, as if reading it off the same page. "Before I spoke. Because you'll want to refuse me, and I needed you to refuse me with your eyes open."

"That's considerate."

"It's honest. You always said honesty was the only liturgy worth keeping." Maren's mouth did something that was almost a smile and mostly wasn't. "You taught me that, in the novitiate. Before either of us had opinions worth killing for."

The fire cracked. Somewhere in the dark, the wind breathed through the arrow-slits, in and out, in and out. Sera became aware that she was gripping her knife hard enough to ache and made herself loosen her fingers one at a time.

"Say your piece," she said.

Maren nodded once, formally, like a herald opening proceedings, and Sera understood that she had rehearsed this. That somewhere behind those gray-shot temples there was a version of this speech that had been written by other hands, polished by committees, and that Maren was delivering it faithfully the way she delivered everything—perfectly, and slightly against her own grain.

"The Hand of the Usurper offers amnesty," Maren said. "Full amnesty. To Sera Vane, defected choir-singer, for the crime of desertion and the theft of Inquisitorial property." Her eyes flicked, briefly, to Sera's chest—to the shard sleeping under her coat—and back. "Restoration of rank. Restoration of voice." And here the rehearsal faltered, just barely, a seam showing. "You'd sing again, Sera. Really sing. In the Cathedral of the Ascended Word, under the dome, where the acoustics are—" She stopped. Started again, flatter. "The terms are these: the singer returns, and the conduit surrenders himself to the Inquisition for assessment and incorporation. No harm is promised to him. No harm is promised to anyone. The war ends. The wards are rebuilt. The stars come back."

Silence. The wind breathed. Jude, at the wall, said nothing, but Sera could feel him listening the way you feel weather change.

"And if we refuse?" Sera asked.

"Then I have orders," Maren said simply. "And you have seen Hollowmere."

* * *

Sera made herself look at Maren properly then—not at the uniform, not at the office, but at the woman, and looking was a mistake, because looking brought it all back at once.

The novitiate dormitory in the Choir's winter house, twenty years gone, two girls sharing a stove-warmed corner because neither could sleep in the cold. Maren, seventeen and ferocious, drilling the old modes until her throat bled because she believed—with the whole uncomplicated violence of the young—that the songs were a weapon God had left lying where the faithful would find it. Sera teaching her the seventh mode instead, the one built on unresolved chords, the one the masters called the Weeping Mode, and Maren hating it, and practicing it anyway, alone, at night, until one evening she sang it back to Sera perfectly and then burst into furious tears and said, *why does it have to be beautiful? Why can't it just be strong?*

And Sera, nineteen and certain of nothing except the note hanging in the air between them, saying: *because that's the whole point. Strength that isn't beautiful is just noise with a body count.*

She wondered if Maren remembered. She suspected Maren remembered everything. That was the cruelty of Maren Kestrel—she forgot nothing, forgave selectively, and carried all of it upright, like a soldier carrying a wounded comrade who kept asking to be put down.

"You've sung for him," Sera said quietly. "For Corrin. You've stood in that cathedral and let the Voice pour through you."

"I've conducted for him," Maren corrected. "There's a difference."

"There isn't."

"There is," Maren said, and now her voice had an edge Sera knew well, the edge Maren got when she was arguing with herself and losing. "What he has—the Voice—it's the most beautiful thing I have ever touched. Do you understand that? Fifteen years I sang in ruins. Fifteen years of singing into silence and calling it faith. And then he opened the vault, and the Voice was *there*, whole, alive, and it came into my chest and I wept for an hour in front of the entire conclave and no one thought less of me for it." Her hands had curled at her sides. "It's real, Sera. Whatever else he is, whatever he's done—the Voice is real, and it works, and my soldiers go into battle without fear, and the cities he takes don't burn, they *hum*. Have you seen a city after incorporation? There's no looting. There's no rape. There's no fear. People walk around smiling."

"People walk around vacant," Sera said. "Hollowmere was full of people, Maren. Alive, fed, unafraid, and *gone*. Their eyes were windows with nobody home. That's not peace. That's amputation."

"It's mercy compared to what the wards were holding back!" And Maren's composure cracked clean down the middle, and for one raw moment Sera saw not the Hand of the Usurper but a tired woman in her forties who had been carrying an argument for fifteen years. "You weren't at the border when it broke. You don't remember what came through before the old covenant sealed it. The Choir remembers—I've felt it remember, in the Voice, fragments, terror—the things on the other side of the night sky. Corrin didn't steal the god's voice to make himself king. He stole it to build a wall of *song* between humanity and the dark, and it has held for fifteen years while the old covenant rotted and the conduits died off one by one and nobody—*nobody*—had a better plan!"

"We had a plan," Sera said. "It was called the covenant. It required consent. It required voices given freely, not harvested from a chained god and poured down soldiers' throats until they forget their children's names."

"And how many freely-given voices are left, Sera?" Maren's voice dropped, went gentle, went terrible. "One. One half-trained charm-peddler with a brand he's spent fifteen years pretending is a scar. Your covenant is a corpse. Corrin's machine is ugly, yes—but it's alive, and it's *holding*. Give me the conduit. Let us assess him, incorporate him gently—we have gentler methods than Hollowmere, I promise you, I will oversee it personally—and the wall stands another century. Refuse, and the wards finish failing, the stars stay dead, and everything your god ever loved comes through the gap."

The fire burned low. Jude moved at the wall—a small shift of weight, nothing more—but Sera heard his breath catch, and she knew he had heard it too: *the stars stay dead.* Maren knew about the Spire. Or she guessed. Either way, the offer was not really an offer; it was a net with generous knots.

"No," Sera said.

"Sera—"

"No." She stepped forward, out of the firelight, so Maren had to look at her without the fire's help. "I buried eleven conduits, Maren. Eleven. I dug half the graves myself. Every one of them died believing the covenant could be reforged, and every one of them was right, and none of them lived long enough to see it. I am not handing you the twelfth so your master can keep his beautiful machine humming. Take your amnesty and—" her voice caught, and she hated it, hated that even now, even here, grief could ambush her mid-sentence— "and take it back to him with my refusal and my pity. Both are sincere."

Something moved behind Maren's eyes—hurt, fury, and beneath them, horribly, relief. As if some part of her had crossed the flats hoping to be refused. As if refusal was the only outcome that let her remain who she had decided to be.

"All right," Maren said softly. "Then we do it the other way."

* * *

She drew her sword, and Sera drew hers, and the space between them collapsed.

Maren had always fought the way she sang—precise, economical, every motion a sentence with no wasted words. Sera had spent two years fighting inquisitors and had learned their grammar, and for the first hundred heartbeats the duel was almost familiar, almost tender, blades speaking a dialect they'd both been raised in, steel ringing off steel in the dead watchtower while the fire threw their shadows huge against the broken walls.

Then Maren began to hum.

Low, at first, under her breath, barely audible over the ring of the blades—and Sera recognized the mode instantly, the marching mode, the one Corrin's armies used, the one that ate fear. And she saw what it did. Saw Maren's exhaustion fall away. Saw the gray-faced, forty-year-old woman become something smoother, tireless, her blade suddenly everywhere, driving Sera back across the frozen mud stroke by stroke with a serenity that was worse than rage.

"Maren," Sera gasped, parrying, giving ground. "Maren, stop singing. *Stop singing.*"

"Why?" Maren smiled, and it was the smile from Hollowmere, the smile of the humming people, and Sera's blood went to ice water. "Isn't this what you wanted? Strength?"

"*Fight yourself!*"

And Maren faltered.

Just a fraction—a half-step, a stutter in the rhythm, a flicker across her face like a candle in a draft—and in that fraction Sera saw the truth entire: the conditioning was not complete. It had never been complete. Maren Kestrel, Hand of the Usurper, terror of the free cities, had been singing Corrin's fear-eating mode so long and so faithfully that it had eaten into *her*, hollowing her the way it hollowed villages, and what remained inside the smooth serene surface was still fighting—still, after everything, fighting.

Sera did the only thing she could think of.

She sang back.

Not the marching mode. Not any mode of power. She sang the seventh mode—the Weeping Mode, the unresolved one, the one Maren had practiced alone at night until she cried—sang it ragged and desperate over their crossing blades, the old melody rising thin and cracked into the starless dark, and the effect was immediate and awful. Maren's sword arm shook. Her hum fractured. Her face contorted as if the melody were a hand reaching into her chest and squeezing something that had been squeezed too many times already.

"*Stop,*" Maren whispered, and it wasn't clear whether she meant Sera or herself.

Sera didn't stop. She sang the phrase again, and again, walking forward now, blade low, singing the song of a winter dormitory and a girl who asked why strength couldn't be beautiful—and Maren Kestrel, Hand of the Usurper, sank to her knees in the frozen mud of a dead watchtower and wept like the novitiate she had been, her sword falling from her hand, her hands pressed over her ears, her whole body shaking with the effort of not joining in.

Behind Sera, Jude's brand flared white through his shirt, and the ghost-choir—she couldn't hear it, but she saw its effect, saw him sway as though a great wind had passed through him—was singing too. She was sure of it. Something in the dark above the ruined tower answered the Weeping Mode, faint and vast, harmonies stacked on harmonies like snowfall, and Maren heard it, Maren must have heard it, because she lifted her tear-streaked face to the empty sky and made a sound that was half sob and half laugh.

"It remembers," she whispered. "After everything. It *remembers*."

Then she was up—one fluid motion, soldier-quick, snatching her sword—and she was retreating, backing away toward her horse with her blade pointed at the ground and her eyes wet and wild.

"This isn't finished," she said hoarsely. "I have a column two days south. When I report—when Corrin learns the conduit is bound for the Spire—" She stopped. Pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes. When she looked at Sera again, the Inquisitor was back, but thinner, stretched over something bleeding. "Kill me next time, Sera. If it comes to it. Don't sing to me again. I can't—I won't survive being reminded twice."

She mounted. She turned the horse south. At the edge of the dark she paused, and did not look back, and said, so quietly the wind nearly took it:

"The dome acoustics really were perfect. You'd have loved it."

Then she was gone, hoofbeats fading down the frozen road, and Sera stood alone in the ruin with her sword arm trembling and the Weeping Mode still dying in her throat.

Jude came to stand beside her. He said nothing for a long time. Then: "Two days."

"Less, if she pushes." Sera sheathed her sword. Her hands wouldn't fully stop shaking. "We march tonight. No fires. If Corrin's column is moving on the Spire, every hour we sleep is an hour we give him."

Jude nodded—but he was looking south, down the road Maren had taken, and his expression was one Sera hadn't seen on him before. Not fear. Not the hunted animal watchfulness of fifteen years. Something more like recognition, and pity, and underneath it a grim settling, as if some question he'd been carrying had finally been answered.

"She's still in there," he said quietly. "Under the song. She's still fighting it." He turned to Sera, and the ember-light of his brand reflected in his eyes. "Which means it can be fought. Which means everyone in Hollowmere is still in there too."

Sera looked down the dark road, toward the south, toward the approaching drums of Corrin's host, and felt the shard of stolen Voice pulse warm against her heart like something impatient to be used.

"Yes," she said. "And we're going to need every one of them before this is over."
