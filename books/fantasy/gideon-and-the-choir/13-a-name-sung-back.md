## 13. A Name Sung Back

The deepest vault of Meridian's hidden archive lay beneath the roots of the city itself, older than the free cities, older perhaps than the war that had broken the world's singing. Jude felt it in his teeth before he saw it: a pressure like deep water, like standing inside a held breath.

The archivist—Brother Ossian, half-mad, magnificent, his robes stained with ink and candlewax and something darker that Jude chose not to identify—led him down a stair that spiraled the wrong way. Not clockwise. Counter to every stair Jude had ever climbed, as though it had been built by someone walking backward through time.

"The founders cut this place out of the mountain before Meridian was Meridian," Ossian said, his voice bouncing strangely off walls that seemed to drink sound rather than return it. "They said the stone remembered. They said—" He stopped on a landing, turned, and his eyes were wet and wild and suddenly terribly clear. "They said a conduit would come one day. Fifteen years I've kept the lamps lit for you, boy. Fifteen years of being called a madman."

"You were a madman," Jude said gently. "You ate a page last week because it was 'out of tune.'"

"It *was* out of tune." Ossian resumed descending. "The copyist had no ear. It's not my fault the rest of you are deaf."

The stair ended at a door of black iron worked with a single symbol: seven vertical lines crossed by one horizontal stroke. The mark of the Choir. The mark that burned on Jude's own forearm beneath his sleeve, the brand that had flared white-hot the night the stars went out.

He pressed his palm against the iron, and the door sang.

Not creaked. Sang—a low, resonant tone that traveled up through his bones and settled behind his sternum where the warmth of the dead god lived, and the door swung inward on hinges that made no sound at all.

* * *

The vault was round, and small, and empty.

Jude had expected shelves. Tomes. Relics. Instead there was only bare stone, a floor of polished slate worn smooth by centuries of feet—or knees—and a single circle inscribed in the center, its grooves filled with silver that still caught the light of the lamp Ossian carried.

"This is it?" Jude's voice came out flat, echoing wrong. "This is what you've guarded your whole life?"

"This is where they waited," Ossian said quietly. All the madness had gone out of him, leaving something old and tired and reverent. "Stand in the circle, conduit. And whatever happens—whatever you hear—do not stop listening. That is the whole rite. That is all it has ever asked."

Jude stepped into the silver.

The ghost-choir, which had hummed at the edge of his hearing since Ashfall—fragments of melody, half-remembered harmonies, a warmth like sunlight through stained glass—went silent.

Then it came together.

It happened the way fog becomes a figure, the way scattered notes become a song: the presence that had trailed him for weeks gathered itself, drew boundaries, took shape. He felt them the way you feel a crowd pressing close in a dark room—not bodies, exactly, but the memory of bodies, dozens of them, scores, each one carrying a name like a coal cupped in bare hands.

And then, for the first time in fifteen years, someone spoke to Jude Yarrow in the voice of the Choir.

*"We are here."*

He staggered. The voice was not one voice. It was many voices braided into one, male and female and neither, young and ancient, and underneath all of it ran a current of grief so vast it had its own weather.

"Who are you?" His own voice sounded small in the stone room. "What are you?"

*"We are the conduits who came before you."* A pause, and within the pause he felt them arranging themselves, choosing how much truth he could bear. *"Every hand that ever bore the mark. Every throat that ever carried the covenant. We are the ones the Choir loved, and we are the ones who failed it, and we have been waiting in the silence for fifteen years for someone stubborn enough to come down these stairs."*

The words landed in Jude like stones dropped into still water, and the ripples went out and out and would not stop.

"The conduits," he said. "All of them. The order was slaughtered at Vell's Crossing. I *saw*—"

*"You saw what Corrin wanted the world to see."* The braid of voices softened, gentled, the way you speak to a man standing at the edge of something high. *"Some died that day, yes. But most of us did not die, Jude Yarrow. We left."*

The lamp flame bent sideways, though there was no wind.

"We left?" The words tasted like copper. "I was there. I heard the Choir go silent mid-battle. I watched the wards fail. I watched Corrin's soldiers cut through us like we were nothing, and I ran, and I have spent fifteen years believing I was the only coward in the entire covenant—"

*"You were not a coward."*

"Don't." His hands had become fists without his permission. "Don't you dare absolve me. You don't know what I did. You don't know what I—"

*"We know everything you did."* The voices did not rise; they deepened, filling the vault the way water fills a cup. *"We have been with you since Ashfall. We heard every charm you sold, every lie you told, every night you lay awake hating yourself for surviving. We know, Jude. We know because we made the same choice you made, and we have had fifteen years longer to live with it."*

"What choice? You're dead. You're ghosts. Whatever you did ended when—"

*"We silenced ourselves."*

The words fell into the room and broke apart slowly, and Jude stood very still inside the silver circle while their meaning assembled itself piece by terrible piece.

"What?"

*"Fifteen years ago, Corrin came to us in secret. Before Vell's Crossing. Before the war you remember. He came with an offer wrapped in a warning: the Choir's Voice was power beyond anything the world had seen, and he intended to hold it. He had already learned how to take it. He had already begun."* The grief in the braided voice crested and receded, tide-like. *"A Voice can be stolen only from those who use it. Every miracle we worked, every ward we raised, every healing song—we were feeding him. We were teaching him the shape of the thing he wanted. So we stopped."*

"No." Jude's heart was slamming against his ribs. "No, the Choir didn't go silent. It was *taken*. Corrin stole it—that's why the stars went out, that's why the wards fell—"

*"Corrin stole what we refused to give freely, and could take only by force, and force takes only part."* Something moved through the vault then, a pressure like a great sorrow turning over in its sleep. *"Do you understand what we are telling you? The silence was not his victory. The silence was our sacrifice. We cut our own god's tongue rather than let him carve it out whole. We unmade ourselves as conduits—burned the covenant out of our own flesh, one by one, in a circle on a hill above Vell's Crossing, while his soldiers butchered the singers below who thought we had abandoned them."*

Jude's legs gave out.

He sat down hard on the cold slate, inside the silver ring, and the world tilted and righted and tilted again. "The singers," he whispered. "Sera's sisters. The choir-singers at the Spire. They fought and died thinking the conduits deserted them."

*"Yes."*

"And me. I was nineteen years old. I was on the field. I felt the god die in my arms like a bird with a broken neck, and I ran, and I have hated myself every single day of my life for running—because I thought everyone else stayed. Because I thought I alone was the coward." He was laughing now, or something adjacent to laughing, a cracked and awful sound. "There wasn't anyone else. There was never anyone else. You all left first."

*"We left together."* The voices were gentle, and that gentleness was unbearable. *"You were not summoned to the hill, Jude. You were too young, too new-marked. The elders decided you should be spared the choice. When the silence fell, you experienced it as death. We experienced it as amputation. Neither of us was spared. There is no version of that night that does not bleed."*

"Spared." He spat the word. "You call it spared. I sold luck-knots to children. I pretended my god was dead for fifteen years. I let the whole world believe the covenant was broken because I was too ashamed to ask questions, too afraid to look for answers, and meanwhile—" He surged to his feet, and the silver circle blazed under him, answering his fury whether he willed it or not. "Meanwhile the stars have been going out. Meanwhile Corrin has been building an army out of *stolen songs*, hollowing people out, turning villages into vacant humming puppets. And you knew. You've been down here in the dark knowing, and you *waited*. You waited for me. Why? Why now? What changed?"

The silence that followed had texture. It had weight. He felt them conferring, the dead conduits, weighing him, and the humiliation of it—the sense of being measured by the very ghosts whose cowardice he was drowning in—made the mark on his arm burn.

Finally:

*"Because the silence is ending either way."*

"What does that mean?"

*"Corrin has nearly finished his machine. The stolen Voice grows stronger as the true Voice starves. Soon he will hold enough of it that our sacrifice will mean nothing—soon he will be able to take the rest by force, and there will be no covenant left to reforge, no god left to wake. We chose silence to save the Voice. That choice is expiring."* A pause, and when the voices resumed they were quieter, almost human, almost ashamed. *"So we must choose again. And this time, Jude Yarrow, we cannot choose for you. This time the covenant passes through you alone."*

"Then tell me what it costs." He gripped the edges of the burning mark on his arm as if he could wring the answer out of his own flesh. "Tell me what waking the Choir requires. Tell me the terms. You owe me that—you owe the world that, after fifteen years of hiding in a hole under a city while it burned—"

*"We owe you more than terms."* For a moment the braid of voices frayed, and he heard them individually: a woman with a lowland accent, a man laughing through tears, a child-conduit who had never lived past her twelfth year. Then they knit together again. *"We owe you the truth, and the truth is this: we were not cowards, Jude. We were afraid. There is a difference, and it took us fifteen years of silence to learn it. Cowardice runs from the price. Fear walks toward it shaking. We walked toward it. We burned ourselves out of the covenant to keep a god's voice out of a tyrant's mouth, and if that was the wrong choice—if silence was its own kind of theft—then we will spend whatever remains of us paying for it. But do not stand in our vault and call us cowards until you know what we gave up. Until you know what the covenant asks."*

"Then tell me!" The word tore out of him raw. "*Tell me!*"

The vault answered.

Light rose out of the silver circle—not lamplight, not starlight, but something older, the color of the sky before dawn, the color of a held note—and the ghost-choir showed him.

He saw the covenant as it had been written at the beginning of things, before kingdoms, before wars: a god made of every voice that ever chose to sing together, and the law of its existence simple and absolute and merciless. *A voice given freely. A voice given wholly. A voice given forever.* Not blood. Not life. Something worse. Something that could not be taken, only surrendered.

He saw what it meant. He saw the shape of the price, and he understood why the dead conduits had chosen silence instead, and he understood—with the first crack of something that was not quite forgiveness but was no longer fury—why they had waited fifteen years in the dark for someone else to climb down these stairs.

The light withdrew. The vision closed like a hand.

Jude stood in the circle, shaking, the knowledge sitting in his chest like a swallowed coal.

"Now you know," the ghost-choir said softly. "Now you know why we hid, and why we waited, and what we are asking. Take what time you need, conduit. But not too much. Because three days from now, Corrin's vanguard reaches the Salt Gate—and when it falls, and it will fall, the last road to the Silent Spire closes forever."

Above them, far above, through a hundred feet of living rock, the bells of Meridian began to ring the alarm.

* * *

Ossian was waiting at the top of the reversed stair, lamp trembling in his hand. He looked at Jude's face and whatever he saw there made the old man step back against the wall.

"Well?" he whispered. "Did they speak? Did they tell you?"

"They told me." Jude climbed past him, moving like a man in a dream, the covenant's price ringing in him louder than the bells. "They told me everything."

He pushed open the archive door into the cold night air of Meridian, where fires were already blooming on the horizon like false stars, and began to walk toward the Salt Gate.

He did not tell Ossian what it cost.

He was not sure he ever would.
