## 14. The Battle of the Salt Gate

The horns came an hour before dawn.

Jude heard them in his sleep first—or thought he did—because for three nights the ghost-choir had been singing in a key he had never learned, low and urgent, like water finding cracks in a dam. He woke on the cot they'd given him in the garrison house with his brand already burning through his shirt, and by the time his boots hit the floor he understood: this was not a warning. This was a countdown.

He climbed to the wall above the Salt Gate with General Vess's old cloak around his shoulders and the taste of iron in his mouth. Below, the salt flats stretched white and endless under a sky still bruised with night, and out there at the edge of seeing, Corrin's vanguard moved like a wound opening across the world. Ten thousand men, maybe more, their armor catching the first gray light so that the whole column shimmered—and beneath the shimmer, something worse. They marched in perfect unison. Not drilled unison. Sung unison. Every bootfall landed on the same beat, and when the wind turned, Jude heard what kept that beat: a hum, vast and flat and wrong, rolling off the army like heat off stone.

It was the Choir's music. Flattened. Bled of everything but rhythm. A hymn with the soul scraped out of it.

"They'll reach the gate by midmorning," Vess said beside him. The old general's voice was steady, but her knuckles were white on the battlement stone. "We have four hundred spears on this wall, two hundred archers, and whatever courage Meridian hasn't spent yet. Against that." She nodded at the column. "I've held gates before, Yarrow. I've never held one against a song."

Jude said nothing. His brand pulsed with each beat of the stolen hum, and he could feel it now, unmistakable, the way you feel your own heartbeat when you're afraid—the Voice inside Corrin's war-machine, wearing his god's throat like a mask. Fifteen years ago he had stood on a different wall and felt that same presence go silent mid-battle, felt it die in him like a snuffed candle, and he had run. He had run from the silence and the slaughter and the shame of both.

The ghost-choir stirred at the base of his skull, a thousand voices murmuring half-words. *Not again,* they seemed to say. Or perhaps: *Again.* With the dead it was hard to tell entreaty from command.

"Yarrow." Vess was watching him. "Whatever you're thinking of doing—don't do it alone."

* * *

Midmorning came gray and windless, and the vanguard hit the outer earthworks like a tide hitting a seawall.

Jude watched from the gatehouse as the first ranks broke apart—not into chaos but into geometry, soldiers flowing around obstacles in patterns too smooth for human command, scaling the palisades with hands that never slipped, never hesitated, never screamed even when the archers found them. That was the worst of it. There were no screams from the attacking line at all. Men took arrows through the chest and kept climbing, faces empty as washed slate, humming.

The Salt Gate itself was oak and iron older than the free cities, banded with ward-stones laid down before Corrin was born. For the first hour those stones held. They glowed sullen amber each time a ram struck or a ladder thudded home, and each glow cost them something visible—a flicker, a dimming, like a man growing pale from blood loss.

"They're singing at the wards," Sera said. She had appeared at his shoulder without sound; she did that. Her face was ash-gray, her eyes fixed on the field where, among the siege crews, certain soldiers stood in rings with their heads thrown back, mouths open, pouring that flattened hymn against Meridian's defenses like water against a door. "Corrin's song-taken. The wards were built to stop force. Nobody built them to stop *music*."

"How long?"

She didn't answer, which was an answer.

The left tower's ward-stones went dark just past noon. Jude saw it happen—saw the amber gutter and die—and then he saw the ladders come up that stretch of wall like a harvest of spears, and he saw the first song-taken soldier crest the parapet, and he saw young Wren Calloway—young Wren, who had bought a luck-knot from him in Ashfall a lifetime ago, who had grinned up at him with a gap in her teeth—step forward to meet it with a spear she barely knew how to hold.

Something in Jude tore loose.

Later, he would try to remember making a decision, and there would be nothing there. No deliberation, no prayer, no bargain struck with the dead. Only the gap-toothed grin, and the arrow-slit of time between the soldier's blade rising and falling, and his own feet carrying him down the gatehouse stairs three at a time while somewhere behind him Vess shouted a name that wasn't quite his anymore.

* * *

The courtyard below the left tower was a churn of smoke and screaming now, the one place in the battle where the song-taken had broken formation enough to make it a fight instead of a mechanism. Jude came through the smoke and found Wren on her back, spear gone, the soldier standing over her with his sword drawn back—and Jude did the only thing his body knew how to do after fifteen years of selling charms and hiding warmth under his skin.

He sang.

Not a spell. He didn't know spells, not really, not the way the mad archivist had tried to teach him with his ink-stained fingers and his fractured liturgies. What came out of Jude Yarrow was rawer than craft. It was the covenant itself, the oldest words, the ones carved into the Silent Spire before the free cities had names—he gave them voice for the first time since Ashfall, since the night the stars went out, and his brand answered like a struck bell.

Sound went out of him in a ring.

It was not loud. That was the terrible, beautiful thing about it—it barely rose above the battle's din, and yet every ear within the courtyard caught it, and every body obeyed it. The soldier over Wren froze mid-swing, his empty face flickering like a lamp in wind, and for one heartbeat something looked out through his eyes—something drowned and desperate and *grateful*—before he crumpled, unconscious, the stolen song knocked loose from him like a bone knocked back into joint. The nearest ladders shuddered and slid. Two more song-taken dropped their weapons and sat down in the mud, weeping without knowing why.

And the ghost-choir rose in Jude like floodwater through a floorboard.

They had been murmuring for weeks, fragments, hints, half-remembered verses pressed against the inside of his skull. Now they came through him whole. He felt them pour along his nerves and out of his open mouth, a thousand dead conduits singing through one living throat, and the sound built and built until it was no longer a ring but a pillar, a column of harmony standing in the courtyard like a second tower, and where it touched the fighting, the fighting stopped. Blades lowered. Spears drooped. Song-taken soldiers staggered and clutched their heads, and some fell, and some knelt, and one—an officer, gold-braided, his face the emptiest of all—began to scream, high and cracked, as if something were being pulled out of him by the roots.

Jude sang the wards back alight.

He didn't know how he knew to do it; the knowledge arrived the way the choir arrived, unbidden, ancient, borrowed. He turned toward the darkened tower stones and put the covenant's name-names into the song—the true names of the ward-layers, dead five hundred years, names the ghost-choir remembered because the ghost-choir *was* the memory of everyone who had ever given itself to the god—and the stones drank the sound and flared from amber to white, brighter than they had ever burned in life, and the ladders on the left wall peeled away like bark.

On the battlements, four hundred spears stopped retreating and started shouting.

* * *

He held the gate for an hour. He would learn later exactly how long—an hour and eleven minutes, measured by a quartermaster who had nothing else to watch—and he would remember almost none of it as time. It came to him afterward in pieces, the way a storm comes to a man who has stood in its eye:

Wren scrambling up behind him, pressing her ruined luck-knot into his hand as if returning a relic, and the knot—which should never have worked, which had been hollow the day he sold it—glowing faintly warm against his palm, feeding him, somehow, the way a candle feeds a flame.

Sera at his other shoulder, singing counterpoint, her trained voice threading through the choir's mass like a needle through cloth, stitching his raw power into shapes that could be aimed. She had buried too many conduits to hope easily, she'd told him once. In that hour she hoped with everything she had, and it sounded like glory.

Vess holding the courtyard with two hundred reserves, buying him the space to stand still, because standing still was all the working required—all *he* required. The magic moved through him; he was only the door, and doors must not flinch.

And underneath everything, always, the hum of Corrin's host grinding against the covenant like a millstone, patient, enormous, hungry. Twice the flattened hymn surged and nearly smothered them, and twice the ghost-choir bent the melody aside, the dead conduits knowing the stolen Voice's every phrase because it had been *theirs*, once, before a usurper cut it out of their god's throat and taught it to march.

When it ended, it ended all at once. Horns from the enemy lines—real horns, human ones, officers' calls—and the vanguard drew back off the earthworks in good order, dragging its ladders, leaving its dead and its broken song-taken scattered across the salt flats like dolls dropped by a giant. The sun hung low and red over the retreat. The wards of the Salt Gate burned white and steady. And in the courtyard, in the ringing quiet, four hundred soldiers turned as one to look at the man in the old general's cloak, the man still standing with his arms raised and light pouring off his brand like heat-shimmer, the man who had done with a song what two days of steel could not.

Jude lowered his arms. His legs nearly went with them. Sera caught his elbow, and her face, when he found the strength to look at it, was radiant and stricken in equal measure.

"You did it," she whispered. "You held it. Jude—" She stopped. He watched her understand it a moment before he did, watched the joy drain out of her expression and leave something colder behind. "Everyone saw."

* * *

They came to him all evening. That was how he thought of it afterward—they *came to him*, the way the sick come to a shrine, the way moths come to glass.

First the wounded, carried up from the infirmary on stretchers, laid at his feet in the garrison hall by soldiers who would not meet his eyes but would not leave either. A woman with a shattered arm. A boy no older than Wren, gray-lipped, breathing in shallow pulls. "Please," someone said, and it was not a request so much as a diagnosis: *please* was simply what people said now, to him, the way they said *sir*. Jude knelt and sang the small covenants—the mending-verses, the ones the archivist had drilled into him until his gums bled—and the arm knit crooked but whole, and the boy's color came back, and the hall filled with a hush that felt less like gratitude than like hunger.

Then the others. Merchants with purses full of silver, offering it awkwardly, not as payment but as tribute. Mothers with infants, asking him to breathe on them. A council aide with a ledger, requesting—politely, terribly—that the conduit record his workings for study, for defense, for *replication*, and Jude heard in the word *replication* the whole future the free cities were already imagining for him: a tool, a resource, a wall that walked and talked and could be owned.

And beneath all of it, threaded through the crowd like wire through wool, the ones who weren't looking at him at all. He felt them the way he'd learned to feel Corrin's spies in Meridian—a prickle at the nape, a wrongness in the room's attention. Three of them by the wine table, drinking little, watching much. One near the doors with a merchant's coat and a soldier's stillness. One who left within minutes of arriving and whose departure Sera tracked with her eyes until the door closed.

"They know," she said quietly, coming to stand beside him. Around them the hall roared on, drunk on salvation. "Every spy in this city saw what happened on that wall. By tomorrow morning Corrin will know your face, your name, and exactly where you sleep. By the day after, Maren will know it too." She paused. "There is no wall in Meridian that means anything now. Not to what's coming."

Jude looked down at his hands. They were shaking—not from the working, or not only. The exhilaration was still in him, bright and shameful, the memory of the choir pouring through his body like a river through a broken boat, and he wanted it again. Gods help him, he wanted it again. And threaded through the wanting, cold as a hook, was the knowledge of what the ghost-choir had told him in the archive vault, the price written into the covenant's deepest terms, the thing he had told no one: *a voice freely given, permanently—one's name, one's memory, one's self.*

He had held the gate. He had saved hundreds. And every hour that passed made the final payment larger, because now the whole world was watching the last conduit of the Choir, waiting to see what he would become.

"Then we don't sleep here," Jude said. "And we don't wait for them to come to me."

Sera searched his face. "What are you saying?"

Outside, far across the darkened salt flats, a single horn sounded—long, low, and answered by another, and another, a chain of them running north along the horizon like fire along a fuse. The main host was moving. Not toward the gate.

Toward the Spire road. Toward the winter passes. Toward everything that lay between Corrin and the Silent Spire except one exhausted man, one defected singer, and a city that had just learned—too late, and loudly—exactly what it was sheltering.

Jude picked up Wren's luck-knot from the table and closed his fist around its faint, impossible warmth.

"We march tonight," he said. "Before they realize they don't need to besiege us. They only need to follow."
