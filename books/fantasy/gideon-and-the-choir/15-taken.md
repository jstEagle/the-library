## 15. Taken

The retreat began in rain and ended in blood.

Sera ran with the last of Meridian's survivors through the salt marshes south of the city, her boots sucking at the mud, her lungs full of iron-tasting air. Behind them the Salt Gate burned—not with ordinary fire but with sound, a column of white noise rising into the low clouds where Corrin's stolen Voice had torn a hole in the world's quiet. She could feel it even here, three miles gone: that pressure against the eardrum, like standing too close to an organ's largest pipe, like being inside a struck bell.

Jude was somewhere behind her. He had to be. He had held the gate—she had seen him do it, seen him stand on the broken parapet with his arms open and his throat working, singing a covenant he'd only half-remembered a season ago, and the gate had *held*, the vanguard breaking against his song like water against stone. Hundreds had lived because of it. And now every spy, every sympathizer, every song-taken ear in Meridian knew exactly what he was.

So they ran. The general was dead; the council was scattered; two cities had already signed their secret terms with the usurper, and their soldiers had simply walked out of the line during the battle, unhurried, humming.

Sera counted heads as she ran. It was a habit from the choir—from the years before she understood what the choir was for. Forty-one survivors in her stretch of the marsh. A cart with a wounded boy whose bandages had soaked through. Old Tam with his bad hip, leaning on his granddaughter. Jude—

She turned to look for him, and that was when the net came down.

Not rope. Song. It fell over the reeds like weather, a descending chord so beautiful her knees nearly buckled, and every survivor within earshot stopped mid-stride as if the ground had remembered them. Sera fought it—she had been trained in this music, she had *sung* this music, before she understood what it unmade—and for eleven heartbeats she held the chord away from her own mind, teeth gritted, hands pressed over her ears as if flesh could stop what entered through the bone.

Twelve heartbeats. Thirteen.

Then hands took her arms, and a hood went over her head that smelled of lamp-oil and myrrh, and the chord withdrew as suddenly as it had come, leaving her kneeling in the mud with forty-one people around her who could not remember why they had stopped.

She heard Maren's voice before the hood muffled everything.

"The singer," Maren said, not to her, not kindly. "Corrin wants the conduit. But he said—" a pause Sera would spend days unpacking, "—he said bring me the one who taught her to run."

* * *

They kept her awake.

That was the first thing Sera learned about Corrin's methods, in the windowless hold of a river barge moving north through drowned country: there was no beating, no irons, no darkness. There was only light—clean, constant, sourceless—and the song.

They sat her in a chair that reclined like a physician's table, and Inquisitor Maren stood at her shoulder, and somewhere beyond the light a machine hummed, and then her own memories began to play back at her.

That was the horror of it. Not pain. *Playback.*

Her mother's kitchen, rendered in perfect detail—the smell of cardamom bread, the chipped blue bowl, the way afternoon light came through the east window in a slanting gold bar—and all of it carried on a melody, so that remembering became involuntary, became *music*, became something happening to her rather than something she chose. She had no defenses against it. You cannot refuse your own past. It rose in her whether she willed it or not, and each memory arrived attached to its note, its phrase, its key, until her whole life was an aria someone else was conducting.

"Tell me about the Spire," Maren said, again.

"I don't know where it is." True. Almost true. She knew how to find it the way you know how to find home—with your feet, with your blood, with a compass made of longing. Coordinates were nothing. But the *way* there, the turning path, the recognition of landmarks that weren't landmarks—that lived in her body, and bodies could be sung.

"You sang the pilgrim route as a novice. I trained you. I know what's in you."

"You trained me to forget it," Sera said. Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "That was the point of the severance rite. You presided."

Something moved behind Maren's face—quick, quickly buried. "The rite can be reversed."

"It can't."

"We've done it nine times."

Sera looked at her then, really looked, through the flat white glare. Maren had aged since the border. Or since Hollowmere. Since whatever moment it was when the conditioning had first cracked and she'd begun fighting her own chords mid-duel, her own hand turning against her own will. There was a tremor in her left fingers now that she hid by clasping them. There was a new gray at her temple. And beneath the inquisitor's composure, faint as a heartbeat under a pillow, Sera could hear it: a second melody running under Maren's speech, off-key, struggling.

Song-taken. Partially. Fighting it.

*Maren,* Sera thought, and almost said the old name—the name from before, the name a frightened novice had whispered to her mentor across a dormitory blanket. *Maru. Little sparrow.* She swallowed it. Pity was a door, and doors opened both ways.

"You're humming," Sera said instead. Quietly. Cruelly, maybe. "Do you hear yourself? Underneath. There's a tune under your voice, Maru. Whose is it?"

Maren's hand closed on the chair's rail hard enough to whiten her knuckles. For a long moment the only sound was the machine's patient hum.

"Again," Maren said to the room, and the light brightened, and Sera's seventh summer came up out of her like water from a well.

* * *

They played her childhood for six days.

By the third day she had learned the shape of the thing: each memory was a locked box, and the song was the key, and between them stood only her silence. They could make her *feel* anything—they could flood her with the grief of her father's funeral, note by note, until she wept without stopping—but feeling was not telling. She anchored herself on that distinction the way a drowning sailor anchors on a plank. Feel everything. Say nothing.

But the song was patient, and it was clever, and on the fourth night it changed tactics.

It gave her the choir.

Her novitiate years, her training, the great vaulted rehearsal hall at the Convent of the Ascending Note where thirty voices had learned to braid themselves into one—and the song played it back so faithfully that Sera felt the other singers beside her, felt the conductor's raised hand, felt the exact moment in the Lamentation of Ashes when her own line rose alone above the texture, suspended, held by the breath of everyone below her.

And her mouth opened. Her body, remembering, *sang*.

"No—" she got out, around the note, and clamped her jaw, but the melody kept moving in her chest, in her diaphragm, in the old trained muscles that knew these shapes better than they knew walking. The song wasn't asking her to speak. It was asking her to sing, and her body wanted to, God help her, her body had missed it for fifteen years, and the relief of letting the line rise was so vast and so sweet that she rode four bars of the Lamentation before she understood what she was doing.

She was harmonizing with the machine. She was giving it her pitch. And a singer who gives her pitch can be tuned.

She bit through her lip to stop. The taste of blood grounded her, and she used it—pain as a drone note, pain as a pedal tone beneath the melody, something true and hers and unsingable. When the memory-flood crested again she fixed her eyes on Maren and thought: *you too. They did this to you too. Somewhere in you there's a girl who sang the Lamentation and meant it.*

On the fifth night, delirious with exhaustion, she dreamed in the machine's key. On the sixth morning, Maren dismissed the technicians and sat down across from her, and for a while neither spoke.

"You hate me," Maren said.

"I pity you," Sera said. Her voice was ruined silk. "It's worse."

Maren studied her hands. The tremor was worse too; Sera could see it now without trying. "I reported that the interrogation was progressing slowly. That you were resistant. I bought you time. I want you to know that, whatever else—"

"Why?" The word cracked out of her. "Why buy time? Why hesitate? What are they holding over you, Maren? What did they put in your song?"

For a moment—one long, naked moment—Maren's composure failed entirely, and Sera saw underneath: terror. Not of Corrin. Of the music. Of the small off-key melody living under her skin that sometimes, in the dark, suggested things to her. Things about doors opening. Things about people who should be protected.

"He knows what the Choir was," Maren said finally, very quietly. "He told me once, before—before I understood. He said the god didn't die of our war. He said it died of *us*, of what we asked it to carry. And I believed him. I believed him for twenty years, Sera. Belief is also a kind of song. You don't notice you're humming it until someone cuts the string."

"And now?"

"Now I hear humming everywhere," Maren said, "and I no longer know which of it is theirs and which of it is mine," and she stood, and the mask came down, and she was the Inquisitor again. "The Lord Corrin arrives tomorrow. He'll ask you himself. I would advise you to consider what your resistance is actually purchasing. The conduit will be found regardless. The Spire will be found. All your silence buys is your own suffering."

"All my silence buys," Sera said, "is time. And time is the only army we have left."

Maren paused at the door. Without turning: "You always were the stubborn one. Even as a novice. Sister Veyl used to say you'd break before you'd bend."

"She was right," Sera said. "Ask her. If any of us are still alive."

* * *

Vael Corrin came at dusk, alone.

That was somehow the worst of it. Sera had imagined the usurper surrounded by guards, by instruments, by the machinery of his stolen god. Instead a single man walked into the white-lit hold—a tall, spare figure in undyed wool, gray going soft at the temples, with the mild face of a schoolmaster—and the machine's hum bent toward him like grass toward wind. He carried no weapon. He carried, Sera realized with a lurch, the Voice itself, folded somewhere in his chest, and the whole room was resonating with it whether he willed it or not.

"Sera Vane," he said. Her name in his mouth sounded like a verdict. "Former first soprano, Convent of the Ascending Note. Defected the winter the wards fell. Companion to the last conduit of the Choir." He drew up a chair and sat, unhurried, as if visiting a sick friend. "You've been very brave. I want you to know I take no pleasure in this."

"Then stop."

"I can't." No drama in it. Simple arithmetic. "There is a war. Wars require certainty. You have information I need, and you are strong enough that ordinary means won't reach it. So I brought the extraordinary means." He folded his hands. "I'm going to tell you something true, because you deserve one true thing before the end. Fifteen years ago, the Choir begged me to stop."

Sera went still.

"It sang to me on the eve of the final campaign—it sang to all of us who were conduits' kin, though none of us had words for it then. It said: *enough*. It said the covenant was never meant to feed on slaughter, that every voice given in battle was a voice it could not give back. And I looked at the enemy at our gates, and I looked at my soldiers, and I decided that a god's scruples were a luxury the living could not afford." His eyes were terribly calm. "So I silenced it. I have spent fifteen years using its voice to do exactly what it begged me not to do. I am not ignorant of what I am, Sera Vane. I am only certain that the alternative was worse. Someone had to save the world from its gods. It fell to me."

"That's not saving," Sera whispered. "That's just a bigger cage."

"Perhaps." He rose. "Now. The Silent Spire. Your body knows the road—I can hear it in you, the way the knowing sits in your marrow rather than your mind. I'm going to sing your marrow, and you're going to walk that road for me, in your blood if not your lips. I regret it. Do you understand? I genuinely regret it. But the conduit must be taken before he reaches the altar, and you are the map."

He began to sing.

It was not like the machine. The machine had played her memories back; Corrin *conducted* her, reached into the place where her body kept its secret geography and turned it into sound. She felt the road rise in her—the marsh causeway, the black pines, the frozen falls, the hidden stair—felt it lift out of her muscle by muscle, note by note, a pilgrimage unwritten from her bones. She fought it the only way left. She thought of Jude. She thought of his terrible raw singing on the Salt Gate, beauty with no training in it at all, and she poured everything she had into holding one syllable of the route, one bend in the stair, one stone.

She held it for an hour. She would remember that afterward, in the years when she needed proof of her own endurance: one hour, against the stolen voice of a god.

Then her body broke—not her will, never her will, but the marrow itself, the deep animal knowledge, and the road came out of her in a rush like a hemorrhage, and she heard it take shape in the air as melody, complete, precise, a map set to music.

Corrin stopped. Listened. And smiled—not triumph; something sadder and more terrible, the smile of a man confirming a diagnosis.

"There it is," he said softly. "Thank you, Sera. Truly."

She hung in the chair, emptied, shaking, her lip bitten through, her eyes burning. And in the wreckage of her silence she found one last thing, one small hoarded coin, and spent it all:

"He'll be ready for you," she rasped. "You're not taking a boy to the Spire, Corrin. You're taking bait to a hook. The dead conduits are waiting there—all of them, every voice you ever stole—and they have been *fifteen years hungry*."

For the first time, something flickered in the usurper's calm. Briefly, Sera saw it: not fear, exactly. Recognition. The look of a man who has heard, at last, a note he cannot conduct.

At the door, Maren waited with the escort. As they led Sera away—as the barge turned north, toward the pines, toward the frozen falls, toward the Spire whose road now lived in the enemy's throat—Sera caught her old mentor's eye.

And Maren, in front of the guards, in front of the machine, almost imperceptibly, began to hum.

Off-key. Off-purpose. A lullaby from a convent dormitory, fifteen years dead.

A song that belonged to no one but them.
