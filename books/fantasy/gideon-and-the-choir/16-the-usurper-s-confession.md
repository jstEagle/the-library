## 16. The Usurper's Confession

The Voice slept in a vault beneath the Citadel of Vail, and every night Vael Corrin descended to it alone.

He told his generals it was discipline. He told his priests—those few he had not yet folded into the machinery of song—that it was vigilance. He told himself, in the first years, that it was stewardship: someone had to sit with the stolen thing, someone had to keep it fed and quiet, the way a man keeps a fire in a hearth that once burned down a city.

Fifteen years of nights, and he had never once spoken the truth of it aloud. Not because there was no one to hear. Because there was no one who would have understood that the truth was not what they thought it was. They imagined a tyrant gloating over a chained god. They were wrong about the gloat.

* * *

The vault was cut from black granite older than any kingdom, and it was cold in the way deep places are cold—not with malice but with indifference, the cold of stone that has outlasted everything warm above it. Torches of white flame burned without flickering along the descent; he had made them so, because a trembling light suggested a trembling hand, and he could not abide the suggestion.

At the bottom stood the reliquary: a sphere of leaded glass the size of a cathedral bell, and inside it, suspended in nothing, the Voice.

It did not look like much. That was the first lie of it. A curl of silver-white radiance, no larger than a sleeping cat, turning slowly on itself like smoke that had forgotten which way was up. When it stirred—and it only ever stirred for him—the vault filled with a pressure that had nothing to do with air. It pressed against the teeth. It pressed against the memory. It pressed against whatever soft organ in a man's chest kept time with music.

Corrin unfastened his gauntlets, finger by finger, and set them on the iron shelf. He always came bare-handed. He had never been able to explain why, even to himself, except that it felt like courtesy, and courtesy was the last ritual of a faith he claimed to have buried.

"You're restless tonight," he said.

The Voice turned. It did not answer. It had not answered him directly in fifteen years—not since the night he took it—but it sang anyway, always, a thread of sound too low for ordinary ears, the way a struck bell goes on ringing long after the room has stopped listening. He heard it because he had made himself into its instrument. Every soldier in his host carried a splinter of this thing in their blood, sung into them at their forging, but Corrin carried the whole weight of the frequency in his own throat. When he conducted the great workings—when he drew the fear out of ten thousand men at once and burned it as fuel—he felt the Voice pass through him like a river through a drowned man.

He had learned, over the years, to call that sensation power. It was easier than calling it what it resembled more closely: drowning.

"I've had news," he said, drawing the plain wooden stool—he kept a stool, absurdly domestic—to the center of the floor. "Maren has taken the singer. Sera Vane. She held out longer than I expected. They usually do, the ones who loved the Choir." He paused. "She broke just enough. The Spire."

The radiance flared. Just slightly. A candle guttering in a draft, or an eye opening behind a closed lid.

Corrin sat, elbows on knees, hands loose between them, and looked at the thing he had spent half his life pretending was a tool.

"You know what's coming," he said. "Of course you know. You've always known things before they happen. That was the whole obscenity of you." He smiled, and the smile had nowhere to go, so it died on his face like a moth against glass. "A conduit lives. One last conduit, out of all the ones we burned. And he's walking toward my Spire—you remember it, don't you? You sang there, before I was born. Before anyone alive was born. The Silent Spire, where the covenant was first spoken." His voice flattened. "Where he means to give you back your tongue."

The silence that followed was not empty. It never was. Silence, around the Voice, was a held breath.

* * *

He had not meant to confess tonight. He had meant to review the muster rolls, to confirm the winter supply lines to the Salt Gate, to do the small gray arithmetic of conquest that filled his days and kept the larger questions sedated. But the news of Sera's capture had cracked something, and cracks in Vael Corrin ran deeper than anyone suspected, because he had spent fifteen years filling them with certainty, and certainty is mortar that shrinks.

"Tell me if I have this right," he said, and his voice came out lower than he intended. "I'll say it plainly, since no one else will ever hear it. Fifteen years ago, at Redford Field, you went silent. Mid-battle. Forty thousand men on each side, and the conduits of the Choir standing in the mud with their arms raised, waiting for the war-song that had ended every battle for nine hundred years." He could still see it. He would always see it. The conduits' faces going from ecstasy to confusion to terror, one after another, like lamps blown out by a wind moving down a line. "And everyone said the god had died. Abandoned us. Turned its face away. The Church said it. My generals said it. I said it."

He leaned forward.

"But that isn't what happened, is it?"

The Voice turned, and turned, patient as water.

"It stopped singing because I asked it to stop," Corrin said. "That's the version I keep in the locked room. I asked. Politely. At first." He laughed—a short, ugly sound with no humor in it, the laugh of a man finding a bone in his own soup. "Do you know what I asked? I was thirty-one years old. I had already won four battles for the Republic, and each one cost more than the last, and I had begun to notice—I had begun to *notice*, that was my crime, noticing—that the war-songs didn't end wars. They ended battles. Perfectly. Beautifully. We never lost a field. And the fields kept needing to be fought. Nine hundred years of perfect battles, and the war never once got smaller."

His hands had closed without his permission. He opened them deliberately, one finger at a time, the way he removed his gauntlets. Control. Always control. A man who controlled everything could pretend he had chosen everything.

"So I climbed the Spire road alone, in the rain, like a penitent, and I stood where that boy is standing now, or will stand, and I spoke to you. To it. To—" His jaw tightened. "*You.* I said: enough. I said: stop feeding on our wars. Stop taking our dead as payment. Find another way to be worshipped or die quietly like everything else does." He shook his head slowly. "And you know what it did? What you did?"

The radiance pulsed, slow, and for a moment—for one obscene, unguarded moment—Corrin let himself feel it as what it was. Not a tool. Not a weapon. A plea. The Voice had been begging him for three hundred years, through every conduit it sang through, in every hymn written by every frightened priest: *stop giving me your dead. I cannot refuse what you offer. So stop offering.*

"And when it wouldn't answer," Corrin said quietly, "I answered for it."

* * *

They had needed seven choirs to do it. Seven circles of trained singers, the finest in the world, arranged around the altar at the old Spire while he stood above them with the severing rite open on its iron stand, a text so old that three archivists had refused to translate it and the fourth had hanged himself afterward. The rite did not kill a god. He had learned that much. Nothing they possessed could kill it. The rite did something worse, or better, depending on which side of the knife you stood: it took the voice away and left the rest.

A god that cannot speak cannot refuse. A god that cannot refuse can only be used.

"We cut out its tongue," Corrin said to the vault, to the dark, to the turning light. "Seven choirs, and I conducted. Do you know how beautiful it sounded? That's the part I have never told anyone. That's the part that wakes me at the fourth hour with my heart slamming like a fist on a door. It sounded *magnificent*. The most beautiful thing I have ever heard. Every note the Choir had ever sung, played backward and all at once, and underneath it the sound of something vast learning silence." His breath fogged in the cold. "I stood there with tears running down my face thinking: I am saving us. This is salvation. No more war-god. No more tithe of the dead. Men will fight for reasons now, or better, they will stop fighting altogether, and I will hand the world back to itself like a man returning a stolen coat."

He was quiet for a long moment. The torches burned. Somewhere far above, the Citadel hummed with the ordinary noise of an empire at dinner.

"And then," he said, "I forged the first soldier."

* * *

It had seemed reasonable. That was the horror of it, the part he circled every night like a wound he couldn't stop touching: every step had seemed reasonable. The Choir's voice, severed, had to go somewhere—it pooled, it wanted shape, and an unused god-voice curdles into something foul, he had seen it happen in the border provinces, in the things the wards used to hold back. So use it. Give it purpose. Pour it into men—not their fear, precisely, but their capacity for fear, the way a surgeon drains an abscess. A soldier who cannot feel fear fights better. A soldier who cannot feel fear sleeps well. A soldier who cannot feel fear comes home to his wife and children and feels—

Nothing. That was the part the arithmetic had missed. Fear does not live alone in a man. It lives in a house with love, with grief, with joy, with mercy, and when you sing the fear out you take the walls down with it. His soldiers were magnificent. His soldiers were serene. His soldiers wrote home letters in a child's flat hand saying *all is well, all is well, all is well,* and their villages began to hum, and the humming spread, and Corrin had stood on the walls of Hollowmere last autumn listening to a whole town sing a lullaby in unison at three in the morning, and he had known.

He had known exactly what he was.

"The Choir begged us to stop feeding it war," he said, and now his voice was very steady, the steadiness of ice over deep water, "and I could not bear a god that asked me to be less than I was. So I silenced it. And then I became the thing I had cut out of the world. I feed on war. I have built a machine that eats fear and excretes obedience, and I have dressed it in the language of peace—no more conscription, no more massacres, my soldiers don't rape and don't pillage and don't desert, ask anyone, ask the occupied cities, life under Corrin is *calm*—" He stopped. Breathed. "Calm," he repeated. "Like Hollowmere. Like a graveyard is calm."

The Voice had gone very still. Even its slow turning had ceased, and somehow that was worse than anything, that was the attention of something ancient fixing itself on him through leaded glass, and Corrin found he could not look away.

"Yes," he said. "Go on. Look at me. You're the only one who ever has."

* * *

He thought, sometimes, about the conduits. Not the ones he had burned in the purges—those he had catalogued, justified, filed under necessity the way a quartermaster files requisitions. He thought about the ones who had come to him voluntarily, in the first year, before the fear set in. Old men and women who had served the Choir for sixty years, who knelt in his audience hall and said: *we heard it stop, lord, we heard it stop and we know it did not die, tell us what you have done and we will help you carry it.*

He had them executed quietly. He told the court they had been extremists.

"They knew," he said now, and his voice finally cracked, a hairline fracture across fifteen years of marble. "The conduits always knew. That was the whole secret of the covenant, wasn't it? It was never worship. It was *consent*. A voice freely given, freely withdrawn. You sang through them because they chose to hold the door open. And I couldn't tolerate a door that could close. I couldn't tolerate a god with a will. So I made a world with no doors at all, and I called it liberation, and I believed it, I *believed* it, right up until—" 

Right up until tonight. Right up until a defected choir-singer broke under her own memories and gave up the Spire, and instead of triumph Corrin had felt, in the private dark of his map room, a feeling he had not permitted himself since Redford Field:

Dread.

Because if a conduit lived—if the covenant could be reforged—then the Voice could be given back. Freely. Which meant it had never needed to be taken. Which meant the entire architecture of his life, the war, the machine, the seven choirs, the hanged archivist, the forty thousand calm-eyed soldiers, the empire of serenity—all of it rested on a premise a farmboy with a luck-knot was about to disprove on an altar of black stone.

Unless he arrived first.

* * *

Corrin rose. He retrieved his gauntlets and put them on, finger by finger, and the ritual of it settled him the way it always did, though less than it used to, though every night a little less, the mortar shrinking, the cracks widening.

"I'm going to the Spire," he said. "I'm bringing the whole host. If the boy gives you back your tongue, you will sing again, and the first thing you will sing is judgment, and I know how that song ends for me." He looked at the turning light, and something almost tender moved through his face, the tenderness of a man regarding the only witness who matters. "So here is my confession, since you cannot repeat it. I knew. From the first night, I knew what you were asking, and I knew what I was doing, and I did it anyway, because I would rather rule a silent heaven than kneel in a singing one. There. It's said. In front of the only witness who matters."

He turned to the stair.

Behind him, for the first time in fifteen years, the Voice made a new sound. Not a word. Not yet. Something smaller and more terrible: a single clear note, high and sweet and grieving, the note a mother sings over a cradle, the note that had opened every covenant rite since the beginning of the world.

It sounded, unmistakably, like forgiveness being prepared.

Vael Corrin climbed the stairs two at a time and did not look back, and gave the order to march before midnight, and told no one why his hands, bare of gauntlets, would not stop shaking.
