## 18. Maren Chooses

The wind at the foot of the Silent Spire did not howl. It *hissed*—a long, sibilant breath through the black stone teeth of the mountain, as though the world itself were whispering a warning it had no courage to finish.

Maren stood at the mouth of the cave they had made into a cell and listened to it, the way she had once listened to her own heartbeat in the novitiate dormitories of the Choir's last sanctuary: patiently, attentively, waiting for it to tell her something she already knew.

Behind her, bound to a chair of iron and salt-wood, Sera Vane was singing.

Not loudly. Not defiantly. That was the worst of it. She sang under her breath, barely voiced, a thread of melody so thin that any ordinary ear would have taken it for delirium—the muttering of a woman three days without sleep, wrists raw from manacles, mind worn down by the playback. But Maren had trained her. Maren had taught her, eleven years ago in a cloister smelling of tallow and lilies, how a choir-singer's voice worked when everything else had been stripped away. It went inward. It became a wall built note by note out of the only material left.

Sera was holding herself together with song. And every note was one Corrin's Voice had not yet eaten, because the stolen thing could not digest what it did not know—and this song, whatever it was, belonged to Sera alone.

"You'll crack your throat doing that," Maren said, without turning around.

The singing stopped. In its place came the sound Maren had been hearing for three days instead: silence with weight to it, silence like a held breath.

"You'd know," Sera said hoarsely. "You taught me the technique."

"I taught you a great many things." Maren turned. The torchlight caught the silver of her inquisitor's collar, the seven rings of rank, each one earned in blood or worse. "Most of them useless, apparently. You still sing when you're afraid."

"You still stand with your weight on your left leg when you're lying."

Maren looked down at her stance. Shifted it. Hated herself for shifting it.

Outside, down the switchback road carved into the Spire's flank, the camp of Vael Corrin's vanguard glowed with cookfires—two hundred men and women who did not dream anymore, who ate and marched and killed with the placid contentment of well-tended cattle. Above them, higher on the mountain where the true column would arrive by morning, the banners were being raised: the sunburst of the Concord, stitched new over the old sigil of the Choir, because Corrin liked his thefts dressed in respectable cloth.

And beneath all of it—beneath the fires and the banners and the hissing wind—there was the other sound.

It had started an hour ago. Faint. Almost nothing. A hum in the stone, in Maren's back teeth, in the hollow behind her sternum where she kept the things she did not examine. She had told herself it was the altitude. The cold. The residue of the conduit's working at the Salt Gate, which had cracked something in the air that had not yet finished settling.

But the hum had a shape. And the shape was getting closer.

"Sera." Maren crouched to eye level with her prisoner, an intimacy she despised herself for allowing. "The playback resumes at dawn. The Hand arrives with the main column. When he asks you again about the Spire's approach—" *when we break you again*, said the part of her that still flinched, "—you will answer honestly. Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

"That I should betray everyone I've spent a year protecting, because it will hurt less than the alternative." Sera's eyes were fever-bright but level. "Yes, Mentor. I understand what you're telling me. I've understood it since the second day."

"Then why do you keep resisting? You know how it ends. Everyone breaks. I broke. You watched me break, if your memories of the cloister are worth anything."

Something moved behind Sera's eyes—not anger. Something older and more tired.

"You didn't break," Sera said quietly. "You were *sung*. There's a difference. I've felt it now, from the inside. The Voice doesn't crush you. It finds the fear and sings it smooth, like water wearing down a stone, until there's nothing left sharp enough to cut yourself on. That's not breaking, Maren. Breaking leaves edges."

Maren rose too quickly. Her knee cracked; the cold had gotten into it the way cold got into everything past forty. "Rest," she said. "Save your voice. You'll want it for lying to the Hand."

She stepped out of the cave into the blue-black cold, and the hum swallowed her whole.

* * *

It was a lullaby.

That was the unbearable truth of it, the truth she had been circling for an hour like a tongue probing a broken tooth: the sound rising out of the Spire's roots was a lullaby, and she knew it, and knowing it was like swallowing ice.

*Sleep, small bird, the watch is sung.*
*The walls are warm, the night is long.*
*No wolf comes down the mountain road.*
*The Choir is keeping hold.*

Her mother had sung it. Not her mother by blood—she had none that mattered—but Mother Cassia, third voice of the western cloister, a broad soft-handed woman who smelled of candlewax and who had pulled six-year-old Maren out of a nightmare about drowning and held her and sung those four lines until the terror went somewhere it couldn't follow.

Mother Cassia, who had died in the Purging of the Cloisters along with two hundred others, whose death Maren had signed the warrant for fourteen years later, whose name she had not spoken aloud since.

The god remembered her. That was the thing she could not get around, the thing that sat in her chest like a stone dropped through thin ice. The god—or the ghost of the god, or the dead conduits, or whatever waited inside that silent tower—remembered a lullaby, and it was singing it here, at the foot of the mountain, in the ears of the woman who had hunted its last faithful servant across half the world.

Not accusingly. That was the worst part. It wasn't a haunting. It wasn't a curse. It was sung the way Mother Cassia had sung it: gently, patiently, with all the time in the world, as if the singer knew something Maren didn't—as if the nightmare were already over and only Maren hadn't woken yet.

"Stop," she whispered.

The lullaby continued. It did not press. It did not demand. It simply stayed, the way a hand stays on the shoulder of someone crying, asking nothing, offering everything.

Maren walked the perimeter because walking was the only prayer she had left. Past the sentries standing their posts with the mild vacant patience of the song-taken—one of them humming, softly, the Concord marching hymn, off-key in a way that made her stomach turn. Past the supply sledges roped under oilcloth. Past the signal-post where a runner waited with a message tube bound for the main column, reporting that the prisoner remained unbroken and the approach was clear.

Clear. She almost laughed. Nothing about this mountain was clear. The Spire stood against the stars like a black finger raised in eternal accusation, and the wards around it—she could feel them even through deadened senses, old and vast and patient—were not Corrin's and never had been. They had let them come. That was the thought that had been growing in her all evening, quiet as mold: *the mountain let us climb it.* Every warding she knew how to read said the same thing. This place was not defended.

It was *waiting*.

At the signal-post she stopped. The runner glanced up, recognized her rank, looked down again. The message tube sat in its cradle, wax seal unbroken, and Maren read the report through the oilskin wrapping as easily as she had read a thousand of them, because she had written the cipher.

*Prisoner resistant. Recommend escalation to Rite of Unmaking upon Hand's arrival.*

Unmaking. Not torture—torture left something, and something was all a choir-singer needed. Unmaking took the music out of a person entirely and left a body that breathed. She had authorized it twice. She could still see both faces.

She looked at the tube for a long moment. Then she sealed the cradle, untouched, and walked on, and told herself the reason she gave—*the Hand should see the prisoner's condition firsthand before deciding*—was the true one.

The lullaby followed her. Patient. Warm. Keeping hold.

* * *

Near midnight, she went back into the cave.

Sera was asleep—truly asleep, finally, her head tipped against the chair's iron post, breath whistling faintly through split lips. In sleep her face lost the defiance and became what it had been at nineteen: a clever, stubborn girl from the river-country farms who sang harmony before she could write her name, whom Maren had picked out of thirty novices because she heard the *underneath* of music, the room below the room.

I made you, Maren thought. And then I sold you. And now I sit here in the dark deciding whether to sell you twice.

She built a small fire. The cave filled with orange light and the smell of resin. She put water on to heat, because Sera's throat was ruined and there would be questions at dawn whether or not the report went through, and a singer needed her voice, and—

She caught herself. *A singer needs her voice.* As if she had already decided. As if some part of her had signed the order days ago and was only now letting the rest of her read it.

"Maren." Sera's voice, thick with sleep. "You're staring at me like I'm a tactical problem."

"You are a tactical problem."

"No." Sera shifted against the bonds, wincing. "I'm a person you're treating as a tactical problem. There's a difference. You used to insist on it. First lesson, first day: *we do not sing people, we sing with them.*"

"I remember my lessons better than you remember your obedience."

"My obedience." Sera laughed—a dry, cracked, terrible little sound. "Mentor. Look at the camp outside. Listen to it. Two hundred people who don't have nightmares anymore, who don't grieve, who don't love anything enough to lose it. That's what obedience bought. That's the beautiful machine. And you've been listening to it so long you've forgotten it isn't music—it's the *absence* of music wearing music's clothes."

Maren poured the hot water over the herbs, watching the steam rise. "You've been talking to the conduit."

"I've been *near* him. That's enough. He sings like someone remembering, not someone performing. You'd hear it too, if you ever got close enough without your collar on." Her eyes flicked to the seven silver rings. "Take it off sometime. I dare you. Hear what's underneath."

"The underneath is silence. I've stood inside the Spire's shadow for three days. There's nothing there."

"There's a lullaby," Sera said.

Maren's hands went still.

Sera watched her, and understanding dawned across her bruised face slowly, like light coming up over water. "You hear it too," she breathed. "Oh. Oh, Maren. Which one is it? Which one did it give *you*?"

"Shut up."

"It's mine, you know. Or—I think it's mine. It's the one my grandmother sang, the harvest-lullaby from the fen country, the one with the boat in it. I heard it the first night, and I thought I was dying, because no living person knows that song. My village burned twelve years ago. Everyone who knew it is ash." Sera's voice shook, and she let it. "It dug that out of nowhere and gave it back to me. Not to break me. To *remind* me I was somebody before all this. What did it give you?"

Maren stood. Her hands were steady. She noted, distantly, that her hands were always steady now, that steadiness was the last discipline left to her, the shell around whatever the lullaby had cracked open.

"Sleep," she said. "Dawn comes early."

At the cave mouth she paused, her back to her prisoner, and said the thing she had carried up the mountain like a stone in her boot:

"*The walls are warm, the night is long.*"

Behind her, Sera went very quiet.

"Mother Cassia," Maren said. "Western cloister. She sang it when the novices had nightmares. Two hundred and six people died there in the Purging. I signed the warrant." The words came out flat, inventory-flat, the way she reported casualties. "Fourteen years I told myself the Choir was a disease. That Corrin cut it out of the world the way you cut out rot, and the screaming was just the sound of surgery. And now its ghost is singing a dead woman's lullaby to the woman who killed her, and it doesn't sound like rot, Sera. It sounds like forgiveness, and I don't know what to do with that, and I am *so tired*."

Silence. Then, very gently: "Come inside the fire, Mentor. Sit down. Tell me about her."

And Maren—who had not wept at the Purging, who had not wept at her own making, who had not wept when the Voice first smoothed away her grief and called it peace—sat down on the cold stone with her back to the cave wall, and did not weep now either, but came closer to it than she had in twenty years.

* * *

She decided at the grey hour, the hour she had always hated most as a novice, when the vigil candles guttered and the body's courage ran thinnest. Decisions made at the grey hour were honest ones. Everything after sunrise was strategy.

The plan assembled itself with the cold clarity of all her best work. The runner's report would go through at first bell—unaltered, because an altered report unravels, but delayed, misdirected, routed through the eastern relay where the weather would eat a day. The Hand would arrive to find his intelligence stale and his prisoner gone. One hour. Perhaps two. It was not much. Against the machinery Corrin had built, it was almost nothing.

But she had seen what the conduit had done at the Salt Gate with minutes. She had felt the covenant move through the air like weather, and she had seen the song-taken soldiers at the gate *stagger*, had seen the vacancy in their faces shiver like a pond struck by a stone. The machine was strong. The machine was not invulnerable. And somewhere above her, in the dark heart of the Spire, a man was preparing to give everything he was to a god she had helped bury, and he deserved more than an hour.

He deserved a road with no army on it.

She freed Sera first. Cut the bonds, rubbed the feeling back into the ruined wrists, wrapped the frostbitten hands in wool without a word. Sera watched her work, and neither of them spoke, because there are moments when speech is a kind of vandalism.

When the last knot fell away, Sera flexed her fingers and said, "You know what he does to traitors. You wrote the protocols."

"I wrote the protocols." Maren buckled her sword-belt. Checked the blade. "He makes them sing. He takes their fear out and marches them home to kill the people they love, smiling." She looked up. "I've been dead for fourteen years, Sera. This morning I'm just making it official."

"Come with me. Up the mountain. The Spire will take you—the lullaby proves it remembers you, and if it remembers you, it hasn't finished with—"

"No." It came out gentler than she expected. "My road ends here. Someone has to hold the switchbacks, and I'm the only one in this camp who can make two hundred song-taken soldiers believe an order." She smiled, and it was an old smile, one from before, one Sera's face answered to like a struck bell. "First lesson, last lesson: we do not sing people. We sing *with* them. Go be sung with. Go wake your god. I'll buy you the hour."

Sera gripped her arm hard enough to bruise. "Maren—"

"Run, Vane. That's an order. The last one I'll ever give you."

* * *

The first bell rang across the camp, and Maren walked to the command post with the seven rings bright on her collar and the lullaby loud in her skull, and she lied.

She lied magnificently. She lied with the full authority of the Hand's own senior inquisitor: the eastern pass was compromised, she had seen movement, the prisoner must be moved to the lower caves for transfer, the vanguard would reposition to the north spur *immediately*. Her voice carried the flat certainty that two hundred emptied minds had been conditioned to obey, and they obeyed. Torches began to move. Columns began to shuffle out of the positions that commanded the Spire's approach, folding toward the north spur like a hand opening.

It bought twenty minutes before the first officer—half-alive enough to think—frowned and asked to verify the order with the advance riders.

By then Sera was a quarter mile up the mountain, a dark figure moving fast through the scree, and Maren drew her sword on the parade ground of her own army, alone, in the grey light, with the Spire rising black behind her like a promise.

They came for her in a wave, and she met them singing.

Not the Concord hymn. Not the stolen Voice's smooth anaesthetic. She sang the western cloister's morning canticle, the one Mother Cassia had led with her big soft hands lifting the notes out of two hundred children, and her voice was not what it had been at nineteen but it was *hers*, unsmoothed, unowned, full of every edge the machine had failed to file away—and the song-taken soldiers faltered. Staggered. Some of them stopped entirely, heads tilting, vacancies flickering like lamps in wind, because somewhere under the smoothing there was still a person, and the person remembered being sung to.

She fought the ones who kept coming. She was good; she had always been good; the sword-work was the one language Corrin's machine had never bothered to translate. She took three of them down the honest way and held the narrow ground above the switchbacks, and when the ring closed and the blades came in from every side at once, she was still singing.

*No wolf comes down the mountain road.*
*The Choir is keeping hold.*

The last thing she saw was not the soldiers. It was the sky over the Spire's summit, where the first pale light of morning touched the black stone—and where, impossibly, faintly, like a lamp being uncovered in a far room, a single star that had been dark for fifteen years began, very slowly, to shine.

Maren smiled. The blades came down.

And an hour later, when Sera reached the Spire's high door with bleeding lungs and burning eyes, she turned once to look back down the mountain—at the camp in chaos, at the column stalled on the wrong road, at the small knot of bodies on the parade ground arranged around a fallen woman whose sword-hand, even in death, had not let go—and she lifted her voice, cracked and ruined as it was, and sent the morning canticle down the wind so that someone would know the hour had been paid for.

Above her, deep in the stone, the ghost-choir took up the tune.

The Spire opened.
