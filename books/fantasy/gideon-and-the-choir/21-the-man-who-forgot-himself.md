## 21. The Man Who Forgot Himself

He woke on the third morning, and the first thing he did was smile at her.

Sera had been sitting in the chair beside his cot in the infirmary of the Salt Gate garrison—had been sitting there, more or less without moving, since they carried him down from the Spire with frost in his hair and no pulse she could find for a full minute that had aged her ten years. She had rehearsed this moment. She had rehearsed it the way you rehearse a battle: what to say first, what to hold back, how to keep your voice level when the ground gives way beneath you.

She had not rehearsed him smiling.

"Hello," he said. His voice was rough from disuse, but it was warm. Curious. He looked at her the way a man looks at a stranger who has been kind to him in a queue, or a fellow traveler sharing shelter from rain—a small, unguarded friendliness with nothing underneath it. No recognition. No guilt. No flinch of memory when his eyes found the scar along her jaw, the one he'd watched her get at Hollowmere and never spoken of because he knew what it cost her to speak of it.

"Hello," Sera said.

"You've been crying," he said, with gentle concern. "For a while, I think. Your eyes are all swollen."

"I have something in them."

"Ah." He nodded, accepting this with the same easy grace, and Sera understood, in that moment, exactly what the covenant had taken and what it had left behind. It had taken Jude Yarrow—the name, the fifteen years of small lies, the desertion, the shame, the whole architecture of a self built out of guilt and survival. What remained was a room swept clean. And in a clean room, everything that entered seemed simple. Kindness seemed simple. Grief seemed like dust someone else had tracked in.

"What's your name?" he asked.

And there it was. The question she had been dreading, arriving not as a blade but as an open hand.

*Jude,* she almost said. *Your name is Jude Yarrow, and you were a battle-priest of the Choir, and you deserted your order the day it fell silent, and you sold hollow charms in Ashfall for fifteen years, and every one of those things is true, and every one of them is yours.*

Instead she said: "They call me Sera."

"That's a good name," he said gravely, as if names were things worth weighing. "It sounds like wind through a doorway."

She laughed—or made a sound that wanted to be a laugh—and pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes until the burning stopped. *Wind through a doorway.* He would have hated that, the old Jude. He would have said something sharp and self-mocking about choir-singers and their poetry. This one just sat there, pleased with himself, waiting to learn what came next.

"They tell me I did something important," he said. "The healers won't say what. They look at me like—" he turned his hands over, studying them, "—like I'm a relic. Like I might crack."

"You did something important," Sera said.

"Did people die?"

"Some. Not as many as would have."

He absorbed this. She watched him do it—watched the information settle into him without any of the old machinery of guilt engaging, because there was nothing for it to engage. A man who could not remember killing could not be haunted by it. A man who could not remember running could not be ashamed.

"Do you know who I am?" he asked. "Beyond my name, I mean. Do you know what I was?"

"Yes," Sera said.

"Will you tell me?"

And here was the thing she had decided three nights ago, alone in the dark of the infirmary with his hand cold under hers: *no.* Not all of it. Not at once. Not dumped into him like a bucket of cold water, every sin and sorrow restored in a single drowning moment. The covenant had demanded his self freely given, and the Choir had accepted, and then—she believed this with her whole exhausted heart—the Choir had *given back*, gently, the way you set down a burden you no longer need to carry. The dead conduits had let him go. She had heard them do it, felt the great warm silence of their blessing pass over the Spire like weather.

Which meant the rest was hers to give. Story by story. Truth by truth. Chosen.

"Not today," she said.

His face fell—not dramatically, just a small dimming, and gods, even that was familiar, even that little crease between the brows was *him*, some residue too deep for the covenant to burn away—and she hurried on before she lost her nerve.

"But I'll tell you some of it. When you're stronger. A piece at a time. There are—" she searched for the words, "—there are things you should get to choose whether to carry. And choosing takes strength."

"All right," he said. And then, with that terrible openness: "Was I bad?"

Sera looked at him for a long time. At the brand on his wrist, faded now to a pale silver shimmer, less a mark than a memory of a mark. At the hollows under his eyes. At the mouth that had sung a god awake.

"No," she said finally. "You were a man. You were frightened, and you were tired, and you made one choice out of fear that you spent fifteen years paying for. And then, at the end, you paid a much larger one without hesitating. That's who you were. That's all anyone is, if you boil them down far enough."

He considered this with the seriousness of a child being told something true about the world.

"Then I'd like to know the good parts first," he said. "If there were good parts."

"There were good parts," Sera said, and her voice cracked on it, and he reached across the space between the cots and took her hand, and held it, and did not know why she was crying, and did not need to.

* * *

She started with Ashfall, because it was the least dangerous story.

"The city where you lived," she told him, on the fifth day, walking him slowly along the garrison wall in the grey afternoon light. His legs worked; his body remembered walking even if his mind remembered nothing. "Ashfall. It's built in the shadow of an old volcano, and the stone there is black, and in the evening the whole city goes copper-colored when the sun hits it. You had a room above a charm-shop in the Tanners' Quarter. It leaked. You complained about it constantly."

"Did I?" He smiled. "I feel like I would complain about that."

"You complained about everything. Quietly. Under your breath. It was one of your defining features."

"And what was I like? Before." He gestured vaguely at his own head, at the blankness behind his eyes. "Before whatever happened."

Sera walked in silence for a few paces, choosing.

"You were careful," she said. "You kept yourself small. You'd learned that being noticed was dangerous, so you made yourself the kind of person nobody looks at twice. But—" she stopped at the wall's edge, looking out over the ruined terraces below the gate, the fields where Corrin's vanguard had broken, "—but you couldn't stop helping people. It was a problem for you. You'd sell someone a luck-knot that was supposed to be empty, a fake, a trinket, and then you'd stay up half the night making sure it actually worked, because you couldn't bear to take a widow's coin for nothing. You pretended to be a fraud so well that you became an honest man by accident."

He laughed—a real laugh, startled out of him—and then went quiet, pressing his fingers to his mouth as if checking that the laugh belonged to him.

"I can almost see it," he said. "The shop. The leaking room. It's like... hearing a song from another room. You can't make out the words but you know the tune."

Something twisted in Sera's chest. *Know the tune.* Of course he did. The Choir was still in him, somewhere beneath the blankness, humming its fragments. Some part of him had survived the giving that was not memory, not name, not narrative—something older and simpler, the way you can forget a language entirely and still weep at a lullaby.

"Tell me another," he said. "Please."

* * *

She rationed herself. That was the discipline of it, the cruelty and the mercy both. One story a day, sometimes two if the day was hard and he needed ballast. She chose them the way an archivist chooses which books to carry out of a burning library—by weight, by what could be spared, by what would break him if it landed wrong.

She gave him the road stories: Hollowmere and the vacant village, softened at the edges, the horror leached out and only the sorrow left. She gave him Meridian, the council chamber, the old general Halvard—who had survived the assassination, barely, and now ruled a fractured city and sent letters asking after Jude's recovery with a formality that fooled neither of them. She gave him the Salt Gate, the battle, the singing. For that one she took him up onto the wall itself, to the place where the stones were still scorched in a long arc, and told him how he had stood there with no training in war-magic and held the line anyway, raw and terrified and magnificent.

"Did I know what I was doing?" he asked.

"No. Not even slightly."

"Good," he said, and grinned, and for a moment he was so exactly, so unbearably *himself* that Sera had to turn away and pretend to study the horizon.

But there were stories she kept locked. She kept the desertion. She kept the order's slaughter at Redford Field, the brothers and sisters of his covenant cut down around him while he ran. She kept the years of shame, the drinking winter, the night in Ashfall he'd told her about—drunk, once, the only time—in which he'd tried to carve the brand off his own wrist and couldn't finish. She kept all of it in the vault behind her ribs, and every day she felt the weight of it, and every day she asked herself the same question: *who am I to decide?*

Because that was the truth underneath the tenderness, wasn't it. She was not saving him from pain. She was curating him. She was building a man out of selected materials, and every story she withheld was a load-bearing wall removed, and someday—she knew this, she dreaded this—he would ask the right question, or meet the wrong person, or find the wrong record, and the whole structure would come down on him at once, unchosen, undiluted.

Better, maybe, to have left him empty. Better a kind stranger than a curated ghost.

But then he would do something—laugh at his own joke, or stand too long watching the sunset with his head tilted like a man listening to music no one else could hear, or press his silver-marked wrist absently, thoughtfully, the way you touch an old scar—and she would think: *no. He deserves to know he was brave. He deserves to know he was loved. Even if he has to take the shame with it. Even if I have to watch.*

* * *

On the twelfth day, he caught her.

She should have known better than to bring him near the archive tent—Halvard's people had salvaged what they could from Meridian's hidden collection, crates of codices and rite-fragments stacked in rows—and she should certainly have known better than to leave him alone in it for ten minutes while she argued supply requisitions with a quartermaster.

When she came back, he was sitting on the floor amid scattered pages, very still, holding a single sheet in both hands. She recognized it from across the tent. A choir roster. Redford Field. Names in a dead tongue, each one struck through in a different hand, except one line near the bottom, where the ink of the strikethrough trailed off into nothing, unfinished.

*Jude Yarrow,* the line read. *Conduit, Third Voice.*

"Who is this?" he asked. His voice was calm. Too calm. He didn't look up. "There's a list of names. Everyone's crossed out except this one. And the writing stops, like whoever was doing it—" he paused, "—like they couldn't finish."

Sera stood in the doorway of the archive tent and felt the entire architecture tremble.

"That's you," she said. Because lying to him had become impossible somewhere around the tenth day, and because she was so tired, and because some part of her had always known this moment would come and had only been praying it would come later.

"Jude Yarrow," he repeated. He said it carefully, tasting it, the way you taste a word in a language you almost remember. "Is that my name?"

"Yes."

"Why does everyone else on this list have a line through their name, and mine doesn't?"

"Because the person making the list believed you were dead," Sera said. "Or needed to believe it. Or—" she exhaled slowly, "—or couldn't bear to cross you out, because crossing you out meant admitting what happened next."

"What happened next?"

And there it was. The door she had kept locked for twelve days, standing open, and him standing in front of it with the paper trembling very slightly in his hands—not from fear, she realized. From effort. From the strain of a mind reaching for something that wasn't there, like a man grasping at smoke.

"Sera." He said her name and it sounded like a plea. "I can feel it. There's something—I can feel the shape of it, like a missing tooth. There's a hole in me shaped like a specific thing and I don't know what it is but I know it *hurts*. Tell me what happened next."

So she sat down on the floor beside him, among the scattered dead, and she told him.

Not all of it. Not yet. But more than she had planned. She told him about the Choir falling silent mid-battle. She told him about the order dying. She told him, and watched his face as she did, that he had run—that he had survived when everyone around him had not, and that he had spent fifteen years believing himself a coward for it.

He listened without moving. When she finished, the silence stretched so long she could hear the wind worrying the tent ropes outside.

"Am I sorry?" he asked finally.

"What?"

"Whoever I was. This Jude. Is he sorry? Did he wish he'd stayed?"

Sera thought of a drunk man in Ashfall with a knife against his own wrist, unable to finish the cut. Thought of fifteen years of charms sold with a hidden blessing inside. Thought of a man on a wall at the Salt Gate singing a god awake with his last breath of self.

"He was sorry every single day," she said. "And then he went back. At the end, when it mattered, he went back and gave everything. So whatever he was in the middle—" her voice caught, steadied, "—in the middle doesn't get to be the whole answer."

He nodded slowly. Then he did something she hadn't expected: he folded the roster along its old creases, carefully, and tucked it into the breast of his shirt, over his heart.

"I'm going to keep this," he said. "Even though I don't understand it yet. I think—" he frowned, working it through, "—I think a person should hold onto the parts of themselves they haven't earned back yet. As a promise."

Sera looked at this man—this stranger with Jude's hands and Jude's laugh and none of Jude's wounds—and felt something shift in her chest that was not quite grief and not quite hope, but lived in the territory between, where all the real things lived.

"One story a day," she said. "That's the bargain. You get the good ones and the bad ones, in whatever order I decide, and you don't go digging through crates behind my back."

"Agreed," he said solemnly. Then, softer: "Thank you. For telling me. For deciding I was worth the trouble of remembering."

"You were never the trouble," Sera said. "Remembering was."

Outside, the wind came down off the mountains carrying the smell of snow, and somewhere far to the north, in a city rebuilding itself around new laws and old songs, the first bells of evening began to ring. He tilted his head toward the sound—that familiar tilt, listening—and after a moment, so quietly she almost missed it, he hummed back.

And the note he chose, out of all the notes in the world, was the opening phrase of the covenant hymn.

He didn't know it. He couldn't have known it. But the Choir knew him, and it rose in his throat like water finding its old channel, and Sera sat among the scattered dead of Redford Field and listened to a man with no past sing the first bar of a song that had ended the world and begun it again.

"Tomorrow," she said, when the last of it faded, "I'll tell you about a woman named Maren."

He looked at her, waiting, patient, his name folded over his heart and his god humming in his blood.

"Who was she to me?"

Sera smiled, and it hurt, and it was enough.

"That," she said, "is tomorrow's story."
