## 22. Gideon and the Choir

The morning bell in Vespergate rang in three notes, because the city had decided that everything worth doing should begin with a song.

Gideon woke to it the way he woke to everything now: slowly, warmly, without a name for the feeling until he found one. *Contentment*, he thought. That was the word. Sera had given him that word too, months ago, sitting on the edge of his cot in the temple-house with her hands folded like a woman delivering a verdict she'd fought hard to reach. "You feel things first and name them after," she'd said. "It's not a flaw. It's how the Choir worked. It's how you still work."

He lay a moment longer under the wool blanket, listening. The temple-house creaked as it warmed—the old stone of Meridian's rebuilt quarter breathing out the night's cold—and beneath the creaking, beneath even the bell, there was the hum.

It was always there now. Faint as heat off a sun-warmed wall, patient as water finding its level. Most people felt it only when they stood very still and wanted very much to listen. Gideon felt it the way he felt his own pulse: constantly, intimately, without effort. When the Choir hummed, he hummed back. He couldn't help it. His chest would fill with a warmth that had no name he knew, and his throat would answer before his mind caught up, a low tone that matched the one inside him the way a key matches its lock.

He did not know why. He knew almost nothing about why.

What he knew was this: his name was Gideon. He had been called that since the night Sera found him kneeling in the ash of the Silent Spire, blinking at her like a man surfacing from deep water, asking who she was, who he was, where this was. What he knew was that his hands were scarred in ways that suggested fire and rope and long use, that his voice could hold a room of forty children in perfect silence, that certain songs made him weep without knowing why, and that a woman named Sera loved him—not the way lovers love, or not only that; the way a cartographer loves a country she is still mapping, patiently, line by line, story by story.

Everything else, she carried for him.

* * *

The schoolroom had once been a granary, then a barracks, then a ruin, and now it was a school, which the children of Vespergate considered the natural and final form of all buildings. Gideon arrived as the sun cleared the eastern wall and found them already waiting in their crooked rows—twelve of them this season, ranging from a girl of five who mostly ate chalk to a boy of eleven named Tam who asked questions like thrown stones.

"Master Gideon," Tam said, before Gideon had even set down his satchel, "Renna says the stars went out once. My da says that's a lie old people tell."

"The stars went out," Gideon said. He said it simply, the way you'd say *the river flooded* or *the bread burned*. "Every one of them. For most of a night."

"Then what?"

"Then they came back." He smiled at them, twelve small faces tilted toward him like flowers toward light. "Sit. We'll sing about it."

They sang about it every day, in fact. The songs were the heart of what he taught—not magic, not yet, though magic was law again in the free cities and any child with the gift could petition the archive-schools at fourteen—but the songs themselves, the old ones, the ones that had survived fifteen years underground in the memories of people who were punished for remembering. Gideon taught them the way he'd been told water teaches: by flowing downhill, endlessly, into every crack.

He began, as always, with the waking-song. Four notes rising, then a fifth held long, then silence—a deliberate silence, a listening silence, the kind of silence that was itself part of the music. The children's voices wavered and tangled as children's voices do, and Gideon lifted his own voice over theirs, steady, warm, and felt the hum rise in his chest to meet them.

And the Choir answered.

It always answered. Not words—it rarely used words anymore, and never with him, which Sera said was either mercy or mystery—but a swelling, a brightening, like a hearth breathed upon. The air in the schoolroom grew thick and golden. Renna stopped chewing her chalk. Tam's next question died unasked in his open mouth. For four heartbeats, everyone in the room was inside the sound instead of around it.

Then it faded, as it always did, gently, and the children blinked at each other, and someone giggled, and the ordinary world resumed.

"What was that?" whispered the smallest boy, Wren, whose voice was still more breath than sound.

"That," Gideon said, and here he paused, because he always paused here, because the true answer lived in a place he could not go, "was being heard."

* * *

Sera came at midday, as she did most days, carrying bread and news in equal measure. She had taken a room in the temple-house two floors below his, and the whole street knew her by now: the woman with the burn-scarred throat and the soldier's walk who bought too many oranges and argued cheerfully with fishmongers. She had refused every title the council offered her—envoy, hero, Voice-of-the-Reconciliation—and settled instead on *teacher*, which she claimed was the only job she'd ever been any good at, though Gideon suspected from the way people straightened when she passed that her history was considerably larger than teaching.

Today she sat on the schoolroom step in the sun, broke the loaf, and watched him with that look she got sometimes—the one that was half tenderness and half grief, worn so lightly now that he could almost pretend it wasn't there.

"You had them do the lament today," she said. "I heard it from the fountain square. Three streets away."

"Was I wrong? It's in the cycle. Day nine."

"You weren't wrong." She tore her bread, didn't eat it. "It's just—" She stopped. Started again. "That one was yours. Before. You wrote it, or your order did, or—I don't actually know if you wrote it or just loved it. You never told me that part. There are parts I don't have."

"I know." He said it without bitterness. He had learned, over these months, to say it without bitterness, and the learning had cost them both something, and the having-said-it had given them both something back. "Tell me another piece anyway. If you want."

She looked out at the street, at the market noise, at the ordinary shining day. Then she reached into her coat and brought out the little book—the one she'd carried since the Spire, the one she'd filled page by page with everything she could remember of a man named Jude Yarrow. Battle-priest. Charm-seller. Last conduit. The man who had walked into the heart of the Silent Spire and given away his name, his memories, his self, freely and forever, so that a silenced god could sing again.

She read to him sometimes from it. Never all at once. She had promised herself that, early on, sitting in the Spire's shadow with his blank and peaceful face turned up toward hers: *I will not hand him a stranger's life like a debt collector. If he gets himself back, it will be as a gift, piece by piece, and he will get to refuse each piece.*

So far he had refused nothing. But she always offered the refusal first.

"There's a piece about Ashfall," she said. "About a luck-knot you sold a child that shouldn't have worked. And did."

Gideon laughed—the easy, surprised laugh that came out of him whenever one of these stories landed, as if humor were a country he kept rediscovering. "A charm-seller. Me. I can't even cheat convincingly at cards; Tam beats me weekly."

"You cheated beautifully," Sera said. "You lied to yourself better than you lied to anyone else. Fifteen years of telling yourself the god was dead while you carried its mark warm under your shirt." She turned the page but didn't read from it; she knew this part by heart. "You were ashamed of the warmth. That's the thing I want you to know today, if you want to know it. You thought surviving on scraps of faith was shameful. It wasn't. It was the bravest thing anyone in this whole story did—keeping one coal alive in the wet and the dark for fifteen years, alone, hunted, hating yourself for it."

Gideon sat with that. The hum stirred in his chest, low and slow, the way it did when something true moved through the air nearby. He had learned to trust that stirring. It was, Sera said, the closest thing he had to memory—not pictures, not names, but resonance. The Choir remembered him even if he didn't. The god he had emptied himself to refill kept a place for him the way a song keeps a place for its final note.

"I don't feel ashamed," he said at last. "When you tell me those things. I keep waiting to. Everyone else would, hearing what they gave up. But I just feel—" He searched. Named it after, the way he did. "*Chosen.* Like the story picked me. Like I'd do it again."

Sera's eyes went bright. She looked away, mastered it, looked back. "That," she said quietly, "is the piece I couldn't give you. Turns out you had it all along."

* * *

Afternoon brought the archive-school's senior students, the ones with the gift, and with them Elder Marenne—the archivist who had taken, with the council's uneasy blessing, the name of an inquisitor redeemed at the last possible hour, so that the word *Maren* would come to mean something other than terror. She was young for the role, sharp-eyed, and had a habit of arriving with questions wrapped inside courtesies.

"Master Gideon. The council asks again about the resonance tests."

"They can ask," Gideon said amiably, chalking the scale on the slateboard. "The answer's the same. I'm not a conduit. Whatever I was, the covenant took it. What's left is—" he gestured vaguely at his own chest, at the air, at everything— "leftover. Echo. A tuning fork somebody forgot to put away."

"Elder Ovid thinks you're being modest. He says the wards on the northern gate sing in tune with you when you walk past. He says the healing-choirs report their workings run smoother on days you visit."

"Healing-choirs run smoother on days anyone visits. Loneliness is bad for magic. Write that down; it'll be in the histories eventually."

But Marenne didn't smile. She stood in the doorway with her arms crossed, and after a moment she said, in a different voice, the careful voice people used with him, "There's something else. The Spire. The pilgrims who climbed it last week—they're saying the silence at the top has changed. That it isn't silent anymore. That if you stand in the heart-chamber and listen, you can hear—" she hesitated— "a name. Being sung. Very softly. Over and over. They can't make out what it is."

The chalk stopped in Gideon's hand.

The hum rose in him—not the everyday warmth but something vaster, older, a tide rather than a ripple—and for a moment the schoolroom seemed to widen, the walls going distant and gold, and he heard it too: far off, at the top of a broken-white tower a hundred leagues north, a single voice singing a single name into the stone, tender as a lullaby, patient as water.

He could not make out the name. He knew, with a certainty that lived deeper than memory, that it was his.

"Gideon?" Marenne said. "Are you all right?"

He let the tide recede. It went willingly, gently, the way a parent sets down a sleeping child. "I'm fine," he said, and found that it was true, and marveled, as he marveled daily, that it was true. "Tell the council whatever you like. Tell them the Spire is singing to itself. Gods do that, apparently. Tell them—" he smiled— "tell them it sounds happy."

* * *

Evening, and the temple-house kitchen smelled of onions and woodsmoke, and Sera cooked badly and cheerfully while Gideon chopped, because chopping was one of the few domestic arts his hands remembered without instruction. Through the window the first stars were coming out—all of them, every night, in their ancient stations, and every night Gideon greeted them like neighbors, because Sera had told him once that they had gone out, all of them, and that he had been there when they came back, and some part of him that had no past understood that this was why he could never see a star without gratitude.

"Tam asked me today," Gideon said, "if I was in the war. The real one, the old one. I told him I didn't know. He said, *how can you not know?* And I said, *because I gave it away,* and he looked at me like I'd grown a second head, and then he said—" Gideon laughed softly— "he said, *was it worth it?*"

Sera turned from the stove. Flour on her hands, firelight on her face. "What did you tell him?"

"The truth. I said I didn't remember the price, so I couldn't weigh it. But I said—" he set down the knife— "I said, *everything I love, I love because of what was given. So ask the stars if it was worth it. Ask the children. Ask the lady who tells me stories.*"

Sera came around the table and took his floury hands in her floured ones, and for a while neither of them said anything, because the Choir was humming through the walls of the house and the street and the whole warm evening, and when the Choir hummed, Gideon hummed back, and tonight—for the first time he could recall—Sera hummed too. Her voice was rough and low and scarred, and it wavered, and it did not matter. Two notes against the god's great chord. Enough. More than enough.

Later, when the dishes were done and the lamps were out, Gideon lay in his narrow bed and listened to the dark.

He had a name. It wasn't the one he'd been born with—he knew that much, knew it the way you know a coat isn't yours but fits anyway—but it had been given freely, by a woman who stayed, and so it was not a hollow thing. He had work he loved and children who needed him and scars he hadn't earned and stories he was earning slowly, one evening at a time, gathered like shells along a shore.

He did not miss what he couldn't remember. That was the strange mercy at the center of everything: you cannot grieve a door you don't know exists. What he had instead was better than restoration. It was new-made. A belonging built from scratch, on ground he had paid for without ever seeing the bill—paid by a man named Jude Yarrow, whom Sera described as stubborn and ashamed and brave, and whom Gideon honored nightly the only way he could:

By keeping the coal alive. By singing the waking-song to twelve children. By humming back.

Far to the north, the Spire sang his name into the stone, and the stars burned in their stations, and somewhere between the two, in a bed in a free city where magic was law and mercy was policy, a man called Gideon slept without dreams, because he was already home.

And if, some mornings, he woke with tears on his face and a tune in his throat that no one had taught him—if some dawns the hum in his chest shaped itself, briefly, unmistakably, into two syllables that were almost a name, *Ju-de, Ju-de*, sung soft as a lullaby from a god who keeps what is given to it, always, and gives it back only in its own time—

Well. He hummed along. He always hummed along.

Some songs, he suspected, were still being written.
