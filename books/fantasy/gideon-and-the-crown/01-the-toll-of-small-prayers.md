## 1. The Toll of Small Prayers

The mule was named Duchess, which Tamsin Quintana considered the single greatest lie in the Thornfell passes, and he had once watched a man sell a bridge that didn't exist.

"She's a duchess because she has opinions about everyone," said Fen, who drove her, "and because she's never worked an honest day."

"She works," Tamsin said. "She just makes sure everyone knows how much it costs her. That's not dishonesty, Fen. That's aristocracy."

Fen snorted and hauled on the lead rope. Duchess turned her great gray head and gave Tamsin a look of profound, personal grievance, as though he had said something unforgivable in front of the servants. Behind her came six more mules, each carrying a quarter of its weight in sealed crates, and behind them came the crew: Fen; old Marta, who claimed to have driven every pass from here to the sea and lied about at least half of them; the twins, Pip and Poppy, who were neither twins nor named Pip and Poppy but had answered to both for three years now and saw no reason to complicate things; and young Col, who was nineteen, earnest, and the only one among them who still crossed himself when they passed a shrine.

Tamsin didn't cross himself anymore. There was nothing wrong with the gesture — it was just that the god he'd sworn to had been dead for fifteen years, and crossing yourself before a dead god felt less like devotion and more like talking to a portrait.

"Quintana." Marta fell into step beside him, squinting up at the ridgeline where the morning fog was peeling off the granite like wet paper. "Weather says snow by dark."

"Weather's been saying snow by dark for four days."

"And weather's been right twice." She spat with the precision of long practice, missing his boot by a hand's width, which was her way of affection. "We should make the Hollow Steps by noon if Duchess doesn't stage another protest."

"She'll protest. She always protests. It's tradition." Tamsin adjusted the strap of his pack and let his eyes do what they always did on this stretch — read the mountain. The scree slope to the east, stable. The treeline, quiet. The old watch-cairn above the switchbacks, unoccupied, its stones undisturbed since the last time he'd checked, which was yesterday, because paranoia was a habit like any other and he'd stopped apologizing for his habits somewhere around the same time he'd stopped being anyone worth introducing.

The cargo was ordinary, or looked it. Salted fish, iron nails, lamp oil, wool. The crates at the center of the line — the ones wrapped in oilcloth and marked FISH, SALT, DO NOT TILT, a touch of labeling Tamsin was rather proud of — held something else entirely. A reliquary box no bigger than a hymnal. A cracked chalice. Three finger-bones of a saint whose name had been scraped off every record Tamsin could find, which either meant the saint had been very holy or very inconvenient. He'd stopped asking his clients questions years ago. Questions got you answers, and answers got you hanged in Thornfell, where the crown collected relics the way other kingdoms collected taxes.

Not that anyone much wanted relics these days. That was the strange part, the part that kept him fed. Two centuries ago the shrines had gone silent — all of them, all at once, in the reign of some king or other — and ever since, prayer in Thornfell had been a letter posted to an empty house. People still prayed. People always would. But nobody expected an answer, the way nobody expected the dead to write back, and so the old holy things had become curiosities, then contraband, then cargo. The gods' bones went into crates and over passes, bought by collectors and hidden by priests and stolen by the crown, and men like Tamsin Quintana made their living carrying the leftovers of heaven through weather that would kill you for standing still.

He'd been something else once. He didn't talk about that, and his crew knew better than to ask, mostly because the one time Col had asked, Marta had hit him with a bread roll and said, "We don't ask the captain about before. Before is before," and that had settled the matter for three years running.

"Captain." Poppy dropped back from the head of the line, walking backward in front of him with the easy insolence of someone who hadn't yet fallen off a cliff and therefore believed she couldn't. "Col's doing it again."

"Doing what again?"

"The thing. At the shrine." She jerked her thumb toward the bend ahead, where a waystone chapel crouched against the mountainside — one of hundreds scattered through the passes, little stone teeth grown into the rock, built back when the gods were awake enough to appreciate the architecture. This one was dedicated to Vessa-of-the-Fords, or had been, and its niche was empty except for bird droppings and two centuries of silence. "He's praying. Out loud. You can hear him from here."

"What's he asking for?"

"That's the thing." Poppy's grin faded a notch, which for Poppy was roughly equivalent to another person weeping. "He's asking for his sister. She's got the lung-rot. Back in Lowmark. He prays every shrine we pass. Every single one. I told him it's a waste of breath, and he said—" she pitched her voice into a fair imitation of Col's earnestness— "'It's not a waste if even one of them is listening.'"

Tamsin said nothing for a few steps. The path bent. The chapel came into view, small and gray and patient, and Col stood before it with his cap in his hands, murmuring, and Duchess had stopped — which was unusual, because Duchess did not stop for chapels, or kings, or anything short of a superior patch of grass.

"Huh," said Tamsin.

"What?"

"Nothing. Keep moving."

But he watched the boy pray, and something in his chest did the thing it did sometimes, the old ache, the scar-tissue itch of a covenant that had once meant everything. He remembered praying like that. He remembered meaning it. He remembered the last morning of the order, and the silence afterward, and the particular quality of a silence that used to be full.

*It's not a waste if even one of them is listening.*

"No," Tamsin said quietly, to no one. "It isn't."

* * *

They made the Hollow Steps by early afternoon, which was late, which was Duchess's fault, which everyone agreed upon loudly within earshot of Duchess, who appeared gratified.

The Steps were Tamsin's own discovery, more or less — a stair of natural stone shelves cut into the mountain's flank by water and patience, too narrow for carts, too steep for soldiers, invisible unless you already knew where the world folded. Smugglers' roads existed all through Thornfell, but the Steps were his, and he guarded them the way a miser guards a coin: jealously, silently, and with a knife nearby.

The crew climbed in good spirits. Marta sang half a ballad and was booed offstage by unanimous vote. Pip — or possibly Poppy — nearly lost a crate of lamp oil and blamed the mountain, the mule, and the government, in that order. Col laughed at the right moments and glanced back at the low country whenever the path allowed, toward Lowmark, toward the sister with the lung-rot, and Tamsin pretended not to notice, because noticing out loud would have required a conversation, and there were conversations he'd spent fifteen years learning to avoid.

The snow Marta had promised arrived at dusk, fat and unhurried, dusting the ledges white. They made camp in a hollow beneath an overhang, built a fire small enough to hide, ate stew that Marta insisted contained mutton and that everyone else chose not to investigate. The talk wound down the way it always did — jokes getting slower, pauses getting longer — until it was just the fire crackling and the wind worrying the rocks above and Duchess making a sound like a sawmill digesting.

Tamsin took first watch, because he always took first watch, because sleep came hard to him and easier after midnight anyway.

That was when he heard the bell.

There was no bell. He knew there was no bell. The nearest chapel was a day behind them and the nearest bell in it had been sold for scrap before Col was born. But the sound arrived anyway, thin and silver, threading down through the snowfall from somewhere above the camp — above the treeline, above anything human — and it rang once, and then again, patient as dripping water.

Tamsin was on his feet with his hand on his sword before the second ring finished. Fifteen years of instinct don't ask permission.

Nothing moved in the snow. The fire burned. Duchess slept standing up, which she claimed was work.

"Captain?" Col, propped on one elbow, blinking.

"Go back to sleep."

"What was—"

"A bird. Go to sleep."

Col went back to sleep, because he was nineteen and trusted people, which was either his best quality or the one that would eventually get him killed. Tamsin stood his watch and did not sit down again, and the bell did not ring again, and he told himself several reasonable things about wind and ice and echo, and believed approximately none of them.

Because here was the thing Tamsin Quintana knew about bells, the thing they taught you in the first year of an order that no longer existed: the gods of Thornfell had never spoken in thunder. They spoke in small things. A bell with no rope. A candle that leaned against the draft. A name remembered at the exact moment you needed yours. The old texts called it *the toll of small prayers* — the idea that divinity wasn't a shout but an accumulation, prayer upon prayer upon prayer, weightless alone, mountainous together.

And the toll had stopped two hundred years ago. Everyone knew that. It was history, it was theology, it was the entire architecture of the modern realm: the gods had withdrawn, the crown endured, and the world ran on salt and iron and honest human stubbornness.

So there was no bell. And yet.

Tamsin watched the snow fall until dawn, and the mountain held its breath around him, and he could not have said why he was sure it was holding its breath. Only that it was. The way a room goes still when someone says your name in the next one.

* * *

They came down out of the high country on the third day, into a valley where the pines grew thick and the air smelled of resin and coming thaw, and that was where they found the boy.

Not Col — a local child, eight or nine perhaps, from the shepherd steadings that clung to the valley walls. He lay at the foot of a shrine Tamsin had passed a hundred times without a second glance: a roadside chapel to Oren-the-Warden, moss-eaten, roof fallen in, its niche empty for longer than anyone living could remember. The boy had been gathering firewood, by the look of the scattered bundle, and had slipped on the shrine's wet steps, and lay with his leg bent wrong below the knee and blood matting his hair where he'd struck the stone lip, and he was alone, because shepherd children worked alone in the winter margins, and he was dying, or close enough to it — cold taking him faster than the bleed, lips gone gray, breath coming in shallow pulls.

Marta found him first and shouted, and the camp broke apart into motion, and Tamsin reached him second, kneeling in the slush, hands already moving — pressure, position, the field-medicine of a man who'd learned triage in a war nobody talked about.

"Fever's not the problem, shock is. Col — blankets, my pack, the oilcloth. Marta, splints, and heat stones from the fire. Now." His voice did what it always did in emergencies, went flat and clear, a blade laid on a table. "He's breathing. We've got him."

"He's a mile from anywhere," Marta said, already moving. "His folk won't miss him till dark."

"Then we're his folk till dark. Move."

The boy's eyes fluttered. Found Tamsin's face. Something flickered there — fear, then something past fear, the desperate arithmetic of a child who knows how far help is and how far away he is from it.

"Please," the boy whispered. It cost him almost nothing to say it, because children haven't learned yet that prayer is embarrassing. "Lady Oren. Please. Please, Warden, please—"

"Shh. Don't waste breath on her, lad." Tamsin kept his hands steady on the ruined leg. "Save it for walking."

"But—" The boy's eyes went past Tamsin's shoulder, to the empty niche, and widened. "She's *listening.*"

"She's stone, love. She's been stone for two hundred—"

The chapel warmed.

There was no other word for it. Not heat — warmth, a specific, localized, deliberate warmth, like sunlight finding you through a window, pouring down over the broken steps and the broken boy and the two of them kneeling in the slush. Tamsin felt it on the back of his neck and every hair on his body stood up at once, and his hands — his steady, reliable, fifteen-years-steady hands — went still.

In the empty niche, where there had been nothing but moss and droppings, there was light. Soft. Gold-white. Shaped like nothing and somehow like a woman standing in a doorway, waiting, patient as the mountain itself.

The boy sighed — a long, loosening sigh, the sigh of someone set down gently — and his color came back like ink spreading through water, and the terrible shallowness of his breathing deepened, and the bleeding slowed, and slowed, and stopped.

Nobody spoke. Pip and Poppy stood frozen with the blankets. Col had his cap off and pressed to his chest without appearing to know he'd done it. Duchess, at the edge of the clearing, had turned to face the shrine and stood utterly still, ears forward, the way animals face a thing they recognize.

Tamsin knelt in the snow with a dying boy under his hands and stared at two hundred years of silence breaking like ice on a spring river, and felt the ground tilt beneath everything he knew.

Because he knew shrines. He knew dead gods the way a widower knows a house — every room, every shadow, every place the silence lived. And this was not a trick of light, not a smuggler's flare, not exhaustion or wishful thinking or any of the ten explanations queuing up in his mind wearing sensible coats. This was *presence*. This was the thing he had sworn himself to at fourteen and buried at twenty-five, warm on the back of his neck like a hand.

"*No,*" he said softly. It came out before he could stop it. "No, no, you're dead. All of you. I carried you. I *buried* you—"

The light did not answer. It simply stayed, holding the boy the way you hold something you intend to keep, and after a moment it dimmed — not vanished, Tamsin noticed, *dimmed*, banked like a fire left deliberately burning — and the warmth eased, and the ordinary gray afternoon closed back over the clearing like water over a dropped stone.

The boy blinked up at Tamsin, alive, confused, and asked if he was in trouble.

"You're in trouble," Tamsin managed. "Your mother's going to be in trouble. The rest of us are fine."

But his voice shook, and Marta heard it, and gave him a look he'd remember later.

They carried the boy down to the steadings that afternoon, splinted and bundled and complaining, which was the healthiest sound any of them had made in days. His family wept and fed them and would not hear of payment, and the story spread ahead of them down the valley the way stories do, growing a tail of candles and choirs by the time it reached the next village, and Tamsin let it go, because correcting it would have required explaining it, and he could not explain it, not to them, not to himself.

They walked on south, toward Lowmark, toward the buyer and the crates marked FISH, SALT, DO NOT TILT. The crew talked of nothing else for a day and then, being practical people, went back to mules and weather and Marta's singing. Col prayed at the next shrine too, and the next, louder now, hopeful, and Tamsin didn't tell him to stop.

On the evening of the sixth day, cresting the last ridge above Lowmark, with the town's smoke rising soft and gray in the valley below, Tamsin stopped and looked back the way they'd come.

The Thornfells stood against the sunset, blue-black and enormous, crowned in snow, exactly as they had stood every day of his life. Silent. Permanent. Indifferent.

Except that Tamsin Quintana had spent fifteen years learning the language of that silence, and he knew — the way you know a room has been entered, the way you know a letter has been opened and refolded — that something in those mountains was awake now. Had been awake, maybe, for a while. Listening to every prayer the realm had posted to its empty houses. Collecting them. Tolling them, small upon small upon small, into something with weight.

The world he trusted had just blinked. And whatever opened its eyes on the other side of that blink, Tamsin had a bad feeling it was about to start answering.

"Captain!" Fen called from below. "You coming? Duchess is threatening mutiny!"

"Coming," Tamsin said, and turned toward Lowmark, and the lights of the town, and whatever was waiting for him there.
