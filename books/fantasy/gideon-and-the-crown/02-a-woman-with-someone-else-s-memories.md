## 2. A Woman with Someone Else's Memories

Lowmark smelled the way border towns always smelled: woodsmoke, wet wool, and the particular desperation of people selling things to other people who had no money. Tamsin Quintana had crossed into it a hundred times over fifteen years, and every time he thought the same thing — *if I ever get rich, I will never again smell this town.*

He was not rich. He was standing in the market square trying to convince a cheese merchant that a reliquary box was a spice chest.

"Cardamom," Tamsin said, tapping the lid. "Very rare. Grown on cliffs. The cliffs are the expensive part."

The merchant squinted at the box, which was silver, engraved with saints, and about as subtle as a church bell. "What kind of merchant ships cardamom in a box with a saint on it?"

"A devout one."

"A devout liar."

"Those are the best kind," Tamsin said. "They lie for God."

Behind him, Doss — his mule-driver, a man shaped like a rain barrel and about as easy to move — snorted loudly enough to scatter pigeons. "He's got you there, friend. Last week he told a toll-keeper our cargo was funeral urns, and then he wept. Actual tears. I nearly believed him myself."

"The tears were real," Tamsin said. "I was thinking about my margins."

The merchant laughed despite himself, which was how most of Tamsin's negotiations ended, and haggled down to a price that let everyone walk away feeling they'd robbed the other man equally. The reliquary went into the merchant's back room; the coin went into Tamsin's coat; and Tamsin allowed himself, briefly, the luxury of believing this shipment would be as uneventful as the last forty.

This is worth noting, because Tamsin Quintana's entire philosophy of life rested on the belief that nothing interesting would ever happen to him again, and that this was a *good* thing. Interesting things, in his experience, came with funerals attached.

* * *

It happened at the shrine of Saint Vess, which was not really a shrine anymore so much as a roofless square of stones where the townsfolk left turnips they didn't want and blamed the birds. Tamsin stopped there out of habit — an old swordspeaker's habit, touching base with the quiet places, the way a man might knock on the door of a house where nobody had lived for years, just to hear the emptiness echo.

He was adjusting his collar against the drizzle when a woman said, "Hello, Corren."

Tamsin did not turn around immediately, because the name meant nothing to him, and because in his experience the people who said strange names behind your back were either mad, drunk, or about to sell you something. He finished with his collar first. Priorities.

Then he turned, and forgot all three theories at once.

She was perhaps thirty, dressed in the grey-and-blue of a working priestess — the kind who mended roofs and delivered babies rather than the kind who stood on altars — with rain-dark hair coming loose from its braid and eyes the color of the sky just before snow. She was looking at him the way you look at a door you've been knocking on for years when it finally opens.

"I'm sorry," Tamsin said. "You have me confused with someone else."

"No," she said, with terrible gentleness. "I don't."

"My name is Tam Wicker. I'm a spice merchant." He gestured vaguely at himself, at the nonexistent spices. "Cardamom. Cliff-grown."

"Your name is Tamsin Quintana," she said. "Last sworn of the Order of the Speaking Sword. And before that—" she hesitated, and something flickered across her face like a shadow of pain, "—before that, you were Corren Vale. You had a scar through your left eyebrow from a falcon, and you whistled when you were nervous, and you once walked eleven miles in the wrong boots because you refused to admit you'd taken them from the wrong peg."

The market noise seemed to recede, the way sound does underwater. Tamsin became aware that his heart was doing something complicated.

"That's a very specific story," he managed.

"It's not a story. It's a memory." She pressed her hand flat against her sternum, as if steadying something inside her. "My name is Ilse Vane. I'm the priestess here. And twenty-six years before I was born, I knew you."

* * *

They sat in the shrine of Saint Vess, on stones that had never answered anyone, while the drizzle thickened and Doss hovered at a tactful distance pretending to inspect a turnip.

"You can see why I'm skeptical," Tamsin said.

"I can see why you're frightened."

"I'm not frightened. I'm a businessman assessing a very unusual sales pitch."

"What are you selling?" Ilse asked, and there was humor in it, warm and unafraid, and Tamsin hated how much he noticed that.

"Nothing. That's the problem. You've caught me between shipments." He rubbed his jaw. "All right. Let's say — purely for argument — that you believe you remember a previous life. People claim stranger things. There's a woman in Harrowgate who insists she was a goose."

"Were you a goose?"

"Not currently."

"Then we have more common ground than you and the goose-woman." She leaned forward. "Corren—"

"Tamsin."

"Tamsin." She said it carefully, tasting the shape of conceding. "Ask me something. Something only someone who truly knew you would know. Not a fact — facts can be stolen, bought, overheard. Ask me something *true.*"

Tamsin considered several questions and discarded them. Then, because cynicism is often just courage wearing work clothes, he asked the one he actually wanted answered.

"When I was young — when *he* was young — there was a night I don't talk about. A night on the wall at Cairn Hollow. What happened?"

Ilse's face changed. The warmth stayed but something older moved underneath it, grief with dust on it.

"You held the watch alone because Merric had fever and you sent him to bed, which was against orders, and you'd have taken the whipping for it. And near dawn the mist came up the valley wrong — moving against the wind — and you were seventeen and terrified, and you did the thing they train you never to do." Her voice dropped. "You spoke to the dark. You said, 'If anything is listening: I am small, but I am here.' And the mist stopped. It stopped like a dog called to heel, and it waited until sunrise, and it never touched the wall. And you never told anyone, because you thought if you said it aloud it would mean the dark had heard you, and might answer again someday."

The rain fell on the roofless shrine. Somewhere in the market a cart squeaked by, indifferent to the fact that Tamsin Quintana's entire understanding of his own life had just developed a crack down the middle.

He had never told anyone that. Not Roderic. Not his master. No one living knew it, because the only person who'd witnessed it had died at Cairn Hollow two hundred years ago, along with everything else.

"How," Tamsin said quietly.

"That's what I've spent my whole life asking." Ilse pulled her cloak tighter. "I was four when the dreams started. A walled town. A sword I could feel in my hand though my hands were a child's hands. By ten I could draw the layout of Cairn Hollow in the dirt, gates and wells and all, and my mother thought I was possessed and took me to three different priests, and the third one — old Father Ambrose — listened to me for an hour and then went white as salt and told her, 'Your daughter isn't possessed. Your daughter is *remembering.*' He gave me books. Forbidden ones. He taught me what the Order was, and who served in it, and one name he made me promise never to forget." She looked at him. "Yours. Both of yours."

"And you waited," Tamsin said. "Twenty-some years."

"I watched the roads. I learned which merchants ran the passes. And when a spice merchant with a soldier's walk and a liar's smile started crossing into Lowmark twice a season—" she smiled, and it was crooked, and Tamsin felt it land somewhere below his ribs with an unprofessional thud, "—I started leaving turnips at this shrine, because I remembered you always complained there were never any vegetables on the road."

"There *were* never any vegetables on the road," Tamsin said faintly. "That's — that's a real complaint. I make that complaint."

"I know. I *remember* you making it."

Doss, from across the square, called out with the elaborate casualness of a man who has been listening to every word: "Tam! Are we buying the turnip or not?"

"We are not buying the turnip, Doss."

"Because it looks haunted."

"Everything in Lowmark looks haunted. It's the local industry."

But he didn't stand up. That was the thing he noticed about himself later, turning the hour over: every instinct he owned said *stand up, laugh, leave, stay small, stay boring, stay alive,* and instead he sat on a cold stone in the rain and looked at a stranger who wasn't one, and asked:

"You said the gods are waking. What does that mean? Because I'll tell you what I know. I know the shrines have been silent for two centuries. I know prayers go up and nothing comes down. I've carried relics for fifteen years and never once felt anything off any of them except damp. The gods aren't sleeping, priestess. They're dead. I swore my oath to a corpse, and I've made my peace with it, and my peace involves not talking about it."

"Three nights ago," Ilse said, "a boy in the low quarter prayed at the shrine of Saint Aldous. His sister was dying of lungfever. The shrine has been silent since the Silence began — every record agrees, two hundred years, nothing. But that night the candle flame bent toward the boy, and a voice came out of the dark and said, *'Bring her water boiled with white moss, and do not be afraid.'*" She paused. "His sister lived. Half of Lowmark saw the light over the shrine. The temple hierarchy is calling it a trick of refraction. Refraction, that healed a girl and spoke in the Old Tongue."

Tamsin's mouth had gone dry. "That's not possible."

"No," Ilse agreed. "And yet."

"Voices don't come back after two hundred years. Whatever answered that boy, it isn't a god waking up. Gods don't wake. If something is wearing their voices—"

"Then we need to know what, and why now, and why *you,*" she said. "Because here is the part I couldn't say in front of your driver. Three nights ago, when the voice came, it said one more thing. The boy told the whole street, because children repeat everything. It said—" and now her steady composure finally cracked, just at the edges, and Tamsin saw how much it cost her, "—it said, '*Tell the swordspeaker the debt is called in. Tell him the crown is not what it seems. Tell him to look at what they set on the king's head.*'"

The rain kept falling. Tamsin sat very still.

"I haven't spoken a word of swordspeaking in fifteen years," he said at last. "I broke my oath so thoroughly that even the god I broke it to is dead. Why would whatever-that-is want *me?*"

"Because you're the last," Ilse said simply. "Because a covenant needs a hand to hold it. And because—" she reached into her sleeve and drew out a small object wrapped in oilcloth, and set it on the stone between them without unwrapping it, "—because I found this in the temple archives six months ago, and I've been carrying it waiting for you, and I am so tired of waiting."

Tamsin looked at the bundle. It was the size of a ledger book. It weighed nothing visible, and somehow it seemed to weigh enormously, the way certain doors do.

"What is it?"

"A royal ledger," Ilse said. "Stolen pages, copied in a dead clerk's hand. Accounts of the coronation of King Edmun the Second, one hundred and ninety-eight years ago. What was collected that day. What was *bound* that day. And what the crown of Thornfell is actually made of." Her eyes held his, snow-colored and certain. "Read it, Tamsin Quintana. Read it, and then tell me the gods are dead — and tell me which of us is lying to herself."

He should have walked away. Every year of careful, dull, profitable survival he'd built stood up inside him and voted for walking away, unanimously, with motions and minutes.

Instead Tamsin Quintana picked up the oilcloth bundle, and it was warm, which no bundle of paper had any right to be, and somewhere under his breastbone something that had been silent for fifteen years turned over in its sleep.

"All right," he said. "One reading. One. And then I go back to being a spice merchant."

"Of course," said Ilse Vane, who was smiling like a woman who had already read the ending.
