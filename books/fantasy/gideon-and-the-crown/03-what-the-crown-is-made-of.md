## 3. What the Crown Is Made Of

Ilse Vane had stolen exactly three things in her life, and she was beginning to suspect the third one would kill her.

The first had been a honey cake at age seven, from her own mother's kitchen, which hardly counted. The second had been a psalter from the chapel at Lowmark — hers now, technically, since the chapel had burned and no one else had survived to claim it. And the third sat wrapped in oilcloth beneath the floorboards of a rented room above a dyer's shop, radiating guilt the way a hearth radiated heat.

She heard Tamsin Quintana on the stairs before she saw him: the particular heavy-tired tread of a man who had spent all day pretending to be a mule driver and resented every minute of it. She counted his steps out of habit. Eleven stairs, a pause outside her door long enough for him to reconsider, then three sharp knocks.

"You said dawn," he said through the wood.

"It is dawn."

"It's barely *dusk* wearing a disguise." A pause. "The sun isn't even up. I checked. I have a professional interest in the sun."

Ilse opened the door and stepped aside. "Then you'll want to see this while there's still time to be professionally interested in it."

He came in the way he came into everything — sideways, like a man entering a room that might contain either an ambush or a bill. His eyes went first to the window, then to the corners, then to her, and only after all that did they soften by a degree that another person might have missed entirely.

"You look terrible," she said warmly.

"I've been sleeping in a stable loft with a man who snores in liturgy. Brannic snores in *verses*. There's a call-and-response structure to it." He dropped onto the room's single stool, which creaked a protest, and looked around. "Where's the ledger?"

"Patience."

"I'm a smuggler, priestess. Patience is for people whose cargo doesn't kick."

Ilse knelt at the floorboard she'd pried loose two nights ago and lifted out the oilcloth bundle. Even now, even expecting it, her hands wanted to shake. She made them not. That was most of what being a priestess of a dead god amounted to, she'd found: making your hands do what you needed while everything else in you screamed.

She set the ledger on the table between them.

It was a royal ledger — the royal ledger, or rather one of its bastard children, a working copy kept by a crown auditor named Fellwick who had developed, late in life and quite against his nature, a conscience. Ilse had found him dying last spring in a hospice in Marrow's Ford, his lungs full of dye-fumes from thirty years of honest work before the crown hired him, and he had gripped her wrist with surprising strength and said, *They told me it was accounting. They told me it was just accounting.*

Then he'd pressed the book into her hands and wept, and died by morning, and Ilse had been running ever since.

Tamsin didn't touch it. He looked at it the way he'd looked at the silent shrine on the mountain two days ago — like something that had blinked when it shouldn't have.

"What am I looking at?" he asked.

"Crown accounts," Ilse said. "Coronation expenses. Two hundred years of coronation expenses." She untied the cord and opened the cover. "Every realm keeps ledgers like this. Cloth for banners, wine for feasts, fees for the priests who bless the crown. Thornfell's are unusual in one respect." She turned pages until she found the column she wanted, and turned the book to face him. "Look at the disbursements under *Rite of Binding*."

Tamsin leaned in. Ilse watched him read. He had a smuggler's way of reading — fast, suspicious, hunting for the trick in the contract — and she saw the moment the hunting stopped and the reading began.

"*Payment to the Under-Chanter of the Vault,*" he read slowly. "*For services rendered during the Rite of Binding. One hundred crowns.*" He frowned. "Binding what?"

"That's the question I asked. Well — that's the question I screamed at a dying man, but he was past caring about my volume." She reached over and turned back through the pages, coronation by coronation, reign by reign. "Here. King Osric, sixty-one years ago. *Rite of Binding, payment rendered.* Here, Queen Maren, ninety years before that. Same line. Same amount, adjusted for inflation, which I confess I find darkly funny. Someone in the treasury has been dutifully cost-of-living adjusting the theft of a god for two centuries."

Tamsin had gone very still. It was a stillness she was learning to read, and it wasn't the stillness of disbelief. It was the stillness of a man standing very carefully on ice he'd already heard crack.

"Ledgers lie," he said. "I've forged enough of them to know. A line item proves nothing."

"No," Ilse agreed. "A line item proves nothing. So look at the marginalia." She turned to the oldest entry, the coronation of King Aldous the First, two hundred years ago, the founding of the current line. The handwriting here was cramped, hurried, the hand of an auditor writing faster than his courage could follow. She read it aloud so there could be no mistake, so it would be in the air between them where neither of them could pretend it away:

"*Bound at crowning per the Old Compact, the Name of the Keeper of Paths, extracted whole. Yield sufficient for three reigns' drawing. Remainder to be held in trust beneath the Vault until consumed.*"

Silence. Somewhere below them the dyer's shop was waking up; she could smell the vats heating, that sour vegetal stink of madder root that had soaked into the walls of the whole building. Tamsin stared at the page.

"The Keeper of Paths," he said at last. His voice had gone flat and careful, the voice of a man carrying something fragile across a bad bridge. "That's — "

"The god the swordspeakers served. Yes." Ilse closed her eyes briefly. "I know what I'm telling you, Tamsin. I've had two days to think about how to tell you, and I still don't know how, so I'm going to simply say it badly and let you hate me for the manner instead of the matter. Your god isn't dead. Your god was *robbed*. Its name was torn out of the sky at the first Aldric's coronation and hammered into a crown, and every king and queen since has been drinking from it. Slowly. Carefully. Like a household sipping its way through an inheritance."

"That's not possible." But he said it the way men say such things — not as a claim but as a plea, testing whether the world would agree with him if he only asked nicely enough.

"Why do prayers go unanswered?" Ilse asked gently. "You've sworn yourself to a dead god for fifteen years. You must have asked yourself why a god dies."

"Gods die. Everything dies. That's the whole — " He stopped. Rubbed his jaw. "That's what Master Corren taught us. The gods fade, the covenant frays, and the swordspeakers keep faith anyway because faith that needs a reward isn't faith, it's commerce."

"And did you believe it?"

"I *had* to believe it." The words came out rougher than he'd intended; she saw him hear that too. "Because the alternative is that I spent fifteen years hauling relics through frozen passes for a god that was lying somewhere bleeding out while I told myself it was natural. While I told *families* it was natural. Do you know what I say to them? At the shrines? I tell them the gods sleep deeper now. I tell them their prayers aren't refused, just unheard. I have a whole — " his mouth twisted — "a whole gentle vocabulary for it. And you're telling me it was never sleep. It was a *draw account*."

"Yes."

"And the crown has been — " He stood abruptly, walked to the window, stood there with his back to her. Below, the dyer's apprentice was arguing with a cart. Ordinary sounds. Ilse let him stand.

When he spoke again, he didn't turn around. "The boy. The child-king. The coronation in the spring."

"Will consume the last fragment." She said it plainly, because he deserved plainness. "The ledger tracks the yield. Each binding draws down the reserve — the reigning monarch takes what they need to hold the throne, to keep the realm *obedient*, though the ledger uses softer words. Look." She turned to the final pages, the current entries, in fresh ink. "Under Aldric's father. *Yield low. Drawing strained. Recommend completion at next succession.* And here, in the Regent Veyra's own hand — she signs as Lord Treasurer — *the remainder will suffice for one final binding. After which the asset is exhausted.*"

"*Asset,*" Tamsin repeated.

"She's a treasurer. To her it genuinely is one." Ilse found she couldn't keep the bitterness entirely out. "I've thought a great deal about that word. It's easier than *god*, I suppose. Easier than *prisoner*. An asset doesn't haunt you."

Tamsin turned from the window at last. His face had settled into something she suspected few people ever saw: not the wry smuggler, not the tired oath-keeper, but the thing underneath both, the fifteen-year-old who had spoken his vows in a mountain chapel and meant every word.

"How much is left?" he asked. "Of the name."

"Enough for one coronation. The boy's. When they set the crown on Aldric's head and speak the Rite of Binding, whatever remains of the Keeper of Paths goes into the crown entire, and the covenant ends not with a death but with an *accounting entry*. Closed. Balanced. No one will even know to mourn it, because the grief will have been drawn off along with everything else." She spread her hands. "Two hundred years of theft, finished neatly, in front of the whole realm, with trumpets."

"And the boy."

"What about the boy?"

"Does the binding hurt him? Is he — " Tamsin hesitated, and Ilse watched him decide to ask the real question. "Is he complicit? Is a nine-year-old complicit?"

"He's a vessel," Ilse said. "And I suspect he's as much a prisoner as the god. But that's a question for later, and later is a country we may not reach." She came around the table. "Do you understand now why I stopped you on the road? Why I knew your name — your *other* name? The Keeper of Paths is the god of roads and thresholds and crossings. Of the finding of lost things. I lived a life under that god, Tamsin. Whatever I am now, some part of me was shaped in its keeping, and when its name started *fraying* — when the edges started coming loose — those parts of me started remembering. The god is leaking, and I'm downstream."

He studied her for a long moment. Outside, the cart argument resolved itself into grudging cooperation, which struck Ilse as a decent omen.

"You said the other night the gods were waking," Tamsin said. "This says the opposite. This says there's almost nothing left to wake."

"It says both." Ilse touched the ledger's worn cover. "A name can't be destroyed, only bound — that's the oldest teaching and apparently the treasurers' too, or they wouldn't need a rite at all, they'd just melt the crown down. Bound things strain. Bound things *leak*. Every prayer that's landed wrong lately, every shrine that answered when it should have slept — that's the name pushing at its chains because the chains are nearly empty of the strength that holds them. The theft is failing, Tamsin. Failing loudly. And Veyra knows it, which is why the coronation is rushed, why the passes are crawling with soldiers, why a royal warrant went out for a smuggler and a heretic." She smiled, and knew it wasn't a kind smile. "We're not trying to save a dying god. We're trying to reach a prisoner before his jailers finish the job."

Tamsin was quiet again. Then, in a voice stripped of all its irony: "If I carry the name back — if it can even be carried — what happens to the crown? To the throne? To the *realm*, Ilse? Kingdoms run on more than obedience. Or do you imagine they don't?"

"They run on rain and harvests and people being decent to each other when no one's watching. The obedience was always a tax levied on top." She held his gaze. "But yes. It will shake. Thrones crack when their foundations turn out to be stolen goods. I won't pretend otherwise."

"And you need a smuggler because — "

"Because the name has to travel from the vault beneath the palace to the summit shrine where the covenant was first sworn, and every road between here and there belongs to the crown. Except," she said, and waited.

He sighed the sigh of a man watching his last exit close. "Except the ones that exist on no map and belong to no one with the sense to write them down."

"You flatter yourself."

"Somebody has to."

Ilse laughed despite herself, and saw him register the laugh with a small, startled pleasure that he immediately pretended he hadn't. She was learning his tells too. It seemed only fair; he'd been learning hers since Lowmark, whether he admitted it or not.

"There's one more thing," she said, and the humor drained out of the room like water through sand. "Before you decide anything. You deserve the whole weight of it."

"Those are my favorite things," Tamsin muttered. "Go on."

"Fellwick — the auditor — he didn't just give me the ledger. He gave me a name. The Under-Chanter of the Vault, the one paid to perform each binding. The office passes from chanter to chanter, and the rites pass with it, and Fellwick audited the payments for thirty years, and once — once — he was drunk enough and frightened enough to ask the old Under-Chanter what the binding actually *said*." She took a breath. "The rite requires a speaker. A living voice to hold the name steady while it's drawn. And for two hundred years, the crown has used swordspeakers for it. Sworn speakers, whose oaths make their voices true. Corren's order, Tamsin. Your order. They didn't just fade. They were *used*."

The color left his face by degrees, like a tide going out.

"My master," he said. "Corren. He — "

"Died refusing, I think. Fellwick believed so. The ones who refused were purged, quietly, and the ones who consented were told it was a blessing, a final service to the realm." Ilse reached across the table and, after a moment's hesitation, laid her hand over his where it rested beside the ledger. His hand was cold. She didn't withdraw hers. "I'm telling you this in a rented room over a dye shop because there's nowhere kinder, and because you'll need your anger pointed in the right direction, and I'd rather aim it now than have it find you on a mountainside."

Tamsin looked down at her hand on his. He didn't pull away. He also didn't speak for a long moment, and when he finally did, his voice was low and steady in the way she was learning meant danger — not to her, but to whoever had earned it.

"The coronation," he said. "How long?"

"Eleven weeks. Twelve, if the roads stay snowed."

"Then we leave tomorrow." He turned his hand over, briefly, and squeezed her fingers once — a smuggler's seal, a bargain struck — and let go, and stood, and gathered himself back into the wry tired man the world was allowed to see. At the door he paused, one hand on the frame.

"Priestess. If this ledger is a forgery, and you're mad, and I ride into the high country to steal a god's name for nothing — "

"Then you'll have had a lovely walk," Ilse said.

Something moved at the corner of his mouth. "That's what I was afraid of," he said, and was gone down the eleven stairs, taking the steps two at a time, like a man who had suddenly remembered he was young.

Ilse stood alone with the ledger and the smell of madder root and the sound of the city waking. She put her hand flat on the worn cover, over the cramped marginalia, over two hundred years of careful, dutiful horror.

"Hold on," she whispered, to whatever was left down there in the dark beneath the palace, wearing a borrowed voice and waiting. "Hold on. We're coming."

Below, the dyer's apprentice began to sing, badly, cheerfully, a song about spring.

* * *

She spent the day doing the ordinary work of staying alive and unnoticed: buying bread, mending a strap, sitting in the chapel yard — the new chapel, timber and tar, barely a year old — because her feet still went to chapels the way rivers went downhill. Toward evening she wrote a letter she couldn't send, to a sisterhood that no longer existed, and burned it in the dyer's furnace, and felt marginally better, which was the entire function of letters like that.

Tamsin came back after dark, smelling of straw and horse, with news arranged in order of increasing unpleasantness.

"First," he said, dropping onto the stool, "Brannic has agreed to drive us as far as the Hollow Steps and no further, mostly because I lied to him about the destination and he knows I lied and respects the craftsmanship. Second, the garrison at Lowmark doubled its patrols today, which means someone in the capital knows a priestess is loose. Third — " he paused, and she watched him weigh whether to soften it, and watched him decide she'd earned it straight — "there's a warrant posted in the market square. Not for you. For me. *Tamsin Quintana, formerly of the Order of the Sword-Sworn, wanted for questioning regarding matters of state.* Signed by Ser Roderic Hale."

The name landed in the room like a dropped stone. Ilse knew it — knew it from Tamsin's silences, which were more eloquent than most men's speeches. She said nothing. Let him carry it.

"My oath-brother," Tamsin said, to the middle distance. "Fifteen years ago we swore the same vows on the same stone. Now he's riding warrants for the crown that killed our god, and I'm stealing relics in the night, and neither of us has said his name aloud to the other since the order fell." He rubbed his eyes. "He'll have brought soldiers. Roderic never travels light. He thinks armor is a form of correspondence."

"Then we're gone before his letter arrives," Ilse said. "Dawn?"

"Dawn," Tamsin agreed. Then, with the ghost of his usual crooked smile: "The actual dawn, priestess. I checked twice."

Outside, far off toward the capital road, a bell began to ring — not the chapel bell, not any hour she knew. Just a bell, ringing and ringing, and neither of them said what they both thought: that bells had been silent a long time in Thornfell, and silence, lately, was getting harder to keep.
