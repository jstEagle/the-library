## 4. The Oath-Brother's Warrant

The rain came to Lowmark the way bad news always did: slowly at first, then all at once.

Tamsin watched it from the window of the Drowned Bell, where the innkeeper watered the ale and pretended not to know what Tamsin smuggled and Ilse pretended to be nobody in particular. Below, the market square emptied with the practiced speed of a border town that had learned, over two centuries, exactly how fast a royal rider could cover ground.

"Someone's coming," said Brannic, from his chair by the fire, without opening his eyes. Brannic was seventy if he was a day, mostly beard, entirely ears. "Horse. Shod. Military shoeing, by the sound of it. Two more behind."

"You could have let me enjoy my ale," Tamsin said.

"You're drinking tea."

"That's how much I was enjoying it."

Ilse looked up from her corner table, where she'd been copying something in a small, careful hand — prayers, he suspected, though she claimed they were recipes. Her eyes met his, and there it was again: that look she gave him when she knew something about him that he hadn't told her. It happened so often now that he'd stopped counting, which was its own kind of alarming.

"Ser Roderic?" she asked quietly.

"You know the name?"

"I know *a* name. The one you say in your sleep, sometimes, like an apology." She closed her book. "I'll be upstairs."

"Ilse—"

"Tamsin." She smiled, and it didn't reach anywhere near her eyes. "Whatever he offers you, remember that I already know what you'll do. You told me. In another life. Literally."

She went up the stairs, and Tamsin stood at the window and watched three riders come through the rain into the square, and felt fifteen years fall on him like weather.

* * *

He knew the horse before he knew the man. A grey mare named Wren, sixteen hands of bad temper and good heart, who had once carried them both out of a river gorge with a broken stirrup and a dead man's body across her rump. Roderic had wept over that horse when they'd thought her lost at Cael's Ford. Tamsin would have bet his own life that Wren had been put down years ago.

So either Roderic had found a twin, or the crown had given him back his horse along with everything else, and Tamsin wasn't sure which possibility made his chest hurt worse.

Roderic came through the door of the Drowned Bell trailing rain and two soldiers who had the look of men who'd rather be anywhere else. He'd gone grey early — Tamsin had too, but Roderic wore it differently, like armor instead of damage. There was a scar through one eyebrow that hadn't been there at Cael's Ford. His sword was crown-issue, plain hilt, no oath-knots on the scabbard.

Swordspeakers knotted their scabbards. Dead orders didn't.

"Tamsin Quintana," Roderic said. Not a greeting. An inventory.

"Ser Roderic Hale." Tamsin didn't rise. "You've gotten old."

"You've gotten *found*." Roderic waved his soldiers toward the bar with a flick of two fingers — *give us the room* — and the innkeeper, reading the room better than most men read scripture, suddenly discovered urgent business in his cellar. "Sit down, Tam. This goes easier sitting."

"I'm comfortable."

"You're standing like a man with a door behind him. Sit anyway." Roderic pulled out a chair opposite, turned it, sat astride it with his arms folded across the back — the same way he'd sat through every war-council for ten years, the same way he'd sat the night before Cael's Ford telling Tamsin that Master Aldous was wrong, that the order could survive bending, that survival wasn't shame. "There's bread here. Eat something. You look like a fence post that's learned to worry."

Despite himself — despite everything — Tamsin almost laughed. That was the cruelty of it. Fifteen years, a warrant, a dead god between them, and Roderic still talked to him like they were twenty-two and sharing a watch-fire.

"I ate," Tamsin said. "Talk."

Roderic produced the warrant from inside his cloak. He laid it on the table between them, face-up, and smoothed it flat with one hand, the way you'd smooth a sheet over a body.

It was a handsome document. Heavy paper, the crowned stag of Thornfell in wax the size of a plum, and Tamsin's own description written out in a clerk's careful hand: *Tamsin Quintana, formerly of the Order of the Speaking Sword, wanted for questioning in matters touching the security of the realm.* No charge listed. That was the tell. When the crown couldn't name your crime, it meant the crime was knowing something.

"Questioning," Tamsin said.

"The Regent is thorough."

"The Regent is Veyra. Thorough is one word for it." He didn't touch the warrant. "And if I come along quietly to be questioned?"

Roderic held his gaze. To his credit — and Tamsin noted it, filed it away in the place where he kept the things about Roderic that still hurt — the man didn't pretend.

"You'd hang," he said. "Not for anything you've done. For what you might know. The Regent doesn't gamble with might-knows." He tapped the warrant once. "But that's the stick. You know me, Tam. I never liked leading with the stick."

"So lead with the carrot."

"The carrot is this." Roderic leaned forward, and his voice dropped into the register he'd used for confessions and battle-plans, low and even and terribly sincere. "Give me the priestess. Ilse Vane. She walks out of this town under my protection, in custody, alive and unharmed as far as the capital — I give you my word on that, and my word has rotted some but it hasn't rotted all the way through. And in exchange—" he exhaled, "—you get your name back."

The fire popped. Somewhere upstairs, faintly, a floorboard creaked, and both of them glanced at the ceiling at the same instant, and then away, and neither acknowledged it.

"My name," Tamsin said carefully, "was struck when the order fell."

"It was struck because you *ran*." Roderic's jaw tightened. "Because when Master Aldous refused the crown's terms, when he told us all to scatter and bury our swords, you were the one who carried the relics out the back of the chapel while the rest of us stood in the yard and were *counted*. You got away clean. We got attainted. Every man who swore beside you lost his lands, his name, his right to carry steel. Do you know what that does to a man, Tam? Do you know what it did to *me*?"

"I know what it did to you," Tamsin said quietly. "I've spent fifteen years knowing it."

"And here's the thing." Roderic spread his hands. "The crown will unmake it. All of it. Your name restored, your attainer reversed, a grant of land — the old order lands, the ones at Harrowmere, they've sat empty for years, the Regent will sign them over. And the priestess walks south under guard instead of under a headsman, because I'll swear she's worth more breathing. Everyone lives. You get your life back." He paused. "You get *us* back. What's left of us. There are four of us still drawing breath, Tam. Four. And every one of them would take this bargain tomorrow."

* * *

That was the knife of it, and Roderic knew it was the knife, and Tamsin loved him for using it and hated him for being right.

Four. He'd thought he was the last. He'd built fifteen years on being the last — it was easier that way, cleaner, grief with a floor under it. Now there were four, and each of them was a debt, and Roderic was holding all of them out like a hand of cards.

"There's a problem with your carrot," Tamsin said.

"One of several, I imagine."

"She hasn't done anything."

"No," Roderic agreed. "She's *going* to do something. Or she knows something that unmakes something else. Either way, the Regent wants her, and the Regent wanting someone is weather, Tam. You don't argue with weather. You get under a roof."

"Whose roof? Hers is burning."

"Then help me build her a different one." Roderic's voice roughened. "Gods' teeth, Tam, listen to yourself. You're smuggling trinkets through passes for coin like some cutpurse with a conscience. Is that what Aldous died for? Is that what the covenant was *for* — so you could spend thirty years bribing mule-drivers and sleeping in ditches? Come home. Come *back*. Be Ser Tamsin Quintana again, with a hall and a hearth and men who'd die for you, and let the dead god stay dead. He's been dead longer than we've been alive. Nobody's coming back from that. Not him, not the order, not any of it. The only thing that comes back is what the living choose to bring back. So choose it."

For a moment — a long, treacherous moment — Tamsin let himself see it. Harrowmere in summer. A hall with his banner on it, except it wouldn't be the speaking-sword crossed with the seven stars anymore, it would be the crowned stag, because nothing came free. Roderic across a table, older, grayer, laughing at some old joke about the time Tamsin had fallen off Wren into a bog in front of a visiting dignitary. Four old swordspeakers growing old together instead of dying scattered.

It would be so easy. That was the whole horror of it. The crown had priced it precisely, the way the crown priced everything, and the price was fair, and that was how you knew it was poison. Fair prices were for things that existed. What Roderic was selling was a ghost of a life that had never actually been lived.

"And her?" Tamsin said. "When the Regent finishes questioning her?"

Roderic was quiet for a long moment. Rain ticked against the shutters.

"I can protect her as far as the capital," he said finally. "I can't protect her past it. I won't lie to you. I've done enough lying for one lifetime."

"There it is."

"There it is," Roderic agreed wearily. "One honest offer, one honest flaw. Take it or don't."

Tamsin stood. Slowly, so the movement wouldn't read as reaching for a weapon, though they both knew where his knife was and Roderic had known since before the scar over his eye. He picked up the warrant, looked at his own face described in a stranger's handwriting, and set it back down on the table.

"Here's my answer," he said. "I'm going to tell you a thing I've never told anyone, and then you're going to leave, and we're both going to pretend this conversation was about a warrant."

Roderic's eyes narrowed. "Tam—"

"At Cael's Ford. The night before. You asked me why Aldous wouldn't bend, and I told you I didn't know." Tamsin kept his voice level, though something behind it was shaking. "I lied. I knew. He told me. He told me what the crown was wearing on its head, and what it cost, and where it came from. He made me swear to carry the truth out if the order fell, and I swore, and I've been carrying it ever since, and it's heavy, Roddy. It's so heavy. And every year I hoped someone would come take it off me, and every year nobody came, and I told myself the order was dead and the weight was mine alone."

Roderic had gone very still. "What did he tell you?"

"That's the question, isn't it." Tamsin smiled, and knew it was a bad smile, the kind that showed teeth. "That's the question the Regent sent you to ask. And I'm not going to answer it. But I'll tell you this for free, brother — and I still get to call you that, whatever the law says, because oaths outlive attainers: the priestess knows more than I do. The gods aren't dead. They're *starving*. And the difference matters, and in a while — soon — everyone in Thornfell is going to find out the difference matters, and the men standing next to the Regent when it happens are going to wish they'd stood somewhere else."

"You're threatening me with a prophecy."

"I'm warning you with one. There's a difference. Prophecies threaten; warnings grieve." Tamsin walked around the table and stood close, close enough to smell rain and leather and the particular soap Roderic had used since he was nineteen, because some things survived even a crown's forgiveness. "Take the warrant back. Tell Veyra the trail went cold. Buy me a week, Roddy. I know you can. I know you've buried worse truths for better pay."

"And if I don't?"

"Then arrest me. Here, now. Drag me south in chains and hang me, and Ilse Vane slips out of this town tonight through a door I've already paid for, and you trade one prize for nothing." He held out his wrists, mockingly, gently. "Well? I'm told I'm wanted for questioning."

For a heartbeat he genuinely didn't know which way Roderic would go. That was the grief of it, distilled: fifteen years ago he'd have known to the second. They'd fought back to back at Cael's Ford; you learned a man's timing that way, learned it in your bones. Now there was a stranger's pause where the certainty used to live.

Roderic looked at the offered wrists. Then he laughed — short, bitter, real — and shoved Tamsin's arms down.

"Get those out of my face. You always were a theatrical bastard." He stood, gathered the warrant, tucked it away. At the door he stopped, one hand on the frame, back still turned. "A week," he said. "I can give you a week. After that the Regent sends people who aren't me, and Tam — the woman with her. Vane. Whatever she is to you." He glanced back, and his eyes were the old eyes, the Cael's Ford eyes, tired and fond and afraid. "Don't let her be a reason you die stupid. You've earned a smarter death than that. We all have."

Then he was gone, into the rain, and Wren's hooves rang on the cobbles, and the Drowned Bell exhaled around him.

* * *

Tamsin climbed the stairs and found Ilse waiting in the dark of the little room, not pretending to sleep, sitting by the window with her book closed on her knee.

"Well?" she said.

"He gave us a week."

"And what did it cost you?"

Tamsin sat down on the bed, which complained, as everything in Lowmark complained, and put his head in his hands. "He offered me everything," he said. "Name, lands, the order's ghosts, pardoned and fed and warm. All I had to do was hand you over."

"And?"

"And I told him the truth for the first time in fifteen years, which is that I've known about the crown since the night the order fell, and I let him believe he'd failed to pry it out of me." He laughed, hollow. "Fifteen years of lying to myself that I ran because I was a coward. Turns out I ran because I was carrying something. Doesn't feel braver. Feels exactly the same, actually. Just heavier."

Ilse crossed the room. She didn't touch him — she never touched him first, some old courtesy of hers he'd never understood — but she sat beside him, close enough that her shoulder warmed his arm, and said nothing, which was the thing about her that undid him most thoroughly. Most people filled silence like leaking boats. Ilse just held it open, like a door.

"He called me a reason you might die stupid," Tamsin said at last.

"Did you correct him?"

"Haven't decided yet."

She laughed — quiet, startled out of her — and in the dark of the rented room in a wet border town, with a week on the clock and a mountain between them and everything that mattered, Tamsin thought: *oh.* Thought it the way you think a dangerous thought, all at once and too late.

"We leave before dawn," he said, standing quickly, busying himself with straps and buckles that didn't need him. "There's a pass east of here that isn't on any map. The Hollow Steps. My master showed me when I was seventeen and swore me to forget it, which I did, professionally, until just now."

"A pass only a smuggler would know," Ilse said, "and only a swordspeaker could open."

"Something like that." He cinched a strap. "Why?"

"Because," Ilse said softly, "if the gods are waking, Tamsin, then the mountain already knows you're coming. And I should very much like to see what it says to you when you knock."

Outside, the rain kept falling, steady as a held breath, and somewhere south a grey mare carried an oath-brother back toward a crown that had bought him twice.
