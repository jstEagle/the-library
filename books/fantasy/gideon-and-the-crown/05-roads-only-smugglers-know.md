## 5. Roads Only Smugglers Know

The Hollow Steps began, as all the best roads did, with a lie.

"You're telling me," said Doss, the eldest of Tamsin's mule drivers, squinting at what appeared to be a solid wall of granite and mountain ash, "that there's a road in there."

"I'm telling you there's a road behind there," Tamsin corrected. "Whether it's still in there is between the mountain and me."

"And if it isn't?"

"Then we've had a lovely walk and a bracing conversation about disappointment."

Doss grunted, which was how he said *I hate you* without wasting breath on it. Behind him, the other three drivers — Ferro, the twins Pell and Pem — were doing their best to look like men who had not just fled a border town ahead of a royal warrant. They were failing. Ferro kept checking the road behind them every forty steps, which was thirty-nine more than was useful.

Tamsin let them grumble. Grumbling was ballast; it kept a crew steady in bad weather. He had learned that from Master Corren, back when the swordspeakers still had masters, and back when Tamsin had believed everything Corren taught him — including the part about the gods being worth dying for.

He didn't believe that anymore. He believed in mules, in passes that existed on no map, and in the general principle that anything the crown wanted badly enough to issue a warrant for was worth taking away from it. That was a complete philosophy. It had kept him fed for fifteen years.

It was currently being tested by a priestess who walked beside his lead mule like she'd been born to it, which annoyed him more than he could say.

"You're going left at the split," Ilse Vane said.

"I'm going right at the split."

"Left."

"The right-hand path has better footing."

"The right-hand path has a widow-maker snow bridge over a drop you could lose a cathedral down." She said it mildly, the way one comments on the weather. "Left."

Tamsin stopped. The mule, whose name was Duchess and who had opinions about everyone, stopped too, because even Duchess liked the priestess, which was another mark against her.

"How do you know that?"

Ilse looked at him. She had gray eyes, unremarkable until they weren't — until she looked *at* you instead of near you, and then they seemed older than her face, older than the town, possibly older than the mountain. "Because I crossed this pass two hundred years ago," she said, "and I remember falling through that bridge, and I remember Brother Anselm fishing me out by the ankle while swearing oaths that would blister your ears."

"That's a charming story."

"It's true."

"People say lots of charming things are true."

"They do," she agreed pleasantly, and turned left at the split.

Tamsin stood a moment longer, then followed, because the alternative was arguing with a woman who remembered things that hadn't happened yet in the lifetime of anyone breathing, and he'd already lost three arguments to her since Lowmark. He was keeping count. He suspected she was too.

* * *

They made camp that first night in a hollow beneath an overhang that Tamsin had used a dozen times, dry and defensible and invisible from below unless you knew where the smoke went. He built the fire small. Old habits.

The fellowship Ilse had gathered was five souls besides herself: a lay brother named Anselm — young, earnest, and no relation to any dead monk as far as anyone could prove — who kept the relics they'd salvaged from Lowmark wrapped in oilcloth like newborns; a one-eyed hedge-witch called Maren who cooked as though seasoning were a moral failing; and three former shrine-keepers from villages along the Thornfell spine, quiet people with quiet hands, the kind who swept floors and lit candles and had spent two centuries tending altars that never answered.

Now the altars answered. Tamsin had seen it himself, in Lowmark: a dying boy, a silent shrine, and then a voice out of two hundred years of nothing, saying *yes*. The whole town had gone still around it, like a forest when a wolf walks through.

He didn't like thinking about it. So naturally he thought about nothing else.

"You knew the Hollow Steps," he said to Ilse, when the others had turned in. They sat with the fire between them, and Duchess had planted herself closer to Ilse than to him, the traitor. "Before. In your other life."

"In my first life, yes."

"Don't you find that strange? Walking a road your feet remember but your head doesn't?"

She considered it honestly, which was worse than a glib answer. "Sometimes I dream in a language I can't speak awake," she said. "Sometimes I reach for a door handle that isn't where my hand expects it. It's like wearing someone else's coat. It fits, mostly. But the pockets hold things I didn't put there." She glanced up. "Does that frighten you?"

"Nothing frightens me. I'm a professional."

"Mm. Your mule-driver checked the back trail eleven times today."

"Ferro's nervous. It's a character flaw."

"Tamsin." She said his name carefully, the way you set down something breakable. "You don't have to believe me about the past life. Not yet. But believe this much: the gods are waking. Something answered that boy in Lowmark. And whatever wears the old voices now, it knows your name."

"Everyone knows my name. There's a warrant."

"Not the name on the warrant."

The fire popped. Somewhere above them, wind moved through the pines with a sound like distant speech, and Tamsin found he couldn't make himself dismiss it, which frightened him slightly more than he admitted.

* * *

The second day was the hard day, the reason the Hollow Steps existed on no map. The pass wasn't climbed so much as threaded — a sequence of ledges and chimneys and shelf-roads stitched along the mountain's flank, some cut by hands long vanished, some made by nothing but water and stubbornness. Twice they roped the mules across scree fields where a misstep meant a very long lesson in gravity. Doss cursed continuously and creatively. Pell fell into Pem. Pem fell into Ferro. Ferro swore off ever working for Tamsin again, for the fourth time that year.

By late afternoon they reached the place Tamsin had been aiming for since Lowmark: a shelf of rock above a hanging valley, wide enough for camp, sheltered by a leaning slab of stone that the smugglers called the Deacon, for reasons nobody remembered. From there, a single day's travel would bring them out past the crown's last checkpoint, into country where warrants were considered suggestions.

And from the Deacon, if you knew where to look, you could see the old shrine.

It was nothing much — a niche in the cliff face across the valley, marked by a carved lintel worn nearly smooth, the way all the swordspeaker shrines were worn. Tamsin had passed it perhaps fifty times in fifteen years of running this road. It had never once done anything. Shrines didn't. That was the arrangement: the gods were dead or gone or stolen, the prayers went nowhere, and honest men carried relics for coin and told themselves stories about covenant and duty to make the carrying feel like something other than theft.

He sat looking at it while the others made camp, and the question he'd been swallowing since Lowmark finally worked its way up.

"Ilse."

"Hm?"

"If I asked the mountain for a sign — a real sign, proof of what you're claiming — what would happen?"

She came and sat beside him, following his gaze to the shrine. For a long moment she said nothing, and he could feel her deciding how much truth he could carry.

"What did your order teach you about asking?" she said at last.

"That you don't. A swordspeaker speaks when spoken *to*. Asking is presumption. Master Corren used to say the covenant was a door that opened from the other side, and our whole discipline was learning to stand politely by the hinges."

"And do you still stand by the hinges?"

"I don't stand anywhere near the house. The house burned down. I told you."

"You told me the god you swore to died." Her voice was gentle. "I'm telling you it was murdered, and murder leaves a body, and bodies can be found. And Tamsin — the thing in Lowmark that answered that boy? It used the old words. The *true* forms. Whatever answered, it remembers the covenant better than you do."

He looked at the little worn shrine across the valley, at the evening light going amber on the lintel, and felt the old ache — the one he'd spent fifteen years burying under freight manifests and toll bribes — shift in its grave.

"One sign," he said. "That's all I'm asking. If there's anything left up there — anything that isn't a trick of the crown or a fever of yours — give me one sign I can't argue with. Because I am extremely good at arguing."

Ilse smiled faintly. "Careful. Doors open from the other side."

"I know. I'm knocking anyway."

* * *

He almost didn't sleep. When he finally did, the dream took him without ceremony, the way weather takes a valley.

He stood on the shelf-road in daylight that wasn't quite daylight, and the mountain was talking.

Not in words, exactly. Not at first. It was more that the stone itself had become language — the grain of the rock, the fall of the scree, the slow green insistence of the mountain ash splitting the ledge, all of it saying the same thing in different registers, the way a bell says one note through bronze and air and bone. And underneath it, unmistakable, patient, amused in a way that made his chest hurt:

*Tamsin Quintana.*

Not the name on the warrant. The other name. The oath-name, spoken only twice in his life — once when he knelt, and once when he ran — and both times by a man now fifteen years dead.

*You knocked,* the mountain said. *Nobody knocks. Two hundred years, and nobody knocks.*

"I'm not—" His voice came out wrong, young. "I don't believe in you."

*I know. It's one of your best qualities. Belief is cheap. You brought doubt, and effort, and a truly unreasonable attachment to a mule.* A pause that felt like a smile. *Ask your question, hinge-stander.*

"Why now?" It came out of him raw. "Two hundred years of silence. Why answer prayers *now*, why wake now, why drag a smuggler and a priestess and a boy king into it — why now?"

The mountain considered. He felt it considering, the way you feel a storm deciding.

*Because the thief is nearly finished,* it said. *Because the crown holds the last of my name in a band of gold, and when the child is crowned, the holding becomes keeping, and the keeping becomes forever. Because I have been whispering through cracks for two centuries, and the cracks are closing.* Then, softer, in a register that bypassed his ears entirely and landed somewhere behind his ribs: *And because you asked. Do you understand what that means, Tamsin? Of all the sworn ones scattered and sold and forgotten — you stood at the hinges and knocked.*

"I broke the covenant. I ran. I—"

*You carried,* the mountain said. *Fifteen years, through my passes, past my shrines, with my relics in your packs. You thought you were smuggling. Child. You were the last courier of a faith that forgot it had one.* The vast attention of it settled on him like snowfall. *One sign, you asked. Very well.*

The dream-light bent. Across the valley, the little worn shrine — fifty years of passing it, fifty years of nothing — began, very quietly, to glow. Not firelight. Not moonlight. It glowed the way a face glows when someone you mourned walks through the door: warm, impossible, familiar. And out of the niche, thin as thread and clear as a struck bell, rose a sound Tamsin had heard exactly once before, at nineteen, kneeling on cold stone:

the opening line of the Swordspeaker's Vigil, sung in a voice that had been waiting two hundred years for an audience.

He woke with tears frozen on his cheeks and the whole camp already standing, staring across the valley, where the shrine's light was fading but the echo still hung in the cold air like a held breath.

Doss was the first to speak. "Tell me you planned that."

"No," Tamsin said. His voice cracked on it. He cleared his throat and tried again. "No. That wasn't me."

"Then what was it?" Ferro whispered, back-trail forgotten.

Tamsin looked at Ilse. She was watching him with an expression he couldn't read — hope and grief and something fiercely tender, all held very still, the way you hold a bird you're afraid to startle.

"That," he said slowly, "was a door opening from the other side."

He shouldered his pack. Above them the Deacon leaned against the sky, and beyond it lay the last checkpoint, the capital, a crowned child, and a theft two hundred years in the finishing.

"We move now," he said. "While the mountain's feeling talkative."

And far overhead, so faint that only he caught it, the wind through the pines laughed.
