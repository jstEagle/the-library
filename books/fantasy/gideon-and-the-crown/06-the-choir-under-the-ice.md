## 6. The Choir Under the Ice

The cold found them two days into the Hollow Steps, and it did not knock.

It came down through the throat of the pass like something poured, filling every hollow of the mountain, and by the third morning Tamsin's breath froze in his beard before it finished leaving him. The mules had been left behind at the shepherd's stone with young Piet to mind them — the Steps were no place for animals past a certain point — so now they climbed on their own legs, six of them: Tamsin, Ilse Vane, old Marta with her herb-satchels, the brothers Fen and Aldous who could carry twice their weight and complain about it in harmony, and quiet Tammas Wren, who had not yet told anyone why he'd left the salt trade, and whom nobody had yet been rude enough to ask.

"Tell me again," said Fen, teeth chattering, "why we couldn't have taken the southern road. The southern road has inns."

"The southern road has soldiers," said Tamsin.

"The southern road has *heated* soldiers."

"That's just the fever from sleeping in ditches," said Aldous. "You had it last winter too."

"I had a *warm* ditch last winter."

Tamsin let them fill the silence with their grumbling, because that was what a good crew was for. A quiet crew meant fear. A complaining crew meant they still believed they'd live long enough for the complaints to matter.

Ilse walked beside him without complaint, which worried him more than he liked to admit. She had a way of looking up at the ice-glazed walls of the gorge as though she were reading them — as though the frozen waterfalls and the blue-veined stone were pages she'd read once, long ago, in another life that was not a figure of speech.

"You know this place," he said. It wasn't a question.

"I've never been here," she said. "But I dreamed of this bend when I was nine years old. There's a chapel ahead. Under the ice. My grandmother used to sing a hymn about it." She paused. "I didn't have a grandmother who sang. That's how I knew the memory wasn't mine."

Tamsin said nothing. Ahead, the gorge narrowed to a slot of grey sky, and the wind dropped all at once, the way it does when a mountain decides to listen.

"There," Ilse said softly.

He saw it a heartbeat later. Set back in the north wall, half-drowned in a curtain of ice, a doorway. Not carved — *built*, the old way, stones fitted without mortar, and above the lintel a worn relief that made his chest tighten like a fist closing: seven swords crossed over an open hand.

The sign of the Order of the Quiet Blade. His order. Dead fifteen years, scattered and burned and sworn-at, its name struck from the rolls of Thornfell.

"That's not possible," he heard himself say. "The reliquary of the Quiet Blade was lost at Redford Bridge. Burned. Everyone knows—"

"Everyone knows what the crown paid everyone to know," Ilse said gently.

* * *

Getting inside was work. The ice had grown over the doorway like scar tissue over an old wound, and it took Marta's salts and Fen's shoulders and the better part of an hour of chipping before the passage opened enough to admit them one at a time. The air beyond was still — not dead-still, but held, the way breath is held. Warmer than outside by some small mercy, and utterly dry.

Then Tammas Wren lit the first lantern, and Tamsin forgot how to breathe.

It was a chapel. A real one, whole, untouched. Rows of stone benches worn smooth by generations of kneeling. An altar of black granite with seven shallow grooves where sword-points had rested during the swearing. And along every wall, in niches cut with loving precision, reliquaries — dozens of them, small caskets of iron and bone-white wood, each one labeled in the old script:

*BROTHER HALLAM, WHO HELD THE PASS.*
*SISTER ORENNA, WHO SANG THE DYING HOME.*
*BROTHER CAEL OF THE FORD, WHO NEVER SWORE TWICE.*

Two centuries of the Quiet Blade, sleeping in the dark under the ice while the world above forgot them.

"They weren't lost," Tamsin said. His voice came out strange, flattened. "They were *hidden*. Brought here before Redford Bridge ever burned. Someone saved them."

"Someone who knew what was coming," Ilse murmured. She was standing very still, her eyes on the altar. "Tamsin. Do you feel that?"

He did. He'd been trying not to. Beneath the stillness of the chapel there was something else — a pressure, faint as a pulse through a wall, rhythmic and patient. Like a choir holding its note between verses.

"Don't touch the reliquaries," he said. "Not yet. Let me—" He didn't finish the sentence, because his feet were already carrying him toward the altar, because some part of him that had sworn oaths in blood and silence recognized the room the way a hound recognizes home, and because — gods forgive him — he wanted to know if anyone was left in there. If anything of his brothers and sisters had survived the burning of the order besides guilt.

His palm touched the black granite.

The chapel went white.

* * *

He stood on a bridge in high summer.

He knew it instantly, the way you know your own hands — Redford Bridge, the day the order died. But it was wrong. It was wrong in every particular. The sun shone. The river ran gold. And the bridge was crowded with swordspeakers in their grey cloaks, alive, laughing, turning to face him as he arrived.

"Tamsin!" Brother Hallam raised a hand in greeting, big and bearded and warm as a hearth. "You came after all!"

"We thought you'd gone over the mountains," said Sister Orenna, smiling. "We told them you wouldn't. We told them you'd come."

There were hundreds of them. Every oath-brother and oath-sister he had ever trained beside, prayed beside, bled beside — and more, generations of them, faces from the old portraits in the chapter houses, all of them gathered on the sunlit bridge and all of them looking at him with such open, uncomplicated gladness that it was worse than hatred. Hatred he could have carried. He'd been carrying it for fifteen years; it fit his shoulders like a pack.

This — this was unbearable.

"I didn't come," Tamsin said. His voice cracked in the middle of the word *come*. "I wasn't there. I was in Lowmark buying grain. I got the summons late and I rode anyway and I was three days too late and I—"

"We know," said Hallam, kindly.

"You died believing I abandoned you."

"No," said Orenna. "We died believing you would carry us. And you have. Every relic you smuggled, every shrine you kept swept, every prayer you pretended not to pray — we felt all of it. You've been saying our funeral rites for fifteen years, brother. In actions instead of words. It's the longest eulogy ever spoken."

"But you're angry," Tamsin insisted, because somewhere beneath the vision he could feel his body shaking, could hear someone calling his name from very far away, and panic was rising in him like floodwater. "You must be. Look me in the eye and tell me you're not—"

Hallam's great hand settled on his shoulder, and it weighed exactly as much as it always had.

"We're tired," Hallam said. "That's different from angry. Two hundred years is a long time to hold a note, Tamsin. Even for the faithful." He leaned closer, and his voice dropped, and behind his eyes Tamsin saw something vast move, something older than the man, something that wore his friend's face the way dawn wears a window. "*The crown took our voices one coronation at a time. But the vault under the palace holds the last of the name, and the boy will be crowned in spring, and when the crown closes over his head the door shuts forever.*"

"Hallam—"

"Wake up, brother. Wake up. The woman is holding you. Hold on to her."

* * *

"—amsin. *Tamsin.* Breathe. You're in the chapel. You're on the floor. You're safe."

He surfaced gasping, and the first thing he saw was Ilse Vane's face, close above his, framed by lantern-light, wet-eyed and absolutely steady. Her hands were on either side of his jaw, firm and cool, and she was speaking in a low, even rhythm — not words, quite. A cadence. Something older than language, that his bones understood before his ears did.

"Breathe with me," she said. "In. Now out. Again."

He breathed. The chapel assembled itself around him piece by piece: the benches, the niches, the brothers Fen and Aldous hovering with the wide eyes of men who had seen a comrade fall down and start weeping and reciting names — dozens of names, they'd tell him later, hours' worth of names, every swordspeaker the order had ever lost, spoken in order of death like a roll being called.

"How long?" he rasped.

"A few minutes. It felt longer to you?"

"It felt like fifteen years." He tried to sit up. His arms shook. Ilse slid her shoulder under his arm without asking, and he let her, which frightened him more than the vision had. "I saw them. All of them. On the bridge. They weren't angry, Ilse. They were *glad* to see me. Do you understand how much worse that is?"

"Yes," she said quietly. "Because anger you can pay off. Gladness you owe forever."

He looked at her then, really looked, and saw that her hands were trembling too — had been, probably, the whole time she'd held him steady. Anchoring him had cost her something. She'd done it anyway.

"What did you do? When I was under?"

"My grandmother's hymn. The one about the chapel under the ice." She gave a small, crooked smile. "Turns out I actually know it. Turns out I've known it my whole life and only just noticed. It's a rope, Tamsin. A rope thrown across the water. I just kept singing until you caught it."

Fen cleared his throat from near the door. "Begging pardon, but are we going to talk about the fact that Quintana just spent four minutes naming the dead in a dead language none of us speaks? Because I'd like that conversation to happen while I'm sitting down."

"Later," Tamsin said. He got his feet under him, with Ilse's help, and stood swaying in the lantern-light among two centuries of his sleeping order. "Later, and with wine."

But he didn't move toward the door. Instead he walked — slowly, leaning once on a bench — to the nearest reliquary. *Brother Hallam, Who Held the Pass.* He put his palm flat on the lid, the way you'd touch the shoulder of a sleeping friend you hadn't dared wake.

"Hold on a little longer," he said softly. "All of you. Spring is coming, and I'm going to give you back your voices. Every one. I swear it on the blade and the quiet."

The pressure in the chapel shifted — eased, the way a held note resolves into a chord. And in the silence that followed, faint as frost forming, Tamsin heard it: not one voice, but many, layered, patient, singing the same low line his bones already knew.

A choir, keeping faith under the ice.

Ilse came to stand beside him. "What did they say? At the end?"

"That the woman is holding me." He glanced sideways at her, and for once didn't dress the look in irony. "And that I should hold on to you."

Something moved through her face — hope and grief sharing a single expression, the way they do when they've lived together too long. "Then they're wiser dead than most people living," she said.

They were turning to go — there was nothing more to carry from this place except the knowing of it, and the reliquaries were safer hidden than moved — when Tammas Wren, who had been standing watch at the entrance, came back inside at a run, and his face had gone the color of the ice outside.

"Smoke," he said. "Down-valley. A lot of it. And riders — thirty, maybe forty, flying the boar-and-crown."

The Regent's colors. Hunting season had opened early.

Tamsin looked at Ilse, and Ilse looked at the dark mouth of the pass, and somewhere far below, the first war-horns of the hunt began to sound.
