## 7. A King Who Is Only Nine

The city of Carrowmere smelled of roasting chestnuts and horse dung, which Ilse Vane decided was as honest a description of any capital as she'd ever encountered.

She had never seen so many people in one place. Lowmark on market day felt crowded; Carrowmere during a coronation week felt like the world had tilted and everyone in it had slid downhill into the same bowl. The streets ran silver with bunting. Every window hung cloth — blue for the crown, white for the old faith that everyone politely pretended still mattered, gold for the Regent's household, because Regent Veyra's colors got hung twice as high as anyone else's.

"Smile," Tamsin murmured beside her. He wore a drover's coat two sizes too big and a hat that made him look like a man who apologized to furniture. "You're a pilgrim's widow come to see the crowning. Widows smile. It's in the scriptures somewhere."

"I am not your widow."

"Then you're my sister."

"I'm older than you by at least ten years."

"Family comes in all shapes." He adjusted the strap of the basket on her hip — full of candle-stubs and cheap incense, the props of their disguise, and underneath the false bottom, the ledger that had started all of this. "Walk like your feet hurt and no one will ever look at you twice."

Ilse walked like her feet hurt. It was not difficult; they had come up from the Salt Road in four days of hard marching, and her boots had opinions about it.

Behind them came the rest of the fellowship, strung out along the pilgrim road like beads on a fraying cord: Bren the mule-driver, who had discovered upon entering the city that he was terrified of crowds and was currently gripping the tail of Tamsin's coat with the devotion of a drowning man; Old Sef, who was enjoying himself enormously and kept pointing out pickpockets to the pickpockets as a form of professional courtesy; and Marta and young Fen, the twins from the Thornfell valleys, who had never seen buildings taller than a barn and were walking backward down the street staring at the bell tower until Bren hauled them around by their collars without breaking stride.

"We're going to die here," Bren announced.

"You said that about the pass," Tamsin said.

"The pass didn't have forty thousand people in it."

"The pass would have killed us faster, though. There's something to be said for efficiency."

Ilse let their voices wash over her and watched the city instead. She was looking for what she always looked for: shrines.

There should have been shrines everywhere. A city this old, built in the shadow of the gods' mountain itself, should have had a chapel on every corner, a wayside stone at every crossroads, niches in the walls where travelers could leave a candle or a coin or a whispered word. Two hundred years ago, the chronicles said, you couldn't spit in Carrowmere without hitting something holy.

Now she counted. In the length of the Pilgrim's Way, from the South Gate to the great square, she found three shrines. One had been converted to a fruit stall. One was boarded over with a tax notice pasted to the planks. The third still stood open — a little alcove of grey stone with a worn statue of the Shepherd of Paths, hands outstretched — but its offering dish held nothing but rainwater and a single button.

"Look," she said quietly.

Tamsin glanced. His jaw did the thing it did when he was keeping his face pleasant and his thoughts elsewhere. "Button's probably an offering," he said. "Somebody loved that button."

"Tamsin."

"I know." Softer now. "I see it. Keep walking."

* * *

They lodged at the Drowned Bell, an inn tucked into a tanner's court where the smell discouraged official attention. Sef knew the owner from some previous life involving smuggled brandy and a fire nobody talked about, and so they got two rooms, the stable loft, and a warning: the whole quarter would be locked down tomorrow night for the Procession of the Heir, and anyone on the streets without a festival token would be arrested first and asked questions eventually.

"So we get tokens," Fen said.

"So we get tokens," Tamsin agreed. "Sef?"

"I know a man."

"You always know a man."

"That's why you keep me around instead of feeding me to the mountain."

Ilse sat by the window that evening, mending a seam in Marta's sleeve while the girl slept, and listened to the city breathe. Somewhere distant, choirs were rehearsing. Bells tested themselves in fragments, one note at a time, like men clearing their throats before saying something important.

Tomorrow, a nine-year-old boy would be crowned King of Thornfell.

Tomorrow, whatever fragment of the stolen name still lived in the crown would be drawn into him, and the theft would be complete, and the gods would be silent forever — not dead, which would at least be clean, but *emptied*, their names worn like coats by a line of kings who told themselves it was tradition.

She thought about the ledger again. She had read it so many times she could recite the entries. *Year 201 of the Vane Reckoning: the Rite of Assumption performed. Yield: sufficient.* And beneath, in a different hand, smaller, almost ashamed: *The vessel wept.*

She had assumed, when she first read those words, that "the vessel" meant the crown itself. Stone and gold, weeping. A figure of speech.

She was beginning to suspect it hadn't been a figure of speech at all.

* * *

The Procession of the Heir began at dusk, when the lamps were lit and the whole city poured into the great square like water finding its level.

Their festival tokens got them a spot behind a rope near the square's eastern edge, wedged between a sausage vendor who had abandoned all pretense of selling sausages and a family of six from the river country who shared roasted chestnuts down the line with the generosity of people who had decided, provisionally, that strangers were friends. Ilse ended up holding somebody's baby. She wasn't entirely sure how. The baby gripped her finger with the fierce authority of a small emperor and refused to relinquish it, and she found she didn't mind.

"There," Marta breathed. "There he is."

The procession came up the Processional Way under a canopy of white silk, and the crowd went quiet in that particular way crowds do — not silent, but hushed, a thousand murmurs folding down into one held breath.

Ilse saw the Regent first. Veyra rode a grey mare half a length ahead of the palanquin, tall and straight in black and gold, her face composed into an expression Ilse recognized because she'd practiced it herself in mirrors: serenity worn like armor. Behind her, the palanquin swayed on the shoulders of eight bearers, curtained in white.

And inside it, visible through the parted silk whenever the lanterns swung just right —

A boy.

He was small. That was the first thing. All the banners and bells and the weight of two centuries of ceremony, and the person it was all for was a small boy in a white robe too big for his shoulders, sitting very straight with his hands folded in his lap, the way children sit when someone has told them exactly how to sit and they are concentrating on it with everything they have.

He was waving. Someone had told him to wave, and he waved, a small mechanical motion of one hand, and the crowd roared back at him, and Ilse watched his face each time the roar came.

He flinched.

Just slightly. Just at the corner of his eyes. Each time the crowd thundered, the boy flinched like a man expecting a blow, and then smoothed his face back into the smile he'd been given, and waved again.

"Oh," Ilse said. It came out of her before she could stop it, soft and horrified.

"What?" Tamsin was scanning the rooftops, the guards, the sightlines — doing his job, cataloguing exits and threats. "What is it?"

"Look at him. Really look."

Tamsin looked. She watched him look. She watched the smuggler fall away from his face, and the swordspeaker rise up underneath it, and then something older than both — the expression of a man seeing a wound he recognized.

"He's frightened," Ilse said. "He's nine years old and he's being carried through a screaming crowd toward the worst thing that will ever happen to him, and everyone is cheering, and he thinks — " Her throat tightened. "He thinks he's supposed to be grateful."

"He's the heir," Bren said uncomfortably. "Kings aren't—"

"Kings are children first," Ilse said. "Someone seems to have forgotten to tell this one that part ends."

The palanquin drew level with them. For three heartbeats — four — the boy was close enough that Ilse could see the shadows under his eyes, the chapped place on his lower lip where he'd been biting it, the way his folded hands trembled very slightly, pressed together to hide it.

And she felt it.

Not saw. *Felt.* Like standing near a door left open onto winter. Something in the boy was *thin*. Stretched. Hollowed the way a gourd is hollowed — carefully, from within, leaving the shape intact. She had spent her life around holiness in its long silence, tending altars that no longer answered, and she knew the texture of absence the way a sailor knows weather.

But this wasn't absence. This was absence *being made*, actively, patiently, the way a moth makes lace of wool. Something was drinking from the boy even now, in these last days before the crowning, preparing him, thinning him out so that when the crown came down and the last fragment of the stolen name poured home, there would be room enough for it.

Room enough for a god's name in a space the size of a child's soul.

The baby in her arms fussed, and she rocked it absently, and the boy-king's eyes swept across the crowd and — passed over her, paused, came back.

For one instant, across thirty feet of rope and cheering strangers, Aldric of the House of Thornfell looked directly at Ilse Vane.

His eyes were grey. Ordinary grey, a child's eyes, red-rimmed from crying somewhere private recently. And in them was not malice, not ambition, none of the cold things she had dressed the enemy in through four hundred miles of hard road.

In them was a question. The wordless, desperate question of a child in deep water watching adults stand on the shore: *do you see me? does anyone see me?*

Then the palanquin swayed on, and the silk curtains shifted, and he was gone up the Processional Way into the light of the cathedral steps, and the crowd roared, and the baby cried, and Ilse stood holding someone else's child with tears running freely down her face.

* * *

Back at the Drowned Bell, the fellowship argued plans by lamplight while Sef dealt cards to himself in the corner and lost with impressive consistency.

"The vault entrance is under the cathedral," Tamsin said, tapping the table. "Which means tomorrow, while every eye in the city is on the crowning, that's our moment. We go in during the ceremony."

"Under the cathedral," Bren repeated. "During the coronation. Of the king. While the entire royal guard stands around feeling important."

"When else? Everyone who can arrest us will be busy watching a nine-year-old put on a hat."

"It's not a hat," Ilse said quietly. "It's a wound."

The room went still. Tamsin turned to her. "Say that again?"

She wrapped her hands around her cup of wine she wasn't drinking. "I saw him today. Truly saw him. Tamsin — the crown isn't just holding the name. It's *feeding*. It's been feeding on the royal line for two hundred years, siphoning them drop by drop, and tomorrow it takes the rest. That boy isn't a thief. He's not even a willing vessel. He's — " She searched for it. "He's a meal that's been taught to set its own table."

Marta, curled in the window seat, hugged her knees. "Can't we just... warn him? Tell him not to wear the crown?"

"And then what?" Sef said, not unkindly, laying down another losing card. "Veyra puts it on herself? Or finds another nephew? The crown doesn't stop wanting just because one boy says no."

"Then we take the name out of it," Fen said. "That's the whole plan anyway, isn't it? Carry it to the mountain."

"The plan," Tamsin said slowly, "was to steal a relic from a vault. I'm starting to think the relic has a heartbeat."

Ilse met his eyes across the table. "You saw him too. On the procession route. Don't tell me you didn't."

Tamsin was quiet for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice had the flat carefulness of a man handling something sharp. "I spent fifteen years telling myself the order died for abstractions. Principles. Oaths. Words old men carved into stone because they liked the sound of them." He turned his cup in a slow circle. "Today I watched a child flinch at his own coronation, and I thought: *this* is what Master Corvin wouldn't kneel for. Not theology. A boy who's afraid."

"Then we're agreed?" Ilse asked.

"We're agreed that tomorrow we go under the cathedral." He rubbed his eyes. "I haven't yet worked out how to steal from a vault guarded by the entire realm while also making sure the realm's nine-year-old king doesn't get hurt in the process, but I've done stupider things with worse odds."

"You once smuggled a reliquary disguised as a cheese," Sef offered.

"A wheel of cheese. It was very good camouflage."

"It was eaten, Tamsin. By customs officers. We lost the reliquary to lunch."

"The point is," Tamsin said with dignity, "I remain calm under pressure."

Ilse laughed despite herself — a short, surprised sound — and caught him almost smiling, and looked away quickly, because there was no time for whatever that warmth was, and because she was afraid of how much she wanted there to be.

* * *

She woke in the grey hour before dawn to the sound of weeping.

It was faint, thread-thin, coming through the wall — the kind of crying that tries very hard to be silent and fails in small broken pieces. She lay still, listening, certain it was Marta having a nightmare, already composing the comforting words she'd offer.

Then she realized the sound wasn't coming through the wall at all.

It was coming from everywhere. From the floorboards, from the beams, from the space behind the plaster. From the city itself, lying outside the window like a sleeping animal, and somewhere in its heart, in a palace on a hill above the cathedral, a child was crying in a room full of guards, and the whole city heard it without hearing it, the way a body hears its own heart.

The shrines. She thought of the empty alcoves, the rainwater, the button. The old stones of Carrowmere had stood silent for two hundred years, and tonight, on the eve of the final theft, they had grown thin enough — or the boy's suffering had grown loud enough — that grief was bleeding through the walls of the world.

Ilse rose quietly, dressed, and knelt at the window with her palms against the sill, the way she had knelt at a thousand dead altars, expecting nothing.

"Little king," she whispered, to the dark, to the city, to whoever might hear. "Hold on. Hold on one more day. We're coming."

Far away, in the direction of the palace, the weeping stopped.

And in the silence that followed, clear as a bell struck once, she heard — or dreamed she heard, and would spend the rest of her life unsure which — a child's voice, small and exhausted and terribly polite, answer her:

*"Hurry."*

Downstairs, a door opened. Footsteps on the stairs — Tamsin, she'd have known the rhythm anywhere, awake too, drawn up out of sleep by the same sound. He appeared in her doorway with a lamp, hair wild, face pale.

"You heard it too," she said.

He nodded once. Then: "Get the others. We move tonight — not tomorrow. Whatever they're doing to him, it's already started."
