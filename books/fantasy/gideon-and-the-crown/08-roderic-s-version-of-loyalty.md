## 8. Roderic's Version of Loyalty

The tavern was called the Drowned Bell, which Roderic had always thought was a stupid name for a place in a landlocked town, until he learned that the original bell had been stolen from a flooded chapel and hung above the bar as a trophy by a man who was later drowned in a horse trough. Lowmark's sense of humor had always been like that: buried, slightly mean, and only visible if you knew where to dig.

Roderic knew where to dig. He'd grown up two streets from here.

He sat in the corner with his back to the wall and his face turned toward the door, because some habits outlive the institutions that taught them. The swordspeaker order was fifteen years dead, but the habits were still alive, still doing their work, still keeping his back covered in rooms full of strangers who might be friends of Tamsin Quintana.

Which was the whole problem, really. Every room in Thornfell was potentially full of friends of Tamsin Quintana. The man collected loyalties the way other men collected debts — carelessly, without meaning to, and always on someone else's ledger.

The warrant was folded inside his breastplate, against his heart, which Roderic considered an appropriate place for it. It named Ilse Vane, priestess, heretic, seditionist. It did not name Tamsin Quintana, because officially Tamsin Quintana did not exist. Unofficially, every soldier in Veyra's hunt had been given his description twice, once in words and once by having Roderic point at him.

*Find your oath-brother,* the Regent had said, with that smile she kept in a drawer somewhere and took out for special occasions. *He'll come to you eventually. Men like that can't help themselves.*

She wasn't wrong. That was the worst part. She was never wrong about people; she just used them wrong.

Tamsin came in at half past nine, which told Roderic two things. First, that he'd spotted the watchers at the salt gate hours ago and had spent the evening making sure they saw him go nowhere near the Drowned Bell before doubling back through the tannery yard. Second, that he wanted to talk too. A man running didn't choose a tavern with one exit unless he wanted the conversation more than he wanted the escape.

He looked terrible. That was new. Tamsin had always worn exhaustion the way other men wore a good coat — elegantly, like it had been tailored for him. Tonight it just hung off him. There was something else, too, something in the way he moved through the crowded room, careful of his left side, and Roderic felt a cold finger trace down his spine.

Someone had hurt him. Someone had nearly killed him, maybe. And Roderic hadn't been there, because he'd been the one holding the paper that sent them.

He ordered two cups of the house red, which was less wine than apology and vinegar, and when Tamsin slid into the opposite bench, he pushed one across.

"You look like a man who lost an argument with a landslide," Roderic said.

"I won, actually." Tamsin drank, winced, drank again. "That's what losing an argument with a landslide looks like."

"Congratulations."

"Don't congratulate me yet. I haven't heard why you're here." Tamsin set the cup down and turned it slowly, watching the lamplight swim in it. "You've been in this room since sundown. You ordered food you didn't eat. You're sitting with your sword hand free and your back to the wall, which you learned from Master Corvin, who learned it from a man he later executed for treason, so make of that lineage what you will. You're not here to arrest me, or you'd have brought more than four soldiers, and you wouldn't have let them drink themselves stupid at the Salt Gate inn."

"They're not mine."

"No," Tamsin agreed. "They're hers. Which is worse." He looked up, and for a moment the old humor was there, thin as frost on a window. "So. Ser Roderic of the Crown's Justice. To what do I owe the honor?"

Roderic let the silence sit for a while. Around them the tavern went on being a tavern — dice clattering, someone singing badly about a miller's wife, the drowned bell giving its flat, cracked note whenever the door swung hard enough. He'd missed this. That was the humiliating truth of it. Fifteen years of royal service, of clean barracks and cleaner politics, and what he missed was the smell of bad wine and the sound of Tamsin Quintana turning a sentence over like a coin he suspected was clipped.

"Do you remember the last time we sat across a table like this?" Roderic asked.

"The night before Hallowfen. You told me Master Corvin was going to get us all killed."

"He was."

"And I told you loyalty wasn't a matter of arithmetic."

"It isn't," Roderic said. "I've done the sums since. They don't come out."

Tamsin's hand stopped moving on the cup.

"There were forty-one swordspeakers at Hallowfen," Roderic continued, quietly enough that only their table could hear. "I've carried the number for fifteen years. Forty-one, and then thirty-six after the first week, and then nineteen, and then seven, and then me. Do you know what the crown's histories say happened? One line. 'The order was dissolved following the regrettable events at Hallowfen.' Regrettable. Like a spilled cup. Like weather."

"That's how crowns write," Tamsin said. "You know that better than anyone."

"I know exactly how crowns write." Roderic leaned forward. "Here's how I write it. Master Corvin found out what the regency was doing with the coronation rites. Not all of it — not the name, not then — but enough. Enough to refuse. And when a swordspeaker master refuses the crown, the crown doesn't argue. It arranges. It put us at Hallowfen knowing what was coming down out of the passes, and it counted on our oaths to keep us standing in the gap until the gap closed over us." He paused. "And it worked. Because we were loyal. Because *I* was loyal, and I made sure the ones who wavered stood anyway. I held the line, Tam. I held it so well that there was no one left to ask what the line was for."

The singer had finished. In the quieter room, Roderic could hear his own voice sounding strange to himself, rougher than he intended.

"You want to know my version of loyalty?" he said. "It's this. Everyone I loved died at Hallowfen believing the order mattered, believing the crown would honor the sacrifice, believing that someone would come asking questions afterward. Nobody came. So the only way any of it means anything — the only way those thirty-six deaths are anything but a mistake — is if the realm they died for is worth dying for. That's what I have left, Tamsin. Not the order. Not the brotherhood. Just the belief that it wasn't wasted. And you—" his voice caught, and he hated it, and pushed through— "you walk into my jurisdiction with a heretic on your arm and a smuggler's route through my mountains, and you start telling people the gods are waking and the crown is a theft, and every single thing you say takes the ground out from under the only thing I have left to stand on."

Tamsin was silent for a long moment.

"So this is grief," he said finally. "I thought it was duty. It moves faster than duty."

"It's both. Duty is just grief with a uniform on."

"That's the bleakest thing you've ever said, and I once watched you describe soup as 'wet disappointment.'"

"Wet disappointment is accurate."

"It is," Tamsin conceded, and almost smiled, and the almost was somehow worse than nothing. Then he straightened, and Roderic saw him gather himself the way he used to gather himself before a hard sparring bout — not bracing against pain, but deciding how much of it to allow. "Then let me tell you my version, Roddy. Since we're trading."

"I wish you wouldn't call me that."

"I know. Let me tell you anyway." Tamsin turned the cup once more, then left it alone. "Master Corvin didn't die at Hallowfen believing the crown would honor anything. He died knowing exactly what the crown was. He told me the night before. He said, *the realm is a story the powerful tell so the rest of us stand in orderly lines.* And then he said, *but the gods are real, boy, and real things can be robbed.* That's what the order was for, in the end. Not the crown. Not the realm. The covenant. We were the crown's swords for two hundred years, but underneath that we were the gods' witnesses, and when the crown started robbing the gods, somebody had to notice." He met Roderic's eyes. "Corvin noticed. That's why Hallowfen happened. Not because he refused to serve. Because he refused to be blind."

Roderic sat very still.

He had built fifteen years on a foundation, and he had known for some time that the foundation had cracks in it — small ones, hairline ones, the kind you can live above if you don't jump. But he had never heard it stated plainly, in a tavern, over bad wine, by the one person whose opinion his whole skeleton was secretly arranged around.

"You believe her," he said. "The priestess. You believe the crown is — what she says it is."

"I've seen things, Roddy."

"I've seen things too. I've seen a shrine in the capital that hasn't answered a prayer in two hundred years get dusted every morning by a servant who doesn't know why she bothers. I've seen the Regent pray before executions, and I used to think it was theater." He rubbed his eyes. "Lately I think she prays because she knows something answers. Something that isn't a god."

Tamsin went pale. Actually pale, the color draining out of his face like water out of a cracked jug, and Roderic realized he had just handed his oath-brother a piece of information that Tamsin had been hunting for weeks.

"What does she pray to," Tamsin said. It wasn't a question so much as a man grabbing a rope.

"I don't know. I wasn't supposed to hear. She thinks the old gods are coming back, and she wants—" Roderic stopped. Careful now. The crack in the foundation widened a little, and he felt it, cold air rising out of it. "She wants whatever comes back to arrive *managed*. On her terms. Bound the way it's always been bound. If you take the name to the mountain, you're not freeing anything, Tamsin. You're unbinding something she has spent her whole life keeping in a cage, and she will burn every pass and every village between here and the summit to stop you."

"She'd burn her own realm?"

"She'd call it the realm." Roderic laughed, and it came out bitter. "That's the trick of it. That's the trick I fell for at nineteen. You say *realm*, and everyone hears home. But the realm isn't home. The realm is the cage, and home is what's inside it, and I have spent fifteen years polishing the bars and calling it devotion."

The tavern noise washed around them. Somewhere a dice game ended in shouting and laughter. Tamsin reached across the table — slowly, telegraphing it, the way you move around a wounded animal — and gripped Roderic's forearm, swordspeaker-fashion, thumb finding the old scar on the inside of the wrist.

"Come with us," he said.

And there it was. The offer Roderic had been dreading, the door he'd been keeping his back to all evening. He looked at the grip on his arm, at the hand of the last person alive who had known him before the uniform, and he felt the full weight of everything he'd just confessed settle onto him like snowfall.

"No," he said.

"Roddy—"

"No. Listen to me, because I'm only going to say this once, and then we're enemies again until one of us is dead or the world changes shape." He pulled his arm free, gently. "If I leave now, then Hallowfen meant nothing. Then I grieved for nothing, I served for nothing, and the thirty-six died for a lie I helped hold upright. I can't do that, Tamsin. I'm not strong enough to be wrong on that scale. Maybe you are. Maybe that's the difference between us — you can lose everything and walk uphill. I need what I lost to have *bought* something." He exhaled. "But I'll tell you what I can do. This once."

He took the warrant from his breastplate and laid it on the table between them, face down, like a card he refused to play.

"This once, I report that I found you, that you were alone, that the priestess's trail goes north toward Marrow Ford, which is a lie, and that I lost you in the tannery district, which is true, and which will cost me a fortnight of Veyra's suspicion. This once." He held up one finger. "One. The next time we meet, I draw, and you'd best be faster than you were at nineteen, because I've had practice."

Tamsin looked at the warrant, then at him. "Why?"

"Because you bought me a truth tonight and I don't pay in less than the same coin." Roderic stood, dropped coins on the table for the wine, and then, because he couldn't help himself, added a second handful. "And because somewhere under all this armor there's still a nineteen-year-old who believes you, God rot him. Get him out of my mountains before I find him again."

He was three steps from the table when Tamsin spoke behind him, low.

"The Salt Gate. Two of my people didn't make it out."

Roderic stopped. He hadn't known. He'd read the report — *skirmish, casualties among the fugitives* — and filed it, the way you file things, the way you file everything, until the cabinet is full of drawers you can't open.

"I'm sorry," he said, without turning around.

"I know." A pause. "Roddy. When the world changes shape — and it's going to, soon, sooner than she thinks — I'll come find you. There'll be a place for you on the right side of it."

"There's no right side," Roderic said. "There's just sides you can live on." And he walked out into the cold, past his soldiers snoring at the Salt Gate inn, past the shrine with its two-hundred-year silence, and stood a long while in the empty street looking north toward the mountains, where the peaks wore the moonlight like unsheathed steel.

Behind him, faintly, the Drowned Bell rang as the door swung shut — that flat, cracked, half-dead note.

Except tonight, he could have sworn, it rang clear.

* * *

He wrote the false report by candlelight in the barracks at midnight, and it was good work, detailed and dull, the kind of lie that survives inspection. He signed it. He sealed it. And then he sat looking at the wax, thinking about a nineteen-year-old who had believed in something, and about the drawer where Veyra kept her smile, and about what a man does when he discovers the floor of his life is a lid.

In the morning, a courier arrived from the capital with new orders under the Regent's seal. Roderic broke it open, read it twice, and felt the hair rise on his arms.

The coronation had been moved forward. Eleven days.

And the second paragraph, in Veyra's own hand, gave him instructions that no loyal man could follow and no honest one survive: *Kill the priestess. Kill Quintana. Bring me the name they carry — intact.*

He looked north. Eleven days, and winter coming early off the peaks, and somewhere in the high country a man with a broken rib was walking toward the gods' mountain carrying the most dangerous word in the world.

Roderic began, quietly, to plan his sabotage.
