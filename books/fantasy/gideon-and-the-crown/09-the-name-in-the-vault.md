## 9. The Name in the Vault

Ilse had died once before. She was mostly at peace with it.

The trouble, she had learned, was not the dying. The trouble was the remembering — the way her first life sat inside her like a stone dropped in a well, always there when she leaned over to look, always a little deeper than she expected. And the trouble tonight was that the stone was *pulling*.

"Tell me again why we're doing this the honest way," Tamsin murmured beside her, crouched in the shadow of the palace's east cistern stair. He smelled of lamp oil and the pine resin he chewed instead of smoking, and he was looking at her with that expression he wore whenever his cynicism and his hope were arm-wrestling. "We're smugglers. Well. You're a priestess with a fellowship of smugglers. There are doors in this city that open for coin."

"There are doors in this city that open for coin," Ilse agreed, "and then there is the vault under the coronation hall, which opens for one thing only." She pressed her palm flat against the cold stone of the stairwell wall. "A name."

"A word."

"A word that has been alive for two thousand years and hungry for two hundred. Words like that don't open for coin, Tamsin. They open for their own kind."

Behind them, down the stair, Brenn the mule-driver — promoted, over his strenuous objections, from mule-driver to infiltrator — shifted his considerable weight and whispered, "I want it noted that I left three good animals tied outside a city where they impale smugglers, and I did it on the strength of a dream a priestess had two lifetimes ago."

"Noted," said Sefa, the youngest of them, who had appointed herself the fellowship's official notary of grievances sometime around the Hollow Steps.

"It'll be in the minutes," Brenn said darkly.

Ilse almost smiled. That was the strange mercy of these people: they joked the way other men prayed, constantly, and without much expectation of being answered. She had known them barely a month and already their voices were stitched into her days — Tamsin's dry drawl, Sefa's bright irreverence, Brenn's mournful inventory of everything going wrong. Which made what she was about to do feel less like courage and more like spending money she hadn't earned.

Because the truth was simpler and worse than any plan. She wasn't leading them to the vault because of strategy. She was leading them because something under the palace was calling her by her first name.

Her first *first* name.

* * *

It had started three nights ago, in the safehouse above the dyer's shop, when the dreams changed.

For years her past-life memories had come the way memories do — sideways, unbidden, triggered by smells and songs and the particular angle of winter light. A market in a city that no longer existed. Hands braiding her hair, hands she could almost feel. A voice teaching her prayers in a language the crown had spent two centuries pretending was never spoken.

But three nights ago, the memory had turned around. Had looked back at her.

She had dreamed she stood in a corridor of white stone beneath the earth, and the corridor was singing, and the song knew her. Not her name — Ilse Vane was a name the temple gave a foundling girl, a placeholder, a coat borrowed off a hook. The song knew the name underneath. It sang it the way you'd sing a lullaby to a child you'd raised.

And when she woke, her mouth was full of the taste of candle smoke, and her hands were shaking, and the words were still there, sitting behind her teeth like a secret she'd been keeping even from herself:

*A door beneath the hall of crowns. A stair of nine steps. Speak, and it opens.*

"Tamsin," she'd said to the dark room where he kept watch by the window, because he always took the watch nearest the window and never explained why, "how do you feel about blasphemy?"

"I'm a swordspeaker sworn to a dead god who isn't answering his mail," he'd said. "Blasphemy is my mother tongue."

* * *

Now they descended.

The cistern stair was older than the palace above it; everyone in Lowmark's archives agreed on that much and nothing else. The steps were worn into shallow bowls by feet that had been climbing and descending since before Thornfell had kings. Ilse counted without meaning to, the way you count stairs in a house you grew up in.

At the ninth step, the air changed.

Not colder. Warmer, if anything — a warmth with no source, like standing near a sleeping animal. And underneath the drip of water and the far-off festival noise of the capital preparing for a child's coronation, there was a sound. Faint. Rhythmic. Almost like breathing.

Almost like humming.

Sefa's hand found Ilse's sleeve. "You hear it too? Good. I was worried it was just me and also possibly haunted."

"It's definitely haunted," Brenn said. "I've decided. I've made peace with it."

Tamsin said nothing. He had gone very still in the way Ilse had learned meant the opposite of calm — the stillness of a man listening with his whole body. His hand rested on the hilt of the old sword he carried wrapped in dyer's cloth so it would pass for a bundle of fabric, which fooled no one, least of all gods.

"That's a shrine-song," he said quietly. "Or the corpse of one. We used to sing it before an oath. I haven't heard it since—" He stopped. Recalculated. "I've never heard it."

"You have," Ilse said gently. "Fifteen years ago. Everyone in your order sang it. It's in the reliquary chants. You told me you'd forgotten all of them."

"I lied about forgetting things," Tamsin said. "It's most of my personality."

They went on, down a passage the maps had never held, following a sound like someone humming a lullaby through a wall of centuries.

* * *

The door was exactly as she remembered it, which was the problem.

White stone, though every stone around it was grey. No lock, no hinge, no seam except the outline of the door itself, drawn on the rock as if by a child with chalk. And carved — no, *worn*, worn smooth by ten thousand touches across a thousand years — a single line of script in the old tongue.

She knew what it said. She knew it the way she knew her own heartbeat, which was to say: intimately, constantly, and with a creeping suspicion that she might be mistaken about the whole arrangement.

"What does it say?" Sefa whispered.

"It says *speak, child, and be known*," Ilse said.

"And if we'd rather not be known?" Brenn asked.

"Then we should have stayed home with the mules," said Tamsin.

Ilse stepped forward. Her heart was hammering and her mind was doing that thing it did lately — splitting into two voices, the priestess and the archaeologist of her own soul, each offering commentary on the other. *This is a holy place,* said one. *This is a trap built out of your own nostalgia,* said the other. *You were loved here.* *You were used here.* *Those might be the same thing.*

She spoke the old tongue. Three syllables. The ones the lullaby had taught her.

The door opened the way a flower opens, or an eye — slowly, silently, with the sense of something deciding to.

Warm light spilled out. Not lamplight. The light had a color she had no better word for than *remembered*: gold shot through with green, the gold of harvest and the green of spring thaw, the palette of a god of growing things and kept promises. The god of the old covenant. The god whose name the crown had stolen.

"Oh," said Sefa, in a small voice.

Beyond the door lay a round chamber, and in the center of the chamber, on a plinth of black basalt, sat the crown of Thornfell.

It was smaller than she expected. That was the horror of it — it fit in two cupped hands, a circlet of silver and iron set with river pearls, the kind of thing a museum would label *ceremonial, provincial workmanship*. It looked harmless. It looked like something a child-king might wear to his own crowning while everyone smiled and pretended the weight was nothing.

And it was singing.

The same lullaby. The same melody that had lived in her dreams, in her first life, in the white corridor. But here, at the source, Ilse could finally hear what she had been refusing to hear for three nights:

The crown was singing in *her* voice.

Not similar. Not reminiscent. Hers. The exact timbre of her throat, the little catch she got on the third note of anything, the habit of leaning sharp on rising phrases that her choir-mistress in Lowmark had spent two patient years correcting. Every flaw, every ornament, every private signature of her voice — coming out of a circle of metal on a plinth, in a room under the earth, in a palace full of people who wanted her dead.

The floor tilted. Or she did. Tamsin's hand caught her elbow, solid and warm and real, and she gripped it like a railing in a storm.

"Ilse." His voice came from very far away and very close at once. "Ilse. Look at me."

"I'm looking at me," she said. Her own voice sounded wrong in her ears, doubled somehow, harmonized with something. "Tamsin, that's my voice. That is — I know how that sounds, I sound mad, but that is *my voice*. That's the catch. Nobody else does the catch."

"I believe you." He said it simply, immediately, without the pause most people needed to decide whether believing her was safe. It was one of the things she — no. Careful. One of the things she was fairly sure she liked about him. "Why would it sing in your voice?"

And the answer arrived, and it arrived whole, the way terrible answers do, all at once and fully furnished.

"Because it learned it from me," she said. "From her. My first life. I sang to it." The chamber swam. "I think — Tamsin, I think I was its keeper. Before the theft. Before the crown. Someone had to hold the name between the god and the world, the way a cup holds water. And I sang to it, and it learned me, and then the kings bound it in silver and iron and carried it away, and it has been down here for two hundred years singing my voice to itself in the dark like a bird that only knows one song."

Nobody spoke. Even Brenn, who had opinions about everything, had gone reverently silent.

Then the crown sang a new line.

It was still her voice. But now it was speaking, not singing, and the words were in the old tongue, and Ilse understood them the way you understand your own name called across a crowded room:

*Little cup. Little keeper. You came back. You always said you would.*

The vertigo hit her like a wave over a seawall. Because those were her words. *Her* words — words she remembered saying, in another body, in another century, kneeling before a presence of gold-and-green light, promising, promising, *I will keep you, I will carry you, I will never leave you—*

And the god had believed her. The god had waited. Two hundred years in a box, wearing her voice, waiting for the one person who had promised.

*Tamsin thinks the god abandoned us,* she thought wildly. *He's spent fifteen years grieving a god that walked out on him. But it didn't walk out. It was *taken*. It's been down here the whole time, in the dark, singing my lullaby, waiting for me to keep a promise I made before I was born.*

"Say something," she whispered, and realized she'd said it aloud, and that her knees had stopped consulting her.

Tamsin knelt with her on the cold stone, heedless of his cloak, of the mission, of everything. Up close his eyes were the grey of the mountain passes, and right now they were frightened — not of the crown, she realized. For her.

"Ilse Vane," he said, using her whole name like an anchor-line. "Listen to me. Whatever it is, whatever it remembers, whoever you were — you are not that person. You are the woman who argued with a border guard for an hour to get Brenn's mules better hay. You are the woman who fixed Sefa's boot stitching by firelight and pretended it was magic. That's who's standing in this room. Not a cup. Not a keeper. *You.*"

It was, she thought distantly, the kindest speech anyone had ever made her, and it was also completely beside the point, and she loved him a little for both reasons.

"No," she said softly. "That's the thing, Tamsin. Don't you see? It's not that I was her. It's that I'm not entirely *me*." She pressed her hand against her own sternum, feeling the traitor heartbeat. "Every memory I have of her — I thought they were mine. A gift. A second life layered on the first, like — like a book with writing on both sides of the page. But what if it's not a gift? What if the god put her in me? What if it reached across two hundred years and *planted* her, planted her memories, her promises, her *voice*, in some foundling girl at a Lowmark temple, because it needed a key and it had exactly one shape of key that worked?" Her breath hitched. "What if I'm not a person who remembers a past life? What if I'm a *lock* that thinks it's a person?"

The crown hummed, warm and content, in her voice. *Little cup. You came back.*

"Stop it," Ilse said to it, and her voice cracked down the middle. "Stop singing that. Stop using — that's *mine*. My voice is mine. Whatever else you are, whatever else I am, the catch on the third note is *mine*, I worked for that, Sister Maren trained it out of me and I put it back, it's the only thing I've ever—"

The crown went quiet.

And in the quiet, Ilse felt something worse than the singing: she felt it *listening*. The way the mountain had listened to Tamsin in the Hollow Steps. The way a god listens — totally, patiently, with the whole weight of the sky.

Then, gently, in her voice, it said: *I am sorry. I did not know it hurt you. When they closed the box, you were all I had. I practiced. So I would not forget what kindness sounds like.*

Ilse put her face in her hands.

* * *

They did not take the crown. Tamsin was clear-eyed about it in the way of a man who has thought about theft professionally for fifteen years: the vault would be missed within hours, the coronation moved up, the hunt doubled. They had come to learn, and they had learned more than they could carry.

What they learned was this: the crown held the name, but thinly — a fragment, a sip, the dregs of a theft two centuries long. Each coronation poured more of the god into the metal, and each king wore the remainder like a ring of keys worn smooth. The child-king Aldric, crowned in six weeks, would drink the last of it. After that, the name would be sealed beyond reach forever — not destroyed, which would have been mercy, but *owned*, passed down like a title, a god in escrow.

"We get the name back to the mountain before the crowning," Tamsin said on the climb back up the cistern stair, "or we lose it for good. And we can't steal it, so we have to make the crown give it up. Which means—" He glanced at Ilse, and she saw him choose honesty over comfort, which she was learning was hard for him and which he did anyway. "Which means it has to want to leave. And it will only listen to one voice in this world."

"Mine," Ilse said. "The voice it stole."

"The voice it *kept*," Tamsin corrected quietly. "There's a difference. I've been on the wrong side of it."

They emerged into the night above the dyer's shop, into festival lanterns and the smell of roasting meat, the capital glittering and oblivious. Brenn went immediately to check on the mules' replacements. Sefa began, faithfully, the minutes.

Ilse stood at the window of the safehouse, looking down at the city, and felt the new knowledge settle into her like sediment in water.

She was not entirely her own. Somewhere in her was a promise she hadn't made, a grief she hadn't earned, a voice she shared with a prisoner under the earth. She might be a lock that dreamed it was a woman. She might spend the rest of her life unable to tell which wants were hers and which had been planted, seed by careful seed, by something ancient and lonely and — she was almost sure — kind.

But kind or not, stolen or given, the voice was hers now. And in six weeks, she was going to stand in front of a child-king and sing a god free, or die trying.

"Tamsin," she said, without turning around. "When we leave the city — we'll need a road out that the Regent doesn't know. Something only a smuggler would—"

She stopped.

Below, at the corner of the dyer's lane, a man in plain travel clothes stood talking quietly with the night-watchman, and even at this distance, even after fifteen years, Ilse knew the set of his shoulders. She had watched Tamsin's face go through five kinds of weather when he spoke of his oath-brother. She knew Ser Roderic the way you know a story you haven't finished yet.

And the night-watchman was pointing up. Toward the dyer's shop. Toward them.

"Tamsin," Ilse said. "We have a problem."
