## 10. Betrayal at the Salt Gate

The trouble with trusting people, Tamsin had learned over fifteen years of smuggling, was that it worked. That was the trap of it. You could spend a decade trusting the wrong mule, the wrong bridge, the wrong weather, and pay for it in lost cargo. But trust a person, and when it broke, it broke something in you that no amount of lost cargo ever touched.

He thought about this a great deal in the days that followed, and not at all in the hours before, because in the hours before everything had still been fine.

* * *

They had come down out of the Hollow Steps into the grey hill country north of the Salt Road, nine of them now, and the fellowship had developed the comfortable friction of any small company that has been cold and frightened together for too long. Old Wren, the ex-soldier with the bad knee, had stopped calling Ilse "the priestess" and started calling her "the girl," which she tolerated with the particular grace of someone who remembered being alive in three different centuries. The twins — Ferro and little Maren, who were not twins at all but had decided the title suited them — had taught Tamsin a dice game that was either very simple or a long con, and he still hadn't determined which.

And there was Brack.

Brack had joined them in Lowmark with a mule, a crossbow, and a story about a wife and two daughters in a crown garrison town, waiting on him. He was broad, slow-smiling, unremarkable in every way a man can be unremarkable, which in the smuggling trade was a compliment. He cooked well. He laughed at Ferro's jokes even when they were bad, especially when they were bad. He had once given his own blanket to Maren on a night cold enough to crack stones and pretended in the morning that he hadn't.

Tamsin had liked him. That was the thing he kept returning to, later, turning it over like a coin with a flaw in it. He had *liked* him.

The Salt Gate was a crack in the hills where the old kings had once cut a road through a ridge of white stone, back when Thornfell had kings who did things besides consume their own gods. It was the fastest way into the high country, and the only way that didn't add six days of exposed ridgeline to their journey, and Tamsin had told no one the plan except the fellowship, around a fire, because a fellowship that didn't know the plan was just a queue of people walking in the same direction.

He told them on the last night before the Gate. He remembered that. He remembered the fire, and Wren's snoring, and Ilse sitting beside him with her shoulder almost — not quite — touching his, and he remembered standing up and saying: *We go through the Salt Gate at dawn. Single file. Nobody speaks inside; the stone carries sound like a grudge.*

And Brack had nodded along with everyone else, and poked the fire, and asked a sensible question about the mules.

Tamsin would think about that nod for the rest of his life.

* * *

The dawn came up the colour of old pewter, and the Salt Gate opened before them like the throat of something patient.

White stone on both sides, thirty feet high and closing to a passage barely wide enough for the mules, the walls streaked with mineral weepings that looked, in the half-light, like old tears. The road through was the original kings' road, its paving stones worn to a polish by two hundred years of feet that had stopped coming.

Tamsin went first. Then the mules, led by Ferro. Then Ilse and Maren. Then Wren, limping but game. Then Brack, on the rear, which was where Brack always walked, watchful and quiet, because that was the kind of man he seemed to be.

Halfway through, Tamsin heard the whistle.

It was a small sound. Three notes, high and thin, echoing down off the ridge above the Gate, and it should have meant nothing — a shepherd, a bird, the wind finding a crack in the world. But Tamsin had spent fifteen years listening for the sound of a trap closing, and his whole body understood it before his mind caught up.

"Off the road," he said. "Now. *Now.*"

The crossbow bolts came a heartbeat later.

They came from both rims of the cut, a dozen soldiers on each side, and the white stone that had been a road became a kill-lane with the efficiency of something designed by people who had done this before. The first volley hammered into the paving where they'd been standing. Ferro hauled the mules flat against the eastern wall, screaming at them in the low crooning voice he used for frightened animals, which apparently worked on mules and, Tamsin noted, on Maren, who had gone white and was moving because Ferro's hand was on her collar.

"Brack!" Tamsin shouted, because Brack had the crossbow, because Brack was their rear guard, because that is what you did — you called your people's names in a fight, so they knew they weren't alone.

He found out much later what Brack was doing in that moment. He found out that Brack had put his crossbow down on the paving stones, deliberately, gently, the way you put down something you no longer had any right to carry, and had stepped out into the open lane with his hands up, shouting to the soldiers on the rim: "Not the girl! Not the child! You said — you *said*—"

He found that out later. In the moment, all he knew was that the rear of their column was open, and soldiers were rappelling down the western wall on knotted ropes like spiders coming down threads, and Ilse was pushing Maren behind her with one arm and reaching for the knife at her belt with the other, and a bolt took her through the meat of her left shoulder and spun her against the white stone.

The sound she made was not a scream. It was worse. It was surprise — a small, hurt, *oh* — as though the world had broken a promise she hadn't known she'd been relying on.

Something in Tamsin went very old and very cold.

* * *

He had not truly swordsung in fifteen years. The bridge was still ahead of them; the mountain had not yet asked him for anything. What he did in the Salt Gate was not swordspeaking. It was just a man with a sword and a fury, and it was nearly enough.

He killed the first soldier off the ropes before the man's boots touched stone. The second and third he drove back against the wall with a violence that surprised even him — not clean work, not the neat forms of the old order, but the ugly arithmetic of a narrow place and a longer blade. Wren, bad knee and all, fought like a man settling a personal account with the crown, and between the two of them they held the lane's throat while Ferro got the mules and Maren moving toward the far mouth.

"Go!" Tamsin roared at Ilse. She was on her feet, grey-faced, her left arm hanging wrong, pressing her knife into her right hand because she was Ilse and she would fight with her teeth before she'd be carried.

"I'm going," she said, "you're going, *everyone's* going, *move*—"

They moved. The lane was forty yards of white stone and it took a year to cross. Bolts skipped off the walls around them. Wren took one through the thigh at the halfway mark and did not fall, which Tamsin would think about later too, the way the old soldier simply *declined* to fall, as if falling were for other people.

And Brack —

Brack was standing in the open, hands raised, arguing with the officer on the rim. Tamsin saw him in glimpses, between parries: the big slow smile gone, the face beneath it naked and pleading. *Not the girl. Not the child. You said my family. You said—*

The officer's answer was a gesture, and the gesture was a crossbow, and the bolt took Brack through the chest and put him down on the kings' road like a dropped sack of grain.

Tamsin did not stop. That was the thing he could not forgive himself for, in the nights after. He saw Brack fall and he did not stop, because Ilse was bleeding and Maren was ahead and stopping meant everyone. He killed one more man at the Gate's far mouth — a boy, really, younger than his oath-brothers had been when the order died — and then the white stone was behind them and the high country opened grey and vast and mercifully empty, and they ran until the running was done.

* * *

They lost Wren two miles on.

The bolt had gone deep, and the old soldier had known it the way old soldiers know things, without any priestess needing to say. They stopped in the lee of a boulder field and he made them stop — made it a joke, because Wren made everything a joke. "Sit down before I make it an order, Quintana, I was breaking sergeants like you when you were in swaddling."

Ilse knelt beside him with her shoulder soaked red and did what she could, which was nothing, and they both knew it was nothing, and Wren patted her good hand and told her she had kind hands, "for a girl who's older than my grandmother."

"You could come with us," Maren said, in the small voice of someone who has just learned the world takes people. "We could carry you."

"And slow you down by a day, with the whole crown army behind us?" Wren grinned with bloody teeth. "Girl, I've been a burden to every column I ever marched with. Today I finally get to be a *convenient* burden. Let an old man have his dignity."

He gave Tamsin his sword — a plain, honest blade — and told him to give it to someone who'd use it, "not one of your fancy speaking types, a proper soldier," and then he told Ferro where he'd buried a cache of coin outside Lowmark, "for the twins, and I don't mean you and Maren, I mean the actual twins, my daughters, don't you dare drink it," and then he sent them all away except Tamsin, and what he said to Tamsin then, quietly, was:

"You're angry. Good. Be angry. But be angry at the right man, Quintana."

"The officer on the rim—"

"The officer on the rim was a hammer. Hammers don't aim themselves." Wren's eyes were going glassy and his voice was going soft. "Somebody told the crown which cut we'd take. Somebody who ate our bread. Find him. Not for me. For the next company you trust, so you don't spend the rest of your life flinching at friendly faces."

He died at noon, sitting up against the boulder with his sword across his knees, facing back down the road toward the Salt Gate, and Tamsin closed his eyes for him and could not, for a long moment, remember how to open them.

* * *

They walked through the afternoon and into the dark, because the crown's soldiers would walk too. Ilse would not let anyone carry her, which lasted until the third hour, when her knees simply informed her that the arrangement was over, and then Tamsin carried her, because he was the biggest and because — though he'd have denied it under oath — because he wanted to.

She was feverish by midnight. The bolt had gone through and through, which was lucky, but the shoulder was torn and the road had been long, and by the time they found the shepherd's bothy — a dry-stone hut tucked into a fold of the hills, empty, smelling of old peat smoke — she was shivering and talking a little too much, the way feverish people do.

"You should leave me," she said, as he laid her down on the shepherd's pile of straw blankets. "Logistically. I'm slowing the column. Wren would say—"

"Wren would say shut up and let me work."

"Old men get to say anything they want. That's the whole privilege." She tried to smile and it came out crooked. "Tamsin. Listen. If the fever—"

"It won't."

"If it does. There's something you should know. About the name in the vault. It whispers in a voice you'll recognize, and when you hear it, I need you to remember that I'm *me*. Whatever it sounds like. Whatever it—"

"Ilse." He was cleaning the wound with the last of their spirit, and his hands were steady, which surprised him, because nothing else about him felt steady. "You're going to tell me about the vault yourself. In the morning. With a headache and a complaint about my bedside manner. That's the plan. The whole plan. Everything else is weather."

She looked at him for a long moment. In the dark of the bothy, with the wind worrying the dry-stone walls, her fever-bright eyes held his, and something passed between them that neither of them named — the same something that had been passing between them since Lowmark, patient and unspoken, a debt accruing quietly in a currency neither of them had agreed to.

"All right," she said. "In the morning."

He sat up with her through the fever's worst hours. He fed her willow bark tea and watered the wound and held her when the shivering came, and somewhere in the small hours she slept, truly slept, and Tamsin sat in the dark with his back against the wall and finally let the day land on him.

Brack. He made himself think the name, made himself hold it. *Brack, who gave his blanket to Maren. Brack, who laughed at bad jokes. Brack, who had a wife and two daughters in a garrison town, and who had sold nine people's route to the crown to buy their freedom, and who had died on the kings' road anyway, because the crown does not honor bargains, it only makes them.*

And the worst of it — the part that sat in Tamsin's chest like a swallowed stone — was that he understood. Not forgave. *Understood.* He knew what it was to love people on the far side of a war you couldn't win. He knew what a man would trade. The crown hadn't bought Brack with gold. It had bought him with the oldest coin there was, and Tamsin, carrying the sleeping priestess's weight against his shoulder, knew with sick certainty that if someone had offered him *her* life in exchange for his betrayal, there was no honest answer he could give about what he'd have done.

That was the cost of trust made flesh: not that it breaks, but that it turns out to have been the same flesh all along.

He must have slept, near dawn, because the dream came.

He stood on the gods' mountain, though he had never climbed it. Snow fell upward. And a voice that was not a voice — the old voice, the dead god's voice, the one that had answered the dying boy at the shrine — said to him, with something that in a person he would have called gentleness:

*You gave your bread to a hungry man and he fed your enemies with it. This is what covenants are. This is what they cost. Come up the mountain, swordspeaker. Come, and bring what you love, and I will ask you for one thing of it, and you will give it freely, and you will not know until you stand at the summit what thing I will ask.*

Tamsin woke with the grey light on his face and Ilse's hand — her good one — resting on his wrist.

"You were talking in your sleep," she said. Her voice was hoarse but level; the fever had broken in the night. "It sounded like a prayer."

"It wasn't," Tamsin said. He looked out the bothy's low door at the high country, at the road ahead climbing toward the white shoulders of the gods' mountain, and he thought of Wren facing the Salt Gate with his sword across his knees, and Brack falling on the kings' road, and a voice that promised to take from him one thing he loved.

"It was an answer," he said. "And I think we just ran out of road where it's safe to ignore those."
