## 11. What the Mountain Asks

The bothy sat in a fold of the high country like something the mountain had coughed up and then felt bad about — a low stone hut with a turf roof, an empty byre, and a view of nothing but more mountain pretending to be sky. It belonged, according to the charred mark above the door, to a shepherd named Brannoc who had evidently abandoned it sometime before the invention of hope.

"It's perfect," Ilse said.

"It's a ruin."

"It's a ruin with a roof." She was leaning on his arm as they came through the door, which she would deny later, and which he intended to remember forever. The wound in her side had gone from bleeding to seeping to that ugly pink weep that meant the body was arguing with itself about whether to live. Three days of carrying her uphill had settled the argument in favor of living, mostly.

"Roof leaks," Tamsin said, poking the ceiling with his walking staff. A fine gray drizzle of old thatch sifted down onto them both like the mountain's opinion of their plans.

"Then we'll sleep where it doesn't." She looked around the single room with the brisk cheer of a woman cataloguing assets: hearth, one stool, a straw pallet that smelled of sheep and decades, a shelf holding a clay jug, a chipped bowl, and a mouse skeleton arranged with what Tamsin could only assume was artistic intent. "And there's firewood outside, and a stream, and no soldiers. I've slept in worse chapels."

"You've slept in worse chapels," Tamsin said, "because you're a priestess, and priests are professionally optimistic. I'm a smuggler. We're professionally pessimistic. It's how we stay alive."

"And yet here you are, alive, in a bothy, with me." She lowered herself onto the pallet with a hiss and immediately began rearranging the straw as though she were furnishing a palace. "Your pessimism is clearly decorative."

He went out for wood because it was easier than answering, and because his hands needed something to do that wasn't shaking. Not from fear. From the other thing. The thing he'd been not-naming since the Salt Gate, when she'd gone white and heavy against him and he'd discovered that the sound of her breathing mattered more to him than the sound of his own.

* * *

By evening the fire was going, the wound was cleaned and bound with the last of the priestess's blessed linen — which she insisted still worked even though its god had been dead two centuries, and which Tamsin privately suspected worked because Ilse Vane refused on principle to be infected — and the rain had come down properly, drumming the turf roof like impatient fingers.

She was propped against the wall, wrapped in his spare cloak because hers was ruined, watching him attempt to cook.

"That's burning," she said.

"I'm aware."

"That's *very* burning."

"Barley porridge is supposed to have character."

"It's supposed to have *edibility*." But she took the bowl when he handed it over, and ate, and between bites she watched him with that look she got sometimes — the one that made him feel like a page in a book she'd read before and loved anyway.

"You keep doing that," he said.

"Doing what?"

"Looking at me like you're checking my work against something."

She was quiet long enough that the fire popped twice. Then she said, "I told you about the bridge. What you saw there. The voices."

"The reliquary." He kept his eyes on the pot. "Yes."

"You saw my first life, Tamsin. You stood inside my memory of dying on those steps at Vael Corren, and you haven't said one word about it in four days. You've carried me over a mountain instead. Which is very gallant, but I'm a priestess, and silence is my native language, so I know all its dialects, and yours is the one that means *afraid*."

"I'm not afraid."

"You're terrified," she said gently. "It's all right. So am I. But we're going to die eventually — possibly sooner than planned, given your cooking — and I refuse to arrive at the gods' mountain with this unsaid between us. So. Say it. Whatever it is."

Tamsin set down the spoon. Outside, the rain went on. Somewhere below them, faint as a held breath, the world waited for a coronation.

"When I was in the reliquary," he said, "I heard my brothers. Every swordspeaker who died thinking I'd abandoned them. That was — that was the worst thing that has ever happened to me, and I include the day the order fell." He turned to face her. "But underneath the voices, there was something else. A memory that wasn't mine. A woman on temple steps, holding a sword she didn't know how to use, telling a young man — telling *me*, a man with my face and someone else's name — that she'd wait for him. That whatever he was sworn to do, she'd be there after. And then the arrows came, and she died waiting, and the last thing in her head wasn't fear. It was annoyance. At herself. For not kissing him sooner."

Ilse had gone very still.

"That was you," Tamsin said. "That was you, and I've known it since the bridge, and every day since, I've been trying to work out what a man does with that. Because you're not her. You're Ilse. You're stubborn and funny and you reorganize other people's belongings without asking and you think optimism is a moral position. She was — she was quieter. Sadder. But when you laugh, it's the same laugh. I'd know it anywhere now. I'd know it blindfolded, underwater, dead." His voice did something embarrassing and he let it. "I love you. Both of you. Or — no. That's a coward's arithmetic. I love *you*. The past life just taught me how to recognize it faster than sense would have allowed."

The fire crackled. The rain drummed. Ilse looked at him for a long moment, and then, being Ilse, she said:

"Took you long enough."

"I've been busy. Carrying you. Over mountains."

"Mm." She set aside the bowl and reached out and took his hand, and her grip was weaker than it should have been, and it undid him completely. "For the record: I'm not her either. I remember her the way you remember a house you lived in as a child. The shape of it. Where the light came in the morning. But I'm me, and if you love me, I want it to be the current model, dents and all."

"Dents noted."

"And Tamsin." Her thumb moved across his knuckles. "I love you too. Have since Lowmark, probably, which is mortifying, since at the time you were mostly rude to me in front of mule drivers."

"In my defense, you greeted me with a dead man's name."

"A beloved dead man's name."

"*My* name, technically."

"Yes," she said softly. "Yours. In both lives. That's rather the point, isn't it?"

And then, because neither of them was a fool and both of them were tired and hurt and alive, he leaned across the space between them and kissed her, and it was not the kiss of a memory or a prophecy or a god's plan. It was clumsy and careful of her wound and tasted faintly of burnt barley, and it was the truest thing Tamsin Quintana had done in fifteen years.

* * *

Later — much later, the fire burned to embers, her head on his shoulder, her breathing finally easy — he should have slept like the dead.

Instead he dreamed.

He dreamed of the summit. He knew it was the summit of the gods' mountain although he'd never climbed it; he knew it the way you know your own heartbeat. Snow underfoot that wasn't cold. Sky overhead that was closer than sky should be, close enough to touch, close enough to *answer*. And standing in the snow, or perhaps simply being the snow, being the mountain, being the whole patient weight of the world —

— the god.

It wore no face. It had tried faces, he understood suddenly, in the way you understand things in dreams: it had worn the crown's stolen name like a mask, it had whispered through shrines in borrowed voices, it had even, once, long ago, walked briefly in a priestess's skin because a priestess's soul had brushed against it and left an impression, the way a hand leaves an impression in snow. But its own face, its true one, was simply *attention*. Vast, old, and aimed entirely at him.

*TAM SIN QUINTANA.*

Not spoken. Arrived. Like weather.

"Here," Tamsin said, and was surprised to find his dream-self dropped to one knee, the old reflex of the order rising through fifteen years of cynicism like water through stone. "I'm here."

*YOU CARRY MY NAME HOME.*

"I do. If the road allows."

*THE ROAD ALLOWS. THE ROAD HAS ALWAYS ALLOWED. IT IS MEN WHO BARRICADE IT.* The attention shifted, and he felt the whole of Thornfell in it — the shrines, the silent altars, the child-king in his hollowing crown, the soldiers in the passes, Roderic somewhere in the dark with a warrant and a conscience at war. *BUT HEAR ME NOW, LAST OF MY SPEAKERS. NOTHING IS RETURNED FOR NOTHING. THAT IS NOT CRUELTY. THAT IS BALANCE. THE NAME WAS TAKEN WITH A LIFE'S WEIGHT BOUND TO IT. TWO CENTURIES OF STOLEN PRAYERS. TWO CENTURIES OF MY SILENCE, PAID FOR IN FAITH THAT WENT UNANSWERED.*

"What do you want?" Tamsin asked. "Her? Is that the price?" His hands had become fists in the dream-snow. "You can't have her. I don't care if you're a god. Find another—"

*I DO NOT WANT HER.* Something moved through the attention that might, in a smaller creature, have been amusement. *SHE IS HERS. SHE WAS ALWAYS HERS. EVEN WHEN I WORE HER MEMORY AS A MASK, THE WOMAN UNDERNEATH WAS NEVER MINE TO TAKE. NO, TAM SIN. THE PRICE IS YOURS TO SET. ONE MEMORY. OF YOUR CHOOSING. GIVEN FREELY AT THE SUMMIT, TO WEIGH AGAINST WHAT WAS STOLEN.*

"One memory," Tamsin repeated. "Any memory."

*A MEMORY OF YOUR CHOOSING,* the god agreed, and there was something in the vastness of it, some terrible gentleness, that made him understand the trap before he saw its shape. *CHOOSE WELL. YOU WILL NOT KNOW WHAT YOU HAVE LOST. ONLY THAT SOMETHING IS MISSING. A ROOM IN THE HOUSE OF YOU, EMPTY, AND YOU WILL NEVER REMEMBER WHAT KEPT ITS LIGHT THERE.*

The snow began to fall upward. The attention began to withdraw, folding away like a hand closing.

"Wait," Tamsin said. "Wait — what happens if I choose wrong?"

*YOU CANNOT CHOOSE WRONG,* said the god, almost kindly. *THAT IS WHAT MAKES IT A SACRIFICE.*

And the dream let him go.

* * *

He woke before dawn with the taste of snow in his mouth and Ilse warm against his side, and he lay very still in the dark and did the arithmetic he'd been avoiding since the bridge.

One memory. Of his choosing.

His mother's face — gone already, half-gone, he hadn't seen her clearly in years and losing the rest would hurt but wouldn't kill him. His first oath, spoken at fourteen with a wooden sword and his whole foolish heart. The taste of his master's terrible camp coffee. The names of the dead brothers whose voices he'd finally heard — no, he couldn't give those up, not now, not when he'd only just learned to carry them.

Or.

Or the bothy. The burnt porridge. The confession by firelight. Her thumb moving across his knuckles. The kiss that tasted of barley and truth.

Because here was the thing he understood with terrible clarity, lying in the dark while she breathed beside him: the god had said *a memory of your choosing*, and it had said it gently, and gods that gentle were gods that already knew. It had worn her first life as a mask. It had listened to four days of a man carrying a wounded priestess over a mountain and saying nothing. It knew exactly which room in the house of him held its light.

And it would take it without malice. Without cruelty. With balance. And Tamsin would wake on the mountainside whole, covenant restored, name returned, world saved — and he would greet Ilse Vane politely, the way one greets a stranger, and she would carry everything alone.

"No," he said aloud, very quietly, to the dark.

Beside him, Ilse stirred. "Mm? What?"

"Nothing," he said. "Go back to sleep."

She made a sound that acknowledged this as a lie and chose, mercifully, not to pursue it, and drifted off again. Tamsin lay staring at the smoke-blackened rafters until the window went gray, turning the bargain over and over, looking for a seam, a loophole, a third option.

There wasn't one. There never was. That was what made it a sacrifice.

He was still awake, still turning it, when the sound came — faint, far below, threading up through the rain-washed morning air. Horns. Many horns, spread out along the lower passes, blown not in hunt-cadence but in the long rolling signal-call that meant a cordon. A net, drawing closed.

And beneath the horns, thinner, stranger, carried on wind that shouldn't have reached this high: bells. Dozens of bells, ringing from towns he couldn't see, ringing out of season, ringing the pattern that every priestess in Thornfell would recognize and every smuggler had been taught to dread.

Shrines, waking. Word spreading. And behind the word, riding it like a wolf rides a scent —

Ilse sat bolt upright, hand pressed to her bandaged side, eyes wide and already fully awake. "Tamsin. The bells. What —"

"Hunt's found our trail," he said, and reached for his boots. "And I think the Regent just declared exactly what she intends to do about it."

Outside, the horns sounded again — closer now — and the mountain, patient as ever, waited to see what he would carry up its slopes: the name, the covenant, and the price he had not yet dared to say aloud, even to himself.
