## 12. The Regent's Hunt

The proclamation went up in every market square between the Salt Gate and the high passes, and Ser Roderic Halloway read it three times before the words stopped rearranging themselves into something he could stomach.

*By order of Her Grace Veyra, Regent of Thornfell: the priestess Ilse Vane is declared heretic and enemy of the realm. She is to be taken dead. A bounty of two hundred crowns shall be paid upon proof.*

*Taken dead.* Not captured. Not brought to trial. The clerk who'd drafted it hadn't even bothered with the old formula — *dead or alive* — that had covered a hundred years of warrants for bandits and border reavers. Someone had crossed out *or alive* with a pen stroke as casual as swatting a fly.

"Two hundred crowns," said Corporal Fenwick at his elbow, whistling through the gap in his teeth. "That's a lot of money for one woman."

"It's not for the woman," Roderic said. "It's for what she knows."

Fenwick considered this. "So it's a very expensive secret, then."

"It's an expensive secret," Roderic agreed, "and we're going to spend forty men and most of the autumn trying to buy it."

He said it lightly, because that was what officers did, and because if he said it any other way — if he said it the way it actually sat in his chest, heavy and wrong-shaped, like a stone swallowed sideways — Fenwick would have asked him what was bothering him, and Roderic did not currently possess an answer he could say aloud in front of forty men.

The truth was this: three days ago, in a tavern in Lowmark, he had let Tamsin Quintana walk out the back door. Had stood in the smoke and the noise and watched the space where his oath-brother had been, and had told himself it was strategy. Let him run. A running man leads you to his den.

And the truth beneath that truth, which he had not yet permitted himself to look at directly, was that Tamsin had looked at him across that table with something worse than hatred in his face. He had looked at him with grief. As though Roderic were already dead and didn't know it yet.

*You think loyalty is a place you stand,* Tamsin had said. *I used to think so too. Then I found out whose ground I was standing on.*

Roderic turned from the proclamation board and gave the order to move out.

* * *

The Regent's plan, laid out on the campaign table in her field tent, was beautiful the way a well-made trap is beautiful. Veyra herself presided over it — a small, precise woman with iron-grey hair pinned like a fortification and eyes that weighed everything and paid for nothing. Roderic had served under her for six years and had once believed, sincerely, that she was the finest mind in Thornfell.

"The passes close by first snow," she said, moving wooden markers across the map with a silver letter-opener. "We seal them before they seal themselves. Four columns. The Hollow Steps are the likely route — no other pass gets them to the gods' mountain in time."

"The Hollow Steps don't appear on any survey I've seen, Your Grace," Roderic said.

"No," Veyra said, "because smugglers keep their roads the way misers keep coin. Fortunately, we bought a miser." She set a marker on the eastern approach. "A man named Doss. He sold the route for his family's debts. He'll guide Captain Merrin's column to the mouth of the Steps within the week."

Roderic looked at the marker. A man named Doss. One of Ilse's fellowship, unless the Regent's intelligence was wrong, and it was never wrong; that was rather the point of her. He thought about what a man must be carrying — what weight of love or fear — to sell out people who had slept beside him on cold ground.

"And the priestess?" he asked. "When we find her?"

Veyra did not look up from the map. "Dead, Ser Roderic. The warrant is explicit."

"A trial would serve better. Publicly. It would—"

"A trial would give her a pulpit." Now she looked up, and there was nothing cruel in her face, which somehow made it worse. Cruelty he knew how to answer. This was arithmetic. "She claims the crown is built on theft. That our kings have been feeding on a god for two centuries. Do you understand what happens if she says those words in an open court, Roderic? Half the realm still leaves offerings at the old shrines out of habit. Grandmothers who can't remember why. You do not put that kind of fire in a room full of dry timber and hand out torches. You smother it. Quietly. Thoroughly. And you grieve afterward, if you're the grieving sort."

"Yes, Your Grace."

"You hesitate."

"I was thinking about the boy," Roderic said. "The king. If the priestess is spreading sedition, someone taught it to her. Someone with access. The coronation—"

"The coronation will proceed as planned, and the realm will have a king, and twenty years from now no one will remember there was ever a question." Veyra returned to her markers. "Take your column through the northern approach. Merrin takes the Steps. If your paths cross the priestess—" the letter-opener came down on the map like a small silver executioner "—you know your orders."

Roderic saluted and left the tent, and outside, under a sky the color of old pewter, he stood for a long moment breathing air that tasted faintly of coming snow, and tried to locate, somewhere in himself, the man who had heard those orders and felt nothing but the clean purpose of service.

That man had existed six days ago. Roderic kept checking, the way you check a pocket for a coin you're fairly sure has fallen out.

* * *

They found the first shrine on the third day of the hunt.

It was nothing — a niche in the cliffside above the trail, a worn stone figure no bigger than a loaf of bread, the kind of roadside godling travelers touched for luck without breaking stride. Two centuries of silence, same as all the rest. Roderic had ridden past a hundred of them in his life and never once seen one do anything but sit there being weathered.

But one of his scouts, a farm-boy named Pell, had stopped at it that morning, and — because his little sister was sick at home and because soldiers are superstitious creatures and because it cost nothing — had pressed his palm to the stone and asked it, half-joking, to watch over her.

The stone was warm.

Not sun-warm. They were in shadow when he touched it. Warm like a hand held too long near a fire, and when Pell snatched his fingers back, the little carved figure's weathered face seemed — he swore this later, swearing on everything he owned — seemed to be looking at him. Kindly. The way you look at someone who has just remembered you exist.

Pell reported it because Pell was honest to a fault, and the column had a quiet, uneasy laugh about it, and Roderic laughed too, because that was what officers did.

Then, that evening, Pell's sister — sixty miles away in a village none of them could find on a map — woke from her fever, hungry, asking for soup.

Roderic lay awake in his tent that night doing the arithmetic he had spent six days avoiding.

The priestess claimed the crown had stolen a god's name and starved the others into silence. The crown called her a heretic. Fine. Clean. But the shrines were waking up, and they weren't waking up angry. They were waking up *answering*. A dying boy in the passes, the story went — answered. A sick girl sixty miles from anywhere — answered. Whatever the old covenant had been, whatever Tamsin had spent fifteen years pretending was merely broken, it was not broken anymore. Something was coming home, and it was mending things as it walked.

And the Regent wanted him to kill the woman carrying its message, quietly, thoroughly, and grieve afterward if he was the grieving sort.

"Pell," he said the next morning, falling into step beside the scout. "Your sister. Truly well?"

"Eating like a horse, sir. My mother says the priest at Lowmark can't explain it." Pell grinned. "My mother says a lot of things can't be explained lately."

"They certainly can," Roderic said.

That night, studying his maps by lamplight, he made his first decision as a traitor, though he would not call it that for some time yet. The northern approach forked at a place called Greywether Rise. The western branch was faster. The eastern branch added two days and crossed country so boggy that half his column would spend them cursing and extracting mules.

He ordered the column east.

"We lose time, sir," his lieutenant said.

"We gain sure footing," Roderic said. "Merrin's pushing hard through the Steps. If the priestess doubles back west, we'll want to be positioned to cut her off." Every word of it was plausible. Every word of it was also, in the specific sense that mattered, a lie. He signed the marching order and discovered that betraying your regent felt remarkably similar to filling out paperwork, which was perhaps the most damning thing he learned all autumn.

* * *

The second act of sabotage required more nerve, because it involved Merrin himself.

Captain Merrin caught up with him at a waystation on the fourth day, flushed with success, waving a dispatch. "Found the smuggler's mark at the mouth of the Steps, just where Doss said. We're inside. There's a chapel of some kind up-slope — old, abandoned. Burned offerings, recent tracks. We've got them, Halloway. A week, maybe less."

"Congratulations," Roderic said, and meant only part of it ironically, because Merrin was a decent man doing an ugly job with enthusiasm, and enthusiasm like that deserved at least a nod.

Inside the Steps. Which meant Merrin's thirty men were now threading a single-file pass that Roderic happened to know — because he had been a swordspeaker once, before the order died, before the name became a curse word in polite company — narrowed in three places to gaps barely wide enough for a loaded mule. Places where a few dislodged stones, a few cut ropes, a rockslide arranged with a soldier's understanding of terrain, could close the road behind a column and leave it spending days digging instead of hunting.

"I'd take care in the narrows," Roderic said. "Those passes are old. The swordspeakers built defenses into them, back when they guarded the mountain. Counterweight stones. Some of them may still function."

Merrin frowned. "Function? They're two hundred years old."

"So is the bridge at Cael Ford, and it holds a supply train every week." Roderic shrugged. "The order built things to last. It was rather their whole character. Annoyingly durable men." He had been one of them. The sentence sat strangely in his mouth, sweet and rotten at once.

"I'll post warnings," Merrin said, and wrote it down, and Roderic watched the captain's faith in him — plain, unexamined, the faith of a good soldier in a decorated officer — and felt it go through him like a splinter working toward the heart.

That night he sat by the fire with Fenwick and the others and let them talk, and the talk turned, as it inevitably did, to the priestess.

"They say she remembers another life," said a young trooper named Cass. "A whole other life. Before this one."

"Superstition," said the sergeant.

"My aunt saw her preach at Lowmark," Cass insisted. "Said she talked about the old days like she'd been there. Said she cried for people dead four hundred years like they were her own kin."

"If she remembers lives that aren't hers," the sergeant said, "how does she know which ones are real?"

The fire popped. Somewhere upslope, wind moved through pines with a sound almost like voices.

"She probably doesn't," Roderic said, before he could stop himself.

They all looked at him.

"I mean," he said, and took a slow pull of tea to cover the slip, "that's the horror of it, isn't it? If a person can carry memories that were never planted in her — if she can live a whole life and find out it was borrowed — how would any of us know ours were sound?" He gestured vaguely with the cup. "You'd wake up one morning and realize the floor you've been standing on belongs to somebody else. Everything you built on it — your name, your service, your loyalties — you'd have to ask who those actually belonged to, too."

Silence around the fire. Cass looked troubled. The sergeant looked thoughtful. Fenwick looked at Roderic a moment too long, with the particular attention of a man filing something away.

"Deep thoughts for a march, sir," Fenwick said at last, mildly.

"Long roads breed them," Roderic said. "Get some sleep. We move at dawn."

He lay in his bedroll staring at the stars and understood, with the clarity that comes only at miserable hours, that he had just described himself to his own men without their knowing it. A loyal man discovering his loyalty serves a lie. He had spent his whole adult life standing on the crown's ground and calling it holy because the crown said so. His name, his lands, his restored honor — all of it held by the same hand that had written *taken dead* on a market-square board without troubling to include the alternative.

Tamsin had tried to tell him. In the tavern, with grief in his face. *Whose ground are you standing on, Roddy?*

He had answered like a soldier. He wished, lying under the indifferent stars, that he had answered like a brother.

* * *

On the sixth day, the mountain answered the question for him.

It came as a report from the rear guard, delivered breathless: the shrine at Greywether Rise — the little roadside niche, the loaf-of-bread godling — was gone. Not destroyed. *Gone.* The niche stood empty, swept clean as a hearth, and in the dust before it were tracks. Small ones. Bare human feet, going up the slope, toward the high passes, toward the gods' mountain, in fresh snow. Heading the same direction the priestess had fled.

Someone — something — had picked up its own statue and carried it home.

His men stood around the empty niche in a wide, uneasy semicircle, and nobody said anything for a long time, and the wind came down off the heights carrying a smell like rain on hot stone, though there had been no rain and no heat.

"Well," said Fenwick finally. "That's new."

Roderic looked at the small bare footprints, each one perfect, each one patient, walking up into the clouds, and he thought: *two centuries of silence, and now they're packing their bags.* The gods were not rising in wrath. They were rising the way a family rises when the thief finally leaves the house — quietly, gathering their belongings, taking back what was always theirs.

And the Regent's whole magnificent machine — the columns, the warrants, the arithmetic — was built to stop a heretic, and had no doctrine at all for a homecoming.

"Ser?" Pell said. "Orders?"

Roderic looked north, where the passes climbed into weather, where somewhere ahead a woman carried a stolen name and a man he had loved like a brother carried it beside her, and where a nine-year-old boy waited in a palace to be hollowed out and crowned.

The lie had gotten heavy enough to set down. He found, examining himself one last time, that the pocket where his old loyalty used to live contained nothing but the shape of it, like a coin-rubbing left in paper.

"Change of plans," he said. "We're going to lose Merrin's trail. Thoroughly. And then, gentlemen, we're going to get lost ourselves — so comprehensively that no dispatch from the Regent can find us until spring." He swung onto his horse. "It's remarkable country. Easy to do."

Fenwick grinned slowly. "Lost, sir."

"Catastrophically," Roderic said, and turned his horse north, toward the storm, toward the mountain, toward whatever remained of a friendship he intended — gods willing and passes permitting — to kneel down and mend.
