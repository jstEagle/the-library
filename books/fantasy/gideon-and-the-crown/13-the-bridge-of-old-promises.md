## 13. The Bridge of Old Promises

The gorge announced itself long before they saw it — a low, continuous roar like a giant snoring under the earth, growing louder with every switchback they climbed through the pines.

"Cheerful sound," said Doss, the youngest of the fellowship, a former mule-thief with a face like an apology waiting to happen. "Is that a river or is the mountain digesting someone?"

"River," Tamsin said. "The Greywash. It runs through the gorge all year and freezes partway down in winter, which means the top half falls and the bottom half doesn't, and where they meet there's spray that'll freeze your eyelashes shut by midmorning."

"You've been here before."

"I was stationed here before." He kept his eyes on the trail. "Different life."

Behind him, he felt Ilse's attention settle on his back like a hand. She had gotten good at that lately — saying nothing, but making her silence so pointed it might as well have been a question shouted from a rooftop. He ignored it with the skill of long practice, which was to say, not very well at all.

They came out of the trees at midmorning onto a shelf of bare granite, and there it was.

The bridge spanned the gorge at its narrowest point, maybe forty feet of weathered rope and cedar planking strung between two stone anchor-posts carved — even now, after two hundred years of wind and ice — into the shapes of kneeling figures. Swordspeakers, Tamsin knew. The old order had built these crossings all through the high country, and sworn to keep them: *no traveler turned away, no toll taken, no plank left broken.* It had been one of the smaller vows. Nobody had ever written songs about it. But somebody had come up here every spring for two centuries, before the order died, and checked every knot.

The bridge looked, at present, like something a spider had abandoned out of shame.

Half the planks were gone. The ropes were gray-green with lichen, frayed to whiskers in places. One of the anchor-post figures had lost its head entirely; the other gazed across the gorge with a serene, blank patience that Tamsin found suddenly unbearable.

"Well," said Marta Osk, their hunter, who had a voice like gravel being polite. "That's a no, then."

"There's a ford three days south," offered Fen, the quiet one, the one who'd sold them out at the Salt Gate and been forgiven for reasons Tamsin still wasn't sure he understood. Fen always suggested the safe option. It was almost touching how consistently wrong he expected things to go.

"Three days south is where Veyra's soldiers are," Ilse said. She stood at the edge of the shelf, wind pulling at her gray traveling cloak, looking at the bridge the way a scholar looks at a difficult page. "And behind us, I'd wager, are more of them. We crossed the treeline an hour ago and I haven't heard a single bird since."

She was right about the birds. Tamsin had noticed too, and had been carefully not mentioning it, because mentioning it would have meant discussing what birds going quiet usually meant, which was men with crossbows moving uphill.

"So we fix it," Doss said brightly. "We've got rope. We've got—"

"We don't have two days to re-rig a crossing," Marta said. "And we don't have the hands. That's a master's job, that rigging. Took four swordspeakers a week, back when there were swordspeakers."

Everyone looked at Tamsin.

He hated when they did that. He hated it slightly less than he used to, which was itself an alarming development.

"I'm out of practice," he said.

"You're out of *everything*," Marta said. "Fifteen years. You told us yourself you can't even remember the forms."

"I remember the forms." He heard his own voice come out flat and strange. "I just don't use them."

Ilse came to stand beside him at the edge. Below, the Greywash thundered through its frozen throat, white water and blue ice grinding against each other like old enemies forced to share a bed. Spray rose in columns and hung in the air, glittering, refusing quite to fall.

"The bridge was sworn-guarded," she said quietly. "You told me that on the Hollow Steps. You said the words your order spoke over every crossing."

"*No traveler turned away,*" Tamsin said automatically, and then stopped, because the words had come out in the old cadence, the rising-falling rhythm of swordspeech, without his permission.

Ilse smiled. It was a small smile, and sad around the edges, and it did things to his chest that he had decided not to examine.

"The mountain answered you once already," she said. "On the Steps. You asked for a sign and it gave you one personally, and you've spent every day since pretending that was weather." She nodded at the ruined bridge. "I don't think this is a rope problem, Tamsin. I think it's a remembering problem."

* * *

They made camp on the shelf rather than risk the descent in failing light, which everyone pretended was a tactical decision and nobody believed. Fen built a fire in the lee of the headless anchor-post. Marta took first watch facing back down the trail, because whatever had eaten the birdsong would come from below, not above. Doss tried to teach Fen a dice game and was rebuffed with the gentle, immovable courtesy of a man refusing to lose money he didn't have.

Tamsin sat apart, as he usually did, working a whetstone along a blade he hadn't needed to sharpen in years.

Ilse sat down next to him without asking, which was also usual.

"In the reliquary," she said. "In the ice chapel. When you heard them — your oath-brothers. What did they say to you?"

He kept working the stone along the steel. *Long strokes. Even pressure. The blade does not care about your feelings.*

"They said I left," he said finally. "Not in anger. That was the worst of it. They weren't accusing me. They were just... stating it. Like reporting a casualty count. *Quintana left. Quintana lived.*" He set the whetstone down. "Two hundred of them died holding the passes in the last year of the Purging, and the god went silent, and I put my sword in a river and walked away and became a smuggler, and none of them ever got to do any of those things. And then in that cave they all spoke at once, and what they wanted—" His jaw worked. "What they wanted was for me to say their names. Just that. Just to know they'd been people."

"And did you?"

"I got through sixty-one of them before I couldn't anymore." He looked at her. "You held on to me. In the vision. I'd have drowned in it if you hadn't."

"Yes," Ilse said simply, as though she were confirming the time of day. Then, softer: "I'm not asking you to speak two hundred names tomorrow, Tamsin. I'm asking you to speak one vow. To a bridge. Bridges are much less demanding than the dead."

Despite everything — the cold, the coming soldiers, the hollow feeling under his ribs that had been there since the reliquary and was getting harder to ignore — Tamsin laughed.

"Bridges," he said. "Right. I'll add it to the list of things I never thought a priestess would talk me into."

* * *

Dawn came up gray and hard, and with it came the spray-freeze, exactly as promised, icing the rocks and turning the gorge rim into a lacework of white. The Greywash roared on beneath, indifferent.

Tamsin stood at the near anchor-post with his boots planted on frost-slick granite and his sword drawn for the first time in front of the fellowship, and discovered that fifteen years of avoidance had not, in fact, prepared him at all.

"Talk us through it," Marta said, arms crossed. "If you're going to fall off a bridge, I want to know whether to build a sled or a coffin."

"Charming."

"I'm practical."

He looked at the far post, forty feet away through freezing mist. He looked at the dead ropes. He looked at the kneeling figure beside him, headless, patient, still keeping its watch after two hundred years of no one watching with it.

*No traveler turned away.*

He raised the sword.

He didn't plan what happened next. Later, he would try to describe it to Ilse and fail, because the truth was that the words arrived the way water arrives in a dry channel — not summoned, exactly, but *admitted*, let in through a gate he'd forgotten he was holding shut. The old cadence rose in his chest, in his throat, in the sword itself, and when he opened his mouth, what came out was not his voice alone.

"*Karath venn, karath dor.*" Bridge of the vow, bridge of the road. The syllables rang off the rock walls and came back doubled, tripled, as though the gorge had been waiting two centuries for an excuse to make noise. "*No traveler turned away. No toll taken. No plank left broken. I am the hand that keeps the knot. I am the word that holds the weight.*"

The lichen-covered ropes shivered.

Then they *tightened*.

It happened with a sound like a great inhale — the whole span drawing taut, frayed fibers knitting, whiskered strands rewinding themselves into cord, and along the length of the bridge the missing planks did not reappear so much as become *remembered*, ghost-cedar thickening into real wood grain by grain, frost steaming off them as though the bridge had a body temperature and had just woken up.

"Oh," said Doss, in a small voice.

"*By root and river, by rope and oath,*" Tamsin went on, and his voice was shaking now, because he could feel it — feel *it* — the old power moving through him like a second pulse, vast and patient and *hungry*, hungry the way a long-starved thing is hungry, and it wanted more than a bridge, it wanted names and vows and years, it wanted everything he had withheld from it for fifteen years, and it was taking the bridge as merely a down payment—

"*This crossing stands while I stand. This promise holds while I hold. So swore the order. So swear I.*"

Silence.

The bridge hung across the gorge, whole and dark-wooded and new, swaying gently in the wind like something breathing in its sleep. The kneeling figure beside Tamsin — he could have sworn — seemed less blank now. Less patient. More like a colleague.

His knees buckled. Ilse caught his arm before he went down, and he realized distantly that he was laughing, or crying, or both, breath smoking in the freezing air.

"It remembers me," he managed. "Ilse. It *remembers me*. Fifteen years and it remembered me like I left yesterday."

"Of course it did." Her grip on his arm was steady and warm. "Where else would it have learned to wait?"

* * *

They crossed in pairs, then singly, Marta insisting on protocol and Fen insisting on going last, which everyone allowed because Fen's luck was legendary and mostly bad. The bridge held firm under boot after boot, solid as a tavern floor, humming faintly underfoot like a struck bell fading.

Tamsin went third, after Marta, because he refused to send anyone over a miracle he wouldn't ride himself. Halfway across, with the gorge yawning below and the spray rising around him in frozen constellations, he stopped — just for a moment — and put his palm flat against the rope.

*Thank you,* he thought, not daring to speak it aloud.

The rope pulsed once under his hand, warm as a living thing, and he hurried the rest of the way across before it could ask him for anything else.

Marta had reached the far side and gone straight to covering the exit trail, professional to the marrow. Doss came over grinning like a man who'd been baptized. Ilse crossed with her eyes closed and her face tilted up, and when she stepped onto solid rock she looked at Tamsin with an expression he decided, again, not to examine.

"Half," she said. "We're halfway. The pass beyond the gorge is a day's climb to the high shrine road, and from there—"

"Horses," Marta cut in.

They all turned. She was crouched at the lip of the far shelf, staring back down the trail they'd climbed yesterday, and her voice had gone flat and cold in the way it only did when the news was worst.

"Dust," she said. "A lot of it. Coming fast."

Tamsin turned and looked back across the bridge. Through the thinning morning mist, down among the pines where the birds had gone silent, he could see it now: a column of riders winding up the switchbacks, banners snapping, maybe thirty strong, maybe more, and at their head a banner he knew better than his own name — the crowned tower of House Veyra.

The vanguard. Not following them. *Herding* them. Driving them at the gorge like beaters driving game, and now the game had nowhere left to go except across a bridge that could carry perhaps one person every ten heartbeats.

"Fen," Tamsin said quietly. "Take Ilse. Go now, both of you, don't look back."

"Tamsin—" Ilse started.

"That's not a request, priestess. That's a swordspeaker keeping a vow." He drew his sword again, and this time the old power came up his arm eagerly, hungrily, glad to be aimed at something. "No traveler turned away. Which means no soldier crosses either. Not while I'm standing on this end of it."

Below, faint but carrying across the roar of the gorge, a horn sounded — twice, short, then long.

The bridge began, very softly, to sing.
