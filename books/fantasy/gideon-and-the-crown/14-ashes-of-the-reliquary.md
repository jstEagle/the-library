## 14. Ashes of the Reliquary

Ilse Vane had always believed that grief, properly tended, was a slow thing — a candle burning down through the long hours, giving light as it gave itself away. She had buried a mother, a mentor, and once, in a life she could not entirely claim as her own, an entire family whose names surfaced in her dreams like fish rising to feed.

She learned on the third day after the burning that some griefs are not candles at all. Some are wildfires, and all you can do is stand at the edge of them and feel your eyebrows go.

The smoke was visible from two ridges away.

They had come back for the reliquary. That was the whole of it, the simple and terrible arithmetic of it: Tamsin had left the cave chapel behind them on the road to the gods' mountain because carrying two centuries of swordspeaker memory through a country flooded with soldiers seemed like madness, and he had reasoned — out loud, in that dry voice he used when he was talking himself into something — that no one would find it. The Hollow Steps kept their own secrets. The ice kept its own dead.

Then a shepherd boy had come running down from the high pasture with his eyes wide and his words tumbling, saying soldiers, saying fire, saying *they found the chapel*, and Ilse had watched Tamsin Quintana go very still in the way that mountains go still before they shrug off their snow.

Now they stood on the ridge called the Shepherd's Shoulder, six of what remained of nine, and watched the smoke rise black and greasy against a sky that ought to have been blue.

"They used oil," said Bren, the mule-driver, who had a shepherd's eye for such things and a shepherd's habit of reporting them whether anyone wanted him to or not. "See how it's rolling? Wood smoke's thinner. That's lamp oil, casks of it, hauled up the Steps on their backs." He shook his head with professional disgust. "Hauling oil up the Hollow Steps. Do you know what a misery that climb is? Carrying *oil*?"

"Bren," said Ilse gently.

"I'm just saying, if you're going to commit sacrilege, at least have the decency to be efficient about—"

"*Bren.*"

He subsided. Below them, half-hidden in its fold of rock, the chapel burned.

* * *

She had only been inside it once. That mattered to her now, she found — the smallness of her claim on the place. One evening, one night's shelter, one morning's light coming through the ice-veined crack in the ceiling and falling across the reliquary like a blessing that didn't know it was one.

The reliquary had been a long chest of black oak bound in silver, older than any kingdom, and it had held the bones of the first swordspeakers along with the things they'd carried: oath-blades snapped at the hilt, prayer-cords worn to threads, letters written by dying men to gods who were already going quiet. Tamsin had knelt before it that evening and not touched it, and Ilse had understood even then that the not-touching was the devotion. You did not open your ancestors' wounds to warm your hands.

And now Regent Veyra's soldiers had opened it, or burned it closed, or both, and the smoke that rose from the fold in the mountain was the smoke of every letter those dead men had written and never had answered.

Tamsin hadn't spoken since the shepherd boy arrived. He stood at the ridge's edge with his arms folded, watching, and Ilse watched him instead, because someone had to, and because watching him had become — she admitted this to herself with the wry honesty she reserved for private moments — something her heart did without asking permission first.

His face wasn't doing anything. That was the frightening part. Grief makes most people loud, even the silent ones; it moves their hands, their mouths, their breathing. Tamsin's face had gone still as a pond in winter, and beneath the stillness she could see the whole depth of him, and what she saw there frightened her more than the fire.

"Tamsin," she said.

Nothing.

"Tamsin Quintana."

"Two hundred years," he said. His voice came out level, which was worse than shaking. "Two hundred years we kept that place. My master's master's master swept the snow from its door. Men starved rather than eat the votive bread. And she—" The word *she* carried all of Veyra in it, the regent, the hunt, the warrants, the fires. "She spent a week's oil to erase a room."

"She didn't erase it," Ilse said. "Smoke isn't erasure. Ask any priestess."

That got his eyes to move. They came to her slowly, like a man turning in deep water, and there was something raw in them that he would have hated to know was visible. "What's that supposed to mean?"

"It means I've spent my whole life tending things that exist mostly in memory. Memory is stubborn, Tamsin. It's the most stubborn material we work with. Harder than oak. It burns badly." She stepped closer, close enough that the wind off the burning valley pulled at both their cloaks together. "Veyra thinks she's fighting a religion. She's not. She's fighting remembering, and nobody has ever won that war, not with oil, not with anything."

"You sound very sure."

"I'm a priestess. Certainty is my occupational hazard."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth — not quite a smile, but the place where a smile lived, the way a hearth stays warm after the fire's raked. It was small. It was enough to keep her standing there.

Below them, a section of the chapel roof went in with a soft whump that they felt through the soles of their boots, and the smoke column leaned and thickened, and Tamsin turned back to watch it, and Ilse watched the last of the smile die on him and thought: *no*. Not this time. She had seen men swallow grief whole before; it sat in them like stones and shaped everything they built afterward. She was not going to let this man build his covenant out of swallowed stones.

"Come away from the edge," she said.

"No."

"Tamsin."

"There are things down there I should be looking at." Still that terrible levelness. "Names. I should be reading the names while they burn. Somebody in my order should be looking at them."

"And you think standing here will do them honor?"

"I think turning my back might dishonor them, yes!"

It burst out of him louder than he'd meant, and the fellowship behind them went quiet — Bren frozen mid-fidget, old Marta with her hand stilled on her walking stick, the twins Dara and Sef exchanging the look of siblings who know when to become furniture. Even the wind seemed to hold its breath.

Tamsin closed his eyes. When he spoke again it was barely above the wind. "I heard them, Ilse. In the ice. Every man who died thinking I ran. I heard them, and I told myself I'd carry them, and now—" His jaw worked. "Now they're smoke, and I'm standing on a ridge doing nothing, and it turns out I can't even grieve correctly."

"Oh, my dear fool," Ilse said, and took his arm, and turned him bodily from the edge, because sometimes the gods help those who help themselves and sometimes they send a priestess with good boots and a firm grip. "You're grieving exactly correctly. Badly. In the wrong order. Saying the wrong things. That's how it's done. Anyone who tells you otherwise is selling something."

* * *

They made camp that night in a hollow on the far side of the ridge, out of sight of the smoke, though the smell reached them anyway — woodsmoke and lamp-oil and underneath it something sweeter and older, like incense, like the inside of churches. Nobody suggested moving farther. It felt too much like leaving.

Marta made tea, because Marta made tea the way other people prayed, and the little camp settled around the small fire with the particular hush of people being careful near a wound.

It was Dara, of the twins, who broke it — Dara, who was nineteen and had joined the fellowship in Lowmark mostly, Ilse suspected, because Sef had, and who asked questions with the fearless bluntness of the very young.

"What was in it? Really?"

"Dara," Sef warned.

"No, I want to know. We walked three days out of our way past that chapel and everyone got quiet and respectful and nobody ever actually said what was in the box."

Tamsin looked up from the fire. Ilse braced herself for silence, for the pond-face again. Instead, to her surprise, he said: "Letters."

"Letters?"

"The swordspeakers wrote to the gods. That was part of the oath — you spoke your prayers aloud, that was the speaking part, but at the end of your life you wrote one letter. To whatever god you'd served. Sealed it. Put it in the reliquary unopened." He turned his hands toward the fire. "The idea was that when the covenant was renewed — when the gods woke — the letters would finally be delivered. Read aloud on the mountain. Two hundred years of men and women telling the gods exactly what it cost to keep faith with them."

"And the gods never read them," Dara said.

"Not yet."

"But the chapel's burned. So they never will."

The fire popped. Somewhere up-ridge, a stone clattered down the scree — just the mountain settling, probably, though everyone glanced up anyway.

"No," Tamsin said slowly, and Ilse heard the change in his voice, the way ice hears spring water moving underneath. "No, that's not right either. The letters were copies."

Marta frowned. "Copies?"

"Every swordspeaker wrote their letter twice. One went into the reliquary. One stayed with the order's speaker — the senior oath-keeper — because the founders weren't fools. They knew a single chest in a single cave was one bad winter from oblivion." He was staring into the flames now, and Ilse could see him assembling the thought piece by piece, like a man building a bridge ahead of his own feet. "My master had the second set. When the order broke, he sent them... somewhere. He wouldn't say where. He said, *the day you need them, you'll know where they are*, and I was twenty-two and furious at him and I didn't ask."

"Do you know where?" Ilse asked quietly.

"I think I've known since the ice." He looked up at her then, and the rawness was still there, but it had changed polarity somehow — grief becoming direction, the way ore becomes a blade only under fire. "The bridge. The Bridge of Old Promises. The swordspeakers swore to guard it, and nobody ever knew why, because it goes nowhere. Both ends land in empty high country. Unless they don't. Unless there's a cache on the far side that only an oath-sworn speaker could open, and my master hid the letters there, and the reason the order guarded a useless bridge for two hundred years is that it was never useless."

The camp had gone very still. Even Bren had stopped fidgeting, which Ilse suspected was a historical event worth recording.

"So Veyra burned a copy," Tamsin said. His voice was low and steady and absolutely certain, and Ilse felt her chest ache with something that was pride and love and fear all braided together. "She burned the box. She didn't burn the voices. There is not enough oil in Thornfell."

* * *

Later — much later, when the twins slept back-to-back like puppies and Bren snored in a key that would have frightened wolves — Ilse sat beside Tamsin at the fire's edge, and neither of them spoke for a long time, which was its own kind of conversation by then. They had gotten good at it. She treasured that more than she could safely say.

"You meant it," she said finally. "On the ridge. About finishing it."

"I always meant it."

"No. You meant it the way a man means to repay a debt — dutifully, grimly, because it hangs over him." She turned to look at him, and the firelight made his face flicker between young and old. "Tonight you meant it differently. What changed?"

He was quiet for a while. When he answered, he didn't look at her; he looked at the dark where the mountain was, where the smoke had been, where the bridge waited somewhere in the high country with its useless beautiful span.

"When I was a boy, my master told me what the final vow of a swordspeaker was. Not the letter — the vow you make before it. He said: *when everything you were sworn to protect is taken from you, swear it again anyway. That's the only time the oath is real. Any fool can keep faith in peacetime.*" A short breath, almost a laugh, with no humor in it. "I thought that was just the sort of thing old men said. Tonight I watched two hundred years burn and I understood he was describing today. This exact day. He knew. Maybe he always knew how it would end for us, and he trained me for it anyway, and I've been angry at him for fifteen years for dying, and I think tonight I forgave him."

"That's a lot to get done in one evening."

"He'd have appreciated the efficiency. He was always after me about wasting daylight." Then Tamsin did turn to her, and what was in his face made her breath catch — not the pond-stillness, not the rawness, but something settled and shining underneath both, like a blade finished at last. "So yes. I mean it completely now. Whatever the mountain asks me on that summit — whatever price — I'll pay it. I'll speak the name back to the sky if it kills me, and if it doesn't kill me I'll spend the rest of my life teaching children to do it better than I did."

Ilse felt the words land somewhere below her ribs and stay there, warm and heavy. Because she knew something he didn't. She knew what the mountain had whispered to her in dreams lately, in a voice that wore her own first life like a borrowed coat. She knew the price would not be death. Death would be easy, and the gods of Thornfell, waking hungry and honest after two centuries of theft, were not interested in easy.

She said none of it. There would be time — or there wouldn't, and then it wouldn't matter — and tonight he had given her his whole resolve like a man laying a sword on an altar, and she would not dim it.

Instead she said, "Then we'd better reach that bridge before Veyra figures out there's a second set of letters. She strikes me as thorough."

"She's worse than thorough. She's *organized*." But he smiled as he said it, tired and real, and knocked the ash from his hands. "We move at first light. The high route, past the Shepherd's Shoulder — four days to the bridge if the weather holds."

The weather did not hold.

They were an hour into the morning's climb when a figure came stumbling down the switchbacks above them — alone, on foot, bloodied at the temple, wearing the torn-off tabard of the royal vanguard over a swordspeaker's old training coat. Roderic. Tamsin's oath-brother, Veyra's hound, the man with the warrant and the offer and the warning.

He dropped to one knee at the trail's edge, gasping, and looked up at them with the eyes of a man who has already burned his own life down and is only now smelling the smoke.

"Tamsin," he rasped. "I need to tell you what she's planning. And you're not going to like any of it."
