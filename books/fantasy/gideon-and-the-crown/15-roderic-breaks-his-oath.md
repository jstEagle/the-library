## 15. Roderic Breaks His Oath

The order came at dusk, written in the Regent's own hand, and it was only three lines long.

*The village of Harrowmere has harbored the heretic Vane. Execute one villager in ten as example. Begin with the elders. Ser Roderic to oversee.*

Roderic read it twice by lamplight in his command tent, while outside his soldiers cooked their supper and sang a marching song about a milkmaid with poor judgment. He read it a third time hoping the words would rearrange themselves into something else. They did not.

Three lines. Two hundred years of his family's service to Thornfell, twenty years of sworn brotherhood, every drill ground and winter watch and shared flask of terrible wine — all of it had led here, to three lines in a woman's neat, impatient handwriting.

"Ser?" His lieutenant, Bram, poked his head through the tent flap. He was a big, cheerful man who named his boots. "Supper's on. There's a goat involved."

"I'll be along."

Bram lingered, which he never did. "It's about Harrowmere, isn't it."

"It's about Harrowmere."

"They're shepherds, Ser." Bram said it carefully, the way a man handles something that might bite. "Half of them are older than my mother. The heretic came through in spring, bought bread, healed a child's fever, and left. That's the whole of their treason."

"I know what they did," Roderic said.

"Do you want me to draw up the list?"

Roderic looked at him for a long moment. Bram had been with him eleven years. Bram had once carried Roderic two miles on his back after a fall in the Greymarsh, singing the whole way so Roderic wouldn't pass out from shame before the pain set in.

"No," Roderic said. "I'll draw it up myself."

He sat alone with the lamp until it burned low, and he thought about Tamsin Quintana, whom he had last seen across a tavern table, telling him the truth with a face like an open wound: *the crown is eating your god, Roddy, and you're holding the knife.* He had let Tamsin walk out of that tavern because some part of him — some part he'd been feeding scraps and calling loyalty — had already known it was true.

And now the truth had arrived in writing, with a seal on it, asking him to kill grandmothers.

* * *

Harrowmere at dawn smelled of woodsmoke and sheep and the particular stubborn peace of a place that had decided, generations ago, not to be afraid of anything smaller than a blizzard.

They brought the villagers into the square at swordpoint, forty-one of them, arranged in rows by soldiers who wouldn't quite meet anyone's eyes. An old woman in the front row was knitting. She did not stop knitting when the swords came out. Roderic found himself staring at her hands, at the small blue yarn, at the sheer magnificent indifference of it.

Veyra's captain of the vanguard, a narrow man called Doss, unrolled the warrant and read it aloud in a voice pitched for ceremony. The villagers listened the way people listen to weather.

"The Regent is merciful," Doss concluded. "One in ten. Not more."

"How generous," said the knitting woman, without looking up.

Doss flushed. "Ser Roderic will select the first."

Every eye turned to him. Roderic stood in his armor in the pale morning light and felt, with terrible clarity, the exact weight of the moment — how easily it could still go the old way, how many years of habit were leaning on him like a hand between the shoulder blades. *Draw up the list. Do your duty. Be restored to your name and lands.* Veyra had promised him that much, weeks ago, in a room that smelled of ink and ambition. *Your name cleared, Ser Roderic. Your lands returned. All you must do is what you've always done.*

He drew his sword.

The villagers flinched. Bram went very still. And Roderic walked — slowly, so there could be no mistake about it — past the front row, past the knitting woman, past all forty-one of them, and stopped in front of Captain Doss.

"No," he said.

"Ser Roderic—"

"I said no." His voice carried down the valley. Somewhere a sheep objected to the drama. "I have served this realm since I was fourteen years old. I have held every line I was ever given, and I have never once asked whether the order was convenient or kind, because I believed the realm behind it was worth the cost. I was wrong. The realm is a nine-year-old boy in a palace full of thieves, and the woman holding his leash just ordered me to murder shepherds to frighten a priestess who healed their children." He turned, addressing the whole square now, soldiers and villagers alike. "There will be no executions in Harrowmere. Not today, not ever, not by me."

"You are relieved of command," Doss said, reaching for his own blade. "By authority of—"

"Yes," Roderic agreed. "I expect I am."

What happened next happened fast, the way bad things do. Doss swung; Roderic caught the blow and put him down with the flat, not the edge — a mercy he'd remember later with something like surprise. Then two of Doss's men came at him together, and then Bram was there, and Bram's boots (left boot: *Constance*, right boot: *Griselda*) were doing violence to someone's ribs, and half of Roderic's own company — his company, men he'd trained, men whose weddings he'd attended — chose, in the space of six heartbeats, to stand with him against the rest.

It should have broken his heart. Instead it felt like setting down something heavy he'd forgotten he was carrying.

They fought their way out of the village — seven of them, then five, leaving two bleeding in a doorway with the knitting woman already kneeling beside them, needles finally stilled — and into the high pasture beyond, where the world opened up into grey-green slopes and the thin bright air of the passes. Behind them, Harrowmere's bell began to ring. Not an alarm. Roderic knew the difference. It was the ringing they did for deaths and for miracles, and he supposed this morning qualified as both.

"Bram," he said, when they'd gained the ridge and paused to breathe. "You didn't have to."

"Ser." Bram wiped blood from his mouth and grinned at him, huge and uncomplicated. "I carried you out of the Greymarsh. You don't get to die for the wrong side after that. It'd make my carrying meaningless."

Roderic laughed — a rough, cracked sound, the first in longer than he could measure. "Where will you go?"

"With you, obviously. Wherever that is." Bram hesitated, and for the first time looked uncertain. "Is there somewhere that is?"

Roderic looked north, toward the high country, toward the gods' mountain standing white and remote above the cloud line like a thought too large to finish. Toward Tamsin Quintana, oath-brother, traitor, truth-teller, the man he had spent fifteen years learning to hate because hating him was easier than admitting he'd been right.

"There might be," Roderic said. "God help me. There might be."

* * *

They traveled by night and slept in folds of the hills by day, five soldiers-turned-fugitives moving through country Roderic had patrolled for two decades and now, officially, owned nothing of. On the third night, sheltering in a shepherd's bothy with a fire small enough to hide, Bram sat down beside him and asked the question Roderic had been waiting for.

"Why now, Ser? If you don't mind my asking. You've had doubts before. Everyone has. But you always—" Bram made a gesture like a man closing a gate. "You always closed it again."

Roderic fed a twig to the fire. Outside, wind moved through the rocks with a sound almost like voices, and he thought of the reliquary chapel Tamsin had described, the choir under the ice, the dead brothers still speaking. He had refused to believe it. He had wanted very badly to refuse to believe it.

"When we were young," he said, "in the order, Tamsin and I had a master named Corwin Hale. Best swordspeaker of his generation. He used to say the covenant wasn't a contract, it was a conversation — that the god spoke and we answered, and the answering was the whole point. When the order fell, the crown told us Hale died refusing to serve. A traitor's death. And I built my whole life on top of that story, because the alternative was believing the realm I served had murdered the finest man I ever knew." He watched the fire eat the twig. "Tamsin told me otherwise. In the tavern. Said Hale died loyal, and the crown lied, and everything I've been loyal to ever since was built on the lie. And I called him a traitor and walked out." He smiled without any humor in it. "But I checked, Bram. Quietly. Pulled old records through a friend in the archive. Hale died with his sword drawn facing the palace guard. Facing *inward*, Bram. You don't face inward if you're running."

Bram was quiet a moment. "So the heretic priestess—"

"Isn't a heretic. Or if she is, she's ours." Roderic leaned back against the wall. "The coronation, Bram. I pulled the schedule before we ran. Veyra moved it. It's not months away anymore — it's nineteen days. She's crowning the boy early, before the priestess can reach the mountain, and there's worse. There's a rite attached, an old one, sealed. Whatever they mean to do to that child on that throne, it isn't a crowning. It's a finishing."

"A finishing of what?"

"Of him." Roderic stared into the fire. "Of whatever's left of Aldric that's still Aldric."

The wind rose outside, and somewhere far off — so far it might have been imagination, but none of them moved, and all of them heard it — something in the high passes answered the wind with a sound like a great slow breath, like a mountain remembering it was awake.

Bram looked at Roderic. "So where are we going?"

"To find my oath-brother," Roderic said, and stood, and took up his sword. "And to beg his pardon with whatever dignity I've got left. Which, fair warning, may not be much."

* * *

They found the fellowship on the sixth day, or rather the fellowship found them — Tamsin's smugglers' instincts being what they were, the fugitives woke on the fifth night to find themselves surrounded by shadows with crossbows and one extremely unimpressed mule.

The parley happened by firelight on a shelf of stone above the tree line. Roderic came alone, unarmed, walking the last fifty yards with his hands open at his sides, and stopped when Tamsin stepped into the light.

For a moment neither of them spoke. Fifteen years stood in the space between them, wearing armor.

"You look terrible," Tamsin said at last.

"You always did have the family gift for understatement." Roderic's throat was tight. "I broke my oath, Tam. The real one. The one we swore before either of us knew what we were swearing to. I turned my sword on my own company to keep them from killing shepherds, and I've got four men behind me who'll hang for it, and I have nowhere in this world left to stand except—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "Except wherever you're standing. Which I imagine is inconvenient for you."

Tamsin studied him for a long time. Behind him, a dark-haired priestess watched with an expression Roderic couldn't read — recognition, maybe, or grief, or the strange patience of someone who has seen this moment before in another life.

"Harrowmere," Tamsin said. "We heard the bell."

"One in ten," Roderic said. "Elders first. Written orders." He shook his head. "I spent fifteen years telling myself you were the traitor, Tam. Turns out I just didn't want to look at the ledger."

"No," Tamsin said softly. "Turns out you looked at it late. There's a difference, and it matters." He stepped forward, and extended his hand — the same hand that had clasped Roderic's at their swearing, fifteen years and one lifetime ago. "Nineteen days, Roddy. Tell me you brought more than an apology."

Roderic took the hand, and gripped it, and felt something in his chest come loose that had been locked since the order fell.

"I brought the date," he said. "I brought Veyra's plan — she means to crown him early, and there's a sealed rite, Tam, an old one, and what it does to that boy isn't a crowning. It's a hollowing. She means to finish what the crown started two hundred years ago, and she means to do it in nineteen days." He met his oath-brother's eyes. "And I brought four soldiers who are very good at being trouble, and one of them names his boots."

From the darkness behind them, Bram waved cheerfully.

Tamsin laughed — a short, startled sound, as if the laugh had escaped custody. "Then welcome to the fellowship, Ser Roderic. Gods know somebody has to be respectable."

"Respectable," Roderic repeated, looking around at the smugglers, the mule, the fire, the mountain looming vast and patient above them all. "Yes. I can see we'll be drowning in it."

Above them, the peak caught the first light of dawn and held it, gold and silent, like a door waiting to be opened — and beneath Roderic's feet, faint but unmistakable, the mountain hummed.
