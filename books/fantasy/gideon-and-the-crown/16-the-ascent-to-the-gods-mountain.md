## 16. The Ascent to the Gods' Mountain

The storm found them at the tree line, the way storms always did on the gods' mountain — as if it had been waiting politely at the door and could finally come in.

Ilse Vane pulled her hood lower and told herself, for the third time in an hour, that the cold was only weather. Weather had no opinions. Weather did not reach inside a person and rearrange the furniture.

"Tell me again why we couldn't have done this in summer," called Doss, the smuggler's mule-man who had followed them all the way from the Salt Gate out of what he claimed was debt and everyone knew was loyalty. He was shouting over wind that wanted very much to be the only voice on the mountain.

"Because," Tamsin called back, "in summer the Regent's soldiers can climb too."

"So we chose the season where *we* can barely climb?"

"I chose the season where I can barely climb. You volunteered."

Doss said something unrepeatable about volunteering, and Marta — the quiet one, the one who'd lost her brother at the Salt Gate and hadn't spoken above a whisper since — made a sound that might have been a laugh. Ilse held onto that sound. Small things. She was learning to hold onto small things.

They climbed.

The path was not a path anymore. It had been one once — she knew this the way she knew too many things lately, suddenly and without permission — a pilgrim road worn smooth by ten thousand feet, marked by wayshrines every half-mile, each one carved with the open hand of the covenant. Now it was scree and ice and the occasional stubborn ghost of a stair, and the wayshrines stood like old men forgotten at a wedding, their hands worn faceless.

She stopped at the first one they passed. She couldn't help it. Her fingers found the groove of the carved palm and something in her chest turned over.

*I knelt here,* she thought. *Not me. The other one. The first one.*

That was the trouble with borrowed memories: they didn't feel borrowed. They felt like coming home to a house you'd never seen, and finding your own name scratched into the doorframe.

"Ilse." Tamsin's hand on her shoulder, gloved and steady. "The shrine will still be here when the storm isn't trying to kill us."

"It's not the shrine I'm worried about." She hadn't meant to say it aloud. But the altitude had stripped away her talent for silence somewhere below the clouds.

He looked at her then — that look he'd been giving her more often these last two days, the one that wasn't quite worry and wasn't quite fear but lived in the neighborhood of both. "How bad?"

"Bad enough that you should ask."

* * *

They made camp in the lee of a fallen wayshrine, its stone slab tilted like a book someone had abandoned mid-sentence. Doss got a fire going through sheer stubbornness and muttered profanity, which Ilse privately considered a form of prayer and possibly the most effective kind.

Tamsin sat beside her, close enough that their shoulders touched, and handed her his second glove without being asked. Her hands had gone the color of candle wax. She took the glove and didn't pretend she didn't need it. There was no pretending left between them; there hadn't been since the bothy, since the night they'd finally said the plain true things out loud while the god listened at the window like an eavesdropping aunt.

"The unraveling," he said quietly. "Describe it to me. Precisely. Roderic's reports say the god reclaims its mask as it nears the summit shrine. I want to know what that looks like from the inside, so I know how long we have."

"You want to know if I'll still be me when we get there."

"I want to know how fast you're losing the seams."

Honest man. It was one of the thousand things she loved about him, and she was keeping a list against the dark, though lately she kept forgetting where she'd put the list.

"All right." She flexed her fingers in his glove. "This morning I remembered my mother's kitchen in Lowmark. The blue crockery. The way she sang off-key on feast days. That's mine. Born of Bren and Sefa Vane, apprenticed at twelve, took vows at nineteen. Mine, mine, mine." She counted them off like coins. "And then, an hour later, I reached for my mother's face and found a different kitchen. Stone instead of timber. A woman grinding grain, singing off-key in a language that hasn't been spoken since before Thornfell had kings. And I grieved for her, Tamsin. I grieved for her the way you grieve for your own mother, because for three full heartbeats she *was* my mother, and I couldn't remember which grief was the counterfeit."

Tamsin was silent. In the firelight his face did the thing it did when he was holding something heavy and refusing to drop it on her.

"There's more," she said. "There's worse. Yesterday I called Doss by a name. Old name. A name from the first life. He thought I'd hit my head. Maybe I had. But Tamsin — I'm starting to lose track of which memories are load-bearing. I know I love you. That one holds. But I can't always remember *when* I learned to. Was it the bridge? The cave? Or was it four hundred years ago, in another body, at a wedding neither of us attended?" She laughed, and hated how thin it came out. "I keep waiting for the moment I look at you and feel nothing. Like checking a wound to see if the numbness has spread."

"And?"

"And so far it holds." She leaned her head against his shoulder. "So far, you're the thing that stays."

Above them the storm screamed past the crags like a choir rehearsing for judgment day. Somewhere up there, past the cloud ceiling, was the summit shrine where the covenant had first been sworn — where a god had given its name into mortal keeping, and mortals had spent two centuries proving unworthy of the gift.

"We leave at first light," Tamsin said. "Whatever the weather does."

"The weather," Ilse said, "is the least of our problems, and I include the Regent's vanguard in that assessment."

Doss, from across the fire: "I heard that, priestess, and I resent being ranked below weather."

* * *

First light was theoretical at best. They climbed into a world the color of drowned iron, ropes strung between them, Tamsin leading because he claimed to remember the route from his swordspeaker days and Ilse chose to believe him because the alternative was admitting none of them knew where they were going.

By midmorning the unraveling got worse.

It happened between one step and the next. She was counting her breaths the way the old discipline taught — *four in, seven hold, eight out*, a rhythm older than the crown, older than the theft — and somewhere in the holding she lost the count of her own age.

She knew she was twenty-six. She knew it the way you know a fact written on a card. And underneath the card, rising like water through floorboards, was the knowledge that she was also thirty-one, and fifty-four, and dead at sixty in a plague winter, and nine years old watching her village burn, and —

She sat down in the snow. She didn't decide to. Her legs simply resigned their post.

"—lse. *Ilse.*"

Tamsin was crouched in front of her, snow to his knees, her face in his bare hands — he'd taken off the glove to touch her, which in this cold was either devotion or madness, and with Tamsin it had never been possible to tell the difference.

"What year is it?" he asked.

"Thornfell reckoning, eleven forty-one." Her teeth chattered around it. "Year of the boy-king's coronation. Year of the theft nearly complete."

"What's your mother's name?"

"Sefa. She burns the bread on Sundays and blames the oven." Good. That one held. "Her kitchen is blue. Blue crockery. Timber walls."

"And the other kitchen?"

"Stone." The word came out of her like a stone itself. "Grinding grain. Singing off-key. I don't know her name anymore, Tamsin. I knew it yesterday. I've *had* it for four hundred years and I knew it yesterday and now it's gone, and I'm grieving a woman whose name I can't retrieve, and I don't know if that grief belongs to me or to her or to the god wearing both of us—"

"Breathe." His forehead came down against hers. Wind tore at them both. "Four in. Seven hold. Eight out. You taught me that on the Hollow Steps, do you remember? You said it was older than the crown."

Something steadied in her chest. "You were a terrible student."

"I was an adequate student."

"You asked if the rhythm could be used to time drinking games."

"I was young."

"You were thirty-two."

Behind them, Doss tactfully examined the sky. Marta did not tactfully examine anything; she simply watched Ilse with those grave dark eyes and mouthed, silently, *hold on*. Ilse held on.

But as they rose and climbed on, she understood what the morning had shown her. The god wasn't taking the big things first. It was taking the small ones — names, kitchens, the taste of bread — erasing its mask from the edges inward, the way tide takes a sandcastle. And when it reached the center, when it reclaimed the face it had lent her—

She would not die. That was the horror of it. She would simply stop having ever been.

* * *

They sheltered at noon in the shell of a wayshrine larger than the others, its dome half-collapsed, and it was there that Ilse found the carving.

She'd wandered to the inner wall while the men saw to the ropes, and her lantern caught it: a frieze, ancient, wind-scoured, running the length of the wall. Figures ascending a mountain. And among them, unmistakable even under four hundred years of weather, a woman with a priestess's knot in her hair, walking beside a man with a sword.

She knew them. Of course she knew them. She had *been* her.

"That's us," she said, and her voice came out strange. "Tamsin. Come see what we looked like."

He came. He stood beside her in the cold dome and looked at the two small figures climbing toward a carved summit, and he was quiet for a long time.

"They gave you a sword," he observed finally.

"They gave *you* a lantern."

"Historians will argue about it."

"Tamsin." She touched the woman's carved face. Beneath her fingers the stone was cold, and beneath the cold, faintly, almost imperceptibly, the stone was *warm*. "When we get to the top. When I speak the name back — because it will be me, won't it? The name knows its mask. It will want my mouth." She made herself go on. "When that happens, promise me you won't try to bargain it out of taking me. I know you. I've watched you negotiate with mule-drivers and border guards and one memorable customs official in Lowmark. You'll try. Promise me you'll let the covenant seal clean."

"No."

"*Tamsin.*"

"I said no." He turned to face her, and the lantern light made his eyes fierce. "You don't get to spend yourself buying back the gods and then dictate the terms of my grief in advance. I'm a swordspeaker, Ilse. Bargaining with powers beyond my station is the entire job description."

"This isn't a joke."

"Nothing about this is a joke." His voice cracked, just slightly, on the word *nothing*, and she realized he was terrified — had been terrified since the bothy, since the dream, since the god named its price — and had been carrying it uphill in silence the whole way, the way he carried everything, the way he'd carried the reliquary guilt and the Salt Gate dead and fifteen years of believing himself abandoned. "But I will not stand on that mountain and agree to lose you before I have to. If the god wants a clean bargain, it can take a clean bargain. What it doesn't get is my consent in advance. That's mine to give or withhold, and I withhold it."

She should have argued. She was, after all, the theologian.

Instead she kissed him, there in the ruined shrine under the carved image of their own first climbing, and the warmth in the stone spread, just slightly, like something listening.

* * *

The storm broke at dusk — not ended, but torn open, a seam of violet sky splitting the clouds above the summit ridge. Through the seam they could see it: the shrine. A ring of standing stones crowned with a single broken arch, silvered with ice, exactly as she remembered building it.

Exactly as she remembered *building* it.

She stopped dead on the ridge trail.

"Tamsin. Ask me something. Quickly. Anything."

He turned. "What did you eat for breakfast?"

"Oats. Doss's oats, which were terrible, which I said so." Fine. That was hers. "Ask me something else."

"What's the first life's name? The one you lost this morning."

"I—" She reached for it and found the hole where it had been, a socket with nothing in it, and worse, she could feel the hole *growing*, feel adjacent memories loosening like teeth. "Gone. Still gone. Spreading. Tamsin, it's accelerating. Every hour closer to the shrine, it takes more. By tomorrow—"

"Then we climb tonight."

"In this?" Doss gestured at the storm with both arms, a gesture encompassing wind, sleet, darkness, and existential despair.

"In this," Tamsin said. "Rope length thirty feet. Nobody argues. Ilse in the middle. Move."

And Ilse, checking her own hands in the violet light — hands that had ground grain in a stone kitchen and burned bread in a timber one and would, if the god had its way, soon belong to neither — found that she wasn't afraid.

That frightened her more than anything. Fear was hers. Fear of heights, fear of drowning, fear of the specific sound a rope makes when it parts — all of it hers, earned, personal. If the god took her fear, it was already taking the architecture of her.

She fixed her eyes on Tamsin's back ahead of her on the rope, on the set of his shoulders, on the way he climbed like a man arguing with the mountain and winning on points.

*Hold onto him,* she told herself. *Names go. Kitchens go. Hold onto him.*

They climbed into the seam of light. Behind them the storm closed like a mouth. And somewhere above, in the broken arch of the first shrine, something ancient stirred and began, gently, patiently, to call her home — using, she realized with a lurch, her own voice.

The last thing she heard clearly, before the wind took everything, was Tamsin shouting her name into the dark.

Her name.

One of them, anyway.
