## 17. The Reforging

The storm broke an hour before dawn, which Tamsin took as a good sign. Gods, in his experience, were like mule-drivers: they complained loudest right before they did the work.

The summit shrine was smaller than he had imagined it for fifteen years. A ring of standing stones, each taller than two men, worn smooth on their inner faces where generations of swordspeakers had laid their palms. Snow had drifted between them in long white ribs. At the center stood a shallow basin of black stone, and in the basin — cradled like something sleeping — lay the name.

It didn't look like much. That was the trouble with divine things; they never looked like much until they were looking back at you. It was a sliver of light the color of old honey, no bigger than a knife-blade, and it hummed against the stone with a sound Tamsin felt in his teeth rather than heard. They had carried it up from the vault beneath the palace wrapped in Ilse's scarf, in a box lined with wool from her own flock, because the god had been wearing her first life as its mask and seemed, in whatever way gods have preferences, to find her comforting.

Ilse knelt at the edge of the stones, breath steaming, hands bare despite the cold. She had insisted on that. "You don't bargain with your gloves on," she'd said, and Tamsin hadn't argued. He was learning not to argue with her about sacred things. Mostly.

Behind them, huddled in the lee of the largest stone, waited what remained of the fellowship: Bren the mule-driver, who had come all this way on the promise of a story and a share of whatever smugglers got paid for saving the world; Marta, who had lost her brother at the Salt Gate and walked anyway; and Ser Roderic, sword across his knees, watching the storm-dark slopes below for Veyra's soldiers, who would be climbing even now.

"They'll reach the tree line by midmorning," Roderic said. "Whatever you're going to do, do it before then."

"Thank you," Tamsin said. "I was planning to dawdle."

"You always dawdle. You dawdled through the whole siege of Carren Ford."

"I was *eighteen*, Roddy."

"And I was nineteen, and we nearly died." But there was something almost soft in it. Almost. Fifteen years of grief doesn't melt in a day, but it had thawed enough to show the shape of what was underneath, and the shape was still friendship, worn thin as old coin but recognizable.

Tamsin turned to the basin.

* * *

He had rehearsed this moment on the climb. In the bothy, in the passes, in the frozen chapel where his dead brothers' voices had nearly unmade him — he had rehearsed the words. Swordspeaking wasn't magic exactly, or not only. It was memory made into voice, oath made into weight, and the mountain listened the way an old judge listens: patiently, and without mercy for liars.

So he knew the words. What he hadn't rehearsed was the last thing the god had said to him, three nights ago, in a dream that had felt less like sleep than like being summoned into a cold bright room.

*A balance,* the god had said, in a voice like wind through a shrine, in a voice like Ilse's, in a voice like every oath-brother he had ever buried. *A name returns to the sky, and something of equal weight must rise from the earth to meet it. Not her life.* The god had been very clear about that, and Tamsin had hated how relieved he'd been, and hated more that the god had clearly noticed. *She has already given enough of herself to my hunger. Choose a memory. One. Or many. Weight, not number.*

And Tamsin Quintana, last swordspeaker of a murdered order, had lain awake until dawn doing arithmetic of the soul.

Now he stepped into the ring of stones, and the wind dropped as if a door had closed, and the humming in the basin rose to meet him like a dog that knows its name is about to be called.

Ilse rose too. "Tamsin—"

"Stay at the edge," he said. "Please. If this goes wrong, someone has to tell Bren he was right about everything, and he'll be insufferable."

"He's already insufferable."

"Yes, but he'll be *vindicated*."

That earned him half a smile, the kind she gave him when she was frightened and refusing to say so. He had catalogued her smiles without meaning to. There was the wry one, the tired one, the one she wore when a past-life memory surfaced and she needed to look like a priestess instead of a woman being haunted. And there was this one — small, crooked, brave — that he suspected she saved for moments when she thought she might lose something.

He was going to lose all of them. He knew that now, standing in the ring, and he had known it since the dream, and he had told no one, because some prices you carry alone until the moment comes to pay them.

He put his palms flat on the black stone.

* * *

The name came up through his hands like heat.

Not words at first. Weight. Two centuries of theft compressed into a sliver of honey-colored light, and underneath the theft, older and deeper, the thing itself: a presence vast and patient and *listening*, the way the mountain listened, the way the bridge had remembered him, the way every silent shrine in Thornfell had been not dead but *waiting*, all this time, for someone to speak honestly into the dark.

Tamsin opened his mouth, and the first true word came out of him like blood from a reopened wound.

"*Vaelith.*"

The storm above the summit stopped mid-spiral. Snow hung in the air, unmoving, each flake lit from within by a light that had no source. Below them, far below, he felt rather than saw the land wake — shrines kindling one by one down the valleys like lamps being carried room to room through a dark house.

The second word. He had pried this one out of the royal ledger, out of Ilse's memories, out of the whispering vault beneath the palace where the crown's makers had bound it.

"*Orenda-vel.*"

The light in the basin rose. It rose the way smoke rises, the way grief rises when you finally let it, and it gathered above the stones into a shape that was almost a figure and almost a wind and mostly just *presence*, and every hair on Tamsin's arms stood up, and somewhere behind him Bren whispered a prayer to a god who was, at this exact moment, standing thirty feet away, so that was efficient at least.

"*Third word,*" said the presence, and it used Ilse's voice, and Tamsin's chest clenched, because he had asked it once — *why her? why wear her?* — and it had answered: *because she offered, in a life you never knew, and I forgot to give her back.*

"The third word," Tamsin said, "is mine to give. And then we settle accounts."

*We settle accounts,* the god agreed, and the agreement rolled through the stones and down the mountain and Tamsin felt it pass through Roderic and Marta and Bren like a shiver through a rope.

He spoke the third word. He would not write it here, even in memory; some words are doors, and you don't leave doors open behind you. But the sky heard it. The hanging snow shivered. And the name — the stolen, siphoned, two-centuries-devoured name — tore free of the world entirely and went up, up, a ribbon of honey-light climbing into the broken clouds, and the clouds *opened*, and dawn came down through the gap like a blade being unsheathed.

Then the presence turned to him.

* * *

*Balance,* it said. No wind now. No theater. Just the quiet voice of something very large being very careful, the way you're careful with a child, or a friend, or a debt.

"I know," Tamsin said.

*You dreamed it. You have not spoken it aloud. Speak it.*

"There's a difference between knowing a price and paying it."

*There is,* the god said, with what might have been gentleness. *I have watched mortals confuse the two for ten thousand years. Speak it anyway. Debts named are debts halved.*

Tamsin laughed — a short, cracked sound, surprising even himself. "Did you learn that from a smuggler?"

*I learned it from everyone. You are all the same animal, in the end. You carry things too long and call it strength.*

He looked at Ilse.

She stood at the edge of the stones with her bare hands open at her sides, snow melting on her shoulders, watching him with an expression he had seen only once before — in the ice cave, when the voices of his dead brothers had been pulling him under and she had gripped his wrist and said, *Come back. Whatever they need from you, come back first, and grieve after.* She was watching him the way you watch someone standing at the edge of something, and she didn't know yet — couldn't know — what he was about to choose.

Or maybe she did. Maybe she had done the same arithmetic he had, lying awake in the bothy. Maybe that was why her hands were open. Open the way hands are when they've already let go of something and are waiting for the rest of the world to notice.

"A memory," Tamsin said. "Of my choosing. Equal in weight to the name."

*Yes.*

"And if I refuse?"

*Then the covenant does not seal, and the name falls back into the world, and the crown keeps its hunger, and the boy-king is hollowed on his throne, and your order stays dead, and the shrines go silent again.* A pause, vast and unhurried. *I will not pretend the choice is harder than it is. It is only heavier.*

"That's the most honest thing a god has ever said to me."

*I know. I've been practicing.*

Despite everything — despite the storm-light and the hanging snow and the price sitting in his chest like a swallowed stone — Tamsin grinned. "Bren's going to love you."

*Bren already prays to me. He thinks I don't notice. I notice everything now. It's very strange. I recommend it.*

And that was the moment. That stupid, warm, ridiculous moment — the god making a joke, the dawn pouring through the torn clouds, Ilse's crooked brave smile waiting at the edge of the stones — that was the moment Tamsin understood the shape of the price, truly understood it, the way you understand a fall only once you're falling.

Because the god had said *weight, not number*. And Tamsin had spent three nights taking inventory. The night in Lowmark when she'd greeted him by a name from a life before her own, and the world had tilted. The Hollow Steps, and the mountain answering him personally, and her face when it did — not triumphant, *relieved*, like a woman setting down a load she'd carried alone. The ice cave, her hand on his wrist, her voice cutting through the voices of the dead. The Salt Gate, carrying her bleeding through the high country, counting her breaths because he couldn't count anything else. The bothy, and the plain speaking, and the word *love* finally said out loud between them like a door opening onto a country neither had dared map.

Every memory of Ilse Vane. Their whole brief history. That was the weight. Nothing else he owned came close — not fifteen years of exile, not the order, not the mountains themselves. The scales were not subtle. The scales were not negotiable.

The god had known. Of course the god had known. It had been wearing her first life as a mask; it knew better than anyone what she weighed.

"You could have told me differently," Tamsin said quietly. "You could have lied."

*I do not lie to swordspeakers.* A pause. *Also, you would have caught me. You lie beautifully to everyone else. To yourself you are hopeless.*

"Yeah," he said. "Roddy says the same."

He turned to face her fully.

* * *

"Ilse."

"Tamsin." Her breath fogged. "Your face is doing something. I don't like it."

"My face is fine."

"Your face is doing the thing it does before you volunteer for something terrible. I've known you six months and I've catalogued it. Don't."

"It's a short list of options," he said. "Either I pay, or the crown eats a nine-year-old boy, and my order stays a pile of ash in a burned cave, and every shrine in Thornfell goes back to pretending it's deaf." He tried for the wry tone and mostly missed. "I've done worse bargains for less. I once traded a reliquary for a mule. The mule was a bad mule, Ilse. The mule bit people."

"Tamsin."

"But this one—" His voice caught. He let it. There was no point lying to her; the god had just said he was hopeless at it. "This one costs the only thing I've acquired in fifteen years that I actually wanted to keep."

Her eyes went wide. Then wider. Then they filled, and she didn't wipe them, because her hands were still open, still empty, still holding nothing because they had already given up holding.

"All of them?" she said. Very quietly.

"All of them."

"The bridge?"

"The bridge."

"Lowmark. My name for you. The— the story I told you in the bothy, about my first life, the one nobody else knows—"

"Especially that one." He smiled, and it hurt, and he let it hurt, because the pain was proof the memory had weight, and weight was what the scales required. "You see the problem. I'm trading away the only treasure I have, and I can't even resent it properly, because the alternative is letting a child be hollowed out on a throne to feed a two-hundred-year-old theft."

"You could resent it a little. For balance."

"I'll ask the god for a resentment allowance."

*Tempting,* said the presence. *Denied.*

Ilse crossed the space between them. Nobody stopped her; there was no rule against it; there was perhaps never going to be a rule against it again, and that thought went through Tamsin like cold water. She took his face in her bare, freezing hands.

"Listen to me," she said. "In my first life — the life the god borrowed — I made a bargain too. I gave something up for these mountains, and I never regretted it, and I never got to tell anyone why, and I died with it in my mouth." Her thumbs traced his cheekbones, memorizing. "So I'm telling you now, for both of us: what you're about to do is not a loss. It's a payment. Payments build things. This builds a covenant. It builds a boy's whole life. It builds—" her voice broke and reassembled, "—it builds every honest prayer anyone in Thornfell will ever whisper again. You're not losing me. You're spending me. There's a difference, and the difference is everything."

"When did you get wise?"

"I've lived twice. It helps."

He laughed, wetly. "One condition."

"Name it."

"Somebody tells me. After. When I'm standing on this mountain wondering why a stranger is crying on me. Somebody tells me what I paid and why." He covered her hands with his own. "Don't let it be nothing. Don't let me be a man who lost something and doesn't even get the dignity of knowing what."

Ilse pressed her forehead to his. "I'll tell you every week," she said. "Whether you want to hear it or not. I'll be insufferable. Bren and I will compete."

"That's the spirit."

*It is time,* said the god, gently, the way you say it when the surgeon is ready, when the tide is turning, when the debt has come due and been acknowledged and can wait no longer.

Tamsin kissed her — once, thoroughly, the way you sign a document — and stepped back into the center of the stones, and raised his open hands to the torn-open sky.

"Balance," he said. "Freely chosen. Every memory I hold of Ilse Vane — her face, her voice, her names for me, her stories, the bridge, the bothy, the road, all of it, the whole brief history — weighed against the name returned. Take it. Seal it. Let the covenant stand."

*Spoken and witnessed,* said the god, and the world inhaled.

* * *

It didn't hurt. That was the obscene part. He had braced for tearing, for burning, for the ice-cave pull of the dead — and instead it was like snow melting off a branch. One moment the branch bent with weight; the next it lifted, springing upward, lighter, lighter, unbearably light.

He felt the memories go. He felt each one lift free — Lowmark, the Steps, the cave, the gate, the bothy — and he felt himself reach for them even as they rose, the way you reach for a door closing, and the last thing to leave was not a scene at all but simply the sound of her laugh, unattached to any moment, just the sound itself, rising—

—and then it was gone, and the covenant sealed with a sound like a bell struck inside the earth, and light ran down the standing stones and off the summit and down the mountain in rivers, and far below, in a capital city Tamsin could not currently have found on a map, a crown was about to learn what it felt like to be empty.

The god's presence thinned into weather. Into snowfall. Into the ordinary miracle of a mountain at dawn.

Tamsin Quintana sat down in the snow, because his legs had decided the matter without consulting him, and looked around at the ring of stones, at the strangers — a big mule-driver grinning through tears, a hollow-eyed woman, an exhausted knight — and at a priestess kneeling in front of him, gripping his hands, saying his name over and over.

"Tamsin. Tamsin. Look at me. Do you know me?"

He searched her face. It was a good face. Kind, tired, familiar in the way a song is familiar when you can't place it — a tune he was sure he'd heard, humming at the edge of some room in him that now stood empty, swept clean, its door open onto nothing.

"I'm sorry," he said politely, the way one greets a stranger. "Should I?"
