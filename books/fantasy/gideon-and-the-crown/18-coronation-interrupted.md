## 18. Coronation Interrupted

Roderic Vane had attended exactly one coronation before this one, and he had spent most of it holding a horse.

He'd been eleven, a stable boy at the time, and the crown's procession had passed close enough that he'd seen the old king's face — gray, distant, like a man listening to something far away. Roderic had thought, in the simple arithmetic of childhood, *that is what majesty looks like.* He had built a life on that arithmetic. He had buried friends for it. He had, three days ago, put his sword to the throats of his own company for it, and run into the mountains bleeding from two places he preferred not to name.

So he stood now in the Great Basilica of Thornfell, in a borrowed guard's tabern over his ruined armor, and waited to learn whether the sum of everything had been worth carrying.

The basilica was full to its vaulted throat. Every banner house, every bishop, every merchant guild master who could rent a decent doublet. Incense rolled down from the high thuribles in silver rivers. And there on the dais, small and straight-backed and terribly alone, sat Aldric — nine years old, crowned only with a circlet until the moment came, his hands folded in his lap the way children fold them when they've been drilled for weeks and are terrified of getting it wrong.

Regent Veyra stood beside him in cloth-of-gold, and she was not looking at the boy at all. She was looking at the crown.

It rested on a cushion of black velvet on a table of white marble, between them. The Crown of Thornfell. Two centuries of kings had worn it, and Roderic had worn a swordspeaker's oath beside men who died saying what it was made of, and he had not believed them. He believed them now. He could feel it from thirty feet away — a wrongness like a toothache in the air, like a word being held underwater, thrashing.

*Hold still,* he told himself. *Do your one job.*

His one job was simple. When the covenant broke — if it broke, when it broke — the guards would panic, the bishops would scream heresy, and Veyra would move. She would declare the realm under spiritual attack, seal the basilica, and vanish into the palace with the boy and whatever fragment of the name remained. Roderic's job was to be standing next to her when that happened, wearing a face she trusted, and to take her wrist before she finished her first sentence.

He was very good at taking wrists. It was, he reflected, essentially the whole of his career.

The High Cantor began the Litany of Unbroken Rule. The congregation knelt in waves, like wind through wheat. Roderic did not kneel. Guards didn't kneel during ceremony; everyone knew that; no one looked twice at him. He used the privacy of standing to scan the gallery where the foreign envoys sat, then the west door, then the great rose window above it, through which the morning light came down in reds and golds.

Somewhere beyond those windows, up in the high country where no army could follow, a man who had once shared his bread and his bedroll and his blood-oath was climbing a mountain with a stolen god's true name in his keeping.

*You'd better have made it, Tam,* he thought. *Because if you didn't, I have done something today that cannot be undone, and I would very much like it to have been for something.*

The Litany reached its third verse. The boy-king rose. Veyra lifted the crown from its cushion.

And the candles went out.

Not all of them. That was the thing Roderic would remember afterward, the detail he would tell in taverns for the rest of his life: not all of them. Only the tall ceremonial tapers around the dais — twelve flames snuffed in the same instant, without smoke, without wind, as if breathed out by something patient and enormous. The light through the rose window remained, red and gold, and by that stained-glass glow the whole assembly watched Veyra freeze with the crown half-raised.

She recovered quickly. Too quickly. "A draft," she announced, to four thousand people. "Continue."

The Cantor continued. His voice shook only a little.

Veyra lowered the crown toward the boy's head.

The bells rang.

There were no bells scheduled. Roderic knew the coronation rite better than he knew his own family's names, and there were no bells at this point in it, and yet every bell in the Basilica of Thornfell — and, he would later learn, every bell in the city, and in Lowmark, and in shepherd villages so small they owned only one goat and one bell — began to ring at once. Not the clanging of alarm. Something slower. Deeper. A rhythm like breathing, like tide, like a heartbeat heard through a wall.

The sound went through Roderic's chest and rearranged something in it.

The crown touched Aldric's hair.

The boy screamed.

It was not the scream of a child frightened by noise. Roderic had heard that scream, on battlefields, in burning houses. This was different. This was the scream of a man waking from twenty years of sleep to find hands around his throat — except the hands were already gone, and the sleeper hadn't been told, and for one terrible moment he couldn't tell whether he was free or still held.

Aldric tore the crown off his own head and threw it.

It struck the marble steps and rang — actually *rang*, a clear bell-note, and then cracked. A single line ran across it, fine as a hair, and from the crack poured light. Not firelight, not lamplight. Light like the last hour of a summer evening, gold going green at the edges, and it moved through the basilica like water finding its level, running down the steps, along the flagstones, up the pillars, touching everything.

Where it touched, things became honest.

Roderic saw it happen to other people first. A bishop whose robes were richer than canon law allowed suddenly seemed to wear them like an apology. A guild master who had spent the litany calculating tariffs flinched as though someone had read his ledger aloud. Lies, all the small daily lies, the mortar of every court in the world — the light found each one and made it visible, briefly, the way frost makes breath visible.

Then it touched him, and Roderic learned what it was to be seen by something two hundred years defrauded.

It didn't hurt. That was the astonishing part. It simply *knew* him — the stable boy, the oath-brother, the man who had chosen a warrant over a friendship, the man who three days ago had turned his sword on Captain Dree and her company rather than burn a village — and it weighed all of it together, and what Roderic felt pouring back into him was not judgment.

It was grief. Old, patient, enormous grief, the grief of a thing that had been eaten slowly and had never stopped loving the eaters.

Roderic wept. He wasn't the only one. Half the basilica was on its feet, and half of those were crying, and nobody was ashamed of it, because the light was still moving among them and shame had nowhere to stand.

On the dais, Regent Veyra drew a knife.

Later, everyone agreed on this. What they disagreed on was what she intended — to strike the boy, to strike herself, to cut her own palm and speak some counter-word her husband had taught her before the fever took him. It didn't matter. Because Roderic was already moving, had been moving since the crown cracked, thirty feet of marble between him and the regency of Thornfell, and he arrived with his hand closing around her wrist exactly as planned, exactly as rehearsed in his head for three sleepless days.

Except her wrist turned in his grip like a snake, and the knife came around for his throat instead, and what saved him was not skill but the light — because the whole basilica saw it, saw the Regent of Thornfell try to open a guardsman's neck in front of the gods' own returning, and there was no unseeing that, no explaining it, no version of events in which she walked away.

"Ser Roderic," she said. Quietly. Just for him. Her eyes were wild and, horribly, lucid. "You don't understand what they'll do. Without the name, the borders—"

"I understand exactly what you did," Roderic said. "I've understood since the chapel burned. I just didn't want it to be true."

He took the knife. He took her other wrist, because he was thorough. And he turned to face four thousand people with the Regent of Thornfell held like a misbehaving child, and heard his own voice come out steady, which surprised him, because inside he felt like the bells sounded.

"In the name of the covenant reforged," he said, "and by witness of every soul in this hall — I arrest Veyra, Regent of Thornfell, for the theft of a divine name, for the feeding of the royal line upon it for two hundred years, and for the murder of the Order of Swordspeakers."

The silence lasted three heartbeats.

Then the basilica broke like a dam.

* * *

Afterward — after the soldiers came, after Veyra was taken away still speaking in that calm lucid voice about borders and necessity, after the bishops fell over each other declaring that they had always known, always, that the old gods were not dead but sleeping, praise be, how faithful they had been — Roderic found himself on the basilica steps with the boy-king.

They sat on the cold stone while the city roared around them. Somewhere a mob was pulling down Veyra's banners; somewhere else, someone had already started selling roasted chestnuts, because commerce recognizes opportunity faster than any god. Aldric had the broken crown in his lap. He was turning it over, studying the crack.

"It doesn't feel like anything now," the boy said. "Before, when I touched it, it felt like being full of somebody else's thoughts. Like having a guest who never left." He looked up. He had his mother's eyes, people said. Roderic wouldn't know. "Was I bad? While it was in me?"

Roderic considered lying, discovered the light was apparently still working on him, and said, "No. You were a child wearing a crown full of a stolen god. You slept badly and you liked eel pie and you once gave your dessert to a scullery maid whose father had died. I checked. I check on things."

"You're the traitor," Aldric said. Not accusing. Curious. "Everyone's saying it. Ser Roderic the Traitor, who arrested the Regent."

"They're also saying Ser Roderic the Hero. Crowds can't make up their minds. Give them a week." He paused. "Which do you think?"

The boy thought about it with the terrifying seriousness of nine-year-olds. "I think you did the right thing in a way that got people hurt, and you know it, and you're going to carry it." He shrugged. "My tutor says that's most right things."

Roderic laughed — a rough, startled bark that hurt his ribs, two of which were probably cracked. "Your tutor sounds smarter than my entire order was."

"What happens to me now?" Aldric asked. Quieter. The real question, under the question.

"Now? Now there will be councils, and arguments, and men with beards explaining precedent to you until your ears bleed. And eventually, if you want it, a coronation with a crown that isn't stolen. Made new. Of plain gold and nothing else." Roderic looked out at the city, at the thin threads of woodsmoke rising straight in the still air, at the way the light lay on the rooftops — ordinary light, honest light, and yet everything seemed slightly more *present* than it had yesterday, as if the world had been viewed through gauze for two centuries and someone had finally wiped it clean. "But that's years away, and you'll be older, and you'll choose it yourself. Nobody will put anything on your head that you haven't agreed to. That's the whole point of what happened today."

Aldric nodded slowly. Then, with the gravity of a king issuing a decree: "Can the new crown have jewels? Just regular ones. Not god ones."

"Regular jewels," Roderic agreed solemnly. "I'll put it in the council minutes myself."

* * *

He left the capital two days later, officially to escort Veyra's papers to the archives at Lowmark, unofficially because the city had begun to feel like a bell that wouldn't stop ringing, and because there was a question he could not ask anyone at court.

The question was this: the covenant had reforged. The light had come down the mountain like an avalanche of evening. But no one had seen the man who carried the name. No one had seen Ilse Vane, or the smuggler roads, or the fellowship that had gone up into the storm with half its members dead. The mountain had answered, and mountains do not send word of their messengers.

In Lowmark, the rebuilt chapel smelled of fresh-cut timber and pine pitch. Ilse wasn't there — she was up in the high country, the acolytes said, tending shrines, walking the passes, doing whatever it was the last priestess of a returned god did now that the god was home. But one of the old women who kept the candles told him, in the tone of someone sharing excellent gossip, that a swordspeaker had been seen at the shepherd bothies below the summit, alive, whole, and — here she leaned in, delighted —

"— strange, poor lamb. Doesn't remember a blessed thing about the priestess. Looks right through her like she's a stranger asking directions. Can you imagine? After all they went through, the stories she tells—"

Roderic stood very still among the candle racks.

*He offered something,* he thought. He didn't know how he knew; he knew the way he now knew other things, the way the whole world knew things, faintly, like weather coming. *He carried the name up that mountain and the god took its price, and the price was her. All of her. Everything she was to him.*

He walked out of the chapel into the bright cold morning, an old traitor with cracked ribs and a warrant that no longer meant anything, and started walking toward the mountains.

Somebody had to help carry what was left. It was, he reflected, essentially the whole of his career.
