## 19. A Stranger's Face

Ilse Vane had rehearsed this moment for three days.

She had rehearsed it on the descent from the summit, when the storm finally broke and the sky turned the color of a rinsed-out wound. She had rehearsed it in the shepherd's bothy where they'd stopped to bind Tamsin's frostbitten fingers. She had rehearsed it walking the last mile of the mountain road, composing sentences the way other people composed prayers — carefully, desperately, and with no real expectation of being answered.

*He will not know you,* she told herself. *That is the price he paid. He knew it when he paid it. You must not make him carry your grief as well as his own.*

And then she crested the ridge above the high shrine road and saw him sitting on a flat stone in the morning sun, eating an apple, and every sentence she had composed flew out of her head like startled birds.

He looked *well*. That was the first cruel thing. He looked rested in a way she had never seen him look — not once in all their weeks of flight, not once in all the years before that, if the stories were true. The lines around his eyes had softened. His shoulders sat easy. He was watching a marmot argue with a boulder with the mild interest of a man who had nowhere in the world to be, and when the marmot won, whatever argument marmots could possibly have with rocks, Tamsin Quintana smiled.

Ilse stood very still on the ridge and let herself look at that smile for exactly as long as it took to break her own heart cleanly, the way you break a wishbone: one motion, both hands, no lingering.

Then she walked down to meet her beloved for the first time.

* * *

"You're awake," she said, which was not any of her prepared openings, and also made no sense, because obviously he was awake; he was eating an apple.

"I am," he agreed pleasantly. "Have been since dawn. Are you with the shrine? I don't remember a keeper being assigned up here."

The question landed in her chest like a dropped stone. *I don't remember.* Of course he didn't. That was rather the entire point. But hearing it said so easily, so *politely*, was different from knowing it was coming. It was the difference between reading about drowning and being underwater.

"No," she managed. "Not the shrine. I'm — I came looking for you. We were told a man had come down off the gods' mountain alive, and I wanted to see for myself."

"Alive is generous." He gestured at himself with the apple core. "I'm mostly bruise. I woke up up there—" he nodded back toward the summit, white against the sky "—with frost in my beard and no idea how I got there. Which is either a miracle or a very long night I should be embarrassed about."

"It's the first one," Ilse said quietly.

His eyebrows rose, amused, waiting for the joke to land. When it didn't, his expression shifted into something more careful. "You know something about it."

"Yes."

"Will you tell me?"

She sat down on the stone beside him — not too close; she had measured that distance too, three days of measuring — and looked out at the valley below, where the first honest shrines in two centuries were lighting their lamps again. Somewhere down there, in Lowmark, the chapel bell was ringing for morning prayers, and people were actually coming, and the god was actually listening, and none of it would have happened without the man beside her who did not know her name.

"There was a covenant," she began. "Between Thornfell and its gods. Two hundred years ago the crown stole a divine name and bound it into the coronation crown, and every prayer since has fallen into silence, and the old orders died — the swordspeakers, the keepers, all of them. And there was a man sworn to the last of the dead gods who found out what the crown was made of, and carried the stolen name back up the mountain through storm and soldiers and a burning reliquary, and gave it back."

Tamsin had gone very still. Not the stillness of disbelief — she knew that stillness, the flinch of a cynic bracing for nonsense. This was the stillness of a man listening to a story that fit him like a coat.

"And the price," he said slowly.

"The covenant demanded balance. A memory, freely given, of the giver's choosing." Her voice held. She had practiced that too. "He chose to give all of them. Every memory of a woman he loved. Their whole history, start to finish, so that nothing else of his would be owed."

The apple core sat forgotten in his hand. He was staring at the summit now, and his jaw worked once, twice.

"That's a stupid bargain," he said at last, roughly. "Whoever he was. A whole history. For — what? A name in the sky?"

"For everyone," Ilse said. "For every dying child whose prayer gets answered now instead of falling into the dark. For the boy-king, who woke up during his own coronation and found himself alone inside his own head for the first time in his life. For the shrines." She nodded down at the valley lights. "For all of it."

"He couldn't have kept some of them? One or two? Bargain a little?" There was anger in it, sudden and fierce, and Ilse had to close her eyes briefly, because that anger was *his*, that exact flavor of stubborn indignation on behalf of other people, and he was aiming it at himself without knowing it.

"He bargained everything else away first," she said. "There was nothing left but her."

Silence. Wind moved through the high grass. Down in the valley, the bell finished ringing.

"What was her name?" Tamsin asked.

And there it was. The moment she had rehearsed for three days, arrived at last, and all her careful sentences scattered again. She had planned to say it lightly. She had planned to say it with dignity, with a small smile, with grace. What came out instead was barely more than breath:

"Ilse."

"Ilse," he repeated. He said it carefully, the way you handle something borrowed. Then he turned to her fully for the first time, and offered his hand, and said, "Tamsin. Tamsin Quintana. Though I suppose you knew that already."

She took his hand. His grip was warm and calloused and entirely unburdened by recognition, and she held it perhaps half a heartbeat longer than a stranger would, and he noticed — she saw him notice — and said nothing, because he was kind even when he wasn't hers.

"Thank you for coming to find me," he said. "Whoever sent you."

"No one sent me," said Ilse Vane, priestess of a waking god, keeper of a history only she could carry. "I volunteered."

* * *

They walked down together, because he was going down and she was going wherever he went, which was a sentence she kept firmly inside her skull.

It was strange, learning him again from the outside. She had spent weeks inside his habits — knew how he took his tea (barely tea at all, mostly hot water with regret), knew that he talked to mules more fluently than to people, knew that he counted his steps on bad days without realizing he did it. Now she watched him discover these things about himself in front of her, fresh, and it was like watching someone read aloud from a book she'd written.

At the first switchback they met a mule train coming up — old Doss and his string, hauling offerings to the newly lit summit shrine. Doss took one look at Tamsin and nearly fell off his own animal.

"By the nine silent saints — *Quintana!* You're alive! We thought — after the storm, after the soldiers, we thought—" He dismounted, grabbed Tamsin's arm, shook it. "You beautiful stubborn wreck of a man!"

"Hello," said Tamsin, with the cautious warmth of a man greeting someone he felt he ought to know. "I'm sorry — have we—"

Doss stopped shaking his arm. His face did something complicated. "You don't—"

"He doesn't," Ilse said softly. "He gave a great deal on that mountain, Doss. More than most. Be glad to see him anyway."

Doss looked at her. Doss looked at Tamsin. Something passed over the old mule-driver's face — grief, and then a decision, and then a grin assembled over both like scaffolding over a ruin. "Well," he said loudly, "any man who comes down off that mountain alive owes me a drink regardless of what he remembers. Come to Lowmark when your legs work. First cup's mine. Second cup's yours, because I'm not made of money."

"I'll hold you to it," Tamsin said, and smiled, and Ilse watched Doss turn away quickly and fuss with his mule's harness for longer than any harness required.

They walked on. After a while Tamsin said, "He seemed like a good friend."

"He was," Ilse said. "Is. He drove mules for you for six years. He named his best mule after you. The mule resents it."

Tamsin laughed — startled out of him, bright and real — and then stopped, hand going to his chest as though the laugh had surprised him from inside. "Huh," he said. "That felt familiar. Laughing at mules."

"Everything about you is mules, deep down," Ilse said. "We'll discover the rest as we go."

He glanced at her sidelong. "You talk like you've known me a long time."

"I have," she said. "You haven't known me at all. It evens out."

"That's not how time works."

"You'd be amazed what stops working properly on that mountain," said Ilse, and he laughed again, and she filed the sound away next to the first one, hoarding.

* * *

Lowmark received him the way a village receives a ghost it is fond of.

Word ran ahead of them — Doss, naturally, telling everyone — so that by the time they reached the chapel square there were people standing in doorways, and someone had rung the bell early, and old Marta from the pie stall abandoned her pies entirely to come stare. Nobody rushed him. Ilse had sent word ahead of that too, in a letter she had written four times before getting right: *He remembers nothing. Do not crowd him. Do not weep at him. Let him arrive as a stranger and choose to stay.*

They mostly obeyed. Marta pressed a pie into his hands and said only, "Eat. You're thin," in the tone of a woman issuing a royal decree, and Tamsin, holding the pie like a shield, said, "Thank you — I think?"

"You think correctly," said Marta, and went back to her stall, and dabbed her eyes with her apron where she thought no one could see.

The chapel itself had changed. Ilse had been rebuilding it for months — new roof beams, the old cracked altar stone righted and cleaned, candles in every window. Above the altar hung no image at all, just empty space, because the god had made it clear through several increasingly pointed answered prayers that it preferred to be imagined individually. On the wall beside the door, though, there was a new carving, plain and recent: a sword point-down, and beneath it seven words.

*He gave what he loved so love would remain.*

Tamsin read it standing in the doorway, pie in hand, for a long time.

"That's me," he said finally. Not a question.

"That's you."

"I don't feel like that." He frowned at the carving. "I feel like a man holding a pie."

"The feeling comes and goes," Ilse said. "Mostly goes. That's rather the point of sacrifice — you pay when you can afford it least and never get a receipt." She stepped past him into the chapel, into the smell of beeswax and cut pine, and lit the evening candle with hands that only slightly trembled. "Come in. Sit. If you're going to be a legend, you may as well be a comfortable one."

He came in. He sat, on the third bench, which was — she noted, and said nothing, and carried it — the bench he had always chosen.

"Can I ask you something?" he said, after the candle caught.

"Anything."

"Why you? Out of everyone. Why did you climb a mountain to find a man who wouldn't know you?"

Ilse considered lying. She was, by trade and temperament, quite good at it. But the chapel was quiet, and the god was listening — she could feel it the way you feel sun through a window, a warmth with attention in it — and lies felt heavy here now, in a way they never had before the covenant returned.

"Because I loved him," she said. "Because he gave up every memory of me so that the world could have its prayers back, and someone has to remember for both of us, and I'm the only one who can. Because—" her voice caught, and she let it, dignity was not the same thing as a steady voice, dignity was doing the thing while the voice shook "—because he is worth finding. Even as a stranger. *Especially* as a stranger. Strangers get to begin honestly."

Tamsin was quiet for a long moment. In the window, evening light came through colored glass and lay across the floor between them like a bridge.

"That's the saddest and bravest thing anyone's said to me," he said at last, "and I've apparently done things worth carving on walls, so the competition must be stiff." He hesitated. Then, awkward, sincere, terribly gentle: "Would it help — I mean, I don't remember anything, but I'm told I'm a fast learner. If there are stories. About him. About... us, I suppose." He winced at his own phrasing. "If you ever wanted to tell them. I'd listen. I'd listen properly."

Ilse Vane looked at the man she loved, who had offered her the one thing left in the world she wanted, wrapped in the politeness due a stranger, and she smiled at him with her whole broken, mended heart.

"As it happens," she said, "I know a great many stories. And I've decided to tell you one every week. Same bench. Whether you ask or not."

"That sounds like a sermon schedule."

"It's a courtship schedule," said Ilse, "and I'll thank you not to confuse the two in front of the god."

Above the empty altar, quite distinctly, the candle flame bent sideways — though there was no wind — and Tamsin stared at it, and then at her, and then at the flame again, and Ilse saw the exact moment the first crack of wonder opened in him, the crack she had waited three days to see.

"All right," said Tamsin Quintana, stranger to his own heart, leaning forward with the light of the beginning of everything in his face. "Start with the mountain. Start with why I climbed it."
