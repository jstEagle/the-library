## 20. The New Covenant's First Winter

The first snow of the season came down on the training yard at Hollowmere like an apology — soft, unhurried, and slightly late.

Tamsin Quintana stood in the middle of six students who were, collectively, failing to hold a single stance, and wondered for perhaps the thousandth time that autumn how a man went from smuggling relics through mountain passes to teaching children how to stand still. The answer, as always, was: badly, and by degrees, and because a god had asked him to and he'd been too tired to refuse.

"Again," he said. "Feet like roots. Not like *stumps* — roots. A stump is dead, Bren. Roots drink."

Bren, fourteen years old and built like a well-fed calf, adjusted his feet with the enthusiasm of a boy being asked to drink gravel. Beside him, Sera — thirteen, sharp-eyed, the only one of the six who might actually amount to something — shifted her weight almost correctly and then ruined it by looking pleased with herself.

"You moved," Tamsin told her.

"I improved."

"Improvement is not permission." He walked the line slowly, boots crunching on frost-hardened mud. "A swordspeaker doesn't speak first with the sword. The sword is the last word in a long conversation. Everything before it — the feet, the breath, the patience — that's the grammar. Get the grammar wrong and the whole sentence comes out as screaming."

Milo, at the end of the line, raised his hand. Milo raised his hand for everything. Tamsin had once caught him raising his hand during a lightning storm.

"Yes, Milo."

"If the conversation is so long," Milo said, "when do we get to say anything?"

Tamsin looked at the sky, which was doing its best impression of a wet grey blanket, and considered the question seriously, because Milo's questions were usually serious questions wearing a child's coat.

"When you have something worth saying," he said. "Which, if you're lucky, is never. The best swordspeakers I ever knew died old and quiet, having spoken maybe three true words apiece."

"What were they?"

"Nobody knows. That's rather the point."

He let them out early, because the snow was thickening and because he had learned — slowly, painfully, over months of trial and error — that cruelty made worse teachers than cold did. They tramped off toward the hall, arguing about whether a sentence could be a scream if you screamed it politely, and Tamsin stayed in the yard alone, watching the snow fill his own footprints.

Two hundred years of silence, broken. Shrines answering prayers honestly now, all across Thornfell — honestly being the operative word, since the gods had apparently decided that honesty included telling farmers their crops would fail and telling young men in love that the girl in question could do better. Attendance at services had dropped by half and then climbed back up, because it turned out people preferred a cruel truth to a kind lie once they'd gotten used to the taste of it.

And him. The last swordspeaker, sworn to a dead god, now the first teacher of a new order, standing in a training yard he'd built himself on ground the mountain had offered him personally, wondering what exactly he'd agreed to.

He knew what he'd agreed to. That was the trouble. He remembered every term of the bargain except the one that mattered.

* * *

The chapel at Lowmark had been rebuilt on the bones of the old one, which was fitting, Ilse supposed, though she tried not to suppose things out loud anymore. Supposing out loud had gotten her called a heretic by a regent, hunted across two mountain ranges, and nearly erased from her own history. She'd earned the right to keep her suppositions private.

She spent her mornings tending the shrine, which answered prayers now with a gentleness that still startled her. She spent her afternoons copying the recovered ledgers into a fair hand, because someone had to write down what the crown had done or the realm would do what realms do, which is forget. And she spent her evenings doing the thing she had promised herself she would do, the thing that had become the spine of her week:

She wrote stories.

Not sermons. Not histories. Stories — small ones, true ones, each one a piece of a man's life given back to him one week at a time, wrapped in enough ordinary detail that he could take it without knowing why his hands shook.

Tonight's story was about a bridge.

* * *

Tamsin came down to Lowmark twice a month, officially to escort the new swordspeakers' supply train past the bandit country, unofficially because the road passed the chapel and the chapel had a priestess and the priestess had tea and a way of looking at him that he couldn't parse and couldn't stop walking toward.

He arrived this time with snow in his collar and Milo in tow, because Milo had begged — actually begged, hand raised the whole time — to see a real shrine that answered prayers.

"He wants to ask it something," Tamsin said, stamping snow off his boots in the chapel doorway. "I told him shrines aren't wishing wells. He said he wasn't going to wish, he was going to *inquire*. His word. I've created a monster."

"Then we shall inquire responsibly." Ilse came around the altar with two cups already poured, because of course she'd known he was coming; the shrine probably told her, or the tea simply brewed itself in anticipation. "Sit. You look frozen."

"I'm not frozen. I'm weathered."

"You're frozen, Tamsin. Drink."

He drank. The tea was spiced with something he didn't recognize and warmth went through him like a sentence finishing properly. Across the nave, Milo stood before the shrine with the gravity of a small ambassador, hands folded, lips moving. After a moment the shrine's candle — the one that had burned without oil for eight months now — flickered, and Milo came back wearing the expression of a boy who has received an answer and isn't sure he wanted it.

"Well?" Tamsin asked.

"It said my father's leg will heal if he stops trying to plow with it for one winter." Milo scowled. "That's not a wish. That's just sense. My mother says the same thing."

"The gods are rarely more original than our mothers," Ilse said. "It's one of their most endearing qualities."

Milo, mollified by theology, went off to examine the reliquary wall, and Tamsin and Ilse sat in the quiet that had become their native language — comfortable, courteous, and haunted by a room neither of them could name.

"How are the students?" she asked.

"Failing beautifully. One of them may be a swordspeaker. The rest will be excellent at standing in fields."

"And you?"

"Tired. Grateful. Confused." He turned the cup in his hands. "You?"

"Same, but with better handwriting."

He laughed — and then stopped laughing, because there it was again, that shape behind the laugh, the missing room. He'd learned to feel it the way you feel a tooth that isn't there: the tongue keeps going to the gap. Something in him leaned toward this woman across the tea like a plant toward light, and he had no idea why, and she watched him lean and said nothing, and the courtesy between them was so careful it was almost a form of grief.

"There's a story," she said quietly, "that I tell sometimes. At evening service. About a bridge."

"Oh?"

"A rope bridge the old swordspeakers guarded. In the story, a man crosses it and the bridge remembers him — remembers him so hard the ropes sing." She smiled at her own cup. "The congregation likes it. Children especially. They've started calling it the singing bridge."

"Is it true?"

"Some of it." She looked up. "Does it frighten you, that some of it might be true?"

He considered the question honestly, the way she always made him consider things honestly, which was either her finest quality or his greatest inconvenience.

"No," he said. "It feels like... hearing a song you don't know, and humming along anyway. It doesn't frighten me. It makes me miss something. I just can't find the thing to miss."

Ilse set down her cup. For a moment — one moment, no longer — her composure slipped, and he saw underneath it: a woman carrying a whole history alone, every day, in both arms, smiling while her arms shook.

"Then come back in two weeks," she said, "and I'll tell you another one."

* * *

Winter deepened. The students got better — Sera spoke her first true word in December, a single syllable that made the frost on the training yard bloom outward in a perfect ring, and Tamsin had gone back to his rooms afterward and sat in the dark for an hour, feeling something between pride and vertigo.

Roderic came at midwinter, riding up through the snow with his royal warrant finally retired and a new commission from the boy-king Aldric, who at ten was turning out to be the sort of monarch who asked questions in council meetings that made grown ministers sweat. Roderic inspected the training yard, watched Sera work, and pronounced judgment.

"They'll do," he said. "They're undisciplined, superstitious, and they trust each other too much."

"That's the curriculum."

"Hm." He stood beside Tamsin at the fence, two men watching children fall down in the snow, and after a while he said, quieter: "You ever get it back? What you gave?"

Tamsin didn't pretend to misunderstand. Roderic knew. Roderic was one of perhaps four people alive who knew what the reforging had cost, and he had never once offered pity, which Tamsin appreciated the way you appreciate a knife that stays sharp instead of rusting.

"No," Tamsin said. "But I'm told it's being kept safe."

"By whom?"

"A very stubborn woman with excellent handwriting."

Roderic nodded slowly, watching Sera put Bren on his back in the snow for the fourth time. "Then it's in better keeping than most crowns," he said, and rode away, and Tamsin stood a long time in the cold thinking about crowns and keeping and the difference between having a thing and knowing you have it.

* * *

He went down to Lowmark for the solstice service, because the whole valley went and because not going would have required an excuse, and he was out of excuses. The chapel was full, candles everywhere, the honest gods receiving honest prayers from honest people, and Ilse led the service in a voice that filled the room without raising itself, and Tamsin stood at the back among the farmers and felt the missing room open its door inside him again, just a crack, just wide enough for the draft.

Afterward, in the emptying nave, she found him.

"You hummed," she said. "During the third hymn. You don't know it, but you hummed it perfectly. It's not a hymn anyone here knows. I taught it to—" She stopped. Recalibrated. Smiled. "It's an old mountain tune. I'm surprised you know it."

"I don't know it," Tamsin said. "But apparently parts of me kept notes."

Something passed across her face — hope, quickly governed, the way she governed everything now, carefully, with both hands. "Would you like to hear a story? It's solstice. Tradition demands one."

"Whose tradition?"

"Mine. I'm inventing it as we speak."

So she told him one — a short one, about a man and a woman in a frozen cave full of voices, and how the woman had held the man's hands and named him back to himself when the voices tried to take his name away. She told it plainly, without ornament, the way you tell a thing you've told yourself many times in the dark. And Tamsin listened, and felt the shape of it fit against the missing room like a key fitted to a lock that no longer has a door.

When she finished, the chapel was empty except for them and the candles, and the shrine's flame stood very still, listening.

"That man in the story," Tamsin said slowly. "Did he ever get those memories back?"

"No," Ilse said. "He gave them away. On purpose. To save everything." She met his eyes. "But the woman kept them. Every one. And she decided — she *chose* — that a memory isn't only alive in the mind that holds it. It's alive in the telling. As long as she tells the stories, nothing is truly lost. It's just... waiting. Being loved somewhere else until it can come home."

Tamsin sat with that. Outside, the snow came down on Lowmark, soft and unhurried, covering the roads he'd smuggled along and the yard where children fell down learning grammar, covering everything equally, the way snow does, keeping nothing and promising everything.

"Will you tell me another?" he asked. "Next time I come down?"

Ilse smiled, and it cost her something, and she paid it gladly.

"Every time," she said. "As many as it takes."

And far above them both, on a mountain whose summit no longer needed guarding, a god who kept honest accounts noted a debt of stories accruing against a debt of memories — and found, in its ancient and patient way, the arrangement good.
