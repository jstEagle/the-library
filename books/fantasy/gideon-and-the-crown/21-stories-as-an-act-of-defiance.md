## 21. Stories as an Act of Defiance

The first story she told him was about a mule.

Not the mule itself — though the mule featured prominently — but the morning in the Hollow Steps when Tamsin had tried to teach her how to load a pack-frame and she had loaded it wrong on purpose, just to see what he'd do.

She told it to him in the rebuilt chapel at Lowmark, three weeks after Midwinter, while snow ticked against the new glass windows and the smell of pine shavings still hung in the rafters from the carpenters' work. He had come to deliver candles, because that was apparently a thing he did now: the last swordspeaker of Thornfell, terror of smugglers' dens, courier of devotional tallow. He stood by the door with his hood still up, all politeness and distance, and she could see him deciding whether he was allowed to stay.

"Sit down, Tamsin," she said. "You look like a man waiting for a verdict."

"I'm delivering candles."

"You've delivered them. The box is on the table. Sit."

He sat. That was the thing about him she was learning again from the outside — he grumbled like a landslide coming, but he always sat.

"The first story," she said, "is about a mule named Duchess, who hated you."

"She didn't hate me. She respected me. There's a difference."

Ilse laughed before she could stop herself, and then had to look away, because the laugh had come out of her whole body, unguarded, the way it used to come out of her on the mountain, and for one terrible second she saw him flinch — not at her, but at the sound, the way a man flinches at a bell rung in a room he's never been in but somehow knows.

*Steady*, she told herself. *This is the work.*

"Duchess," she went on, folding her hands in her lap like a woman settling into a long sermon, "bit you twice that morning. Once on the shoulder and once, memorably, on the backside, in front of Sera Doss and two of her drovers, who talked about it for the rest of their lives."

"They did not."

"Sera Doss named her second daughter after the mule."

"That is — no." But he was fighting the smile now, and losing, and she counted it as a victory worth more than any she'd won with a sword. "Why are you telling me this?"

"Because it happened," Ilse said. "And because you can't hold it anymore, and I can, and I have decided that I am going to be insufferable about it. One story every week. That's the arrangement. You don't have to believe them. You don't even have to listen properly. You just have to be here."

He looked at her for a long moment. Snow-light moved across his face, and she watched him do the arithmetic he must do constantly now — *how much of me does she know that I don't know; is it kind or is it cruel that she knows it* — and then he nodded once, small, like a man agreeing to a wound because refusing it would be worse.

"All right," he said. "One story a week. But I get editorial rights."

"No."

"I remember being lied about. By Roderic, mostly. Someone should defend my honor."

"Your honor will survive. It's very well-armored. Like the rest of you." She picked up the candle box and pressed it into his hands as a dismissal, and as a gift, and as an excuse to touch him briefly on the wrist, which she permitted herself exactly once per visit and rationed like medicine. "Same time next week. Bring more candles. The gods of Lowmark go through them like children through honeycake."

* * *

The second story was about rain.

They were on the chapel steps because the interior was full of novices practicing their kneeling, which sounded absurd until you heard forty people doing it badly. Tamsin had brought the candles, plus, without comment, a sack of oranges, which he set down as if it were nothing, as if he hadn't walked four miles through slush to bring citrus to a woman he couldn't remember loving.

"It rained for nine days on the Salt Road," she said. "After the gate. After we lost Bren and Oskar." His face went carefully still; grief for men he'd carried but couldn't picture. "Don't worry. This isn't that story. This is the part after. We were sheltering under a rock overhang outside Harrow Fen, and you were trying to keep the fire alive, and you were failing, and you were so angry about failing. Not sad. Angry. You talked to that fire like it was a soldier who'd disgraced himself."

"I would never talk to a fire."

"You called it 'a cowardly heap of wet sticks.' You apologized to it afterward. I heard you."

"I apologize to animals and occasionally to doors. It's a discipline."

"And then," she said, leaning forward, because this was the part, this was the part she'd been carrying alone for months, "you gave up on the fire entirely, and you took off your cloak, and you put it around my shoulders, and you said — and I want to be accurate here, I've had months to polish this sentence in my head and I've decided not to change a word of it — you said, 'Well. If we're going to be miserable, we may as well be miserable efficiently.'"

Tamsin stared at her.

"That's not funny," he said slowly.

"No," she agreed. "It isn't. It's the least funny thing anyone ever said to me. And I laughed so hard I cried, right there in the rain, with two of our friends dead behind us, because it was so perfectly *you* — that you would try to organize our grief into something with good posture." Her voice caught and she let it, deliberately, because hiding the cost of these stories felt like lying about them. "I loved you very much by then, Tamsin. I think I'd loved you since the bridge. But that was the moment I knew I'd love you for the rest of my life, however long either of ours turned out to be."

He was quiet for a long time. The novices inside had finally gotten their kneeling synchronized, a soft rhythmic thump like a slow heart.

"I don't feel it," he said at last, and his voice was rough. "When you tell me these things. I want you to know that. I hear them and they're — they're good stories. They're about someone who sounds like a decent man. But there's no floor under them. No weight."

"I know."

"It doesn't hurt you? Saying them?"

"Every week," she said honestly. "Like pulling a splinter out the same place over and over. But here's what I've learned about wounds, Tamsin Quintana, from a man who taught me field-dressing in a frozen cave while complaining about my technique: you don't leave a splinter in just because removing it stings."

That startled a breath of laughter out of him — real laughter, brief, quickly smothered, but real — and she filed the sound away where she kept all the others, in the growing archive of him that she was rebuilding piece by piece, like a woman reassembling a shattered window one pane at a time and refusing to mind the drafts.

* * *

By the sixth week, the stories had become a fixture, and the town had noticed.

Lowmark noticed everything; it was a border town, and noticing was its chief industry after smuggling. Old Marta from the wool shop started calling Thursdays "the priestess's courting days," loudly enough to be heard across the square. Tamsin turned a color usually reserved for sunburn and denied everything with such formality that Marta charged him double for ribbon out of pure delight.

"It's not courting," he told Ilse, arriving with candles and, this week, a jar of plum preserves he claimed a widow had forced upon him.

"Of course not."

"We're conducting a — a restoration. A historical project."

"Naturally. Very scholarly. Is the scholar going to open those preserves, or shall I?"

They ate plum preserves with wooden spoons on the chapel steps like a pair of children, and she told him the story of the night in the shepherd's bothy when he'd confessed, half-asleep, that he was afraid of quiet — that silence reminded him of the reliquary cave, of all those dead voices waiting for him to answer — and how she had stayed awake talking nonsense until dawn so that he wouldn't have to be quiet. She told it lightly, with humor, the way she'd learned to tell the hard ones, because the hard ones told straight made him go somewhere she couldn't follow.

But afterward he didn't leave. He sat turning the empty preserve jar in his hands, and then he said, without looking at her:

"There's a fear I can't place."

She went very still.

"Not a memory. Just — a shape. Like a room in a house I've only seen from outside. When certain things happen. When you laugh a particular way. When someone says my name from behind. There's a — a reaching. Like my hand knows where something is and the rest of me doesn't." He looked up, and his eyes were the same grey-brown they had been on the summit, and just as frightened. "Is that normal? For what was done to me?"

"I don't know," Ilse said. "Nobody's ever done what was done to you. You're the first man in two centuries to pay a god's price and live beside the receipt." She reached out — her one touch, spent early today, and worth it — and closed his fingers around the empty jar. "But I'll tell you what I think. I think a memory isn't only in the head. I think it's in the hands too. In the way you stand. In what makes you laugh. The god took the story, Tamsin. It couldn't take the teller."

He looked down at his own hand, curled around the jar, as if it might say something.

"Tell me another one," he said quietly. "Off schedule. I know the rule is one a week, but—" He stopped, jaw working. "There's a laugh I miss. Yours. I heard it earlier, at the mule story, and I missed it. How can I miss something I've only heard once?"

Ilse had prepared herself for many things in the months since the mountain. She had prepared herself for him to stop coming. For the stories to curdle into obligation. For the day she'd catch him wincing at her instead of at the loss. She had not prepared herself for him to ask for more, hungry, ashamed of the hunger, and it undid her so thoroughly that she had to pretend a sudden interest in the horizon for several seconds before she trusted her voice.

"Tell you what," she managed. "New rule. One story a week, as agreed. But if you ever ask for an extra one — off schedule, like today — you have to trade me one of yours. Anything. A story about your week. Your students. Something true."

"That's extortion."

"That's covenant," she said, and watched the old word land in him like a stone dropped in a well. "We're reforging something, you and I. Smaller than a god's name. Same principle."

* * *

His stories, it turned out, were terrible.

Not in content — in craft. He told them the way he did everything, in clipped tactical reports. *The novice Tam broke his practice sword. The novice Tam blamed the sword. I explained that swords are honest.* She laughed until her ribs ached, and he watched her laugh with an expression she recognized from the bridge, from the bothy, from a hundred moments she owned alone now — the expression of a man witnessing something he doesn't understand and has decided to protect anyway.

Weeks stacked up like cordwood. The story of the rope bridge, which she saved, saving it, telling herself she wasn't ready and knowing she was waiting for something else. The story of the choir under the ice, softened at the edges, its guilt shared out between them until it weighed less. The story of the salt gate, which she told only once and never repeated, and which he received in silence and then, the following week, brought her white heather from the high country without explanation, which was its own kind of answer.

Spring came late to Lowmark and came all at once, the way border springs do, arriving overnight like a smuggler with goods to declare. The chapel windows were finished. The novices could kneel in unison. Marta had begun openly referring to Tamsin as "our lad," which he endured with the grim fortitude of a man under siege.

On a bright Thursday in the thaw, Ilse met him on the steps with the story she'd been carrying since winter, the one she'd been saving, and found she couldn't tell it yet — because he got there first.

"I want to trade," he said. "An extra story. Mine."

"Go on."

He shifted his weight. Candles under one arm; the other hand working at his belt like a man checking for a sword that wasn't there. "It's not really a story. It's more of a — an observation. Filed under things I've noticed." A breath. "I've noticed that Thursday is the best day of my week. Every week. Without variation. And I've noticed that I plan my routes so I pass through Lowmark on Thursdays, and that I've started bringing things — preserves, heather, once a truly hideous carved duck that Marta assured me you'd love — and I've noticed that when I'm teaching the novices and someone laughs a certain way, I turn around to check whether it's you, and you're never there, because you're twenty miles away, and I don't know why I expected—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "I don't remember you, Ilse. But I appear to have arranged my entire life around you. And I thought someone ought to say it out loud."

The thaw dripped off the chapel eaves. Somewhere behind them, the novices had abandoned kneeling in favor of an argument about whether saints could sneeze.

Ilse looked at this man — her man, her stranger, her stubborn, courteous, candle-bearing love — and felt the whole winter of held grief shift into something else, something with better posture.

"Well," she said. "Since we're filing observations. Here's mine: I'm going to tell you the story of the bridge next week. The anniversary of the reforging is coming, and it's time, and I think—" her voice did not shake, she made sure of it, "—I think you're ready. And I think when you hear it, that reaching feeling you described, that room you've only seen from outside — I think the door's going to open."

He was silent a moment. Then, carefully, like a man testing ice:

"And if it opens? What then?"

"Then," said Ilse Vane, priestess of a waking god, keeper of every story he'd lost, "you'll have to decide what to do about the woman standing in it."

She held out her hand for the candles, and after a heartbeat, smiling faintly, warily, like a man stepping onto a bridge that remembers him — he gave them over.
