## 22. The First Word of a New Oath

The snow came early that year, the way it always did now that the mountain had opinions again.

Tamsin stood at the window of the Lowmark chapel and watched it fall on the town like a benediction — slow, unhurried, the kind of snow that meant to stay. A year ago he would have cursed it. Snow on the passes meant dead mules and frozen fingers and clients who wept about their schedules. Now it just meant the hearth in the chapel's back room needed another log, and that Ilse would be late coming up from the shrine because half of Lowmark would want to confess something before the anniversary bells rang.

He was right on both counts. He fed the fire, and she was late.

When she finally came through the door, shaking snow from her hood, her cheeks were red and her eyes were bright with the particular exhaustion of a woman who had spent three hours listening to a farmer explain, at length and in tears, why he felt guilty about a pig.

"The pig," Tamsin said, "lived."

"The pig lived." She hung her cloak on the peg he'd carved for her — he'd carved it badly, and she kept it anyway, which was its own small mystery. "But Bram swears the animal *knew*. That it looked at him when he was deciding whether to sell it to the knacker, and there was forgiveness in its eyes, and now he can't eat pork without weeping."

"That's not a sin, that's an improvement."

"Tamsin Quintana." She pointed at him with the gravity of a woman who had rehearsed this. "You are a sworn swordspeaker of the reforged covenant, teacher of six students, the man the mountain itself spoke to, and you are making jokes about a grieving pig."

"Somebody has to. The mountain's very solemn these days. It never laughs at anything."

"It laughed at you last week. When you fell off the bridge."

"The bridge laughed at me. The bridge is a show-off." He handed her a cup of the spiced wine he'd been keeping warm, and their fingers touched around the cup, and neither of them made anything of it, because they'd gotten good at not making anything of it, which is what a year of careful orbiting teaches you.

She sat by the fire. He sat across from her. Between them, on the table, lay the little book.

It was a cheap thing — smuggler's stock, bought from a peddler who'd overcharged her because she haggled like a scholar instead of like a thief. Its spine was already cracked from use. Inside, in her handwriting, were stories. Forty-nine of them. One for every week since the mountain.

*The Story of the Woman Who Greeted a Stranger at the Shrine.* *The Story of the Salt Gate.* *The Story of How Tamsin Quintana Snores (a Tragedy).* He knew the titles because she read them to him, one each week, in this room, by this fire, and every week he listened the way you listen to a tale of a country you once sailed past in fog — aware that it mattered to someone, unable to feel why.

Except tonight was different. Tonight was the anniversary.

"I've been thinking," he said.

"Dangerous. It's nearly evening. You usually save that for morning."

"Ilse." He turned his cup in his hands. The wine steamed. Outside, the bells of the little shrine began to ring — not the coronation bells of the old regime, all bronze and thunder, but the new ones, cast thin, tuned soft, the way the god apparently liked them. "Tell me the story of the bridge."

She went still.

For fifty-two weeks she had offered, and for fifty-two weeks he had said yes the way you say yes to medicine: dutifully, braced for the taste. But he had never asked. Not once. And she looked at him across the fire now the way a person looks at a door they'd stopped expecting to open.

"You're sure?"

"No," he said honestly. "But I'm asking anyway."

* * *

She opened the book to a page near the middle. He watched her finger find the place, watched her take a breath the way his students took breaths before speaking to the shrines — gathering themselves, offering themselves up.

"The Bridge of Old Promises," she said. "All right. It was winter then too. We were eleven days ahead of the Regent's vanguard, and I was so tired I'd stopped being afraid, which I'm told is a bad sign. There were nine of us left. Marta carried the reliquary key against her heart. Old Fen had a fever. And you—"

She glanced up. Checking, as she always checked, whether the name landed anywhere.

"Go on," he said.

"You stood at the head of the bridge and you looked at it for a long time. Ropes and planks and a drop into cloud, and frost on everything like the world had been salted. And you said — you said, 'I know this bridge. I swore on it when I was nineteen years old, and I have spent fifteen years pretending I didn't.' And then you asked me if I believed the old things could still hear."

"And what did you say?"

"I said I didn't know. I said I'd spent my whole life praying into silence, and silence is a hard habit to break faith with." Her voice did something gentle here, some turn he didn't have a map for but could feel the shape of. "And you said, 'Then we'll find out together,' and you stepped onto the first plank, and you spoke."

"What did I speak?" His own voice had gone quiet. The fire popped. Somewhere out in the snow, the shrine bell finished its round.

"The first words of your oath. The words every swordspeaker says when they're bound — the ones nobody had spoken in fifteen years. *Stone, remember me. Rope, remember me. Wind, carry my name up where the old ones wait.*" She said them slowly, carefully, the way you handle something borrowed and precious. "And the bridge remembered you, Tamsin. The ropes went taut like a held breath let out. The frost lifted off the planks in a single breath, all at once, like the bridge had been waiting to exhale since before either of us was born. Fen crossed on legs that shouldn't have held him. Marta wept. And you stood in the middle of it, over all that empty air, laughing — actually laughing — and you looked back at me and you said, 'Priestess, your gods are rude, dramatic, and entirely alive. Get over here.'"

Tamsin realized his hands had stopped moving.

He realized something else too, something that started low in his chest like the first tremor of snow shifting on a slope. Because he could see it. Not remember it — remembering wasn't the right word, remembering was a door opening onto a room you owned. This was different. This was standing outside a lit window in the snow, watching warmth move behind glass, and finding — impossibly, absurdly — that the shape of the people inside fit his hands.

He could see the frost lifting. All at once, one breath. He could see a young man with a fever crossing rope like a born dancer because the bridge decided to hold him. He could hear himself laugh.

"Ilse," he said.

Her eyes had gone wide and wet. "Yes."

"I can see it." He heard his own voice crack and didn't care. "I can't — it's not memory, I don't *have* it, but I can see it. The frost coming off the planks. Your face when you stepped on. You were terrified."

"I was absolutely terrified."

"But you stepped on anyway."

"You asked me to."

He put his cup down because his hands weren't steady enough to hold it. Fifty-two weeks of stories, and he had listened to all of them politely, kindly, the way you listen to a widow describe a house that burned — respectful of the loss, untouched by the flame. And now, on the anniversary, on the story of the bridge, something had caught.

"Why this one?" she whispered. "Why tonight? What's different?"

"I don't know." He laughed, unsteadily. "Maybe the mountain finally got bored of being subtle. Maybe a year of hearing you tell them planted something. Maybe—" He stopped. Looked at her properly. At the firelight in her hair and the cracked little book in her lap and the year of stubborn, patient, defiant love written across her whole face. "Maybe love isn't a thing you lose. Maybe it's a thing you keep almost losing, and someone keeps handing it back."

She didn't cry, quite. But she came around the table and took his hand, and her grip was the grip of a woman who had carried something alone for a long time and had just been offered help carrying it.

"Say it again," she said. "What you saw."

"The frost. Lifting all at once." He closed his eyes. "Your boots on the planks. You walked like you were angry at the bridge. Like you dared it to drop you."

"I did dare it. I was furious at it. I was furious at everything that winter except you."

"And me? What was I like? In the parts I can't see?"

She considered him for a long moment, and then she smiled — not the careful smile she wore for strangers, not the patient smile she wore for storytelling, but a third one, private and crooked, that he suspected belonged entirely to the version of him who no longer existed.

"You were insufferable," she said fondly. "You were brave and reckless and funny at exactly the wrong moments, and you gave away the only thing you had left without hesitating, and I have spent a year loving two men — the one who forgot me and the one sitting in front of me — and I will tell you a secret, Tamsin Quintana."

"Tell me."

"They're the same man. They always were. The memories were just furniture."

* * *

They talked until the fire burned down to embers and the snow thickened past the window into a white hush. She told him the bridge story twice more — once slowly, so he could stop her and ask questions (*Did I really call the god rude? To its face?* — *You called it worse later; that's a different story*), and once straight through, fast, the way she'd told it the first time, and both times the window-glow of it brightened, the shapes inside fitting his hands a little better.

Near midnight, he walked her the forty steps from the chapel back room to her cell above the shrine, because that was the ritual, the nightly courtesy, the orbit. But at her door he stopped, and the snow settled on his shoulders, and he heard himself say:

"Tomorrow. Tell me another one."

She turned. Snowflakes caught in her lashes. "Which one?"

"Any of them. All of them. Start at the beginning and work forward. I'll bring the wine." He swallowed. "Ilse — I don't know how much comes back. I'm not promising you the man from the bridge. I might get pieces. I might get nothing but this — this seeing-through-glass. But I want to hear them. Not because I owe you. Because tonight, for the first time, listening felt like *coming home to somewhere*, and I—" His voice failed, restarted. "I'd forgotten what that felt like. Or maybe I never knew. Either way. Tomorrow. And the day after. And the one after that. However many it takes. However many there are."

She reached up and brushed the snow from his hair, an old gesture he had no memory of and recognized anyway, the way you recognize a melody you've never heard.

"Fifty-two weeks," she said. "Fifty-two stories. And I'll write more. I intend to keep writing them until my hand gives out, and then I'll dictate them to your students, and they'll tell them to *their* students, and in a hundred years there will be swordspeakers on that bridge telling the story of the fool who talked to a god like it was a late client."

"He probably deserved it."

"He absolutely did."

He leaned down and kissed her forehead, and it was the first time in a year he had initiated any touch beyond a hand or a shoulder, and she made a small sound — surprise, and relief, and something older than both — and he thought: *there. That sound. I want to learn every sound she makes. I have time.*

Because that was the thing the mountain had taught him, in its rude, dramatic, entirely alive way. The old covenant hadn't come back the way it was. Nothing ever did. It came back chosen — sworn again, plank by plank, word by word, by people who could have walked away and didn't. Memory might return or it might not. But this — the asking, the telling, the showing up tomorrow with wine — this was an oath too. Maybe the only kind that had ever really counted.

"Goodnight, Ilse Vane," he said.

"Goodnight, Tamsin Quintana." She smiled her third smile. "Teacher of six students. Insufferable man. My favorite story."

He was halfway down the shrine steps when he heard her door close, and he stood in the falling snow a moment longer, looking up at the dark bulk of the gods' mountain, at the summit where a man had once traded everything and gained, somehow, this.

"Thank you," he told it. Not a prayer, exactly. More of a nod between colleagues.

The mountain, in the manner of mountains, said nothing at all.

But far above, at the summit shrine, the eternal flame that had burned for a year now — the flame Ilse tended every solstice, the flame the boy-king Aldric had climbed through a storm to light with his own un-hollowed hands — flickered. Just once. Just enough.

Like laughter.

And down in Lowmark, in a cold cell above a warm shrine, Ilse Vane opened her cracked little book to a fresh page, dipped her pen, and wrote the fiftieth title in a hand that shook only a little:

*The Story of the Night He Asked.*

She underlined it twice. Then she blew out her candle, and slept, and dreamed — for the first time in a year — of a bridge in winter, holding.
