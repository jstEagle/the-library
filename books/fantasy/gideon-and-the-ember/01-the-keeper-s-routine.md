## 1. The Keeper's Routine

Orsen Dunmore woke before the sun did, which was the correct way to do things, and he had been doing things correctly for thirty years.

He lit the stove first — always the stove, because a cold house made for cold hands, and cold hands fumbled with seals that deserved better. Then the kettle. Then the small ritual of standing at his window while the water heated, looking down the length of the Hollow Valley as the light came up over the eastern ridge and slid, slow as honey off a spoon, across the grass.

The valley was beautiful in the way that an empty room is quiet. It held one road, one village at its mouth, one river that ran thin and quick over grey stones, and one gate.

The gate was his.

Not his in the sense of ownership — he owned nothing, really, except a house, a coat, a kettle, and forty-one years of habit. But it was his in the way a lighthouse belongs to its keeper, or a grave to its gardener. The Hollow Gate stood at the valley's far end where the cliffs pinched together like a book closing, a slab of black stone twice the height of a man, set into the rock face without frame or hinge or any visible reason to be there at all. It had no handle. It had no keyhole. It had seven seals, and Orsen knew every inch of them better than he knew his own face.

He drank his tea standing up, because sitting felt like lingering and lingering felt like wanting, and wanting was not part of the routine.

* * *

The walk to the gate took twenty minutes if he let himself dawdle, so he didn't. He passed the shepherd's cairn, passed the crooked rowan tree that grew out of a crack in the limestone like a man leaning out of a window to eavesdrop, passed the flat stone where — according to village children, who were unreliable narrators at best — a witch had once been pressed under a cartload of turnips. The witch stone was just a stone. Everything here was just what it was. That was rather the point of the valley.

Magic could not follow you into the Hollow. That was the first thing his father had taught him, back when teaching still happened, back when his father's voice still worked on ordinary days. *No charms, no curses, no cleverness. A man can be only a man here, and that is why we keep it.* Orsen had been nine years old and had understood perhaps a tenth of it, and had spent the intervening decades deciding that the tenth was enough.

At the gate, he set down his satchel, unrolled his inspection cloth — clean linen, folded in thirds — and began.

The first seal sat at knee height, a disc of pale metal no bigger than a saucer, sunk flush into the black stone. He laid two fingers against it. Cold, as always. Steady, as always. It gave back nothing, which was exactly what it was supposed to give back. A seal that gave something back was a seal with opinions, and seals with opinions got people hanged, or so his father had implied on the one occasion he'd been moved to imply anything at all.

"First seal," Orsen said aloud, because the litany required speaking, "sound."

The second seal, at chest height, slightly larger, worn smooth as a river pebble by three generations of Dunmore fingertips. Sound.

The third through sixth went the same way, each with its word, each with its touch, his voice settling into the rhythm of a man saying grace in a language he'd stopped translating years ago. There was comfort in it. He would admit that much, privately, in the privacy of his own skull: there was real comfort in a task that never changed. The world outside the valley — he gathered, from the almanacs and the cloth merchant's catalogs that arrived by mule cart four times a year — was a churning place. Kings fell. Borders wandered about like drunkards. Fashions in hats rose and collapsed with terrifying speed. But the second seal of the Hollow Gate was sound today, and it would be sound tomorrow, and there was a kind of wealth in that that no hat-fashion could touch.

The seventh seal was different.

It sat at the height of his own heart, and it was not metal but something older — a dark ring the size of a wagon wheel, carved directly into the stone, its grooves filled with a substance that was neither paint nor ash nor anything he had ever been able to scrape loose and examine. His father had called it the Mouth. Orsen called it the seventh seal, because calling it the Mouth invited questions, and he had retired from questions around the age of eleven.

He pressed his palm flat against it. The stone was cool. The grooves were silent. Nothing hummed, nothing shifted, nothing looked back.

"Seventh seal," he said, and then, because the litany demanded it and he had never once skipped it, not in thirty years, not even the winter his ribs cracked on the ice and breathing was a negotiation: "*Vessany sleeps. Vessany is empty. Vessany is no one's country now.*"

The words came out smooth as beads down a string. He did not think about what they meant. Thinking about what they meant was, he had long ago decided, a form of dawdling, and he did not dawdle.

He rolled up his inspection cloth, noted the date in his ledger — *All seals sound. Weather fair. Rowan tree still crooked* — and walked home to mend a fence.

* * *

The village of Marrow's End sat at the valley's mouth like a cat that has decided to tolerate your presence. Forty-odd roofs, a mill, a chapel with a bell that rang slightly flat, and a population who regarded Orsen with the particular wariness reserved for men who lived alone at the wrong end of a valley and talked to stones.

He went down twice a month for salt, lamp oil, flour, and the news sheets, and every trip followed the same choreography. Tam Hedley at the mill would nod at him the way you nod at weather. Old Mistress Corrigan would sell him his flour without meeting his eyes, though she would talk freely to the flour itself ("There's my good girl, mind the scoop"). And whichever children were loitering would fall silent as he passed, then erupt into whispers behind him, of which he invariably caught fragments.

*"—keeper of the dead door—"*

*"—my gran says his da buried a king under the floor—"*

*"—he talks to it, I heard him, he TALKS to it—"*

That last one was true, which Orsen felt took some of the sting out of it. A lie would have been more dignified.

He had tried, in his younger years, to be neighborly. He had helped raise the mill roof beams after the storm of some fifteen years past; he had pulled two of Tam Hedley's sheep out of the river with his own arms; he had once carried Mistress Corrigan's sick grandson eight miles through snow to the physician at Lowbridge. All of it had been received with gratitude of the most carefully rationed kind — thanks offered quickly, eyes averted, doors closed a shade sooner than necessary. As if kindness from the keeper might be catching.

His father had explained it once, in one of his rare explanatory moods, staring into the fire: *"They don't hate us, boy. They forget us. That's the arrangement working as intended. You'll be lonely, and they'll be safe, and both of those are the price."*

Orsen had thought, at nineteen, that he understood the price. At forty-nine, he suspected he had merely gotten good at paying it.

Today was a market day, so he went down. Salt, oil, flour, news sheets. Tam nodded. The children whispered. And then, near the well, a thing happened that had never happened in thirty years of market days:

A woman spoke to him.

She was young, broad-faced, with flour on her forearms and a baby on her hip, and she said, "Warden — beg pardon — Warden Dunmore. Is it true there's frost coming? My man says the gate-stone sweats before a hard frost. Says his father swore by it."

Orsen stood very still, the way a man stands when a wild bird has landed on him.

"I — the stone doesn't sweat," he managed. "I inspect it daily. It's quite dry."

"Oh." She looked faintly disappointed, and shifted the baby. "Well. Frost anyway, likely. Good day to you."

She was gone before he assembled a reply, and he stood by the well holding his salt like a man who has forgotten what salt is for, aware that half the market had watched the exchange and was now pretending very hard not to have done so.

*A conversation,* he thought on the walk home. *I've just had a conversation.*

It had lasted eleven seconds. He counted them all the way up the valley.

* * *

Evening. Stove lit, kettle on, ledger updated, supper of bread and cheese and the pickled onions he was quietly proud of. He read the news sheet by lamplight — trouble in the south, some Chancery business, inquisitors reassigned, a name he skimmed past without absorbing — and then banked the fire, checked the bolt on a door that had never once been troubled, and went to bed at the same hour he went to bed every night, which was early, which was correct.

And he dreamed.

He dreamed rarely, and when he dreamed it was usually the ordinary inventory of a life: fences, ledgers, the rowan tree, occasionally a vast and formless anxiety shaped like an unpaid debt. But this dream was none of these.

In the dream he stood before the gate, and the gate was open.

Not broken — open. Swung inward like a door in a fine house expecting guests, and beyond it lay a country he had no memory of and recognized instantly: terraced hillsides strung with lanterns, orchards heavy with fruit he could taste in the dream's strange way of letting you taste light, a city built up the flank of a mountain in white and gold, and everywhere — everywhere — people. Hundreds. Thousands. Singing, in a language that was not a language so much as a key turning in a lock the size of the sky.

And in the dream, someone was calling his name. Not *Orsen*. Something older than that, a name with weight to it, a name that fit him the way his coat never quite had —

He woke with his hand pressed flat against the wall above his bed, palm burning, heart going like a hammer on a stuck door.

The room was dark and ordinary. The stove ticked as it cooled. Outside, the wind moved through the rowan tree, and somewhere far down the valley a dog complained about the moon.

Orsen lay back down. He arranged the blanket correctly. He closed his eyes.

But sleep did not come back easily, and when it finally did, it came thin and shallow, and the last thing he saw behind his eyelids, over and over, was the open door — and the terrible, unbearable warmth of the light coming through it.

He woke again at his usual hour, before the sun, and told himself firmly that dreams were not inspections, and inspections were the only things that counted.

He almost believed it. But when he walked up to the gate that morning, satchel on his shoulder, litany ready on his tongue, he found himself — for the first time in thirty years — pausing with his fingers an inch from the first seal.

Listening.

The stone said nothing. Of course it said nothing. Stone says nothing; that is the whole arrangement.

But the morning air in the Hollow smelled, very faintly, of woodsmoke — and there was no hearth within ten miles of the gate but his own.
