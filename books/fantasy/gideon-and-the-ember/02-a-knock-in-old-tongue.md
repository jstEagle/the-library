## 2. A Knock in Old Tongue

The dream came again, and this time Orsen Dunmore woke with his heart hammering like a fist on a door.

He lay still in the dark of the keeper's house and listened to the rain. It was a habit thirty years old: wake, listen, count. One breath. Two. The house settling. The wind worrying at the eaves. No third sound, because there was never a third sound. In all his years as warden of the Hollow Gate, no one had ever come to his door after dark except the weather.

In the dream, though, someone had knocked. That was new. Usually the dream was only the door — the Hollow Gate itself, vast and black and seamless in its frame of living stone — and in the dream it stood *open*, just a crack, warm light spilling through like honey from a tipped jar, and Orsen would stand before it with his inspection lamp shaking in his hand, knowing with a certainty that lived below thought that he must not look inside, and looking anyway, and waking before he saw.

But tonight someone had knocked. On the gate? On his own front door? He couldn't say. Dreams are slippery that way; they hand you a feeling and take away the details, like a merchant who sells you the sack but keeps the grain.

"Ridiculous," he told the ceiling, in the tone he used for the ceiling perhaps twice a year. "A door can't knock."

He got up anyway, because routine was the spine he hung his life on, and lit the lamp, and went through the motions he had performed every morning and every night for three decades. Coat. Boots. Lamp trimmed. Keys on their iron ring, eleven of them, ten of which had not turned anything since his father's funeral. The eleventh — the long one, worn smooth as a river stone by two generations of Dunmore thumbs — went into the inner pocket, against his chest, where it could be felt like a second heartbeat.

Outside, the valley was doing what the valley always did: being beautiful and empty. Rain silvered down through the lamplight. The terraced hills stood dark and patient. Somewhere up-slope a fox was making an argument about territory, and losing. Below the keeper's house, half a mile down the winding track, the village of Lowmark slept under its thatch, its windows shuttered, its people — Orsen had long suspected — pretending very hard to be asleep whenever his lamp passed by.

He walked the path to the gate.

It never got easier to describe, so over the years he had simply stopped trying, even to himself. The Hollow Gate sat in the cleft of the valley's end where the hills folded together like hands closing over something precious — or something stolen, depending on who you asked, and nobody asked, because nobody in Lowmark talked about the gate at all if they could help it. It was a door without a wall. Black stone, taller than three men, set into the raw rock of the gorge, carved — if carving was the word — with lines that seemed to have grown rather than been made. No hinge. No handle. No seam wide enough to slide a knife-blade into, and Orsen knew, because in his first year, young and arrogant and certain his father was a superstitious old goat, he had tried.

His father had caught him at it. Had taken the knife out of his hand gently, the way you'd take a hot coal from a child, and had said only: *"We keep it shut, Orsen. We don't ask what it's shut."*

Orsen had spent thirty years not asking. He'd gotten quite good at it. Not-asking was, if he were honest, his single greatest accomplishment, and he suspected it was the accomplishment his father had actually been training him for all along.

He held the lamp up. He inspected.

The seals were the hard part to explain to anyone who'd never seen them — which was everyone, since he explained them to no one. Seven of them, one for each day of the litany, laid across the gate's face like bands of pale metal, though they were not metal. They looked, tonight as every night, unbroken. Unwarmed. Unbothered. Orsen put his palm flat against the lowest seal, as the litany required, and recited the first line under his breath:

*"Stone remembers what flesh is made to forget."*

Nothing happened. Nothing ever happened. That was the whole point, the entire architecture of his life: nothing happening, faithfully, forever. He moved to the second seal. *"What was hidden, keep hidden. What was paid for, keep paid."* Third seal. Fourth. His voice dropped into the rhythm of it, the way a man's feet find a road he's walked ten thousand times, and somewhere around the fifth line the rain softened and the fox gave up its argument and Orsen felt the familiar evening peace settle over him like a coat —

— and then the gate hummed.

There was no other word for it. A hum, low and round, felt more than heard, rising up through his boots and his palm and the bones of his arm, the way a bell keeps ringing after the rope has gone still. Orsen snatched his hand back as if the seal had bitten him. The lamp flame leaned sideways though there was no wind.

"No," Orsen said, to the gate, reasonably. "No, we don't do that."

The hum continued, gentle and enormous, like a sleeping animal turning over. Thirty years. Thirty years of nothing, and now this, on the night he'd dreamed of knocking. He stood there dripping in the rain with his heart going like a smith's hammer and every instinct his father had installed in him screaming *seals, litany, walk away, report nothing, feel nothing,* while underneath that older voice — the boy's voice, the one he'd spent a lifetime muzzling — whispered *it's awake, it's finally awake, look.*

Duty won, the way duty always won, and Orsen turned his back on the humming gate and walked quickly back to the house, telling himself he would check the records, verify the seals' maintenance schedule, do all the sensible things. He did not run. Wardens do not run from doors. But it was, he would later admit, a brisk walk of a kind usually reserved for men fleeing bees.

* * *

He did not sleep. He sat at his father's old desk with the maintenance ledger open before him and read the same entry four times without absorbing a word of it, and every time the wind gusted he lifted his head like a deer scenting smoke.

Near midnight — or what he judged was midnight, his clock having died years ago and never been replaced, since time in the valley moved at the pace of chores and seasons and needed no gears — the third sound came.

Not weather. Not fox. A knock.

Three knocks, small and polite, on his actual front door, and Orsen Dunmore, warden of the Hollow Gate, veteran of thirty silent years, experienced a moment of pure and undignified panic. Because in thirty years, no one had knocked. The Chancery sent no riders up this road — the road didn't properly go anywhere anymore, the maps having quietly agreed among themselves to forget the valley existed. The villagers did not visit. His father was twenty years dead. And yet here was a knock, small and patient, followed — after a decent interval — by three more.

Orsen picked up the fire poker, considered it, decided a poker communicated entirely the wrong message to whoever stood outside, and set it down again. Then he opened the door.

The child on his doorstep could not have been more than eight or nine years old, and she was, by any measure, a disaster. Mud to the knees, mud to the elbows, mud in her hair and — he would swear later — mud in her expression, some quality of stubborn bedragglement that suggested she had been muddy for days and had made her peace with it. She was soaked through despite a cloak that might once have been fine, embroidered along the hem with patterns Orsen's eye snagged on and refused to release: interlocking lines, grown-looking, gate-looking. She swayed slightly on her feet, the sway of someone who has walked far past the point where walking was wise.

And she was speaking to him. Rapidly, urgently, in a language he did not know and recognized instantly, the way you recognize a face from a photograph you were never supposed to see.

Because he *did* know it. Not the words — but the shape of them, the music of them, the particular way they rose and fell. His father had owned a book he was never allowed to touch, kept wrapped in oilcloth at the bottom of a locked chest, and once — once, when Orsen was nine himself, and quiet, and forgotten — he had seen his father reading it aloud, alone, by lamplight, moving his lips over syllables that sounded exactly like the ones tumbling out of this mud-streaked child now. His father had closed the book when he noticed, and had looked at Orsen for a long moment, and had said, "You saw nothing," and Orsen, trained early in the family trade, had seen nothing ever after.

"You'll want to come in," Orsen heard himself say, in Common, uselessly, and the child stopped mid-word and stared at him with an expression of furious exhaustion, and tried again — slower, louder, the universal dialect of the desperate — and none of it meant anything to him except that one word kept recurring, aimed at him like a finger:

*"Dunmore. Dunmore. Dunmore."*

The poker, he reflected, might have been useful after all, if only to lean on. He stepped aside and gestured her in, and she marched past him into the keeper's house as though she owned it, leaving a trail of mud that Orsen, absurdly, found himself cataloguing for later cleaning, because when the world tilts a man grabs whatever small order is in reach.

She stopped in the middle of the room, dripping, took stock of him with those fierce dark eyes — and then her gaze went past him, through the window, toward the cleft in the hills where the gate stood invisible in the night. Her whole body changed. The fury drained out of it and something else rushed in behind it, something that looked terribly like hope, and she said one more word, soft this time, almost reverent, and pointed at the window, and at him, and back at the window.

Then, before Orsen could decide whether to fetch bread, blankets, or the Chancery (he had no idea how one even went about fetching the Chancery; there hadn't been cause in decades), the child's legs simply quit. She went down like a dropped puppet, and Orsen lunged and caught her, and she weighed almost nothing, and she was burning with fever, and through the window behind them both, faint but unmistakable, the Hollow Gate was still humming — and the hum had changed. It had risen. It sounded, Orsen thought with a terror that felt disgracefully like joy, like something answering.

He laid her on the settle by the fire, and pulled off her ruined boots, and covered her with the good blanket, the one he'd never had occasion to use, and sat back on his heels and looked at her — this impossible, mud-caked, feverish child who spoke his father's forbidden language and called him *Dunmore* like an accusation — and he understood, with the clarity that arrives only at the worst possible moments, that thirty years of nothing-happening had just ended, and that whatever came next, he had already, in catching her, chosen a side.

The child murmured in her sleep. One word, over and over, soft as rain:

*"Dunmore. Dunmore. Dunmore."*

Outside, the gate hummed on, patient as a held breath, waiting for morning — and so, though he did not yet know it, was she.
