## 3. What the Valley Forgets

Ember had decided, somewhere between the third fence and the frozen creek, that the man at the gate was a liar.

Not a wicked liar. She'd met wicked liars in Vessany — well, she'd heard about them, mostly, from Gran Ilsa, who told stories the way other people threw stones, fast and hard and aimed at something. The wicked liars were the ones who smiled while they did it. This man didn't smile at all. He looked like someone had handed him a bucket of cold water and told him it was soup, and he was too polite to complain.

But he was still lying. Ember could tell, because he kept saying *empty*.

"The far side of the gate is empty," he said again, for the fourth time since dawn, as if saying it more would make it truer. He'd given her bread and tea and a blanket, and then he'd sat across from her in his narrow keeper's house with its one window and its smell of lamp oil and old paper, and he'd said *empty* four times, each time a little quieter, like a man arguing with an echo.

Ember pulled the blanket up to her chin and looked at him over the top of it.

"You say that word a lot," she said. "Empty."

"It's what I was taught." He said it stiffly, the way people recite things they've said so many times the words have worn smooth, like river stones. "The Hollow Gate seals off nothing. There is no country behind it. There never was."

"Then where did I come from?"

He opened his mouth. Closed it. His hands, which were big hands, keeper's hands, with burn scars on the knuckles from tending lamps in all weather — those hands went still on the table, and Ember watched them go still, and thought: *there it is.* Gran Ilsa said you could always find the truth in a person by watching their hands, because hands were honest even when mouths were paid to lie.

"I don't know," Orsen Dunmore said finally. And then, so quietly she almost missed it: "I don't know" — again, but different this time, like the first time was the answer and the second time was the question.

"You do know," Ember said. "You just don't know you know."

"That doesn't make sense."

"Most true things don't, at first." She said it the way Gran Ilsa said it, with her chin up, and was pleased to see it land. The warden flinched like she'd flicked water at him.

They sat there in the grey morning light. The house was very small, Ember decided. Smaller than any house in Vessany, and Vessany was a country that fit inside a wall. There were no pictures on the walls, only a calendar with thirty years of days crossed off, and a shelf of ledgers, and above the hearth a single iron key on a nail that was clearly decorative, because the real keys would be on his belt, and they were. He was a man who lived inside his own routine the way a snail lives inside its shell, and Ember had been raised on stories about men like this. Keepers. Wardens. The ones who stood outside the door so nobody inside would have to look at them.

She should hate him. That was what she'd been told to do, in the last lesson before she left. *Find the last Dunmore, and remember what his family took.* But hating him was difficult work, because he'd given her bread with honey on it, and he'd knelt down to talk to her instead of looming, and when the gate had hummed in the night he'd gone white as flour but he hadn't run. Running was what cowards did. Her father had run, and look where that got everyone.

So instead of hating him, she did what Gran Ilsa did to people she couldn't hate: she asked questions until they broke.

"What's your name?" she asked, though she knew.

"Orsen. Orsen Dunmore."

"And what does that name mean?"

He frowned. "It's just a name. It means nothing. It's a valley name. My father chose it because—"

"Because it sounds like nothing," Ember finished. "Like fog. Like a place that isn't anywhere." She set down her tea. "In my language, Dunmore isn't a valley name. In my language it's two words stuck together. *Dun* and *mór*. Do you want to know what they mean?"

His hands had gone still again. "I imagine you'll tell me whether I want to or not."

That surprised a laugh out of her, quick and bright, before she remembered she was supposed to be angry. "Fortress," she said. "And great. Dun-mór. Great fortress. Your family wasn't named after a valley, Warden. Your family was named after the thing they were guarding. You're not the keeper of the door. You're the last brick in the wall."

The silence that followed was long enough that Ember counted her own heartbeats in it. Eleven. Twelve. Outside, wind moved through the pines, and beneath the wind — faint, patient, like breathing under a blanket — the gate hummed.

"My father spoke it," Orsen said at last. His voice had changed. It was lower now, careful, like a man walking on ice. "The language. Old Vessani. He had a book. A prayer book. He burned it when I was nine, or he tried to. Some pages survived. I memorized them without knowing what they meant. Words like—" he stopped, and his mouth worked around shapes that clearly embarrassed him. "*Dun-mór.*"

"*Dun-mór*," Ember agreed. "You've been carrying my country around in your mouth your whole life and calling it nothing."

* * *

He made her tell it slowly, after that. Not because he wanted it slow — she could see he wanted it fast, wanted to grab the whole story in one fist like a man snatching papers from a fire — but because he'd learned, somewhere, that fast truths shatter, and slow ones bend.

So Ember told it the way she'd been taught to tell it, in order, the way the teachers in Vessany taught everything: beginning, middle, end, and then the part that hurts.

"There's a country called Vessany," she began. "It has terraced hills, seven of them, and lanterns on all of them, so at night from the high pastures it looks like the sky fell down into the valley and couldn't get back up. There are four hundred and six of us now. There used to be more. There was a purge, a long time ago — before your grandfather, maybe. Soldiers came with lists. So the elders did a thing that had never been done. They sang the whole country small."

"Sang it small?"

"Folded it. Hid it. Put it behind a door and sang the door shut and sang the memory out of every map and every head outside. That's why your maps call this wilderness. That's why your village can't say what's beyond the ridge — they literally can't. The forgetting is in them, like a stone in a shoe." She leaned forward. "But forgetting has a cost, Warden. Somebody has to hold the other end of the song. Somebody has to stay outside and be the hinge. One family. One line. Paid in silence, sworn to silence, so the door stays shut and the country stays hidden and everybody inside stays alive."

Orsen Dunmore had gone very pale. "My father's litany," he said. "The words I recite at the seal every morning. I thought they were a prayer against decay. Maintenance verses. Instructions for the seals."

"They are instructions for the seals," Ember said. "Instructions for keeping a country locked up. Every word you say every morning is a word somebody in Vessany hears through the stone, and knows the door is still shut, and knows it's working." She tilted her head. "Do you want to know the funny part? The really funny part?"

"I suspect I don't have a choice."

"Nope." She grinned, and it wasn't a kind grin, and she let it not be. "The funny part is that you think you're alone out here. Thirty years, one keeper, no family, no visitors, a village that won't look at you. You think that's because you're strange. You're not strange, Warden. You're *lonely on purpose*. They made you lonely so you'd never bring anyone to the door. Loneliness is part of the lock."

Something moved behind his eyes then — something big and slow turning over, like a boulder shifting at the bottom of a pond. Ember watched it happen and felt, underneath her anger, a small unwanted thread of pity. Because she knew what it was to be built into a plan without being asked. Everyone in Vessany knew that. It was practically their national sport.

"I walked here," she said, softer now. "Through the gate. Last night. It opened for me a crack, and I walked through, and I kept walking, and I found your house, and you gave me honey bread." She held his eyes. "I'm the first person to come out in thirty years. Do you understand? The door doesn't open for anyone. It opened for *me*. And you want to know why?"

"Why?" His voice was barely there.

"Because I'm the key-song," Ember said. "The elders sent me out. On purpose. Through the crack. Carrying the song that opens the door all the way. They voted on it. Four hundred and six people, and they picked me." She spread her arms, blanket and all, like a small furious bird. "So no, Warden. The far side of your gate is not empty. It has never been empty. It is full of lanterns and goats and grandmothers and one extremely annoyed council of elders, and every single one of them knows your name."

* * *

He stood up then. Walked to the window. Stood there with his back to her, shoulders rigid, looking out at the valley he'd tended for thirty years, and Ember watched him and understood that she was watching a man's whole life quietly come apart at the seams — and that some part of him, some deep part, had always known. Had recited the litany every morning and felt the words humming in his teeth and never once asked himself what they were *for*.

"You keep saying *empty*," Ember said to his back. "Say it again. Right now. Out loud."

He didn't turn around. "Empty."

"Does it feel true in your mouth?"

A long pause. "No," he said. "It hasn't for years."

"There," Ember said, almost gently. "That's the first honest thing you've said since I got here."

He turned. And here was the thing Ember hadn't expected, the thing that cracked her anger straight down the middle like river ice in spring: he wasn't angry back. He looked wrecked, and frightened, and older than yesterday, but underneath all of it there was something awake. Something that had been asleep for thirty years and had just heard its name called.

"The last Dunmore," he said slowly, testing the words. "Is that what you called me? When you arrived?"

"The last Dunmore," Ember confirmed. "With accusation in my voice. Gran Ilsa taught me how. She says your family took our country's name away and wore it as a coat."

"Did we?" He asked it honestly, terribly honestly, and Ember hated him a little for making her answer honestly back. "What did my family take, exactly? Tell me truly. If I'm going to be accused, I'd like to know the charge."

Ember opened her mouth to give the charge — the whole speech, the one she'd rehearsed on the road, the one about silence and payment and thirty years of a child growing up behind a wall while the keeper grew old outside it —

and stopped.

Because the truth was, she didn't know the details. Nobody inside knew the details. The bargain had been made on the far side of the forgetting, and the terms had been sung shut along with everything else. All Vessany knew was that a family named Dunmore held the hinge, and the hinge held the door, and the door held them in, and freedom was a word from songs.

"I don't know," she admitted, and it came out smaller than she wanted. "I know you're the lock. I don't know what you were paid. That's why the elders sent me. To find out. To open the door and see what's on the outside of the outside." She lifted her chin. "So you're going to help me, Warden. You're going to open your father's papers, and your father's letters, and whatever else he hid in this house, and you're going to read them to me in both languages, and we're going to find out what the Dunmores actually did."

For a moment she thought he'd refuse. She braced for it — the warden's face closing like a shutter, the routine snapping back over him like armor, thirty years of habit saying *no, empty, sealed, nothing.*

Instead, Orsen Dunmore looked at the shelf of ledgers. Then at the hearth. Then, briefly, at the floorboard near the corner — a glance so quick most people would have missed it, but Ember had been raised by grandmothers, and grandmothers miss nothing.

"Third board from the corner," she said.

He stared at her. "How—"

"Your eyes went there twice. Guilty floors always get looked at twice." She pulled the blanket tighter and settled in, fiercely alive, furiously curious, angrier than ever and somehow hopeful anyway. "Go on, then. Open it. Let's find out what your family bought with our country."

He knelt. Pried up the board. And from the dark beneath it he drew out a packet wrapped in oilcloth, tied with cord gone soft with age, and on top of the packet, in faded ink, in a hand Ember recognized from the surviving pages of a burned prayer book, was written a single line:

*If you are reading this, the door has opened, and you must know what we were paid.*

Outside, the wind rose. And beneath the wind, low and steady and unmistakable, the Hollow Gate began — for the second time in thirty years — to hum.
