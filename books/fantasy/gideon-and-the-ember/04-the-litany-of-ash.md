## 4. The Litany of Ash

Orsen Dunmore had never been a man who enjoyed surprises, and so he had arranged his life to contain as few of them as possible. He woke at the same hour the sun did — a little before it, actually, because he liked the small satisfaction of being first. He ate porridge that was neither too thick nor too thin because he had spent thirty years learning exactly how much water a certain dented pot required. He inspected the Hollow Gate's seals in the same order every morning: north hinge, south hinge, the keystone, the threshold stone, and last of all the little carved face above the arch that he privately called Old Grumpy, because whoever had chiseled it had clearly been having a bad century.

The routine was the point. The routine was armor.

But this morning Orsen did not go to the gate.

This morning he sat at his father's desk with the prayer book open in front of him, and his hands were not steady, and he found himself thinking — absurdly — about the porridge pot, because it was easier than thinking about what was under the false bottom of the book.

Ember slept in the loft above him. He could hear her breathing, quick and light, the way children breathe when they have finally stopped being afraid for long enough to sleep deeply. She had eaten three bowls of stew the night before and fallen asleep mid-sentence, her mouth still shaped around a word in Old Vessani that he did not know but had written down on a scrap of paper by candlelight, like a schoolboy copying out a lesson.

*Vessany*, she had said. *You are the last Dunmore.* And then she had looked at him with such accusation in such a small face that he had nearly laughed, except that laughing would have meant admitting how badly his hands wanted to shake.

The gate had hummed. In thirty years it had never once hummed.

Orsen pressed his palms flat against the desk, took one long breath the way he did before reciting the litany, and lifted the prayer book's back cover away from its binding.

His father had been a careful man. Careful men hide things well, and Aldous Dunmore had hidden this so thoroughly that Orsen had lived under the same roof with it for nineteen years without ever suspecting. It was only now — knowing there was something to find, knowing the shape of the hole to look for — that he could see the seam where the leather had been split and re-glued, thin as a hair.

Inside lay papers. A dozen of them, folded into quarters, gone soft and brown at the edges like autumn leaves that had been handled too often. And beneath them, wrapped in oilcloth, a key.

Not an iron key. A bone-white sliver of something — antler, maybe, or old ivory — no longer than his thumb, drilled through with holes in a pattern that made his eyes ache if he tried to follow them. It was warm. That was the thing that made his stomach drop. Everything in the valley was cool; magic could not follow past the gate, and warmth of that particular kind belonged to the world outside, the world of lanterns and humming doors and mud-streaked children who walked out of countries that did not exist.

He unfolded the topmost paper. His father's handwriting — cramped, upright, the handwriting of a man who wrote as though someone might read over his shoulder, which, Orsen realized with a cold spreading feeling, someone always might.

*My son,* it began, and then stopped. There was a blot where the pen had rested too long. When the writing resumed, it was smaller, faster.

*If you are reading this, then I am dead and the seal has spoken to you, or something has come through, or both. I hoped it would be neither. I hoped you would keep the gate your whole life and die an old man who never learned why.*

*I will tell you plainly, because plainness is all I have left to give you.*

*Vessany was not destroyed.*

Orsen read the sentence four times. Each time it rearranged itself into something worse.

*The Chancery's purge was real. The burning of the archives, the unmaking of the roads, the killing of the singers — all of it happened, and I will not soften it for you. But the Chancery learned, as we learned, that a kingdom is not its land. A kingdom is its people. And you cannot burn people you cannot find.*

*In the last year of the purges, the survivors went into the hills beyond the Hollow Gate — into the country the maps call wilderness and the songs call home. The Chancery wanted them erased, not dead. Dead leave graves, and graves leave questions. So a bargain was struck. The Chancery would declare Vessany destroyed, strike it from every record, forbid its name. In exchange, a keeper would hold the door shut from the outside, so that no survivor could ever come out and testify to what had been done, and no inquisitor would ever need to go in and finish the work.*

*The keeper would be paid. We were paid, Orsen. Every year, on the first frost, a rider came with a purse and a list of names — names of the people inside, so that the Chancery could be sure the bargain held. I copied those lists. They are here. I could not bring myself to destroy them, because destroying them felt like agreeing that these people never existed, and they existed, boy. They sang. They argued. They made terrible wine. I heard them through the gate some nights, before the seals were fully set, and I have thought of their voices every day since.*

*They gave me gold and they took my silence, and I let them, because the alternative was a war we could not win and a massacre we could not stop. I told myself it was protection. Some nights I even believed it.*

*Do not believe it. Do not do what I did. If the gate ever speaks to you — if anything ever comes through it speaking the old tongue — then the bargain has broken on its own, and you owe the Chancery nothing. You never owed them anything. We were paid in silence, and silence is the one coin that spends worse the longer you hold it.*

*Forgive me. Or don't. But whatever you do, do not be quiet.*

Below the letter were the lists. Orsen unfolded them one by one, and each one was worse than the last, because each one was full of names — hundreds of names, whole families, whole streets, written in his father's hand with dates beside them. Not dates of death. Dates of *confirmation*. Once a year, for thirty years before Orsen took the wardenship, Aldous Dunmore had confirmed to the Chancery that the people of Vessany remained safely hidden, safely silenced, safely erased.

And after his father died? Orsen searched until his fingers went numb. There. A final page, newer, in his own hand — no. Not his own hand. A hand imitating his own, badly, the way a forger imitates a man whose letters he has only seen secondhand. Every year since Orsen had taken up the wardenship, someone had delivered confirmations in his name. He had signed nothing. He had known nothing. He had been a signature in someone else's ledger, a lock that walked and talked and believed itself free.

He sat very still for a long time.

Outside, the valley did what the valley always did. Wind moved through the pines. Somewhere down the slope a woodpecker knocked its idiot knocking. The village smoke rose straight and gray in the cold air, and none of it — not one leaf, not one bird, not one villager — knew that the ground beneath them was built on a lie the size of a kingdom.

Orsen discovered that he was angry. This surprised him. He had expected grief, and grief was there, sitting in his chest like a stone he'd swallowed. But underneath the grief, rising through it like water through chalk, was anger — hot and specific and entirely new. Thirty years. He had given thirty years to this valley, to this gate, to a litany he had recited every morning of his adult life without once being told what the words were *for*. He had memorized a muzzle and called it a duty. He had been proud of his silence. He had polished it daily.

*Paid in silence.* His father had at least known what he'd sold. Orsen hadn't even been told the price. He'd just been handed the coin and told it was honor.

He got up, because sitting still had become unbearable, and put the kettle on, because a man's hands needed to do something and the alternative was breaking things. While the water heated he laid the papers out on the table in order — letter, lists, more lists, a map drawn in faded ink showing the Hollow Gate and, beyond it, terraced hills and a river and a town marked with a name he recognized from the litany's ninth verse: *Corvane*. Corvane, capital of Vessany, city of bells. The maps said there was nothing there. The map in front of him drew the streets.

The kettle began to sing. Orsen stood looking at it and thought, with a strange floating clarity: *I have spent my life keeping a door shut on people who made terrible wine and sang in the evenings, and I called it faithfulness.*

Upstairs, Ember stirred. He heard the creak of the loft ladder, and then small bare feet on the floorboards, and then she appeared in the doorway wearing his spare shirt as a nightgown, sleeves rolled six times, hair like a crow that had lost an argument with the wind.

"You didn't sleep," she said. Her Common Tongue was getting better by the hour, which frightened him more than the Old Vessani had. Children soaked things up. Children were proof that nothing stayed sealed forever.

"No," he admitted.

"You found the papers." She wasn't asking. She padded to the table, climbed onto a chair, and looked at the spread of documents with an expression far too old for her face. "The lists."

"Yes."

"My grandmother's name is on the third one," Ember said. "Sera of the Lantern Terraces. She used to say a Dunmore wrote her name down every winter like counting sheep." She looked up at him. "She didn't mean it kindly."

"No," Orsen said. "I imagine she didn't." He poured hot water over the tea leaves — real tea, hoarded, meant for emergencies, and this qualified — and pushed the cup toward her. She wrapped both hands around it and blew on it with great ceremony, a child's ritual performed by a child's mouth while her eyes kept asking adult questions.

"What happens now?" she asked.

"I don't know yet."

"Grandmother says a man who doesn't know should say so quickly and then decide anyway."

"Your grandmother sounds wise."

"She's stubborn," Ember said, with deep approval. "It's the same thing."

Orsen almost smiled. Then, through the window over the sink, he saw the road.

The valley road ran along the river, single-file wide, rutted, and for thirty years it had carried precisely three kinds of traffic: the mail cart on Thursdays, shepherds in autumn, and nothing else. What it carried now was a horse. A tall gray horse, moving at a canter, ridden by a figure in a traveling cloak the color of wet slate, and behind the rider, at a rather less dignified pace, came two more horses carrying men in matching slate.

Chancery grey. Orsen had seen it exactly twice in his life, both times at funerals of Chancery officials passing through, both times from a distance. You did not forget that color. Grey was the color of ink, of ash, of things officially ended.

"Ember." His voice came out level, which he counted as a small miracle. "Go to the cellar."

Her head came up sharply. She had gone still in the way animals go still, and he remembered suddenly that she had walked alone out of a hidden kingdom, across a country, through a war's aftermath, at perhaps eight years old. Stillness like hers was earned.

"The cellar," she repeated.

"There's a loose stone behind the preserves, third shelf. Space beneath. Magic can't follow in this valley — nothing can find you here if you're quiet. Can you be quiet?"

"I was quiet for eleven days once," she said, already sliding off the chair. "Under a bridge. With a goose."

"A goose?"

"It was also hiding," she explained, as if this made everything clear, and disappeared down the hall. A moment later he heard the cellar door close with exaggerated softness.

Orsen folded the papers back into the prayer book, slid the book into the bread box — the one place in the house no Chancery clerk would ever look, being beneath their dignity — and stepped outside, pulling the door shut behind him.

The riders came up the track at an unhurried pace. The lead rider reined in at the gate of his yard, and Orsen saw that it was a woman — forty or so, weathered, with the calm eyes of someone who had asked many questions and been lied to many times and found the whole business mildly interesting. On her breast, worked in thread slightly darker than the cloak, was the Chancery seal: a closed book.

"Warden Dunmore," she said. Her voice was pleasant. That was somehow the worst part. "I'm Serah Vole, First Inquisitor of the Greywater Bench. I wonder if I might trouble you for a cup of tea."

Behind her, one of the men dismounted and began, without being asked, to walk a slow circuit of the house.

Orsen Dunmore, keeper of the Hollow Gate, keeper of thirty years of perfect silence, heard himself say:

"We've only just finished the pot."
