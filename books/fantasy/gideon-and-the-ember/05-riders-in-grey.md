## 5. Riders in Grey

Orsen Dunmore had faced many terrors in his thirty years as warden of the Hollow Gate. He had faced the winter the seals frosted over and he'd spent four days chipping ice from stone that was not, strictly speaking, cold. He had faced the year a wolf denned under his porch and he'd negotiated with it — badly — until spring. He had even faced the dream, three nights running now, of a door opening.

He had never faced a Chancery rider.

So when he saw them coming up the valley road at midmorning — three figures in grey, riding horses too fine for the Hollow's rutted track — his first instinct was to hide in his own house, which struck him, even as he did it, as profoundly undignified.

"They'll see the smoke," Ember said from the window, her voice flat with a child's terrible practicality. "You should have let me put out the porridge."

"The porridge is *breakfast*."

"The porridge is a *signal*." She turned to look at him, mud still dried in the creases of her knuckles despite two scrubbings, and Orsen felt the familiar lurch of unreality. Four days she had been in his house. Four days of Old Vessani prayers and questions he couldn't answer and a gate that hummed when she walked past it like a dog greeting its master. Four days of a life that had been quiet for thirty years suddenly full of noise and crumbs and a small furious person who ate like a starling and looked at him as though he owed her something.

Which, apparently, he did. He just didn't know what yet.

"Under the floor," he said. "The cellar. Now."

Ember crossed her arms. "I don't take orders from the last Dunmore."

"Then consider it a request from the only Dunmore, who is currently asking you *very nicely*."

Something in his voice must have landed, because she went — though not before snatching the heel of yesterday's bread off the table, which Orsen chose to interpret as trust rather than theft. She dropped through the trapdoor behind the pantry, and he pulled the rug over it, and then stood in the middle of his own kitchen feeling like a man who has hidden a stolen candle inside a lantern shop.

The knock came.

Three raps, measured, courteous. The kind of knock that expected an answer and would be polite about waiting for one, right up until it wasn't.

Orsen opened the door.

There were three of them, as he'd counted. Two soldiers in grey livery with the Chancery's ash-tree sigil stitched at the shoulder, faces blank as slate. And between them, a woman on a dun mare, also in grey but grey of better cut — a long coat, a scholar's satchel, and eyes the color of rainwater over iron. She was perhaps fifty, with silver threading her dark hair and the unhurried air of someone who had never once needed to raise her voice.

"Warden Dunmore," she said. Her accent was lowland Chancery, every word filed smooth. "Serah Vole. Inquisitor Second Rank. I hope we're not intruding."

"You are," said Orsen, whose talent for lying had always been roughly equivalent to his talent for dancing, which was to say: present in theory, catastrophic in practice.

Vole smiled. It was a good smile. That was the worst part.

"Honesty," she said. "How refreshing. May we water the horses? The road from Merrowfen is dust all the way."

*Merrowfen.* That was two valleys east, past the toll bridge, past the smuggler country where maps gave up and ballads took over. Two valleys was nothing. Two valleys was a morning's ride.

Orsen stepped aside because there was no version of this conversation in which he didn't.

* * *

They watered the horses at his trough. The soldiers did not speak. Vole spoke enough for six people, pleasantly, about the weather, the state of the high passes, the price of wool — and through all of it her eyes moved around Orsen's kitchen like a hand reading braille. The second bowl at the table. The small bootprints by the door that he had, in a fit of idiocy, swept into a neat pile rather than out of existence. The rug over the trapdoor, sitting perhaps half a finger-width prouder than rugs usually sat.

"You keep a clean house," she observed, "for a man who lives alone."

"I have standards."

"Mm." She accepted the cup of tea he offered because refusing would have been rude, and Orsen understood with sinking clarity that courtesy was her weapon of choice and she carried it the way other people carried knives. "Warden, I'll come to the point, since your honesty invites mine. The Chancery has received reports. Anomalous children. Children appearing in border valleys — the Weft, the Sorrowlands, now here — speaking a dead language. Old Vessani."

The word went through him like a splinter finding its depth. *Old Vessani.* Spoken aloud, in his kitchen, by a stranger. His father had died rather than say it above a whisper.

"Dead language," Orsen repeated. "Sounds dramatic."

"It is a dead language," Vole said, "because the country that spoke it is dead. Vessany fell two hundred years ago. Burned, scattered, struck from the rolls. A mercy, some historians call it — a kingdom so broken it could never again break others." She sipped her tea. "You'll forgive the history lesson. I find most wardens haven't had cause to think about Vessany."

"No cause at all," said Orsen, thinking about nothing else, thinking about it so loudly he half expected her to wince.

"And yet." She set down the cup. "The reports describe children walking out of wilderness that has no roads. Children who know things they shouldn't. The Chancery's position is simple: these children are confused, or orphaned, or worse — used, by remnant cults who nurse old grievances. We collect them. We place them. We ask gentle questions and we record the answers, and if the answers are nothing, we leave the valley in peace." Her rainwater eyes settled on him, mild as milk. "Have you seen such a child, Warden?"

Here is what Orsen Dunmore knew about himself: he was a man who had recited the same litany every dawn for thirty years without once understanding it, a man whose father had taught him silence so thoroughly that silence was the only fluent language he owned. He was not built for this. He was a lock built to hold, not a mouth built to speak.

"No," he said.

It came out approximately one octave too high.

Vole regarded him for a long moment. Somewhere below the floorboards, Orsen was certain he could hear Ember breathing, though he knew — he *knew* — that the cellar was the one place in the valley where sound didn't carry, where magic didn't reach, where his father had told him once, cryptically, *nothing can be found that hides below the hollow*. He had thought it meant the gate. He was beginning to suspect his father had meant the cellar all along, and had dug it for exactly this day, thirty years ahead of schedule, the way the elder Dunmore seemed to have done everything.

"No," Vole echoed pleasantly. "No child."

"Nope."

"How long have you lived alone?"

"Thirty years."

"And before that?"

"My father. Also alone."

"A family tradition." She rose, smoothing her coat, and Orsen's heart performed a maneuver he associated with falling off roofs. "May I see the rest of the house? It's procedure. I'm required to note the condition of every warden-station in the march. You'd be surprised how many have fallen into disrepair."

"There's really only the one room—"

"The door behind the pantry," said Vole.

"That's a cupboard."

"May I see the cupboard?"

Orsen looked at her. She looked back with the patience of a woman who could stand in his kitchen until the mountains eroded. Behind her, one of the soldiers shifted, and Orsen saw, for the first time, the coil of grey rope at the soldier's saddle — not rope, he realized. Chain. Fine-linked, silver-grey, coiled like a sleeping thing.

His father's papers were in that cellar. Hidden beneath the loose stone beside the wine rack, along with the prayer book in Old Vessani, along with everything that would turn *anomalous child* into *confirmed survivor*, and turn Orsen from eccentric warden into accessory.

"No," Orsen said.

And then, because his gift for lying was a gift that kept giving: "It's full of pickles."

* * *

Ember, in the dark, counted her breaths.

She had gotten good at counting breaths. Back home — back in Vessany, back *through* the gate, in the terraced valley where the lanterns burned green and everyone spoke her grandmother's words — the elders had taught the children stillness games. *Be the stone. Be the root. Be the dark under the stair.* She had hated them. She hated them more now, huddled between a wine rack and a wall that sweated cold, listening to boots cross the floor overhead like slow heartbeats.

The bread helped. She ate it in tiny bites, silently, the way the elders had taught, because a girl who couldn't be still might as well be loud.

Through the floor she could hear the woman's voice — smooth as river glass, saying pleasant things that were not pleasant. Ember had heard that kind of voice before, or voices like it, in the stories the elders told when they thought the children were asleep. The Chancery. The grey riders. *They came with lists,* Grandmother Sef had said, *and everything on their lists stopped existing.* Ember had thought it was a story, the way the song of the key was a story, the way the whole world outside the gate was a story.

Then she had walked out of the gate and found the last Dunmore burning porridge, and the stories had started becoming true one at a time, like lamps being lit down a long hall.

Above her, the floor creaked toward the pantry.

Ember went very still. Be the stone. Be the root. Be the dark.

But the dark, she discovered, was not empty. The dark was humming. Faintly, from everywhere and nowhere — from the walls themselves, from the packed earth, from the stone under her palm — came a vibration she recognized the way you recognize your own name. The same hum the gate made. The same note, buried deep, that ran under the whole valley like a river under ice.

Her father's voice, in memory: *The Hollow keeps what the Hollow holds, little ember. Nothing finds its way down here. Not light, not law, not—*

Not magic. That was what the elders whispered about this side of the gate. This valley was a blind spot, a hole cut out of the world's attention. Which meant the woman upstairs could sing every finding-song in the Chancery's books and get nothing back but her own echo.

Ember pressed her ear to the cold earth and grinned in the dark, fierce and silent, and finished the bread.

* * *

"—pickles," Vole repeated.

"Forty jars," said Orsen. "My father's recipe. I continue it in his memory. It's a whole thing."

For a moment — one shining, absurd moment — Orsen believed it had worked. Vole's expression flickered through something that might, in a less controlled face, have been astonishment.

Then she laughed. It was a warm laugh, genuinely warm, and somehow that was more frightening than anything else she'd done.

"Warden Dunmore," she said, "in nineteen years of service, you are the first man to defend a cellar with pickles. I almost admire it." She gathered her satchel. "Very well. I won't press. The Chancery respects private property; that's rather the foundation of everything we do." At the door she paused, one hand on the frame, and looked back at him with those rainwater eyes, and the warmth drained out of her face so completely and so quickly that it was like watching a lamp go out.

"But I will tell you what I tell every warden in the march," she said quietly. "Anomalous children are not punished. They are *helped*. What is punished — what cannot be helped, what must be ended — is concealment. A child hidden is a child being harmed, whether the hider knows it or not. Unfinished kingdoms breed wars, Warden. Grief kept secret ferments. If such a child comes to your door — and valleys like this one, doors like yours, are exactly where they come — you will send word to Merrowfen. And you will be thanked." She smiled again, and it reached her eyes this time, and that was worst of all, because the smile was sincere. "I would hate for anything to happen to you. You seem like a man who has suffered enough already."

"I pickle," said Orsen, "to cope."

She mounted her dun mare. The soldiers folded in behind her like ink closing over a written line, and the three of them went back down the valley road at an unhurried walk, taking their time, looking at everything — the treeline, the gate's mossed bulk in its hollow, the smoke of Orsen's ruined porridge still rising from the chimney.

Orsen watched them until the road bent and took them. Then he watched the road a while longer, in case the road produced more of them.

Then he went inside, threw back the rug, and opened the trapdoor.

Ember looked up at him from the dark, bread crumbs on her chin, entirely unrepentant.

"She bought the pickles," Orsen said.

"She absolutely did not buy the pickles."

"No," Orsen admitted. "She did not buy the pickles."

Ember climbed out, brushed herself off, and fixed him with the look she reserved for his most profound failures — a look, he was learning, with a great deal of range. "You said *nope*."

"I panicked."

"You said *nope* like a goose."

"Inquisitors make me nervous!"

"They should," Ember said, and for once there was no anger in it, just a child's flat certainty that made his stomach drop. "Grandmother Sef says the grey riders don't leave. They circle. They circle until they find the thing they're looking for, and then they stop circling." She picked a crumb off her sleeve and considered it. "She'll come back. Next time she'll bring more riders. And next time after that, she won't knock."

Orsen looked at the rug, at the trapdoor, at the cellar that his father had dug thirty years before anyone needed it — dug, and lined with whatever old craft kept it deaf and blind and dark. His father had known. His father had spent a lifetime preparing for a knock at the door, and had told his son nothing except *keep the litany, keep the silence, keep the gate.*

Well. The silence had just ridden away down the valley road wearing a scholar's coat.

"We can't stay here," Orsen heard himself say, and was surprised by his own voice — steady, decided, a stranger's voice. "She'll be back within the week. Maybe sooner." He looked at Ember, at this small furious person who had walked out of a sealed kingdom and into his porridge and turned his entire life into a lie with pickles on top. "Which means I need to know everything. What's behind the gate. Why they want you. What the song is for. All of it."

Ember tilted her head, studying him the way she studied unfamiliar food before committing.

"Why?" she asked. "Yesterday you wanted me gone."

"Yesterday I thought the gate was empty and my life was boring." He rubbed his face with both hands. "Now I know the gate hums, my father lied to me for thirty years, and the Chancery chains children. Boring is officially over."

Ember considered this for a long moment. Then she held out her hand, palm up, the way the elders did when striking a bargain — and Orsen noticed, with a small cold shock, that the lines of her palm were faintly marked, in old faded ink, with a spiral like a closed door seen from above.

"Then we have a deal, last Dunmore," she said. "But we start tonight. Because there's something you need to hear, and it needs the gate open a hand's width to hear it properly."

"Open the—" Orsen stared at her. "Absolutely not. The seal hasn't been cracked in two hundred years. There are *procedures*."

"There are *songs*," said Ember. "And one of them is mine."

Far to the east, down a road neither of them could see, Serah Vole paused at the valley's mouth and looked back once at the smoke rising thin from the keeper's house. Then she took out her journal, wrote a single line, and rode on toward Merrowfen — where, as it happened, the toll was truth, and she intended to pay it.
