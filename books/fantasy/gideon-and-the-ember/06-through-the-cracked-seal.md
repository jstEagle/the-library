## 6. Through the Cracked Seal

Ember had learned three things about Orsen Dunmore in the days since she'd walked out of the dark and into his life: he counted things when he was nervous, he made tea when he was frightened, and he talked to the gate when he thought no one was listening.

She was listening. She was always listening. Listening was how you stayed alive in a country that had been folded up and hidden away like a letter nobody wanted to read.

"You're doing it again," she said.

Orsen froze with his hand flat against the Hollow Gate's cold stone, mid-litany, lips still moving around a word that meant *remain* in Old Vessani. He turned slowly, the way a man turns when he hopes the thing behind him has gotten bored and left.

"Doing what."

"Talking to my door."

"It is not your door." He said it automatically, the way other people said *the sun rises in the east*. Then his ears went red, which they did whenever he caught himself being wrong about something he'd believed for thirty years. "It is the door of which I am keeper."

"Keeper of a door you've never opened all the way." Ember hopped up onto the low wall beside the gatehouse steps and swung her feet. "That's like being a baker who never eats bread. A shepherd afraid of sheep. My grandmother would have laughed at you for a week."

"Your grandmother," Orsen said carefully, "is a woman I have never met, from a country that does not exist, on the far side of a gate I have spent my whole life keeping shut. Forgive me if her opinion of my shepherding doesn't keep me up at night."

But his hand was still on the stone. That was the thing about Orsen — he lied with his mouth the way other people breathed, easy and constant, but his hands told the truth if you watched them. His hands wanted to open the gate. His hands had probably always wanted to open the gate, and thirty years of counting seals and reciting litanies had been one long argument between Orsen Dunmore and his own fingers.

Ember had won arguments with harder opponents than that. She'd once argued a river ferryman across a flooded ford by insisting, with total conviction, that she could swim. She could not swim. She'd crossed anyway.

"Open it a little," she said.

"No."

"A hand's width."

"No."

"I'll sing the key-song and you can stand there and count your seals and pretend it isn't happening, and then we'll both look through the crack, and then we'll close it, and you can write it down in your ledger under *irregularities* and have something interesting to think about for the next decade."

He looked at her then — really looked, the way he only did when he forgot to be careful. He had tired grey eyes and a face built for frowning, but underneath the frown there was a boyish sort of hunger, the hunger of a man who had been fed duty his whole life and had never once been offered wonder.

"The last time this gate moved," he said quietly, "I was four years old. My father put his hand over mine on the seal and told me that if I ever heard it hum again, I was to run to the village and fetch the priest, and neither of us was ever to speak of it." He paused. "The gate has been humming for six days, Ember. I have not run anywhere. I have not fetched anyone. I have stood here every night talking to a door."

"I know," Ember said. "You say *good evening* to it. It's very sweet. It doesn't understand you, though. It only speaks Vessani."

Something cracked across his face — half a wince, half a laugh. "Does it now."

"Everything here speaks Vessani, if you ask it properly. The stones. The water. The gate most of all. It's been waiting so long it's practically vibrating." She slid off the wall and held out her hand. "Come on, then, last Dunmore. Let's give it something to be happy about."

* * *

They waited for full dark, because Orsen insisted, and because Ember had learned that some of his insistences were worth honoring. The valley at night was a bowl of silence — no owls, she'd noticed, no foxes, nothing that magic might follow, as though the whole hollow had been swept clean of anything wild enough to remember. Only the gate remembered. Only the gate had ever been allowed to.

Ember pressed both palms to the seal and began the key-song.

It wasn't really a song, not the way the Chancery hymns were songs, all order and obedience. It was more like remembering out loud. Her mother had taught it to her in pieces, a phrase at a time, the way you teach a child to carry water without spilling — *this part is for the hinges, this part is for the lock, this part you must never sing alone.* Ember sang the hinge-part and the lock-part together, and the gate woke under her hands like an old dog hearing its name after years of strangers calling it something else.

The humming deepened. The runes along the arch — the ones Orsen dusted every morning without knowing what they said — began to glow a soft ember-orange, which Ember privately felt was the universe having a joke at everyone's expense.

"Step back," Orsen said, and then, absurdly, "mind the hinges," as though a gate sealed for three decades might swing on well-oiled iron.

The seal parted.

Not wide. A hand's width, exactly as promised — a thin vertical slice of darkness that wasn't darkness at all, because light came through it. Warm light. Lamplight. And a smell that hit Ember so hard she grabbed the doorframe: woodsmoke and baking bread and cut hay and river-water, the whole complicated smell of people living their lives, a smell she hadn't known she'd been starving for until it filled her mouth.

"Oh," she said. It came out very small.

Orsen leaned toward the crack. Ember watched him do it — watched a man who had spent thirty years being the wall lean toward the window. His breath fogged the cold air. Through the gap, past his shoulder, she saw it too:

A valley. Not this bare swept hollow, but a green one, terraced up the hillsides in silver-green steps, every terrace hung with lanterns, hundreds of lanterns, strung on wires and set in niches and floating — actually floating — above the fields like tame stars. Figures moved among them. Farmers, children, a woman shaking out a rug from an upper window, a man leading a mule down a lane that glowed faintly where its stones remembered being a road. Somewhere a fiddle was playing, badly, stopped, started again.

Vessany. Alive. Lantern-lit. Full.

"That's not possible," Orsen whispered. His voice did something strange in the middle of it. "There's no one left. There's no one — the records say—"

"Your records are ash and lies," Ember said, "and also there's the bread smell, which no lie ever managed."

She put her eye to the crack. Closer now, she could see faces — a girl her own age chasing a smaller boy across a bridge; old men playing a board game outside a tavern whose sign showed a door with wings. Home. All of it home. Her chest hurt with a feeling too big for the space it was given.

Then a bell rang.

Not a friendly bell. A short, hard, triple-strike, the kind of sound that makes mothers call children indoors. The fiddle stopped. Lanterns began going out along the nearest terrace, one by one, quick and practiced, and figures turned toward the gate — dozens of them, then scores, standing very still in the sudden dimness, all facing the thin bright line where two strangers' eyes were pressed to the crack in the world.

"They see us," Orsen breathed.

"Of course they see us. We're standing in their doorway holding it open like salesmen." Ember raised her voice and called through the gap in Old Vessani — *hello the valley, hello the hearths, hello the long-kept lights* — the greeting her mother had drilled into her, the greeting that was supposed to make any Vessani heart glad.

Nobody answered glad.

Instead, a woman came down the near terrace at a hard walk. She was grey-haired and broad-shouldered and carried a staff with a lantern set in its head, and behind her came two men with the particular stiff-legged stride of people who had trained for violence and hoped never to use it. The woman stopped ten paces from the gate and looked at Ember for a long moment — looked, Ember realized, not at her face but at her hands, at the way she held them, reading something in her stance the way Orsen read his ledgers.

"You're Maren's girl," the woman said at last. Her voice was rough as bark. "The singer. You were sent through the dark passage nine days ago and the passage closed behind you and we mourned you at the evening table." Her eyes flicked to Orsen, and everything soft in her face went away. "And you are the keeper's son."

"I am the keeper," Orsen said.

"You are the jailer's whelp," said one of the men behind her, "come to count our lanterns for your masters."

"I don't have masters."

"Everyone has masters. Yours simply pay in silence."

"*Tomas.*" The grey-haired woman didn't raise her voice, but Tomas shut his mouth as though it had been locked. She looked back at Orsen, and her gaze was the kind that weighed. "Thirty years, keeper. Thirty years you have fed your fire and trimmed your lamp and sung your father's litany on the near side of this stone, and in all that time you never once wondered what the litany was *for*?"

"I wondered," Orsen said. His voice was steady, but Ember saw his hands, and his hands were fists. "Every day of my life, madam, I wondered. Nobody answered."

"And now a child answers, and you open the door a crack and look in like a beggar at a wedding feast." She shook her head slowly. "Do you know what happens if the Chancery learns what stands behind this stone? Do you know what they did at Corvane? At Sennet Ford?" She let the names sit there. They clearly meant something to Orsen, because he flinched. "We are four hundred souls, keeper. Four hundred. We survived the purge because one Dunmore took their coin and buried us alive in the dark, and we have kept the bargain he made on our behalf because the alternative was graves. And now his son stands in our doorway with a child who should have come home singing, and the gate is *humming*, and there are riders on the far roads asking after anomalous children."

Ember's stomach dropped. "Riders? You can see the far roads?"

"We can see everything the gate sees. It is our gate too, girl, whatever the near side believes." The woman stepped closer, close enough that the lantern on her staff threw light through the crack and across Orsen's stunned face. "Here is what will happen. The child comes home, tonight, through this door, where she belongs. And you—" the staff tilted, just slightly, toward Orsen, "—you will reseal your gate, and trim your lamp, and go on being the last Dunmore, alone, exactly as your father arranged. And none of us will speak of your face."

"But—" Ember started.

"Maren's girl." The woman's voice gentled, just barely, and it was somehow worse than the harshness. "You did what you were sent to do. You found the key. Now give it to us and come inside, and in the morning you'll eat eggs and complain about your chores like any other child, and in a year you'll hardly remember the cold side of the door."

In a year she'd hardly remember. Ember looked at the woman — at the kind, terrible certainty in her face — and understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that these people loved her. That they had mourned her. That they would fold her up safe inside the hidden country and lose the key forever, and call it rescue.

Behind her, Orsen said nothing at all. She glanced back. He was looking through the crack at the lanterns — at all those living lights in a country he'd guarded without knowing it existed — and his expression was one Ember suspected he himself couldn't have named. It was the look of a man watching a feast through a window in winter. Not angry. Just certain, in the marrow-deep way of the long-practiced, that none of the chairs would be pulled out for him.

He didn't belong on the near side — the village feared him, the Chancery hunted him now, his house was a lie built over a lie. And he didn't belong on the far side either. He was the son of the man who'd sold them. He belonged nowhere at all, except in the exact width of a doorway, holding it open for other people to pass through.

"Well," Ember said loudly, into the ringing silence, "that's the rudest welcome-home I've ever had, and I once got bitten by a goose at my own naming-day."

"Ember—" Orsen began.

"No." She planted herself squarely in the gap, one shoulder against each side of the door, small and immovable as a keystone. "I walked nine days through the dark passage with a song in my teeth and no supper, and I am not going to be handed through a crack like a parcel and told to forget. And *he*—" she jerked a thumb at Orsen without turning around, "—is coming in too, or nobody's closing this gate, because I'll tell you a secret, madam: the key-song isn't a thing you can take off a person. It's in my bones. And while my bones are on the cold side of this door, your beautiful hidden valley is going to stay cracked open like a dropped egg."

The grey-haired woman's jaw tightened. Behind her, Tomas's hand drifted toward something under his coat.

And that was when the bell rang again — the hard triple-strike, twice more, urgent — and a runner came pelting down the terraces shouting words that made every lantern on the hillside gutter at once:

*Riders. Grey cloaks. On the far road — and they've brought a truth-reader.*

Through the crack, past the woman's shoulder, Ember saw the lanterns begin to go out — all of them now, terrace by terrace, the whole remembered country snuffing itself dark — and felt Orsen's hand close gently around her arm.

"Ember," he said quietly. "I think we may have just introduced them."
