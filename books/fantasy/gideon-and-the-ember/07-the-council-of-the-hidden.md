## 7. The Council of the Hidden

The other side of the Hollow Gate smelled like rain on warm stone and woodsmoke from a hearth someone had tended for thirty years without interruption.

Orsen stood in the doorway — the actual doorway, the thing he had polished and prayed at and kept sealed his whole adult life — and felt like a man who had spent decades guarding a locked box only to discover it was a window.

Terraces climbed the far valley in green steps, each one lit by paper lanterns strung on wires that hummed faintly in the evening air. Houses of pale timber stacked themselves up the slopes like a child's careful blocks. Below, along a river he had no name for, people moved between gardens with baskets on their hips, unhurried, ordinary, alive.

"Close your mouth," Ember said. "You'll catch moths."

He closed his mouth. It did not help much. "There are hundreds of you."

"There are thousands of us." She said it the way she said most things — as an accusation wrapped in a fact. "You counted wrong your whole life, last Dunmore."

"I never counted anything. I was told there was nothing to count."

"Yes," Ember said, with a bitterness far too old for her small mud-streaked frame. "You were *told*."

She took his sleeve — she had taken to doing that, gripping him like a fisherman grips a rope in weather — and led him down a path of flat stones toward the largest building in view: a long hall with a roof of blue-grey tile, its eaves carved with birds Orsen half-recognized from his father's forbidden prayer book. Lanterns were being lit inside. Word of their arrival had clearly traveled faster than their feet.

At the door, two guards crossed spears in front of them. Not hostile, exactly. Careful. The way one is careful with weather that might turn.

"They speak Old Vessani," Ember told him, in the common tongue of the valley, which was apparently also her tongue when she chose. "Don't say anything stupid."

"That's a great deal to ask of me this evening."

For the first time since he'd met her, Ember almost smiled. Then the doors opened, and the smile went wherever children keep such things.

* * *

The council chamber was round, which struck Orsen as sensible; he had spent his life in square rooms built by people who liked corners to hide in. Nine elders sat in a crescent on low chairs, and behind them, on the wall, hung a tapestry so old its colors had gone soft as watercolor: a kingdom rendered in thread, with a gate at its edge and a figure standing before it holding a lamp.

A figure wearing, unmistakably, the Dunmore coat.

"You are the son," said the eldest, a woman with white braids coiled like bread dough and eyes the color of river slate. She spoke the common tongue slowly, precisely, each word set down like a coin on a table. "I am Maren Ostrevi. I sat in this room when your father was younger than you are now. Sit."

Orsen sat. Ember did not sit; she stood beside his chair with her arms crossed, radiating the particular fury of a child who has been told to wait outside and has declined.

"You knew my father?" Orsen asked.

"We knew what he was paid to be." Maren let that settle. Around the crescent, the other elders watched him with expressions Orsen could not sort into categories — grief, suspicion, weariness, and in one young man's face, something dangerously close to hope. "Warden. Keeper. The Chancery had many polite words for jailer."

"The purge ended," Orsen said. His father's papers had told him that much, in his father's cramped hand: *the burning stopped, but the forgetting did not.* "Thirty years ago. The edicts were rescinded. There's no one left to hunt you."

The elders exchanged glances, and one of them — a heavyset man with ink-stained fingers, a scribe by the look of him — made a sound that was not quite a laugh.

"Child," Maren said, "do you know why we are still here?"

"Because going back is hard. Because you've built lives—"

"Because they would kill us," said the scribe simply, "and then burn the evidence, and there is no tribunal left that remembers there was ever anything to testify about. The purge ended because it succeeded everywhere else. We are the loose thread. And the Chancery does not leave threads."

Orsen thought of Serah Vole's grey riders on the valley road, her polite questions about anomalous children. He thought of how carefully she had not threatened him. That was the thing about the Chancery, his father had written. They never threaten. They schedule.

"Vole knows about Ember," he said. "She came asking before Ember ever reached my door. Anomalous children. Plural."

"Then the gate has leaked more than once," Maren said grimly. "Small leaks. A song heard through stone. A dream. The seal was never perfect — your father maintained it well, but well is not forever." She leaned forward. "Which brings us to why you are here, warden's son, and why you will not enjoy hearing it."

"I suspected as much when nobody offered me tea."

Someone at the end of the crescent — an ancient man who might have been asleep — snorted. Orsen decided to count him as an ally.

"The bargain," Maren continued, "was this. The Dunmores keep the gate sealed. In exchange, the Chancery leaves the valley alone and pays a pension that your family has collected for three generations. Your father believed, when he was young, that he was protecting us. By the end, I think he understood he was keeping us buried. Either way, the terms have not changed. Seal the gate. Keep the silence. Live out your life comfortable and alone."

"And now?"

"Now the seal is cracked, a child walked through it carrying a key-song in her mouth, and a Chancery inquisitor is camped on the other side asking questions." Maren's voice did not rise, but the room grew colder anyway. "So we require you to do your family's work one final time. Reseal the gate. All the way. Permanently. And the child stays here, where she belongs, where she is safe."

The words went into Orsen like a splinter, sliding clean past skin and muscle to lodge somewhere he couldn't reach.

"She doesn't want to stay."

"She is nine."

"Ten," said Ember.

"She is ten," Maren agreed, without looking at her, "and she was sent through that gate by her mother with a song in her head and no explanation, because her mother believed the world outside had changed. Her mother was wrong. The world outside is the same world that burned the temple at Sarrowfen and salted the orchards at Ilvet and wrote *no such place* across every map. The girl is safe here. Safe is the whole of what we have left to give our children."

"She can sing the gate open," Orsen said. "That's what the key-song is. If she stays, you're not sealing a door. You're sealing a voice."

"A voice that leads inquisitors to us," said the scribe sharply. "Every year she spends out there, singing stones awake, making roads remember themselves — every year is another chance for grey riders to follow the music home."

"She lost nearly everything getting here," said a thin elder near the end, a woman whose hands were twisted with age. "Let her choose."

"She chose to run through a cracking seal into a stranger's arms," Maren said. "Children choose badly. That is why they are given to councils."

Ember made a sound then, small and furious, and Orsen put a hand on her shoulder before she could launch herself at the eldest elder of a hidden nation. She trembled under his palm like a kettle deciding whether to whistle.

"With respect," Orsen said, and found, to his surprise, that he meant it — respect was easier to feel in this room than it had ever been in any Chancery office — "I didn't come here to reseal anything. I came because a child knocked on my door speaking a language I'd only ever seen in a forbidden book, and my gate hummed for the first time in thirty years, and I discovered that everything I kept was a lie I was paid to maintain." He stood. His knees complained. He ignored them. "I'm the last Dunmore. The bargain dies with me if I say it does. And I'm saying it does."

The silence that followed had texture. It pressed against him like deep water.

"You would unmake thirty years of safety," Maren said quietly, "in an evening. Because a child looked at you accusingly."

"Because I read my father's letters," Orsen said. "Because I stood on the other side of my own door and saw lanterns. Because I have spent my entire life being the lock on a cage and calling it a calling." He looked around the crescent, at the tapestry, at the lamp-bearing Dunmore stitched into its border. "You want safety. So do I. But you're asking me to buy yours with her freedom, and I've seen what this family buys with other people's freedom. It comes with a bill. Thirty years late, but it comes."

The old man at the end of the crescent opened one eye. "I like him," he announced, to no one in particular.

"Hush, Pellam," said three elders at once.

Maren studied Orsen for a long moment. Outside, through the high windows, the first stars were coming out over terraces full of people who had never once, in thirty years, been able to go home.

"You misunderstand us if you think we enjoy this," she said at last. "We are not cruel. We are *careful*. Cruelty is quick; carefulness takes generations. Every person in this valley lost someone to the purges. Some of us watched the smoke from the hills while the Chancery's clerks recorded our names as deceased. We will not walk out into that again on the strength of one warden's change of heart and one child's stubbornness."

"Then don't," Orsen said. "Walk out, I mean. Stay. Stay hidden, stay safe, stay however long you need. But leave the gate able to open. Leave the choice where choices belong — with the people making them. Seal it against soldiers if you must. Don't seal it against songs."

"The Chancery will burn this valley," the scribe said flatly. "You've met Vole. You know what she is."

"I know she asks polite questions," Orsen said. "And I know that polite questions are how it started last time, too."

Something moved through the crescent at that — not agreement, exactly, but a loosening, like ice shifting on a river. Maren closed her eyes. When she opened them, she looked, for just a moment, very tired, and very old, and like a woman who had been holding up a ceiling for thirty years and had forgotten ceilings could be set down.

"The council will deliberate," she said. "You will be given a room. The child will be given a room. Separately."

"I'd rather—"

"*Separately,*" Maren said, with the mild iron of a woman who had out-argued better men than him, "so that the council may speak with her without a Dunmore standing over her shoulder teaching her defiance. Which, I note, she hardly needs."

Ember lifted her chin. "I'm not staying."

"You are ten."

"Ten," Ember confirmed, "and I already ran away from one council. I have references."

Two guards appeared to escort them, gently, immovably. As Orsen was led down the hall toward a guest room that smelled of cedar and clean linen — the first bed not his own that he had slept in since boyhood — he passed a window, and stopped.

Below, in the lamplight of a lower terrace, children were playing a game with a knotted string, chanting the counting rhyme his father had whispered once, drunk, and wept over, and never explained. *Oren, doren, selvi-may.* Their voices rose in the warm dark, unafraid, certain that the night belonged to them.

Orsen pressed his hand to the cool glass.

They wanted him to close the door on that. To make the rhyme forbidden again, the voices contraband, the whole bright valley a secret kept by a dead man's signature and a pension paid in silence.

He was still standing there when he saw them: far off, beyond the valley's mouth, on a ridge that should have held nothing but wilderness — three points of light moving in formation. Too steady for stars. Too disciplined for shepherds.

Grey riders did not camp. They advanced.

Down the corridor, faint but unmistakable, he heard Ember begin to sing.
