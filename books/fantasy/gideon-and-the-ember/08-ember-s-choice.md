## 8. Ember's Choice

The council chamber smelled of beeswax and old decisions.

Ember sat on a stool too small for her in the middle of a ring of elders who had been talking about her for two hours as though she were a weather pattern. A difficult one. A storm front that had blown in from the wrong side of the world and would need to be managed.

"—and the girl will be settled by spring," Elder Maren was saying, in the round, patient voice of someone reading a verdict they'd written before the trial. "The eastern terraces have room. She'll learn a trade. In time, she'll forget—"

"I won't."

The word came out smaller than Ember wanted it to. Everything in Vessany made her feel small — the lantern-lit terraces stacked up the valley walls like a wedding cake built by giants, the sky that wasn't quite sky, the way everyone spoke Old Vessani so *easily*, without the careful, chewed-out syllables Orsen used. She'd dreamed of this place her whole life without knowing it existed. Now that she was here, it kept trying to fold her up and put her in a drawer.

Maren paused, the way you pause for a child interrupting at dinner. "You will learn, little wren. That is what children do."

"My name is Ember." She stood up off the stool, which put her eye level with exactly no one. "And I'm not staying. I was never supposed to stay. I was sent *out*."

A murmur went around the ring like wind through wheat. Old Corvath, whose beard could have sheltered a family of four, leaned forward. "Sent out. By whom?"

"By my mother." Ember's throat tightened, but she pushed through it, because she'd rehearsed this on the long dark walk and if she stopped now she'd cry, and crying in front of the council would prove their point. "Before the fever took our camp. Before the Chancery riders found us outside. She taught me the key-song and she said, *you go to the Hollow Gate and you find the keeper, because the door only opens for a Dunmore's ear and a Vessani voice.* She said it like it was the most important thing anyone had ever said, and then she said, *and Ember — don't let them put you back in the box.*"

She looked around the ring, at all those kind, tired, frightened faces.

"And here you are," she said, "with a box."

* * *

They gave her honey cakes after that, which Ember felt was an insult dressed as kindness. They gave her a room in Maren's house with a window that looked out on terraces glowing gold under lantern-light that never dimmed and never dawned, and a bed softer than anything she'd slept on in her life, and she lay on it fully clothed and stared at the ceiling and thought about boxes.

Here was the thing nobody in this beautiful buried country seemed to understand: she had *been* outside. She'd walked through the purge-country with ash in her hair and her mother's song stitched into her memory like a second heartbeat. She'd found Orsen Dunmore — the last Dunmore, tall and sad and stiff as a fence post, reciting his litany to a door that hummed when she sang. He'd looked at her like she was a miracle and a catastrophe at the same time, and then he'd fed her stew, badly, and asked her a hundred questions in a language he clearly loved and barely understood.

He didn't belong out there either. That was the joke of it. The keeper of the door belonged on neither side of it, and neither did she, and somehow between the two of them they made almost one whole person who did.

"You're not sleeping."

Ember rolled over. Maren's granddaughter, Sefa — nine years old, gap-toothed, fearless — was sitting cross-legged on the floor beside the bed, watching her with the frank interest of the very young.

"I'm thinking," Ember said.

"About leaving."

"About how everyone keeps deciding things for me." Ember sat up. "Do you like it here?"

Sefa shrugged. "It's safe."

"It's a cellar," Ember said, and immediately felt bad, because Sefa's whole world was this place, and calling it a cellar was like calling someone's mother ugly. She softened. "It's a very nice cellar. But my mother didn't teach me a song so I could grow beans under a fake sky. She taught me so the door could open. Both ways. For everybody."

Sefa considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. "Elder Corvath says the outside eats people."

"Elder Corvath hasn't been outside in sixty years."

"Have you?"

Ember grinned despite herself. "I'm nine years older than you and I've been outside my whole life. It has bees. And rain that comes from actual clouds. And a keeper who makes stew like he's punishing it."

Sefa giggled, then clapped a hand over her mouth, glancing at the door. Then, in a whisper: "If you run, they'll catch you at the gate. There are watchers."

"Not tonight there aren't." Ember had spent three days learning the rhythms of the hidden valley, and its rhythms were the rhythms of people who had been hiding for eighty years: predictable, careful, asleep early. "Tonight everyone's at the vigil for the seal."

"The vigil you caused."

"The vigil I *caused*," Ember agreed warmly, and swung her legs off the bed.

* * *

Getting down through the terraces was easier than getting into the council chamber had been, mostly because Sefa came with her, walking point through lanes she knew like her own teeth, hushing chickens with a look that suggested the chickens had learned respect. Ember felt a pang about leaving the girl behind — but Sefa belonged here, really belonged, and besides, someone had to feed the chickens a story about where Ember went.

The gate passage smelled of cold stone and, faintly, of woodsmoke from the far side. Orsen's side. Her chest did something complicated at that. His side meant him, and him meant the strange, stubborn, gentle man who had taken in a mud-streaked stranger because it was the decent thing even though decency was actively trying to kill him.

The gate itself stood ahead, a seam of darkness in the living rock, sealed to a hand's width — the width Orsen had opened for her, the width the council had raged about and then, in their endless caution, left alone rather than risk the noise of closing it fully while the Chancery sniffed around the valley road.

Ember pressed her palm against the cool stone and sang.

Just a thread of it. The first phrase of the key-song, the one her mother had drilled into her until she could sing it backwards, upside down, mid-sneeze. The stone warmed under her hand. The seam breathed. Somewhere deep in the rock, something vast turned over in its sleep and sighed.

*I was never sent home,* she thought, watching the crack widen by a finger's width, a knuckle's width. *I was sent through.*

That was the part the council couldn't hear. The gate didn't want keeping anymore. It wanted opening — she could feel it in her teeth, in the song's hum in her bones, the way you can feel a held breath wanting out. Eighty years of hiding, and the country behind the door had grown strong enough to be born. It just needed a voice on both sides of the threshold. A Vessani voice to call it, and —

And a Dunmore's ear. Which meant Orsen. Which meant going back through, alone, small, nine years old and armed with a song, into a valley where grey riders were already asking polite questions.

Her mother's voice, in memory: *Don't let them put you back in the box.*

"No," Ember said aloud, to the stone, to the council, to the whole buried, beautiful, frightened country. "I'm not the treasure. I'm the key. And keys don't live in drawers."

She squeezed through the gap sideways, ribs scraping ancient rock, and dropped onto the cold flagstones of the Hollow Gate's antechamber, on the world's honest side, where the sky was real and the dark was just dark.

Behind her, the seam whispered shut to a hairline.

Ahead of her, torchlight was coming up the valley road.

* * *

She found Orsen halfway down the hill from the keeper's house, running, which for Orsen meant moving at the speed of a very anxious wardrobe. When he saw her he stopped so abruptly he nearly fell over his own coat.

"Ember." His voice cracked down the middle. "Ember, the council — I told them — I said I'd bring you back myself, I only needed — you can't be *here*, there are riders, Vole's riders, I saw them at the turnpike, six of them, maybe more, they're coming up the valley road right now—"

"I know." She caught his sleeve before he could start pacing. "Orsen. Listen. I'm not going back."

He blinked at her. Of everything in the collapsing evening, this appeared to be the piece that wouldn't fit. "Not — the council expects—"

"The council expects a lot of things. They expect me to grow beans under their lantern-sky and forget my mother's name and thank them for the drawer." She was shaking now, and let herself shake; there was no time to be dignified. "I was sent *out*, Orsen. On purpose. With the key-song. Because the gate doesn't open from hiding — it opens from hope, and they've been rationing hope for eighty years until it's gone thin as broth. Somebody has to stand on your side of the door and sing toward them. Somebody has to be the reason."

"Ember—"

"They'll hate it. They'll hate *you*. They already do, a bit, because you're the keeper and keepers are the lock." She gripped his sleeve harder. "But you opened the gate a hand's width for a stranger's child, and that's the most hopeful thing anyone in either country has done in eighty years, and I am *not* letting them seal it over your head. Over mine. Not again. My mother didn't die so I could be furniture."

For a long moment Orsen just looked at her, torchlight from the village below flickering on his face, and Ember watched thirty years of litany and duty and loneliness rearrange themselves behind his eyes into something new and terrified and awake.

"You sound like your mother," he said finally, hoarsely.

"You remember her?"

"I remember everyone who ever passed the gate." He swallowed. "Your mother sang to the stones once, when you were a baby. I heard it through the seal. I wrote it down phonetically and got half of it wrong and wondered about it for eight years."

Ember laughed — a wet, cracked laugh, but a laugh. "You got it wrong. It goes—" and she hummed the phrase, and Orsen's face did the thing it always did when the old tongue landed right on him, like a man hearing his own name called across water.

Down in the valley, a horn blew. Once, twice. Then a third time, longer, uglier.

Orsen went still. "That's not the village horn. That's — they're not asking anymore. They're *coming*."

Torchlight was boiling up the valley road now, not a line of it but a spill, riders in grey fanning out across the lower fields, and beneath the hoofbeats came a sound Ember had heard once before, outside, the night her camp burned: a low chanted cadence, rhythmic and wrong, the Chancery's null-hymn, the song that ate songs. She felt it hit the valley like a wave hitting a seawall. The grass flattened. A bird fell silent mid-note. Somewhere behind them, faint through the rock, the hidden valley's lantern-glow guttered.

"They've brought a chanter," Orsen breathed. "They've never — in thirty years they've never come past asking—"

"Because of me." Ember's stomach dropped, but underneath the fear something else was rising, hot and stubborn. "Because I got out. Because I'm proof. Vole isn't hunting a child, Orsen. She's hunting a *loophole*."

"Then we get you back through—"

"*No.*" It came out of her like a struck bell, louder than she'd ever been, and the gate-stone behind them actually rang in answer, a soft harmonic shiver through the rock. Orsen stared at her. Ember stared at the stone. Slowly, deliberately, she planted her feet on the flagstones of the antechamber, facing down the valley, facing the torchlight, and drew a breath big enough to sing with.

"Then we do it now," she said. "All the way open. Tonight. While they're still riding and the hymn hasn't reached us and the door still remembers my voice. Sing with me, keeper. Your half's the ear. Mine's the call. That's how it works — that's how it always worked, before everyone got scared."

"Ember, if we open it and they're here—"

"Then they see a door open," Ember said, "and a country step out of it. Let them write *that* report."

The first rider cleared the tree line below the house, close enough now that she could see the grey of his cloak and the white mask-plate of the Chancery, and behind him another, and another, and the null-hymn rolling up the hillside like a tide that intended to take the whole shore.

Ember lifted her chin, filled her lungs, and began to sing the key-song at full voice — not a thread this time, not a whisper through a crack, but the whole blazing first verse, loud enough to hurt, loud enough to answer a hymn.

The Hollow Gate began, slowly, joyfully, to open.

And Orsen Dunmore stepped up beside her, put his hand flat against the singing stone, and joined in — off-key, half-wrong, and absolutely unafraid.
