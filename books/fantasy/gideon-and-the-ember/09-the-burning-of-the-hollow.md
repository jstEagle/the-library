## 9. The Burning of the Hollow

The smoke reached them before the fire did.

Orsen saw it first as a smudge against the western sky, a grey thumbprint where the valley's evening should have been gold. He had been teaching Ember to skip stones on the millpond — badly, on both their parts — and she stopped mid-throw, stone still pinched in her small fingers, and turned her head like a deer catching a scent.

"That's your house," she said.

It was not a question. Children who have walked out of sealed kingdoms do not ask questions they already know the answers to.

They ran.

* * *

The keeper's house had stood for three generations of Dunmores, and it burned in eleven minutes.

Orsen learned this because he counted. It was the sort of thing his mind did when his heart refused to participate — he counted the minutes from the moment they crested the ridge and saw the flames standing up out of the roof like orange wheat, and he counted until the eastern wall folded inward with a sound like a giant sighing, and the total was eleven. Somewhere in those minutes went forty years of litany records, his father's ledgers, the seal-rubbing tools worn smooth by his grandfather's hands, the chair by the window where he took his morning tea and pretended, every single morning for thirty years, that this was enough of a life.

He did not cry. He noted that too, distantly, the way one notes weather. *No rain today.*

"They're still there," Ember whispered. She was pressed against his side behind a hawthorn at the top of the pasture, her fingers hooked into his sleeve with surprising strength. Below, in the yard, figures moved through the firelight — grey cloaks, the pale glint of Chancery buckles. Four of them. And apart, watching the house burn with her hands clasped behind her back like a woman admiring a garden, stood Serah Vole.

"She didn't even knock," Orsen said. His voice came out strange and flat.

"You were expecting manners?"

"I was expecting *questions*. That's what she does. She asks polite questions and writes down your answers and then—" He stopped. The eastern wall folded again, and something inside it that might have been his father's bookcase made a sound like a snapped bone. "Then she does this."

Ember looked up at him. In the light of the burning house her face was half gold and half shadow, and her expression was older than her body by several centuries. "You're doing the thing," she said.

"What thing?"

"The counting thing. You do it when you're sad. You count things." She said it with the gentle brutality only children can manage. "You counted the stairs last night. You counted the spoons."

"I did not count the spoons."

"Seventeen," said Ember. "You said it out loud in your sleep."

Behind the hawthorn, the most celebrated warden in the history of the Hollow Gate briefly put his face in his hands.

* * *

They could not go back for anything, which was fortunate, because there was nothing left to go back for. But Orsen found himself cataloguing anyway, helplessly: the kettle. The good boots. The prayer book — no, he had the prayer book, it was in his coat, it had been in his coat since the night Ember knocked, and he touched its shape through the wool like touching a wound to be sure it still hurt.

The litany records, though. The records were ash now, or would be by morning. Thirty years of names and dates and seal-inspections, his own careful hand marching across page after page, and every line of it a lie he hadn't known he was telling. *Gate secure. No anomalies. All quiet in the Hollow.* He had written *all quiet* four thousand times. He wondered if Vole had come specifically for the records, or if burning them was simply tidiness — the way you sweep a floor after sweeping away the family that lived on it.

A shout rose from the yard. One of the riders had found tracks — theirs, from the afternoon, along the pond path — and Vole turned her head toward the hills with the unhurried precision of a compass needle.

"Move," Orsen breathed.

They moved.

* * *

The hills above the valley were sheep country, which meant they were stone country, which meant they were country where two fugitives could move without leaving much of anything behind except curses. Orsen knew these paths the way he knew the litany — by rote, by thirty years of solitary walking, by all the evenings he'd climbed here to avoid the village and its sideways looks. He had resented these hills once, he realized. Now they were the only thing in the world that had never lied to him.

They climbed until the fire below was a red coin, then an ember, then a memory. The moon came up fat and indifferent. Ember, who had walked out of a hidden kingdom alone at what age? eight? nine? — kept pace without complaint, but around midnight her steps began to drag, and Orsen caught her elbow before she caught the ground.

"Here." There was a shepherd's hut below the second tor, abandoned so long the door had fallen off and the sheep had opinions about the interior. He cleared a corner with his boot, spread his coat, and sat her down on it. "Sleep. I'll watch."

"You always say that," she mumbled, already horizontal.

"I've only said it twice."

"You'll always say it." Her eyes closed. "You're a keeper. Keeping is the whole thing you know how to do."

He sat in the doorway with his back against the jamb and watched the valley glow faintly below, and thought about that. *Keeping is the whole thing you know how to do.* Was it a compliment? An accusation? With Ember it was generally both, delivered in the same breath, like weather from two directions at once.

Somewhere past the third hour, grief finally arrived — not the numb counting-grief of the ridge, but the real kind, sudden and total, like stepping off a stair that wasn't there. His house. His father's house. His father's *grandfather's* house. Every morning tea, every winter fire, every night he'd lain awake listening to the gate hum and told himself the hum was nothing. Burned. And him officially, formally, irreversibly an outlaw, his name presumably already inked onto some Chancery list between horse thieves and worse.

He waited for the grief to crush him.

It didn't. It sat on his chest like a cat — heavy, warm, oddly companionable — and beneath it, shamefully, unmistakably, he felt something else.

Lightness.

"Oh no," he said quietly, to nobody.

*What,* said the sleeping shape on his coat, who was apparently not sleeping at all.

"Nothing. Go back to sleep."

"You said 'oh no' like a man having a realization he doesn't like." She rolled over and squinted at him. Her hair was full of straw. She looked, he thought, like exactly what she was: a child sent out of paradise to save it, currently sleeping in a sheep hut. "Tell me."

"It's nothing. It's inappropriate."

"I'm eight. I don't know what inappropriate means, which makes me the perfect audience."

He looked out at the dark valley. "I'm relieved," he said. "The house is gone and I'm — relieved. Thirty years I kept that place like it was keeping *me*, and I hated half of it, and I told myself the hate was loyalty, and now it's smoke and I feel—" He searched for the word and failed, and settled for honesty. "*Lighter.* What kind of man feels lighter when his home burns?"

Ember considered this with the gravity of a magistrate.

"The kind whose home was also a cage," she said. "We had a word for it. In Vessany. When someone grieves a thing and is free of it at the same time." She yawned enormously. "It takes four words to say properly. You're not ready. Ask me when you've slept."

"Do you ever just *not* have an answer?"

"No," said Ember, and was asleep before the echo settled.

* * *

Dawn found them higher, moving north along the spine of the hills where the bracken grew tall enough to hide a man and a child if the man remembered to crouch. Orsen had a plan, of sorts, and the plan was: away. It was not a good plan. It had the structural integrity of wet bread. But the alternative plans all ended in Chancery cells or resealing the gate, and he had spent one night thinking about resealing that gate with Ember inside it, and his stomach had rejected the idea the way a body rejects poison.

Below and behind, the valley smoked gently. From this distance it looked almost peaceful. He could see the village — his village, the village that had feared him politely for decades — and tiny figures moving in the lanes, and he understood with a lurch that by tonight the story would be fixed and polished: the warden had been a traitor all along, harboring an anomalous child, and the Chancery had saved everyone. Some of them might even believe it gratefully. Fear makes excellent soil for that kind of story.

"They burned the records," Ember said. She'd been quiet all morning, walking in his shadow, and when she spoke it startled a grouse into blasphemy. "Did you see? They carried the chest out first and burned it separate. In the yard. Vole watched it herself."

Orsen stopped walking. "You saw that?"

"I see everything. It's very tiring." She kicked a stone off the path with unnecessary violence. "Why burn paper first? Paper isn't dangerous. Houses aren't dangerous. Only what's *written in* the paper."

And Orsen, who had spent the night feeling lighter, felt the lightness tilt suddenly into vertigo. Because she was right, and he should have seen it. Vole hadn't burned the house in rage. She'd burned it in *editing*. The litany records weren't a warden's logs — he'd known that since the cellar, since the papers, since the truth about his father's bargain. They were evidence. Thirty years of dates and seal-states and supply entries that proved, in his own dead father's hand, that the Hollow Gate had opened regularly, received goods, sheltered a living people. Proof that Vessany survived. Proof that the purge had failed.

Vole wasn't destroying a crime scene.

She was destroying the crime's *witnesses* — starting with the paper ones.

"We can't stop walking," Orsen said aloud, mostly to organize his own thoughts. "But we need to understand where we're going. Ember. The songs. Your songs — the ones about the road, the old country. Are they directions? Actually directions?"

Ember looked up at him, and for once there was no cleverness in her face at all, just a child's fierce, frightened hope.

"You want to learn them?" she said. "The old tongue? Truly?"

"I memorized a litany for thirty years without knowing what it meant," Orsen said. "I think I can manage a few songs."

She grinned — the first grin since the fire, crooked and incandescent — and grabbed his hand, and pulled him north, uphill, into a country that existed only in ballads, while behind them the smoke of everything he'd lost thinned into the ordinary blue of morning.

"Lesson one," she announced. "There's a song for rivers. But you sing it wrong, rivers get *ideas*."

"...Ideas."

"Very bad ones. Walk faster."

Far to the south, small on the valley road, a single grey rider turned north and began, unhurriedly, to follow.
