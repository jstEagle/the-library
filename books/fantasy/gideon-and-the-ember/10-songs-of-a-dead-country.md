## 10. Songs of a Dead Country

The maps called it the Thornwaste. Ember had seen one once, in the Chancery schoolroom in Corvane, before anyone realized what she was — a great blank stretch of parchment colored the dull yellow they used for places where nothing worth taxing grew. *Here be scrub and stone,* the mapmaker had written, with the smugness of a man who had never bothered to look.

Ember looked now, from the crest of a hill that the maps insisted did not exist, and saw orchards.

They were old orchards, gone wild, apple trees grown tall and leggy as herons, but they marched in rows down the valley below in patterns no accident could make. Terraces cut the hillsides in long silver curves. A road ran along the valley floor — not a track, not a suggestion of a road, but an actual road, paved with fitted stones, disappearing into morning mist at both ends.

"Orsen," she said. "Look."

He was behind her, doing his morning inspection of their small camp with the same grave thoroughness he'd once given the Hollow Gate: checking the fire pit was cold, folding the blanket into a square so precise it could have been used to calibrate instruments. Thirty years of routine didn't die just because the house that held the routine had burned down. He'd tried to rebuild it on the road — same litany at dawn, same checks, same careful distance from everything alive — and Ember had appointed herself, without asking anyone's permission, the agent of its demolition.

He looked up. He followed her finger. And she watched him do the thing he always did when the world misbehaved, which was to narrow his eyes at it as though it were a seal that had developed a crack.

"That's not on any map," he said.

"No," Ember agreed cheerfully. "It's on a song instead."

* * *

She had been teaching him for six days, and he was the worst student she had ever had, which was a remarkable achievement considering she was nine years old and had only ever taught one other person anything, and that person was a goat named Bristle who had learned to open latches out of pure spite.

"It's not that the words are hard," Orsen said for perhaps the fortieth time, sitting down heavily on a flat stone among the wild apple trees. "I *know* the words. I've known them since I was younger than you. My father made me recite them every night." He rubbed his face. "I know the litany by heart. All of it. Every name, every gate, every—"

"But you don't know what they *mean*," Ember said. She hopped up onto the stone beside him, because if you let Orsen sit alone on a rock looking tragic, he would do it all day, and daylight was for walking. "That's like knowing how to carry water without ever drinking any. You've been hauling buckets your whole life, warden. Time somebody got you to the well."

"I liked being a bucket," Orsen muttered.

"You absolutely did not."

He didn't argue, which was how she knew she'd won. That was another thing about Orsen Dunmore: he lied badly, even to himself, especially to himself. It was going to be useful someday. Ember had a feeling about that, the way you have a feeling about weather coming over a ridge.

"Say it," she said. "The first line. The way your father taught you."

His mouth went stiff. She knew why. The litany wasn't just words to him; it was the sound of a cold room and a father who never smiled and thirty years of standing in front of a door that hummed. But he said it anyway, because underneath all that flint, Orsen Dunmore was obedient right down to the marrow, and Ember intended to spend that obedience very carefully.

"*Ael Vessan doru, ael Vessan mora.*"

The old tongue came out of him strange — flattened, careful, each syllable set down like a coin counted out to a stranger. But it was correct. It was perfectly, heartbreakingly correct, and hearing it in his deep keeper's voice made something in Ember's chest go tight and hot.

"Now sing it," she said. "Not say. Sing. Like this." And she sang it back to him, the way her mother had sung it, the way every child in the hidden valleys sang it — rising on *doru*, falling soft on *mora*, letting the last note hang in the air like smoke.

*One country remembered, one country mourned.*

The orchard answered.

It was small, the first time — she'd felt it before, little shivers of the world sitting up and paying attention. The grass around their stone turned greener in a slow ring, like ink spreading through cloth. One of the apple trees, gnarled and half-dead since before Ember was born, straightened with a creak like an old man's knees, and blossoms broke out along its branches all at once, white and startled, out of season and glad of it.

Orsen went very still.

"It remembers," he whispered. "When you sing the names, it — the land remembers what it was."

"Not just what it was." Ember picked up a fallen apple — the tree had made apples too, small and hard and real — and tossed it to him. He caught it on reflex, keeper's hands, hands that had spent three decades catching things that fell. "What it *is*. The songs aren't sad stories, Orsen. They're instructions. My grandmother used to say a country isn't a place, it's a promise everybody agrees to keep. The Chancery burned the records and killed the singers and redrew the maps, but they couldn't get into the songs, because songs live in people, and people are slippery." She grinned at him. "You're learning Old Vessani from a nine-year-old. See? Slippery."

"The Chancery is going to be furious," Orsen said. There was something new in his voice when he said *the Chancery* now — not just fear. Something with edges.

"Good," said Ember.

* * *

They walked the road that wasn't on the map, and all that day the country kept waking up around them.

It worked like this: Ember would come to a place, and she would sing its name. Not from any book — from the ballads her mother sang while grinding grain, from the round-dance songs children screamed in the hidden terraces, from the long mourning-chants the elders performed at dusk. Each name fit its place the way a key fits a lock, and when the key turned, the land remembered itself.

At a bend in the valley where the maps promised only scrub, she sang "*Sorrel-harrow, Sorrel-harrow, wheels of the west wind turn*," and a mill rose out of the bracken — not whole, never quite whole, but the shape of it, the stone walls standing to shoulder height, the wheel-pit still cut clean into the hillside, and for one breathless moment Ember saw the wheel itself turning, ghost-bright, throwing spray of light instead of water.

At a ford across a stream that had no name on any chart, she sang the naming-song, the silly one, the one children sang while splashing — "*Cold-ankle, cold-ankle, tell me where you've been*" — and the stream laughed. There was no other word for it. The water over the stones made a sound like giggling, and Orsen stopped dead in the middle of the crossing and stared at his own boots as though they had betrayed him.

"Did the river just—"

"Yes."

"Rivers don't giggle."

"This one does. You'd know that if you'd ever been a child near it." Ember skipped ahead across the stepping stones. "That's the trouble with you warden-types. All duty, no splashing."

"I splashed," Orsen said with dignity, wading after her. "Once. In 841. I was reprimanded for it."

Ember stopped and turned around so fast she nearly went off the stones. "You were *reprimanded*? For splashing?"

"My father believed play invited chaos. Chaos invited the Chancery." He said it flatly, the way he said all the terrible things about his father, in the tone of a man reading an inventory. But Ember had gotten good at listening under the flat tone, and underneath this one there was a small boy standing in a cold room being told that joy was a security risk.

"Well," she said, "your father is not here." She cupped her hands, dipped them in the giggling water, and threw it directly into Orsen Dunmore's solemn face.

For a moment nothing happened at all. He stood dripping, blinking, wearing the expression of a man whose entire cosmology has been splashed. Then — slowly, rustily, like a gate that hasn't opened in thirty years — Orsen Dunmore laughed.

It was a terrible laugh. It was mostly coughing. It was the best thing Ember had heard since the burning.

"There," she said, satisfied. "Now you're learning."

* * *

They camped that night in the ruins of a town the ballads called Miren's Crossing, and singing its name woke up more than either of them expected.

The name-song for Miren's Crossing was a long one, a weaving song, and by the time Ember reached the third verse the street they'd chosen for camp had remembered its cobbles, and the well at the corner had remembered its roof, and the houses on either side stood up out of their own rubble like tired people rising from chairs — hollow, yes, windowless, yes, but *standing*, with their doorways intact and their chimneys pointing properly at the sky, and in the blue hour between sunset and dark, Ember could almost see lamplight in the windows that weren't there.

Orsen walked the street slowly, touching the doorposts.

"There were people here," he said quietly.

"There are people *from* here," Ember corrected. "Half the families behind the Hollow Gate came out of these valleys. Old Marta who makes the cheese — her grandmother's house is probably right there, the one with the crooked chimney. When we get everyone home, they'll walk these streets and finish remembering them. That's how it works, my gran says. Songs hold the shape. People fill it in." She poked the fire together. "A country's like bread dough, sort of. You can punch it down, but the yeast is still in it."

Orsen sat down across the fire from her. The light moved on his face, and Ember studied him the way she studied everything now — openly, rudely, with a nine-year-old's license — and thought again about how strange he was, this big grey man who had guarded a door his whole life without ever once asking what was behind it.

"Warden," she said. "Can I ask you something?"

"You generally do whether I permit it or not."

"Why didn't you ever look? Behind the gate. Thirty years, and you never once—" She stopped, because she already knew part of the answer, the part that was duty and fear and his father's voice. But she wanted the rest of it.

Orsen was quiet for a long time. A log settled. Somewhere up the empty street, a shutter that belonged to no house creaked in a wind that hadn't decided to exist yet.

"Because I was afraid of what I'd find," he said finally. "And then, later, because I was afraid of what I'd *want*." He looked into the fire. "If the far side was empty, then my life was guarding nothing, and that was bearable, in a grim sort of way. If it wasn't empty — if there were people, a whole country, and my family had helped hide them away and been paid for it — then my life was guarding a prison. Do you understand? As long as I never looked, I could keep believing I was a keeper."

"And now?"

"Now," Orsen said, "I know better. And it turns out the truth is heavier than either lie, but it's also—" He paused, hunting for the word, this man who had memorized ten thousand words of a language without understanding one of them.

"Usable," Ember supplied.

Something moved through his face — surprise, and then something warmer. "Yes. Usable." He shook his head slowly. "Your grandmother taught you that?"

"My grandmother taught me everything twice," Ember said, "once in words and once in bread dough. But no — that one I figured out myself. On the road." She hugged her knees. "Everyone thinks the past is dead weight. Carry it or put it down, those are your choices. But it's not weight, Orsen. It's *road*. Every true thing anybody ever did is a path somebody else can walk. That's why the Chancery burns the records. Not because the past shames them. Because the past is *infrastructure*."

Orsen stared at her.

"What?" said Ember.

"Nothing. It's only that I spent thirty years thinking silence was safety, and you've just explained in one evening why silence is surrender, and you did it with bread dough." He rubbed his eyes, but she could see the smile hiding at the corners of his mouth, trying to get out. "I'm beginning to suspect the Chancery should be more frightened of you than of me."

"They will be," Ember said comfortably, and lay back against her pack to sleep.

* * *

Two more days of road, two more days of names. She taught him the greeting-songs and the field-songs, the lullaby that made a ruined watchtower remember its stairs (which was lucky, because otherwise they'd have had to sleep in the rain), and the long rolling harvest chant that woke an entire hillside of terraces at once, so that for a quarter mile they walked through a landscape standing at attention, walls and sheds and olive trees all holding their remembered shapes around them like an honor guard.

Orsen learned slowly and then suddenly, the way he seemed to do everything — years of nothing, and then all at once a flood. By the third day he was singing the place-names himself, badly, in his foghorn keeper's voice, and the land answered him anyway, because the songs didn't care about beauty; they cared about truth, and nobody had ever meant the words harder than Orsen Dunmore meaning *forgive me* to a valley full of ruins.

But on the fourth day, the country gave them something else.

It was near dusk, and they had come down out of the hills into a wide shallow vale, and Ember knew this place. She knew it the way you know a story you've heard a hundred times — the vale of Aldenmere, where the last free council of Vessany met before the hiding, where the decision was made to close the gates and scatter the keys. Every hidden child knew the ballad. It began:

*Aldenmere, Aldenmere, where the candles outnumbered the stars—*

She sang it. Orsen sang it with her, gravel and off-key and utterly sincere.

And the vale remembered.

Not just walls this time. Not just roads. The mist that pooled in the low ground took shape — lanterns, hundreds of lanterns, hanging ghost-gold above the grass where a market green had been, and figures moving among them, translucent as breath on glass: a woman carrying a basket, a man leading a horse, children running a hoop between the stalls. They weren't really there. Ember knew they weren't really there. Her hand passed through a lantern-flame and felt only cool air.

But they turned toward her anyway. All of them, gently, all at once, the way flowers turn toward sun.

"*Vessani*," the woman with the basket said, in a voice like the memory of a voice, and it wasn't a question, and it wasn't a warning. It was a welcome.

Beside Ember, Orsen made a sound she had never heard him make — small and cracked, a boy's sound. "They're waiting," he whispered. "Ember, they're still *waiting*."

"Of course they are," Ember said. Her throat was tight and her eyes were hot and she was grinning anyway. "That's what countries do. They wait for us to come home and finish the sentence."

The lanterns held until full dark, and then the vale sighed and let them go, and the mist lay down flat again, and the maps' version of the world closed back over the true one like water closing over a stone. They made camp on the edge of the ghost-market, and Ember slept better than she had since the burning, dreaming of candles outnumbering stars.

* * *

It was Orsen who spotted the bridge, the next noon.

They had crested a ridge to find a river below them, broad and brown and unhurried, and strung across it — impossibly, wonderfully — a long wooden toll bridge on stone piers, hung with lanterns and wind chimes and drying fish, with a squat round tollhouse at the near end and smoke rising from its chimney.

Ember knew it from the songs too, and her stomach did a small complicated flip. "*Merrowfen*," she breathed. "The toll bridge at Merrowfen. Oh, warden. Oh, warden, you're going to hate this."

"Hate what?" said Orsen, in the voice of a man who has learned that this question never brings good news.

"The toll." Ember was already skipping down the slope, because dread was no reason to walk slowly. "Merrowfen doesn't take coin. Coin's for people with something to hide. Merrowfen takes *truths*. You pay the bridge by telling it something true that costs you."

Orsen stopped walking.

"Ember," he said carefully, "what happens if a traveler has no truths left to tell?"

"Nobody has no truths left," Ember said. "Everybody's got at least one they've never said out loud. Most people have a whole cellarful." She glanced back at him, at the grey face and the folded-down mouth, and gentled, a little. "Yours might be the biggest cellar on the road, warden. But you know what my gran says about shame."

"I'm certain I don't want to."

"She says shame is a load you're carrying for someone else, and the second you set it down in front of witnesses, you find out it weighs half what you thought." Ember spread her hands. "Also she says the toll-keeper at Merrowfen pours a very decent plum brandy, but that's probably not doctrinally important."

Below them, the wind chimes on the bridge rang, though there was no wind. And in the doorway of the tollhouse, a figure had appeared — broad, aproned, watching them come with the patient stillness of someone who had been expecting travelers exactly like these for a very long time.

Orsen looked at the bridge. He looked at the toll-keeper. He looked, last and longest, at Ember.

"All right," he said at last, and squared his shoulders like a man walking up to his own tribunal. "Let's go pay the bridge."

What none of them saw — what no song had warned them of — was the grey rider on the far bank, dismounting beside a lean woman in a Chancery travelling cloak, who raised a small brass instrument to one eye and began, with great courtesy, to read the truth drifting across the water.
