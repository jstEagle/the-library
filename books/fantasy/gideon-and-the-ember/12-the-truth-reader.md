## 12. The Truth-Reader

Serah Vole had a theory about mercy, and it was this: most people mistook it for kindness.

Kindness was a warm hand on a fevered brow. Kindness was soup. Kindness was letting a man keep his illusions because the truth would hurt him, and what harm could a small illusion do?

Vole had spent nineteen years in the Chancery's service learning exactly what harm a small illusion could do. She had seen a border town burn because its people believed a treaty still existed. She had watched two villages go to war over a well that neither of them had ever owned, each armed with songs about it. Unfinished things festered. Half-buried kingdoms bred half-buried grievances, and grievances did not stay buried. They waited, patient as seeds, for someone to come along and water them with a story.

So when people called her cruel — and they did, behind her back, in the careful way frightened people spoke of inquisitors — she did not take offense. She took notes.

*They think I am unkind,* she wrote in her journal that morning, on the road into Merrowfen, *because I am willing to hurt them now instead of letting history hurt them worse later. A surgeon is not unkind to the limb.*

She underlined the last sentence twice, then closed the journal and looked out at the valley.

Merrowfen was a pretty little lie of a place. That was her first impression and, as it turned out, her lasting one. It sat where two rivers braided together, all whitewashed walls and willow trees and a bridge that looked like it had been built by someone who loved bridges very much and understood them not at all — crooked railings, mismatched stones, a toll-house perched on the far bank like a fat brown wren. Smoke rose from chimneys. Children waved from a hay cart. Everything about it said *this is an ordinary place where ordinary things happen*, and Vole had been in the Chancery long enough to know that places which insisted too loudly on their own ordinariness were usually hiding something in the cellar.

"Pretty," said Corporal Denny beside her, meaning it.

"Mm," said Vole.

"You don't like it?"

"I don't trust it. Look at the bridge."

Denny looked. "It's... a bridge?"

"It's a smuggler's bridge," Vole said. "Look at the toll-house. One keeper, no guards, no Chancery charter posted. Bridges like this don't collect coin, Denny. They collect something else." She nudged her horse forward. "Come along. Let's find out what the going rate is."

* * *

The toll-keeper was a broad, comfortable-looking man with grey in his beard and flour on his apron, as though he'd been interrupted mid-baking, which he had, which he mentioned twice in the first minute, as though apologizing for having a life. He stood in the doorway of his toll-house wiping his hands and looking at the six grey riders with the expression of a man who has seen weather coming and decided to be pleasant about it.

"Morning," he said. "Toll's a copper a head, or—"

"We're Chancery," Vole said pleasantly.

"Aye, I can see the grey. Copper's still a copper, even off you lot." He smiled. It was a good smile. Warm around the eyes. Vole catalogued it automatically: *practiced, but not false. A man who has needed to be liked by strangers.* "You'll be wanting the crossing, then?"

"Actually," Vole said, dismounting, "we're wanting a conversation. Two travelers came through here recently. A man in his fifties, weathered, keeps to himself. And a girl, perhaps ten or eleven years old, dark-haired, speaks oddly. They'd have crossed within the last three days."

The toll-keeper's face did something remarkable. It did nothing at all. Not the frozen blankness of a guilty man — the smooth, settled blankness of a man who had rehearsed emptiness the way other men rehearsed speeches.

"Don't recall anyone like that," he said.

"No?" Vole smiled back. "That's strange. Because your ledger says otherwise."

She nodded at Denny, who was already inside the toll-house, and a moment later he emerged holding a battered book open in both hands. "Ma'am. Entry dated four days back. 'Two heads, one grown, one child. Paid in kind.'"

"Paid in kind," Vole repeated. "How interesting. What does a bridge want with payment in kind, Master...?"

"Harrow." The name came easily. Too easily; it had the flatness of a word said so many times it had worn smooth. "We're flexible here. Folk pay what they've got. Eggs, news, mending. It's a poor district."

"And what did these two pay?"

Harrow's hands kept wiping each other on the apron. "I told you. Don't recall."

"You have a ledger entry."

"The ledger says they crossed. Doesn't say their faces."

Vole sighed — softly, regretfully, the sigh of a woman who genuinely wished the world were simpler — and reached into her saddlebag.

What she brought out was small and unimpressive to look at: a disc of grey glass the size of a saucer, rimmed in plain pewter, hanging from a leather cord. Toll-keepers up and down the frontier had seen a hundred such trinkets. Scrying glasses, fortune-tellers' baubles, Chancery theater.

This one was a truth-reader, and it was the only one of its kind in the province, and Serah Vole had carried it for eleven years.

"Master Harrow," she said, "do you know what this is?"

"Looks like a hand mirror somebody sat on."

Denny snorted. Harrow didn't. That, too, went in the catalogue. *Unafraid. Or very good at seeming so.*

"It's a truth-reader," Vole said. "An heirloom of my office. When I hold it and ask a question, it shows me the shape of the answer. Not the words — the shape. A lie looks like a knot. A truth looks like a straight line." She turned the disc over in her fingers, letting the morning light slide across it. "It doesn't read minds. It reads *conviction*. Which means clever men can sometimes beat it, if they truly believe their own lies. So I'll ask you plainly, and you can decide whether to waste both our mornings."

She held the glass up between them.

"Did two travelers — a man and a child — cross this bridge in the last four days?"

The glass stayed clear as river water. No knot. No line. Nothing at all.

Vole lowered it slowly, and felt the first real prickle of interest she'd had in weeks.

"Well," she said. "That's new."

"What's new?" Harrow asked.

"The glass found nothing to read. That happens one of two ways. Either you know nothing—" she watched his eyes, "—or you are so thoroughly shielded that the question slid off you entirely. And Master Harrow, nobody is naturally shielded. Shielding is taught. Shielding is *old*." She let that sit in the air between them like smoke. "Where did you learn to keep your mind so tidy?"

"Fifty years of marriage," Harrow said. "You learn not to think anything."

And there — there it was, just for a heartbeat, before he smoothed it away. Something behind his eyes that wasn't fear. Vole had expected fear. Fear she knew how to work with; fear was a lever like any other. This was something rarer and much more useful.

Grief. Old, worn-smooth grief, the kind a man carries so long it stops hurting and starts *furnishing*.

"You've lost someone," Vole said quietly.

Harrow's hands stopped moving on the apron.

"A long time ago. Someone the world told you never existed." She kept her voice gentle, almost tender. "I'm not your enemy, Master Harrow. Whatever you think the Chancery is — whatever stories you've been fed — my work is ending griefs like yours. Permanently. So that no more children grow up mourning countries that were taken from them before they were born."

Something flickered across the toll-keeper's face — disbelief, mostly, and under it a tired, bitter amusement, as if she'd told him a joke in a language he'd stopped speaking. But he said nothing, and the silence itself was a confession shaped like a man.

Vole held up the glass again.

"Which way did they go? The man and the child."

Nothing. Clear glass.

She tried a different angle. "Did they cross north?"

Clear.

"South?"

Clear.

"East, toward the hills?"

Still clear — but this time, for less than a breath, the glass *shivered*. Not a knot. Not a line. A tremor, like a plucked string deciding whether to sing.

Vole lowered the reader very carefully, as though it were a sleeping animal.

"Thank you," she said warmly. "You've been very helpful."

"I haven't told you anything," Harrow said.

"No," Vole agreed. "But your bridge has." She swung back into the saddle and looked down at him with what she hoped was genuine kindness, because some part of her — the part that remembered being a girl in a burned town, the part that had joined the Chancery in the first place — meant it. "Master Harrow. When this is finished, I'll come back through. And I'll bring the Chancery's surveyors, and they'll put this bridge on a map, and give you a charter, and make this a real place with real roads and real protection. You've spent your whole life standing in a doorway between two worlds. Wouldn't it be a mercy to finally close the door?"

Harrow looked up at her for a long moment. And then he smiled again — that same warm, practiced smile — and said, "Safe travels, mistress. Mind the stones past the fen. They sing when the wind's wrong. Folk say it's nothing. Folk say a lot of things."

* * *

They made camp that evening in the lee of a hill, and Vole sat apart with her journal while Denny and the others saw to the horses.

*The toll-keeper is Vessani,* she wrote. *Or descended from them. Shielded since childhood — the technique predates the purge; I've only seen it in the oldest records. He protected the fugitives without lying once. Remarkable discipline. Remarkable conviction.*

*Note: he fears me, but he pities me. That is worse. Pity means he believes I am the deceived party.*

She paused over that line, pen hovering, and finally added:

*I am not deceived. What I do is necessary. Unfinished kingdoms breed wars. A wound cleaned and closed heals; a wound hidden under bandages rots. If Vessany must die, better it dies cleanly, in the record, than lingers as a ghost that some future generation resurrects with a flag and a grievance. I am the closing of the wound. They will hate me for the sting. That is acceptable. Surgeons are not loved.*

She read it back and found, to her mild irritation, that the words sounded less certain written down than they had in her head. She underlined them anyway. Underlining helped.

"Ma'am?" Denny crouched at the edge of her firelight, awkward. "The girl. The one we're chasing. Reports from the Hollow say she's a child. Ten, maybe. You really think one child's worth all this?"

Vole closed the journal.

"Denny," she said, "do you know what happened at Greywater, ninety years ago?"

"Before my time."

"Before everyone's time. That's rather the point." She stirred the fire with a stick, watching the sparks climb. "There was a kingdom there. Small. Proud. Singing-magic, the old sort — the kind that gets into stone and stays. The Crown decided it was a threat, and the Chancery was sent to erase it. Not conquer it. *Erase* it. Maps, records, names, graves. Ninety years later, nobody remembers why the Greywater valley is haunted. Nobody remembers that the ghosts have faces." She tapped ash off the stick. "Except the ghosts do remember. And every year there are fewer people alive who can tell us what was done there, and more people alive who will tell you it never happened at all. Do you see? We didn't end that kingdom, Denny. We *orphaned* it. And orphaned things grow up angry."

"So the girl—"

"The girl is a key," Vole said. "Perhaps literally. Perhaps only in the way children are always keys — to doors their elders locked and forgot. And somewhere on the other side of those doors is a country full of people who have been told for thirty years that they are dead. Thirty years, Denny. That is long enough to raise a generation. That is long enough to raise an *army*. If that door opens and I have done nothing, what comes through it will not be refugees. It will be a reckoning." She met his eyes, and let him see — honestly, because she was honest, that was the whole cursed point of her — how much she wished it were otherwise. "I am trying to prevent a war. That's all. I have been trying to prevent the same war for nineteen years, one erased name at a time."

Denny shifted. "And if preventing it means... the child?"

Vole looked into the fire.

"Then I will carry it," she said quietly. "That is what the office is. Somebody has to be willing to be the one who did it, so that no one else ever has to be." She managed a small, tired smile. "Go sleep, Corporal. We ride at dawn."

* * *

Dawn came up grey and cold, and Vole stood at the edge of camp working the day's route through her head. East, past the fen. The singing stones. The toll-keeper had handed her that much — whether he meant to or not, whether the glass had caught a tremor of truth or the man himself had chosen, in his pitying, infuriating way, to *warn* her off, which was its own kind of confirmation.

East, then. Through country the maps called wilderness and the ballads, if you knew where to dig for them, called something else entirely.

She was tightening her saddle girth when one of the outriders came down the hill at a canter, hat in hand, face pale.

"Ma'am. There's a shepherd up on the ridge. Says he saw them yesterday. The man and the girl." He swallowed. "Says they weren't alone on the road. Says the ground was — ma'am, he says the ground was *remembering*. Old roads rising out of the moor where there aren't any. Ruins standing up whole. He thought he was mad. He'd been drinking. But ma'am—" the rider hesitated, "—he sang a bit of what he heard the child singing. Just a scrap. And Corporal Pell swears the horses all turned their heads at once."

Vole stood very still.

Then she opened her journal to a fresh page, and wrote, in her neat, disciplined hand:

*Day 19. The country is waking up ahead of schedule. The child is not carrying a key. The child is the key, and she is using it.*

*We ride east. Hard.*

She closed the journal, mounted, and gave the order, and six grey riders went down out of the hills toward the singing stones like a line of ink drawn across a page that had not yet decided what it wanted to say.
