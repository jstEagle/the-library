## 13. Ambush at the Singing Stones

The stones had been singing to themselves for an hour before either of us noticed.

That was the thing about the old country — it hummed when it thought no one was listening. Orsen said it was like living inside a house where the walls remembered every conversation you'd ever had and occasionally repeated them back to you at inconvenient moments. He'd said it that morning, over a breakfast of hard bread and river water, and I'd laughed so hard I choked on my crust, because he was right, and because it was still strange and wonderful to hear him say things like *the walls remember* without flinching.

He'd been learning fast. That was the other thing.

"Say it again," I'd told him, pointing at the ridge ahead of us. "What do you hear?"

Orsen had closed his eyes the way he did when he was being careful — which was most of the time, though less than before, which I counted as progress — and listened. "*Carneth-vel,*" he'd said slowly. "Singing stones. The song says... singing stones." Then, after a pause: "It also says something about not whistling near them, but I may be translating wrong."

"You're not," I said. "Grandmother Vess told me. You don't whistle near the stones or they'll follow you home."

"Follow us *home*?"

"They're lonely," I said, as if this explained everything, which it did.

So we came up into the standing stones in the grey light of late afternoon, and they were exactly what the song promised: a ring of tall grey pillars on a bald hilltop, each one carved with lines of Old Vessani so worn by weather that only the deepest strokes survived, and each one humming its own low note, so that the whole hilltop sounded like a room full of people humming in their sleep. Moss grew in the letters. A crow sat on the tallest stone and looked personally offended by our arrival.

I loved it immediately. It felt like walking into a crowd of grandmothers.

Orsen walked the circle slow, reading what he could, touching the stone with two fingers the way he touched everything now — like he was asking permission. "This one's a boundary marker," he murmured. "This one's... a complaint? Someone named Aldous-Vess is complaining about a neighbor's goats."

"That's a prayer," I said.

"It's about goats."

"Prayers can be about goats." I put my hand flat against the warm stone and let the hum come up through my palm into my teeth. It tickled. "Hello," I whispered to it, in the old tongue. "We're passing through. Don't mind us."

The stone hummed a little louder. Pleased, I think. Stones are easy to please if you actually talk to them, which almost nobody does anymore.

That was when Orsen went still, the way he went still when his warden instincts woke up — all at once, like a door slamming shut inside him.

"Ember." His voice had dropped to almost nothing. "How many ways up this hill?"

I looked. One path behind us, winding up through gorse. The rest of the hill fell away steep on three sides into bracken and shadow. "One," I said. And then, because I'd spent six years learning to read grown-ups' silences: "Why?"

"Because there are men coming up it," he said, "and they've been following our tracks since the ford."

* * *

They came out of the gorse the way Chancery soldiers do everything — in neat rows, quietly, as if the world were a filing cabinet and we were papers to be collected. Grey cloaks, grey hoods, iron-tipped staves, and at their head a sergeant with a voice like wet gravel who called up the hill:

"Warden Dunmore! By order of the Chancery of Greywater, stand where you are!"

Orsen stepped in front of me. It was such a quick, automatic thing, the way a wall stands in front of whatever is behind it, and part of me wanted to tell him I wasn't a door he needed to guard anymore, and another part of me — the smaller, younger, more honest part — was glad of it.

"There's nothing here for you," Orsen called back. "Pilgrims. We're pilgrims."

"Pilgrims," the sergeant repeated, in the tone of a man tasting something spoiled. "To what shrine?"

"The stones," Orsen said. "Obviously."

There was a pause. Somewhere below, a soldier snorted, and the sergeant turned his helmeted head just enough to make the snorting stop. Then, gravelly, patient, terrible: "Warden. We have been sent to find a child. A child who speaks a dead language. A child seen crossing the Merrowfen bridge with a tall man who paid his toll in confessions." He began to walk up the path, unhurried, boots crunching. "You confessed a great deal at that bridge, Warden. It traveled faster than you did."

I felt Orsen's shoulders change. Not fear exactly. Something older and harder, something that had been waiting thirty years behind a sealed door for exactly this moment and had rehearsed nothing at all.

"Ember," he breathed, barely a sound. "The far side of the circle. Behind the goat-complaint stone. Don't run — running tells them everything."

I moved. Slowly, sidelong, the way you edge along a wall when the floor might be listening. Twelve soldiers fanning out across the lower slope. My heart was doing something unhelpful in my chest, hammering out a rhythm like a drummer boy who'd never been taught to march.

And the stones were humming.

All around me, the whole ring of them, holding their low sleepy notes, and the words carved down their sides catching the last light, and I thought — the way you think things in the worst moments, suddenly and completely — *they know the words too.*

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about the old tongue: it isn't a language the way Chancery clerks understand languages, as a list of words with meanings attached like price tags. It's older than that. Grandmother Vess used to say the first Vessani didn't speak *to* the world, they spoke *as* it — that a word spoken truly enough didn't describe a thing, it *became* the thing for as long as the word lasted. That's why the songs worked. That's why roads appeared when we sang place-names. The country wasn't remembering itself because of magic laid over it like a blanket.

It was remembering because someone was saying its name.

And I was standing in a ring of stones carved full of names, and the stones had been holding those names in their mouths for a thousand years, keeping them safe, the way you keep soup warm for someone who hasn't come home yet.

The soldiers reached the lip of the hilltop.

"Hold them," the sergeant said.

The first soldier grabbed Orsen's arm. Orsen didn't fight him — I saw him decide not to, saw the arithmetic go through him, twelve against one and a child to protect — and then a soldier came toward me, reaching, and something in me stood up.

Not me. Something older than me. Something that had been waiting in my chest since the night I walked out of the Hollow Gate carrying a song I didn't fully understand.

I opened my mouth, and I sang.

* * *

I don't remember choosing the song. That's the truth of it, and I've gone over that moment a hundred times since, lying awake under hedges and inn floors, and I never chose it. The stones chose it. Or the country chose it. Or the words had simply waited so long to be said truly that when a mouth finally opened nearby, they poured in.

I sang the boundary-song. The one carved around the whole circle, the one whose broken pieces I'd been reading all afternoon without knowing I was reading instructions.

"*Carneth-vel, carneth-dun, keleth varran, keleth-von—*"

Stone, hold. Stone, stand. What was set, let it stay set.

The ring answered.

I don't have better words than that, and I am going to be a person who needs good words someday, so let me try anyway. Every stone in the circle took up the note I was singing and made it *true*. The hum became a chord, the chord became a voice, and the voice was the size of the hill. The crow left. The ground under my feet shivered like a horse shaking off rain. And the carvings — the worn, mossy, half-forgotten letters — filled with light from the inside, faint gold, like windows lit in houses where everyone had long ago gone to bed.

"*Keleth varran!*" I sang, and meant it with everything in me. *Hold.*

The soldiers stopped. Even the sergeant stopped. There is a particular kind of silence men make when the world stops behaving like the world, and I heard it then — twelve men forgetting how to breathe at the same time.

Then the hilltop remembered itself.

That's the only way I can say it. The stones had been a ring once — I understood this suddenly, completely, the way you understand a dream while you're still in it — not a ring of markers but a ring of *keepers*, set here by masons of old Vessany to hold the hill against exactly this: against strangers, against erasure, against grey men with iron staves who wanted the past to lie down and be quiet. The song was the instruction. The singer was the hand that pulled the rope.

The standing stones leaned.

Slowly — terribly slowly, the way a tree falls, the way a decision becomes real — the tallest pillars tilted inward, downslope, toward the path, toward the gorse, toward the neat grey rows of men who had come to collect a child like a misfiled paper. Light ran down the carvings like water finding channels. The hum deepened until I felt it in my ribs, in my back teeth, in the soles of my feet.

"*CARNETH-DUN!*" I sang, and my voice cracked on it, and the crack didn't matter, because the stones carried the note for me. *Stand. Stand. STAND.*

And the hillside stood up.

Not the stones. The *hillside*. The whole shoulder of the slope below the circle shrugged — there's no gentler word for it, though nothing about it was gentle — and a hundred tons of remembered rock let go all at once, boulders and slabs and the long-buried bones of an older wall, everything the hill had ever swallowed, everything the country had been holding onto, released in one great exhale of stone.

The soldiers ran. To their credit, they ran well. Grey cloaks streaming, staves abandoned, the sergeant shouting orders that the hill declined to hear. The landslide took the path, took the gorse, took the place where the front row had been standing four seconds earlier, and roared down into the darkening valley with a sound like the end of a very long sentence.

Dust rolled over the hilltop. It got in my eyes, my hair, my mouth. Somewhere in it, Orsen found me — I knew it was him because his hands landed on my shoulders the way they always did, both of them, careful even now, even at the end of the world.

"Ember. Ember, look at me. Are you hurt?"

"I'm fine," I tried to say.

No sound came out.

* * *

I tried again. Opened my mouth, pushed air through it, shaped the word the way I'd shaped ten thousand words before. *Fine.* Nothing. Not a whisper, not a croak. Just air, moving through an empty room.

Orsen saw my face do whatever it did, and he went white under the dust. "Ember?"

I shook my head. Touched my throat. Shook my head again.

He held my face in both hands then, dust and all, and looked into my eyes the way he'd looked into the gate — like something precious that he was terrified of breaking. "Hey. Hey. Breathe. Was it the song? Did it take it out of you?"

I nodded. Because yes. I could feel it, the way you feel a tooth that isn't there anymore — the shape of the song still in me, the knowing of it, every word and every note, but the pipe that carried it gone cold and empty. I had sung a country back onto its feet, and the country had taken the payment straight out of my throat.

Grandmother Vess would have known what to say about that. She probably would have said it was fair, and she would have been right, and I hated her a little for not being there to be right at somebody else.

Orsen wrapped his cloak around my shoulders — it smelled of woodsmoke and thirty years of a locked house — and steered me away from the ring, away from the dust still settling over the ruined path. Behind us, the stones had gone quiet again, their lights fading, their work done. But as we passed the goat-complaint stone — the prayer-stone, I corrected myself, prayers can be about goats — I could have sworn the hum bent toward us. Followed us a few steps. Stopped at the edge of its own ground, the way a dog stops at the gate.

Lonely, I thought. They're lonely. And now they know my voice, and I can't answer them.

We came down the far side of the hill in the last light, Orsen half-carrying me over the bracken, and he talked the whole way, low and steady, the way you talk to a frightened animal, except I wasn't the frightened one — I figured out after a while that he was talking to keep himself from thinking.

"It'll come back," he said. "Voices come back. Voices are — there are remedies, honey and salt, rest, my mother lost hers for a month when I was small, after the fever, and it came back, mostly—" He stopped. *Mostly* hung in the air between us, and he heard it too, and I watched him decide not to pick it up. "We'll get you somewhere warm tonight. There was a shepherd's ruin marked on the song-road, half a mile east. Stone walls. A hearth. Possibly goats, historically speaking."

I squeezed his arm. It was supposed to be a laugh. It came out as a squeeze.

He looked down at me, and in the dusk his face did something complicated — grief and fury and a fierce, stubborn tenderness all fighting for the same space — and then he said, quietly, in Old Vessani, the accent still crooked but the meaning true:

"*Keleth varran, Ember-varra.* Stone, hold. Hold on."

The country around us — the bracken, the boulders, the tired old hills — seemed to lean in and listen.

And far behind us, down in the valley where the landslide had fallen silent, a lantern kindled. Then another. Then a line of them, strung out along the edge of the rockfall like beads on a grey thread, moving slowly, methodically, around the buried road.

Looking, I understood, for another way up.

They hadn't brought twelve men. They'd brought however many it took.
