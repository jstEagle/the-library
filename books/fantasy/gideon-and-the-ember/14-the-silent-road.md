## 14. The Silent Road

We climbed for two days, and Ember did not speak.

Not the sulking silence of a child denied supper — I knew that silence well enough from my own boyhood, and this was nothing like it. This was a silence with weight to it, a held-breath silence. She walked with her mouth closed like a door that had been nailed shut from the inside, and when she looked at me her eyes asked a question she could no longer say aloud: *is it gone? Is it all gone?*

I did my best to answer without words. I pointed out the good patches of bilberries on the north-facing slopes. I made a show of testing every stream before we drank from it, which was mostly theater but seemed to comfort her. On the second evening I caught two grey trout with my hands, clumsily, and she laughed — a small, cracked sound, more breath than voice — and for one moment the old Ember flickered in her face like a lamp behind a shutter.

Then the laugh ended and she pressed her lips together again, and I understood that even laughing frightened her. As if her voice were a purse with a hole in it, and she was afraid of spilling what little remained.

The Singing Stones had taken their toll. She had sung the country awake, had called up a landslide of remembered rock that buried Vole's ambush whole, and the song had gone out of her the way heat goes out of a stone after sundown. The healers' lore I knew — such as it was, keeper's lore, moss and willow-bark and prayer — said nothing about voices. But I had watched her work magic for weeks now, and I had begun to understand its shape. It was not fire she spent. It was *self*. Every true word in the old tongue cost her something real, and she had paid with the thing she could least afford to lose.

So we climbed, and I carried both our packs, and I talked enough for the two of us, filling the silence with whatever came into my head — the names of birds, the proper way to mend a bootlace, an increasingly elaborate theory about why smugglers always build their bridges in the least convenient places. She listened. Sometimes she squeezed my arm at the funny parts. It was not conversation, but it was company, and company is not nothing.

On the third morning we came down out of the high fells into a country that maps call wilderness and ballads call something else entirely.

I felt the change before I saw it. There was a texture underfoot, a rightness, as though the ground had been expecting us. And then, around a shoulder of hill, I saw the road.

It should not have been there. No map in the Chancery's survey offices showed a road through these hills; I had checked, years ago, out of a warden's thoroughness, and found only blank vellum and the notation *unproductive moorland*. But there it was: a paved way, wide enough for two carts, running straight as a plumb line between the hills, its stones fitted so finely you couldn't slide a knife between them. Moss grew over it in places, but the moss grew *around* the paving, respectfully, the way ivy grows around a monument.

Ember stopped dead. Her hand found my sleeve.

"I know," I said. "I see it."

She tugged my sleeve twice — her new language, a vocabulary of tugs and squeezes we were building day by day — and pointed ahead, then at herself, then made a shape in the air with both hands: a square, a roofline.

"A house?" I said. "There's a house?"

One tug. Yes.

The house appeared within the hour, exactly where she'd promised: a long, low farmhouse with a slate roof, standing in a fold of the hills beside the ghost-road, its chimney smoking. Around it, terraces cut the slope — fallow now, but fallow in the orderly way of fields merely resting, not abandoned. An orchard stood behind the house, trees heavy with fruit nobody had picked.

A woman was in the yard, splitting kindling. She straightened when she saw us, and I watched her take in the mud-streaked child and the gaunt grey man beside her, and I braced for the fear I had grown used to seeing in valley faces.

Instead she set down her axe and said, in accented but fluent Common, "You'll be wanting bread, I expect. You look like bread-wanters."

* * *

Her name was Maren Ostrey, and she was, by her own cheerful account, the last soul living on the Old Merrow Road — or rather, the only soul willing to be seen on it. She fed us barley soup and dark bread and honey from her own hives, and she watched Ember with a softness that told me she understood the situation before either of us explained it.

"Stones took her voice," Maren said, not asking. "Happens. The country asks a price and pays no change." She clicked her tongue at Ember, who was eating her third slice of bread with the concentration of the truly hungry. "You sang the stones up at the pass, didn't you, little one? We heard it down here. Whole hillside remembering itself at once — my grandmother used to say you could feel that kind of singing in your teeth."

Ember nodded. Then, carefully, she touched her throat and shook her head.

Maren's face did something complicated — grief and anger sharing a chair — and smoothed itself away. "Well," she said briskly. "There are other ways to carry a song. Always were." She looked at me then, directly, with the frank appraisal of a woman who has lived alone too long to waste time on politeness. "And you're the Dunmore. The keeper. I can tell by the way you stand, like you're waiting for someone to inspect you."

"I'm not the keeper anymore," I said. "The Chancery burned the house."

"Mm. Burned the house, did they." She snorted. "They burned houses on this road three hundred years ago, lad. The road's still here. They never do learn."

That night she gave us her bed and slept by the hearth herself, and in the morning she sat me down at her table across a pot of nettle tea and said, "Now. Tell me what you know of the litany."

"The litany?"

"Don't play the fool with me, Orsen Dunmore, I'm too old for it. Your family kept the Hollow Gate for nine generations. Nine generations of keepers, reciting the same words at the same stone, dawn and dusk, rain or snow. You think that was dusting?"

I wrapped my hands around the tea bowl because they wanted to shake. "My father taught me the litany before he taught me letters. All of it. Every name, every date, every — every crime." I heard my own voice go flat and careful. "He made me repeat it back until I could do it asleep. He said—" I stopped.

"Say it."

"He said a keeper doesn't need to understand the words. Understanding comes later, if it comes at all. He said the words would hold their meaning better than my head would."

Maren nodded slowly, without surprise, and something in her nod made me angry — a small, hot, unexpected anger. "You knew," I said. "You people knew what the litany was."

"We hoped," she said. "There's a difference, and the difference kept us alive. We hoped some Dunmore, someday, would turn out to be a vessel instead of a lock." She leaned forward. "Child, what do you think the litany *is*?"

"A record. A list of crimes. Names and dates."

"It is a key-song," Maren said. "The longest one ever made. Your fathers took everything the Chancery wanted erased — every name struck from the rolls, every village pulled off the map, every date they falsified — and they sewed it into a recitation, word by word, line by line, and hid it inside a duty so dull and so holy that no inquisitor in three hundred years thought to look at it. The gate doesn't open because a Dunmore guards it. The gate opens because a Dunmore *remembers*. Out loud. In order. Without flinching."

The kitchen was very quiet. Somewhere outside a bee worked the late flowers, industrious and indifferent.

"That's why Ember was sent out," I said slowly. Pieces moving, settling, refusing to settle. "Not to escape. To fetch."

"To fetch a voice that could sing the key-song home to the true door, yes. And the child did her part — she opened the Hollow Gate, she led you out, she bought you your road at the Singing Stones. But the last door needs the full litany, Orsen. Beginning to end. Every name." Maren's eyes were steady and not unkind. "And the little one can't sing it now. So."

"So," I said.

"So the question is whether thirty years of memorizing without understanding counts for anything." She sat back. "You know the words, keeper. Do you trust them?"

I thought about my father. I thought about his hands on my shoulders, his voice patient and iron, making me say the names again, again, again — *say it as if the person hearing it had died yesterday, boy, say it as if it matters, because someday it will matter* — and how I had hated him a little, all those years, for wasting my childhood on a list. How I had recited the litany at the Hollow Gate dawn and dusk for thirty years, believing it was a prayer, or a penance, or simply a habit, the way a man believes a wall is just a wall until the day it becomes a door.

"He knew," I said. My voice came out rough. "My father knew what he was training me for. Maybe not the whole of it. But he knew the words had to live somewhere, in somebody, ready."

"And do you trust them? The words? What they mean?"

I looked through the window at Ember, who was sitting on the orchard fence in the pale sun, teaching herself to whistle — badly, hopefully, two notes and a sputter — and something turned over in my chest, slow and enormous, like a millstone beginning to move.

"No," I said honestly. "But I don't think trust is required. I think *saying* is required. Trust can come along after, walking behind, the way it always does."

Maren laughed, a short bark. "Ha. You're more your grandfather than your father, then. He said nearly the same thing, once, to my grandmother." She stood and began clearing bowls, briskly, businesslike. "Rest today. Tomorrow I'll walk you as far as the crossroads, and after that—" she paused, and her cheerfulness dimmed to something more careful, "—after that you'll want to move quick. There's smoke on the southern horizon this morning. Could be nothing. Could be farmsteads burning. With the Chancery, it's rarely nothing."

* * *

We left at first light, and Maren walked with us as far as the standing stone at the crossroads, where the ghost-road met the modern track, and there she pressed a parcel of bread and cheese into Ember's arms and a small carved wooden bird into her hand.

"For luck," she said. "Hold it when you can't speak. It remembers being a tree; it knows about keeping things in."

Ember hugged her fiercely, and Maren held on a long moment, and then shooed us off like geese, calling after us that if we died she'd be very annoyed, having wasted perfectly good bread.

We walked south all that day, and the country walked with us.

I cannot describe it any other way. Wherever Ember passed, touching the old stones, humming her broken half-notes, the land remembered: a bridge arched itself out of a bramble-choked gully as we approached, as if it had been holding the pose for three centuries and was relieved to finally stretch. A wellhouse stood up out of a sheep pasture, complete and whole, bucket still on its chain. Once, crossing a bare hillside, we came upon an entire village street — doors, windows, a smithy with cold ashes in its forge — standing in air that the evening before had been empty moor, and Ember walked down it touching each doorpost in turn like a woman greeting neighbors, and I swear the doors leaned toward her hand.

And each time, I practiced.

Quietly, under my breath, while we walked: the litany. Not the gate-litany I had recited for thirty years — that was only the opening movement, the first hundred names — but the whole of it, the deep version, the one my father had drilled into me in the cellar winters and made me swear never to speak aloud except at the stone. *Vessan of the Nine Terraces, taken in the year the river froze. Aldessa Brightwater, midwife, taken with her daughters. The choir of Corvane, forty-one voices, taken on the eve of the Feast of Lanterns.* On and on, thousands of lines, a country's worth of grief arranged in perfect order, and I had carried it all my life the way a mule carries books — faithfully, without reading them.

Now I read them. Now, walking the remembered roads of the country those names belonged to, past the doors of the people those names had been torn from, I let myself understand, line by line, and it was like being unmade and remade with every mile.

Ember noticed, of course. Children notice everything. That evening, camped in the shell of a remembered inn with a real fire and unreal rafters, she sat across from me and listened to me recite in a whisper, and when I stumbled on a name — one I had never stumbled on in thirty years — she reached across the fire and took my hand and held it until I got it right.

"You don't need to say anything," I told her. "You can't, and you don't need to. I just—" I stopped, and started again, which is the honest way. "I spent my whole life thinking I was guarding a door. Turns out I was carrying one. Inside my head, all this time, and I never once opened it and looked."

She squeezed my hand. Then she pointed at me, and then at her own chest, and then made the gesture I was coming to know: hands cupped, then opened, like a bird released.

"You think I can do it," I said. "Carry it to the door. Say it all."

Two firm squeezes. Yes.

"I'm an old man who talks to himself for a living, Ember-varra. But—" and here I found, to my surprise, that I meant it, "—I know every word. And I know now what they weigh. And I find I'm willing to carry them."

She smiled then, the first full smile since the Stones, and it was like sun coming through after a week of rain, and I thought: *whatever it costs, whatever waits at the end of this road, that smile alone has already paid for the walking.*

Then she went stiff. Her grip tightened on my hand hard enough to hurt, and her eyes went past my shoulder to the inn's remembered window, to the darkness beyond it, where — far off, down in the southern dark — lights were moving.

Not lanterns strung out in a searching line, the way the Chancery worked. These moved fast, and bunched, and there were too many of them, and they were coming up the old road behind us with the unhurried confidence of people who already knew where the road went.

And above them, catching the light as it rode, something glinted that I recognized from my father's papers, from the Chancery seals, from thirty years of nightmares: the grey lacquer of a truth-reader's mask.

They had brought the reader to the hills. Which meant they weren't searching anymore.

They were listening.
