## 15. The City That Isn't There

*Ember*

The city did not exist, and it was the most beautiful thing Ember had ever seen.

She had learned by now that "does not exist" was a slippery thing in the old country. The maps said *wilderness*. The ballads said *Corvane, pearl of the vale*. Both were telling the truth, which was the sort of arrangement Vessany had always made with reality.

They came over the last ridge as the sun went down, and there it was — spilling across the valley floor like someone had poured a kingdom out of a jug. Towers of white stone and copper roofs gone green. Bridges that arched over a river that only ran at night. Lanterns, thousands of them, hanging from every eave and balcony, burning without flame.

"Orsen," she whispered, tugging his sleeve. "Orsen, look."

He was looking. His face did something complicated.

"It's not on any map," he said. "I've walked this valley. There's nothing here but bracken and an old riverbed."

"There's nothing here *by day*." Ember spread her hands, feeling the hum of it against her palms, the way you feel a beehive through a fence. "The song says Corvane keeps dusk hours. *The city sleeps when the sun is high, and wakes when shadows lie.* It's in the third verse of 'The Lament of the Nine Gates.' You'd know that if you'd ever let me finish a verse instead of asking eleven questions."

"I ask questions because your verses are full of impossible things."

"Yes," Ember said patiently, the way you'd explain rain to a fish. "That's what makes them verses."

They went down together as the light failed, and with every step the city grew more solid. First the lanterns, then the smell — woodsmoke and baking bread and river water — then the sound, which was the sound Ember had been dreaming of since she'd walked out of the Hollow Gate: voices. Hundreds of voices. A market being taken down for the night, a fiddle tuning up somewhere, a mother calling a child home in Old Vessani.

Orsen stopped walking.

"That's—" He swallowed. "That's the language my father prayed in. On the street. People are just *talking* in it."

"What did you think they'd talk in?" Ember asked. "Chancery clerkspeak? *Pursuant to the erasure of subsections one through nine?*" She put on her sternest voice, the one she used on him when he got gloomy, which was often. "Come on, last Dunmore. The dead have been waiting thirty years to yell at you. Least we can do is be punctual."

"I don't want to be yelled at by ghosts."

"Nobody does. But you're going to be anyway." She took his hand, because his hand was shaking, and because hers was too, and two shaking hands make almost one steady one. "Besides. Maybe some of them will yell at me instead. I'm very yell-at-able. Ask anyone."

* * *

*Orsen*

The dead of Corvane-in-exile looked, at first glance, entirely alive.

That was the worst part, Orsen decided somewhere between the lantern-lit square and the fountain plaza. If they had been pale and wailing, translucent and terrible, he could have managed. But they were a woman arguing cheerfully with a fruit-seller about the price of plums. They were old men playing tiles under an awning, cheating, complaining about the cheating. They were children chasing a hoop down a street that would vanish by morning, laughing in a language Orsen had spent thirty years reciting without once understanding.

"They don't know they're dead," Ember murmured beside him. "Or — they know, but not all the time. The songs say the purge caught the whole capital in one night. The city just... kept its evening. Forever."

"One night," Orsen repeated. The Chancery records he'd tended, copied, filed, believed — the ones that said Vessany was destroyed in a war, honorably, regrettable but necessary — had never mentioned a single night.

A woman detached herself from the market crowd and came toward them, and Orsen felt his stomach drop, because she was looking directly at him. Not at Ember. At him. With recognition. With relief.

With joy.

"Aldric." Her voice cracked on the name. "Oh, thank the Nine Gates. Aldric Dunmore. We thought — after the gate sealed, we thought you'd never come back. We thought you'd abandoned us."

"I'm not—" Orsen started.

"You promised," the woman said, and now she was close enough to touch, and her eyes were wet, and she was gripping his sleeve the way people grip things when they're afraid the thing will be taken away. "You promised you'd find the way back out. You promised you'd tell someone. Anyone. Thirty years, Aldric. My daughter was six years old. She waited at the window every dusk for a month."

"Ma'am," Orsen said helplessly, "I'm not Aldric Dunmore. I'm his son. Orsen."

The woman blinked. And Orsen watched something happen in her face that he would remember for the rest of his life, however long that turned out to be: the joy draining out of it, and underneath the joy, grief, and underneath the grief, something older and colder than either.

"Oh," she said. "His son."

Ember stepped slightly forward, the way she did when she was about to defend him from something, including himself. "He didn't know," she said. "Any of it. He found out three weeks ago. He's been walking toward this since."

"He walked *away* from it for thirty years," the woman said. Not cruelly. Worse than cruelly — reasonably. Like a clerk reading figures from a ledger. "Thirty years, keeper. Do you know how long a dusk is, when you're waiting for someone to open a door?"

"No," Orsen admitted. "But I know how long a night is, alone, in a house with one lamp. I've had thirty years of those. I think—" His voice caught, and he was surprised to find he meant the next words more than anything he'd ever said. "I think we may have been keeping the same vigil, ma'am. On opposite sides of the wall."

The woman studied him for a long moment. Around them, the market had gone quiet, and other faces had turned — dozens of them, hundreds, drawn by the name Dunmore the way iron filings draw to a lodestone.

"My name is Sefa Ilvane," the woman said at last. "My daughter is Maret. She's thirty-six now. Ghosts age strangely — we keep our hurts but lose our years. Come. If you've truly come to help, there are people who will want to hear it. And if you haven't—"

"If he hasn't, I'll yell at him," Ember offered. "I'm told I'm very yell-at-able."

Something flickered at the corner of Sefa's mouth — not quite a smile, but the ghost of one, which seemed fitting. "You'd get along with my daughter," she said. "Come on, then. Both of you. And walk quickly. The city starts forgetting itself an hour before dawn, and I'd rather you weren't standing in a street when it does."

* * *

They gathered in a hall that Orsen recognized from his father's prayer book — a marginal sketch, the Hall of the Nine Voices, where the council of Corvane had met since before the Chancery had existed to erase anything. Inside, it was lit by the same flameless lanterns, and it was full. Word traveled fast in a city with nowhere else to go.

They stood him in the middle of it, and the dead looked at him, and Orsen understood suddenly why his father had never taught him the meanings of the words he memorized. Because meanings were heavy, and this was what they weighed.

An old man with a tile-player's crooked fingers spoke first. "The gatekeeper's son. Tell us, boy — when your father sealed the door, did he say anything? One word about us? One apology?"

"He never spoke of Vessany at all," Orsen said. "I thought it was a fairy tale until three weeks ago. I thought my job was keeping an empty door shut so the wind wouldn't get through."

"The wind," someone said bitterly, from the back.

"Why?" The question came from a young man near the front, and it wasn't bitter, it was raw, torn straight out of him. "Why did he stay away? Why did none of you ever come? We sang at the gate. For years. We sang the key-song until our voices gave out. Did he hear us?"

And Orsen, who had spent three weeks learning the shape of the truth and still wasn't ready to hold its weight, heard himself answer honestly, because lying to the dead seemed both impossible and obscene.

"He heard you," Orsen said. "I believe he heard you every dusk of his life. And I believe that's why he drank, and why he never spoke your language above a whisper, and why he made me memorize the litany without telling me what it meant. Whatever he was doing on that side of the gate — I don't think it was guarding it. I think it was serving a sentence. And I think he couldn't bear to let me know I was serving one too."

Silence. Then Sefa Ilvane, quietly: "Serving whom?"

"I don't know yet." Orsen's hands were fists at his sides. "But I'm going to find out. There's an archive here — the ghost-archives, Ember called them. Records of the purge. My father kept papers, hidden ones, and I think whatever they say will match what's written here. When I know the whole of it, I'll bring it out. Past the gate. To the Chancery itself if I have to."

A murmur ran through the hall — disbelief, hope, fear, all tangled.

"The Chancery," the old tile-player scoffed. "The Chancery wrote the erasure. You'd carry our names to the men who burned them?"

"Yes," Orsen said. "Because they're the only ones who can unburn them. And because—" He faltered, then steadied. Ember's shoulder pressed against his arm, small and warm and absolutely certain, and he drew on it like a well. "And because I spent thirty years being paid in silence. Mine and everyone else's. I'm done taking the wage."

From the back of the hall, a voice — young, sharp, familiar in a way that made Orsen's chest ache: "Prove it, then. Prove it or leave, Dunmore. We've had enough of Dunmores and their promises."

"I intend to," Orsen said.

Above them, through the high windows, the first grey hint of approaching dawn touched the copper roofs. All around the hall, the lanterns dimmed — not going out, but *leaning*, like candles in a draft. Sefa Ilvane looked up, and her face tightened.

"You need to go," she said. "Now. The archive is beneath the Hall of Records, three streets east — follow the blue lanterns, they last longest. Take what you need and be outside the walls before full light, or you'll spend the day as part of the city. And Orsen Dunmore—" She caught his sleeve again, and this time her grip was different. Not desperate. Deliberate. "In the archive, look for the correspondence. Your father wrote letters. The last one never left the city."

"How do you know that?"

"Because I was the one who was supposed to carry it," Sefa said. "The night everything ended. It's still in the archive, sealed, addressed to the Chancery tribunal at Greywater. He wrote it the week before the purge, and whatever's in it, he wanted *them* to read it. Not you. Not anyone." Her eyes held his. "Read it anyway. Some promises are worth breaking."

The blue lanterns led them east through streets that were already softening at the edges, houses going translucent, the fiddle music thinning into memory. Behind them, faint through the dissolving morning, Orsen heard the city singing — the key-song, hundreds of voices, following him out like a hand laid between his shoulders.

Three streets east, in a hall that would not exist by noon, a sealed letter waited with his father's name on it.

Orsen walked faster.
