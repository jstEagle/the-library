## 16. What His Father Knew

The archives of Corvane-in-exile smelled the way all dead things smell when they refuse to admit it: like dust and candle smoke and something faintly sweet underneath, the way a pear goes just before it turns.

Orsen had been walking among the shelves for what felt like hours, though time in the ghost-city was a rumor at best. The city existed only between dusk and dawn — that much Ember had told him, that much the songs agreed on — and so the light here was permanent twilight, amber and forgiving, the kind of light that made everything look kinder than it was. The shelves rose out of sight into a darkness that was not empty. He could feel it watching. The whole city watched. That was the trouble with ghosts: they had nothing to do but remember, and they did it thoroughly.

"Third alcove," said a voice at his elbow, and Orsen did not jump, because he had learned by now that jumping only encouraged them.

The speaker was a woman in the grey dress of a Vessani archivist, her sleeves rolled as if she'd stepped away from her desk a moment ago to answer a question. She had been dead for thirty years. Her ink-stained fingers were the most alive thing about her.

"Third alcove," she repeated, with the patience of someone who had waited three decades for a patron. "Official correspondence of the Keeper's line. Your father filed everything, Warden Dunmore. Everything. It was rather his defining quality."

"He wasn't my father yet," Orsen said. "When he started filing."

"No," the archivist agreed, and something moved behind her eyes that might have been pity or might have been amusement; with the dead it was often both. "But he knew he would be. Men who make bargains always know." She turned and drifted down the aisle, and Orsen followed, because there was nowhere else to follow, and because the alternative was standing alone in a city of the dead thinking about what he'd come here to find.

He had come for the truth. He had told himself that on the road, through Merrowfen, across the singing stones where Ember had spent her voice like a spendthrift heir spending the last of the family silver. He had told himself it again this evening when the city assembled itself out of the dusk around him, lantern by lantern, street by remembered street. But now, standing before the third alcove, he admitted to himself — in the privacy of his own skull, which even ghosts were supposed to respect — that he had come hoping not to find it.

There is a particular cowardice in wanting the truth and wanting it buried in equal measure. Orsen Dunmore had thirty years of practice at it.

* * *

The alcove was narrow, walled with ledgers bound in grey leather, each spine stamped with the seal of the Hollow Gate: a door within a circle, the circle broken at the top. His father's seal. Orsen had pressed it into wax ten thousand times himself, reporting seals inspected, litany recited, valley quiet, nothing to declare. Thirty years of *nothing to declare*, filed in triplicate.

He pulled the earliest ledger down and it came away light, too light, and when he opened it the pages were blank.

Of course they were. This was Corvane-in-exile. Nothing here was paper. The books were memory wearing the shape of books, and memory could only be read by the people it belonged to. He was a Dunmore. These were Dunmore records. He set his palm flat against the first page and waited, the way you wait at a door you have no right to knock on.

The ink bloomed up under his hand like frost forming in reverse.

*Year One of the Silence. Inspection of the Hollow Gate completed. Seals hold. Litany recited in full. The valley remains forgetful. Nothing to declare.*

His own handwriting, or near enough — he recognized the small stubborn loop he made on his *g*s, a habit inherited without knowing from whom. He turned pages. Year Two. Year Five. Year Twelve, the year the fever took two goats and half the orchard and he had written, in a boy's careful hand, *the valley remains forgetful, though I confess I do not know what we are forgetting.* Someone — his father, reviewing the report — had struck that last sentence through with a single hard line, and beneath it written: *Do not confess. Do not wonder. Wonder is how doors open.*

Orsen stood in the amber light of a dead city and read thirty years of his own life summarized in a dead man's marginalia, and the strangest part, the part he would carry longest, was the tenderness in those corrections. His father had not scrawled. He had underlined gently. He had corrected the way you correct a child's letters — *this stroke goes thus, this thought goes nowhere* — and every correction was a muzzle fitted a little tighter.

"You're reading them out of order," said the archivist, materializing beside him with a candle she did not need. "Everyone does. They start at the beginning, because beginnings feel safe." She reached past him, her sleeve passing through his arm with a sensation like cold water remembering it had once been a river, and drew out a slim folio from the back of the shelf. "This one is last. Filed the night he died. He walked here himself, you know. Before the gate closed fully. Before the forgetting took the roads. He carried this in his coat and he read it aloud to us — to the council, such as we were — and then he asked us to keep it where no living Dunmore would ever look."

"Why?" Orsen asked. His voice came out smaller than he intended.

"Because he was ashamed," she said simply. "And because shame, properly filed, is just another kind of lock."

She held the folio out. For a long moment neither of them moved, and somewhere in the stacks a ghost sneezed, and the whole archive shushed him with the fond exasperation of people who had been shushing each other for eternity.

Orsen took the letter.

* * *

It was written in his father's hand, but not the careful hand of the ledgers. This was the hand of a man writing fast at a table with a candle burning down, the letters leaning like drunks, blotted twice, a third blot where something — a drop of wax, or worse — had fallen.

*To whoever comes after me,*

*If you are reading this, then either the bargain has failed or you have disobeyed me, and I find I cannot decide which I would prefer.*

*You will have been told, all your life, that the Hollow Gate protects Vessany. That the kingdom was purged and its people slaughtered and that we Dunmores stand watch over their grave so that the Chancery cannot dance on it. I told you this myself. I want it written plainly, in my own hand, that this was a lie I chose because it was a kind one, and kindness is the coin with which cowards pay their debts.*

*Here is the truth, and may it sit heavy on whoever inherits it:*

*I was not hired to protect the survivors of Vessany. I was hired to bury them.*

*The purge ended before you were born. The Chancery lost its appetite for slaughter — not from mercy, but from arithmetic; dead kingdoms make poor provinces and restless martyrs. What the Chancery feared was not Vessany living. It was Vessany testifying. A survivor who walks out of a hidden country and says* this was done to us, *with names and dates and a living face, is a wound in the Chancery's story that no amount of erasure can close. So they did not hunt the survivors. They hired a man to make sure none ever emerged.*

*They hired me.*

*I was young and the money was real and they told me a version of the truth, enough of it to bind me: that the gate must be kept, that the litany must be recited, that the valley must stay forgetful. They let me believe I was a shepherd. I signed the oath of silence with a full heart, God forgive me, and it was only later — after your mother, after you — that I understood what I had signed. Not a guardianship. A muzzle. My oath does not keep Vessany safe. It keeps Vessany silent. Every year the seals hold is a year no survivor can walk out and say what was done. I am not the keeper of a door. I am the cork in a bottle, and the wine inside is testimony.*

*You will hate me for this. Good. Hate is honest. What I cannot bear is what I did instead, which was to raise you inside the lie so carefully that you would never think to question it — to give you the litany like a rosary, duty like a spine, and call it love. Perhaps it was love. It was also a cage built by a man who could not afford to let you see the bars, because if you saw them, you might ask who built them, and I could not survive being seen.*

*There is a key-song. There is always a key-song; the Chancery's engineers were thorough but the old country was more thorough still. It is sung, not spoken, and it cannot be written down — I tried, and the ink would not hold it. Somewhere in Vessany there is a child being raised to sing it, and every year I keep the gate shut is a year that child grows older behind a door I locked for wages.*

*I have paid the Chancery back every coin. It changes nothing. Some debts are not counted in coin.*

*If you are my son: I am sorry. If you are anyone else: the gate opens from the far side too. Remember that. Whatever they told you about one-way doors, the old country built better than that. The door was never meant to be a wall.*

*— Aldous Dunmore, who was paid to forget, and forgot everything except this*

The letter ended. The candle the archivist held guttered, though there was no wind in a city of the dead.

Orsen discovered he was sitting on the floor. He had no memory of sitting down. The folio lay open in his lap and his hands were shaking, and the shaking was not grief, or not only grief. It was rage — rage with nowhere to go, filling him like water fills a sealed jar, pressing against the inside of him looking for a crack.

Thirty years. Thirty years of seals and litanies and *nothing to declare*. Thirty years of believing himself the last loyal servant of a murdered kingdom, and he had been the padlock. His father had not guarded the door. His father had *stood in front of it*, and been paid, and called it duty, and taught his son to call it duty, and died — Orsen was almost certain of this now — died of the weight of it, the way a beam dies of a house.

"Warden." The archivist crouched beside him, which ghosts did not have to do, and he understood it as a courtesy. "You should know the rest of it. The night he read this to the council, they voted. They voted to forgive him."

"They shouldn't have."

"They knew that," she said. "They forgave him anyway. That is rather what forgiveness is." She paused. "They also voted to send the key-song out. With a child. The youngest singer they had. Because a letter can be burned, Warden, and a song has to be caught."

Ember. Ember, mud-streaked and furious, calling him *the last Dunmore* with accusation in her voice — she had known, or guessed, or been told enough. She hadn't come to the gate by accident. She had been sent to the one man in the world whose whole life was the lock, carrying the key in her throat, and she had sung at his door until it hummed.

And he had nearly sent her back inside. Had stood in the council hall while elders demanded her resealed behind stone, and said nothing, because saying something meant admitting what the silence was *for*.

"I testified at Merrowfen," he said numbly. "At the toll bridge. I confessed my father's bargain aloud. I thought that was the worst of it. I thought I'd already said the shameful thing."

"You said his shame," the archivist said gently. "Not yours. Yours is still ahead of you. Most men's are." She helped him up — or pretended to; her hands passed through his elbows and left cold where they touched, but the gesture lifted him anyway, the way intention can lift a body when strength won't. "There is one more thing in the folio. Under the letter. He hid it there because he could not bring himself to file it honestly."

Orsen looked down. Beneath the letter, folded small, was a single sheet in a different hand — a clerk's hand, neat and official, stamped with the Chancery's grey seal.

A receipt.

*Received of Aldous Dunmore, Keeper of the Hollow Gate, the sum owed against services rendered, in full. The office of the Chancery acknowledges the continued closure of the subject territory and considers the matter concluded. Should any person emerge from the subject territory bearing testimony, the obligation of the Keeper and his heirs shall be deemed renewed, and enforcement shall pass to the Office of Inquisition.*

Dated thirty-one years ago. Signed by a name Orsen didn't know, above a title he did: *Undersecretary, Erasure of Record.*

Enforcement shall pass to the Office of Inquisition.

Serah Vole. Grey riders. Polite questions about anomalous children. She wasn't chasing a runaway girl. She was collecting on a debt — and the debt, according to a clerk's neat hand, ran through the Dunmore bloodline like a name woven into cloth.

"She'll be here by dawn," Orsen said. The words came out flat and calm, which surprised him; the rage had gone somewhere deep and gone still, the way a river goes still just before it goes over the edge. "The city dissolves at dawn. She knows that. Everyone knows that."

"The dead know it," the archivist agreed. "Whether the living have told her—" She stopped. Turned her head toward the archive doors, toward the streets beyond, where the lanterns of Corvane-in-exile were burning lower than they should, dusk-light thinning at the edges like breath on glass.

And Orsen heard it too, faint under the hush of the shelves: hoofbeats. On streets that existed only between dusk and dawn, hoofbeats — which meant someone had found a way to ride into a city that wasn't there, or had been waiting at its edge since sundown, patient as arithmetic, for the sun to rise.

He folded the letter into his coat, over his heart, where his father had carried it the other direction. Then he ran for the doors, shouting the only name that mattered, into a city beginning to forget itself:

"Ember!"
