## 17. Vole Closes In

Serah Vole had been awake for three days, and she found she rather liked it.

Not the headaches — those were considerable, arriving now on a schedule like a punctual clerk — but the clarity. Sleep was where sentiment crept in. Sleep was where a person's mind wandered off its leash and came back carrying things it had no business carrying: her mother's kitchen, the smell of tallow and bread, the particular way her brother had laughed before the fever took him at nine years old. Awake, she was only what the Chancery had made her, which was to say, useful.

"Tell me again," she said to the toll-keeper, who sat trussed to his own bridge post with the resigned expression of a man who had made a living out of bad bargains and finally met one that collected.

The man — Merrowfen's toll-master, a smuggler of forty years' standing named Odo Brack — sighed through his nose. "You've asked me eleven times."

"And you'll tell me twelve, because the twelfth time I'll believe you're not lying." She held up the truth-reader's lens between two fingers. It was an ugly little instrument, a disc of smoked glass in a brass ring, and it did nothing so dramatic as glow or hum. It simply showed her, when she looked through it at a face, whether the words coming out of that face were true. Most people were mostly true, which was the great disappointment of Vole's career. Liars were rare. What people did instead was worse: they told the truth in shapes designed to mislead, like giving directions that were accurate and useless.

Odo Brack had told her the fugitives' route four times, and every time, the lens had shown her a man telling the truth about a road he clearly wished did not exist.

"They went north along the old drover path," he said, for the fifth time, wearily. "Toward Corvane."

"There is no Corvane," said the young lieutenant beside her, a round-faced boy called Pellin who still apologized to furniture he bumped into.

"There is no Corvane *on maps*," Vole corrected. "Maps are the Chancery's first and most successful lie, Lieutenant. We wrote the world down the way we wanted it remembered, and then we forgot we were the ones holding the pen." She turned back to Brack. "What is Corvane?"

Brack looked at her for a long moment. Through the lens, Vole watched him decide something — watched the truth assemble itself behind his eyes like weather.

"Corvane," he said, "is the capital of Vessany. And Vessany is the country your Chancery burned off the world thirty years ago, and it never entirely agreed to be burned." He smiled, and it was not a pleasant smile, but it was an honest one; the lens confirmed even that. "It comes back at dusk. Every night. Between dusk and dawn there's a city on the moor above the Singing Stones, full of people who should be dead, and they have been waiting thirty years for someone from outside to walk in and tell them why."

Vole felt the headache shift gears.

"And the girl," she said. "Ember. The child. Is she one of them?"

"She's better than one of them." Brack's voice dropped, and for the first time since the interrogation began, he sounded afraid — not of Vole, which was sensible, but of something else. "She's the key-song. There's a door, Inquisitor. A real one. And that child can open it, and everything your masters buried comes walking out into the daylight wearing its own name." He leaned forward against his ropes as far as they allowed. "So let me ask *you* something, since we're trading truths. When you find them — and you will, because you're good at this, gods rot you — what exactly do you plan to do with a country?"

Vole stood, brushed ash from her grey coat, and gave him the answer she had given a hundred frightened clerks and one weeping archivist over the course of her career.

"I plan to finish the mercy," she said. "Properly, this time."

* * *

They reached the moor above the Singing Stones an hour before dusk, and Vole ordered the company to halt and watch.

Pellin thought she was being cautious. The truth was she wanted to see it herself, with her own unassisted eyes, before she trusted it to any report. Thirty years ago, as a junior clerk in the Office of Boundaries, she had personally inked the word WILDERNESS across the space where Vessany used to be. She had been twenty-two years old and proud of her steady hand. She had believed, then, that erasure was a kind of kindness — that a kingdom wiped clean could not fester, could not breed pretenders and grievances and wars of restoration. Unfinished business, her first mentor had called it, tapping the map. Unfinished kingdoms bleed.

Now she stood on a cold moor and watched the sun go down, and the wilderness finished itself.

It did not happen all at once. First there was a shimmer, like heat off a summer road though the evening was freezing. Then a line — a wall, low and ancient, running along a contour of the hill that had been empty a moment before. Then roofs. Then spires. Then light, hundreds of windows lighting themselves in sequence, as if the whole city were a lamp being lit wick by wick by some enormous patient hand. The stones of it were dark gold. The streets below the walls were cobbled, and she could hear — she pressed her hand flat against her own sternum to check that the sound wasn't coming from inside her — she could hear bells.

"Gods," whispered Pellin. "Inquisitor, the men are—"

"I can see what the men are doing." Half her soldiers had knelt. Two had begun, quietly and without any apparent awareness of doing so, to pray — to gods whose names the Chancery had struck from the calendar a generation ago, and which they nevertheless seemed to remember perfectly.

"Stand them up," Vole said. "Gently. We don't enter tonight. Tonight we learn the city's habits." She settled onto a rock, produced her journal, and began, in her steady clerk's hand, to take notes. "Everything that returns must also leave. Find me the hour of its leaving."

* * *

The city left at dawn.

That was the pattern, established over two nights of patient watching: Corvane-in-exile assembled itself at dusk out of nothing and memory, lived its brief nightly life — lights, bells, market stalls that sold nothing to anyone, figures in the streets going about errands thirty years stale — and then, as the sky paled, it dissolved. Not violently. That was the worst part, Vole thought, watching through her spyglass from the ridge. It dissolved the way frost leaves a window: gracefully, reluctantly, thinning from the edges inward until the last thing visible was a single lit window high in a tower, which held on for nearly a minute after everything else had gone, and then went out.

And in that last minute, the ghosts did something she hadn't seen on the previous nights.

They gathered in the main square. All of them — hundreds of figures, translucent at their edges, solid-seeming at their hearts — and they turned, all together, toward the tower window, and they waited.

"Something's coming out of that tower," Pellin murmured. "Every night. Look — there."

A figure emerged onto the tower steps. Small. A child, unmistakably, even at this distance, even through glass: the quickness of the movement, the stubborn set of the shoulders. Beside the child, taller, slower, a man in a keeper's drab coat, moving like a man carrying something heavy that no one else could see.

"The fugitives," Vole said. Her heart did not speed up; she had trained that out of it decades ago. But her hand, holding the spyglass, was perfectly steady in the way that cost effort. "They've been sleeping in the ghost-city. Clever. We couldn't raid it — my orders stand, nobody enters while it stands — but it won't stand much longer." She lowered the glass. "Lieutenant, take twelve men and circle wide to the north road, the one the toll-keeper described. If they run when the city goes, they'll run for the road. The rest come with me, down into the square. We take the child alive and unhurt. Am I understood? Alive and *unhurt*. Whatever else happens this morning, we are not the ones who give this story a martyr."

"Yes, Inquisitor." Pellin hesitated. "And the man? Dunmore?"

Vole considered. She had read the file on Orsen Dunmore twice — thin file, sad file, thirty years of a man's life reduced to inspection logs and ration requisitions. A warden who kept a door shut. A son who kept a silence he'd inherited without ever being told what it purchased.

"Dunmore," she said, "I'd very much like to talk to. Whether he wants to talk to me is another matter. Move."

* * *

The trouble with descending into a dissolving city, Vole discovered, was that the city objected.

Not with violence. With *memory*. As her boots crossed the threshold of the outer wall — a wall that was, by daylight logic, a tumble of mossy stones and nothing more — the air thickened around her, and the streets filled. Not with ghosts blocking her way; they passed through her patrol like smoke through fingers, barely noticing. With sound. With names. Somewhere a woman was calling a child in from supper in a language Vole did not know and yet understood, the way one understands a tune heard once in childhood. Shop signs flickered into legibility overhead. A fountain appeared in the middle of the square, mid-splash, water that fell and vanished before it landed.

"Keep formation," Vole said, and hated how her voice echoed, how the city seemed to lean in and listen to it. "Eyes on the tower."

The square was emptying. The ghosts were streaming toward the tower steps, and the child and the man were coming down those steps, and Vole saw the exact moment Ember saw the grey coats.

The girl grabbed Orsen's sleeve and said something sharp in Old Vessani — Vole caught the shape of it, a warning, a curse, possibly both — and the two of them broke for the north side of the square, straight toward Pellin's waiting net, and Vole permitted herself one small cold moment of satisfaction. Fleeing targets chose doors, and she had locked all the doors but hers.

Then the child stopped.

Vole saw it happen and would spend many nights afterward replaying it. Twenty feet from the alley mouth, twenty feet from freedom, Ember skidded to a halt and turned — turned *back*, toward the fountain, where a ghost had become visible in a way the others weren't. An old woman, kneeling at the fountain's dry basin, scrabbling at the stone with her hands, and even at this distance Vole could see the difference: the other ghosts were fading evenly, gracefully, but this one was *stuck*, snagged on something, her outline tearing like cloth on a nail, and she was making a sound —

She was making a sound like a kettle left too long on the fire, rising toward a scream that had been interrupted thirty years ago and never finished.

"Oh no," said Orsen Dunmore, audibly, from across the square. "No, Ember, we don't have time — Ember!"

The child did not listen. Children, in Vole's long experience, almost never did; it was one of the few constants of her profession. Ember ran to the old woman, dropped to her knees beside her, took the ghost's hands in her own small ones, and began to sing.

Vole had heard the key-song described by the toll-keeper, by informants, by a half-mad shepherd who claimed to have heard it through a hillside. None of them had prepared her. It was not a complicated melody. It was four notes, maybe five, arranged in a way that made the morning feel briefly like an argument she was losing — and every ghost in the square turned toward it, and the stuck old woman went quiet, and then, slowly, like ice releasing a held branch, she came unstuck. Her torn edges smoothed. She looked down at the child. She said something — one word, soft, in Old Vessani — and Vole, who did not speak Old Vessani, understood it anyway, the way you understand a bell:

*Thank you.*

Then the woman faded properly, the way the others faded, and was gone, and the song ended, and the city ended, and the dawn came down like a verdict.

And Ember was still kneeling at the fountain. Alone. Visible. Surrounded.

"To her," Vole said. "Gently. *Gently.*"

* * *

They took her without a blow struck, which Vole counted as the day's single mercy. Four soldiers, closing in a quiet ring while the girl was still looking after the place where the old woman had been. By the time Ember understood what was happening, it was over; a corporal had her hands, not roughly, and Pellin was saying "it's all right, it's all right, nobody's going to hurt you" in the voice of a young man who desperately needed it to be true.

Ember did not cry. Vole noted that. The girl went rigid, then calculating, her eyes raking the square, the alleys, the ridge — looking for one person, and finding the square empty.

Because Orsen Dunmore was gone.

Vole walked to the fountain and stood where the child had knelt. The stone was ordinary stone. The city was ordinary ruins. On the far side of the square, the north alley mouth gaped, empty, leading — according to Pellin's breathless runner — to a road that was already empty, hoofprints in frost already going soft in the strengthening sun.

"He left her," said the corporal, and there was disgust in it. Soldiers had simple arithmetic: you didn't leave a child.

"He didn't," Vole said quietly.

Through the truth-reader's lens — she had raised it without quite deciding to — she looked at Ember's face, and the lens showed her what it always showed her: truth. The girl's face was a document written in a language of fury and grief, and every line of it was honest, and what it said was this: *he didn't want to leave. She pushed him. She sang him away. She spent her last song not on the ghost, or not only on the ghost — she spent it making sure the man ran, because the man was carrying something that mattered more than either of them.*

"What did you send him away with?" Vole asked the child.

Ember looked up at her. Mud-streaked, furious, eight years old at most, and utterly unafraid in a way that Vole found genuinely unsettling, because fear was a tool she knew how to use, and this child appeared to have been built without the handle.

"You wouldn't understand it," Ember said. "It's a song. You'd have to have lost something to sing it right."

"I've lost a great deal," Vole said, and was surprised to find it was true; the lens, had anyone been reading her, would have confirmed it.

"Then maybe," said Ember, chin lifting, "you should think about *why* everyone around you keeps losing things, Grey Lady."

Vole crouched down to the child's level. Around them the camp was striking itself, horses stamping, the moor returning to its mapped and lawful emptiness. Somewhere north, a man was running along a drover road with a song in his head and no voice left in the world that could sing it.

"Here is what happens now," Vole said, gently, because gentleness was also a tool and she had never once needed to sharpen it. "You'll ride with us to Greywater. You'll be fed, warmed, treated with every courtesy. And in a few weeks, you'll watch a tribunal — a fair one, I promise you, I have served under worse and I am not lying — where a man named Orsen Dunmore has asked to testify." She paused. "He sent word ahead. Did you know that? A rider overtook us at the Stones. Your keeper has traded himself for you. His testimony for your release and safe passage for his hidden kingdom."

For the first time, the child's armor cracked. Not into tears — into something worse, something Vole recognized from her own mirror at nineteen: the look of someone realizing the bill has come due for a bargain they didn't know they'd signed.

"He can't," Ember whispered. "He doesn't know what it costs. He doesn't know what *they'll*—"

"He knows exactly what it costs," Vole said, standing. "That's what testimony is, child. It's the only coin that spends everywhere, and it always spends the person who pays it." She turned away, then paused, and added — because the truth-reader on her belt hummed faintly, obligingly, confirming it — "I intend to see the trade honored. Whatever else I intend."

They rode south through a country that did not remember its own name, and Serah Vole rode at the head of the column with a silent child behind her and a headache like a second skull, thinking about songs, and keys, and the peculiar, catastrophic honesty of keepers.

Behind them, far to the north, a door with no singer waited in a valley, humming softly to itself, patient as stone.

Orsen Dunmore had three weeks to learn to sing.
