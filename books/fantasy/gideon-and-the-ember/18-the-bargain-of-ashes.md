## 18. The Bargain of Ashes

They kept Ember in a tent at the edge of the camp, which told Orsen two things: that the Chancery still thought of her as evidence rather than prisoner, and that Serah Vole had not yet decided what to do with either of them.

He learned this from the smuggler's boy who brought him his supper — bread, hard cheese, and a cup of wine so thin it might have been apologizing for itself. The boy set the tray down on the crate that served as Orsen's table in his borrowed cell, which was not a cell at all but a disused smokehouse with a new lock on the door and a view of pig troughs through a gap in the boards.

"She's in the grey tent," the boy said, in the tone of someone reciting a lesson he'd been paid to learn. "The one with the lantern outside. There's only two guards, and one of them snores."

"Thank you," Orsen said gravely. "That is the finest intelligence I have received all day."

"It's the only intelligence you've received all day."

"All the more precious, then." He fished a coin from his pocket — he had exactly three left, and gave away the largest, because generosity was easier when it was nearly free — and the boy pocketed it with the smooth efficiency of a customs official and was gone.

Orsen sat down on the crate and did not eat.

He looked at the bread instead, and thought about bargains.

His whole life, he understood now, had been shaped by one he'd never been party to. His father had traded silence for safety, and Orsen had spent thirty years paying installments on a debt he didn't know existed. He had tended the Hollow Gate the way a man tends a grave — faithfully, daily, without ever asking what was buried there. And when the answer finally climbed out of the ground, mud-streaked and furious and singing, he had discovered that everything he'd called duty was really just the shape of his cage.

So he was done with other people's bargains.

He was going to make his own.

* * *

Serah Vole received him in the command tent an hour after dawn, because of course she did. Inquisitors, Orsen was learning, believed that suffering improved before breakfast the way wine did.

She sat behind a folding desk with a ledger open on it, and she did not look up when the guards brought him in. She let him stand. It was a small cruelty, expertly applied — the kind of thing you could do to a man for ten minutes and leave no mark, and yet he would remember it longer than the burns on his hands from the burning of the Hollow.

"You look terrible," she said at last, still writing.

"I've been sleeping in a smokehouse."

"The pigs complained about the competition." She finished her line, blotted it, and folded her hands. "Sit."

There was one chair. He took it. Up close, Vole looked exactly as she had in the keeper's house a lifetime ago — grey eyes, greyer patience, a face built for listening to lies and finding them wanting. But there were new lines around her mouth now, and Orsen, who had spent decades reading seals and knew something about cracks, thought they looked less like age than like doubt.

"You want to say something," she said. "You've got the face of a man rehearsing a speech."

"I've had two days to rehearse it."

"And a locked door to rehearse it against. Go on, then."

Orsen laced his fingers together to keep them steady. "I'll testify."

Vole went very still. It was the first genuinely human thing he'd seen her do.

"Before your tribunal," he said. "Sworn testimony. Everything I know about the purge of Vessany. The dates. The orders. The names of the dead — I have them, all of them, my father kept the litany and I've carried it in my head since I was nine years old. Every village. Every fire. Every name the Chancery struck from its own records."

"Why," Vole said quietly, "would you hand me the rope?"

"Because I'm naming conditions."

"Of course you are." Something flickered across her face — not quite a smile, but the ghost of one, the way a fish ghosts under ice. "Let's hear them, then, Keeper."

"One. Ember goes free. Not paroled, not watched, not 'released into Chancery custody,' which is a phrase I've noticed means the opposite of released. Free. She walks out of this camp and keeps walking, and nobody follows her."

"Two?"

"Two. Safe passage for the people behind the Hollow Gate. All of them. They come out of hiding under Chancery protection — real protection, written into law where your successors can't pretend they never saw it. They are citizens of somewhere again. That's non-negotiable. If you strike it, I walk out of here and you spend the next twenty years chasing a country that exists only in songs, and I promise you, madam, I have seen what those songs can do to standing stones."

Vole's pen had stopped moving. "And three?"

Orsen leaned forward. This was the part he'd rehearsed against the smokehouse door until the hinges knew it too.

"Three. You don't get to decide afterward that it didn't happen. My testimony is entered into the permanent record. Copies distributed. The purge of Vessany becomes a fact the Chancery has to live with, the way a man has to live with a scar — visible, permanent, and his own fault."

The tent was very quiet. Outside, a guard coughed; somewhere farther off, the camp's cook-fire crackled, indifferent as the sun.

"You understand," Vole said slowly, "what you're offering me."

"I'm offering you the end of it. That's what you want, isn't it? You told me once that unfinished kingdoms breed wars. Well, here's your chance to finish one properly — not with ash and erasure, but with a record. A closed account. You burn me after the tribunal if you like. Hang me, exile me, whatever pleases the Chancery's sense of theater. But the testimony stands, and the Vessani stand under your protection, and the girl walks free. Three things, Serah Vole, and then you can close the book."

"And why," she said, and now the stillness in her had a different quality, sharper, "do you imagine I would believe anything a Dunmore swears? Your family's word bought thirty years of silence. Your father sold a kingdom for a pension and a title. Why should the last Dunmore be any different?"

It was a fair question. It deserved better than a fair answer.

"Because," Orsen said, "my father made his bargain sitting safely behind a locked gate, trading things that belonged to other people. I'm making mine in your tent, trading the only thing I have left that's actually mine." He spread his hands. "My name. My freedom. Whatever's left of my life. I'm not selling silence, Inquisitor. I'm buying testimony, and the price is everything I've got. You've interrogated enough men to know what that looks like. Liars hold something back. I'm holding nothing."

Vole studied him for a long moment. Then she did something he hadn't expected: she closed the ledger.

"Do you know what I found in your house?" she asked. "After we burned it."

"Ashes, mostly."

"In the cellar. Under the floor where the child hid. A strongbox." She opened a drawer and set something on the desk between them: a small iron box, dented, its lock sprung. "Your father's papers. The ones you didn't take when you ran."

Orsen's chest tightened. "Those are—"

"Evidence. Which makes them mine. But I read them." She said it flatly, without apology, and he realized that was the closest she could come to confession. "Thirty years of reports, Keeper. Signed receipts. The Chancery paid your father annually, and every year he signed a statement confirming that the survivors of Vessany remained contained, and every year some clerk filed it and forgot it. Do you know what that means?"

"That the Chancery always knew."

"It means the Chancery *paid*. For three decades. Out of a budget line labeled 'Provincial Stability.'" Her mouth twisted. "Erasure is expensive, Master Dunmore. It requires maintenance. And it requires men like my predecessors, who understood that the crime wasn't finished until the last witness was either dead or complicit." She tapped the strongbox. "Your father chose complicit. You're choosing witness. I confess I find the distinction interesting."

"Is that a yes?"

"It's a *perhaps*, which from an inquisitor is practically a marriage proposal." She stood, walked to the tent flap, and looked out at the morning. When she spoke again, her back was to him, and her voice had lost its courtroom edge. "Do you know why I hunt survivors, Master Dunmore? Truly?"

"Enlighten me."

"Because I was six years old when the border wars ended. I remember the refugee camps. I remember what happens when a defeated people are left stateless in the hills for a generation — the raids, the reprisals, the children raised on grievance like it was mother's milk. I have stood in the ruins of towns that burned because somebody's grandfather remembered a wrong, and I swore, when I took the grey cloak, that I would spend my life making sure no wrong outlived its witnesses." She turned. "I thought forgetting was mercy. I still think it can be. But your father's version of forgetting—" she nudged the strongbox with one boot, "—that was just murder with good filing."

"So help me finish it honestly."

"I am going to ask you a question," Vole said, "and I want you to understand that your answer will determine whether I spend the rest of my career defending this bargain or burying it. Are you doing this for the girl? For the Vessani? Or are you doing it because a broken man wants to die loudly rather than quietly?"

Orsen considered lying. It lasted about a second, the way a candle lasts in a high wind.

"Yes," he said.

Vole waited.

"All three. The girl because she deserves a sky bigger than a valley. The Vessani because they've paid thirty years of rent on a prison their jailers forgot they owned. And myself—" he exhaled, "—because I spent my whole life being the lock, madam, and I would like, before the end, to be the key. Even if the key gets snapped off in the door afterward."

For a moment — just a moment — Serah Vole looked at him the way one soldier looks at another across a field that both of them survived by luck.

"Terms accepted," she said. "Preliminarily. The tribunal convenes at Greywater in eleven days. You'll swear your testimony before three judges of the High Bench, under truth-oath, and I warn you, the oath is not ceremonial. Lie under it and it will show, and then everything we've agreed dies in that room."

"I haven't lied in eleven days," Orsen said. "I'm out of practice and improving."

Something that was almost, almost a laugh escaped her. She smothered it professionally.

"One more condition," she added, gathering the strongbox. "The girl stays in the grey tent until Greywater. Under my personal watch — not the guards'. I give you my word she comes to no harm. I know what my word is worth to you," she said, before he could speak, "which is why I'm giving it anyway. Spend it or don't."

"I'll spend it," Orsen said. "But I'd like to see her. Before we travel."

Vole considered. Then she lifted the tent flap and said something to the guard outside, and a minute later they brought Ember in.

She looked smaller than he remembered, which was absurd, because nothing about Ember had ever been small — but two days in a cage will shrink anyone. Her chin was up, though. Her chin was always up; he suspected it had been welded that way at manufacture. She took in the scene — Orsen seated, Vole standing, the strongbox, the ledger — with the swift, total assessment of a general reviewing a battlefield.

"What did you do?" she demanded.

"I made a deal."

"What kind of deal?"

"The kind where I talk a lot and you go free."

Her eyes narrowed. "How much talking?"

"Eleven days' worth. Maybe twelve, if the judges are chatty."

Ember looked at Vole. Vole looked at Ember. Two professionals, Orsen thought, each estimating the other's weight class.

"He's telling the truth," Ember said at last, grudgingly, to Vole. "His ears go red when he lies. Right now they're just cold."

"Thank you," Orsen said, "for that rigorous verification."

"You're welcome." But she crossed the tent and gripped his sleeve, and her voice dropped to the register she saved for things that mattered. "Orsen. The litany. When you say it — don't say it like your father wrote it. Say it like the songs go. Names first, then places, then what was done. The judges won't understand it, but the room will. Rooms remember faster than people do."

"I'll say it like the songs," he promised.

"And Orsen?" Her grip tightened. "When it's over. When they've heard everything. Don't let them hang you out of politeness. Fight. You're terrible at it, but fight anyway."

"I'll be terrible at it with distinction."

She held his eyes a moment longer, then let go, and the guards walked her back toward the grey tent, and Orsen watched her go with the peculiar lightness of a man who has finally put down something heavy — not because his burden ended, but because he had, at long last, chosen it himself.

* * *

They traveled to Greywater in a Chancery coach, which Orsen found darkly funny. Thirty years guarding a hidden kingdom, and his first ride in comfort came as a prisoner. The seats were padded. The windows had glass in them. There was a small decanter of wine, and he drank it entirely out of spite.

Vole rode opposite him, reading dispatches. On the second day, without looking up, she said:

"The tribunal judges arrived yesterday. Three of them. Chancellor's appointees, all. One of them, Aldous Renwick, signed the original budget line for your father's payments."

Orsen lowered his cup. "You're telling me one of my judges helped pay for the crime I'm testifying to."

"I'm telling you," Vole said, turning a page, "that when you speak the litany, you should watch his face. Because whatever happens in that room — whatever they rule, however they vote — a man cannot un-hear his own signature being read aloud."

Outside the window, the hills rolled past, green and ordinary, the sort of country that maps described confidently and completely, having forgotten it had ever known better.

Orsen touched the place inside his coat where the litany lived — not on paper anymore; paper burned — and began, silently, to practice.

Names first, Ember had said. Then places. Then what was done.

At Greywater, in front of three judges and a hall full of clerks, he intended to say all of it.

And he was beginning to suspect — watching Vole watch him, watching the road unspool toward a city that did not yet know what was coming — that the room would not stay quiet.
