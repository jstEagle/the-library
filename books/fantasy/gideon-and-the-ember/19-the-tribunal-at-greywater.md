## 19. The Tribunal at Greywater

They shaved me the night before.

Not my face — they left that alone, as if a beard were somehow load-bearing to my testimony — but everything else. My pockets. My boots. The seams of my coat, picked apart stitch by stitch by a clerk with small quick hands and an expression of profound apology. When she found nothing but lint and a button I'd been meaning to sew back on for eleven years, she bowed, gathered her scissors, and said, "Thank you for your cooperation, Keeper Dunmore."

"Orsen," I said. "Just Orsen."

She paused at the door. "That's not how the records list you."

"No," I agreed. "It isn't."

The cell they gave me in Greywater was better than my own house had been, which I tried not to hold against anyone. A real bed. A window that looked out on the river, grey-green and patient, carrying barges of coal and cabbages past a city that had never once thought about Vessany. Somewhere below, gulls argued over something. I lay awake most of the night listening to them, and it occurred to me — somewhere around the third hour, in the way that terrible clarity arrives only when you are horizontal and helpless — that I was not afraid.

I kept waiting for the fear. It was the sensible thing to feel. In the morning I would stand before three High Chancellors of the Chancery of Records and Reconciliation and tell them, in detail, what their institution had done, what my father had been paid to bury, and where several hundred living people were hidden behind a door their predecessors had sworn did not exist. Serah Vole stood to lose everything if I spoke well. So, frankly, did I. Fear seemed appropriate.

But all I could think about was Ember's voice going out of her like a candle pinched between wet fingers, and the way she'd still mouthed the words of the key-song at me through the bars of the wagon, shaping each syllable with fierce silent precision: *you know this, old man, you've known it your whole life, sing it.*

And beneath that, quieter, my father's letter, which I had read so many times in the ghost-archives of Corvane that the paper had gone soft as cloth in my hands:

*They did not pay me to protect them, boy. They paid me to make sure no one could ever say what happened. You are not a keeper. You are a gag with a salary.*

I had spent thirty years reciting a litany I believed was a prayer. It was not a prayer. It was a witness statement, worn smooth by repetition, waiting for someone to deliver it.

I slept two hours and dreamed of nothing at all, which after the year I'd had felt like a luxury.

* * *

The tribunal chamber at Greywater had been a granary once, before the Chancery bought the town. You could still see it in the bones of the place — the great timber roof arched like a ribcage, the high slit windows meant for throwing down grain, now fitted with glass and hung with banners of grey silk. Three tiers of benches rose on either side, and they were full. Clerks, advocates, observers from half a dozen ministries, all of them here because word had gotten out that the warden of the Hollow Gate had *asked* to testify. Nobody asked to testify before the Chancery. People were dragged to it. Asking was either madness or a trap, and the room had come to find out which.

Serah Vole sat at the prosecutors' table, grey as ever, hands folded, spine straight as a plumb line. She looked rested. That frightened me more than anything else in the room. She caught my eye as they led me to the witness floor, and she gave me a single nod — courteous, almost warm — the nod of a woman who has read every document in the building and knows precisely how this ends.

For her, I mean. Not for me.

The three High Chancellors sat above us on a dais of black stone. Chancellor Ossory presided, a wide soft man with the face of a patient ox; to his left, Chancellor Ives, thin and dry as winter wheat; to his right, Chancellor Maren, who was young for the office and had a reputation — I would learn later — for asking questions she shouldn't. Between them, on a lectern of its own, lay the object the whole morning turned on: the Great Register of the Realm, the master map-book of the Chancery, four hundred years of borders and names, open to the page marked *Northern Provinces*.

Where Vessany should have been, there was nothing. Not blank — blank would have been honest. There was a valley drawn in careful ink, labeled *unproductive upland*, with a note in the margin about poor drainage. Thirty years of my life, dismissed as bad soil.

Ossory cleared his throat. "Orsen Dunmore. You have petitioned this tribunal to give testimony under the Rite of Full Disclosure. Understand what you ask. Under the Rite, every word you speak is bound. If you lie, the binding will find it, and the penalty is forfeiture of name and goods. If you tell the truth—" he paused, and something flickered behind his heavy face, "—the truth is also binding. On everyone who hears it. Do you understand?"

"I understand," I said.

"You are not obliged to do this. Inquisitor Vole has offered terms. Your silence, your return to the valley, no further charges—"

"With respect, Chancellor." I looked at Vole. She met my eyes without blinking. "Her terms require me to forget. I've done thirty years of forgetting. I'm told I'm very good at it. I'd like to try the other thing."

A rustle went through the benches. Somewhere a clerk dropped a pen.

Ossory sighed like a man watching weather come in. "Then proceed. The tribunal hears you."

I had expected a podium. There wasn't one. They simply left me standing on the granary floor, in the middle of all that attention, and I discovered that thirty years of talking to a door is excellent preparation for talking to a room. You learn to speak without expecting an answer. You learn that the words matter more than whether anyone is listening.

Except this time, someone was listening. Everyone was listening. And the words —

I took a breath, and began.

"In the year the Chancery calls 411, in the month of Frostmelt, soldiers of the Third Purification entered the kingdom of Vessany by the pass at Carrow's Teeth."

The words came out in Old Vessani first. I hadn't planned that. I had rehearsed in Common, sensibly, practically, the way you rehearse anything you want to survive. But the litany lived in me in its original tongue, learned at my father's knee from a book I'd been told was a prayer book, and when I opened my mouth the old language came up like water finding its level.

I watched it move through the room.

It started small. A clerk in the second tier — a round-faced young man with ink on his fingers — went still over his notes, head tilting, the way a dog tilts its head at a sound it almost knows. Then a woman near him. Then the advocate beside Vole, whose pen stopped moving entirely.

Because here is the thing nobody in that room knew, and I only half-knew it myself until I said it aloud: the litany was not written to be understood. It was written to be *remembered*. Every name in it was a hook set in the language itself, and the language had been waiting, patient as my father's god, for ears that carried even a drop of Vessani blood. And everyone carries some. That was the whole horror of the purge — you cannot burn a country out of the people without leaving ash in them. The Chancery had spent four centuries grinding the name off the maps and off the stones and out of the songs, and had never once thought to check the blood.

"—and at Carrow's Teeth the soldiers were commanded by Warden-Lieutenant Hask, who ordered the bridge burned with the harvest carts upon it, that the winter might do what the sword could not. I name Hask. I name the date. I name the dead: Aldous Merrin, cartwright. Sefa Merrin, his wife. Their daughters, Bree and Tansy Merrin, aged nine and six."

Each name landed in the room like a stone dropped in still water.

The map changed first.

I saw it happen — I don't expect anyone to believe me, but I saw it, and so did three hundred other people, which is why you can check. The Great Register lay open on its lectern, and as I spoke the names, the ink on the Northern Provinces page began to *move*. Slowly, like frost spreading across a window, lines redrawed themselves. The little valley marked *unproductive upland* grew roads. The roads grew bridges. Names surfaced in the margins like fish rising — Carrow's Teeth, Merrowfen, the Singing Stones, Corvane — and then, in letters that deepened from grey to black as I spoke, one name across the whole of it:

**VESSANY.**

Chancellor Ives stood up so fast his chair went over backward. "Stop the record! Someone stop the—"

"The record," said Chancellor Maren quietly, "is doing exactly what the record is for." She had risen too, but slowly, and she was looking at the map the way people look at the sea during a storm: unable to look away.

I kept speaking.

I told them about the granaries sealed with the people inside. I named the clerks who had signed the requisitions for the sealing — and here I watched three families' worth of descendants in the benches go white, because their ancestors' signatures were in the litany, and the litany does not distinguish between the sword and the pen. I named the Chancery archivists who burned the birth-rolls. I named the song-collectors who traveled village to village buying ballads for fair prices and feeding them to fires. I named my father, Aldric Dunmore, and the sum he was paid, and the year he was paid it, and the exact wording of the contract, which I had memorized at fourteen while believing I was learning devotions.

My voice held. I want that recorded somewhere, since the tribunal apparently records everything now whether it consents or not. My voice held, and I am not a brave man, and my hands shook so badly I had to clasp them behind my back like a schoolboy, but the voice held, because it turned out that thirty years of recitation had done its work. The litany didn't need courage. It needed delivery.

Halfway through the fourth hour, the weeping started.

Not mine. I was past weeping; I was somewhere beyond it, in the clear cold country where the words come easy because they're the only true things left. It was the clerks. The round-faced young man first — he made a sound like a man remembering a dream, put both hands over his mouth, and sang. Four notes, over and over, a fragment of a lullaby I knew, *hush now, the river remembers your name*, except he couldn't have known it, no one alive outside the gate knew it, and yet there it was coming out of him in perfect Old Vessani while tears ran into his ink-stained fingers.

Then the woman beside him. Then a bailiff by the doors, a big grey-mustached man who stood at rigid attention and wept like a child and sang like one too, badly, joyfully, getting the words wrong and mending them as the language remembered itself through him.

It moved through the benches like fire through dry grass. By the time I reached the final section of the litany — the roll of the hidden, the seven hundred souls sealed behind the Hollow Gate, every name, every family — half the tribunal chamber was singing or sobbing or both, and the map on the dais had finished redrawing itself, and the great seal of the Chancery on the wall behind the Chancellors, the one with the motto *All Things Recorded, All Things Reconciled*, had begun, very softly, to hum.

The same note, I could not help noticing, as the Hollow Gate.

Vole had not moved.

That was the worst of it, or the best, depending on the hour and my mood. Through all of it she sat with her hands folded, watching me, and her face never changed, and I understood finally that I had misread her from the beginning. I had taken her stillness for certainty. It wasn't. It was the stillness of a woman standing in a burning house deciding whether to save the furniture or the people, and knowing she had built the house.

When I spoke the last line — *"and I, Orsen Dunmore, last warden of the Hollow Gate, do swear that all of this is true, and ask that it be unhidden"* — the binding closed over the words like water over a thrown stone, and the silence afterward was enormous.

Ossory, the ox-faced man, wiped his eyes with the heel of his hand and looked, for a long moment, less like a chancellor than like a farmer watching a flood take his fields and bring new soil.

"Well," he said at last, to no one in particular. "Well."

Chancellor Maren leaned forward. "Inquisitor Vole. Your office has maintained for four hundred years that no such kingdom existed. The Register now disagrees. The binding now disagrees." She gestured at the singing bailiff, gently. "Half our staff disagrees, in a language none of them were taught. What does the Office of Reconciliation wish to reconcile?"

Vole rose.

She straightened her grey coat. She looked at the map — at Vessany, whole and named and undeniable — and I saw her lips move once, silently, reading the name as if tasting it. Then she looked at me, and for the first time since the valley, since the cellar, since the burning of everything I owned, Serah Vole smiled. It was a tired smile. It was, I realized with a jolt, almost kind.

"What my office wishes," she said, "is irrelevant. What matters is that the girl is in my custody, and the gate stands open, and there are men under my command — men who did not sit in this room today and hear what we heard — who have orders regarding both." She turned to the Chancellors and inclined her head, precise as a blade being sheathed. "I request immediate authority to withdraw those orders. And I request a fast horse."

Ossory blinked. "You request—"

"She sang the key-song to open that door, Chancellor," Vole said, and her voice cracked, just slightly, just once, on the word *sang*. "And she lost her voice doing it. And my orders say the gate is to be destroyed with whoever stands in it." She was already moving toward the doors, already unbuckling the grey sash of her office as she walked, letting it fall behind her on the granary floor like a shed skin. "So unless any of you would like to explain to seven hundred people why their door burned while we sat here admiring the map — *someone lend me a horse*."

The whole chamber surged up at once, chairs toppling, clerks shouting, the bailiff by the door bawling directions to the stables in a language he'd learned an hour ago. And through the middle of it all I stood on the granary floor with the litany finished and the binding humming in my chest like a struck bell, thinking one thought, over and over, with a clarity I had not felt since I was a boy:

Ember was at Greywater. In a cell. Unable to speak. And the fastest road to her ran through the hills, past the Hollow Gate, where my father's bargain still held the door shut against everyone it had ever claimed to protect.

I grabbed the nearest clerk by the sleeve — the round-faced one, still crying, still singing — and said, "You heard the lady. Find me a horse too."

He looked at me with his streaming eyes and answered in flawless Old Vessani, "*Which way, warden?*" and I laughed, actually laughed, for the first time in a year.

"Toward the door," I said. "It's time somebody opened it properly."
