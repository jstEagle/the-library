## 20. The Door Out

The rope bit into Ember's wrists, but ropes were a Chancery habit, and habits could be broken.

She had learned that much in the ghost-city: everything the Chancery built was built to be forgotten by someone else. The wagon they'd loaded her into at dawn — when Corvane-in-exile dissolved like sugar in rain and Vole's soldiers had snatched her off the steps of the archive — was a tithe-wagon, its sides stamped with the grey seal of the Office of Records. It smelled of lamp oil and old paper and, faintly, of Serah Vole's ink.

Ember worked her wrists against the rope and thought about Orsen Dunmore walking away alone down the white road, carrying her song in his chest because she no longer had a voice to carry it herself.

*Sing it for me,* she had tried to tell him, and nothing came out, and his face had done something terrible and gentle all at once before the soldiers pulled them apart.

Well. She wasn't dead, and she wasn't quiet forever, and the country was remembering itself. She could feel it even here, even bound in a forgetting-wagon: a warmth under the world like bread rising. Every mile the horses walked, the land grew louder. Grass that had been only grass for thirty years remembered being *meadow of the ninth terrace*. A hill remembered it had a name that rhymed. Somewhere behind them, she was almost sure, a river was relearning how to run uphill out of spite.

The rope frayed. Ember smiled at it encouragingly.

"You're wasting your effort," said a voice from the front bench. One of Vole's clerks, a young man with spectacles and the apologetic posture of someone who had joined the Chancery for the pension. "Inquisitor Vole ordered double knots."

"Then she should have ordered triple," Ember rasped.

Her voice. It came out cracked and small as a sparrow's, but it *came out*. She sat very still, the way you do when a wild thing lands on you, afraid to breathe it away. The clerk twisted around to stare at her.

"You shouldn't talk," he said. "It'll hurt."

"It already hurts." She flexed her jaw. Words were coming back the way feeling comes back to a frozen hand — painful, prickling, miraculous. "Everything worth doing hurts a little. Didn't they teach you that at the Chancery?"

"They taught us filing."

Ember laughed, and the laugh scraped her throat raw, and she didn't care, because laughing meant singing was possible, and singing was the whole point of her. She had been sent through a sealed door at seven years old with a song stitched into her memory like a coin sewn into a coat lining, and every road since — the hills, the stones, the silent weeks when Orsen had spoken for both of them — had been leading back to this wagon, this rope, this road to the Hollow Gate.

Because that was where Vole was taking her. Of course it was. The gate was the last proof, and Vole did not leave proofs standing.

"The inquisitor isn't cruel," the clerk offered, apparently unable to bear the silence. "She thinks she's saving everyone trouble. She says unfinished kingdoms are like unhealed wounds."

"She's right," Ember said sweetly. "That's exactly why I'm going to finish mine."

The rope gave way. She kept her hands folded in her lap, demure as a chapel mouse, and watched the valley come.

* * *

She smelled the Hollow Gate before she saw it: woodsmoke where there should have been none, and under the smoke, the particular cold mineral breath of the gate itself, the smell of a door that has been shut so long it has forgotten weather. Then the wagon crested the ridge and there it all was below — the keeper's house a black skeleton, still smoking from the burning; the litany records scattered across the yard like a flock of slaughtered birds; and the Hollow Gate in the cliff face, grey and patient, with Serah Vole's soldiers swarming around it and sappers' carts drawn up and barrels of the Chancery's grey unmaking-paste stacked in neat, tidy rows, because the Chancery was tidy even at the end of the world.

And there — small, alone, standing between the gate and forty crossbows with nothing in his hands but a book — was Orsen.

Ember's heart did something complicated.

He looked terrible. His coat was scorched, his hair was full of ash, he was bleeding from a cut above his eyebrow, and he stood the way he had always stood at his inspections: spine straight, chin level, absolutely certain of his own routine even when the routine was *dying*. He was reading aloud from his father's prayer book. Even at this distance she knew the cadence. The litany. Names, dates, crimes. Each word fell on the air and the air *kept* it — she could see the soldiers nearest him wavering, lowering their bows a fraction, frowning as if trying to remember their mothers' faces.

"He can't hold them long," whispered the clerk, who was, Ember was beginning to understand, constitutionally incapable of not narrating. "The truth-reader says—"

"Where is the truth-reader?" Ember asked sharply.

"Gone strange. After Greywater. He read the tribunal records and then he sat down in the corridor and wept in Old Vessani, which he does not speak, and now he won't do readings anymore. He keeps saying the words taste like home."

Ember filed that away in the place where she kept miracles. The country was remembering *through people now*. Good. That would matter.

The wagon rolled down into the yard. Soldiers turned. Vole turned last of all, the way she always did, unhurried, her grey coat immaculate amid the ash, and when her eyes found Ember over the wagon rail there was no triumph in them. That was the worst thing about Serah Vole. There never was.

"You should have stayed hidden," Vole said. Not unkindly. That was the second worst thing.

"You should have stayed honest," Ember said, and climbed down without waiting to be helped, because her legs were hers and her voice was hers and today, of all days, she would not be carried anywhere like luggage.

Vole studied her. Behind them, Orsen's voice went on, steady as a millwheel: *"...and on the ninth of the Ash Month, the village of Tellerin, four hundred souls, removed by order of—"*

"Stop the reading," Vole said, without raising her voice.

Nobody moved. A soldier near the left flank had tears running down his face and an expression of profound personal embarrassment about it.

"Inquisitor," said one of the sappers carefully, "the paste won't take on the gate while he's — while the words are — the stone keeps *arguing back*, ma'am."

"Then arrest the reader."

They came for Orsen then, six of them, and Ember felt the whole day tilt toward disaster, and she did the only thing left. She filled her lungs with ash-smoke and cold morning air and the rising-bread warmth of a waking country, and she sang.

* * *

It was not a good voice. Not yet. It came out of her like water through a cracked jug, thin and leaking, and the first notes were barely more than breath. But the key-song had never needed beauty. Her grandmother had told her that, pressing the melody into her the way you press a seed into soil: *It doesn't want a singer, little ember. It wants a key. Keys just need to fit.*

The first true note left her mouth and the valley answered.

It started under her feet — a hum in the packed earth, the same hum she had felt the night she knocked on Orsen's door, grown now into something with bones and intention. The grass in the yard stood up straighter. The blackened beams of the keeper's house creaked, and for one impossible moment Ember saw them as they had been, whitewashed and sound, a ghost of a house laid over its own corpse like a hand laid over a wound. The scattered litany pages lifted, all together, and hung in the air turning slowly, hundreds of names catching the light.

The soldiers stopped moving toward Orsen. All of them. One by one they turned to look at the gate, and their faces were the faces of people hearing music from a house they thought empty.

Orsen looked at her across the yard. He was crying, which she had never seen him do, and he was smiling, which she had seen perhaps four times, and he did not stop reading. If anything the litany came stronger, and now she understood what he was doing, what he had understood somewhere on the silent road while she slept against his shoulder: the litany and the key-song were two halves of one door. Truth and melody. The names told the stone *what* it was; the song told it *to open*. He had spent thirty years memorizing the words without knowing why, and now he knew, and he was pouring thirty years of memorized devotion into the rock like a man pouring a lifetime of saved-up rain into a single furrow.

Ember sang the verse of hinges. The gate's seams began to glow, a thin gold light tracing the outline of a door within the door.

"Fire the ballistae!" Vole shouted, and for the first time her voice cracked. "Bring it DOWN—"

"No," said the soldier who had been crying.

Vole turned on him. "Soldier—"

"I know that song," he said helplessly. He touched his own throat as if checking whether the voice coming out of him belonged to him. "My gran used to — I don't *have* a gran, I don't remember a gran, but I know the harmony to that song, Inquisitor, I've known it my whole life and I never knew until right now—"

He began, badly, beautifully, to sing along.

Then another soldier. Then the clerk from the wagon, whose voice was surprisingly lovely. Then the sappers, dropping their tools, the unmaking-paste barrels standing abandoned and suddenly obscene. The truth-reader appeared from wherever he had been hiding and knelt in the ash and sang in a language he had never learned, fluently, sobbing.

Serah Vole stood alone in the middle of her mutiny, and Ember, singing, watched the exact moment the inquisitor understood that erasure had failed — not been defeated, *failed*, the way a dam fails, quietly and then all at once — because you cannot burn a song out of people who have just discovered they've been humming it all their lives.

Vole did not draw a weapon. She put her hand on the hilt of her sword and then took it off again, deliberately, the way you set down something heavy, and she stood very straight and listened to the key-song with an expression that Ember would spend years trying to describe and never quite manage: grief and relief fighting to a draw.

The gate blazed gold along every seam.

"Orsen!" Ember called, her voice stronger now, fed by the hundred-voice chorus rising around her. "Together!"

He closed the prayer book. He crossed the yard to her through the crowd of singing soldiers, and nobody stopped him, and he took her hand, and his grip was shaking.

"I don't know if I'm allowed," he said. "I spent my whole life keeping this shut. I don't know how to be the man who opens it."

"You're not," Ember said. "You're the man who *witnessed*. That's better. Open the door, witness."

He laughed — a wet, cracked, wonderful laugh — and together they pushed.

* * *

The Hollow Gate did not swing. It *remembered*.

The stone forgot thirty years of sealing the way a fist forgets clenching, and opened — wide, wide, wider than any escape hatch, wider than any crack or seam or smuggler's gap — until the whole cliff face stood aside like a curtain, and beyond it was not darkness.

Beyond it was lantern-light.

Terraces climbing the far valley in golden tiers, orchards heavy with fruit that had ripened unseen, smoke from a thousand chimneys rising straight into evening air, and people — hundreds, thousands, the hidden Vessani pouring out of their houses and down their terraces and across the fields because they had felt the gate open and the country wake, and they were running toward the light with their arms out, and the oldest among them were falling to their knees in the wheat, and the youngest were simply *running*, the way children run when there is finally somewhere to run *to*.

The sound that came out of the gate was the sound of a country exhaling after holding its breath for thirty years.

Ember stood in the doorway with Orsen's hand around hers and the key-song still ringing in her healed throat, and she sang the last verse — the verse of welcome, the verse her grandmother had saved for last, the one that named every family waiting on the other side — and the Vessani sang it back, and the valley sang it back, and the soldiers in the yard sang it back in harmonies they hadn't known they carried, and somewhere in the middle of it all Serah Vole closed her eyes and let one tear fall, precisely, economically, like everything else she did, and did not wipe it away.

The gate stood open. Not a crack. Not a secret. A *doorway* — tall as a cathedral arch, gold-edged, framed in stone that now bore carved words in Old Vessani that everyone in the yard could suddenly read:

**WE WERE NEVER GONE. WE WERE WAITING TO BE ASKED.**

"Oh," said Orsen Dunmore, warden of thirty years, keeper of nothing now, staring at the carved words with the prayer book hanging forgotten from his fingers. "Oh, that's going to require some explaining."

"You testified," Ember said, elbowing him gently. "Explaining is sort of your whole job now."

From the far side of the gate, an old woman in a wheat-dusted shawl was pushing through the crowd, moving with terrible purpose, and Ember recognized the set of her shoulders because Ember had inherited those shoulders. And behind the old woman came the council elders, and behind them what looked suspiciously like several hundred people who had opinions about the man who had kept their door shut for three decades, and not all of those opinions appeared to be grateful ones.

Orsen followed her gaze and went pale. "Ember," he said carefully, "how many of them know it was my father's signature on the bargain?"

"All of them," said Ember. "We had thirty years and very good record-keeping. Also, the tall one with the staff? That's Maren. You arrested her grandson once. By accident. Through the gate. He's been telling everyone."

"Ah," said Orsen.

The old woman reached the threshold, stopped, and looked up at him — at the last Dunmore, ash-covered, tear-streaked, standing in the door his family had locked. The whole valley held its breath.

"So," she said, in the old tongue, loud enough for every soldier and every elder and every ghost of the purge to hear. "You're the one who finally opened it."

"Yes," said Orsen.

"Well," she said, and her voice broke, and she seized him by the collar with both hands. "You're *late*. Supper's cold. Get in here."

And she pulled the last Dunmore through the door of Vessany, into the light, home.
