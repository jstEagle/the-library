## 21. Kingdoms Remembered

The doors of the tribunal hall had never been meant to open all the way.

Orsen had noticed that in the days before his testimony — the great bronze leaves of them, tall as three men, standing ajar by only a narrow gap, as if even architecture at Greywater had learned to be careful. Chancery halls were built to admit one petitioner at a time, one truth at a time, weighed and portioned. Now the doors stood flung wide, and through them came a country.

They came in the grey of morning, blinking, holding each other's sleeves. Old men who had spent thirty years tending lantern-lit terraces behind a sealed door walked out into a world with weather in it — real weather, unrationed, falling from an open sky. Children who had been born inside Vessany and had never once seen a horizon stopped at the threshold and refused at first to cross it, as though the outside might be another wall painted very well.

Orsen stood on the steps and watched them come, and did not know what to do with his hands.

He had spent a lifetime knowing exactly what to do with his hands. Polish the seals. Turn the key. Fold the litany pages along their worn creases. For thirty years his body had been an instrument of keeping things shut, and now there was nothing left to keep, and his hands hung at his sides like tools whose trade had ended.

A woman came down the steps toward him. She was old in the way of people who had worked hard indoors for decades, her back curved like a drawn bow, and she wore the layered shawls of the high terraces. She stopped in front of him and looked him up and down with frank, unhurried appraisal.

"You're the Dunmore boy," she said. Her accent was thick as honey; he understood her because Ember had spent weeks hammering the old tongue into him, but hearing it spoken here, in the open, under the Chancery's own eaves, made his chest ache. "The warden's son."

"The last Dunmore," Orsen said. It still felt strange in his mouth. True, but strange — like a coin from a country that no longer existed.

She nodded slowly. Then she reached up — she was small enough that she had to reach — and took his hand, and pressed something into it: a twist of dried fruit wrapped in waxed cloth. Terrace plums. He knew them now. He'd eaten them on the far side of the gate, once, in the brief astonished hour when it stood open before the council demanded it be closed again.

"For your father," she said, "I have nothing. For you — you look like a man who has forgotten to eat."

"He wasn't—" Orsen began, and stopped. The speech he had prepared, the defense of his father's name, the careful distinctions between the man and the bargain — he had delivered all of it already, days ago, before the tribunal, and the words had come out of him like splinters, one by one, until the room remembered. There was no use re-arming himself now. "Thank you," he said instead. "It was good fruit."

"It was," she agreed, "the best in Vessany, and I grew it, so I would know." And she patted his arm twice, briskly, the way one pats a horse that has done its work, and went on down the steps into the world.

* * *

By midday the plaza below the tribunal steps had become something Greywater had no word for.

The clerks came out first, drawn by the noise — a noise like a market, like a festival, like a language being spoken freely in quantities. They stood in their grey robes among the Vessani and listened to conversations they could not follow, and then, one by one, they began to follow them. That was the thing about the restored memory, Orsen had learned. It didn't arrive all at once. It arrived the way dawn arrives, seeping in at the edges, and every clerk who had wept during his testimony now found old words surfacing in them like fish rising to feed — words their grandmothers had hummed over cradles, words the Chancery had scrubbed from ledgers but never quite from lullabies.

A young clerk sat on the fountain rim teaching a Vessani child to fold a document bird, and the child was teaching him, in exchange, the names of the seven winds off the eastern terraces. An old stonemason of the hidden valley stood before the tribunal's facade with his head tipped back, reading the architecture aloud — "Chancery work, second period, competent but timid" — and two junior clerks took notes, delighted.

And everywhere, everywhere, maps.

That was the strangest part. The cartographers' shops had thrown their doors open, and inside them the great printed sheets of the realm were changing. Not burning, not rewriting themselves in fire — simply remembering. Rivers that had been drawn as guesswork straightened into their true courses. A mountain range that official geography insisted was impassable wilderness acquired roads, bridges, terraces, a capital. CORVANE, said the ink now, in letters that deepened as you watched, as though the word were drinking. And beneath it, smaller, in a script the printers swore they had never set: *and her daughters, Merrowfen and the Singing Stones and the Hollow Gate.*

Ember stood in the middle of the map shop with her hands on her hips, surveying a wall-sized rendering of her country like a general reviewing troops.

"They got the river wrong," she announced to the trembling shopkeeper. "It bends east at the willows. Here, give me the pen."

"Child," the shopkeeper began weakly, "that is a state document—"

"It's *my* state," said Ember, and corrected the river.

Orsen watched from the doorway and felt the thing in his chest that had been grief for thirty years turn over and become, cautiously, something else. Not joy exactly. Joy was too simple a shape for it. It was more like the feeling of setting down a load so heavy you had forgotten you were carrying it, and discovering that your shoulders still held its outline, and would for a long time, and that this was all right.

* * *

Serah Vole found him at dusk on the tribunal steps.

He heard her boots before he saw her — she had soldier's boots, practical ones, and she had never once tried to disguise what she was. He turned and found her standing a few steps below him, and was surprised, though perhaps he shouldn't have been, at how tired she looked. Not defeated. Tired, the way a person is tired after carrying a conviction up a very long hill and arriving to find the view is not what they were promised.

"Warden Dunmore," she said.

"Not anymore," Orsen said. "There's nothing left to ward. The door's a door now."

"So I've seen." She came up two more steps, unhurried, and stood beside him rather than facing him, which he recognized as a courtesy, or possibly a tactic; with Vole it had always been difficult to tell the difference, and he suspected she cultivated that deliberately. Below them the plaza glowed with lanterns the Vessani had strung without asking anyone's permission. Someone was singing. Several someones, badly, in harmony.

"I want you to understand something," Vole said. "I am not resigning."

"No?"

"I considered it. Briefly." Her mouth did something that was almost a smile. "Then I decided that would be cowardice wearing a nicer coat. I gave thirty years to the doctrine of erasure. I told myself it was mercy — that unfinished kingdoms breed wars, that a wound heals cleaner when no one picks at it. I believed a clean map was a kind map." She paused. "I interrogated a toll-keeper in Merrowfen. An old man. He confessed his whole life to me in exchange for passage, the way your law requires, and I wrote it all down as evidence against him, and somewhere in the writing my hand began to shake, because everything he said was true, and I could feel it being true, and I had built a career on the premise that truth of that kind was a disease."

"What happens to you now?" Orsen asked.

"That depends on the tribunal." She said it flatly, without drama. "They have their memory back. All of it. Including the parts I carried out for them. I expect a hearing. I expect worse than a hearing." She turned to look at him then, and her eyes were the same pale, patient grey they had been in his kitchen, the day she asked polite questions about anomalous children while Ember hid below the floorboards. "I could contest it. I know the procedures better than anyone alive. I could bury the hearings in procedure for a decade. I've decided not to."

"Why?"

"Because I was there," Vole said simply. "When your testimony finished, and the maps changed, and the clerks wept in a language they'd been forbidden — I stood in that room and I witnessed it. And I find that I would rather stand down than stand up tomorrow and deny the one thing in thirty years I am certain was real." She looked out at the lantern-light. "Erasure is a terrible amount of work, Warden. You spend your whole strength making the world forget. And then someone speaks one true sentence, and it all comes back, and you realize the forgetting was never the world's condition. It was only yours."

Orsen thought about his father. Thirty years of silence bought and paid for, a muzzle dressed up as an honor. He thought about how the elder Dunmore must have died still calling it duty.

"She forgave me, you know," Orsen said. "The plum woman. This morning. My father helped build her cage and she gave me fruit."

Vole was quiet a moment. "Vessani are like that," she said. "It's the most dangerous thing about them. It's why we were afraid." She straightened her coat — an old habit, armor of a kind — and descended two steps, then paused. "The girl. Ember. She sang the gate open with a burned voice and a broken throat, according to the report I will be signing. The healers say the voice returns slowly. She doesn't care. She's been at the gate every day, singing anyway, croaking like a frog, and the door opens a little wider each time regardless." Something moved across her face that in a softer woman might have been called tenderness. "Determination like that doesn't need a voice. It just needs somewhere to go."

She walked away across the plaza, past the lanterns, past the singers, and no one stopped her, and several people nodded to her, and she nodded back, stiffly, like a woman learning a foreign gesture.

* * *

The feast, if it could be called that, happened on the tribunal steps themselves, because the Vessani had never developed much reverence for buildings that weren't theirs.

Tables appeared from somewhere — Orsen never learned where; hidden kingdoms apparently traveled with furniture the way snails travel with shells. Bread, terrace plums, a fish stew that a clerk's grandmother had apparently known how to make all along and had been making secretly for forty years, thinking it was merely a family recipe. Lanterns everywhere. The seven winds off the eastern terraces, all in attendance.

Ember found him sitting slightly apart, as was his habit, on the lowest step with a bowl in his hands.

"You're doing it again," she said.

"Doing what?"

"Sitting on the edge like a cat that isn't sure it lives here." She dropped down beside him, took his bowl, ate a strategic handful of stew, and returned it. Her voice was still rough — a sandpaper voice, a voice like gravel poured slowly — but it was hers, and every day a little more of it came home. "You testified against your own blood. You gave up the valley. You think nobody wants you."

"Nobody's said otherwise," Orsen said.

"Everybody's said otherwise. You just haven't been listening, because listening would mean admitting you were wrong, and you're very stubborn. It's your worst quality. Well. Second worst. The worst is the way you fold papers."

"The council wanted me gone," Orsen said quietly. "Three weeks ago. *Reseal the gate forever.*"

"The council," said Ember, with the magnificent scorn available only to children and prophets, "was scared. Scared people want small doors. But the council's down there right now—" she pointed with her chin at the plaza, where the eldest of the elders was attempting to dance with a Chancery clerk half his age and losing gracefully— "eating fish stew with the people they locked the door against. People change when the door opens. That's sort of the entire point of doors."

An old man approached them then — the mason who had critiqued the tribunal's architecture, stone dust still in the creases of his knuckles. He crouched in front of Orsen with the unhurried gravity of his trade.

"We've been talking," he said. "The elders and I. About the Hollow Gate."

Orsen's stomach tightened. Some habits of dread died hard. "What about it?"

"It should come down," the mason said. "The seal-work, the locks, the prison-machinery your family built into it. Strip it all out. Leave the arch." He smiled, and it transformed his face into something young. "An arch is a promise. A lock is a fear. We'd rather have the promise."

"And the keeper's house?" Orsen asked. "What's left of it?"

"Rebuild it," said the mason. "Bigger windows. And we'll need someone to live nearby who knows the valley, knows the road, knows which of the seven winds knocks tiles loose in autumn." He shrugged elaborately. "We don't know anyone like that. Unless you do."

Orsen opened his mouth. Closed it. Opened it again.

"He does," said Ember, answering for him, "but he's going to be insufferable about it for at least a week, so act like you haven't noticed."

* * *

Late — very late, when the lanterns had burned to embers and the plaza slept in drifts of contentment — Orsen climbed alone to the top of the tribunal steps and looked north, toward the hills where the Hollow Gate waited with its arch full of morning.

He thought about belonging. He had spent his whole life believing it was a place — the valley, the house, the routine, the litany recited at the same hour in the same order until the hours became walls. He had lost all of it, and grieved it, and discovered the grief was strangely light, and now he understood why. Belonging had never been the valley. Belonging was the plum woman pressing fruit into his hand. It was a clerk folding paper birds. It was a mason saying *we'll need someone*, and meaning *we'll need you*. It was a child stealing his stew and correcting rivers.

It couldn't be granted. That was what his father had never understood — you couldn't be paid in belonging, couldn't hold it behind a lock. You earned it the slow way, door by open door, and it was waiting for you on both sides.

Behind him, on the dark steps, small feet came padding up.

"You're not sleeping," Ember said, settling beside him and pulling his cloak over her shoulders without negotiation.

"Thinking."

"Don't hurt yourself." She leaned against his arm. For a while neither spoke. Then, in her ruined, mending, stubborn voice, barely above a whisper, she began to sing — the key-song, or the beginning of it, the first three notes that had opened a kingdom. And far to the north, Orsen would swear all his life afterward, the wind shifted, and carried back to them faintly, impossibly, the sound of a great door swinging wide, and voices on the far side calling the old tongue toward the morning:

*Come home. Come home. The door stays open.*

"Tomorrow we start for the valley," Ember murmured, drowsy against his shoulder. "There's a house to rebuild. Bigger windows. And I'm picking mine."

"Of course you are."

"The one facing east." Her eyes were closing. "For the sunrise. Somebody in this family ought to watch doors *open* for a change."

Orsen sat with her under the stars, on the steps of the empire that had forgotten and now remembered, and for the first time in thirty years, the last Dunmore had nowhere he needed to guard, nothing he needed to keep shut, and nowhere at all that he was unwelcome —

and the road home, when morning came, would be the easiest road he had ever walked.
