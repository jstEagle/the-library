## 22. The New Keeper

The gate had a bell now.

Ember had argued for it for two full years before the council relented — a small bronze thing, cast in Merrowfen by a toll-keeper who charged her one truth as payment and then refused to take it, saying she'd already paid enough truths for three lifetimes. She'd hung it from a post beside the Hollow Gate herself, on a morning in early spring when the valley still smelled of thawed earth and woodsmoke, and she had rung it exactly once to see what it would do.

What it did was bring Orsen Dunmore out of his house at a dead run, half-dressed, holding a bread knife like a sword, convinced the Chancery had come at last.

She laughed so hard she had to sit down on the gate's threshold stone. Orsen stood there in the road with his hair sticking up and his dignity in ruins, and after a moment he started laughing too — that rusty, surprised sound he made, like a door that hadn't been opened in thirty years and needed oiling.

"That," he said, "is the last time I trust you with metal."

"You trusted me with a kingdom."

"The kingdom doesn't ring bells at dawn."

* * *

That had been six years ago. The bell post was taller now, because travelers kept nailing things to it — ribbons, prayer-knots, a horseshoe someone swore had come off a Chancery courier's horse at the exact moment the man decided to turn around and go home. The gate itself had changed too. It no longer looked like a door in a wall that led nowhere. It looked like what it was: an archway of grey stone standing free in a green valley, its far side opening onto terraced hillsides where lanterns came on every dusk like a slow constellation being lit.

People walked through it both ways now. Vessani came out to trade salt-fish and indigo dye and songs nobody else knew. Lowlanders came in to buy wool and learn words, if they were respectful about it, and to be gently but firmly turned around if they weren't. The council had rules. Ember had helped write them, which she privately considered the funniest thing that had ever happened to anyone, given that she had once been the reason the rules existed at all.

Because Ember was the keeper now.

Not warden. She was very clear about the difference, and so was everyone else, mostly because she corrected them loudly whenever they got it wrong. A warden kept people *in*. A keeper kept a door *well* — oiled hinges, swept threshold, a lamp burning on either side so no one ever came through into darkness. Her mother had been a keeper, back before the hiding, back when Vessany had doors instead of walls. Ember had learned this only afterward, in the years of letters and reunions, and when she'd learned it she had cried for an hour and then informed the council that the position was hers and they could fight her for it if they liked.

Nobody fought her for it. She was fourteen, then fifteen, then seventeen, and somewhere in there she stopped being the child who came through the gate and became the woman who stood beside it.

* * *

On the morning this story properly begins — though it isn't much of a story, being mostly about bread — Ember was teaching Old Vessani to a merchant's daughter named Tilly, who had crossed the gate eleven times and could count to twenty, name nine kinds of weather, and curse with impressive fluency.

"Again," Ember said. "*Shen varrath.*"

"*Shen varrath.*" Tilly frowned. "Which one is the bad word again?"

"All of them are good words. That's the whole point of a language." Ember tapped the slate where she'd written the phrase in careful old script. "Say it means *the rain remembers us*. Now say it like you believe it."

Tilly squared her little shoulders and said it like she believed it, and the words did what they always did now, what they had done more easily every year since the tribunal — they made the air lean in. Not magic, exactly. Not anymore, not since the country had come back to itself and didn't need to shout to be heard. But a listening. The grass by the gate grew a shade greener. Somewhere up the hillside, a bird sang a phrase that sounded suspiciously like a line from the Lament of Corvane, which birds had absolutely no business knowing.

"There," Ember said. "You've made the valley pay attention. Don't waste it."

"What does it cost?" Tilly asked. She was a serious child under the freckles. "The toll-keeper said words used to cost something here. Truths. Voices."

Ember looked at her own hands. They were a keeper's hands now, ink-stained and rope-callused, and they worked perfectly, and her voice sat behind them warm and whole, and some mornings she still woke up and said one word aloud just to feel it land — usually *home*, sometimes something less polite.

"It cost me once," she said. "It doesn't cost you. That's the point of what we did. You get to say it cheap." She grinned. "So say it a lot."

* * *

Orsen came down the path from his cottage at midmorning, carrying a basket, wearing the expression of a man who had lost an argument with his own garden. He was fifty-eight now and had gone grey at the temples in a way that Ember maintained was entirely performative, since he'd been grey at the temples at forty-five too and simply hadn't mentioned it.

"The slugs," he announced, "have taken the lettuce."

"Have you tried singing to it?"

"I have tried *everything*. I have tried ash and beer and copper tape and asking nicely. The slugs are unmoved by the restoration of a nation." He set the basket down on the keeper's table outside the gatehouse, which was really just a wide porch Ember had built, and peered at Tilly's slate. "*Shen varrath.* Good accent. Better than mine was, my first year."

"Your first year you called it 'the humming language,'" Tilly said, delighted. She'd heard the story eleven times and demanded it weekly.

"I called it that respectfully."

"He called it that while hiding under a table," Ember told her.

"I was *taking cover*. There's a difference." But he was smiling, settling onto the bench with the ease of a man who belonged to a bench, to a porch, to a valley. Six years ago he had testified for four days before the Chancery tribunal at Greywater, naming names and dates and crimes until the maps on the walls redrew themselves and grown clerks wept in a tongue they'd never learned. He had traded everything for it — his office, his silence, his father's name, any hope of a quiet life. And then, having nothing left to lose, he had walked back into the valley that had feared him for thirty years and knocked on the first door and asked, in terrible Old Vessani, whether anyone needed help bringing in the harvest.

They had. They always did. That was the secret of belonging, Ember had decided: it wasn't granted, it was *carried*, season after season, sack after sack, until your shoulders got used to the weight and then forgot it was there.

"Tilly," Orsen said, "your mother will want you home by noon."

"She'll want me home when the shop bell rings."

"Then let's not keep a shop waiting." He dug in the basket and produced a loaf, still warm, wrapped in cloth. "Bread tax. All students pay it."

"That's not a real tax."

"It's extremely real. I invented it. Ask the council."

"The council says he invented it," Ember confirmed, accepting her share. "He also invented the rule that the keeper gets the end piece, which is why I allow it."

* * *

Afternoon brought travelers, as it usually did. A pair of dyers from the lowland towns, come for the indigo. An old Vessani woman going home the other way, who stopped at the threshold to touch the stone the way people touched the shoulders of friends. And, near dusk, a young man in travel-worn grey with a Chancery satchel, who stopped ten paces from the gate and stood there looking uncertain in a way that made Ember's hand drift toward the bell rope out of pure old habit.

But he wasn't wearing the grey of the inquisitors. Just grey cloth. And he was young — younger than the purge, younger than the hiding, younger than all of it.

"Is it true," he asked, "that you teach?"

"Depends who's asking."

"My name is Aldous Fen. I'm — I was — a junior archivist at Greywater. Third drawer down, records remediation." He said it the way people said *I was a soldier*, which is to say carefully, watching for the flinch. "When the tribunal restored the files — when the truth went through the building like weather — I read everything. Everything that was in my keeping. Names I'd catalogued as *anomalous persons*. I thought—" He stopped. Started again. "I thought I should learn the names properly. In their own language. If you'd have me."

Ember studied him. Behind her, faintly, the far side of the gate glowed with evening lantern-light, and the smell of someone's supper drifted through — fish and onions and woodsmoke, the smell of a country that had spent thirty years being a rumor and was now, stubbornly, dinner.

Somewhere in Greywater there was a file with her name in it. *Anomalous child, recovered subject, status unknown.* Some clerk had written those words about her, in a neat hand, meaning nothing by it, meaning everything by it. Maybe this young man's hand. Maybe a drawer he'd tended.

"We start at dawn," she said. "And you'll sweep the threshold first. Keepers earn the language; we don't hand it over."

Aldous Fen bowed — a real bow, low, the kind you gave a person and not an office — and went to find lodging in the village, where Mrs. Harrow would feed him too much and interrogate him thoroughly and decide, by morning, that he was acceptable.

Ember watched him go, then rang the bell twice — the dusk signal, *the door stays open, come home if you're coming* — and watched the lanterns answer across the terraces on the far side, one by one, like the country blinking awake.

* * *

Orsen found her there after full dark, the way he often did, bringing two cups of cider and no conversation, which was their oldest ritual and still the best one. They sat on the threshold stone with their backs against the gatepost, the archway cool at their shoulders, the whole valley quiet except for the creek and the far-off sound of the Vessani side singing something with a lot of clapping in it.

"Do you remember," Orsen said eventually, "when I thought this gate was a prison?"

"You thought *you* were the prison."

"I thought I was the lock, anyway." He turned his cup slowly. "Thirty years of polishing a cage and calling it duty. Sometimes I still wake up reaching for the litany. Reciting it before I'm even awake enough to know it's over. All those names." He shook his head. "They don't need reciting anymore. They're on the maps. Children learn them in school and complain about the spelling."

"They're not over," Ember said. "They're just finished being secrets. There's a difference." She bumped her shoulder against his arm. "Besides. Somebody has to keep the door. And I learned from the best warden in the history of bad jobs."

"Warden," he said, with a shudder. "Don't use that word on the porch."

"Keeper," she amended, grinning. "Better?"

"Infinitely."

They sat a while longer. Down the road, the village lamps were going out one by one, and across the gate the lantern-constellation burned steady, and between the two lights the archway stood open with nothing to fear on either side of it. Ember thought about the girl she had been, walking out of a hidden country with a song in her chest and anger in her mouth, calling a stranger *the last Dunmore* like an accusation. She thought about how he had flinched, and fed her soup anyway, and lied badly to an inquisitor to protect her, and burned his whole life down rather than reseal a child behind a wall.

Belonging earned, not granted. That was the whole of it. That was the lesson under every lesson she taught at dawn.

"One more thing before bed," Orsen said, standing, brushing crumbs from his coat. "The slug situation has escalated. If you hear screaming in the night, it's me, losing honorably."

"Sing to them."

"I *am* going to sing to them. *Shen varrath,* you horrible little creatures. Let's see if the rain remembers *them*."

He shuffled off up the path, lantern bobbing, cursing mollusks in two languages, and Ember laughed and banked the threshold lamp and left the bell within reach of her hand, the way she always did.

The door stayed open. That was the rule above all rules, the one she'd carved into the gatepost herself in old script where every traveler could read it:

*Shen varrath. The rain remembers us. So does the door.*

And far up the dark hillside, faint but unmistakable, a bird began to sing the Lament of Corvane — and got the spelling wrong, and kept going anyway.
