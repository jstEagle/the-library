## 1. The Charm-Seller's Winter

There were three rules Casper Dunmore had lived by for ten years, and he recited them every morning like a litany, because a man who forgot his own commandments was a man who ended up on a Warden's pyre.

One: never touch the weather.

Two: never let your hands be warm.

Three: never, under any circumstances, sell anyone a charm that actually worked.

He repeated them while he dressed, in the dark, in his narrow room above the shop, and then he went downstairs and broke rule three before breakfast, because a man had to eat.

The luck-knots were harmless. That was the whole genius of them. A luck-knot was a loop of braided cord — red thread for love, green for harvest, grey for safe roads — tied in a pattern that looked ancient and meant nothing at all. He sold them for two coppers apiece, four if the customer looked prosperous, and he always said the same words as he handed one over: *Tie it where you'll see it. Luck is mostly remembering to look.*

It was even true, which made it better than most charms ever sold in Vell. Half of fortune was attention. The other half was nerve. Casper had plenty of the second and had spent ten years teaching himself the first.

His shop sat on Tallow Lane, wedged between a chandler who smelled of tallow (naturally) and a widow named Mrs. Hobb who sold pickled everything and smelled of vinegar and gossip in equal measure. The sign above his door read **DUNMORE'S KNOTS & SMALL GOODS**, painted by a sign-writer who had charged him extra for the ampersand and done it crooked anyway. Casper had decided the crookedness was good for business. It suggested humility. Nobody trusted a tidy magician.

Not that anyone in Vell believed in magicians. That was rather the point of Vell.

Vell was the largest city in the lowlands that had never signed the old covenants, never kept hedge-witches, never burned anyone either, because you cannot burn what you insist does not exist. The city's founding charter — read aloud every Founding Day by a mayor with a voice like a wet boot — declared magic *a superstition of lesser peoples*, and its citizens took a fierce civic pride in not believing in anything they couldn't tax. There were no temples worth the name. No wise women on the hills. When someone fell ill, they sent for a physician who bled them and charged double; when someone fell in love, they wrote poetry, which was worse.

And so a charm-seller in Vell was not a sorcerer. He was a curiosity. A knickknack-man. A fellow you bought a red string from for your sweetheart and laughed about afterward over ale. Casper had spent a decade cultivating exactly that reputation, watering it daily like a stubborn houseplant, and it had grown so thick around him that even he sometimes couldn't see through it.

Which was, he told himself each morning, the safest place a storm-caller could live: inside a joke.

* * *

The morning the story properly begins — though mornings rarely know when they're beginning things — Casper woke to the year's first cold that meant business. Not the polite chill of autumn, but a thin, whetted cold that came down the mountains and stood in the streets like a creditor.

He noticed it first in his hands.

They ached. They always ached now, in deep cold, in a way he had never once mentioned to a physician or a friend, because aching hands were nothing, everyone's hands ached, but *his* hands ached in a particular way — a pressure behind the knuckles, like something coiled there turning over in its sleep. Ten years ago those hands had called lightning out of a clear sky. Now they complained about frost and he complained about them, and between the two of them they managed.

"Behave," he told them, flexing his fingers over the basin of water he'd broken the ice skin off. "We're a shopkeeper today."

His breath fogged. Outside, Tallow Lane was doing its early-morning arithmetic: carts and coughs, the chandler's boy hauling tallow blocks and dropping exactly one, Mrs. Hobb's shutters banging open with the punctuality of a siege engine. Somewhere a bell counted seven.

Casper opened his shutters, checked the sky — pale, hard, no snow yet, though it wanted to — and did what he did every morning of the last ten years.

He looked at the clouds and told them, silently, firmly, *nothing from me*.

It was a strange prayer to un-pray. Once, weather had come to him the way music comes to a struck bell; he had only to feel a thing and the sky felt it too. Grief had been rainstorms. Rage had been hail. He remembered — distantly, guiltily, the way you remember a debt — standing on a ridge with his arms open and the whole horizon bending toward him like iron to a lodestone.

Now he kept his feelings small on principle. He was a man of modest joys and carefully rationed annoyances, and if a cloud so much as darkened when he stubbed his toe, he apologized to it.

So far, the arrangement held. So far.

* * *

By midmorning the shop had its usual traffic, which was to say: Mrs. Hobb, twice, ostensibly about a disputed fence post and actually about everything else in the world.

"Grey knot," she announced, planting herself at his counter. "For my nephew. He's traveling."

"Where to?"

"North." She said it the way people said *plague pit*. "Fool boy. Nothing up north now but winter and ruins."

"Then he wants a grey knot and probably a scarf," Casper said, measuring out cord. "I can help with one of those."

"You could help with both, if you stocked wool like a sensible man instead of—" she waved at his shelves, which held knots, candles of dubious scent, dried lavender, pewter buttons, and a jar of river stones he labeled LUCKY, ASSORTED, "—this."

"The stones are very lucky."

"They're rocks, Dunmore."

"Rocks that have been *selected*," Casper said gravely, and Mrs. Hobb snorted, paid her coppers, and stayed another quarter hour to inform him that the baker's wife was leaving the baker, that the salt-tax was rising again, and that Wardens had been seen at the East Gate.

That last one landed differently than the others.

"Wardens," Casper said, keeping his voice at the exact pitch of mild civic interest. "In Vell? I thought they didn't bother with us. We don't believe in anything."

"They don't believe in anything *here*," Mrs. Hobb corrected. "But things walk through, don't they. Things going north." She leaned in, vinegar-scented, delighted. "My cousin's wife's brother is a gate-clerk. Says they've turned back three wagons this week. Checking papers. Checking *hands*."

"Hands," Casper said.

"For marks. You know. Old covenant folk mark their palms, don't they, my cousin's wife's brother says. Orders are orders." She collected her parcel, wished her nephew luck via proxy, and left Casper alone with a counter full of grey cord and a heartbeat that had picked up speed without his permission.

He looked at his right palm.

Nothing there. Of course nothing there. He'd burned the sigil out of his own hand with a candle flame and sheer hatred the night he crossed into Vell, and the scar had faded to a pale ghost no clerk would notice. But his hands were the problem in another way entirely, weren't they — the way they were never quite warm, never quite cold, always holding that faint coiled pressure like a fist deciding whether to open.

*Never let your hands be warm.* Rule two existed because warmth drew attention, and attention drew questions, and questions, in Casper's experience, ended with men in pale grey coats writing things down.

He finished braiding the nephew's knot. His fingers did the work without him, quick and sure, and he caught himself tying the pattern with one loop too many — an old working, a road-blessing, the kind of thing that would keep sleet off a traveler's shoulders for a hundred miles — and stopped, and undid it, and retied it plain.

"Luck is mostly remembering to look," he told the knot quietly. "That's all you get. I'm sorry."

The knot, being string, said nothing. But Casper had spent enough years talking to weather to know what silence sounded like when it was disappointed.

* * *

They came at noon, which was insulting. Everyone knew inspections happened at dawn or dusk, when shops were caught half-ready; coming at noon meant confidence, and confidence meant quotas. Somebody in the Warden hierarchy needed numbers this month.

There were three of them. Two soldiers in the pale grey coats and blank round masks that made every face identical and every voice strangely loud, and between them a clerk — actual flesh-faced, ink-stained, tired-eyed clerk — carrying a ledger under his arm like a man carrying a cat he didn't trust.

"Caspar Dunmore?" the clerk read off a list. "Proprietor?"

"Just Dunmore. One S. My mother was optimistic."

The clerk didn't smile. The masks didn't do anything, being masks. One of them drifted to the doorway and stood in it, filling it, which was the entire art of Warden soldiering.

"Routine survey," the clerk said. "Trade goods, provenance, materials. Open your stockroom, please."

"Happy to." Casper's heart was performing some complicated gymnastics, but he'd gotten very good at making his body lie — it was, after all, the one skill he'd practiced more than any other. He led the clerk past the shelf of assorted lucky rocks, past the lavender, into the back room where the cord hung in ranks and the workbench sat under its dust sheet.

The clerk consulted his ledger. "You sell... charms."

"I sell *knots*," Casper said. "Charms imply they work. Knots are honest. It's basically jewelry with better marketing."

"And these are—" the clerk touched a hanging bundle of red cords, "—braided how?"

"With my hands."

"Dyed how?"

"With madder root. Bought from Ferro the dyer, down on Cordwainer Street, who will tell you I'm his best customer and also that I haggle disgracefully."

The clerk wrote something. The mask by the door shifted its weight, floorboards complaining beneath it. And then the second mask — the one Casper had privately nicknamed Quiet, because it hadn't spoken and somehow that made it worse — stepped forward and extended one gauntleted hand.

"Palm," it said.

The word dropped into the room like a stone into a well. Casper heard it fall all the way down.

"There's a charming local custom," the clerk said, with the weariness of a man reciting something he'd said forty times since the East Gate, "of checking tradesmen against the registry. Marks of the old orders. Covenant sigils. You understand. Purely administrative."

"Of course," Casper said.

Here is what Casper Dunmore thought in the three seconds that followed, laid out honestly because he would want it recorded somewhere, even if only in his own head:

He thought about the candle flame, ten years ago, and the smell of his own burning skin. He thought about the fact that his palm bore only a white ghost of a scar, the kind any man might have from rope or tools or an accident with a kettle. He thought about running — there was a back window, a drainpipe, a route he had mapped and remapped until he could walk it blindfolded — and he thought about how running confirmed everything, and how the masks moved faster than men should move, and how there was a boy asleep above a chandler's shop two doors down who owed him for candles and didn't deserve to watch a shopkeeper die in the street.

Mostly, though, he thought about how tired he was. That was the strange part. Standing there with his heart slamming and a masked Warden waiting for his hand, what rose up in him wasn't fear, or not only fear. It was the exhaustion underneath the fear, ten years deep — the daily accounting of it, the temperature of his own hands monitored like a fever, the jokes rehearsed, the knots deliberately ruined, the weather apologized to, the self folded smaller and smaller each year to fit inside a life built entirely of concealments, until some days he wasn't sure there was a Casper left under all the careful lies, or just a shape where a man used to be, wearing an apron.

He put out his hand.

The gauntlet closed around his wrist — cold, impersonal, strong as a door hinge — and turned his palm up to the light from the back window.

The clerk leaned in. The scar sat there, pale and ordinary, a ghost among ghosts. Casper watched the clerk's eyes move across it and thought, with a detachment that surprised him: *if he asks where I got it, I'll say a plow. I've never held a plow. I'll say a plow.*

"Hm," said the clerk.

And then the bell above the shop door rang, and Mrs. Hobb's voice sailed through the shop like a ship coming into harbor — "*Dunmore! Are you back there? There's a child at your door, the strangest little thing, barefoot in this cold, and it won't speak a word to me—*"

The gauntlet loosened. Just slightly. Just enough.

"Barefoot," Casper repeated, already moving, already reaching for his coat, gratitude and confusion arriving together in equal measure. "Excuse me — if there's a child — you understand—"

The clerk glanced at the ledger, at some inner tally, and visibly decided that a charm-seller with a rock collection was not worth a scene. "Registry copy by month's end," he said. "Don't leave the city without filing travel papers."

"No," Casper said, which was true, and meant nothing, and got him out the door.

* * *

Mrs. Hobb stood on the step pointing dramatically at the street, and the street was empty except for slush and the departing Wardens, and Casper's stomach performed a slow, cold roll.

"Gone," Mrs. Hobb reported, aggrieved. "Just — gone. Between one blink and the next. Barefoot, mind you, in *this*." She gestured at the sky, where the first flakes had begun to come down — fat, slow, deliberate flakes, the vanguard of real snow. "No shoes. No coat. No sense. Stood right there looking at your door like it expected it to open. And when I spoke to it—" she lowered her voice to the register reserved for scandal, "—it made a sound at me. Not crying. Not baby-talk. *Words.* Words I've never heard. Like water going backward."

Casper looked at the empty doorstep. Snow was gathering on it already, unhurried, laying its white claim across the stone.

Something in his chest — the coiled, sleeping thing behind his knuckles, the thing he fed small meals of silence every morning — turned over.

"Did it now," he said.

"It gave me quite a turn," Mrs. Hobb said. "You'll want to lock up, I expect. Strange times. Wardens at the gate, snow in *Vell* — we never get proper snow, you know, the charter says—" and she continued, but Casper had stopped hearing her, because he was looking at the snow settling on his doorstep, and the snow was falling in a pattern.

A small, deliberate, spiraling pattern.

Like a knot.

Like a greeting.

"Well," Casper said softly, to nobody, to the weather, to ten years of careful lies stacked up like cordwood around a spark. "Hello."

Behind him, forgotten on the workbench under its dust sheet, the lamp he'd left burning guttered once — and flared green.
