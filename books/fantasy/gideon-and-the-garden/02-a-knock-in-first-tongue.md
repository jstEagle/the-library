## 2. A Knock in First Tongue

The first snow of the year came to Vell on a Tuesday, which Casper Dunmore considered typical. Snow, like tax collectors and bad news, had never once respected his schedule.

He was restocking his shelf of luck-knots when it started—small red cords braided into patterns that meant nothing at all but sold beautifully to people who wanted very much to believe otherwise. He'd learned long ago that a charm didn't need to work. It only needed to be *plausible*. A knot tied with steady hands and sold with a tired smile could carry a fisherman through a whole season of storms, so long as nobody asked him to actually calm one.

In Vell, that was the whole religion: nothing real, please, we're magic-free.

Casper wound the last cord around his wrist pegs and watched through the fogged window as the snow came down in fat, unhurried flakes. Ten years in this city and he still flinched at snow. Other people saw weather. He saw a sentence being spoken, slowly, in a language he'd tried very hard to forget.

"Closing early," he announced to the empty shop, because announcing things to empty rooms was another habit he'd never managed to break. "Snow. Terrible for business. Wonderful for me."

The bell over the door jingled anyway, and Mrs. Halloway from the dyer's shop bustled in, shaking flakes off her shawl.

"Casper, love, you're not closing? It's barely midday."

"It's snowing, Marta. In Vell, that's practically a holiday."

"It's *Tuesday*," she said, as if this settled everything, and bought three luck-knots—one for her husband's boat, one for her daughter's exams, and one, after a pause and a lowering of her voice, "for the dog. He's been off his food."

"The red one," Casper said gravely. "Red is best for animals. And for dogs with opinions."

She left happier than she'd come, which was the entire trick of his trade, and Casper turned the sign, banked the stove, and climbed the stairs to his rooms above the shop with a pot of barley tea and the particular satisfaction of a man who has successfully hidden from the world for one more day.

That was when he heard the knock.

Three knocks. Small ones. The kind made by a fist that hadn't yet grown into its knuckles.

He stood very still with his cup halfway to his mouth. Nobody knocked on his door upstairs. Customers came to the shop below; neighbors knew him well enough to shout up through the stairwell; and no one else in Vell had any reason at all to visit Casper Dunmore, who had spent ten years cultivating the personality of a slightly dull houseplant.

The knock came again. Three small knocks, patient as falling snow.

He set down the cup. He told himself—firmly, in the voice he used for lying to himself—that it was probably a lost child, and that he would send it home with a biscuit and directions, and that would be the end of it.

He opened the door.

A boy stood on his landing. Six years old, perhaps seven. Barefoot in the snow, though the snow on the landing lay undisturbed around him, as if it had declined to touch him. He wore a rough grey tunic with no coat, no scarf, no anything, and his hair was white—not old-man white, not pale-blonde white, but white the way frost on a windowpane is white, as if color simply hadn't been invited. His eyes were green. Not ordinary green. Green the way spring is green, the specific, unbearable green of the first leaf after a long death, and Casper felt that color land somewhere behind his ribs like a stone dropped down a well.

The boy looked up at him and spoke.

"*Síla veth amaru. Síla veth an dorath.*"

Casper's cup-hand went numb. Not metaphorically—he actually lost feeling in his fingers, and the tea would have spilled if he hadn't been gripping the doorframe with the other hand.

He knew those words.

Not the way you know a foreign phrase from a merchant's tongue. He knew them the way you know your own heartbeat: *Síla veth amaru*—the opening line of the Rite of Returning, spoken by every child of the garden-orders at spring planting, at every dawn of every festival, in a language older than any kingdom that had ever taxed him. The First Tongue. The speech of Amsel. A language that had been dead for ten years, buried under the same winter that had buried everything else, and here it was coming out of a barefoot child's mouth on his landing in magic-free Vell, clear as a bell, casual as asking for bread.

"*An dorath*," the boy repeated, when Casper didn't answer. *Open the door.* Which Casper supposed was fair, since he was standing in it.

"You can't—" Casper's voice came out cracked. He cleared his throat and tried again, in Common, the local trade-language, slow and simple. "Hello. Are you lost? Where are your parents?"

The boy tilted his head. He listened with his whole body, the way you listen to music rather than instructions, and then he answered—and it was more First Tongue, a flowing little stream of it, words Casper half-remembered from lullabies and half-dreaded from liturgies, and none of it, not one syllable, was Common.

"No," Casper whispered. "No, no, no. Try again. *Common.* You know—Vell-speech. *Bread. Home. Mother.*"

The boy frowned, concentrated with visible effort, opened his mouth—

—and produced something that sounded like a seagull arguing with a hinge.

Then he looked so crestfallen that Casper's heart did a thing he resented deeply.

"All right." Casper pinched the bridge of his nose. "All right. Come in before you freeze, because if the neighbors find a frozen child on my landing I will never hear the end of it, and also because—" he gestured helplessly at the boy's bare feet, which were, he now noticed, perfectly pink and warm-looking, "—apparently frostbite has decided to make an exception for you, which is *fine*, everything is fine."

Nothing, of course, was fine.

* * *

He fed the boy bread and honey because it was the only hospitality he could think of that required no conversation. The child ate with the solemn concentration of someone encountering food for the first time, or possibly food worth eating, and made a small sound of wonder at the honey that went straight through Casper's defenses and lodged there.

"Slowly," Casper said, out of pure habit. "Nobody's taking it from you."

The boy looked up. And said, softly, in that impossible language, "*Thavi ren.*" — *no one takes* — a phrase from the garden-creed, the promise the old orders made to every growing thing. Casper put down the knife very carefully.

"Where did you learn that?" he asked, knowing there was no point. "Who taught you the rites? Who—"

The boy touched his own chest, then pointed out the window, northward, and said a longer phrase, one Casper recognized with a cold sliding sensation in his stomach, because it was from the Litany of Roots, the prayer said over the covenant-stone itself, the one he had last heard spoken by dying men in a burning grove:

"*Amsel toru venn. Amsel toru ven-nai.*"

*Amsel remembers. Amsel remembers us.*

The stove ticked. Snow pressed against the windows. Casper sat across from a child who had just recited the name of the place he had destroyed, in the language of the people he had failed, and the boy watched him with those spring-green eyes, waiting, patient, utterly unafraid, as if Casper were the thing that had been expected all along.

"I don't know what you want from me," Casper said quietly. It wasn't entirely true. That was the problem. Some traitorous part of him, some part he'd starved for ten years, understood every word and was leaning toward the boy like a plant toward light.

The boy reached across the table and patted Casper's hand twice, gently, the way you'd comfort someone, and then pointed at the bread, meaning *more, please*, because apparently he could be both an omen of the apocalypse and a hungry six-year-old at the same time.

Casper got up and cut him more bread.

* * *

By evening he had established several facts, mostly by pantomime, and disliked every one of them.

Fact one: the boy could not speak Common, or any other language except the First Tongue. Casper had tested this with every trick he knew—pointing at objects, naming them, drawing in spilled flour (the boy had watched with interest and then drawn, unhelpfully, a flower). Nothing. The First Tongue poured out of him like water finding its level, and everything else bounced off.

Fact two: the boy was not cold. Casper had wrapped him in a blanket anyway, on principle, and the boy had accepted it the way royalty accepts tribute.

Fact three: the snow refused to land on him. Casper had watched through the window for a full minute while flakes swerved around the boy's head like birds avoiding a cat. Whatever this child was, winter knew about him. Winter was, apparently, *polite* about him.

And fact four, the one Casper kept circling back to while pretending to tidy his already-tidy shelves: the boy had appeared on the year's first snow, barefoot, speaking a dead language, wearing the face of every omen Casper's grandmother had ever used to frighten children into good behavior.

*When the garden needs planting,* she used to say, *it sends what it sends.*

He'd thought those were stories. He'd hoped they were stories. But hope, Casper had learned, was just fear wearing its good clothes.

The boy had fallen asleep in the armchair by the stove, curled up small, the blanket rising and falling. His open palm lay slack against the armrest, and in the firelight Casper could see the lines of it clearly—and stopped breathing.

There, scarred into the skin of the boy's palm, fine as a needle's work: a circle of seven leaves around a single seed.

The sigil of the Gardeners of Amsel. The mark that had been burned into Casper's own palm at nine years old, in a rite he had helped end forever. An order extinct for ten years. A mark that could not be given by anyone alive.

"Oh," Casper said, to the sleeping child, to the stove, to the snow, to whatever was listening. "Oh, you have got to be joking."

The boy slept on, breathing softly, his scarred hand open like a small green promise.

Outside, the snow kept falling—the first snow of the year, patient and unhurried, covering Vell street by street, and somewhere far to the north, Casper knew without knowing how he knew, something old had just felt a seed take root, and had begun, very slowly, to remember.

He sat down across from the boy and did not sleep at all.
