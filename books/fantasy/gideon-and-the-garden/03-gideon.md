## 3. Gideon

The boy did not leave.

Casper had expected him to, honestly. Children were like cats and weather—unreliable, transient, prone to wandering off the moment your back was turned. He'd set out bread and water on the first night, pointed at the door in what he hoped was a universal gesture of *this is available to you, please avail yourself elsewhere*, and gone to bed telling himself that by morning the whole strange business would have resolved itself into a bad dream about snow.

By morning, the boy was sitting cross-legged on Casper's kitchen floor, holding the empty plate like a treasure he intended to keep forever, watching Casper with those pale green eyes that made the hair on his arms stand up in a way that had nothing to do with cold.

"You're still here," Casper said.

The boy said nothing. Of course he said nothing. He opened his mouth, and what came out was that language again—that impossible, rolling, root-deep speech that Casper had heard exactly twice in his life before this week: once from a dying priestess, and once from his own mouth, at nine years old, standing in a circle of robed adults who'd told him the words meant *the ground remembers*.

He hadn't thought of that in ten years. He resented being made to think of it now.

"Right," Casper said, because talking to himself had become something of a specialty over the past decade. "Well. You can't stay."

The boy tilted his head. It was not an argumentative tilt. It was worse—it was the tilt of someone who genuinely did not understand the concept of *can't stay*, as though the words belonged to a foreign tongue, which, in fairness, everything did to him.

"I have neighbors," Casper said. "Neighbors with eyes. Neighbors who already think I'm odd because I close my shutters before dark and don't attend the winter festival and once sold a luck-knot that actually worked, which caused no end of trouble." He rubbed his face. His hands ached the way they always ached when it snowed—the old storm-gift lying dormant in his bones like a bruise that never quite healed. "You cannot be here when Mrs. Halloway comes for her fortnightly charm. She notices things. She noticed I'd gained weight the week I started eating better, and she told me so with great satisfaction, as though my improved diet were a scandal she'd personally uncovered."

The boy watched this entire speech with the grave attention of a scholar. Then he held up the empty plate.

Something in Casper's chest did a thing he did not authorize it to do.

"Oh, don't—" he said. "Don't do that. Don't hold the plate out like that. That's manipulation. That's how they get you." The boy continued holding the plate. His fingers were still blue-tinged at the tips despite the fire Casper had built last night, and there was a smudge of flour on his cheek from where he'd apparently investigated the flour bin at some point during the night, and his hair stuck up at the crown in a way that suggested he'd slept against something uneven, which—the floor, yes, he'd slept on the floor because Casper had given him the cot and then lain awake all night listening to the small sounds of a child breathing in his house, which was a sound the house had never made before and which had kept him awake the way a strange noise keeps you awake, except this noise was pleasant, which was somehow worse.

"Fine," Casper said. "Fine. One more breakfast. As a farewell meal. This is a farewell meal, do you understand? We are saying goodbye with bread."

The boy's whole face changed. It was like watching a shuttered window thrown open—sudden light everywhere, the kind of smile that has no defenses built around it because it has never needed any. Casper felt the sight of it land somewhere beneath his ribs and settle there, unwelcome as a splinter.

*I'm in trouble,* he thought, with the calm of a man reading his own diagnosis. *This is what trouble looks like.*

* * *

He named him on the third day, mostly out of necessity.

It had become awkward, referring to the boy as "you" and "the boy" and, in one moment of weakness, "small intruder," which had earned him a look of such profound hurt that Casper had spent the next hour apologizing in gestures—hand over heart, both palms up, the universal vocabulary of *I didn't mean it*. The boy had accepted the apology with the terrifying grace of someone who forgives easily and remembers completely.

So: a name. Every child needed one, and besides, Mrs. Halloway was due, and if she found a nameless barefoot child in Casper's kitchen she would ask questions, and questions were the one currency Casper could not afford.

He stood in front of the boy that morning with a slate he used for prices and wrote out candidates. *Tomas.* Common enough. *Wren.* A bit precious. *Aldous.* Too many syllables for a child who might need to answer to it quickly while hiding in a cellar.

The boy studied each name with furrowed concentration, then shook his head at all of them—not rejecting them, Casper realized, but confused by them, the way you'd be confused if someone offered you stones and called them dinner.

"They're just names," Casper said. "Sounds people answer to. You'll get used to—"

The boy reached out and took the slate from him. He turned it over. On the back, in the corner, half-erased under layers of old chalk dust, was a word Casper had written months ago and forgotten—a note to himself, a reminder, one of the small private superstitions he kept:

*gideon — watcher, hewer. good stone for walls.*

He'd copied it out of an old etymology book he'd bought for two coppers, back when he was still trying to convince himself that words were safe things to collect. He'd liked the sound of it. Heavy, warm, a name you could lean against.

The boy tapped the word. Once. Twice. Then looked up at Casper with an expression of such plain, uncomplicated certainty that arguing felt like arguing with the tide.

"Gideon," Casper said.

The boy smiled—that open-window smile again—and touched his own chest, and said something in the First Tongue that was soft and round and unmistakably his own name, whatever it truly was, the name some garden or god or grief had given him before either of them existed.

"Well," Casper said, throat unexpectedly tight. "Gideon it is. Gideon Dunmore." He stopped. "No. Not Dunmore. Gideon... of nowhere. Gideon the lodger. Gideon, temporary resident, pending resolution of circumstances beyond my control."

Gideon repeated after him—*"Dunmore"*—in his own silent way, mouthing the shape of it, and Casper gave up on the distinction entirely.

* * *

Hiding a child in a two-room shop-house above a charm-seller's stall turned out to require the logistical genius of a military campaign.

The first problem was sound. Gideon made none—or almost none—but the house itself betrayed him. It creaked differently with two bodies in it. The stair complained on the fourth step only when someone small and quick took it at a run, which Gideon did constantly, having discovered stairs the way other children discover sugar. Casper solved this with a rug and a lecture delivered entirely in mime, which Gideon absorbed with unsettling speed.

The second problem was food. Casper had budgeted for one mouth, a mouth that ate sparingly and without joy, because eating had always seemed to him a thing best done efficiently, like paying taxes. Gideon ate the way spring eats snow—with total commitment and visible delight. He ate crusts first. He ate the heel of the loaf, which Casper had been saving on principle. He ate stewed apples with his fingers until Casper surrendered a spoon, and then he ate stewed apples with the spoon as though the spoon were a revelation vouchsafed to him alone.

"Chew," Casper found himself saying, three times a day, in the tone of a man reciting scripture. "Bread is not a race. Nothing here is a race. We are the slowest household in Vell and proud of it."

And the third problem—the one Casper had not anticipated, the one that crept up on him over the course of that first week like warmth creeping into a cold room—was that teaching a child to hide turned out to be indistinguishable, most hours of the day, from simply teaching a child.

There was no common language. So Casper taught him bread instead of words: how to knead until the dough went smooth and elastic, how to know the oven was ready by holding your palm near the door and counting, how to score the tops so they split pretty instead of ugly. Gideon learned with his whole body, leaning into the kneading with his slight weight, frowning at the dough as though it might confess something. When his first loaf came out lopsided and magnificent, he held it up to Casper with both hands, and Casper said "yes, that's a loaf, well done," in a voice that cracked slightly on *well done*, and pretended it hadn't.

He taught him warmth instead of words too: which side of the bed the draft came from, why you warmed your boots before putting them on rather than after, how to sit near a fire so the heat got into your back and stayed there. Gideon had clearly never learned any of it. He approached heat like something borrowed—grateful, careful, half-expecting it to be taken back. It took four days before he stopped flinching when Casper dropped a blanket over his shoulders in the evening, and even then he flinched only a little, and only at first, and Casper pretended not to see because pretending not to see was a kindness too, sometimes, and he was discovering he had a whole storehouse of them he'd never once opened.

*You're getting attached,* he told himself, scrubbing the counter one night while Gideon slept—actually slept, in the cot now, Casper had lost the argument about the floor without ever realizing an argument had taken place. *This is attachment. This is the first stage. Next comes thinking of him as yours, and then comes losing him, and you know how that goes.*

But knowing how it goes, he was learning, had never once stopped anything.

* * *

It was Mrs. Halloway's visit that forced the discovery.

She arrived on a grey Thursday, punctual as a debt, her basket on her arm and her eyes already working. Casper met her at the stall below with his best approximation of a man with nothing upstairs worth noticing.

"Luck-knot," she said. "Red cord. And don't give me the weak ones, Dunmore, I can tell."

"Mrs. Halloway, I have never in my life tied a weak knot."

"You tied one in autumn. My nephew tripped over a curb wearing it."

"That was a strong knot protecting him from worse than curbs."

She snorted, which was her version of laughter, and while she rummaged through his knots with the suspicion of a customs officer, Casper heard it: the fourth step. The creak. Muffled by the rug, but there.

Mrs. Halloway's head came up like a hound's.

"Cats," Casper said immediately.

"You haven't a cat."

"I've recently acquired one. It's shy. Very shy. Practically invisible." He pressed a red-cord knot into her hand with perhaps too much force. "On the house. For the nephew. For the curbs."

She looked at him for a long moment—up at the ceiling, then back at his face, then at the ceiling again—and Casper watched ten years of careful dullness hang in the balance. Then she tucked the knot away and said, "That child's cough I heard last week through your wall. Yours?"

Casper's mind went white. "Yes," he heard himself say. "My—nephew. Visiting. From the country. He's unwell, so we're keeping him quiet."

"Hm." She settled her basket. "Country air's better for coughs."

"He's attached to the city."

"At nine years old."

"It happens."

Another long look. Then, at the door, without turning around: "Bring him by sometime. Growing boys need feeding, and I bake Thursdays." And she left, and Casper stood in his empty stall feeling as though he'd survived an avalanche by agreeing to become a mountain.

Upstairs, Gideon was crouched at the top of the stairs, listening with his whole body, and when Casper came up he searched Casper's face anxiously—*did I ruin it, are we caught*—and Casper realized, with a sensation like stepping off a ledge into deep water, that he wasn't afraid anymore. Somewhere in the last week the fear had quietly packed its bags, and what remained was something steadier and far more dangerous.

"We're fine," he said. "You did well. Fourth step and all." He hesitated, then, because honesty was apparently contagious: "She thinks you're my nephew now. Congratulations. You're family. Temporarily. Bureaucratically."

Gideon didn't understand a word of it. He understood all of it. He smiled, and Casper's ribs did the splinter thing again.

* * *

It happened that evening, or rather Casper finally let it happen.

Gideon was washing his hands at the basin—another lesson, three days old, which he performed now with ritual thoroughness—and the firelight caught his left palm as he turned it to dry. Casper saw it and went very still.

A scar. Old, deliberate, precise: a circle interlaced with a branching line, like a tree inside a ring of stone. Small, no bigger than a coin, but unmistakable. Casper knew it the way you know your own handwriting. He had one himself, faded to a ghost on the meat of his right thumb, burned there at nine years old by a priestess with warm hands and a voice like banked coals, while the whole order sang and the words meant *the ground remembers.*

The sigil of the Gardeners of Amsel. The mark of an order that had been dead for ten years, whose members had scattered or died or—like Casper—buried themselves alive in cities that hated magic.

He crossed the room without deciding to. Took Gideon's hand in both of his, gently, turning the palm to the light. Gideon let him, watchful, unafraid, and Casper traced the scar with one fingertip and felt the truth of it go through him like cold water: not ink, not paint, not chance. A brand. Given young. Given with ceremony.

Someone had marked this child as a Gardener. Someone had sung the rites over him. Which meant somewhere, impossibly, the order had endured long enough to pass its mark to a boy born after the fall—which meant the garden itself might have—

Casper stopped the thought there, at the edge of the cliff, because the drop below it was the kind of hope that breaks people.

Gideon was watching his face. Slowly, carefully, the boy turned his own hand over and pressed it flat against Casper's scarred thumb—mark to mark, matching them, the way you'd match two halves of a broken seal. Then he looked up, and his expression was not a question. It was an answer, offered patiently, to a question Casper had been refusing to ask since the night of the first snow.

*I know what you are,* that look said. *Do you remember what you are?*

Casper sat down heavily on the stool by the fire. Gideon climbed onto his knee with the unselfconscious entitlement of the very young, and Casper—storm-caller, oath-breaker, the man who ended the green world—put his arm around a child he'd known eight days and held on.

"Tenderness," he said to the fire, disgusted. "At my age. Honestly."

Gideon patted his chest twice, the way you'd pat a wall to test that it would hold.

Outside, the snow kept falling, soft and patient, like something waiting to be answered.
