## 4. What the Wardens Burn

The interrogation room in the Wardenhouse at Vell had once been a counting-house, and it still smelled of ink and old arithmetic. Sera Vane had always found that fitting. Everything in this city came down to sums. What a man earned, what he owed, what he was worth when the ledger closed over his name.

She sat across the table from the smuggler and let the silence do its work.

His name was Osk Brannick, and he was forty if he was a day, with a face like a dropped pie and hands that couldn't stop moving—rubbing his wrists where the salt-irons chafed, tugging his collar, smoothing the table as though it might be persuaded to say something kind about him. He'd been talking for ten minutes without stopping, which meant he hadn't yet said anything true.

"—and I'll swear on any god you like, Warden, on all of them at once if you want, I never carried so much as a luck-knot through the gates, let alone anything *tainted*—"

"Master Brannick." Sera laid one finger flat on the table. It was not a loud gesture. It didn't need to be. "You were stopped at the salt-gates three nights ago with a cart of dried herring."

"That's right, that's right, honest catch, honest coin—"

"You were also carrying," she said, and turned a paper toward him, "eleven vials of river-water blessed by a hedge-priest out of Marrow Fen, two amulets stitched with what our readers identify as covenant-script, and a live hen."

"A *hen*?"

"The hen is not the charge."

"No, no, of course, the hen is—" He laughed, high and wet. "The hen's dinner, Warden, that's all she is. You can't arrest a man for dinner."

Sera looked at him until he stopped laughing.

The lamp between them guttered and steadied. Outside, Vell went about its evening—the clatter of carts on the salt-scoured streets, a fishwife shouting prices, somewhere a fiddle badly played. A magic-free city, the charter said. Clean. The Pale Wardens kept it clean the way a housekeeper keeps a floor: not by loving it, but by sweeping.

"Here is what interests me," Sera said. "Not your hen. Not even your vials—a hedge-priest's blessing is spit and superstition, and we both know it. What interests me is what you said to the gate-sergeant when he asked you where you'd been." She slid the second paper forward. "Read it back to me."

Brannick read. His lips moved. His face did something complicated.

"'A child,'" Sera prompted, when he faltered. "Go on."

"A child on the north road," he recited, in the flat voice of a man walking on ice, "barefoot, in the snow, and it spoke—and it weren't speaking any tongue I knew, and my ears have heard everything from Low Marrow to the Salt Cant, Warden, everything—and then it looked at me, and the snow around its feet—" He stopped.

"Went green," Sera said quietly.

He nodded. Just once. Like a man confirming his own death warrant.

"And you thought," Sera continued, folding her hands, "that the correct response to this was to sell the story to a tavern full of dockworkers for the price of a drink."

"I thought—I mean, nobody believes those stories, do they? It's a good story. People buy you drinks for a good story."

"People," said Sera, "are currently buying each other drinks all up and down the harbor quarter telling a story about a barefoot child who makes snow grow grass. Do you know how many people believe it?"

"...Some?"

"All of them," Sera said. "That's the trouble with miracles, Master Brannick. They're the only stories nobody ever doubts."

* * *

Afterward, when they'd taken Brannick down to a cell to sweat out his sentence—two years' labor on the wall-carts, standard for first offense, merciful because he'd talked—Sera stood at the window of the Wardenhouse and watched the snow come down over Vell.

Her orders sat in her breast pocket, folded twice. She didn't need to take them out. She knew them word for word; she'd read them eleven times, which was ten more than necessary.

*Report of anomalous speech-patterns, juvenile subject, vicinity of Vell. Classification: First Tongue, probable. Directive: locate, confirm, erase. Erasure to include all secondary witnesses.*

Erase. The orders used the word the way a butcher's bill used the word *dressing*. Clean, administrative, final. A child, erased. Dockworkers who'd heard a tavern tale, erased. A smuggler already in a cell, erased, though Sera supposed she could stretch the paperwork there—call his testimony unreliable, keep him alive on the wall-carts where no one would listen to him anyway. Small mercies. She collected them the way other women collected buttons.

"You're brooding, Vane."

Corporal Hesk stood in the doorway with two cups of chicory and the expression of a man who considered brooding a personal failing, like flatulence or poetry. He was twenty-six, earnest, and had joined the Wardens because he genuinely believed in a world without magic, which Sera had always found either admirable or tragic depending on how much chicory she'd had.

"I'm working," she said.

"You're standing at a window looking at nothing. That's brooding. My mother did it. It precedes opinions." He handed her a cup. "So. Is it true? The child?"

"Why would it matter if it were true?"

"Because if it's true, we're going to find it, and you're going to—" He stopped himself, which was decent of him. He was learning, slowly, which subjects she kept locked. "Because I'd rather know now than later," he finished, lamely.

Sera drank her chicory. It was terrible. Everything in Vell was terrible except the bread, and she suspected even the bread was only good by comparison.

"Hesk," she said. "What do you remember of the fall of Amsel?"

He blinked. "I was six."

"So you remember the stories, then. The storm-caller who broke the stone. The winter that never lifted. Every hearth-tale in the north starts with Casper Dunmore's name."

"Casper Dunmore," Hesk repeated, tasting it. "They say the Wardens burned him out of history. No portraits, no records. Like he never lived."

"We're thorough," Sera said.

"But you knew him." He said it carefully, watching her. "Everyone knows you trained under the same master before the Order took you. Before you chose us."

*I didn't choose you,* she almost said. *I chose the only side that was still standing.* But she drank her chicory instead, and let the silence be an answer, because with Hesk silence was usually kinder.

"Find me everything the gate-sergeants logged this month," she said instead. "Every traveler's statement out of the north quarter. And pull the charm-seller registries. If a child like that is in Vell, someone's feeding it, clothing it, or ignoring it, and all three leave traces."

"Charm-sellers." Hesk made a face. "Luck-knots and love-scribbles. Half of them are frauds."

"Half," Sera agreed. "The other half are worse than frauds. Go."

He went. Sera stayed at the window a while longer, cup cooling in her hands, watching the snow bury the streets of a city that had outlawed wonder and gotten very good at it.

There had been a time—not long, a season, maybe two—when she'd believed the opposite. When she'd been a girl in the covenant-halls with cold hands and a hot heart, learning the First Tongue from a master who smelled of cedar and disappointment, and the boy beside her had been quick and laughing and reckless, and she had loved him the way you love a fire: for the warmth, and at a distance, and knowing it would burn something down eventually.

Casper Dunmore had burned something down eventually. An entire world's spring, as it turned out.

And she had chosen the Wardens because grief needs somewhere to go, and duty takes walk-ins.

*Locate, confirm, erase,* said the orders in her pocket.

*A child,* said the smuggler's testimony. *Barefoot. In the snow. Speaking the old words.*

Sera had heard the First Tongue last spoken aloud eleven years ago, in a courtroom, by a young man in irons who refused to kneel. She remembered every syllable. She suspected she would remember them on her deathbed, along with the sound the covenant-stone made when it broke—which was not a crack at all, but a sigh, enormous and relieved, like something finally allowed to stop holding itself together.

She had spent eleven years telling herself that sound had been her imagination.

* * *

The registries told her less than she hoped and more than she feared.

Vell licensed forty-one charm-sellers. Thirty-eight were frauds of the ordinary, harmless kind—knots sold for luck, ribbons sold for love, powders that were mostly flour and confidence. Two were hedge-witches of no particular talent who paid their fees on time and poisoned no one. And one—

One was registered as *C. Dunmore*, which was either the bravest or stupidest thing Sera had seen in eleven years, and she had seen men try to smuggle thunderstones in their boots.

She sat in her office with the page flat under the lamp and read the entry four times. *Dunmore, C. Charm-seller, third ward. Luck-knots, minor blessings (non-operative). Licensed seven years. No violations.*

No violations. Of course not. He would be careful. He had always been careful, after—he'd learned caution the way other men learned piety, late and completely. She could see him now in her mind's eye: thinner, grayer, hands that used to call lightning held steady over a table of knotted thread, selling farmers fair winds he could have given them for free with a word.

*Why?* she wondered, and then immediately: *because penance is a full-time occupation,* and then, because she was honest with herself perhaps twice a year and tonight appeared to be one of them: *and because he can't help it. He never could. He sees something broken and he has to mend it, even broken things that should stay broken. Even covenants. Especially covenants.*

She put the registry away.

The sensible move—the dutiful move—was to report the name upward and let a full company handle it. Locate, confirm, erase. She was a senior Warden; she did not make arrests alone anymore; she made *cases*. All she had to do was write one line on a requisition form and the machinery would grind into motion, vast and pale and impersonal, and by week's end there would be no charm-seller in the third ward and no child anywhere at all.

Sera took out a fresh sheet of paper.

She wrote: *Investigation ongoing. Subject unconfirmed. Possible misidentification—tavern rumor, low credibility. Recommend continued surveillance.*

It was, technically, a lie. It was also, she noted with detached professional interest, the first lie she had told the Order in eleven years of service, and it had taken her less time to write than a grocery list.

She blotted it, sealed it, and set it aside for morning dispatch. Then she sat very still and listened to the building settle around her, and tried to work out what exactly she had just done, and for whom, and whether it was mercy or cowardice or simply the old fire in her, banked but not dead, refusing to let the Wardens have him without her.

All three, probably. She'd never been able to hold a feeling without three more arriving to keep it company.

* * *

Three days later, the surveillance reports started coming in, and Sera stopped sleeping properly.

The third ward charm-seller had a visitor pattern. Ordinary enough at first—farmers, fishwives, a nervous bride. But in the past fortnight, a neighbor had reported lights burning late. A baker swore the man had bought double flour since the first snow. And a child had been seen—this from a laundress whose testimony Sera read five times—*a small boy, dark-haired, never speaks, follows the charm-seller like a duckling. Barefoot once, in the cold, if you can credit it. The charm-seller carried him home under his arm like a loaf.*

Under his arm. Like a loaf.

Sera read that line and had to put the report down and press the heels of her hands against her eyes for a moment, because there it was, whole and unmistakable: Casper Dunmore, oath-breaker, realm-doomer, the most wanted man in two worlds, carrying a mute child home through the snow under his arm like a loaf of bread, and being *seen*, and doing nothing about it, because eight days in he had already gone soft as spring ground.

She knew that softness. She'd trained half of it into him herself, back when training him had been the great work of her life, before it became the great regret.

"Vane?" Hesk, in the doorway again, chicory again. "Dispatch came back from the capital. Your surveillance request—they're sending someone to look at your case files."

Something went cold and quiet in her chest. "Who?"

"Not named. But the seal was the Captain's own, and there's a rider already on the road." Hesk hesitated. "Sera—off the record—is there a case? Because the way you've been writing these reports, it reads like a man describing a dog that isn't in the room."

"The case is developing," Sera said.

"Developing into what?"

Into a choice, she thought. Into a door closing. Into the sound a covenant-stone makes when it breaks, which she had never actually heard, and the sound a woman's loyalty makes when it cracks, which she was hearing right now, faintly, under the chicory smell and the snow and the arithmetic.

"Into an arrest," she said. "Soon. Give me four days."

Hesk nodded and left, satisfied, and Sera sat alone with her lamp and her lies and the growing ledger of everything she owed and could not pay.

Four days. She had bought him four days, maybe, with a lie written faster than a grocery list. After that the Order's machinery would arrive, pale and total, and it would not ask her permission, and it would not care that the child under the charm-seller's arm had spoken the First Tongue, the language of the garden, the language Sera's own mother had sung to her before the winter took the garden and the singing both.

*Locate, confirm, erase.*

She opened her case file, took out a fresh map of Vell and its approaches, and began, with a steady hand and a sick heart, to plan the raid she was now officially required to lead.

Outside, the snow kept falling on the third ward, soft and patient, covering the roof of a certain charm-shop, and Sera Vane caught herself—horribly, unforgivably—hoping it would go on falling forever, just deep enough to hide a set of small bare footprints leading away north.
