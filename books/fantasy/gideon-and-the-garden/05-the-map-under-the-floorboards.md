## 5. The Map Under the Floorboards

The trouble with keeping a child secret in a city built on gossip was that children, by their nature, were loud.

Gideon had been with Casper for eleven days now, and in that time the boy had: knocked over the jar of dried lavender twice, discovered that the kettle whistled if you held it over the fire just so, learned to say "bread" in Vellish with an accent that made the baker's wife across the lane clutch her heart, and developed a deep and abiding feud with the neighbor's cat, a fat orange tyrant named Duchess who considered the windowsill her sovereign territory.

Casper had named him Gideon on the third morning, mostly because the boy kept pointing at himself expectantly until Casper gave in. It was the name of a saint from a story his mother used to tell — a boy who planted a garden in the middle of a war and refused to stop watering it even when the soldiers came through. His mother had liked that story. Casper had always found it impractical. Gardens did not survive wars. He knew this better than most.

He'd told the neighbors the boy was his sister's, come down from the hill country while she sorted out some family matter. This was a lie of middling quality. The neighbors accepted it because the neighbors wanted to accept it; a charm-seller with a mysterious past adopting a silent child was far more interesting than a charm-seller who simply sold charms, and interesting things made for better conversation than suspicion. Mrs. Halloway two doors down had already knitted the boy a scarf the color of a bruise. Gideon wore it everywhere, including indoors, including in summer-warm rooms, as though it were armor.

It was going well. That was what frightened Casper. Things that went well in his life had a documented history of detonating shortly thereafter.

* * *

On the twelfth day, Casper spilled the flour.

This was, in itself, unremarkable. Casper spilled flour with the regularity of tides. His hands — the hands that had once called lightning down out of a clear sky, that had shaped storms like a potter shapes clay — were now the hands of a man who could not keep a sack of milled wheat upright on a shelf. Ten years of deliberate numbness had done that. You cannot bury a gift without burying the fingers that held it. They woke slow, moved clumsily, felt the world through wool.

So when the sack tipped and flour went everywhere across the floorboards of the back room — white dust rising, settling on everything, Gideon sneezing from where he sat cross-legged by the cold hearth — Casper only sighed and went for the broom.

When he turned back, the boy was drawing in it.

Casper stopped in the doorway with the broom half-raised, and something old and cold walked up his spine.

The child's finger moved through the flour with a sureness he had never seen in him before. Gideon, who still fumbled his spoon, who held chalk like it might bite him, was tracing lines in the spilled white with the confidence of someone copying from memory. A long line first — a road. Then branching marks. Then small careful symbols at intervals along it, each one placed without hesitation.

A bridge. Two strokes crossing. A mountain drawn as three peaks, the middle one taller. And then—

Casper set the broom down very slowly, the way you set down a thing when your hands have begun to shake and you don't want the thing to know.

"That's the Marn," he said quietly. "That's the ferry crossing at Hollow Ford."

Gideon didn't look up. His finger kept moving. Past the river, past the shrine symbol — a little circle with a dot, exactly how the old road-wardens marked them, exactly how they'd been marked on maps a hundred years dead — up into the mountains, along a switchback path no living cartographer had charted, to a place near the top where the boy drew a shape Casper knew better than his own heartbeat.

A garden. Walled. With a gate.

And inside the walls, a single mark: a circle bisected by a growing stem, leafed on both sides.

The sigil of the Order of Amsel. The garden-sigil. The same mark scarred into the boy's left palm, which Casper had told himself for eleven days was a birthmark, a scald, an accident, anything other than what it was.

"No," Casper said aloud, to the room, to the boy, to whatever was listening. "No, that's not—"

But it was. Of course it was. Because Casper had burned every map of that road. He had burned them one at a time in the last week before he fled south, feeding them page by page into the great hearth of the scriptorium while the snow came down and Sera stood in the doorway watching him with an expression he had spent ten years not thinking about. Every survey, every pilgrim's guide, every merchant's route-book that mentioned the northern passes. He had burned the master atlas. He had burned his own field notes. He had burned a letter from his mother that happened to mention the region, which was perhaps excessive, but grief makes thorough arsonists of us all.

There was no map to the Garden of Amsel left in the world. He had made certain of it with his own hands.

And now a mute child was drawing one in flour on his floor, accurate to the ford, accurate to the shrine, accurate to the gate.

"Gideon." Casper crouched down beside him, careful, the way you approach a bird or a lit fuse. "Where did you see this?"

The boy looked up. His eyes were the gray-green of new leaves just before they unfurl, and Casper had noticed this before and told himself it meant nothing, eyes were eyes, plenty of people had green eyes, it was a common enough color, stop looking for omens in a child's face.

Gideon pointed at his own chest.

"You've *been* there?"

The boy tilted his head — Casper had learned this tilt meant the question hadn't landed right, that the words didn't fit whatever the boy actually meant. Gideon tried again. He pressed his palm flat against his chest, over his heart, then moved the hand outward, opening his fingers like something blooming.

Not *I have been there.* Something else. Something more like: *it is in me.*

Casper sat down on the floorboards amid the flour, which was the sort of dignity-preserving move available to men whose legs had stopped consulting them. Dust rose around him. Somewhere outside, Duchess the cat was yelling at a pigeon. The kettle ticked as it cooled. The world continued being ordinary, stubbornly, insultingly ordinary, while Casper Dunmore sat in flour and understood, at last, what had knocked on his door in the First Tongue.

* * *

Here is what Casper knew about the covenant, and he knew it better than anyone alive, because he had broken it:

The covenant between the realms was not a treaty. Treaties were paper, and paper burned — he could attest. The covenant was a garden. The Garden of Amsel had been planted at the hinge-point between the two worlds, in the high country where the borders ran thin, and its roots grew down into both soils at once. While the garden lived, the realms breathed together. Seasons crossed the border freely. Magic flowed south into Vell's lowlands as gently as water through sand, and the southern realms sent north their warmth, their trade, their songs. Neither side remembered, after a while, that there had ever been a wall, because the garden had grown over it.

And the covenant had a heart. Not a stone, not a crown, not a sword in a lake — the old stories got all of that wrong. The heart was a seed. A living seed, tended by the order, passed down hand to hand across centuries, planted and replanted whenever the garden needed renewing. The seed carried the whole covenant in it the way an acorn carries a forest: folded small, waiting, patient beyond all reason.

Ten years ago, the realms had decided the seed should be sacrificed. There had been a blight coming up from the deep places, a hunger in the roots, and the council of both realms had determined — calmly, reasonably, with minutes taken and copies distributed — that the way to save the garden was to burn out its heart. Scorch the seed. Cut the covenant loose from its living root and anchor it instead to something that couldn't sicken, couldn't fail, couldn't die: a pillar of raw power, bound and bled forever.

They had asked Casper to be the binding. Storm-caller, strongest of his generation, beloved of the order. They had dressed it up in ceremony and called it honor.

He had said no. And when they moved to do it anyway, he had put his hand against the covenant stone and called down everything he had, and shattered it, and shattered the garden's anchoring with it, because he thought — God help him, he had genuinely thought — that a broken covenant could be reforged, but a murdered seed could not be regrown. He broke the worlds apart to save the seed.

Then the winter came, faster and harder than any of them had predicted, and the seed vanished in the chaos of the fall, and Casper stopped believing in his own reasoning, and he ran south, and he buried his hands in his pockets and his name in a new one, and ten years passed like a long held breath.

And now a barefoot boy was sitting in his flour, wearing a bruise-colored scarf, drawing roads that existed nowhere on paper anymore, pressing his hand to his chest and opening his fingers like a blossom.

*You are the seed,* Casper thought. *You're not carrying it. You aren't a courier or a vessel or any of the words the old texts used. You ARE it. The covenant grew itself a child.*

Because that was the thing about seeds. He should have remembered this; his mother had told him often enough. A seed does not need the gardener to survive. A seed needs soil, and water, and time, and it will wait out empires. And when the moment comes — when the frost lifts just enough, when the ground softens — it sends up a shoot toward the light without asking anyone's permission.

The long winter falling on both realms wasn't weather. It was a dying. The covenant's death throes, dragging both worlds down with it, slower in the south, faster in the north, but everywhere, everywhere. And somewhere in the dying, the last living piece of the covenant had done the only thing left to do. It had germinated. It had become a boy. And the boy had walked — barefoot, through the year's first snow, guided by something older than instinct — to the one person in all the world who knew the road north.

To the man who broke it.

"Oh," Casper said. It came out very small. "Oh, you clever, impossible thing."

Gideon watched him with those leaf-colored eyes, head tilted, and then — deliberately, the way he did everything that mattered — reached out one finger and completed the map. One final stroke, from the walled garden outward, off the edge of the flour-dusted boards, pointing north. Pointing home.

* * *

Casper swept up the flour. He did it mechanically, badly, leaving streaks, and Gideon helped by pushing most of it into a pile and then sitting in the pile, which was the way of children everywhere and in every age, Casper supposed, including presumably whatever strange age the boy had germinated out of.

He told himself, while he swept, that there might be another explanation. Coincidence. The boy might have seen a map somewhere — except no such map existed. Someone might have shown him — except who? The boy had appeared out of the snow with no history and no voice and a sigil scarred into his palm. Casper had spent eleven days constructing smaller and smaller explanations, and the flour had knocked them all down in the space of a hundred heartbeats.

Which left the large explanation. And the large explanation had teeth.

Because if Gideon was the seed — the living heart of the covenant — then everyone who had ever wanted the covenant dead would want the boy dead too. The Pale Wardens, who had spent ten years methodically erasing the old orders, burning hymnals and hedge-witches and half-remembered rites, would burn a child fastest of all. And they weren't stupid. Rumors traveled. A speaking child, a child who spoke the First Tongue, appearing in Vell of all places — Casper had heard the whisper already, in the market, third-hand, a smuggler's tale about Wardens asking questions in the dockside taverns. He'd told himself it was nothing. Wardens always asked questions. It was practically their hobby.

But questions had a way of becoming boots on stairs.

And there was the other thing, the thing he kept circling and avoiding, the way you circle a wound in the bath. If the boy was the seed, then the seed could be *planted.* The covenant could be reforged. Which meant the road north — the road the boy had drawn in flour, the road Casper had burned out of the world precisely so that no one, including himself, could ever be tempted to walk it again — that road was open after all. It had been waiting under the floorboards of him for ten years, the way the map had waited under the flour, and all it took was a child's finger to uncover it.

He could refuse. He could take the boy south, deeper into Vell, lose him in the crowds, raise him on bread and luck-knots and careful lies, and let the winter finish falling on both realms. Let the world end politely, gradually, in longer winters and thinner harvests, in a chill that crept rather than crashed, so that no one would ever have to point at Casper Dunmore and say *there, that's the man who could have stopped it and chose his own skin instead.*

He had gotten very good, these ten years, at choosing his own skin.

Casper leaned the broom against the wall and looked at the boy, who was now attempting to teach Duchess the cat a word in First Tongue through the window glass. The cat was unmoved. Gideon persisted. There was flour on his nose and frost-melt drying in his hair and the bruise-colored scarf wrapped up to his chin, and he was humming — tuneless, contented, a sound with no words in it, though Casper had the sudden, unwelcome certainty that the tune was one of the old covenant-hymns, worn smooth of language, kept anyway.

"The past refuses to stay buried," Casper informed the room. "I should sue it for trespass."

Gideon glanced over, caught the tone if not the words, and grinned — a gap-toothed, entirely human grin, and Casper felt the last of his smaller explanations crumble, because no one had taught this child to grin like that. Grins like that were grown in gardens.

* * *

That night, after Gideon slept — curled in the good bed, because Casper had lost the argument about the pallet on day four and never reopened negotiations — Casper pried up the third floorboard from the hearth.

Underneath, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff with age, was everything he'd saved from the burning. Not much. A pilgrim's token stamped with the garden-sigil. A pressed sprig of amsel-bloom, brown and brittle, that had once been silver-blue. And a letter, unsent, written the night before the fall, addressed to a woman named Sera Vane, which began *By the time you read this I will be the worst kind of traitor, and I want you to know why* and ended, forty pages later, having explained nothing adequately, with *I'm sorry. I'm so sorry. Plant something.*

He had never sent it. He had never been able to decide whether it was a confession or a plea or a coward's apology, so it had stayed under the floorboards, doing what the past does: waiting.

Casper sat on the cold floor with the letter in his lap and listened to the boy breathe in the next room, steady as a tide, and outside the wind was picking up out of the north, and it smelled — he was almost sure, he was nearly certain, he had not imagined things in ten years and he was not imagining this — it smelled faintly of green.

"Right," Casper said to the empty room, to the letter, to the ten years of careful small concealments stacked around him like sandbags against a flood that had finally arrived. "Right."

In the morning, he started packing.

* * *

He was reaching for the flour sack — you could bake hardtack for the road, and hardtack forgave clumsy hands — when he heard the bells.

Not the chapel bells. The ward-bells. Three rings, pause, three rings: the signal every quarter of Vell knew and dreaded. Inspection. And not the routine kind either; the routine kind rang twice. Three rings meant they were sweeping door to door, and they were starting at the end of Charmmonger's Lane.

Casper stood very still in his kitchen, flour sack in his arms, and listened to the boots.

Down the lane, Mrs. Halloway's dog began to bark.
