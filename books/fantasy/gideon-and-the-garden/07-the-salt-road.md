## 7. The Salt Road

The world was too loud.

Gideon had known this before, in the way he knew all things—without knowing how he knew them. The world had always been loud. But the world behind the door with the warm one in it had been a small loud, a folded-up loud, like a bird tucked under a wing. The road was not that. The road was everything at once.

The wind came first. It did not blow so much as *speak*, and it spoke in every voice it had ever borrowed: the hiss of salt crystals grinding against each other for ten thousand years, the rattle of dead grasses, the far-off moan of something that might have been a gate or might have been grief. Gideon pressed his small hands over his ears and discovered that this did nothing at all, because the wind was also inside him, humming along the hollow places where words should have lived.

*Too fast,* said the wind. *Too small. Too far from the green.*

He agreed with all of it.

The warm one walked ahead of him on the white road, wrapped in a coat the color of wet bark, and Gideon watched him the way you watch a fire in a cold room—with your whole body leaning toward it. The warm one's name was a long gray thing, full of corners. *Casper.* Gideon kept it folded up inside himself next to the other important things: the smell of bread, the shape of a hand that pulled blankets higher in the night, the sound the warm one made when he thought no one listened—a low hum, half song, half apology.

The warm one turned around. He always turned around. Every forty steps, maybe fifty, his head would swivel back and there would be a moment where his eyes went soft and worried and counted—one child, still here, still barefoot, why is he still barefoot—and then he would hold out his mitten-less hand, and Gideon would take it, because the warm one's palm was a hearth. Not hot. Hearth. There was a difference, and Gideon knew it the way he knew the First Tongue: completely, and without permission.

*Your feet,* the warm one signed, badly, with his free hand. The signs were wrong. They were wrong the way a foreigner's accent is wrong—all the shapes correct but none of the music. But Gideon had learned to read the warm one's whole body anyway, because bodies were honest in a way mouths were not. *Your feet are cold.*

Gideon looked down at his feet. His feet were fine. His feet were the only part of him that was fine. His feet remembered something the rest of him had forgotten—that soil could be kind, that ground could rise up to meet you instead of just lying there being ground. He wiggled his toes at the warm one, which made the warm one do the face. The face was the best thing on the whole loud road. It was the face of a man who has been defeated by a child's toes and does not entirely mind.

They walked on.

* * *

The salt stretched out forever in every direction, flat and blinding, and the sun sat on it like an egg in a dish. Gideon had never seen the sun be so *present* before. In Vell the sun had come through windows politely, asking permission. Here it simply arrived, everywhere, uninvited, and it made the salt shine so hard that Gideon had to squint until the world went gold at the edges.

And in the gold, things moved.

Not real things. Memory-things. Shimmer-things. When the light bounced off the salt just right, Gideon saw shapes standing out on the white plain: tall green shapes, arching shapes, the suggestion of hedges that went on farther than hedges should. Once, clearly, unmistakably, he saw a tree in blossom—white flowers, thousands of them, a tree wearing snow that was not snow—and he stopped walking and stared at it until the heat-shimmer took it apart piece by piece, petal by petal, until there was only salt again.

His chest did the thing. The chest-thing was new since the door, or maybe old since always and only now big enough to notice. It was like someone had reached into him and squeezed around whatever lived behind his ribs, gently, the way you'd squeeze a fruit at market to see if it was ripe. And underneath the squeezing was a knowing, and the knowing said:

*You have been here before. You were here before you were.*

Which was impossible. Gideon was six, or seven—the warm one had guessed seven, holding up fingers, and Gideon had held up eight, mostly to be interesting—but impossibility had never once slowed down a true thing. He had been here before he was. Something in his blood had walked these paths. Something older than his bones remembered water moving under stone, and bees doing their golden arithmetic among the rows, and a woman singing to seedlings in a language that was his language, the only one he had, the one nobody else could hear.

The garden.

He didn't know its name. Names were the warm one's business; the warm one collected names the way other men collected coins, hoarding them, hiding them, spending them carefully. But Gideon knew the garden the way you know a lullaby—not the words, but the shape of them in the dark. He knew there had been paths. He knew there had been a wall with a gate that opened for anyone who knocked honestly. He knew there had been a stone at the center, and that the stone had sung, and that everyone who heard it forgot they were hearing it, the way you forget your own heartbeat.

And he knew—it arrived in him suddenly, fully formed, terrible and certain as a dropped egg—that the stone did not sing anymore.

Gideon sat down right there in the middle of the salt road and began to cry.

It was not a small crying. It was the crying of someone mourning a country, except the country was inside him and had never once existed outside his own blood. Great silent sobs wracked him, because even his crying had no voice; tears ran down his face and dripped off his chin and hit the salt, and where each tear landed, the salt *hissed*, faintly, like a very small fire being put out.

The warm one was beside him in three strides. He knelt in the salt without caring what it did to his knees—he cared about his coat, Gideon had noticed, but never about his knees—and gathered Gideon in against the wool-and-smoke-and-bread smell of him.

"Hey," the warm one said, uselessly, wonderfully. "Hey. Hey now."

He couldn't ask what was wrong. That was the arrangement between them, the treaty they'd signed without paper: the warm one asked questions with his eyebrows, and Gideon answered with whatever his hands could find. So Gideon pointed—at the place where the blossom-tree had been, at the empty shimmering distance—and then pressed his fist against his own chest, twice, hard, the sign he'd invented for the chest-thing, the homesick-for-a-place-that-was-never-a-place feeling.

The warm one went very still.

Gideon felt the stillness move through him like weather. The arms around him tightened, then loosened, then tightened again, as if the warm one were trying to decide whether to hold on or let go and had compromised on both. When Gideon tipped his head back to look, the warm one's face had done something complicated—grief and fear and tenderness all trying to fit through the same doorway at once.

"You remember it," the warm one whispered. Not a question. A man reading his own sentence of doom off a wall.

Gideon nodded. Then, because honesty deserved decoration, he shook his head too. Both. Neither. He remembered it and he didn't; it was his and it wasn't; he had never seen a garden in his life and he grieved one daily, the way you grieve a grandmother who died before you were born, whose chair you still can't sit in without your throat going tight.

The warm one understood. Gideon watched him understand, watched it land on him like snow. And then the warm one did the thing that made Gideon decide, permanently and forever, that this person was his person:

He didn't say *it's all right*. He didn't say *don't cry* or *we'll get there* or any of the lies adults keep in their pockets like smooth stones. Instead he sat down fully in the salt, in the middle of the road, in the open where anything could see them, and he pulled Gideon into his lap and let him cry about a garden neither of them had ever seen, and he cried a little too, quietly, in the way of men who have practiced.

They stayed like that while the sun crawled. The wind went around them, puzzled, like a dog circling a strange object on its territory.

* * *

Afterward, the warm one produced bread.

This was his solution to most catastrophes, and Gideon approved of it completely. The bread was yesterday's bread, a little crushed, a little salt-dusted from the wrapping, and it was the finest thing Gideon had ever eaten, as yesterday's bread always was. They ate sitting side by side on the road, legs out straight, like two travelers in a story.

While they ate, Gideon studied the warm one sideways, the way you study a fire you're not supposed to touch.

The warm one was sad. This was not news; the warm one was sad the way rivers are wet. But out here on the salt, away from doors and neighbors and Wardens, the sadness had stopped hiding. It sat on him openly, like a second coat. And Gideon—who had no words but had never once lacked for understanding—could see that the warm one's sadness and Gideon's sadness were *related*. Cousins, maybe. Or the same sadness wearing different coats.

Gideon's sadness was for a garden he'd never seen. The warm one's sadness was for a garden he'd seen too well.

Gideon reached over and patted the warm one's knee. Twice. The way you pat a large anxious animal.

The warm one looked down at the pat, then at Gideon, and laughed—a short, startled, rusty laugh, like a gate opening after years shut. "What was that for?"

Gideon shrugged elaborately, palms up, the universal gesture of *the world is mysterious and I am at peace with it*, and the warm one laughed again, longer this time, and something in the air between them loosened and settled, the way flour settles when you tap the bowl.

They walked on. The afternoon aged. The salt plain began, very slowly, to remember it had once been something else—little islands of darker earth rose up, tufted with gray grass, and then a stunted tree, and then a fence post with no fence, standing at attention long after the war it served was forgotten.

And Gideon began to notice the other thing. The following thing.

At first he thought it was a bird, high up, a pale fleck riding the wind. But birds flew in curves, and this flew in lines. Birds flapped; this simply *proceeded*. And whenever Gideon glanced back—which he did often, because the chest-thing had taught him vigilance—the pale fleck was always exactly as far away as before. Never closer. Never gone.

He tugged the warm one's sleeve and pointed.

The warm one looked. Gideon felt him go rigid, the whole hearth of him banking down to coals. For a long moment the warm one stood motionless against the enormous sky, and Gideon watched him do sums behind his eyes—bad sums, frightened sums, the kind where every answer is worse than the question.

"Wardens don't fly," the warm one murmured, mostly to himself. Then, quieter, in a voice that had gone somewhere else entirely, somewhere ten years deep: "But she doesn't need to."

He took Gideon's hand. His grip was careful, but Gideon could feel the tremor underneath the care, a small earthquake looking for a place to happen.

"We'll make the ridge by dark," the warm one said. "There's a river past it. A ferry." He swallowed. "Someone I owe."

Gideon looked back one last time. The pale fleck hung in the darkening east, patient as frost forming on a window. It did not follow them, exactly. It accompanied them, the way winter accompanies autumn—never hurrying, absolutely certain of the appointment.

Gideon squeezed the warm one's hand and did not let go.

Behind them, far off across the salt, the wind found the shape of the pale fleck and carried a single thread of it forward, and the thread settled against Gideon's ear like a cold fingertip, and though it carried no words at all, Gideon understood it perfectly, the way he understood all true things:

*I knew you when you were green. I will know you when you are ash.*

Ahead, the ridge waited, black against the bruised sky, and beyond it, unseen but already humming in Gideon's blood, the river.
