## 8. The Ferryman's Debt

The river Marn announced itself long before we saw it — a gray ribbon of slush and grinding ice that had once, according to every map Casper Dunmore no longer owned, run green in summer.

Gideon heard it first. He stopped on the frozen road, tilted his head like a bird considering a worm, and made a sound I had come to think of as his *yes* — a small exhale through the nose, decisive as a judge's gavel.

"Yes," I agreed. "Water. Very clever. Now we only have to find someone mad enough to row across it."

The ferry landing was less a landing than a suggestion: three pilings wearing hats of ice, a rope strung sagging between posts, and a hut built of driftwood and stubbornness leaning against the bank. Smoke rose from its chimney in a thin, apologetic line. A sign hung over the door, the paint flaked to ghosts of letters, but I could still read what it had once said:

**MARN CROSSING — FAIR RATES — NO REFUNDS — NO DROWNING**

"No drowning," I murmured. "That's reassuringly specific."

Gideon tugged my sleeve and pointed at the sign, then at himself, eyebrows raised in a question he couldn't ask aloud. He'd learned this trick over the past week of travel — conducting entire conversations with his face while I supplied both halves. It was, I reflected, more honest than most dialogues I'd ever been part of.

"It means," I said, "that if you drown, they won't give your money back."

He considered this with the gravity of a scholar, then nodded once, satisfied. The boy had an unshakeable faith in the fairness of transactions. Where he'd gotten it, heaven knew. Not from me; I'd spent ten years selling luck-knots to people who needed them most precisely when they worked least.

I knocked on the hut door. Or rather, I knocked at the general region of the door, since the driftwood had settled into shapes that made *door* more of a philosophical concept than an architectural one.

"Come in or go away!" bellowed a voice from within, "but decide quick, the kettle's on!"

We went in. Everyone goes in when the kettle's on. It's the oldest law of the north.

* * *

The ferrywoman was old the way river stones are old — not frail, but worn smooth by something patient and immense. She sat by the stove with her boots off, thick stockings darned at both knees, and she was already pouring tea before we'd finished dripping on her floor. Three cups. She'd counted our footsteps, I realized. Or heard Gideon's breath. Old women who live alone on frozen rivers miss nothing.

"Sit," she said. "You look like something the wind chewed and spat out twice."

"That's accurate," I said. "Thank you for noticing."

She snorted. Up close her eyes were pale as shallow water, and they went first to Gideon — barefoot, wrapped in my second coat, watching the stove with the rapt attention other children gave to festivals — and then to me, and stayed there.

And stayed there.

I have spent ten years becoming unremarkable. I know the craft of it the way I once knew storm-craft: the stoop you put into your shoulders, the cough you cultivate, the way you let your hands shake slightly so people assume age or drink rather than anything worth a second glance. I had shown this woman nothing but a tired charm-seller and his foundling.

But her eyes narrowed, and the kettle ticked, and outside the ice on the Marn groaned like an old man getting out of bed, and she said:

"Dunmore."

Not a question. A name, set down on the table between us like a coin she'd been keeping under her tongue for years, waiting for the right buyer.

My tea went very still in my cup, which is a feat, because tea never sits still when your hands do.

"I'm sorry?" I said. "I think you have me confused—"

"Casper Dunmore." She said it slowly, tasting each syllable, and Gideon looked up sharply at the sound of it — the first time anyone in Vell had spoken my name aloud in a decade, and here it was, pronounced by a stranger in a driftwood hut, with all the tenderness of a knife being sharpened. "Storm-caller. Last voice of the covenant. The man who broke the world's heart and ran south before the snow could catch him."

"I really must insist—"

"Sit down, boy." She hadn't raised her voice. She didn't need to. There are voices that command rooms and voices that command rivers, and hers belonged to the second kind. "You've been insisting you're nobody for so long you've forgotten how to be somebody. Sit. Drink. I've waited ten years to say what I have to say, and I'll say it whether you're sitting or standing, but sitting's politer."

I sat. Gideon climbed onto the bench beside me and pressed against my arm, warm as a loaf, and watched the old woman with an expression I couldn't read — though lately I was learning that most of what I couldn't read in him wasn't mystery. It was memory. Blood-memory, garden-memory, older than either of us.

"You have the advantage of me," I said carefully.

"Marda." She refilled my cup without asking. "Marda Ostwick. Ferrywoman of the Marn, thirty-one years, same boat, same rope, same blessed cold." She sat back and folded her hands over her stomach. "And before that? Before the winter came down like a lid on a pot? I was a gardener's girl. Amsel. You'd know the place."

I did not move. I am quite proud of that. Somewhere inside me a door blew open and every cold thing I owned came rushing out of it, and outwardly I merely sipped tea that tasted, suddenly, of ash and honey.

"Amsel fell," I said. "Everyone knows."

"Everyone knows the *story*." Her eyes were flat water again. "Snow came, gardens died, realms forgot each other, terrible tragedy, moving on. But I was there, Dunmore. I was fourteen. My mother kept the herb-beds by the west wall. And I remember the last morning — the sky going white at noon, the roses freezing on the stem, whole and perfect, like glass flowers. And I remember the men who came down from the mountain with their hoods up, and how one of them stopped at the gate." Her voice didn't waver. That was somehow worse. "He looked at my mother. She was carrying seedlings. Trying to save them, stupid brave woman, running toward the greenhouse with her arms full of green things while the world turned white behind her. And he looked at her, and he *wept*, and he walked on anyway. Walked on and left us to the snow."

The stove ticked. Gideon's hand found mine under the table. Small fingers, scarred palm, the garden-sigil I'd traced a hundred times now without understanding it any better than the day I first saw it.

"And I remember," Marda continued, softer now, which was worst of all, "that the weeping man had a face I'd know anywhere, because grief carves deep and I'd seen that exact carving in my own mirror every day since. Ten years I've poled this river, and every traveler who crossed, I looked at their faces. Waiting."

"Marda—"

"Don't." She held up a hand, and it shook, just once, and then was steady. "Don't tell me I'm wrong. Don't tell me you're some cousin, some namesake, some poor fool born under an unlucky star. I'm seventy years old, I'm owed the truth, and I will have it before you set foot on my boat."

So I told her the truth. All of it, or as much as fits in a kitchen — the covenant, the stone, the choice I'd been given and the choice I'd made instead, the snow falling on green fields while I ran. I told it plainly, because she'd earned plainness, and because lying to her would have been like lying to the river itself: possible, briefly, and then fatal.

When I finished, she was quiet a long time. Gideon had fallen asleep against my arm, which children can do in the middle of anything, and perhaps that was mercy, or perhaps it was judgment. With him it was hard to say.

"So," Marda said at last. "You'll want crossing."

"If you'll take us."

"Oh, I'll take you." She stood, joints cracking like kindling, and began pulling a coat from a peg — oiled leather, river-stained, older than my exile. "That was never the question. The question is payment."

"I have—" I reached for my purse, that sad little pouch of Vell coppers and two silver bits I'd hoarded against exactly this kind of day.

"Keep it." She didn't even look. "Coin's for strangers. You're not a stranger, Dunmore. You're a *debt*." She turned, and her pale eyes had thawed into something worse than anger — something that hurt to look at, the way looking at the sun hurts, if the sun were sad. "The fall of Amsel took everything from everyone who loved it. My mother. My brothers, who stayed to fight the frost with firewood and died of it. My whole life, the one I was supposed to have among the beds and the bees. And you — you took the rest. You took the *ending*. Do you understand? Everyone else got to lose things. You're the only one who got to choose, and you chose wrong, and the rest of us have been paying your interest for ten years."

"I know."

"Do you?" She leaned on the table. "Do you actually? Because men like you always know, and knowing costs nothing, and I am *tired* of free knowing. So here's my rate. No coin. Coin's too easy — you'd pay it and walk away lighter, and that's not payment, that's shopping." She pointed one gnarled finger at my chest. "A memory. One memory, mine to keep, taken out of your head the way you took the summer out of mine. Fair rates. No refunds."

The stove ticked. The ice groaned. Gideon breathed softly against my sleeve.

"You can't—" I started, and stopped, because of course she could. River-folk kept old crafts the way churches keep relics, half-remembered and all the stronger for it. Memory-taking was hedge-witch work, border magic, the kind that survived winters because it asked so little of the world and so much of the payer. "That's not — Marda, memories aren't *money*—"

"No," she agreed. "They're better. They're the only thing a man can't earn back." She sat down again, and suddenly she looked tired, tired in the marrow, tired the way I felt. "I don't want your guilt, boy, I've got plenty. I want something *warm*. Something from before. You were at Amsel in its living days — you knew it when it was green. Give me one hour of that. One true hour. Let an old woman hold something of the garden that isn't grief."

And that — that was the cruelty of it, or the kindness, I still haven't decided. She wasn't asking me to suffer. She was asking me to share the only wealth I had left that was worth anything, and she was right that it was owed, and she was right that it was small, and it was the smallest enormous price anyone had ever named.

"My sister," I heard myself say.

Her eyebrows rose.

"Liora." The name came out of me like a splinter coming out of skin — relief and pain in the same motion. "My little sister. She was seven the last spring at Amsel. She used to — there was a plum tree by the east wall, older than the order itself, and she'd climb it before dawn and throw plums at the novices during morning rites. Hit the Abbot once. Squarely. He never found out who." I laughed, and it cracked in the middle. "She used to say the tree talked to her. Children say that. But at Amsel it might even have been true. She'd sit up in the branches with her legs swinging and come down with her pockets full and her face sticky and she'd tell me everything the tree said, and I believed her, because I was fifteen and in love with the whole world and why wouldn't trees talk to Liora? Trees would have been fools not to."

Marda had gone very still.

"She died in the first winter," I said. "The cold took the young ones first. Fastest. Almost gently, the healers said, as if that helps, as if *almost gently* is a comfort you can hold at a graveside dug through four feet of frozen ground." I looked at my hands. Storm-hands. Charm-seller's hands. Hands that had thrown plums back at her, once, badly, and been scolded for wasting food. "Take that. Take Liora and the plum tree and the morning rites and all of it. It's yours. It was always going to be spent on the road north anyway — you're just the first toll."

For a moment I thought she'd refuse. Her mouth opened; her eyes had gone bright and dangerous, the way water does right before it freezes.

Then she reached across the table and took my hand, and her grip was a young woman's grip, a gardener's grip, earth-strong.

"One hour," she whispered. "Just the tree. Just the plums. Leave me the rest."

* * *

What it feels like, having a memory taken, is this: it feels like a word on the tip of your tongue that never comes. You know the shape of the absence. You reach for it at odd moments — waking, walking, watching a child sleep — and find smooth wall where a door used to be. You know you loved someone. You cannot recall her face. You know the loss happened *here*, recently, by a river, and you know it was fair, and fairness has never once made a price hurt less.

Marda wept while she took it. I don't remember much after the tree — the taking blurs its edges, mercifully, the way a bruise blurs color. But I remember her crying, silently, holding my hands in both of hers like something rescued from a fire, and saying over and over, "Oh, you *fool*, oh, you brave stupid fool," and I honestly could not tell whether she meant me or herself or the whole doomed green world.

Then it was done, and dawn was graying the windows, and the Marn waited.

* * *

She poled us across herself. Wouldn't trust the boat to anyone else, she said, not with this cargo. The ice had a lead open along the channel — the river keeping its own stubborn thread of life through the winter, the way rivers do — and the boat slid through it slow and quiet, mist rising around us like breath.

Gideon woke halfway across and immediately conducted a thorough inspection of the boat, the rope, the pole, the river, and Marda, in that order. Then he climbed into her lap — no hesitation, no permission sought, the way he did everything — and studied her face from six inches away with those ancient, unhurried eyes.

Marda froze. "What's he doing?"

"Deciding about you," I said. "Fair warning. He's usually right."

The boy reached out and touched her cheekbone, where the tears had dried into salt-tracks, and made his small *yes* sound. Then he patted her twice, the way you pat a horse you approve of, and returned to my side, job complete.

Marta stared after him. "What in the name of all drowned saints was *that*?"

"He likes you," I translated. "It's high praise. He doesn't give it to everyone."

"Hm." She poled on, but I saw her touch her cheek where he'd touched it, and leave her hand there a while. "Strange child."

"The strangest," I agreed fondly.

The far bank came up gray and stony. Marda shipped the pole and held us steady against the current while we stepped off onto the shingle, and for a moment none of us moved, the three of us balanced between one shore and another like a held breath.

"Dunmore." Her voice was rougher now, and quieter. "The debt's paid. We're square. Whatever you're carrying north — I don't want to know it, and I won't pray for it, because I gave up praying when the roses froze. But." She looked away, out over the water. "There's a shrine up the valley road. Half a day. Old one, pre-covenant, the kind even Wardens mostly leave alone. If you're followed — and you will be, because men like you are always followed — the keeper there owes me a favor, and I'll send word ahead that she should owe you one instead."

"Marda—"

"It's not kindness." She cut me off flatly, already pushing off from the bank. "It's interest. You paid principal; this is interest. We're square after this, and don't you dare die before we're square, because I refuse to have gone to all this trouble for a corpse." The boat swung out into the mist. Her voice came back thinner, almost lost in the river's mutter: "The plum tree by the east wall. Was it really as big as they said?"

And I reached for the answer and found the smooth wall, the tip-of-the-tongue nothing, the door that was no longer a door — and understood, finally and completely, what I had paid, and how small it was, and how vast.

"It was the biggest thing in the world," I called back, and it was true, whatever else had been taken.

Something that might have been a laugh, or a sob, drifted back across the water. Then the mist closed, and the boat was gone, and it was just Gideon and me and the north road, climbing away from the river into country I had burned out of every map and was now walking straight into.

Gideon took my hand. He pointed up the valley — toward the shrine Marda had mentioned, or beyond it, toward the mountain that remembered — and made his small decisive sound.

"Yes," I said. "I know. Onward."

Behind us, faint under the river's noise, I could have sworn I heard sleigh bells. Or maybe it was only ice shifting, the way ice does.

Maybe.
