## 9. Sera Catches Up

The shrine at Hollowmere had been a waystation for pilgrims once, back when there were pilgrims and back when there was a road worth walking. Now it was a stone shell with a leaking roof and a bell that hadn't rung in anyone's living memory, and it was exactly the sort of place a fugitive with a child would stop when the snow turned mean.

Sera Vane knew this because she would have stopped there herself.

She crouched at the treeline with her hood down—hoods fogged her vision, and she needed every advantage she could get—and watched the shrine through the falling dusk. Snow came down in that particular heavy, indifferent way of the northern reaches, as if the sky had simply given up on the distinction between winter and everything else.

There. A thread of woodsmoke from the broken eastern wall. A horse tied under the lean-to that wasn't hers—the animal was too small, too shaggy, bought cheap in some border town by a man who didn't want to be remembered buying it.

Casper Dunmore.

Ten years she'd chased rumors of him, and three weeks since the reports out of Vell had finally put flesh to rumor: a charm-seller fled his shop, a child with him who spoke no tongue the Wardens' translators knew. Sera had read those reports four times. She had read them the way you read a letter from someone you buried—not because the words change, but because your hands need something to hold.

*Find the speaking child,* her orders said. *Assess. If it is what we believe, erase it.*

*Erase.* The Pale Wardens loved that word. It sounded so clean. Like chalk off a slate. Like nothing had ever been written there at all.

Sera flexed her fingers inside her gloves and began to move.

* * *

Getting into the shrine without being heard was almost insultingly easy, which told her two things: Casper was exhausted, and he was spending whatever magic he had left on concealment rather than wards. Ten years ago he'd have had three layers of storm-wards singing around any shelter he slept in, tuned so finely they'd taste the difference between a fox and a Warden's boots. Ten years ago he'd been insufferable about it, too. *Layer three is set to my heartbeat, Sera. If anything disturbs it faster than my heart, I'll know before you do.* He'd said it with that crooked half-smile he used when he was showing off and pretending not to.

She slipped through the eastern gap where the wall had fallen, past a rotted pilgrim's bench, and stopped in the shadow of the inner doorway.

The main chamber was small. An altar stone worn smooth by centuries of foreheads. A dead hearth—no, not dead; embers, banked low and careful. And beside the hearth, wrapped in a blanket that had seen better decades, a boy.

He was smaller than the reports suggested. Barefoot even now, even here, his feet tucked under him like a bird's. He sat perfectly still, watching the embers with an attention most children reserved for sweets or cruelty, and Sera felt the first cold finger of *it might be true* trace down her spine. Because children didn't sit like that. Children fidgeted. Children were noise wearing skin.

This one was listening to the fire.

And Casper—she found him last, because he'd positioned himself between the door and the boy, which meant he'd anticipated her, which meant somewhere under all that rust he was still Casper Dunmore—he lay on a bedroll near the altar, asleep or performing sleep. His face had gone gaunt in the way of people who rationed their own meals to feed someone else. There was flour dust still in the creases of his sleeve. Charm-seller's habit. Or baker's. He'd wanted to be a baker once, before the order took him. He'd told her that at a campfire very much like this one, twenty years ago, laughing, and she'd told him he'd have been terrible at it because he could never follow a recipe without improving it, and he'd thrown a pinecone at her.

Her chest did something complicated. She ignored it. She had a job.

She stepped into the light. "Don't reach for the wind, Casper. You'll wake the whole valley doing it badly."

He came awake the way soldiers do—all at once, no groggy middle—and then he saw her and went very still, the particular stillness of a man watching his whole life reorganize itself around a new catastrophe.

"Sera." Her name came out rough, like he'd been carrying it a long way. "You look well."

"I look frozen and underpaid."

"You always did undersell yourself." His eyes flicked—to her sword, to the door behind her, counting exits, counting her escorts. "Alone?"

"Company's camped at the crossroads. Two hours back. They think I'm scouting the shrine for tracks." She let that land. "Which I am. Technically."

"And if you find them?"

"That depends," Sera said, "entirely on you."

The boy moved. Not much—a shift of weight, a turning of his head—but both adults went quiet as if a third presence had entered the conversation, which, in fairness, it had. The child looked at Sera with eyes the color of new leaves, an impossible color, a color that had no business surviving north of the frost line, and tilted his head the way you'd listen to distant music.

"Don't," Casper said quietly. It wasn't clear whether he meant her or the boy.

Sera kept her voice level, her hands loose and visible. "Reports say he speaks. Just not anything anyone knows."

"He speaks plenty." Something fierce moved through Casper's tiredness. "Just not anything *you* know."

"Then translate."

"It doesn't work like that."

"Try me, Casper." She took one step forward, deliberately slow, and watched him weigh stopping her and decide against it. "I've spent ten years reading your old field reports looking for typos in the First Tongue glossaries. I've forgotten more of that language than most Wardens ever learn. So try me."

The boy stood up. The blanket slid from his shoulders. He crossed the floor toward her with that uncanny unhurried certainty, bare feet silent on stone, and stopped just outside arm's reach, and looked up at her.

Sera had interrogated smugglers, deserters, hedge-witches, one extremely uncooperative goat. She knew every species of human fear and defiance. What she didn't know, what no manual covered, was being studied by a child who looked at her as though she were a page in a book he was reading very carefully.

Then the boy opened his mouth and said, in the First Tongue, in a voice like a small bell struck once:

*"Aelira."*

The word went through Sera like an arrow through water—no resistance, straight to the center.

Her mother's name. Not *mother*, not the common endearment, but the actual name, *Aelira*, spoken in the old forms, the naming-forms, the way you said it when you were calling someone home across a distance. Her mother, who had died when Sera was nine, who had sung her scraps of the First Tongue as lullabies without knowing what they were, who had been a hedge-healer in a village that no longer existed, whose grave Sera had not visited in eleven years because the road was bad and the war was worse and there was never time, there was never, ever time—

"How," Sera said. Her voice came out wrong. Thin. "How does he know that name."

Casper had gone white. "Sera—"

"*How does he know it.* Nobody alive knows it. I don't say it. I haven't—" She stopped. Breathed. The shrine was very cold and very quiet and the boy was still looking up at her with those leaf-green eyes, patient as spring. "Nobody living knows my mother's name."

"Maybe," Casper said gently, "that's not quite true."

"What's that supposed to mean?"

"It means I've been trying not to think about what he is for three weeks, and you're making it very difficult." He sat up slowly, hands open, the old negotiator's posture. "He said something to me the second night I had him. I didn't understand it then. I do now. He names people, Sera. People who are... rooted. People the garden remembers. He said my name before I gave it to him. My *true* name, the rite-name, the one I burned the records of." He laughed, and there was no humor in it at all. "He looks at you and he says *Aelira*. Draw your own conclusions. I've drawn mine and I hate every one of them."

The boy reached out—slowly, telegraphing it the way you'd approach a frightened animal, and the absurdity of that, of *this child* approaching *her* like she was the dangerous one, nearly made Sera laugh out loud in a register closer to sobbing—and touched her hand. Just her knuckles. His fingers were warm. Unreasonably, impossibly warm, in a shrine with a dying fire, in a winter that had lasted past all reason.

Warmth spread up from the point of contact like ink dropped in water.

And for a moment—half a breath, no more—Sera smelled woodsmoke and bread and feverfew. Heard a voice humming in the First Tongue, off-key, patient. Felt small hands braiding her hair badly. Saw a kitchen with a blue door.

It was gone before she could grip it. The boy withdrew his hand and folded his own together, and waited, and the waiting was somehow worse than anything he could have said.

"You felt it," Casper said softly. Not a question.

"Shut up, Casper."

"You did."

"I said shut up." She turned away, because her eyes were doing things she refused to permit in front of him, of all people, and stared at the dead altar stone as if it owed her money. Her orders sat in her breast pocket, folded twice, weighing approximately nothing and several tons simultaneously. *If it is what we believe, erase it.*

What did they believe? That the child was a seed of the old covenant. That seeds grew. That what grew from the old covenant had ended the world once already—or so the official history ran, the history Sera had recited at her induction with her hand on the pale stone, believing every word.

But the official history did not include her mother's kitchen. The official history did not explain why a mute child would carry a dead woman's name across a hundred miles of winter and offer it, gift-wrapped, to the daughter. Erasure orders did not account for the smell of feverfew.

"Sera." Casper's voice, careful. "Whatever you're deciding—you should know the company at the crossroads won't be as slow as you. Captain Merek doesn't scout. He arrives."

"I know how Merek operates. I trained half his officers."

"Then you also know he'll burn the shrine with us in it if it saves him an hour."

She knew. That was the thing about the Pale Wardens nobody put in the recruiting ballads: efficiency was a virtue right up until it was yours being efficiently handled.

The boy tugged her sleeve. When she looked down, he pointed—at her, then at his own chest, then made a shape in the air with both hands. Growing. A seed, planted, rising. Then he pointed north, through the broken wall, toward the mountains barely visible in the dark, and his expression held no fear at all. Only the terrible patience of a thing that had been waiting longer than it had been alive.

"He wants you to come with us," Casper translated unnecessarily. "That's 'come' in the old forms. Or possibly 'grow.' The grammar's ambiguous. Everything about him is ambiguous. I've stopped asking."

"I'm not coming with you." It came out automatically, reflexively, the way you swat at a fly. "I have a company. I have orders. I have—"

"Aelira," the boy said again, softly, and Sera's argument died in her throat like a candle in wind.

* * *

They talked for an hour. It was less a negotiation than an archaeology—two people carefully excavating twenty years of shared history while pretending they weren't. Casper told her about Vell, about the charm-shop, about the night the boy appeared speaking the First Tongue on the year's first snow. Sera told him, in exchange, things she probably shouldn't have: the size of Merek's company, its route, its supply schedule, the fact that Merek had standing authorization to use fire without review north of the Marn.

"You're giving me your own army's marching orders," Casper said.

"I'm telling a story," Sera said. "If you happen to act on it, that's your business. I was just talking."

"You were always terrible at lying and worse at pretending otherwise."

"And you were always insufferable when you were right, which was constantly, according to you."

The boy, meanwhile, had fallen asleep against Casper's side mid-crisis, which struck Sera as either profound trust or profound indifference to consequence, and knowing what she was beginning to know about him, it was probably both. Casper's hand rested on the child's shoulder with a protectiveness so naked it embarrassed her to witness it. Ten years ago Casper Dunmore couldn't keep a houseplant alive. Now he was guarding the last green thing in either realm with his body, and the transformation fit him so naturally it was infuriating.

"You could come south instead," he said. "Turn in a false report. Buy us a week."

"A false report gets audited. Merek audits everything. He'd have my badge by spring, and then I'd be no use to anyone, including you." She shook her head. "One night. I can give you one night. I'll report the shrine empty, tracks heading west toward the coast pass. Merek will waste half a day on the west fork before the scouts contradict me. Half a day, plus tonight. After that you're on your own, and Casper—" she leaned forward, and let him see, for once, everything she usually kept behind the Warden's flat calm, "—you have to go faster than you've been going. No more shrines. No more villages. Straight north, hard marches, and pray the weather covers you, because I cannot cover you twice."

"Why?" he asked quietly. "Why any of this? Your career, your oath—"

"Don't." She stood, abruptly, needing to move. "Don't make me itemize it. I'll start crying and then I'll have to kill you out of professional embarrassment."

He smiled—that same crooked half-smile, older now, worn at the edges like a coin passed through too many hands. "For what it's worth, I'm sorry. For all of it. For Amsel. For leaving you to clean up."

"You're not sorry enough to have stayed and cleaned it yourself."

"No," he agreed simply. "I'm not. I was a coward about the wrong things and brave about the wronger ones. It's a specialty of mine."

Outside, the snow had thickened. Through the broken wall, the mountains were invisible, which meant they were also unwatchable, which was the closest thing to mercy the north offered. Somewhere two hours south, forty men were eating cold rations and trusting their scout.

Sera settled her hood, checked her sword out of pure habit, and paused at the wall's edge.

"The name he said," she said, without turning around. "My mother's. Don't tell me how he knew. I've decided I don't want the answer yet. Maybe by the time I want it, I'll have earned it."

"Sera—"

"One night, Casper. Spend it well."

She dropped from the wall into the snow and was gone into the dark before he could answer, which was how she preferred all her goodbyes: fast, in bad weather, with no witnesses.

* * *

The company's camp glowed orange through the trees when she came down off the ridge, and Sera Vane stood a moment in the shadows gathering herself, putting the Warden back on piece by piece like armor. Flat voice. Level eyes. The report, rehearsed: shrine empty, tracks westbound, recommend the coast fork.

Captain Merek met her at the fire, a broad gray man with a voice like a landslide. "Well?"

"Empty. Cold ashes, one set of boot prints, adult male, heading west with the child. Small stride beside it. They're moving slow."

Merek grunted, studying her face the way he studied maps, looking for errors. Sera held still and thought about inventory lists, which had the great advantage of containing nothing whatsoever.

"West," he said at last. "Toward the coast."

"Tracks don't lie, Captain."

"No," Merek agreed. "But scouts sometimes do." He said it mildly, almost idly, and turned back to the fire, and Sera felt the words settle over her like fresh snowfall—soft, silent, and burying.

She walked to her tent on legs that felt borrowed. Behind her, the camp began to strike its fires for a dawn march west, and far to the north, under the blind dark where the mountains waited, two small figures and one impossible child were moving as fast as the snow allowed.

One night. She'd bought them one night.

In her breast pocket, her orders crinkled faintly as she breathed, and for the first time in fifteen years of service, Sera Vane fell asleep praying to a god she'd stopped believing in that she would not see Casper Dunmore again—because the only alternative was seeing him at the head of a Warden column, and she was no longer entirely certain which side of the blade she'd be standing on when it fell.
