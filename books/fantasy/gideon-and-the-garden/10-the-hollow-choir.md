## 10. The Hollow Choir

The village of Hollowmere announced itself by smell before sight: woodsmoke and wet ash and something underneath both, faintly sweet, like apples left too long in a cellar. Casper smelled it from the crest of the hill and stopped walking so fast that Gideon bumped into his back.

"What is it?" he asked, out of habit, though the boy never answered in words. Gideon peered around his coat with the particular alertness he showed whenever Casper's body went tense — the child had learned to read him like weather.

"Nothing," Casper said, which was a lie, and Gideon gave him a look that said, quite clearly, *you are bad at that.*

They had been on the salt road nine days now. Nine days of frozen ruts and dead hedgerows and villages that were either shuttered or empty or worse. The winter here was not the clean cold of the high country they'd left behind; it was a low, gray, patient cold, the kind that got into joints and into hope. Casper had seen three villages since the river Marn, and each one had been quieter than the last. He'd begun to develop a theory about it, and the theory was ugly: the long winter wasn't falling evenly. It fell hardest where people still remembered warmth.

He didn't say this to Gideon. Instead he said, "Smoke means someone's alive," and adjusted the boy's scarf — a proper wool one, bought with the last of the ferrywoman's coin at a crossroads stall — and they went down into Hollowmere together.

* * *

The village was half-swallowed. Snow stood in drifts against the doors of houses whose windows had gone dark, and the roofs sagged under white weight like tired shoulders. But down the single crooked street, past a well with a wooden cover lashed shut against the frost, there was light. Not lamplight. Not firelight.

Candlelight. Dozens of candles, arranged in rows.

And singing.

Casper knew the song before he understood that he knew it. That was the thing about covenant-hymns — they lived somewhere below memory, in the marrow, where you couldn't dig them out even if you wanted to. His mother had sung them over the washbasin. His teachers at Amsel had drilled them into novices until the words wore grooves in their tongues. He hadn't heard one in ten years, and here it was, drifting out of a low stone meetinghouse with its door propped open against all sense, spilling warmth onto the snow.

The voices were thin. Ragged. Out of tune in the way that only exhausted people can be out of tune — not from lack of skill but from lack of anything left to sing *with*. And the words, when Casper caught them, made the hair on his arms stand up under his coat.

*"Green beneath the white, root beneath the stone—"*

They were singing it wrong. Not wrong musically — wrong *entirely*. They had kept the melody and lost everything else, the way a river keeps its course after the water's gone. The words had been worn smooth into nonsense syllables, passed mouth to mouth for generations until nobody knew they'd once meant something. Casper heard an old woman's voice lift above the rest: *"harrow, harrow, harrow the loam"* — and felt his chest tighten, because the original word was *harvest*, and the harvest it named was not wheat.

"Gideon." He crouched, took the boy by the shoulders. "Listen to me. We're going to walk past that building without stopping. We're going to find the inn, or a barn, or—"

Gideon was already looking past him at the meetinghouse door with an expression Casper had learned to fear. It was the expression he'd worn at the ferry, at the shrine, at every threshold between here and Vell: the look of someone recognizing home in places home had no business being.

"No," Casper said. "Gideon. *No.*"

The boy slipped out from under his hands with the boneless ease of children and cats, and walked straight through the meetinghouse door into the candlelight, barefoot as ever, because socks remained a concept he regarded as optional.

Casper stood in the snow for three full seconds, weighing his options, finding all of them terrible, and followed.

* * *

Inside, the meetinghouse held perhaps forty people, and every one of them turned to stare. Farmers, mostly, and a smith with burn-scarred forearms, and a cluster of children wrapped in blankets like small parcels. Their faces had the gray cast of a hard season survived rather than ended. At the front, leading the hymn with a cracked voice and a hand raised like she was hailing a coach that would never come, stood a round old woman in a shawl the color of dried onions.

The singing died mid-verse. Forty pairs of eyes fixed on the strangers: a tall man in a charm-seller's coat with frost in his beard, and behind him a small barefoot boy who looked at the candles with an expression of polite interest, as if auditing them.

"We don't buy," the old woman said flatly. "We don't sell either, if that's your next question. We sing."

"I heard," Casper said carefully. "That's... actually why I'm here."

That was also a lie, but a gentler one, and he was getting good at telling the difference.

"You heard us from the road?" Her eyes narrowed. "Nobody hears us from the road. Marta says we sound like geese arguing."

"Marta is generous," said a man near the back, and there was a ripple of tired laughter, the kind that costs more than it earns.

"The words," Casper pressed. "Where did you learn them?"

The old woman shrugged, unbothered. "From my mother. She from hers. Nobody knows what they mean anymore. Old harvest song, we call it. We sing it when the well freezes, on the chance somebody's listening." She paused, and something flickered across her face — the quick shadow of a joke that isn't entirely a joke. "Nobody ever listens."

Gideon tugged Casper's sleeve. Then, when Casper didn't immediately respond, tugged harder, with the focused determination of a creature who had learned exactly how much force a grown man's balance could tolerate.

"What?" Casper whispered.

Gideon pointed at the well-cover visible through the window. Then at himself. Then he made a gesture Casper had come to know intimately: both hands cupped, opening slowly, like something unfolding.

"No," Casper said again, but his heart wasn't in it, and Gideon could always tell.

* * *

What happened next happened quickly, the way most true things do.

The old woman — her name was Bess, he learned later, and she had buried a husband and two sons to the winter and refused to bury the village too — decided that any stranger who asked about the hymn deserved soup, and soup led to bread, and bread led to forty hollow-eyed villagers watching the two travelers eat in their candlelit meetinghouse while the wind worried at the shutters. Casper told them he was a peddler heading north. It was mostly true. He told them the roads were bad. Also mostly true. He did not tell them about Wardens, or gardens, or the specific reason the well had frozen, and he felt each omission like a stone in his boot.

Gideon, meanwhile, had located the children.

This was another thing Casper had learned: the boy collected people the way other children collected stones. He sat down among the blanket-wrapped parcels and within minutes had established a complete social order through gestures alone — a girl of six named Poppy was apparently in charge, a solemn boy called Tam was her deputy, and everyone else had been assigned roles Casper couldn't follow but they clearly could. Poppy showed Gideon a doll carved from a turnip. Gideon examined it with the gravity of a magistrate, then produced from his pocket — Casper closed his eyes briefly — a luck-knot. One of Casper's own, stolen from the satchel, presumably, sometime in the last week. He presented it to Poppy with both hands.

Poppy looked at the knot. Looked at Gideon. Looked at her mother, who nodded with the weary permission of a woman who had stopped fighting small battles.

"It's for luck," Poppy announced to the room, holding it aloft. "It's got a storm in it. I can feel it humming."

"That's not—" Casper started, and stopped, because the knots *did* hum, faintly, if you knew how to listen, and this child apparently knew how to listen, and there was nothing useful he could say about any of that in front of forty strangers.

Bess settled into the seat beside him with the creak of old wood. "Your boy's quiet."

"He doesn't speak."

"Doesn't, or can't?"

Casper turned his spoon in the soup. "I honestly don't know."

"Hm." She watched Gideon across the room with an expression Casper couldn't read. "My grandmother used to say the quiet ones hear the oldest things. She said it about the deaf miller, mind, and the deaf miller was a cheat and a lecher, so I wouldn't put much stock in it." She snorted. "But she also taught me the harvest song, and she swore it wasn't nonsense once. Swore the words used to *do* something."

"They did," Casper said, before he could stop himself.

Bess went very still. Outside, the wind found a gap in the shutters and moaned through it, low and long, almost harmonizing.

"Do you know them?" she asked quietly. "The real ones?"

"I knew them once." He stared into his soup. "A long time ago. In a place that doesn't exist anymore."

And then, because the universe has a sense of humor and it is not a kind one, Gideon stood up.

* * *

Later — much later, years later, in the life that came after — Casper would try to describe what it sounded like, and he would fail every time. He would say it was like hearing a door open in a house you thought was empty. He would say it was like the first note after a long silence, the kind of silence that has weight. None of it would be right.

Gideon walked to the front of the meetinghouse, past the candles, past Bess's abandoned hymnal — a hand-copied thing, its pages soft as cloth — and began to sing.

In First Tongue.

The words came out of the small body clear and unhurried, and they were the *right* words, the true ones, the ones Casper had burned out of every record he could reach and could not burn out of himself: *green beneath the white, root beneath the stone, the garden sleeps but does not die, and neither do we alone.* The melody was the same one the villagers had worn smooth. The meaning poured back into it like water into a dry channel.

The room stopped breathing.

Forty people listened to a barefoot child sing a song older than their village, older than their kingdom, older than the winter itself, and Casper watched understanding move through them like heat through frost — not comprehension, not yet, but recognition. The deep kind. The kind that lives below knowing.

Then the well thawed.

Casper felt it before anyone heard it — a tremor under the floorboards, a shift in the air, the taste of green things where there should have been only smoke and snow. Someone ran to the window. Someone else ran to the door. And from the well in the square came a sound that made the whole village pour out into the night: a great cracking groan, like ice giving way on a spring river, and then water. Water rising. Water running over the frozen lip of the well and steaming faintly in the cold, and above the well, impossibly, absurdly, the snow on the surrounding rooftops beginning to slump and slide, loosening, as if the houses themselves had relaxed.

The villagers stood in the square in their blankets and their grief and their disbelief, and Poppy's mother began to cry without making any sound about it, and old Bess gripped Casper's arm hard enough to bruise and said, in a voice scraped raw:

"What *is* he?"

Casper looked at Gideon, standing in the meetinghouse doorway with candlelight behind him and steam curling past his ankles, looking pleased and slightly surprised, as if he'd expected the well to thaw and was only gratified to have the timing confirmed.

"He's a seed," Casper said. It was the first time he'd said it aloud to anyone, and saying it made it heavier. "He's — a seed. Of something that's coming back."

Bess stared at him. Then at the well. Then, with the practical ferocity that had evidently kept her village alive, she rounded on the crowd and started issuing orders — buckets, firewood, the widow Ansel's mule, *move*, all of you, unless you'd rather the water freeze again while you gawp — and just like that, Hollowmere had work to do, and work, Casper thought, watching them scatter with something that was almost energy, almost purpose, might be the oldest hymn of all.

* * *

They stayed three days. Casper told himself it was for the roads — the pass north was drifted shut, Bess said, wouldn't clear for days — and the excuse was even true, which he appreciated, because he was running low on truths.

Those three days changed the village, and he let them, and hated himself a little for letting them, and did it anyway.

Because what could he do? Warn them? Tell the woman whose sons had died that the miracle in her meetinghouse would bring soldiers with pale cloaks and orders written in a language of erasure? Take Gideon's hand and walk away and leave them singing to a frozen well?

Instead he fixed things. It was the only penance he knew how to perform. He mended the meetinghouse shutters with a charm stitched into the hinges — small magic, deniable magic, the kind he'd spent ten years perfecting. He re-laced the luck-knots in the satchel and sold them at honest prices and gave three away free and pretended not to notice Bess noticing. He taught Poppy's mother how to keep the well-water moving so it wouldn't refreeze, using methods that were ninety percent common sense and ten percent covenant craft, and if she wondered about the ten percent, she had the grace not to ask.

And Gideon — Gideon sang. Every evening, in the meetinghouse, with the whole village gathered, and every evening the candles burned a little brighter and the frost retreated a little further from the window-glass and someone new arrived at the edge of the square, drawn by rumor, asking what they'd heard was true.

That was the part Casper couldn't fix. That was the part that sat in his stomach like swallowed ice.

On the second night, a drover passing through with a cart of frozen turnips heard the singing from the road and stayed to listen, and wept, and left at dawn. On the third morning, a tinker came through asking pointed questions with a pointed face, and bought a luck-knot, and looked at Gideon a moment too long before riding west — toward Vell, Casper noted, toward the salt-gates, toward the quarter where the Wardens kept their office.

"He'll talk," Sera's voice said in his head, in the tone she'd used for correcting his form, twenty years ago, in a garden that no longer existed. *You've made yourself memorable, boy. Memorable is a slow way of dying.*

"I know," Casper murmured to the empty air, and Bess, passing with an armload of kindling, said, "Talking to yourself? That's the third sign of a hard winter," and he laughed, and the laugh surprised him, because he'd forgotten it was available.

* * *

On the fourth morning the pass had cleared, and there was no honest excuse left, and Casper packed the satchel while Gideon conducted an elaborate farewell ceremony with the children involving the turnip doll, the luck-knot, and what appeared to be a binding oath sworn on a frozen puddle.

Bess walked them to the edge of the village, where the road climbed north through the bare trees. She pressed a parcel into Casper's hands — bread, cheese, a jar of something amber and unidentifiable — and then gripped his arm again, the same bruising grip.

"You're running from something," she said. No question in it.

"Yes."

"And it's big, and it's cold, and it's following you."

"Yes."

She nodded slowly, looking back at her village — at the well with its open water steaming in the morning cold, at the smoke rising straight and confident from chimneys that had slumped for months. "Then take this with you. Whatever you're carrying that boy toward — whatever's coming for him—" her jaw set, "—it should know that it froze my well for three winters and a child unfroze it in one song. Let it chew on that."

Casper found, to his considerable embarrassment, that his throat had gone tight. "I'll tell it," he managed.

"You do." She patted his arm once, briskly, and turned back toward her village, and called over her shoulder, without turning around: "And teach him some socks, for pity's sake! Barefoot in *this*!"

They climbed the north road through the bare trees, and Hollowmere shrank behind them, and Casper did not look back because he was afraid of what looking back would cost him.

Gideon did look back. He looked back for a long time, and then he took Casper's hand — which he almost never did, the boy's affection expressed in shoulder-bumps and sleeve-tugs and the occasional catastrophic hug — and held it the whole first mile.

Near noon, at the top of the ridge, Casper glanced back one final time, unable to help himself, and saw the village small and smoking in the valley, alive, defiant.

And on the road below, climbing the same ridge from the south, at least a mile distant but closing, he saw riders. Six of them. Pale cloaks, moving at a steady canter, and even at that distance he knew the formation — he'd seen it once before, ten years ago, from the far side of a burning gate.

Not searching. *Following.*

Sera had misread the map, or delayed herself, or argued with her orders — he had to believe that, he needed to believe that — but whoever these riders were, they had found Hollowmere. They would find the thawed well. They would find the tinker's story and the drover's tears and forty witnesses to a barefoot child who sang the winter open.

Casper squeezed Gideon's hand and turned north and started to run.

"Don't look back," he said, in First Tongue, the words rough and rusty in his mouth. "*Don't look back.*"

Gideon looked back anyway. And then he looked up at Casper, and smiled — a small, calm, infuriating smile, the smile of someone who knows exactly how the song ends — and ran beside him into the white.
