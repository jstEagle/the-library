## 11. Warden's Net

The company arrived at dawn on the ninth day, and they arrived the way everything arrived in the Pale Wardens: too much, too loud, and entirely convinced of its own necessity.

Sera stood on the frozen steps of the waystation at Coldharbour Row and counted banners. Six. Six banners meant a full company—two hundred soldiers, forty wardens of the second circle, a quartermaster's train, and, because the Wardens had never met a problem they couldn't make heavier, a chaplain.

"Captain Vane." The man himself came up the road at the head of it all, riding a white horse that looked embarrassed to be there. Captain Aldous Rennick was broad as a barn door and about as subtle, wrapped in a grey fur cloak that made him look like a hill that had decided to take up walking. He dismounted before his horse fully stopped, which told her he'd been eager, and eagerness in a Warden captain was never good news for anyone.

"Captain Rennick." She saluted. "I wasn't informed of your arrival."

"No one is informed of my arrival. That's rather the point of me." He grinned. He had the kind of grin that got men to follow him into burning buildings and then blamed them cheerfully for the burns. "The Circle grew impatient. A speaking child, a traitor storm-caller, and my best tracker sending back reports that say, let me see—" he produced a paper from his cloak with theatrical care, "'—no sign of them. Again.' Three times now, Vane. Three reports of nothing."

"The north is large, Captain. Nothing takes time to find."

"Mm." He folded the paper away. His eyes stayed on her a moment longer than comfort allowed. "Well. Now you have two hundred somebodies to help you not-find them faster."

* * *

They gave her a tent of her own, which she noted was pitched within sight of three others, and a map table, which she noted had been set up directly beside the chaplain's fire. Neither of these things was an accident. Sera had spent twenty years learning how the Wardens watched their own, and this arrangement practically sang it: *we trust you, and also we are watching you, and also we trust you.*

Rennick spread the maps across the table that evening and asked her to walk him through the pursuit.

She had known this moment would come. She had rehearsed it in the dark hours, lying awake in waystations, turning the problem over like a stone with a sharp edge. Here it was now, arriving exactly as expected, and it was no lighter for the rehearsal.

"They went north from Vell," she said, setting a marker on the map. Her voice came out steady. She was almost proud of it. "Through the salt-gates. They'll have taken the ferry at the Marn—the bridges are all collapsed this far downriver."

"You're certain?"

"I found footprints at the gate. Small ones. And a charm-seller's mark scratched on the toll post—a luck-knot, badly drawn. He was always bad at drawing them." She heard herself say *he was always*, and something behind her ribs turned over quietly, like a sleeper changing position. Ten years since Casper Dunmore had been anything but a name on a warrant, and here she was talking about his handwriting.

"And after the Marn?" Rennick leaned over the map, his shadow falling across half the northern territories.

"After the Marn the country opens up. Moorland, then the fells." She moved the marker along the western road, the low road, the sensible road any fugitive would take toward the coast and a ship and a new name. "If I were him, I'd hug the fells. Cover is better. Villages are sparse—he can steal what he needs without being seen."

Rennick studied the map. Then he did the thing she had dreaded most, which was nothing. He simply stood there, big hands flat on the table, looking at the route she'd drawn with the expression of a man reading a letter twice.

"That's the long way," he said finally.

"It's the safe way. For them."

"For us, though?" He straightened. "Vane. You tracked the Kesswick smugglers through a whiteout with half a frostbitten ear. You found the ash-witch in a city of four hundred thousand by following the smell of her candles. If anyone in this company knows how Dunmore thinks, it's you. So tell me honestly—" and here his voice dropped, went gentle, which was worse than any suspicion, "—if you were him, where would you go?"

And there it was. The question under the question. *Prove your loyalty, Captain Vane. Show us whose side your heart walks on.*

She thought about lying outright. She considered it seriously, the way one considers jumping a wide river—one clean motion and done. But Rennick was not stupid, whatever his grin suggested, and a lie that snapped under the first tug would drag them both down. No. What she needed was a lie that could bend. A lie with give in it. A lie that looked like truth from every angle except the one that mattered.

So she gave him a piece of herself instead.

"He'd go east," she said. "Up the shepherd tracks toward the pass at Grey Crag. It's brutal country, but Dunmore was raised in it, and he knows the Wardens think like Wardens—we follow roads, we follow supply lines. Nobody follows the shepherd tracks in winter." She tapped the eastern passes, the high ones, the ones that even in a good year ate travelers. "He'll be making for old Amsel. It's the only place left that means anything to him."

That much was true. That was the trick of it—she fed Rennick the true destination and salted the route around it until the whole thing tasted wrong. The eastern passes were a week of climbing. The real road—the one Gideon had drawn in flour, the one Casper must surely be walking—ran gentler through the valley of the Hollow Choir, past villages that still remembered the old hymns. If she led the company east, they would arrive at Amsel late, exhausted, and angry, having climbed a mountain for nothing while their quarry walked a garden path.

It was betrayal performed as protection. She wondered if there was a word for that. She suspected the word was just *betrayal*, and that the protection part was something she told herself at night so she could sleep.

"Grey Crag," Rennick repeated. He looked at the map a long moment. Then he nodded. "All right. We move at first light. Chaplain Ossel will ride with you—I want a second circle warden on the tracker, standard practice when we're this deep."

"Of course," said Sera, who had expected nothing less, and whose stomach dropped anyway.

* * *

Chaplain Ossel turned out to be a small, dry woman with spectacles frosted permanently at the edges and the conversational warmth of a ledger book. She rode beside Sera for two days up the eastern road and spoke perhaps forty words, thirty of which were scripture.

"You're taking us the wrong way."

Sera kept her hands very still on the fire-tongs. "Captain?"

"Not you. Forgive me—habit. I mean no rank by it." Ossel set the spectacles back on her nose and peered at her through them. "The shepherd tracks above us haven't seen a sheep since the winter began. There's no wood worth burning up there, no water that isn't ice, and no reason whatsoever for a man carrying a child to climb into it. And yet you recommended it with great confidence, and you have checked our position against the map eleven times today, which is ten more than necessary for someone who wrote the route herself."

The fire popped. Somewhere behind them, soldiers laughed at something—the ordinary music of an army at rest, absurdly domestic.

"I check because it matters," Sera said.

"Yes," Ossel agreed mildly. "People usually do, when they're lying."

Sera set down the tongs. There were ways to play this—indignation, confusion, the slow offended dignity of a loyal officer—but she was tired down to the marrow, tired in a way that had nothing to do with the road, and some small reckless part of her wanted, just once, to stop performing.

"What happens now?" she asked. "You report me? Another warden takes the lead and we all march back down the way I should have sent us?"

Ossel considered her for a moment over the fire. Then, unexpectedly, she smiled—a small, creased thing, like paper folded too many times. "Now I tell you a story," she said. "When I was a girl, my mother kept a garden. Not a magic one"—she said it quickly, the reflexive disclaimer of everyone in the Pale cities—"just beans and onions and a plum tree that never fruited. When the Wardens came through our district doing the census of gifts, she hid my brother's marks with ash for three years. Three years. She was not a brave woman about anything else. Spiders, thunder, the price of fish. But she lied to the Pale Wardens every season of her life, and died in her bed, and was buried with full rites, and nobody ever knew."

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because you've been checking that map eleven times a day hoping someone will notice, and I'm telling you someone has." Ossel warmed her hands. "Your route is wrong, Captain. Gloriously, deliberately wrong. And I have decided—not reported, *decided*—that I misread the elevation markings. Very embarrassing. Happens to the best of us. We'll have to go round by the valley after all; the passes are clearly impassable. I'll inform Captain Rennick at dawn."

Sera stared at her. "Why?"

"Because my mother lied well and was never thanked," said Ossel, "and because I am sixty-one years old and I have burned enough gardens." She picked up her bedroll. "Get some sleep, Captain. Tomorrow we get lost in the correct direction."

* * *

They got lost in the correct direction for four more days.

It was, Sera reflected, remarkable how slowly a company could move when every decision passed through two officers who each had private reasons for slowness. Rennick chafed but obeyed the terrain; Ossel produced scripture about the virtue of thoroughness; and Sera walked at the front of the column and felt the strangest sensation of her life, which was guilt and relief braided so tightly together she could no longer tell which strand was which.

Each night she took out her personal map—the good one, the detailed one—and marked where they truly were, and calculated where Casper must be, and how far ahead. Four days. Five. The gap held. Sometimes it widened.

She thought about the shrine, and the boy's voice saying *Maren* in that language older than kingdoms, and how she had stood in the snow afterward for a long time without moving, letting flakes gather on her shoulders like a second, softer uniform. She had let them go one night ahead. Then she had written a report saying she'd found nothing. Then the Circle had grown impatient, and now there were two hundred boots on the ground and a chaplain who lied like her mother, and still the net was closing, because nets did not need to know what they caught.

On the sixth day, a rider came up the valley trail from the south at a gallop, horse lathered, and handed Rennick a dispatch tube with the Circle's black seal.

Rennick read it standing in the middle of the road. Sera watched his face change—the grin draining out of it, leaving something older and harder underneath.

"Vane." He didn't shout. Somehow that was worse. "A village called Hollow Choir. On the valley road." He held the dispatch out to her. "Three days ago the frozen well thawed. Whole village singing songs they shouldn't know. The Circle calls it a manifestation." His jaw worked. "They've ordered the village burned. Containment protocol. And they've ordered us there at once—by forced march, direct route, no detours."

The words landed one at a time, each with its own small weight. *Burned. Containment. At once.* The valley road. The direct route. Through the place where Casper and the boy had been, where the miracle had happened, where the people who sang would be standing in their doorways when the company came.

"Captain Rennick," said Chaplain Ossel, from behind her, in a voice like dry paper catching flame, "I believe I may have misread the elevation markings again."

But Rennick was already turning to his sergeants, already shouting the orders, and the company was already moving, and Sera stood in the road with the dispatch in her hand and understood, with terrible clarity, that the net had closed after all—not around Casper, but around her, and around every soft thing she had tried to hide inside her duty.

There was one night left. Perhaps less. One night between here and Hollow Choir, one night in which a woman could be a Warden or could be something else, and could not be both.

She looked east, toward the high passes she had falsely chosen, and then west, toward the smoke that hadn't risen yet but would.

"Well," she said quietly, to no one, to the snow, to a dead mother who had lied well and was never thanked. "I suppose it's time to stop pretending."

She began, very carefully, to plan her treachery.
