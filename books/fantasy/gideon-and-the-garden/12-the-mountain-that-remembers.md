## 12. The Mountain That Remembers

The mountain did not want them there. Casper had known this the way you know a host's mood from the way they hold their cup, and he had said as much to Gideon that morning, pointing up at the grey shoulder of rock rising out of the cloud like something surfacing from deep water.

"It'll test us," he'd said. "Mountains always do. It's not malice. It's just thorough."

Gideon had looked at him with that expression the boy wore when he thought Casper was being deliberately foolish — eyebrows raised, mouth pressed flat, the whole face saying *obviously* in a language older than any kingdom. Then he had tightened the rope around his own middle, taken Casper's hand, and started climbing.

That was three hours ago. Now the wind had found its voice.

It came off the ice fields above them in long, low moans, threading through the broken spires of stone, and it carried — Casper was almost certain it carried — voices. Not words. Not yet. Just the *shape* of words, the way a room holds the shape of a conversation after everyone has left. He stopped on the ledge and listened, and his hands, numb inside two pairs of gloves, began to shake anyway.

"Casper." Gideon tugged his sleeve. The boy couldn't say the name — his mouth wouldn't make the shapes of ordinary speech — but he had invented a sound for it weeks ago on the salt road: a short exhale through the nose, almost a laugh, almost a sigh. *Casper* meant *the warm one*, meant *the one who makes bread*, meant *look at me*. He was looking at him now with the particular impatience of a child who has noticed an adult standing still too long in cold weather.

"I'm coming," Casper said. "I'm coming. Don't look at me like that."

Gideon looked at him exactly like that, then turned and climbed on, small boots finding holds Casper couldn't see until Gideon's weight showed him where they were. The boy climbed the way he did everything — without doubt, without apology, as though the mountain were simply a garden path grown steep. Sometimes Casper wondered if the child felt the cold at all. Other times, watching him hunch against the wind with his fists jammed in his armpits, he suspected Gideon felt everything and simply refused to file complaints about it.

They climbed through the morning. The ledge narrowed. Below them the world fell away into white — the salt road a thread, the frozen river Marn a scar of silver, the little villages they had passed reduced to smudges of smoke. Somewhere behind all that, Vell. Somewhere behind *that*, ten years of small rooms and careful lies and luck-knots sold to people who never once guessed what the knots were actually holding back.

Casper's boot slipped.

It was nothing — a half-inch of scree giving way, caught instantly by the rope, by his own hand slamming flat against stone. But the jolt went up his arm and into his chest, and something in his chest *opened*, the way a door opens when the latch has rusted through, and the memory came through it like weather.

*He is twenty-four years old and the covenant stone is warm under his palms.*

*It should not be warm. Stone is never warm. But the heart of Amsel has been beating for nine hundred years and every initiate learns its rhythm before they learn to read: slow, vast, patient as seasons. His mentor taught him that. Sera's mother taught them both, before —*

*Before.*

*"You don't have to be the one," Sera says beside him. She is young still, her braid coming loose, her eyes bright with fear she thinks she's hiding. "There are others. There are older ones. Casper, the council chose you because you're strong, not because it's right—"*

*"It doesn't matter why they chose me." His own voice sounds calm. That's the horror of it, later — how calm. "It matters that the realms are dying and someone has to pay, and they've decided it should be whoever can least refuse."*

*Below the stone, in the garden's green light, the sacrifice waits. A child. Six years old. Born under the right star, grown in the right soil, marked with the sigil on the palm — the same sigil, Casper realizes with a sickness that will last a decade, that now scars Gideon's hand. The council calls the child a vessel. They call the dying winter a debt. They use words like harvest and offering and renewal, and every word is a door slammed over the fact that they are going to put a six-year-old into the ground so that both realms keep breathing.*

*"Then break it," Sera whispers. "Break the stone and end it all." She means it as despair. She means: we've lost, run away with me, let the world take its chances.*

*He hears it as instruction.*

*His hands are already moving. The stone is warm and alive and it knows him — it has known him since he was a boy running through these paths — and it does not resist. That is the worst part. The covenant heart could have fought him. Nine hundred years of power, and it simply opens, trusting him, the way a door opens for family.*

*He breaks it.*

*And the first snow falls on green fields.*

*Casper stands in the garden with his hands bleeding and watches summer die in a single afternoon. Frost walks across the orchard like a visiting dignitary, touching each tree in turn, and each tree bows and goes grey. The grass silvers. The pond skins over with a sound like a struck bell. And everywhere, everywhere, the small green lights of the covenant — the living threads that ran from Amsel into both realms, binding season to season — go out one by one, like windows going dark in a house where everyone has fallen asleep.*

*Sera screams his name. He does not turn around. If he turns around he will have to see her face, and if he sees her face he will stop, and if he stops the child dies anyway because the council will simply find another stone-breaker, another vessel, another door. No. Better the winter. Better the honest cold than the beautiful lie.*

*Better — he told himself then, and tells himself now, and will tell himself on his deathbed — better the sin I chose than the sin I was ordered to commit.*

"**Casper.**"

The nose-exhale, sharp this time, twice. Hands on his face, small and shockingly warm. Gideon stood on the ledge below him, gripping his collar, and Casper realized he had been standing motionless for — how long? The sun had moved. The wind had changed key. His eyes were wet and freezing in the wind.

"I'm here," Casper said stupidly. "I'm fine."

Gideon did not accept this testimony. He took Casper's hand — the right one, the one that had done it, the one that still sometimes ached in deep winter as though the stone remembered being broken by it — and pressed it flat against the mountainside. Held it there. Looked up at him with those grave, unchildlike eyes and made a small sound: a question.

*I know,* Casper wanted to say. *I know whose mountain this is. I know it remembers my hands.*

But the boy's palm against the rock, and Casper's palm beneath it, and between them the old sigil scarred into Gideon's skin — something passed through that contact that wasn't words. Warmth, maybe. Or recognition. The mountain, which had been moaning all morning, went quiet for the space of three heartbeats, as though listening back.

Then the wind resumed, gentler, and they climbed.

* * *

They ate at midday in the lee of a shattered archway — all that remained, Casper understood with a lurch, of the outer gate of Amsel itself. Pilgrims had built it, once, before the fall. Carved into the fallen stones were vines and birds and small fat children holding fruit, and the frost had gotten into every groove and turned the carvings to lacework. Gideon traced a carved bird with one mitten, then looked up at Casper and made a sound Casper hadn't heard from him before: low, mournful, questioning. A grief with no object.

"You remember it," Casper said quietly. "Don't you. Not with your mind. Somewhere deeper."

Gideon touched his own chest, then the stone, then his chest again.

"Yeah," Casper said. "Me too."

He should have saved his breath for climbing. Instead, because the altitude had loosened something in him, or because the boy deserved it, he heard himself talking — really talking — for the first time since the hollow choir village.

"When I was young — younger than you, even — I used to think the mountain sang. The initiates said that was foolishness, that the sound was only wind in the stones. But I'd lie awake in the dormitory and listen, and it sounded like someone humming while they work. You know? Like a grandmother in a kitchen." He smiled at the frozen carvings. "I used to sneak out at night and sit right here, on this exact bit of wall, probably, and listen until I fell asleep. Someone always came to fetch me. Usually Sera. She'd pretend to be annoyed. She was never actually annoyed."

Gideon leaned against his side, listening with his whole body, the way he listened to everything.

"The garden fed both realms," Casper went on. "That was the whole covenant. Amsel grew the seasons and the realms kept the garden's secrets, and everyone lived, and nobody asked what it cost, because it cost nothing — that was the miracle of it, it cost *nothing* — except it didn't, you see, it never did, because covenants are like fires. They seem free right up until the wood runs out." His voice roughened. "And when the wood ran out, the council looked around for something to burn, and they found a child, and they called the burning holy."

Gideon's hand found his and squeezed. Once. Firmly. The gesture of someone much older.

"They would have done it," Casper said. "Everyone would have watched. Everyone would have called it necessary. So I broke the stone instead, and I've spent ten years telling myself that endless winter for two realms is a smaller crime than one murdered child, and some mornings I believe it, and some mornings—" He stopped. Breathed. The wind hummed through the archway, almost kindly. "Some mornings I look at you and I can't do the arithmetic at all. Because you're what came after. You're the seed the old covenant hid inside itself before it died, and you walked out of the winter and knocked on my door, and I don't know if that's forgiveness or accusation."

Gideon considered this with the seriousness he brought to bread, to warmth, to all important matters. Then he reached into his coat, produced the last heel of the ferrywoman's dark loaf — saved, hoarded, guarded through days of hard climbing — broke it precisely in half, and offered the larger piece to Casper.

Casper laughed. It came out cracked and wet, but it was a laugh.

"That's not how penance works, you know," he said, taking it anyway. "You're supposed to make me suffer."

Gideon gave him a look of profound skepticism and ate his half of the crust with great dignity, and somewhere in the watching of it, the arithmetic Casper couldn't do went quiet — not solved, just quiet, the way a crying child goes quiet when someone finally sits down beside it.

* * *

The last stretch was the worst, because the mountain stopped being subtle.

The wind rose with the afternoon, and this time there was no mistaking it: the voices were in it. Not ghosts, exactly. Echoes. The mountain had soaked up nine hundred years of hymns and footsteps and children laughing in the orchards, and now, as they climbed toward the wound at its summit, it played them back — fragments of the First Tongue, snatches of planting songs, a woman's voice calling names across a field. Casper knew some of those names. He had been one of them, once, called home across the garden paths at dusk.

"*Come in, Casper, the light's going—*"

He staggered. Caught himself on the rope. Kept climbing, because stopping meant listening and listening meant drowning.

"*—the stone, Casper, the stone, hurry—*"

"No," he muttered. "No, I won't hurry, I didn't hurry, that's the whole point—"

"*—snow, look, snow on the orchard, who has done this, who has—*"

"STOP."

The word tore out of him with force behind it — real force, storm-force, the kind he hadn't unleashed since Vell — and for one terrible instant the whole mountainside answered. Wind reversed. Snow lifted off the ledges and hung in the air, suspended, thousands of small white stars waiting on his next word. Far below, the frozen river Marn groaned in its banks. Casper stood in the eye of his own voice, shaking, horrified, ten years of control burning off him like fog in sunlight.

And Gideon stepped into the wind.

Into the hanging snow, into the held breath of the mountain, entirely unafraid, and took Casper's hand, and spoke — one word of the First Tongue, soft, the word for *here*, the word mothers use, the word for *you are located, you are found, you are mine to find* —

and the snow fell, gently, all around them, like blessing instead of fury.

Casper sank to his knees on the ledge. Gideon knelt with him and put both arms around his neck and held on, and the mountain's echoes faded to a murmur, and the two of them stayed like that while the light went gold and then blue, two small figures on a vast grey shoulder of the world.

"I keep thinking," Casper said at last, into the boy's shoulder, "that when we get to the top, I'll finally understand whether I'm the villain of this story. And I think — I think the truth is I'm just the man who was there. The man who was there, and chose, and has to live in the choosing." He pulled back to look at Gideon. "Whatever happens up there — whatever the garden asks of us — I need you to know I'd choose you. Over the realms. Over the spring. Over my own soul, if it comes to trading. Do you hear me?"

Gideon touched Casper's chest, over his heart. Then his own. Then, gravely, solemnly, he pointed up — toward the summit, toward the ruins, toward whatever waited in the frost-dead cathedral of trees.

*Both,* the gesture said. *Why not both.*

"Why not both," Casper repeated, and found, to his astonishment, that he was smiling. "All right. All right, seedling. Why not both."

They climbed the last hour in the blue dusk, and near the top the clouds parted, and Casper saw it for the first time in ten years: the walls of Amsel, silvered with frost, and beyond them the faintest suggestion — impossible, impossible — of green light breathing among the dead trees.

Beside him, Gideon made a sound Casper had never heard from him before. Small. Wondering. The sound of a child seeing home.

And down in the valley behind them, far along the road they had climbed, Casper saw torchlight. Many torches. Moving fast, and organized, and following.

"Sera," he breathed — though he couldn't know that, couldn't know who led them, only that the net was closing. He took Gideon's hand. "Run."
