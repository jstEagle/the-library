## 13. Campfire Confessions

The fire was Gideon's idea.

Casper had wanted to push on through the dark—every hour on the mountain felt borrowed now, and the wind had begun to carry that particular thin whine it made when snow was deciding whether to fall. But the boy had planted himself in the lee of a granite shelf, pointed at the dead branches scattered there, and then pointed at Casper with an expression of such patient, immovable expectation that Casper had laughed for the first time in what felt like a season.

"All right, all right. You're worse than my sister was." The words were out before he could catch them. He crouched and began stacking wood. "Was," he said quietly, to no one. "Is. I don't actually know which."

Gideon tilted his head. He did that a lot—the listening tilt, like a bird working out whether you were worth the trouble of understanding. Then he came over and solemnly handed Casper a handful of pine needles, as though these were a vital contribution to firecraft.

"Thank you," Casper said gravely, and tucked them under the kindling where they would do nothing whatsoever. "Essential."

The flame caught on the third strike of his flint—a small, ordinary, honest fire, no whisper of storm in it—and Gideon made his pleased sound, the one like a kettle deciding to be happy. He wrapped himself in the good blanket and sat so close to the flames that Casper kept having to tug him back by the shoulder, and each time the boy leaned back against Casper's arm as if that had been the point all along.

They ate the last of the river fish from the ferrywoman's parting gift, charred outside and mostly cooked within, and Gideon fell upon his portion with the unselfconscious ferocity of someone who had spent a long time not eating anything at all. Casper watched him and felt the thing he'd been carrying since the Hollow Choir settle lower in his chest, heavier, less deniable.

The boy had thawed a well for strangers. He had sung a hymn older than every kingdom on the map, and ice had wept out of the stones like the world remembering how to cry. And he still thought Casper Dunmore was worth following up a frozen mountain.

That couldn't stand. Not tonight. Not with the Garden two days away and whatever waited there wearing Casper's own guilt like a crown.

"Gideon." He said the name carefully; he always did, as if testing whether it still fit. "There's something you should know before we go any further. Something I should have told you back in Vell, probably. Back at the door, honestly, the first night."

Gideon stopped chewing. He looked up, flour-pale eyes catching the firelight, and waited. He was very good at waiting. It occurred to Casper, not for the first time, that this child had been waiting his whole life for something, and that everything about him—the silence, the patience, the way he pressed his scarred palm flat against tree bark when he thought no one was looking—was the posture of a seed.

Casper fed another branch to the fire and began.

* * *

"You know I was a storm-caller. You've seen me do—" he gestured vaguely at the dark beyond the firelight, where the wind had been carrying them north for days, "—that. What you may not know is what I was *for*."

He pulled off his right glove. In the firelight the old scar across his palm stood out white and ridged, the garden-sigil, twin to the one on Gideon's hand. He held it up so the boy could see.

"This mark means I belonged to the order that kept the covenant. The covenant between the realms—you've been dreaming about it your whole life, I think, even if nobody ever told you its name. Two worlds, one promise. The green world gave the grey world its seasons, its growing things, its spring. The grey world gave the green one... structure. Memory. Stone. Each realm held the other's heart, and neither could fall while both stood."

Gideon's hand crept to his own palm. His fingers traced the sigil without seeming to know they were doing it.

"I was raised inside that promise," Casper went on. "My mother sang the rites before I could walk. My sister Maren and I learned the First Tongue the way other children learn their letters. And when I came of age, they brought me to the covenant stone at Amsel and told me what my work would be." He laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "You'll enjoy this. They told me I'd be a gardener. Storms are just weather doing the garden's heavy lifting, they said. Rain and thunder and the long slow turning of seasons—I was to be a keeper of springs. Of beginnings."

Gideon leaned into him, deliberately this time, a small warm weight. Encouragement. Or maybe just a child who liked leaning. Casper found he didn't care which.

"The stone at the heart of the Garden wasn't decoration, Gideon. It was the hinge. The place where the two realms touched. And a hinge only holds if both doors are willing to bear weight. Every hundred years—maybe every fifty, the records disagreed, and the records burned, so we'll never know now—the covenant asked for renewal. A re-forging. And a re-forging needs..."

He stopped. The fire popped. Somewhere above them, snow finally made up its mind and started coming down in fat, silent flakes that hissed when they drifted too near the flames.

"It needs soil," Casper said. "Living memory. That's what the old texts called it. The person who renewed the covenant didn't just speak the words. They gave the stone something to grow from. Their past. Their name. Everything they were, poured into the roots, so that spring could rise up new and unburdened." He turned his scarred palm over, studying it. "The keepers who did it before—they walked into the Garden whole and walked out as strangers. Beloved strangers. The order cared for them afterward. Told them gentle stories about who they'd been. But the man who broke the stone was never the man who walked away from it."

Gideon was absolutely still. Even his breathing had gone quiet, the way it did when he was listening with more than his ears.

"And ten years ago," Casper said, "it was my turn."

* * *

The snow thickened. Casper pulled the blanket around them both and let the memories come, because he had decided they were going to come anyway, and it seemed cowardly to make them arrive alone.

"There was a vote. There's always a vote, in orders like mine. Twelve keepers around the stone, and the question put plainly: the winter was coming early—we could feel it in the roots, the realms going cold toward each other—and the covenant needed renewing ahead of schedule. Someone had to give themselves to the stone. And do you know what the twelve of us did, Gideon? The great and wise protectors of two worlds?"

Gideon shook his head slowly.

"We voted to give them *you*."

The word landed between them like a dropped stone. Casper made himself keep going, because stopping now would be the same as lying.

"Not you—not you, you weren't born yet, or if you were, you were nothing but a possibility, a line in a prophecy nobody believed. But a child. A seed-child. The texts spoke of them: a living cutting of the Garden itself, born once an age, carrying the covenant in their blood instead of their memory. If such a one existed, the stone wouldn't need a keeper's whole life. It would need only..." He swallowed. "A smaller sacrifice. A younger one. The council found the records, and they got that look councils get, the look of men discovering arithmetic that lets them sleep at night. One child, they said, versus two worlds. One small grief, weighed against endless spring. It wasn't even a hard vote. Eleven to one."

Gideon's hand found Casper's sleeve and gripped it. Not pulling. Just holding.

"I was the one," Casper said. "And I want to be very clear about something, because you're going to hear the other version eventually—from Wardens, from Sera, from every fireside story between here and the sea. The other version says Casper Dunmore broke the covenant to save his own skin. That he feared the stone, or hated the order, or wanted power for himself. Some versions say he was simply weak." He shook his head. "I refused. That's all. I stood up in front of everyone I had ever loved and said *no*. No child pays for our spring. Find another way or lose the spring. And they looked at me the way you look at weather—as something that has arrived where it shouldn't be and must be endured until it passes."

His voice had gone rough. He cleared his throat and stared into the fire.

"So I did the only thing left. If the covenant demanded a monstrous price, then the covenant was already broken—it was broken in the hearts of the people keeping it, and a hinge doesn't hold just because the metal looks fine. I put my hand on the stone and I called the storm down. All of it. Ten years of storms I hadn't called, every thunder I'd ever swallowed, everything. I shattered the hinge rather than let them feed a child into it." He laughed again, and this time it was almost real. "I told myself it was mercy. I told myself the realms would find another way, that spring would come back on its own, that I'd bought time. And then the first snow fell on the green fields of home, and it didn't stop falling, and I understood that I hadn't saved anyone. I'd just chosen which wound the world would bleed from."

The snow hissed into the fire. Gideon had not moved. His grip on Casper's sleeve had tightened until the knuckles went pale.

"They hunted me after that. The order, and then the Wardens when the order fell. Not for vengeance—for *use*. Because a man who can break the covenant might be able to remake it, and a desperate world will forgive anything in a tool. So I ran south, and I buried my name, and I sold luck-knots in a city with no magic in it, and I told myself for ten years that numbness was peace." He finally turned to look at the boy. "That's the truth. That's all of it. I broke the world because I wouldn't let it break a child, and the world has been paying my refusal's bill ever since, and I have been hiding from the collection ever since *that*."

* * *

He expected many things. Anger—he had earned anger. Fear. That particular look children get when the large adult in their life suddenly becomes a stranger wearing familiar clothes.

What he got was Gideon letting go of his sleeve, taking Casper's scarred right hand in both of his small ones, and pressing it flat against his own chest, over his heart. Then he pressed his own palm—sigil to sigil—against the back of Casper's hand.

"That's not—" Casper's voice failed. He tried again. "Gideon, I'm the reason you grew up in the cold. Whatever hollow place spat you out into Vell with no voice and no shoes, that hollow place exists because of me. You don't have to—"

The boy shook his head. Firmly. Twice. The way he refused bread he didn't want.

Then he opened his mouth, and for the first time since the shrine—since Sera's mother's name had fallen out of him like a gift he hadn't known he carried—he spoke. Just one word, in the First Tongue, low and round and certain, and Casper's whole body understood it before his mind caught up, the way you understand rain before you understand weather.

*Anew.*

Again. Once more. Begin again.

"That's not how forgiveness works," Casper whispered. "You don't get to just—hand it out. It has to be earned, there's a whole process, there's penance, there's—"

Gideon patted his hand twice, briskly, the universal gesture of a person humoring a fool, and Casper broke. He laughed and it turned into something else halfway through, and he bowed his head over their joined hands and let the fire and the snow and the boy hold all of it—the shame and the relief and the terrible lightness of having finally set the truth down after carrying it so long he'd forgotten it had weight.

When he could speak again, he said, "Your First Tongue is better than mine, you know. Mine's all book-learning and rites. Yours sounds like it grew in you."

Gideon shrugged, magnificently unimpressed with his own miracle.

"Tomorrow we climb the last ridge," Casper said. "Day after, if the weather's kind—and the weather owes me a few kindnesses, I intend to collect—we see the Garden. And I need you to understand what happens there isn't a debt you owe me. Whatever the stone asks, whatever it costs—if it asks for you, Gideon, if it so much as *looks* at you hungrily, we turn around and I will find another way if it takes the rest of my life. Swear you understand."

Gideon considered this for a long moment. Then he reached over, picked up a pine needle from the pile of his earlier contribution, and pressed it into Casper's palm, closing Casper's fingers around it.

A seed. A promise. *Plant it.*

"You're going to be insufferable when you can talk," Casper said, and meant *thank you*, and was fairly sure the boy knew.

* * *

They slept in shifts, as they had learned to. Casper took first watch, sitting with his back to the granite, the boy's breath rising and falling against his shoulder, the fire burning down to a red and trustworthy glow.

It was strange, how light he felt. Ten years of concealment, and the truth had turned out to weigh less than the lies. He turned the pine needle over in his fingers. Foolish, sentimental, exactly the sort of charm he used to sell to gullible customers in Vell—and he closed his fist around it anyway, and kept it.

The snow eased near midnight. And it was in that hush, in the gap between one gust and the next, that Casper heard it: a sound from down the mountain, faint but rhythmic, wrong for wilderness.

Metal. Many boots. And beneath it, carried on the wind he knew better than his own heartbeat, the thin bright note of a Warden's horn—three short blasts, repeated.

Encirclement. They had found the trail after all.

Casper rose without waking the boy, and stood looking down into the dark where torchlight was beginning to bloom like a disease among the trees, and reached for the storm.
