## 14. The Pale Company

Sera Vane had learned to read captains the way other people read weather.

Captain Aldous Reeve of the Pale Company was the kind of storm that arrived without clouds—clear skies right up until the lightning took the roof off your house. He rode at the head of forty soldiers through the frozen hills north of Hollowmere, his white cloak spotless despite three days of sleet, and he smiled the way men smile when they have already decided how everything will end.

"You're quiet, Warden Vane," he said, drawing his horse alongside hers.

"I'm conserving my voice for the arrest."

"Good. I like a woman who knows what she'll need later." He said it without malice, which somehow made it worse. "The village ahead. Thornhollow. Our scouts say the fugitives sheltered there two nights past. Fed by the miller's family, if you can call what they eat *feeding*. The boy sang in their well and thawed it."

"The well," Sera repeated flatly.

"A miracle, they're calling it." Reeve's smile widened a fraction. "Miracles are contagious. You understand why we can't allow them."

She understood. She had understood for years, in the way one understands a debt—one keeps paying it because stopping would mean admitting what it had cost. Her mother had been a Warden before her. Her mother's oath hung around Sera's neck like a stone on a cord, worn smooth from handling: *The realms were sundered for their safety. What is sundered must stay sundered.*

Her mother had also died with frostbitten hands and a name no one spoke, in a house that smelled of rosemary, and Sera had not thought about that in eleven days, which was exactly as long as she had known Gideon could speak the First Tongue.

*He said her name,* she thought, for the hundredth time. *Maren. He said Maren like he'd known her.*

No child knew that name. Maren Vane had been erased so thoroughly that even Sera sometimes doubted the woman had existed outside her own grief. And yet a mute boy from nowhere had looked up at her in a roadside shrine with eyes the color of new leaves and said, in a language older than kingdoms, *Maren-of-the-rosemary-house*, and something in Sera's chest had cracked along an old seam and never quite closed again.

Which was why she had let them go one night ahead. Which was why she now led a company of forty toward them, misreading her own maps by half a day's ride, buying time with small treasons.

It was working. It kept working. And every hour it worked, the treason grew another ring, like a tree she couldn't stop becoming.

* * *

Thornhollow sat in a fold of hills like a coin dropped in a pocket—thirty houses, a mill, a shrine with a leaning bell, and a well that steamed faintly in the cold, as though the earth beneath it had remembered warmth and couldn't unlearn it.

Reeve did not knock on doors. Reeve rang bells.

His soldiers formed a ring around the village while he stood in the square and announced, in a carrying parade-ground voice, that the Pale Wardens sought two fugitives—a man and a child—and that any household which had sheltered them was guilty of harboring enemies of the covenant's peace.

"They're gone," said the miller. He was a broad, flour-dusted man with a limp and a daughter peeking from behind his coat. "Two nights gone. We fed them because they were hungry. That's all anyone did here."

"That's enough," Reeve said pleasantly. "Harboring is enough."

Sera felt the sentence arrive before it was spoken. She had heard that pleasantness before, in other squares, in other years, and every time she had told herself the same thing: *it is necessary, it is the law, someone must hold the line between the realms or both worlds drown in each other.* She had held the line. She had been good at holding the line.

But the line had moved. She saw that now—or admitted it now. The line had crept, year by year, from *traitors* to *smugglers* to *speaking children* to *villages that fed hungry people*, and she had walked backward alongside it the whole way, calling it duty.

"Captain." She kept her voice level. "The fugitives are half a day ahead. Every hour we spend here—"

"—is an hour spent teaching the countryside what happens to villages that sing in wells." Reeve dismounted, unhurried, brushing frost from his cloak. "Warden Vane, you've been odd since the shrine. Slow reports. Maps that don't agree with themselves. I assumed grief, or fatigue. I'm a patient man." He turned to his lieutenant. "Make the example. The miller's house. Burn it, and the mill. Let the rest watch."

The miller's daughter made a sound—not a word, just a small animal noise of terror—and something in Sera went very still and very clear, the way water goes clear when it freezes.

"Captain," she said. "They're civilians."

"They're kindling," Reeve said, still pleasant, still smiling, and he gestured, and two soldiers stepped forward with torches, and the miller put out his arms to shield his daughter, and Sera Vane reached the point on the map where all her roads ended.

Later, she would not be able to say whether she decided anything at all. It felt less like a decision than like a dam finally admitting what the river had been saying for years.

* * *

The first soldier—Torvald, young, freckled, once confessed to her over camp stew that he'd joined the Wardens because the white cloaks got free boots—turned when she shouted his name, and she hit him across the temple with the weighted butt of her blade, precise, professional, the blow of a woman who had trained twenty years and never once used it on her own side. He folded like laundry.

The second soldier was not young. Hesk had twenty years in the field and read her movement early—he was already turning, torch high, mouth opening to shout the alarm—and Sera did the thing she had rehearsed in nightmares without knowing she was rehearsing: she drove her knife up under the edge of his jaw, quick and clean, and caught him as he fell so he wouldn't make noise hitting the ground.

*I'm sorry,* she thought, absurdly, uselessly. *I'm sorry, I'm sorry, I'm sorry.* It changed nothing. It changed everything. Both things were true and neither helped.

Then the square erupted, because a burning village is loud and dying men are louder, and Reeve turned from the miller's door with his smile finally gone, and looked at her across thirty yards of trampled snow.

"Warden Vane," he said. His voice carried beautifully. It always had. "You are relieved of command."

"No," Sera said. "I don't think I ever accepted it."

She pulled the second thing from inside her coat—the thing she had carried since the shrine, wrapped in oilcloth against her skin like a coal she couldn't put down. Gideon's gift. When the boy had healed her frostbite in the Garden's threshold—no. Not yet. That came later. But here, now, in this moment, what she carried was simpler: the memory of a child's voice saying her mother's name, and the certainty, arriving all at once like spring water through a broken dike, that she had spent her whole life guarding a wall built on a grave.

What she actually pulled from her coat was her Warden's pin. She threw it into the fire the soldiers had started at the miller's fence, and watched her mother's oath curl and blacken, and felt—this was the shameful part, the part she'd carry forever—felt *light*.

"Kill her," Reeve said, without heat, the way a man asks for more wine.

Four soldiers came at her. Sera had trained most of them. She knew Torvald favored his left knee; she knew Bren's grip went loose when he was frightened; she knew that men who burn villages expect their victims to be slow. She fought the way she had always fought—economically, without flourish, giving ground only to take it back twice—and when a sword opened her arm from elbow to wrist she barely registered it, because the pain was honest in a way the last ten years hadn't been.

She broke away at the shrine. Of course she did. She ran for the leaning bell and the frozen ground behind it, where the hillside dropped into pine and shadow, and behind her she heard Reeve shouting orders, and ahead of her—

Ahead of her the sky was doing something impossible.

Clouds had been sitting on the hills all day, gray and inert. Now they were turning. Not darkening—*organizing*, spiraling inward with a slow, deliberate grandeur, like dancers taking their places. Wind came down the valley smelling of ozone and green things, of rain on warm stone, of a garden somewhere remembering itself. Snow lifted off the pines in a single vast exhalation.

Sera stopped running. She knew storms. She had grown up beside a storm-caller; she had watched Casper Dunmore, at nineteen, coax a squall off the sea like a man whistling up a dog. This was not that. This was not a squall being coaxed.

This was a storm answering a summons it had waited ten years to receive.

"Oh," she said aloud, to nobody. "Oh, you beautiful, stubborn fool. You're done hiding."

Lightning walked along the ridge north of Thornhollow, silent, counting itself. The temperature dropped and then, strangely, rose—a breath of thaw threading through the freeze, impossible, tender, furious. Somewhere north, a man who had spent a decade selling luck-knots under a false name had raised his hands and asked the sky for help, and the sky had said *yes, finally, yes*.

The four soldiers pursuing her faltered, staring upward. Even Reeve's parade voice faltered. Men who had never seen magic except in burnings and confiscations were watching the weather change its mind, and every one of them looked, briefly, like children.

Sera used their wonder. She slipped into the pines, blood dripping from her arm, leaving red prints in the snow that the rising wind began patiently to fill.

* * *

She found them at dawn, or they found her—she was never sure which, afterward. She had walked all night on a ruined arm, following the storm's aftermath the way you follow laughter through a house, and collapsed more than arrived at the mouth of a ravine where a fire burned low and a boy sat up suddenly in his bedroll, eyes wide, staring at her as if he'd been expecting her.

Casper Dunmore looked older than ten years should have made him. Frost-burned, exhausted, hollow-eyed with whatever the storm had cost him. He had a knife in his hand before he recognized her, and then he didn't lower it.

"Sera." Her name in his mouth sounded like an accusation and a prayer fighting for the same syllables. "You brought a company."

"I lost a company." She swayed. "Technically I *defected* from a company. There's paperwork."

He stared at her. The boy—Gideon, she reminded herself, he has a name, of course he has a name—tugged Casper's sleeve and signed something quick and urgent with small hands, and Casper's face did something complicated.

"He says you're bleeding," Casper translated slowly. "And that—" he hesitated, listening to more signs, "—that your mother used to hum while she cooked. Rosemary bread. How does he know that?"

"I've stopped asking how he knows things," Sera said. "It's faster that way." Her legs chose that moment to inform her they were done negotiating, and the ground came up, and Casper caught her—caught her the way he'd once caught her falling out of a pear tree when they were apprentices, cursing and laughing, a lifetime ago, before the covenant and the winter and the long silence between them.

"You're defecting," he said. It wasn't a question. "To us. To—to me. After everything."

"After everything," Sera agreed, from somewhere near unconsciousness, "I've decided my side was the wrong side. It only took me ten years and a burned village. I was always a slow student."

Above the ravine, the last clouds of his storm were breaking apart, and through the gap the stars came out—more stars than she'd seen in a decade, as if the sky too had been holding its breath and had finally let it go.

Gideon pressed something into her good hand. A sprig of something green, impossibly green, budding, alive—cut from who knew where, growing in the dead of winter. He closed her fingers around it with great seriousness, the way a priest closes a relic into a pilgrim's palm.

"What is it?" she whispered.

Casper looked at the sprig, and then at the boy, and his face went pale in the firelight.

"It's from the Garden," he said. "Sera—it's blooming. It's already starting."

North, beyond the ravine, beyond the hills, something vast and buried was putting out roots. And behind them, in the south, Captain Aldous Reeve was regrouping his forty, minus two, and writing a report that would bring more than forty.

Sera closed her hand around the living green and let herself, finally, rest.
