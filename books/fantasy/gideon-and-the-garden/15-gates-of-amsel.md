## 15. Gates of Amsel

The mountain stopped pretending to be a mountain on the third morning.

Casper had felt it before he saw it—a change in the way the wind moved against his face, as though the air itself had begun to hold its breath. Gideon felt it too. The boy had been walking half-asleep in Casper's footprints for hours, wrapped in the good wool coat they'd traded a hymn for in the Hollow Choir's village, and now he lifted his head like a hound catching a scent no human nose should own.

"That's it," Casper said quietly, though there was nothing yet to see but scree and cloud. "Isn't it."

Gideon didn't answer—Gideon never answered—but he squeezed Casper's hand twice, which over the long weeks of the road had come to mean *yes*, and once more, quick and urgent, which meant something newer and harder to translate. *Hurry*, maybe. Or *I'm afraid*. With Gideon, those two things often traveled together.

They climbed through the last of the pine line, where the trees grew twisted and small, apologizing for existing at all this far north. And then the pines gave out entirely, and the clouds thinned, and Casper Dunmore stood at the gates of Amsel for the first time in ten years and found that his legs had opinions about it. They simply declined to hold him. He sat down hard on a frost-slick stone and let the sight of it do what ten years of nightmares had never quite managed.

It was still beautiful.

That was the unbearable part. He had expected ruin—he had *made* the ruin, after all, he of all people knew its dimensions—and ruin was there, yes, written across everything. But beauty had gotten there first and refused to leave. The Garden spread below him in a great descending bowl, terraces cut into the mountainside like the seats of an amphitheater built for gods, and every tree in it was dead and silvered with frost and *still standing*. Hundreds of them. Thousands. Rows upon rows of orchard trees, hedgerows, climbing roses frozen mid-bloom on trellises of black iron—all of it preserved in ice the way a moth is preserved in amber, the way grief is preserved in a man who won't stop carrying it.

"It looks like a cathedral," Casper said aloud, because saying things aloud kept them from eating him alive. "A cathedral where everyone died during the service and nobody had the heart to close the doors."

Gideon looked at him with that grave, unblinking attention the boy paid to everything, as if filing the sentence away in whatever language lived behind his silence. Then he tugged Casper's sleeve and pointed down the switchback path, and his whole small body said what his mouth could not: *down, down, we have to go down.*

* * *

The path into the Garden was lined with statues.

Casper had forgotten the statues. That seemed impossible—that a man could forget anything about the place that had broken him—but memory, he was learning, was a coward. It kept the big things and let the small ones slip away, and then the small ones ambushed you at the worst possible moment.

The statues had been gardeners once. That was the old covenant's cruelest joke or kindest gift, depending on who you asked: those who tended Amsel until their dying day were granted the privilege of tending it forever after, turned slowly to living stone, kneeling among the beds they'd weeded, shears still in hand. Casper remembered being nine years old and thinking it was wonderful. He remembered being twenty-six and watching the first snow fall on green fields and understanding, finally, that everything lovely about the covenant was also a trap with better manners.

He walked past a stone woman bent over a rosebush, her face worn smooth by decades of weather, and he did not look too closely at her hands. Some recognitions could wait.

"Careful on the ice," he told Gideon, uselessly. The boy moved across the frozen paths like water finding its level, sure-footed in a way that made Casper's chest ache. Of course he was. This wasn't a ruin to Gideon. This was inheritance.

And then Casper saw it—the thing that stopped him mid-stride and made him grab a dead trellis for support—and he understood that he had been wrong to call the Garden a cathedral after all.

The trees were breathing.

Not metaphorically. Not the way poets breathed life into things. As they descended into the bowl of Amsel, the air grew warmer by degrees, impossibly, gently warmer, and the frost on the nearest apple tree began to weep, thin runnels of meltwater tracing down the black bark. And beneath the bark—if you pressed your palm flat against it, which Casper did, because his hands had always been braver than the rest of him—there was a pulse. Slow. Faint. The heartbeat of something enormous sleeping just under the skin of the world.

"They're alive," he whispered. "Ten years of winter and they're still—" His voice cracked and he laughed, a wet, disbelieving sound. "You stubborn, glorious, *impossible* old trees."

Gideon had both hands pressed to the trunk of the same tree, forehead resting against the icy wood, eyes closed. And from the boy's throat came a sound Casper had never heard him make—not the First Tongue, not exactly, but something older underneath it, a low humming resonance, like a note held by a bell that had been struck years ago and never quite finished ringing.

The tree answered.

It was very small. A single bud, no bigger than a fingernail, swelling at the end of a frost-dead branch—white, tightly furled, trembling. But in a garden where nothing had grown in ten years, it might as well have been a sunrise.

Gideon opened his eyes and looked up at Casper, and he was smiling. Casper hadn't known the boy could smile. It transformed his whole face, made him suddenly, devastatingly young, and Casper had to turn away and pretend to study the terraces because if he didn't he was going to sit down in the snow and cry in front of a child, and he had a reputation to maintain. A terrible, threadbare, entirely fictional reputation.

"Well," he managed. "Don't show off."

* * *

They made camp that night in the gardener's house at the heart of the Garden—a low stone building that had once been full of seed drawers and drying herbs and the smell of bread, and was now full of frost and the particular silence of places where time has given up. Casper got a fire going with the last of their wood and some broken trellis stakes, muttering apologies to the dead gardeners whose roses he was burning. Somewhere overhead, wind moved through the silvered branches, and the whole Garden creaked and sighed around them like a ship at anchor.

Gideon explored.

Casper watched him do it over the top of the fire, and it was the strangest, most tender thing he had ever witnessed. The boy moved through the ruined rooms touching everything—seed drawers pulled open and peered into, a child's wooden toy horse discovered under a collapsed shelf and held up to the light with reverent delight, a wall of hooks where a hundred gardening aprons had once hung, most now rotted to lace. Gideon knew where everything was. That was the part that undid Casper. The boy reached into dark corners and found things without searching, turned left before the turn existed, ducked under a low beam a full second before he needed to. He wasn't exploring a strange place. He was remembering one.

"You've never been here," Casper said softly. "Have you. You've only ever been *of* here."

Gideon paused in the doorway between rooms, silhouetted against the dying light, and did not answer, which was its own kind of answer. Then he crossed the room, sat down beside Casper at the fire, and leaned against his side with the boneless trust of a cat, and Casper put an arm around him and decided that philosophy could wait until spring.

He was nearly asleep when the singing started.

It came up through the floor. Through the roots. Through whatever network ran beneath Amsel connecting tree to tree the way it connected all growing things in the old days. It wasn't sound, exactly—or it was sound, but heard somewhere behind the ears rather than in them, a vibration in the teeth and the breastbone. The covenant-hymns. The ones the Hollow Choir villagers sang without knowing why. Here, in the place they'd been written, Casper could feel their shape again, their meaning, the great slow harmonies that had once bound two realms together like stitches binding a wound.

And threaded through the hymns, faint as a candle seen through fog, was something else. Something wrong. A cold note underneath the warmth, a minor key buried in the major, growing by fractions.

Casper sat bolt upright, heart hammering.

Gideon was already awake, already looking north, toward the high wall of the Garden where the mountains closed in. Toward the pass. The boy's hand found Casper's and squeezed twice—*yes*—and then once more, hard, and this time Casper needed no translation at all.

Something was coming. Something that did not sing.

* * *

Dawn came gray and windless, which in the far north is never a mercy. Windless cold is the honest kind; it doesn't pretend.

Casper walked the upper terraces while Gideon slept, and tried to take inventory of what remained, and found himself instead taking inventory of what he'd destroyed. There was the fountain court, dry now, its central statue—a figure pouring an endless jug—cracked from crown to heel. There was the long glasshouse, every pane shattered, the iron ribs bare as a whale's skeleton. There was the schoolroom wing, where children of the covenant had once learned the First Tongue alongside their letters, its windows dark, its door hanging open on a classroom frozen mid-lesson: slate still bearing chalk marks softened by damp into ghosts of arithmetic, small stools tipped over in patterns that suggested the leaving had been sudden.

His fault. All of it. Every frozen branch, every empty stool. Ten years ago he had stood on the mountain above this place with the covenant stone under his hands, and he had chosen, and the choosing had sounded like thunder, and the world had begun to die politely ever since.

"I know," he told the fountain statue, because it had a face and faces were easier to confess to. "I know what I did. I'd do it again. That's the worst part. I'd do it again, and I still can't decide if that makes me a monster or just a man who loved one person more than the world." He laughed, without much humor. "Sera would say I don't get to pick. Sera would say the picking was done the moment the first snow fell."

The statue poured its invisible water and said nothing, which was the traditional response of statues, and also, Casper reflected, of the divine.

But here was the thing he couldn't explain to the statue, couldn't fully explain even to himself: walking these ruined paths with Gideon's small hand in his, watching the boy greet each dead tree like a grandmother, Casper felt something he had not felt in ten years of exile. Not forgiveness—he doubted forgiveness was on offer anywhere in either realm. Something adjacent to it. Purpose, maybe. The sense of a debt that had finally, after a decade, been issued a due date.

The Garden was breathing. Barely. One bud on one tree. But breathing.

And Gideon was the reason. Gideon was the seed, the living heart of the covenant wearing the body of a mute child who liked bread and warm coats and wooden horses. Whatever ceremony, whatever reforging the old rites demanded, it would happen here, at the cracked covenant stone Casper could see from the upper terrace—a pale scar on the mountainside where the great stone had split down its center like a struck bell.

All they had to do was survive long enough to try.

Which was, of course, the problem.

Because the cold note beneath the hymns had not gone away with the dawn. If anything it had sharpened overnight, resolved itself into something almost melodic, almost intentional. And when Casper climbed to the Garden's northern wall and looked out through the frost-laced gate toward the pass beyond, he saw what the night's silence had been hiding.

On the far ridge, small with distance but unmistakable, lights were moving. Lanterns. Many lanterns, strung out along the pass road in a line that was too orderly to be travelers and too numerous to be anything good.

The Pale Wardens had found the mountain.

Behind him, down in the breathing dark of the Garden, Gideon began to hum the covenant-hymns in his sleep, and every tree in Amsel shivered, shedding frost like a dog shaking off rain, and Casper understood with terrible clarity that the Garden knew they were coming too—and that whatever happened next, it would happen here, among the graves of everything he had loved, with winter closing its fist around the only spring left in either world.

"Stay asleep a little longer," he murmured to the boy below, to the trees, to himself. "Just a little longer."

The lanterns on the ridge did not slow down.
