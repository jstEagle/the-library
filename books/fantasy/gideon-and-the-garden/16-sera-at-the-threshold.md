## 16. Sera at the Threshold

The snow had stopped lying about itself.

That was the thing Sera Vane noticed first, as she dragged herself up the last ridge toward the ruins of Amsel — the snow here didn't behave like snow anymore. It fell soft and slow, unhurried, like it had somewhere better to be. It landed on her shoulders without biting. Somewhere below, past the frost-dead trees that stood like a congregation waiting for a sermon ten years overdue, something green was breathing.

She knew because she could smell it. Earth. Sap. The particular sweetness of things deciding to grow.

Sera laughed, once, a dry cracked sound that startled a bird out of a blackthorn. Laughing hurt. Most things hurt, at this point. Her left hand was a white claw of frostbite she'd stopped being able to feel two days ago, which she understood was a bad sign in the way that drowning men understand water is wet. Her right side carried a Warden's blade-wound from the captain's own sword, delivered with the flat efficiency of a man pruning a hedge. Traitors got pruned. She'd been told so, politely, by a boy younger than her boots.

"You should have run south," she told the bird, or the mountain, or herself. "Everyone sensible runs south."

But south was where the Pale Company was, and where the burned village was, and where the bodies of Hallowmere were stacked under tarps because there wasn't ground soft enough to dig. South was where she had led soldiers with good maps and a bad purpose for eleven years, and where she had finally, on a gray morning beside a frozen well, stopped pretending that duty was a coat that still fit.

So: north. To the Garden. To forgiveness or death, whichever was offered first, and she'd honestly have taken either.

* * *

The gates of Amsel were not gates anymore. They were two pillars of standing stone with a drift of snow between them, and the snow was melting as she watched, pulling back from the threshold like a tide going out. Sera stood at the line where white ended and dark earth began and found she couldn't cross it.

Not because of wards. There were no wards; she'd have felt them, old or new. Because of what crossing would mean. On one side of the line she was Sera Vane, Warden of the Pale Order, keeper of erasures, the woman who had hunted a child across half a kingdom with orders signed in triplicate. On the other side she was just a woman with a ruined hand and no story left that made sense of her.

"Come on," she muttered. "You've faced worse."

She hadn't, actually. That was the joke of it. She'd faced storms and smugglers and the business end of her own conscience for a decade, and none of it had ever required her simply to *walk forward* while everyone she'd been watched.

Her legs decided before her pride did. They buckled at the threshold, and she went down onto her knees in the thawing snow, and then, because kneeling was already accomplished and dignity was a luxury item, she crawled.

The trees took her in. Frost-dead oaks and rowans, silvered and still, but under their bark — she pressed her cheek against one as she passed — a faint pulse, slow as a sleeping heart. The paths between them were clear of snow entirely, swept clean by nothing, and lined with the ghosts of garden beds. She recognized the layout from the old texts she'd been ordered to burn. She had burned them. She remembered the smell of the ash.

"I'm sorry," she said to the trees, generally. It seemed insufficient. It was insufficient. But you started somewhere.

* * *

She heard the boy before she saw him, which shouldn't have been possible, because Gideon didn't speak — except that he did now, apparently, in the First Tongue, words like water over stones, and the sound of it came through the trees ahead of him like light through a window.

He appeared around a frost-dead hawthorn carrying a watering can. An actual watering can, dented, ancient, dredged up from some ruin of the old gardener's shed. He was maybe eight years old, barefoot in the snow — the snow didn't touch him, she saw; it melted an inch from his skin — and when he saw her he stopped.

Sera tried to stand. She managed something between standing and collapsing against a tree, sword-hand going to a hip that no longer held a sword. Old habits. The boy looked at her hand, at the blood on her side, at the gray of her lips, with an expression she could only call *assessing*, the way a gardener assesses a plant that has come in poorly.

"Gideon," said a voice behind him, low and careful. Casper Dunmore stepped into view, and Sera's whole body clenched like a fist.

Ten years, and he looked older and thinner and softer all at once, a storm-caller folded down small enough to fit inside a charm-seller's apron. His eyes went from her face to her wounds to the Warden's sigil she'd scratched off her own collarbone with a knife three nights ago — the scar was still raw — and back to her face.

"Sera." He said her name the way you'd say the name of weather coming in.

"Casper." Her voice came out wrecked. She cleared her throat and tried again. "Dunmore."

"That's more like it." He didn't come closer. Smart man. "How did you find us?"

"The maps," she said, and almost smiled, and it turned into a grimace instead. "I misread them. For six days I misread them very thoroughly. The company is two days south, following my errors, and by tomorrow they'll have figured out I made them on purpose, and then they'll follow my *corrections*." She slid down the tree until she was sitting in the clean earth. "I killed two of them. The ones who were going to burn a farmhouse with a family in it because the family gave us bread. So. That's me now. That's the whole inventory."

Gideon set down the watering can and came toward her.

"No—" Casper's hand came up. "Gideon, wait—"

The boy didn't wait. Children never did; it was one of the many things adults forgot about them. He knelt in front of Sera, tilted his head, and looked at her frostbitten hand with the grave attention of someone reading a difficult page.

"It's gone," Sera told him, because she was apparently determined to be honest even now, even here. "The fingers. Maybe the hand. It doesn't hurt anymore, which they say means—"

Gideon took her ruined hand in both of his small warm ones.

And spoke.

"*Maren.*"

The word went through Sera like a bell through the chest. Not a word — a name. Her mother's name. Maren Vane, who had sung her to sleep in a tongue the Wardens later outlawed, who had died in the first winter after the covenant broke, coughing in a house the cold had gotten into like smoke. Sera had been nine. She had spent thirty years making sure she never thought about it directly, the way you don't look at the sun.

The boy said it again, gently, the way you'd say it across a kitchen table. *Maren.* And then a second word, longer, flowing, and though Sera had no translation for it she understood it completely, the way you understand a lullaby: *she is not gone where you think she went. She is in the roots. Everything you loved is in the roots.*

"Oh," Sera said. Very quietly. "Oh, you little—"

Heat bloomed in her hand.

It started in the fingertips — the dead ones, the ones that had been wax and bone — and spread inward like dawn coming up over a field. Pins and needles first, then ache, then the deep outrageous itch of living flesh remembering itself. Color came back into her fingers in patches, pink flooding white. The wound in her side pulled tight, warmed, quieted. She sat there in the soil of the Garden of Amsel with a child's hands wrapped around hers and wept like a drain, ugly and unhidden, because there was nowhere left to put it and no reason left to hold it.

Grace, she thought dimly, through the noise of her own crying. This is what it is. It's this. It comes without conditions and it finds you kneeling in the dirt and it doesn't ask what you did.

* * *

When she could speak again, Casper was crouched a few feet away, watching with an expression she remembered from twenty years ago, from the novitiate, from the boy he'd been before the stone and the snow: wary hope, worn like a coat two sizes too big.

"She did that," Sera said, flexing her fingers. Whole. Pink. Ugly scars where the frostbite had bitten deepest, but *hers*, alive. "Your seed of the covenant healed a Warden. Do you understand what the Order will make of that? They'll call it corruption. They'll call it a lure. They will march a hundred men through those gates to put it out."

"I know."

"And you're just going to sit there?"

"I'm going to sit here," Casper said, "because running hasn't worked in ten years, and because—" he gestured at the trees, at the faint green misting along the far hedgerow, at the boy now solemnly watering a bed of something that had no business sprouting in winter "—look at it, Sera. Look what's coming up. You think I take this anywhere? It stays where it's planted or it dies."

She looked. She looked properly, for the first time since crawling through the gates. Between the frost-dead rows, life was moving the way time-lapse moves — too fast, wrong, miraculous. Moss greening the north sides of stones. Buds swelling on branches that had been dead wood for a decade. And everywhere, faint as breath on glass, that pulse in the ground.

"It's the reforging," she said. "It's actually happening. You're actually doing it." She laughed again, and this time it wasn't dry. "I spent eleven years serving an Order whose entire doctrine is that the covenant broke because the realms were greedy. And the truth was sitting in a charm shop in Vell selling luck-knots."

"Luck-knots?" One eyebrow went up. "You investigated my luck-knots?"

"I bought one. In disguise. It worked." She shrugged, wincing. "Annoyingly well. I won a bet on a dog race I should have lost. I've felt guilty about it for months."

Casper stared at her, and then — she'd forgotten how rare it was, how much she'd missed it — he laughed. Really laughed, short and helpless, the sound of a man discovering his shoulders had been up around his ears for a decade.

"A Warden bought my charms," he said. "The Pale Order's finest, cheating at dog races with contraband magic."

"In my defense, the dog was very fast."

* * *

They sat together as the light went long and gold through the trees — an impossible gold, a spring-colored light in deep winter — and Gideon moved among the beds, and Sera told them everything. The company. The captain, Voss, methodical and sincere and therefore the most dangerous kind of man. The village executions. Her own maps, bent gently wrong, day after day, until bending them wrong became the truest work she'd ever done.

"Why?" Casper asked at last. Not accusing. Just asking, the way you ask about weather. "Eleven years, Sera. What changed?"

She turned her healed hand over in her lap. Watched the blood move under the skin.

"He said her name," she said finally. "At the shrine, weeks ago. Gideon. He looked at me — I had a blade on him, Casper, I had orders — and he said *Maren*, in the First Tongue, and then he said something else, and I didn't know the words but I knew—" Her throat closed. She pushed through it. "I knew my mother's voice. That's what it was. It was her voice saying goodbye, thirty years late, from somewhere the cold never got into. And I thought: I have spent my whole life serving people who would erase that voice if they heard it. Who *did* erase it. Every hymn, every rite, every mother's song in that tongue — burned, banned, forgotten on purpose. And we called it peace."

Gideon had drifted over during this and installed himself against her side, the way children do, without asking permission, as though she were furniture worth leaning on. She put her arm around him very carefully, as though he might dissolve.

"They'll be here in two days," she said. "Maybe less, once they correct my maps. Voss doesn't negotiate. He doesn't rage, either — that's the thing people get wrong about him. He'll walk in sad and reasonable and he'll do exactly what he came to do."

"Then we have two days," Casper said. He was looking at the broken covenant stone visible down the central path, a great split monolith furred with new moss, and his face had gone somewhere she recognized: the place he went when the storm was building behind his eyes. "Two days to finish what should have taken a season."

"What does it need?"

"Him." Casper nodded at Gideon. "And soil." He paused, and something crossed his face — grief and resolve sharing a chair — that Sera didn't understand yet, and wouldn't, until it was too late to argue. "Soil," he repeated softly. "We'll manage the soil."

Gideon patted her arm, twice, the universal signal of *there, there*, and pointed down the path toward the stone, and then at the darkening sky, and said something in the First Tongue that sounded suspiciously like *supper*.

"He's bossy," Sera observed.

"He's been bossy since the day he arrived," Casper said, hauling himself up. "Barefoot on my doorstep, ordering me around in a dead language. Some things are clearly innate."

* * *

That night, in the gardener's ruined cottage with a fire lit from frost-dead wood that burned green-hearted and sweet, Sera slept for the first time in eleven years without dreaming of doors she'd kicked in. She dreamed of a kitchen instead, and steam on windows, and a voice singing in a language the world had tried to bury, and woke with her healed hand curled open on her chest like something planted.

Outside, far to the south, torchlight was already moving along the salt road — a thin orange thread of it, patient and numerous, winding north through the dark.

The snow above the treeline had begun, very quietly, to fall upward.
