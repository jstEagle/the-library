## 17. The Long Winter Finishes Falling

The blizzard came at dusk, the way the worst things always did.

Casper felt it before he saw it—a pressure behind his eyes, an old ache in his knuckles that he had spent ten years pretending was just age. He stood in the ruined colonnade of Amsel, watching the northern sky turn the color of a bruise, and thought: *there you are. I wondered when you'd find us.*

Behind him, Gideon was laughing.

That was the thing that undid him, a little. The boy had spent the whole day exploring the frost-dead garden like it was a festival ground—running down the broken allées, pressing his scarred palm against every blackened trunk, chattering away in the First Tongue as though the trees could answer. Maybe they could. Casper had seen one of them shiver when the boy touched it, a long slow ripple through branches that should have been stone-frozen for a decade.

"Come look," Gideon said—or rather, said nothing, and Casper understood anyway. Ten days on the road had built a bridge between them that needed no words. The boy was crouched by a dry fountain, brushing snow off something carved into its rim. A bird. A wren, wings folded, rendered in stone so fine you could count the feathers.

"It's beautiful," Casper agreed, coming to stand beside him. "This whole place used to be. There were nightingales here once. Real ones." He crouched, ran a thumb along the carving. "My teacher told me the birds were part of the covenant. That as long as they sang, the two realms would keep their bargain."

Gideon looked up at him with those too-old eyes and made a small sound—not quite a word, not quite a question. *What happened to them?*

"They stopped singing," Casper said. "About ten years ago. Around the time I—" He stopped. He'd promised himself he would tell the boy everything, and he had, mostly, around campfires in low honest tones. But some sentences still snagged in his throat like fishhooks. "Around the time I did what I did."

Gideon reached out and patted Casper's hand twice, the way you'd pat a dog that looked sad. It was, Casper reflected, exactly the level of comfort he deserved, and somehow it helped more than forgiveness would have.

Then the wind arrived.

It came down out of the mountains in a white wall, screaming, and Casper knew it instantly—not as weather but as *message*. This was no ordinary storm. This was the long winter finishing its fall, the last great exhale of a dying covenant, and riding inside it, sharp as needles, he felt the other thing. Boots. Steel. Purpose.

"They're here," he said.

* * *

Sera found them at the old gatehouse, which was less a gatehouse now than a suggestion of one—two crumbling towers and a stretch of wall that might have stopped a determined goat and nothing else. She came up the road half-carrying herself, blood frozen black along her left side, her Warden's grey coat scorched across the shoulder.

"You're hurt," Casper said stupidly.

"Observant as ever. Still the worst student I ever taught." She leaned against the wall and slid down it until she was sitting, which frightened him more than any wound. Sera Vane did not sit down mid-battle. "Company of thirty. Captain Hesse. They're making camp at the tree line—they'll come at first light. Or sooner, if their captain is the man I think he is, which means sooner."

"How did you get ahead of them?"

"I killed two of them and stole a horse." She said it flatly, without drama, the way she'd once reported the weather. Then she looked at Gideon, who had appeared at Casper's elbow, silent and watchful, and her voice cracked slightly on the next words. "They burned Hollowmere on the way here. The village that sang for you. Everyone who sheltered your names."

Casper closed his eyes. The choir village. The thawed well. The old woman who had pressed honey-cakes into Gideon's hands and called him *little spring*. His chest did something complicated and painful.

"Hesse gave them a choice," Sera went on. "Renounce the miracle or burn. They sang instead." Her jaw worked. "I'm telling you because you need to know what's coming up that road, and because I need to say it out loud to someone who'll be angry enough about it to fight properly. I've been angry alone for three days and it's very tiring."

"We'll fight," Casper said.

"No." She pulled herself upright against the wall, wincing. "*You* will do what you came here to do—the reforging, whatever mad ritual the seed-child needs. *I* will hold the gate." She patted the sword at her hip. "Thirty years a Warden. I know exactly how much gate this is." She glanced at the crumbling wall. "Not much. But I'm very good at not much."

"Sera—"

"Don't." For a moment the old mentor looked out of her face, the woman who had taught him to shape wind with his hands cupped like a bowl, who had laughed at his terrible early attempts until she cried. "Don't waste breath arguing with a dying woman about arithmetic, Casper. Thirty against one is bad odds. Thirty against one *and a storm-caller buying time*—" she smiled, thin and fierce, "—those are the best odds I've had in years."

Gideon stepped forward then, and did something none of them expected. He took Sera's wounded hand in both of his small ones, and he spoke.

One word. Two syllables. First Tongue, soft as snowfall.

Sera went absolutely still. Casper didn't know the word—but he knew the shape of it, the way it landed on her, and he understood it was a name. Her mother's name, the one Gideon had spoken at the shrine weeks ago, the word that had bought them their first night's mercy.

"That's cheating," Sera whispered. Her eyes were wet. "You can't just—that's cheating, little one."

Gideon patted her hand twice, in exactly the manner he used on Casper, and Casper realized with a start that the boy had been paying attention to *him*, learning comfort from *him*, and that somewhere along the salt road he had become someone worth imitating. The thought settled into him warm and terrifying.

"All right," Sera said, dashing her sleeve across her face. "All right. Show me these gates I'm supposed to die holding."

* * *

They spent the dark hours preparing, and this was where Casper discovered that ten years of selling luck-knots had not entirely rotted him.

The problem was simple: the reforging required the covenant stone, deep in the heart of the garden, and the reforging would take time—time measured, Gideon's urgent gestures suggested, in hours, not minutes. Thirty Wardens breaching a broken wall took minutes. So the wall needed to become something else. Something slower.

"The storm," Sera said, when he laid it out. "You'll raise the storm."

"I haven't—" He flexed his fingers. Numb. They were always numb now; he'd done it deliberately, years ago, folding the gift down small and small until it fit inside a charm-seller's careful hands. "I haven't raised a true storm since Amsel fell. Since I—" He gestured vaguely at the sky, the ruins, the entire catastrophe of his life. "Since the last time."

"And?"

"And the last time, I used it to break the world."

Sera considered this. Snow gathered on her shoulders. Somewhere below them, at the tree line, a campfire flickered.

"Well," she said at last, "you broke it once. You know where all the pieces go. That's more than most of us can say." She stood, drew her sword, and tested the edge with her thumb. "Besides. You're not doing it alone this time. That's the difference. That's the whole difference, Casper. Last time you were one proud fool with a grievance. This time you're a stubborn old man protecting a child. Storms raised out of love hit harder. Ask any grandmother."

Despite everything—despite the burning village and the coming dawn and the ache in his knuckles—Casper laughed. It came out rusty, like a gate unused for years, but it came.

"Go on," Sera said. "Wake the sky. I'll wake the Wardens. We'll see who's louder."

* * *

He climbed the tallest tower because it seemed right, and because Gideon insisted on coming, planting himself at Casper's side with the stubbornness of a weed growing through pavement. Below, the garden spread out silver and still, the frost-dead trees waiting. Beyond the wall, the plain lay white under a sky full of falling pressure.

Casper held out his hands.

For ten years he had kept them closed. Closed around charms, closed around coins, closed around the truth of what he was. Now he opened them, palms up, the way he had at nine years old in his mother's kitchen, the way Sera had taught him at fifteen, cupped like a bowl—

—and nothing happened.

Panic, cold and familiar. *It's gone. You folded it too small. You killed it yourself, the way you kill everything.* The wind screamed distantly over the peaks and refused to answer him, and he stood there like a man calling a name at an empty house.

Then a small warm hand slipped into his right one.

Gideon didn't speak. He simply held on, and Casper felt it again—that strange current between them, the living-seed hum of the boy, older than kingdoms, patient as roots. And through the boy's touch, the numbness in Casper's fingers began, very slowly, to thaw. Not like fire. Like spring. Like the first morning after a winter you'd stopped believing would end.

"Oh," Casper breathed. "Oh, you clever thing. You're not just the soil. You're the *seed*."

Gideon grinned up at him, gap-toothed and ancient-eyed, and squeezed his hand.

Casper opened himself.

It was not gentle. Ten years of dammed power did not trickle—it *detonated*. The pressure behind his eyes burst outward and the sky answered like a dog hearing its name after a decade of strangers. Clouds boiled down off the mountains. The temperature dropped so fast the snowflakes came down as tiny spears. Lightning walked the ridge line in long white strides, and thunder rolled across the plain of Amsel like the voice of something enormous waking up annoyed.

And Casper Dunmore—who had spent ten years being small, being careful, being nobody—threw back his head and *laughed*, because it was still there, all of it, the wild blue voltage of his gift, and gods forgive him but it felt like coming home.

"There you are," he shouted into the wind, and the wind shouted back.

Below, torches bloomed at the tree line. The Wardens were moving—early, just as Sera predicted, Hesse driving them into the storm because he was the kind of man who believed weather was a morale problem. Casper watched the grey column start up the road toward the broken wall, and he felt the fury rise in him, hot and old, fury at Hollowmere's ashes, at the burned smugglers, at ten wasted years, at his own cowardice—

—and then Gideon tugged his hand.

Just a tug. Just a child reminding an adult of something. And Casper looked down at him—at the boy standing calm in a hurricane because storms were, apparently, his native language—and the fury changed shape. It didn't cool. It *settled*. It became what it should have been all along: not a weapon pointed outward, but a wall stood around something precious. Fury reclaimed as guardianship, Sera would have called it, if she weren't currently downstairs killing people.

"Not destruction," Casper said, tasting the words. "Defense. All right. All right, old friend. Let's show them what a storm is *for*."

He shaped it. That was the art Sera had taught him and he had never forgotten: a storm is not chaos, a storm is architecture. He built walls of wind along the broken ramparts, curtains of sleet that turned the Warden column's advance into a blind stumbling pilgrimage. He dropped the temperature until their crossbow strings snapped and their torches died. He filled the air above the wall with rolling thunder so constant and so close that hardened soldiers sat down in the snow and covered their ears and wept.

But he did not strike them. Not one bolt. He held the line the way a shepherd holds a hill—by making every other option exhausting.

Through the howl, he heard Sera's voice rising from the gatehouse, clear and carrying, pitched for the whole company to hear:

"PALE WARDENS! Your captain burned a singing village! I am Sera Vane, thirty years your sister in grey, and I am telling you there is a child in this garden who makes dead trees breathe! Go home! Go home and plant something!"

A pause. Then, faintly, through the storm, someone in the Warden ranks laughed. Then another. Casper couldn't tell if it was bitter or wondering, and decided it didn't matter.

The column wavered. Held. Began, grindingly, to advance again—Hesse's voice cracking over them like a whip, driving them up the road through Casper's howling architecture.

"They'll reach the wall within the hour!" Sera bellowed up at the tower, bloodied and grinning like a woman twenty years younger. "However long your magic lasts, make it longer!"

Casper gripped Gideon's hand tighter and poured himself into the sky.

* * *

The hour passed the way hours do in battle: in fragments. A gust that threw three soldiers off the road. A lightning strike—his first, unavoidable, a captain's banner and the man holding it reduced to smoke—took something out of him he'd need later. Gideon's hand never left his, warm as a hearthstone, and whenever Casper's knees buckled the boy hummed, low in his chest, a note that was almost the old nightingale-song, and Casper found strength he didn't own.

Which was why he almost missed it.

The storm thinned. Just at the eastern wall—just where the rubble made a natural stair—he felt a hole open in his winds, deliberate, surgical, as though someone who knew his architecture had reached in and removed a single stone.

*Sera,* he thought wildly. *Is she—*

No. Sera's sword was singing at the gatehouse below. This was something else. Through the gap in the storm, small and disciplined, a wedge of Wardens moved—six of them, grey as ghosts, moving with the unhurried confidence of men following a map drawn by someone who had walked this garden before.

Someone who had burned every record of the road to Amsel.

Someone who had known, once, exactly where the covenant stone slept.

"No," Casper whispered. The wind tore the word away. He looked down at Gideon, at the boy whose eyes had gone wide and fixed on the eastern breach, and saw his own dread reflected there.

The wedge of Wardens vanished into the trees, heading for the heart of the garden.

Heading for the stone.

Heading for the place where, at dawn, a seed meant to bloom.
