## 18. The Reforging

The storm Casper had raised still hung over the Garden like a held breath.

He stood at the edge of the great courtyard where the covenant stone had stood—or half of it had. The other half lay in the snow ten paces away, sheared clean, its broken face still faintly warm to the touch after all these years, as if the stone itself refused to accept what had been done to it. Frost-dead trees lined the paths in ranks, their branches bare and white, but when the wind moved through them they did not rattle. They *sighed*. A cathedral of sleeping things, breathing slow.

And in the center, kneeling at the broken stone with both small hands pressed flat against it, was Gideon.

"Gideon," Casper said, and started forward.

The boy turned his head. He was smiling. That was the first wrong thing—the boy smiled the way he always smiled, open and unafraid—but his smile was *fading*, the way a face fades behind fogged glass. His edges had gone soft. Where his bare feet pressed into the snow, there were no prints. There were *buds*. Tiny green things, pushing up through the frost in the exact shape of his toes.

Casper's heart stopped, restarted badly, and stopped again.

"No," he said. "No no no—Gideon, get away from it—"

But the boy shook his head, slow and gentle, the way you'd calm a spooked horse. And he spoke—not with his mouth, though his mouth moved too, but straight into the marrow of Casper's bones, in the First Tongue, in words that needed no translation because they were older than translation:

*The seed asks for soil.*

"I don't—" Casper's voice cracked. He dropped to his knees in the snow beside the boy, hands hovering, afraid to touch him and afraid not to. "I don't understand. You're supposed to *grow*. You're supposed to be the spring. Sera said—you're the seed, I carry you to the garden, we plant you, and—"

*And what does a seed become,* Gideon asked, patient as winter, *if it is never buried?*

The words went through Casper like an arrow through wet cloth.

He looked at the boy—at the soft edges, at the green shoots curling up from the snow where he knelt, at the way the frost-dead trees around them had begun, very slightly, to lean inward, the way flowers lean toward light—and he understood. All at once, completely, the way you understand a fall only once you've already hit the ground.

A seed was not a child. A seed was a promise wrapped in something small. And a promise could not make itself true. It needed soil. It needed something rich and dark and *full* to pour itself into, something that held rain and remembered seasons, something alive enough to rot and give.

There was no soil here. Ten years of unbroken winter had seen to that. The Garden of Amsel was a grave with delusions of architecture.

But Casper Dunmore was full to bursting. Ten years of memories, hoarded like a miser's coins: his sister Maren laughing with her sleeves too long for her arms. The smell of the novices' kitchen on bread-day. Sera at nineteen, furious, brilliant, correcting his grip on the storm-calling stave for the hundredth time. The green fields of home before the snow. The sound of the covenant stone singing under his palm. The terrible blue moment when it stopped.

Every memory he owned was soil. Every memory he owned was *fertile ground*, and the seed was asking.

"Oh," Casper said quietly. "Oh, you clever, cruel little miracle."

Gideon tilted his head. The smile flickered—hurt flickered, briefly, humanly—and Casper realized the boy had heard him. Of course he had. The First Tongue didn't bother with privacy.

"Not cruel," Casper amended quickly, catching one small fading hand in both of his own. The hand felt like holding a handful of early morning. "Not you. Never you. Cruel like—like arithmetic is cruel. Like the truth does sums with people." He laughed, and it came out ragged. "Ten years I've been running from what I broke, and the answer turns out to be me. It was always going to be me. I just thought it would cost my life. Nobody told me it would cost worse than that."

*You can refuse,* Gideon said. His eyes—grey-green, the color of new leaves before they commit to being leaves—were very serious now. *The garden can wait another hundred years. Another thousand. Stone is patient.*

"And the realms aren't," Casper said. "And neither are you. Look at your hands, love."

The boy looked. His fingers had gone translucent at the tips, and through them Casper could see the faint green lattice of roots beginning, delicate as lace, reaching down into the snow toward the broken stone. Growing whether Gideon willed it or not. The seed doing what seeds do.

"You'll fade either way," Casper said. "Unless someone gives you somewhere to *be*."

*It will hurt,* Gideon said. Not a warning. An apology.

"Everything worth doing does." Casper sat back on his heels. He looked out over the courtyard—the dead trees leaning in, the storm churning overhead, the two halves of the covenant stone waiting like an open book. Somewhere beyond the gates, muffled by wind, he could hear the Wardens regrouping. Sera was out there somewhere too, bleeding, having chosen them over everything she'd built her whole life on. He did not have time for a long goodbye. He suspected, if he were honest, that he wouldn't want one. Long goodbyes gave you room to be a coward.

"Soil," Casper said. "Right. Let's see what I've got in the cellar."

* * *

He began, absurdly, with the small things. It seemed right. A garden is mostly small things.

He took Gideon's hands in his, laid them against the broken face of the covenant stone, and pressed his own palms over the boy's—three layers, child, stone, man, like the world's strangest sandwich—and he closed his eyes and reached for the first memory the way you reach for a jar on a high shelf.

*Maren, age six, teaching a frog to swim in the rain barrel, absolutely convinced it was drowning.*

He let it go.

It left him the way water leaves a cup poured out—smoothly, completely, all at once—and he felt it land somewhere below him, felt it soak down through Gideon's small body and into the roots and into the stone, and the stone *hummed*. One low note, deep as a bell struck underwater. In the snow between the boy's knees and the stone, a single crocus pushed up and opened, purple and ridiculous and alive.

Gideon gasped—a real gasp, a boy-sound, not a mystery-sound—and his edges firmed, just slightly, just for a moment. Like a candle flame standing up straighter.

"It works," Casper breathed. Then, giddy despite everything, despite the Wardens and the storm and the fact that he was actively uninstalling himself: "It *works*. Ha! Take that, prophecy. You want soil? I am *extremely* well-composted."

*More,* Gideon said, and this time there was wonder in it, wonder and hunger both, the hunger of a growing thing, which forgives nothing and apologizes for nothing. *The bread-day kitchen. Give me the bread-day kitchen.*

"You liked that one?" Casper grinned. "All right. Bread-day. Coming up."

*The novices' kitchen, flour to the elbows, old Brother Tammsen singing off-key on purpose to make the little ones laugh. The dough rising under damp cloth like something breathing. Casper, twelve years old, sneaking the first heel of the loaf and burning his mouth and not caring.*

It went. The hum deepened. Two more crocuses. A snowdrop. Somewhere in the ranks of dead trees, one branch shivered loose its frost and showed, underneath, the ghost of a bud.

Casper kept going.

*His mother's hands kneading salt into the doorframe each autumn, murmuring the warding rhyme wrong on purpose because the correct version scared the neighbors.*

*Sera, twenty-two, falling asleep in the library with her cheek on an open grimoire, ink on her nose, and young Casper standing over her with a blanket he was too much of a coward to actually put on her shoulders.*

*The first time he called lightning. Sixteen years old, terrified, triumphant, hair standing straight up, and Maren screaming with laughter from the porch: "You look like a startled cat!"*

Each one left cleanly. That was the strange mercy of it—no tearing, no ache of removal. They simply *went*, poured out smooth as oil, and the garden drank them, and the hum grew richer, and the green spread in widening rings through the snow. Crocuses became drifts of crocuses. The nearest dead tree woke with a creaking groan and unfurled three pale blossoms, blinking like something surfacing from a long dive.

But Casper noticed, somewhere around the fortieth memory, that the gaps were starting to show.

He reached for his sister's laugh and found the shape of it without the sound. He reached for the library and found the room but not why it mattered. It was like walking through his own house in the dark and finding doors missing from walls he'd known his whole life. Not painful. Just—absent. Rooms he'd furnished himself, suddenly empty, and no memory of ever carrying furniture.

*Does it hurt?* Gideon asked. The boy's color had come back—rosy cheeks, solid hands, snow melting where he knelt instead of budding—and he was watching Casper with an intensity that had nothing childish in it. *You've gone quiet in places. I can feel where you were.*

"It doesn't hurt," Casper said honestly. "That's the eerie part. It's like... paying with coins you can't remember earning. The purse gets lighter and lighter and you keep thinking, any minute now I'll feel poor." He paused. "Ask me what my sister looked like."

Gideon's expression crumpled. *I won't. I'm sorry. I'm so sorry—I didn't know it would take like this, I thought you'd keep the shape of them even if—*

"Gideon." Casper caught the boy's chin gently. "Look at me. Look at what you're sitting in."

The boy looked. The courtyard had gone mad with spring. Green ran in rivers through the snow. The dead trees were waking one by one, a slow ripple of buds and blossom moving outward from the stone like a shout traveling through a crowd. The air smelled—Casper drank it in greedily, cataloguing it, because soon he might not know to—like rain on warm earth, like the inside of a greenhouse, like every April he'd ever survived.

"This," Casper said. "This is what the coins bought. I'd pay it twice. I'd pay it every year for the rest of my life, if the rest of my life were long enough, which—" he glanced at the thinning stack of memories behind his ribs, "—it may not be. So stop apologizing and hold out your hands. We're not done. Not nearly done."

*What's left?* Gideon whispered.

Casper considered. He went rummaging through himself like a man checking pockets before laundry, and what he found frightened him a little, and moved him more.

"The big ones are left," he said. "The heavy soil. The fall of Amsel. The night I broke the stone. The reason I did it." He met the boy's eyes. "Those are the richest beds I've got, love. Guilt makes good compost. Ask any gardener."

*Casper.* Gideon's hands tightened on his. *If you pour those out—if you forget what you did, why you did it—*

"Then I'll be a man who wakes up in a garden with no idea how he got there," Casper said. "And everyone will tell him stories, and some of the stories will be kind, and none of them will be mine." He shrugged, and found he was smiling, and found the smile was real. "You know what I've learned in ten years of selling luck-knots to people who don't believe in luck? Nobody remembers their happiest days correctly anyway. Memory's a liar even when you keep it. At least this way the lying does some good."

He kissed the top of the boy's head—salt and snow and, beneath it, unmistakably, the smell of growing things—and pressed their joined hands back to the stone.

"Now," Casper said. "Hold on to something. This next part is heavy."

* * *

He gave it the night of the breaking first, because it seemed only fair to lead with his crime.

*Rain that fell upward. The council chamber lit blue by the covenant stone's dying song. His own hands—younger, steadier than he'd ever managed since—pressed to the crack he'd made, feeling the covenant scream through his bones as the tether between realms tore. The elders shouting. The choice that had been given to him, the only mortal ever offered it: one life freely given from each realm, every hundred years, forever—or the door between worlds sealed, and both realms left to fend alone. And Casper, twenty-three years old and certain as only the young are certain, refusing. Refusing to feed the stone anyone. Refusing to choose whose child. Breaking it instead, and calling the storm to hide the sound.*

It poured out of him and the garden *roared*—not with wind but with growth, a sound like a thousand green throats opening at once. Trees that had been dead for a decade exploded into blossom. The two halves of the covenant stone slid toward each other across the snow, grinding, seeking, and where they touched, light ran up the seam like sap.

And Casper felt the hole where that memory had been, vast and clean, and discovered something unexpected in it: relief. Ten years of guilt, and now the guilt was *gone*—not forgiven, not resolved, just gone, paid out into the roots like poison drawn from a wound. He almost laughed. Was this what peace felt like? Was this what everyone else had been carrying around lightly all along?

Then he gave it the rest. The reason. The face of the girl the elders had chosen from his own village—Maren's friend, fourteen years old, who had volunteered with terrifying serenity because she believed, because they'd all been taught to believe. The argument Casper had lost. The door he'd broken rather than let it open. The first snow falling on green fields as the covenant died, and the long winter beginning, and every year since, both realms slowly freezing, because a door cut both ways and he'd slammed it without asking what the house would do for warmth.

He gave it all. He held nothing back. When it was done he knelt in a courtyard drowning in spring, in a silence broken only by bees—*bees*, actual bees, arriving from nowhere, because the world abhors a vacancy—and tried to remember his own name.

"Cas..." he said. And then, honestly, cheerfully, terribly: "Huh."

The man who had broken the covenant was gone. The man who had spent ten years hiding in Vell was gone. Maren, Sera-young, the kitchen, the storm-calling, the guilt, the shame, the exile—all of it, poured out, rooted, blooming. What remained was a kind stranger kneeling in a miraculous garden, holding the hands of a boy he loved with a love that had somehow survived the erasure, anchored deeper than anything else in him, the last root of the last tree standing.

*Gideon,* he said, and the name came easily, sweetly, the one coin left in the purse. "Your name is Gideon. I know that. I know I'm glad you're here. I know—" he looked around at the impossible spring, at the humming stone knitting itself whole, at the boy glowing like a lantern, "—I know we did something good. Whatever it was. I can feel it in my knees."

Gideon was crying. Solid now, rosy, real, ten years old and entirely present, and crying, and laughing, and hugging Casper hard enough to creak ribs.

*Thank you,* the boy said into his shoulder, in the First Tongue, in plain wordless love, in every language at once. *Oh, thank you, thank you—*

"Don't mention it," Casper said. "Literally. I won't know what you're talking about."

And then the gates burst open, and the storm above them finally broke, and Sera Vane staggered into the courtyard trailing blood and snow and Warden steel, with the entire Pale Company coming through the breach behind her like winter's last argument—

—and the garden turned, all of it, every tree and blossom and bee, to look at what had dared follow the spring inside.

Casper stood up, brushed the snow from his knees, and smiled at the oncoming army with the serene, empty, utterly fearless smile of a man who has already given away everything they could possibly take from him.

"Well," he said. "Friends of yours, Gideon?"
