## 19. What Casper Forgot

Sera Vane had spent her whole life learning how to hold a sword, and in the space of one morning she learned that she had never once known what to do with her hands.

The storm was still dying around them. It came apart slowly, the way a held breath comes apart—not all at once but in shivers, the great white walls of it thinning into ribbons, the ribbons into nothing. Through the gaps fell light. Not winter light, which Sera had come to think of as a kind of polite apology, but real light, gold and thick as honey, pouring down through the frost-dead branches of the Garden of Amsel.

And under the light, green.

It came up through the snow like something rising from sleep. First a haze of color along the ground, so faint Sera thought her eyes were failing—ten years of Pale Warden service would do that to anyone's eyes—but then a shoot actually *moved*, uncoiling past her boot with an audible whisper, and she stumbled backward and nearly sat down in what had been, until roughly a minute ago, a battlefield.

She looked at the soldiers. There were perhaps forty of them left standing at the gates, and every single one of them had stopped. Captain Hesse's sword arm hung slack. A young corporal near the front had dropped his pike entirely and was staring at a patch of snowdrops opening six feet away from him with the expression of a man watching his own mother walk out of a grave.

"Nobody," Sera said quietly, "moves."

Nobody moved. She wasn't sure she could have enforced the order if they'd tried. You could fight a storm. You could fight a man, even a storm-caller, because a man bled and tired and made mistakes. But this—this slow green tide coming up through the ruins of the oldest place in either realm, this smell of wet earth where there should have been only ice—this was not a thing you fought. This was a thing you stood very still in front of, the way you stand still in front of something holy, or something grieving, and hope it decides you're harmless.

At the center of it all, at the broken covenant stone, Casper Dunmore lay on his back in the melting snow with his eyes open and empty as two cups.

Sera ran to him. She didn't decide to run; her legs simply went, forty years of discipline dissolving into one undignified sprint through the mud that had been permafrost since before she was born. She skidded down beside him, knees hitting the wet earth, and gathered his head into her lap, and his hair was full of melted snow and he was breathing, thank every patient god, he was breathing, slow and deep, like a man asleep in summer.

"Casper." Her voice cracked on his name. She hadn't said it aloud in ten years. It fit strangely in her mouth, like a key kept for a door that no longer existed. "Casper, look at me."

His eyes found her face. They were gray eyes, storm-colored, the same eyes that had glared at her across a training yard when he was nine years old and furious about being small. But there was no fury in them now. There was no history in them at all. They were looking at her the way you look at weather—attentive, mild, without a single memory behind them.

"You're crying," he said. His voice was hoarse, scraped raw by the storm. He sounded puzzled about it, gently puzzled, the way he'd once been puzzled by arithmetic. "Why are you crying? Are you hurt?"

Sera laughed. It came out of her like something breaking.

"No," she managed. "No, I'm not hurt. Are you?"

He considered this with visible care, the way he considered everything now, she would learn. "I don't know," he said honestly. "I don't know my name."

* * *

Later—and Sera would never be able to say how much later, because time in the Garden had gone strange, moving like honey off a spoon—Gideon came and knelt on Casper's other side.

The boy was changed. Sera could see it even through her tears. He had arrived at the Garden a mute scrap of a child, barefoot in the snow, all elbows and watchfulness. The child kneeling in the mud now was still small, still thin, but he seemed somehow *planted*, rooted, as though the ground beneath him had decided he belonged to it and it to him. Green things grew in a ring around his knees. When he laid his scarred palm flat against Casper's chest, the sigil on his skin—the garden-mark, the old order's sign that Sera had burned into her memory the day she took her Warden oath—glowed faintly, warm as a banked coal.

"Gideon," the boy said.

It was the first word Sera had ever heard him speak. Not First Tongue. Plain, accented, slightly crooked Common, the way a child says a name he has practiced.

"He remembers that," Gideon told her, in the same careful voice. "He kept that. He gave everything else away, but he kept me."

Sera looked down at Casper's face. "Is that true?" she asked him. "Do you know who he is?"

Casper's brow furrowed. Then, slowly, like sun coming over a ridge, he smiled—the old smile, the lopsided one, the one that had gotten him slapped by three different instructors and forgiven by all of them.

"Gideon," he said, trying the name out. And then again, softer, certain: "Gideon. Yes. I don't know how I know that. But I know it the way I know my hands are mine." He reached up—one hand trembling, a storm-caller's hand that had just held back a company of Wardens and could no longer remember why—and touched the boy's cheek. "Hello," he said. "You're cold. Why is everyone letting you be cold?"

"He's not—" Sera started.

But Gideon was already laughing, a bright startled sound, and pulling off—of all things—his own threadbare coat to drape it over Casper's shoulders, and Sera understood that some questions answered themselves.

* * *

The Wardens withdrew at noon.

Sera walked to the gates to see them off, because someone had to, and because Captain Hesse deserved to look a person in the eye while he did whatever he was going to do. She expected arrest. She had killed two of his soldiers the night before—Oren and Brandt, good men, men whose names she would carry in her chest for the rest of her life alongside the knowledge of what she'd done to stop them burning a village that had sheltered a child. She expected irons, or worse.

Hesse stood at the gate with his company drawn up behind him, and he looked older than he had four days ago. They all did. Something about watching spring happen in real time will age a person.

"Vane," he said.

"Captain."

He looked past her shoulder, at the Garden. At the trees—frost-dead for a hundred years—putting out buds even now, tight green fists of them, hundreds along every branch. At the grass knee-high where the courtyard had been. At the fountain running, actually running, water sound carrying across the ruined paths like music from another country.

"The orders say we level it," Hesse said. "Burn it, salt it, take the child and the traitor south in chains." He said it flatly, the way a man reads out a list he already knows is nonsense. "Those were the Lord Warden's orders, given under seal, before any of us saw... this."

"Yes," Sera said.

"I've written my report." He handed her a folded paper, sealed with the company's mark. "You're welcome to read it. Most of it's true. The storm destroyed the company's supplies. We lost too many men to press further north. The Garden was found already dead, as reported. Nothing living here worth the march home." His jaw worked. "I'm too old to write down that I stood in front of a miracle and drew steel on it. Some things a man keeps private, even from himself."

Sera took the paper. Her hands were shaking. "And me?"

"And you're dead," Hesse said. "Killed in the storm, defending your prisoners' escape. It's cleaner that way. The Lord Warden doesn't forgive, and I don't intend to spend my retirement wondering whether you survived my mercy." He paused. "Your mother would have approved of the lie. She lied beautifully."

Sera's breath caught. "You knew my mother?"

"Everyone knew Ilse Vane." For the first time, something moved behind the captain's granite face—something almost like a smile. "She'd have liked the boy. She always said the covenant would grow back someday, like weeds through flagstones. Everyone laughed at her." He turned, then stopped. "Vane. If you see Dunmore—tell him the debt between your houses is paid. Tell him Hesse said so. He'll know what it means, if any of him is left that knows things."

"He doesn't," Sera said quietly. "Know things. Anymore."

Hesse was silent a moment. Then: "Then tell him anyway. Memory's a strange country. Sometimes things get delivered late."

He saluted—actually saluted her, which by every regulation in the Pale Wardens was absurd, she was a dead traitor—and marched his company south through the melting snow, and Sera stood at the gates of the Garden of Amsel and watched the last army of the long winter walk out of her life.

* * *

The first weeks, Casper was like a traveler in his own body.

He knew how to do things without knowing that he knew them. Sera watched him whittle a luck-knot from a piece of pine one evening—quick, sure strokes, the pattern perfect—and then stare at the finished charm in his palm with frank bewilderment, turning it over as though it might explain itself.

"I've seen these," he said. "In the city. People sold them. Little knots, for luck." He frowned. "I don't know why my hands wanted to make one."

"You used to sell them," Sera said. "In Vell. Before."

"Did I?" He smiled at her, easy and untroubled, and the smile went through her like a blade, because it was the same smile and there was nothing behind it, no ten years of hiding, no guilt, no weight. He was lighter than she had ever known him. He was lighter than a man had any right to be. "Was I any good at it?"

"You were terrible at lying," Sera said, "and excellent at knots. It worked out."

He laughed, delighted, as if she'd told him a story about a stranger. In a way, she supposed, she had.

Gideon, meanwhile, was everywhere. The boy rose before dawn and walked the Garden's paths, and where he walked, things grew—not frantically, not miraculously anymore, but steadily, the way a garden grows when someone loves it. He carried water from the running fountain in a rusted old can. He talked constantly, this child who had been mute, words tumbling out of him in a stream—half Common, half First Tongue, switching between languages mid-sentence without noticing, narrating everything: the robin building a nest in the broken bell tower, the shape of tomorrow's clouds, the exact depth at which a certain seed wanted to be buried. Casper followed him around like a large, amiable dog, hauling and digging and asking questions, endlessly, happily, questions a child might ask. *What is this plant called? Why does bread rise? Was I ever young?*

That last one he asked Sera directly, one evening by the fire they'd built in the ruin of the old refectory, while Gideon slept curled against Casper's side like a cat against a warm stone.

"Were you ever young?" Sera repeated.

"Mm. Gideon talks about 'before.' He remembers a garden, he says, from before he was born. Blood-memory, he calls it." Casper poked the fire. Sparks climbed. "I don't seem to have one. I woke up on a stone with a boy's name in my mouth and a woman crying over me, and that's the whole of it. Everything before is just..." He gestured vaguely at the dark beyond the firelight. "Weather."

"Do you mind?" Sera asked. She had to know. She had lain awake three nights running wondering. "What was taken from you. Do you grieve it?"

Casper thought about it for a long time. That was another thing about him now—he never rushed an answer. The man she remembered had answered everything fast, glib, deflecting with jokes. This one let silence sit until it ripened.

"I feel like a house after a flood," he said finally. "Everything's gone, and the walls are still standing, and it smells strange, and I keep finding marks on the doorframes where somebody measured a child's height, year after year, and I don't know who the child was. But the floors are clean." He looked at her. "I know that sounds mad."

"It doesn't," Sera said.

"And I know—" He stopped. Started again. "This is the part I can't explain. I know I did something. Not what. But the shape of it. Like a missing tooth—you feel the gap with your tongue all day. Whatever I gave away, I gave it away *on purpose*. Nobody takes a whole life from a man without his help." He was quiet a moment. "And I know it was worth it. I know that the way I know Gideon's name. All the way down. Does that make sense?"

Sera looked at him—at the man who had broken a covenant and doomed two realms to winter, who had then spent ten years selling luck-knots and flinching at shadows, who had crossed half a frozen world carrying a child on his back, who had poured everything he was into the roots of a dying garden so that a stranger's son could live—and felt grief and love arrive together, inseparable, like twins.

"It makes sense," she said.

* * *

On the morning the first apple tree bloomed, Sera found him standing under it in the grey-pink light, looking up into the branches with an expression she recognized from somewhere she couldn't name.

"You're crying again," he observed, without turning around. "You do that a lot. I've decided not to take it personally."

"You shouldn't," Sera said. Her voice was wrecked. She cleared her throat and failed to fix it. "It's the tree. My mother had one like it. Had a theory about it. She said—" and here the words caught, because she had not spoken of her mother aloud in twenty years, and here was this hollowed-out man giving her back the permission to, simply by having forgotten everything she'd ever needed forgiveness for— "she said the covenant wasn't a wall. Everyone taught it was a wall, a bargain, a fence between realms. She said it was a tree. Cut it down and it grows from the root. Burn the field and the seed waits. She said you can't kill a living promise, you can only starve it, and starving takes both sides agreeing to be hungry." She wiped her face with her sleeve, gracelessly. "Everyone laughed at her too."

"Smart woman," Casper said. "Both of you, laughing at."

"She'd have liked you," Sera said. "Before, I mean. The you that was."

"The you that was," Casper mused. "You talk about him like a cousin who emigrated. Was he awful?"

"He was the most infuriating person I ever met," Sera said. "He broke every rule he was given and half the ones he invented. He argued with masters who could have frozen him solid. He cried at other people's funerals and laughed at his own dangers. He broke the world, Casper. And then—" her breath hitched— "and then he spent ten years trying to mend it, alone, in the dark, without telling a soul why, and he never once let himself be forgiven for it, because he didn't think he'd earned it yet."

Casper was quiet. Under the apple tree, petals were beginning to come loose, drifting down through the early light like slow white rain.

"That sounds exhausting," he said at last.

"It was," Sera said. "Watching him was."

And Casper Dunmore—who had been a storm-caller, a breaker, a penitent, a ghost, and was now none of those things, only a man standing under a blossoming tree with a boy's name in his keeping—reached out and took her hand, the way he must have done a thousand times before, in a life neither of them owned anymore.

"Well," he said. "He's not here anymore. So whoever I am now gets to start clean. And whoever you are now—" he squeezed her fingers, gentle and certain— "gets to stop watching. What do you say we plant something?"

Behind them, across the waking garden, Gideon was calling their names for breakfast, and the sound carried on air that smelled, for the first time in a hundred years, of apples.
