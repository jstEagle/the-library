## 20. Spring, Unremembered

The garden wakes Gideon before the sun does.

It is not a sound or a light, exactly. It is a pulling, like a hand around his finger, like the way the warm one used to hold his wrist when they crossed the ice fields and Gideon's feet went sleepy in the snow. The garden holds him that way all night now, roots wrapped soft around his sleeping mind, and when it wants him up it simply *tugs*.

He goes. He always goes. There is no part of him that minds.

Outside the door of their little stone house — Sera calls it a house, though it was once a gardener's hut with a collapsed roof that Sera fixed by shouting at it for two days — the morning is green in a way Gideon still cannot stop looking at. Green on the trees. Green on the paths. Green pushing up between the flagstones like small hands waving hello. For ten years this place was white. For ten years the frost sat on everything like a lid on a pot, and underneath, the garden dreamed of him without knowing his name.

Now it knows his name. Now everything here knows his name, and says it back to him in growing things.

*Gideon,* says the birch row by the east wall, unfurling leaves one at a time, careful as old men counting coins.

*Gideon,* says the moss on the covenant stone, which is not broken anymore but whole and humming, warm to the touch the way bread is warm.

*Gideon,* says the first strawberry flower, very shyly, because it is new and does not want to seem proud.

He walks the paths barefoot. Shoes are a thing he has tried. Sera found him a pair in the ruins of the warden camp, stiff brown things with buckles, and he wore them for half a morning out of politeness before leaving them neatly by the door forever. The paths are his skin. He can feel where the frost still hides, deep down under the oldest rose bed, a cold knot like a swallowed pebble, and he kneels there and puts both palms flat and *pushes*, gently, the way you push warmth into a shivering bird.

The knot loosens. Somewhere under the soil, something exhales.

"Talking to the roses again?" Sera's voice comes from the gate, rough as gravel and twice as comfortable. She leans there with her arms crossed, sword at her hip even though there has been nothing to fight since the snow turned. She says she keeps it out of habit. Gideon thinks she keeps it the way other people keep a blanket from childhood — not useful, just *hers*.

He signs to her: *Good morning. You slept badly.*

"I slept fine."

He signs: *You said names in your sleep. Sad ones.*

Sera looks at him for a long moment, the way she does when he sees too much, which is often, because seeing too much is most of what he does. Then she shrugs. "Old debts," she says. "Nothing the garden can't grow over." And she comes down the path to stand beside him, and together they watch the sun come up over the wall where last winter the wardens breached, and where now a wisteria vine has climbed the broken stones so thickly that you would have to know exactly where to look to find the scar.

Gideon knows exactly where to look. He touches it every morning anyway. Not to heal it. Just to say: *I remember you were broken, and I am glad you are not.*

* * *

The warm one comes out mid-morning, as he always does, wearing an expression Gideon has decided to call *brave confusion*.

Casper Dunmore — though he does not know that name anymore; Sera uses it only when she thinks Gideon isn't listening, softly, like setting a candle in a window — stands in the kitchen doorway holding a wooden spoon like it might be a weapon or a snake. Flour dusts his sleeves. There is flour also on his forehead, and on one ear, and, inexplicably, on the ceiling.

"Gideon," he says, "I have a question about bread."

Gideon signs: *Ask.*

"The book says let it rise." Casper holds up the book, which is one of three salvaged from the gardener's hut, its pages soft as cloth. "It has been rising since dawn. It has risen past the bowl. It has risen onto the counter. I believe it intends to leave."

Gideon looks into the kitchen. The dough has indeed climbed the bowl's rim and is advancing across the table in a slow pale tide, and Gideon feels the yeast inside it, tiny lives doing exactly what tiny lives do, and he starts laughing — silent, shaking laughter, his shoulders going like birds taking off.

"It's not funny," Casper says, fighting a smile and losing. "This is the fourth time. The bread and I are enemies now. We have a feud."

Gideon signs: *You must punch it down. Like this.* He mimes it: fists, folding, patience.

"Punch it down." Casper regards the escaping dough with narrowed eyes. "You're telling me bread requires violence."

*Bread requires faith,* Gideon signs, *and then violence, and then more faith. Like most living things.*

Casper stares at him. "Where do you *learn* these sentences?" he demands. "You're seven. You're possibly eight. Nobody knows. You sign like a judge."

But he punches down the dough, and folds it, and sets it back in the bowl with a clean cloth over it, and then he crouches down next to Gideon and watches it with the total concentration of a man who has forgotten everything else in the world except whether bread will rise. Which, Gideon supposes, is true. That is exactly who Casper is now. A man with one memory and infinite room, learning the world like a second childhood, asking questions all day long —

— *why does the river go that way, why do bees sleep in flowers, why does Sera hum when she sharpens her sword if she says she doesn't sing* —

— and Gideon answers every one, because answering them is the best thing in his life. Every question Casper asks is a question the world gets asked twice: once by the man, and once by the garden through the man, and the answers come out greener for it.

There is one question Casper never asks. Gideon waits for it some mornings, watching him across breakfast, watching the empty places behind his eyes where ten years of storms used to live. But Casper never asks *who was I*. He wakes, and he sees Gideon, and he says the only word he kept — "*Gideon*" — like a man saying grace, and then he gets up and burns the porridge, and that is the whole of his history, and he seems — this is the strange part, the part Gideon turns over at night like a smooth stone —

— he seems *light*.

Not hollow. Light. Like a tree after pruning. Like the garden itself, which lost everything and came back smaller and stranger and more alive than the old stories say it ever was.

* * *

Afternoons are for lessons, though nobody agrees on who is teaching whom.

Sera teaches letters, using a stick and the packed earth of the south path, because paper is precious and dirt is free. Gideon already knows letters — they live in him the way rivers live in valleys — but he sits through the lessons anyway because Sera needs to teach something, and teaching keeps her shadows quiet.

"B," Sera writes. "Say it."

Gideon says nothing, because he says nothing, ever, anywhere, and everyone has stopped expecting otherwise. Instead he traces the letter larger beside hers, and adds a small drawing of a bee, because B should be allowed to be what it is.

"That's cheating," Sera says.

He signs: *Teaching is better with pictures.*

"Says the boy who taught me eleven kinds of leaf-language last week." But she draws a bee too. Hers has an angry face. Gideon decides not to take it personally.

Then it is Casper's turn, and Casper's lessons are the ones the whole garden attends. He is learning plants — their names, their tempers, which ones sulk and which ones forgive — and he learns them the way he learns everything, with his whole body leaning forward, kneeling in the dirt, getting soil under his nails and not caring, this man who was once (Sera says) so afraid of his own hands that he kept them gloved for a decade.

"This one?" Casper asks, pointing.

*Amsel lavender,* Gideon signs. *It only grows here. It remembers being sung to.*

"Everything here remembers being sung to," Casper mutters, but he cups the lavender bloom in both palms, careful as an egg, and the lavender — Gideon feels it happen — leans into his hands. Trusts him. All of them trust him, the plants, and none of them know why, and Gideon will never tell him why, because some soils are better left unplowed.

What Gideon knows, and carries alone, is this: the memories Casper poured into the roots did not vanish. They became the garden. The storm-caller's childhood is in the apple rows. His sister's laugh is somewhere in the beehives — the bees are unusually happy, and hum in a key no other hive in the world uses. The long terrible years in Vell, the charm-knots and the numb fingers and the fear — those went deep, deepest of all, down where the frost used to hide, and that is why nothing freezes here anymore. His worst winters became the garden's warm floor.

Once, Gideon tried to explain this to Sera. He signed: *He is not gone. He is everywhere. You are standing in him.*

Sera had gone very still, and then she had knelt down and put her bare hand flat on the grass, and stayed there a long while, and afterward she was easier in her body, the way people get when a debt is forgiven. She never spoke of it again. But she stopped wearing boots in the garden, too.

* * *

Evenings, they eat together at the table under the wisteria, because the weather is kind now and the roof leaks in one spot and eating outside means nobody has to fix it yet.

Tonight Casper has made bread that stayed in the bowl, and he presents it like a king presenting a treaty. "Behold," he says. "Bread. Ordinary bread. Bread that obeys."

Gideon applauds silently. Sera tears off a hunk, chews, and delivers her verdict with the gravity of a magistrate: "Edible."

"*Edible?*"

"The highest praise I give," Sera says. "Ask anyone I've ever served with."

"You've never once told me anything nice."

"I'm telling you the bread is edible in front of witnesses. In the old company that's practically a marriage proposal."

Casper laughs — full and easy, a laugh with no shadow in it, and Gideon drinks the sound the way the garden drinks rain. This is another thing he will never tell anyone: how much of the old Casper survives in the new one. The kindness was never in the memories. It was deeper than memory, down where memory can't reach, and so it stayed. The man forgot his sins and kept his goodness. The garden got the rest.

After supper, Gideon makes his rounds.

This is his true work, and he loves it the way some children love running downhill. He walks every path before dark, palm trailing the low hedge, checking each bed the way a shepherd counts sheep. The plum trees by the gate: settled, dreaming. The herb spiral: quarrelsome as always, the mint bullying the thyme, needing a word. The young oak over the covenant stone: growing too fast, hungry, wanting to be a forest all by itself tonight — Gideon tells it *patience*, and it grumbles, and settles.

And at the far corner, behind the old well, the newest bed: the one Sera planted without telling anyone, from seeds she must have carried in her pocket through all those white miles, through all those orders she pretended to follow. Small blue flowers are coming up there. Gideon doesn't know their name. Neither does Sera. They grew from seeds she took from her mother's grave, on the night she stopped pretending, and they are the only things in the whole garden that came from outside it.

He kneels by them. They lean toward him like children toward a fire.

*You're safe,* he tells them. *Nobody erases anything here. That's the whole rule. It's the only rule.*

Behind him, footsteps — Sera, making her evening circuit of the wall, sword bumping her hip. She stops when she sees him there. For a moment neither moves. Then she crouches beside him, awkward as a soldier ever gets, and looks at her mother's flowers glowing faint blue in the dusk.

"They took," she says quietly. Wondering.

Gideon nods, and takes her hand, and presses it — gently, gently — into the soil beside the blooms, so she can feel what he feels: the roots threading down, down, past the frost-line that no longer exists, into the warm floor of a dead man's given-away life, drinking light.

"He'd have liked them," Sera says. Her voice does something complicated on the word *liked*.

Gideon signs with his free hand: *He likes them. He just doesn't know it's them.*

Sera huffs — almost a laugh — and squeezes his shoulder, and rises, and continues her rounds, and Gideon stays a moment longer in the blue dusk with the flowers that remember a woman he never met.

That is when he feels it.

Far off. North and east, past the mountains, past the edge of everything the garden's roots can whisper to. A stirring in the wide world — not danger, not quite, but *motion*, many feet, many wheels, a ripple moving along the roads the way the first thaw-water moves under ice. Travelers. Dozens of them. Drawed by something, following something — a story, spreading from mouth to mouth like spores on wind:

*a garden appeared where the map says ruin. the winter ended overnight and no one knows why. there is a child there. there is a spring that heals. go and see.*

Gideon stands slowly. The garden feels it too — every leaf turned slightly, like ears.

From the doorway, Casper calls, cheerful, oblivious, flour still on his ear: "Gideon! Is the bread supposed to make a second loaf? Because it's doing it again!"

Gideon looks north, where the road will soon fill with strangers carrying cuttings away in their pockets and songs away in their mouths, carrying the garden out into a world that will never know what it owes or to whom.

He smiles, and turns toward the house, toward the warm one and the runaway bread, and lifts his hands to answer.
