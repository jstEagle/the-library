## 21. The Name He Kept

Casper woke the way he always woke now: slowly, like bread rising.

There was a moment—every morning, without fail—when he surfaced from sleep and did not know where he was. The ceiling above him was woven willow, green shoots still curling at the edges because the tree that had given its branches had been alive when Sera cut them, and apparently it had opinions about being furniture. Light came through the leaves in small gold coins. Somewhere below, water moved over stone, and birds were arguing about something with great conviction.

And then, as it did every morning, the knowing arrived. Not all at once. Just one thing, warm and certain as a hand around his own:

*Gideon.*

The boy's name came to him before his own did. It had been that way since the first morning, when he'd opened his eyes in a bed of new grass with no past at all and found a child sitting cross-legged beside him, watching him with the grave patience of someone who has waited out worse things than amnesia.

"Gideon," Casper said now, to the ceiling, just to hear it. His voice was rough with sleep. "Gideon, Gideon."

From somewhere down the garden path, faint but clear, came the boy's answer—not words, never words, but a low humming note that meant *I'm here, you're awake, the kettle's on.* Gideon had a whole language of hums. Casper had learned most of them. The one for *you slept too long again* was particularly pointed.

He got up. His body remembered things his mind didn't—the way his hands knew how to braid a luck-knot without instruction, the way his feet avoided the loose flagstone by the door even though he'd have sworn he'd never seen it before. His body was full of ghosts who knew the house better than he did. He'd stopped finding this frightening around the second month. Now it was mostly useful, occasionally funny, and once, memorably, the reason he'd burst into tears over a wooden spoon.

* * *

Sera was already at the table when he came down, which meant she'd been up since before dawn, which meant either she'd heard something on the wind or she simply didn't know how to rest yet. She sat with her back to the wall—she always sat with her back to the wall—and she was sharpening a knife she never used, which Casper had privately decided was less a weapon than a fidget.

"Morning," he said.

"Casper." She said his name carefully every time, like setting down something breakable. He'd told her, early on, that she didn't have to. She'd said nothing, and kept doing it.

"Anything interesting fall off the mountain?"

"A hawk." She nodded toward the window. "It's been circling the east hedge since yesterday. Gideon fed it."

"Of course he did." Casper poured himself tea from the kettle that was, miraculously, exactly hot enough. "We're going to have every animal in three valleys living here by midsummer. I give it a month before there's a badger at the table."

"There was a badger at the table Tuesday."

"That was a guest, Sera. There's a difference."

Something happened at the corner of her mouth—not quite a smile, but the place where smiles went to consider their options. Sera's smiles were like that. He'd learned not to rush them.

He sat across from her and drank his tea and looked out at the garden, because looking at the garden never got old. It had been snow, they told him. All of it, snow and frost-dead trees, for ten years and more. Now the paths were green and the old trees had come back from whatever place trees go when they die, unfurling leaves like people stretching after a long sleep. Fruit hung heavy on branches that had been bare longer than Gideon had been alive. In the afternoons the whole valley smelled of blossom and warm earth, and Casper would stand in it with his tea and feel a happiness so large it frightened him a little, the way deep water frightens non-swimmers.

He didn't remember making any of this happen. They told him he had. He believed them the way you believe a story about someone else.

* * *

After breakfast, Gideon took him by the hand—which was ritual now, non-negotiable, the boy's fingers small and dirt-crusted and absolutely certain—and led him out to the beds.

This was the other half of every morning: lessons. Except the lessons ran backward. Gideon taught him.

Today it was seeds. Gideon pressed a flat of them into Casper's hands and then stood back, arms folded, wearing an expression of such stern pedagogical authority on a face still round with childhood that Casper had to bite the inside of his cheek not to laugh.

"Right," Casper said. "Seeds. I know seeds. Pointy end down?"

Gideon shook his head slowly, with pity.

"No? I could have sworn—"

Another headshake. Then the boy knelt, took one seed between finger and thumb, and held it up to the light between them, turning it, showing Casper how it caught the sun. Then he tapped his own chest. Then the soil. Then, gently, he closed Casper's fingers around the seed and looked up at him with eyes gone suddenly, unbearably serious.

Casper felt the meaning land somewhere below thought. *You don't put it in the ground like a thing. You put it in like a piece of yourself.*

"Ah," he said quietly. "Yes. All right."

They planted the whole flat that way, Gideon guiding his hands, and if Casper's eyes went damp over a tray of turnip seeds, well, the morning was dusty and nobody mentioned it.

* * *

In the afternoons, Sera told him stories.

That had started in the first weeks, when the gaps in him were canyons and he'd lie awake at night cataloguing everything he didn't know—his mother's face, whether he'd ever been married, what his laugh sounded like before. He'd asked her once, hesitantly, *who was I?* and she'd gone very still, and then she'd pulled her chair closer to the fire and said, "Let me tell you about a man I knew."

She edited. He figured that out fairly quickly—he wasn't stupid, whatever else he'd lost—but he never called her on it, because he understood what the editing was. Once, she described how this man she'd known had once charmed an entire market square into believing it was raining, to cover a friend's escape, and Casper had laughed and said, "He sounds exhausting," and Sera had looked at him with an expression he couldn't read and said, "He was," and later he'd found her outside standing in actual rain with her face turned up, and he'd decided some stories were better left unexamined.

Other times the editing went the other direction. She told him almost nothing about the north, about the winter, about the years before Vell. When he asked directly—*did I do something wrong? Did I hurt people?*—she was quiet for a long time, and then she said:

"You made a choice that cost you everything, to keep a promise most people would have run from. That's who you were. The rest is weather."

"The rest is weather," he repeated.

"The rest is weather," she confirmed, and refilled his cup, and that was the end of that, more or less.

He trusted her version. Not because it was complete—he suspected it wasn't—but because of the way she told it. People lie smoothly. Sera's stories had seams; she worried them like loose threads. A woman editing out your crimes tells you a clean legend. A woman editing out your pain tells you a careful one, and those are different animals entirely.

Besides, there was evidence. He'd found, months ago, tucked behind a beam in the workshop, a child's drawing in charcoal—a stick figure with wild hair holding hands with a smaller figure under a scribbled sun, and beneath it, in a wobbly adult hand, the word *GIDEON*. His handwriting, presumably. He'd shown it to Sera and watched her face do something complicated.

"He drew this?" Casper asked.

"You drew it together," she said. "Before."

"Before what?"

"Before you forgot how to be afraid," she said, and took the drawing, and put it somewhere safe, and that evening she told him a story about a man who'd once carried a sleeping child through a blizzard for two days with his coat wrapped around her and nothing around himself, and Casper had listened and thought: *that sounds like something I would do,* and hadn't known why he was so sure.

* * *

Evenings were the best part, though he'd have denied it under oath.

Gideon cooked—or rather, Gideon directed cooking, standing on a stool and issuing hummed instructions while Casper chopped and stirred and was corrected. The boy had strong opinions about salt. Casper burned things with enough regularity that Gideon had developed a specific sigh for it, a world-weary little exhale that belonged on a grandmother of eighty, and every time it happened Casper laughed so hard he had to sit down, and Gideon pretended to be annoyed, and the pretending lasted four seconds at most.

Tonight it was stew, and the badger—whose name was Duchess, because Gideon had communicated this through a complex mime involving a crown and much dignity—sat by the fire with the entitlement of royalty.

"Do you ever miss it?" Casper asked Sera, over the boy's head. "Before. Whatever it was."

Sera turned her knife over on the whetstone. "Sometimes I miss being sure of things," she said. "I was very sure, back then. Surety is cheap when you're wrong."

"And now?"

"Now I'm sure of exactly one thing." She glanced at Gideon, who was solemnly tasting the stew and issuing a hum of approval that made Casper absurdly proud. "And it sleeps upstairs and eats like a field army."

Casper smiled into his bowl. Outside, the garden breathed. Somewhere in the dark, fruit was ripening that had no business existing, in a valley that every map in the world still marked as dead.

* * *

He woke once, deep in the night, from a dream he couldn't hold on to—something vast and cold, a sound like a mountain cracking, a voice saying *the price, the price, someone must pay the price*—and lay in the dark with his heart going hard until he heard, through the floor, the small familiar sounds of Gideon turning over in his sleep, and the dream let go of him like a fist opening.

*It's all right,* he told himself. *Whatever it was, it's paid. Whatever it cost, somebody covered it.*

He didn't know how he knew that. He just did, the way he knew the boy's name, the way his hands knew knots. Some knowledge lived deeper than memory, in the marrow, where forgetting couldn't reach.

He slept again, and dreamed of nothing but green.

* * *

Three mornings later, Sera came in from the perimeter walk moving fast, and Casper knew before she spoke, because her hand was on her knife instead of her whetstone.

"Riders on the south road," she said. "A dozen or more. Not Wardens—no colors, no formation. Pilgrims, maybe. Or traders." Her jaw worked. "Or the first of something."

Gideon appeared between them, silent as always, and looked up the path toward the gate, and then did something he almost never did: he smiled. Wide and certain, like a child hearing sleigh bells.

Casper looked at Sera. Sera looked at Casper.

"Well," Casper said, setting down his tea. "I suppose we'd better find out who's coming to dinner."
