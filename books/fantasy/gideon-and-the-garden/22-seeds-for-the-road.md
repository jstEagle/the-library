## 22. Seeds for the Road

The first traveler came in the month of thaw, when the roads were still mud to the ankle and every cart in the lowlands complained its way north.

She was a tinker by trade, a broad-shouldered woman with a mended hat and a donkey named Obstinate, and she swore afterward — in every tavern from Vell to the river Marn — that she had not been looking for anything at all. She had been lost. That was her whole story: *I was lost, and then there was a garden.*

Sera Vane watched her come up the mountain path from the wall of the old gatehouse, one hand resting on the pommel of a sword she had not drawn since the snows broke.

"She's alone," Sera said.

"She's always alone," Casper said beside her. He was carrying a basket of early peas he had picked that morning with the intense concentration of a man performing surgery on each pod. "Everyone who finds this place is alone. That's how it works. You get lost, and the garden decides you're worth finding."

"You say that like it's doctrine."

"It is doctrine. Gideon taught it to me." He said it without bitterness, which was itself a kind of miracle, and Sera had learned over the long spring to stop flinching at the shape of what he'd lost. He did not remember teaching anyone anything. He did not remember the storm he had raised at the gates, or the stone he had broken, or the sister whose face he had traded for a river crossing. He remembered bread rising. He remembered where the sweet soil was deepest. And every morning, before his eyes were fully open, he said the name *Gideon* the way other men said their own.

Below them, the tinker reached the garden wall — a wall of frost-dead trees that had spent the winter breathing and now, in the warm months, simply stood there being magnificent — and stopped. Sera saw the woman take off her mended hat. She saw her stand there a long moment with her hand over her mouth.

Then the garden opened a path for her. It did this for everyone. It was, Sera thought, showing off a little.

"Peas," Casper said, holding up the basket as if presenting evidence. "We should offer her peas."

"We should offer her tea first, like civilized people."

"Gideon says peas are more honest."

"Gideon," Sera said, "is four years old and would offer a visiting ambassador a worm."

"He'd be right to," said Casper, and went down through the trees with his basket, whistling a hymn he could not have learned anywhere except in a body that refused to forget what the mind had surrendered.

* * *

By midsummer there were eleven travelers living in and around the Garden of Amsel, though nobody called it that anymore. The tinker — whose name was Brava, and who had appointed herself mayor within a week of arriving because someone had to — called it "the green place," and the name stuck the way useful names do.

They came singly and in pairs. A stonemason whose hands had gone idle in a city with no work. Two sisters who had walked out of a village emptied by winter, following a rumor of a well that sang. An old soldier of the Pale Wardens who arrived shaking, expecting to be killed, and was instead handed a hoe by Gideon, who had opinions about weeds and no patience whatsoever for grown men standing around looking tragic.

That last one had taken some negotiating. Sera had stood in the gateway with her sword drawn for a full minute before Casper put his hand on her arm and said, gently, "You know him?"

"I know his face. I've given orders like the ones that brought him here."

"And now you give different ones."

"Now I give different ones," she agreed, and sheathed the blade, and the soldier wept into the hoe-handle while Gideon patted his boot with the solemnity of a small physician.

The garden gave freely, and asked only one thing back, and it never explained what the thing was; people simply understood it, the way you understand warmth. Take something growing. Carry it somewhere else. Planting is a promise.

Brava left in autumn with cuttings wrapped in wet cloth and a song she'd learned from the sisters — a round about a winter that ended, set to a tune no one could trace, in a language no one could read. She taught it in every tavern between the mountains and the sea. By the following spring, children in Vell were singing it in the streets, and the Wardens' new captain — a tired, decent man who had inherited a company and a great deal of institutional embarrassment — officially declared the song "folk material of unknown origin" and let it be.

Gideon heard about this secondhand, from a pilgrim, and laughed so hard he fell off the wall.

* * *

He was five now, or nearly — they counted his years from the night he knocked on a charm-seller's door speaking the First Tongue, because no one knew any other birthday and Casper insisted that the night the world knocked back was as good a beginning as any.

He had words now, hundreds of them, whole sentences in the common tongue, though he kept the First Tongue for important things: naming plants, greeting the dawn, telling Sera, once a year on a certain gray morning, the name of her mother. He still didn't remember where he came from. He remembered the garden the way you remember a lullaby — not the words, just the feeling of being held. Sera suspected, privately, that this was true of all of them now.

On the evening the story ends — though of course it doesn't, stories being seeds themselves — the household gathered in the kitchen of the rebuilt gatehouse, because Brava's cousin had sent word that a cutting planted outside Vell had flowered two seasons early, and because Casper had made bread, and because Gideon had announced at supper that he had Something To Give Everyone and would accept no postponement.

"Close your eyes," Gideon commanded. He stood on a stool so he could loom over the table, which mostly meant looming over the butter.

"We live in a magical garden," Sera said, closing her eyes obediently. "Whatever it is, I want it noted that I'm bracing."

"Noted," said Gideon, with tremendous dignity, and went around the table pressing things into palms.

Into Sera's hand: a seed shaped like a teardrop, warm as a held coin. "For the shrine," he said. "Where you stopped. So the next person who stops there stops for a good reason."

Sera opened her eyes. "How do you know about the shrine?"

"I know everything," said Gideon, moving on, which was his answer to most difficult questions and, annoyingly, usually correct.

Into the old soldier's hand: a seed like a grain of barley. "For your village. When you're ready to go back. They'll want to see something grow where nothing grew."

The soldier looked at it a long time. "And if they don't want me?"

"Then plant it anyway," Gideon said. "It's not for them. It's for the ground. The ground forgives faster than people. I've checked."

Into Casper's hand — and here Gideon paused, climbed down off the stool, and climbed up again directly in front of him, kneeling on the seat so their faces were level — Gideon placed a seed unlike the others. It was small and brown and utterly ordinary, the kind of thing you'd sweep off a threshing floor, and it sat in Casper's scarred palm glowing faintly, the way embers glow after the fire has gone to sleep.

"This one's yours," Gideon said. "Not for anywhere. For wherever you decide."

Casper turned it over with one finger. His hands, these days, were gardener's hands, blunt and dirt-lined, and the old scars across the knuckles had faded to pale threads. "I don't remember how to choose wrong," he said. "Is that strange? Everyone else seems to carry their mistakes around like stones in their pockets. Mine are just — gone. Sometimes I feel underdressed."

"That's not how it works," Gideon said seriously. "You didn't lose your mistakes. You *planted* them. Everything growing out there—" he waved at the window, at the dark garden full of breathing trees, "—grew out of something somebody gave up. That's what soil is. It's what people gave up so something else could start." He tapped the seed in Casper's palm. "So this isn't a memory. Memories run out. This is a promise. Promises keep working after you forget making them."

There was a silence at the table. Sera found she had to look very hard at her own seed.

"When," said Casper at last, "did you become wiser than everyone in this room?"

"Birth," said Gideon, and finally allowed himself to be fed bread and honey, which was the real ceremony underneath all the others.

* * *

Later, when the boy was asleep and the fire was banked, Sera and Casper sat on the gatehouse step with the last of the tea, watching the garden breathe in the dark.

"Do you ever miss it?" Sera asked. "Knowing?"

Casper considered this honestly, head tipped back toward the stars, which over Amsel seemed closer and more talkative than stars elsewhere.

"I miss knowing *you*," he said. "That's the strange part. Not the storms, not the — whatever I did. I look at you and I know we paid for something together, both of us, something enormous, and I can't see the receipt. But you look at me sometimes like I'm a book you've read twice, and I trust that." He rolled the seed between his fingers. "Besides. Gideon says I'll find out eventually. Everything planted comes up."

"He also says the moon is an egg."

"The moon might be an egg. We haven't checked."

Sera laughed — a real laugh, unguarded, the kind she had rationed for ten years and now spent freely — and pulled her cloak tighter against the mountain cool.

"There's a caravan forming in the valley," she said, after a while. "Brava's doing. People heading south with cuttings and songs, going home. Going *everywhere*, really. Half of them don't even know why they came here. They just got lost, and then they weren't."

"That's the whole covenant," Casper said. "As far as I understand it, which is not far. You get lost. Something finds you. You carry a piece of it away and promise to put it in the ground." He smiled. "Neither realm will ever remember what it owes us. Fine. Let it owe the road instead. Roads are better at keeping promises than kingdoms are."

Down in the dark garden, among the frost-dead trees that were neither dead nor frosty anymore, something rustled — not wind, exactly. More like a page turning. More like a very large, very patient thing settling comfortably into a story it intended to keep telling long after everyone currently in it had become soil themselves, and then flowers, and then songs, and then seeds in the palms of strangers on far roads.

In his palm, Casper's ordinary brown seed glowed softly, waiting to be promised somewhere.

He decided he would plant it in the morning, at the edge of the path where the travelers passed — so that everyone leaving would walk past it, and everyone arriving would too, and it would go on keeping its quiet vow to absolutely no one and everyone, season after season, longer than any memory, longer than any name.

Except one. There was always one name.

He went inside, past the sleeping boy's door, and said it softly, the way he said it every night, the way he would say it every morning of the rest of his life:

"Gideon."

And the garden, listening, grew.
