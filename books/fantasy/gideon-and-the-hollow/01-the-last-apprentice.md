## 1. The Last Apprentice

The fog on Thornfell came in like a debt collector: patient, thorough, and utterly indifferent to your excuses. By the time the moon cleared the eastern ridge it had swallowed the pines to the waist and turned the smuggler road into a rumor. Which was, of course, precisely how we liked it.

"Keep to my left shoulder," I told the Merrow brothers over my own shoulder, not turning around. "Not beside me. Not ahead of me. If you can see me clearly, you've wandered off the road, and if you can't see me at all, you've wandered considerably further."

"And if we see someone else?" Tam Merrow asked. He was the younger one, all elbows and appetite.

"You won't," I said. "That's rather the point of paying us."

Behind him his brother Brannic shifted a crate of unlicensed brandy and said nothing, which was why Brannic got the better rate. The Merrows were wool farmers with a taste for profit and no talent for silence, but they paid in coin that didn't ask questions, and in a year when the Wayfarers' Guild had dwindled from forty sworn guides to three — one of them old enough to remember when the roads had names instead of numbers — coin that didn't ask questions was its own kind of loyalty.

I put my hand to the stone marker at the trailhead, the one shaped like a kneeling shepherd, worn smooth by two centuries of guild palms, and felt for the hum.

Every guild road has a hum. That's not poetry; that's craft. The founders had keyed their waystones to a resonance — a low, warm thrum you feel in the heel of your hand more than hear — and each junction sang a different note. Learn the song and you could walk Thornfell blindfolded, drunk, or fleeing witch-hunters, which in a good year might be all three. Master Aldric used to say the roads were the only honest thing left in the province. Aldric said a great many things, most of them true, several of them treasonous, and all of them delivered with the serene confidence of a man who had outlived everyone qualified to argue.

The shepherd stone hummed true. Third interval, the Greymoss fork. Good.

We walked.

* * *

You should understand something about Thornfell before you judge what we did there. The mountain doesn't loom so much as lean — a vast grey shoulder of granite and pine draped in fog nine months of the year, riddled with hollows and sink-roads where the ground simply gave up pretending to be solid. The crown's maps show eleven roads through it. The crown's maps are wrong by a factor of roughly thirty, and the crown's mapmakers are welcome to keep being wrong until the sun burns out.

The guild kept the real roads. We kept them swept, marked, remembered. Farmers paid us to move goods the excise men would have taxed into poverty. Families paid us to move people — a cousin fleeing a debt, a daughter fleeing a marriage, once an entire choir fleeing a bishop with opinions about hymns. And lately, quietly, in whispers, people had begun paying us to move *gifted* ones. The ones who heard things, saw things, knew things they shouldn't. The ones the criers in Bellharrow had started calling abominations in the same breathless tone usually reserved for plague and tax season.

I didn't advertise that last service. Mostly because I was, myself, the thing being smuggled.

But we'll come to that.

An hour in, the fog thickened from soup to mortar, and I began the work that separated a guide from a corpse. Left hand trailing the low wall — dry-stacked, older than the kingdom, humming its steady second — right hand counting paces. Two hundred twelve to the split oak. Ninety to the cairn with the false top. The fog ate sound as well as light, so I'd taught the Merrows our signals: one tug on the rope means stop, two means duck, three means run and don't ask toward what.

At the split oak I paused, and that's when I heard him.

Not the hum. The hum is stone-deep, wordless, comfortable as a hearth. This was different. This was a voice, thin and frayed as an old blanket, rising out of the shepherd stone's memory the way heat rises off a summer road.

*Cold coming,* it said. *Cold coming down the high pass. Move the flock, girl. Move them before the white.*

I stopped walking. Tam walked into my back and swore with impressive creativity for a man who supposedly feared the dark.

"Problem?" Brannic asked.

"No," I lied, because the truth was complicated and none of their business. "Checking the marker."

The voice faded as voices do, leaving that particular ache behind — the ache of a door closing on warmth. I pressed my palm flat against the cold stone a moment longer than craft required. There was nothing more. There was rarely anything more. The dead in the stones spoke in scraps, in weather-warnings and name-fragments, echoes soaked so deep into the rock that even I couldn't tell whether I was hearing a ghost or just the shape a grief had left behind.

His name had been Hodd, I thought. Or Hodde. A shepherd, two hundred years dead, whose hands had shaped this very marker, and who had apparently spent his last living hours worrying about a cold snap, and whose worry had soaked so thoroughly into the granite that it was still worrying, still warning, long after the flock, the man, and the language he worried in had gone to dust.

*Cold coming down the high pass.*

I know, Hodd, I wanted to say. I know. You've been telling someone for two centuries. Someone always knows, and it never helps, and still you say it, because saying it is all that's left of you.

I didn't say any of that. Instead I did what I always do with the gift, which is fold it up small and shove it in the pocket where I keep everything else I can't afford to be.

Because here is the arithmetic of my life: the crown has declared that hearing the dead is abomination. The penalty is a wagon ride to Bellharrow, a trial measured in minutes, and a fate the criers describe only as "rendering unto the crown," which is the sort of phrase bureaucrats invent when the honest words are too horrible to print. And the guild — my guild, my family, my whole inheritance — survives precisely because nobody knows what I am. A guide who hears ghosts is a liability. A guide who hears ghosts and gets caught is a catastrophe with a very long list of victims.

So I hid it. I have been hiding it since I was seven years old, when I told my mother that grandmother's voice lived in the kitchen hearthstone and my mother went white as flour and made me swear, on the guild oath and her own grave, never to say it aloud again. She burned her letters that same night. All of them. Twenty years of correspondence, gone in the stove, and she watched the smoke with an expression I was too young to read then and read perfectly now.

I hide it the way the roads hide under fog. Thoroughly. Professionally. With pride, even, because hiding well is also a mastery, and I will take my excellences where I find them.

"Coral," Brannic said quietly, at the cairn. "There's a light. North ridge."

I looked. He was right — a pinprick of lantern-glow bobbing along the high path where no lawful traveler had business being at midnight. Excise patrol, probably. Possibly worse. The crown had been doubling its patrols all autumn, and the patrols had been asking new questions. Not *what are you carrying* anymore, but *who are you carrying*, and *do they bleed ordinary*.

"Rope," I murmured. One tug. Stop.

We stood in the fog like three posts while the light crawled along the ridge, and I did the thing I do best, better than walking, better than mapping, better — though I'd deny it under torture — than listening. I thought. Patrol routes, moon phase, wind direction, the drainage tunnel under Greymoss that floods above knee-height but runs lantern-dark. Ten seconds of arithmetic, and the fog that had seemed like a shroud became a corridor built to my exact specifications.

"Left," I breathed. "Off the wall. Hands on the rope, steps short, and whatever you hear in the dark, it isn't interested in you."

"What lives in the dark?" Tam whispered.

"Nothing," I said. "That's what makes it restful."

We cut down through the sink-hollow, waded the flooded tunnel — Brannic held the brandy overhead like a christening, which I chose to find respectful — and came up on the far side of Greymoss exactly where the ridge-light couldn't follow. Total detour: four hundred paces. Time lost: six minutes. Risk incurred: negligible. Somewhere Master Aldric was feeling proud of me without knowing why, the way mentors do.

* * *

We delivered the brandy to the Hollow Keg at half past two, took payment in the back room from an innkeeper who counted coins like other people count rosaries, and were on the home stretch by three. The fog had begun to thin — dawn was still hours off, but Thornfell breathes out around the small hours, exhaling its grey into the valleys — and I could see the guildhall's chimney smoke ahead, a single thread of woodsmoke standing straight up in the windless dark.

Home. Such as it was.

The hall had slept forty once. Now it held three: Aldric, who was seventy and mapped roads in his sleep; Marta, who ran the ledger and the still and, unofficially, the entire southern network; and me. Forty bunks, three occupied. A training yard gone to weeds except for the corner I kept clear. A library where the maps outnumbered the readers ten to one, every one of them drawn by a hand that was now either old, scattered, or — after Bellharrow started its lists — deliberately erased.

Some nights I walked the empty dormitory just to be angry in it. Tonight I was too tired for anger. Tonight it was just loneliness, the specific flavor of it you get when you're the last apprentice of a dying order, walking home through fog with a dead shepherd's warning still stuck between your teeth like a fishbone.

*Cold coming down the high pass.*

And then, because the night wasn't finished with me, the stones near the gate spoke again.

Different voice this time. Not Hodd. This one was newer — decades, not centuries — and sharper, and it came with the particular urgency of the recently dead, the ones who haven't yet figured out that urgency doesn't survive the transition.

*They're making a list,* the voice said. *In Bellharrow. Names in a book, names in ash. They're coming for the gifted, girl. They're coming with wagons.*

I stopped dead at the gatepost, hand flat on the stone, heart going like a drum in a parade.

"Problem?" Marta asked from the doorway, where she materialized the way she always did, as if she'd been standing there since before I was born and merely allowed the world to catch up.

"Stone settled," I said. "Frost-heave. I'll re-key it tomorrow."

She looked at me a moment too long. Marta looks at everyone a moment too long; it's her superpower, and possibly her profession. Then she stepped aside and let me in, and the warmth of the hall closed around me, and I told myself the usual lie, the good lie, the load-bearing lie:

Nobody knows. Nobody will ever know. The fog holds, the stones hold, and I hold.

But the voice stayed with me while I banked the fire. *Names in a book.* And underneath it, fainter, older, patient as frost:

*Cold coming down the high pass.*

Two warnings in one night. In twenty years of walking these roads, the stones had never spoken twice in a single night. The dead, whatever else they were, were economical. They spent their words like misers.

Which meant something was coming. Something worth spending on.

Upstairs, Aldric's lamp still burned. I could hear him scratching at his desk — the old man worked nights now, claiming his eyes preferred the dark, though I suspected the truth was that he'd started dreaming of the roads and hated to lose them to daylight. I almost climbed up. Almost knocked. Almost said *Master, the stones are talking, and I think they're talking about us.*

Instead I stood on the stairs with my hand on the rail, and I did the arithmetic again, the other arithmetic, the one where I weigh my secret against my family, and — as I had every night for fifteen years — I found the scales balanced exactly where fear wanted them.

I went to bed.

The proclamation came to Bellharrow on market day. But I'm getting ahead of the story, and the fog, as always, was already moving in.
