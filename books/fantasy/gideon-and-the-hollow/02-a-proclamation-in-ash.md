## 2. A Proclamation in Ash

The trouble with market days in Bellharrow is that everyone is awake at once.

I'd grown up on the fog-roads where the only company was the drip of water through limestone and the occasional opinionated ghost, so the noise of a town at full commerce still hit me like a wall of wet wool. Fishwives arguing over eels. A tanner's boy singing something obscene about a miller's daughter. The bell above the chandler's shop ringing because someone had leaned on it and no one had the energy to be annoyed properly. Bellharrow didn't whisper. It shouted everything, all the time, to nobody in particular.

Which is why I noticed the silence before I understood what caused it.

It came down Cooper's Lane like a tide going out — voices dropping one by one, the way they do when something official enters a room. I was standing at Maren Ostley's stall buying apples I didn't need, because Maren gave me extra when I bought things I didn't need and pretended not to notice me doing it, and I watched her face change as she looked past my shoulder toward the square.

"Coral," she said quietly. "You'll want to be getting home."

Maren Ostley had smuggled salted cod through three wars and once hid a fugitive priest in her flour barrel for a fortnight. She did not say *you'll want to be getting home* lightly.

I turned. In the center of the market square, beneath the old clock that hadn't told the right time since the Regency, stood a crier on a cart, flanked by four soldiers in crown grey. He unrolled a scroll with the theatrical slowness of a man who enjoyed being looked at, and began to read.

"By order of His Majesty Aldric—"

No. Not Aldric. King Aldous. My mentor's name in my head for half a heartbeat, and my stomach dropped through the cobblestones. A bad omen, if I believed in omens, which I didn't, except on days like this one.

"—by order of His Majesty King Aldous the Second, and by authority of the Office of Sacred Inquiry, a proclamation concerning the detection and disposition of persons gifted with unlawful arts."

*Unlawful arts.* As though hearing voices were a trade you could be fined for practicing without a license. Which, I supposed, was now exactly the case.

I should have left then. Any sensible person would have left. But my feet stayed planted on the cobblestones, and my hands stayed wrapped around a paper bag of apples, and I listened to the whole wretched thing because some part of me — the part that had spent twenty-two years listening to things other people couldn't hear — needed to know precisely how much danger I was in, measured to the word.

"The crown declares an end to tolerance," the crier read, his voice bouncing off the shuttered windows like he'd rehearsed it in a chapel. "The gift of spirit-hearing, called by its practitioners 'the listening,' is hereby declared a corruption of the natural order. Persons found to possess this gift shall surrender themselves to the Office of Sacred Inquiry within thirty days, whereupon their souls shall be examined."

Examined. There was a word doing a lot of work. I turned an apple over in the bag, feeling its weight, thinking about weight in general.

"Persons who conceal their gift," the crier continued, "and persons who harbor or aid the concealed, shall be subject to seizure without examination."

Seizure without examination. That was the phrase that mattered, and I could tell from the ripple in the crowd that I wasn't the only one who caught it. *Examination* sounded clinical, almost gentle, the kind of thing you might survive with a scar and a story. *Seizure* sounded like what happened to sheep.

Then they dragged out Tam Wicker.

I want to be precise about this, because afterward, in the years when I thought about it more than was healthy, the details were the only thing I had that felt solid. Tam Wicker was forty-one years old. He mended pots. He had a limp from a cart accident when he was nineteen, and he whistled while he worked, badly, always the same tune, and every child in Bellharrow had at one point brought him a dented kettle and received it back gleaming along with a sweet from the jar behind his bench. He was also, according to the two soldiers who took him by the arms in front of his own shop door, a listener.

He'd been reported. That was the part that made the crowd go quiet in a different way. Not caught — *reported*. Someone had watched him talking to himself at his bench, which he did constantly, cheerfully, holding one-sided conversations with his late wife the way lonely men do, and someone had decided that this constituted a corruption of the natural order, and someone had written it down and carried it to the Office of Sacred Inquiry, and someone had been paid for it, probably. The proclamation mentioned rewards. It mentioned them twice.

Tam didn't fight. That was the worst of it. He went pale and he said, "There's been a mistake," in the mild, reasonable tone of a man who genuinely believed mistakes got corrected, and the soldiers didn't even answer him, and his wife came out of the shop with a mending hammer still in her hand and one of the soldiers put his palm flat against her sternum and pushed, not hard, just enough, the way you'd move furniture, and she sat down hard on her own doorstep and didn't get up again until the cart was gone.

I stood there holding my bag of apples and did nothing.

I want to be clear: there was nothing I *could* have done. Four soldiers, a square full of witnesses, a guild of three members and one working road. If I had stepped forward, Tam would still be in the cart and I'd be beside him, and the Wayfarers' Guild would be down to two, and the roads under Thornfell would start dying within a season because roads need keepers the way gardens need gardeners. All true. I have repeated these facts to myself many times, usually at night, and they are all completely accurate and none of them help.

But here is what I did do, standing in that square with my heart going like a smith's hammer: I listened. Instinct, older than sense. I reached out with the thing I'm not supposed to have, the thing the crown had just declared a corruption, and I listened to the stones under Bellharrow.

And Tam Wicker's wife's grandmother spoke up from beneath the cobbles, clear as a bell, and she said: *"They're taking him to Greywater first. The garrison at Greywater Span. Then down."*

Down. Everyone in the trade knew what *down* meant, even if we didn't say it. There were stories about the court beneath the mountain, about what the crown paid for souls kept intact, about why the witch-hunters had stopped burning people twenty years ago and started *transporting* them instead. Smugglers traded in those rumors the way we traded in everything else. I'd always filed them under tales told to frighten apprentices.

Standing in the square, listening to a dead woman tell me where the cart was going, I stopped filing them anywhere.

* * *

I walked home the long way, through the tannery district, because the long way had fewer eyes and I'd learned the sightlines of Bellharrow the way other people learn prayers. Our guildhall doesn't look like a guildhall. It looks like a failing chandlery, which is what it pretends to be, with a sign reading NYSTROM & CO., TALLOW AND WICKS in paint so faded that Nystrom might plausibly be anyone. Master Aldric maintained that the best disguise was a business too boring to rob. He'd chosen tallow specifically because, in his words, "no one has ever committed a crime of passion over candle fat."

I let myself in through the cellar, checked the three signs that meant the hall was empty of visitors and the two that meant it wasn't being watched, and went upstairs to my room, which is less a room than a large cupboard with a bed crammed into it, and I shut the door, and I sat down on the floor, and I shook.

Not dramatically. I want that on the record. I sat with my back against the door and my knees drawn up and I shook the way a dog shakes off rain — efficient, involuntary, done. And underneath the shaking, my mind was already doing what my mind does, which is making lists.

List item one: Tam Wicker was taken on a Tuesday. The proclamation gave thirty days for voluntary surrender. Thirty days minus however long it took for someone to report *me*. Because here was the arithmetic I couldn't avoid: I talked to myself too. Everyone who knew me knew it. I muttered on the roads, I answered questions no one had asked aloud, I had been seen, more than once, pausing in empty churchyards with my head cocked like a bird's. Maren Ostley knew. Half of Bellharrow knew, in the vague way people know things they've never bothered to think about. Until this morning, knowing had cost nothing. Now knowing was worth whatever the Office of Sacred Inquiry paid per report.

List item two: the letters.

My mother's letters lived in a box under my bed. Eleven of them, tied with ribbon that had once been blue. She'd written them in the last year of her life, when the wasting sickness made her hands unreliable and her handwriting went from beautiful to brave, and in them she'd written about the gift — hers, mine, her mother's before either of us — how it had come down the family like eye color, how to quiet it when it got loud, how to tell the difference between a dead person's memory and a dead person's will, which is harder than it sounds and matters enormously. She wrote about the gift the way she wrote about everything: practically, lovingly, with occasional complaints about the price of ink.

They were evidence. They were, in fact, a signed confession in eleven parts, in my mother's handwriting, addressed to me by name, describing in detail the exact art the crown had that morning declared a corruption. Anyone who read them would know. Anyone who burned them—

I got the box out and held it on my knees and did not open it for a long time.

Here is the thing about grief that no one tells you: it doesn't finish. You don't complete it like a road and arrive somewhere. My mother had been dead six years, and I had done my grieving, properly, thoroughly, the way I do everything, and I had believed myself finished. And now the crown was asking me to burn her voice. Her actual voice, the one that survived in ink, the closest thing the living get to keeping the dead — and I sat on the floor of a fake chandlery discovering that grief has back rooms I'd never been shown, and one of them contained a fireplace.

I opened the box. I took out the top letter. I read it, because if I was going to do this, I was going to do it consciously, no flinching, the guild way.

*Coral love,* it began. *Your father says I spoil you and he's right, but he also says the dead talk to you more than they talked to me at your age, and I think he's frightened, and I don't know how to make him less frightened except to tell him that fear of a thing is usually just ignorance wearing armor...*

She'd written that eleven years before the crown started hunting us. Ignorance wearing armor. I laughed, which startled me, and then the laugh turned into something else, and I sat there with the letter pressed against my mouth for a while, breathing through linen and ink, being a twenty-two-year-old orphan in a cupboard, which is not a dignified condition but is an honest one.

Then I lit the stove.

I burned them one at a time. Not all at once — that felt like a massacre, and this wasn't a massacre, it was a burial, and burials are done singly, with attention. I read each one through first. All eleven. It took most of the afternoon. I learned things I hadn't known — that she'd once silenced a haunting in the Thornfell tollhouse by *arguing* with the ghost until it conceded the point; that she'd met Master Aldric years before I was born and he'd owed her money; that she'd known, in the last letter, dated nine days before the end, exactly what she was asking me to carry. *Keep the roads, keep the gift, keep your head down, and forgive me for leaving you all three jobs at once.*

The ashes went into the stove and I stirred them until there was nothing but grey, and then I sat with the empty box on my knees and the ribbon, which I kept. The ribbon I kept. Judge me if you like.

* * *

Master Aldric came home at dusk and found me sitting in the dark, which for our household counted as unremarkable, but he took one look at my face and set down his satchel instead of hanging it up, which in our household counted as an emergency.

"They took Tam Wicker," I said.

"I heard." He lowered himself into the chair across from me, the good chair, the one with the springs that had surrendered years ago, and he looked at me with those faded river-grey eyes that missed nothing and commented on less. "And?"

"And I listened. At the square. With the gift, in the open, surrounded by two hundred people." I turned the ribbon over in my fingers. "Greywater garrison first. Then down. To the court, if the stories are true."

Aldric was quiet for a moment. Outside, Bellharrow was quieter than I'd ever heard it — the whole town holding its breath, doors latched early, dogs uncharacteristically silent. Fear does that. Fear is the only thing that can shut Bellharrow up.

"You know what the stories say happens down," he said. It wasn't quite a question.

"That the crown buys souls. Keeps them intact. That there's a court under the mountain that pays for them, and the hunt isn't a hunt at all, it's a harvest." I heard my own voice come out flat and steady and was vaguely proud of it. "I used to think that was apprentice-frightener nonsense."

"And now?"

"Now I heard a dead woman give me the cart's route like she was reading a manifest. Now I burned my mother's letters because they'd gotten dangerous. Now I'm sitting in the dark doing sums, and the sums say thirty days, minus however long it takes for someone to remember that Coral Nystrom talks to empty air." I looked up at him. "We should run. You, me, Old Bess. Take the deep roads north, past the maps, into the wild country where the crown's writ runs thin. Sensible people run."

"Sensible people do," Aldric agreed mildly.

"But the roads don't run themselves. And Tam Wicker is in a cell at Greywater tonight, and there are others like him in every town between here and the capital, and if we run, the guild dies quietly and the roads die after us, and the only people who could have moved freely under Thornfell — the only ones who could find the court, if it exists, and do something about it — will be hiding in the wilds eating pine bark." I heard it coming out of me and recognized it, with some surprise, as resolve. I'd expected fear to win. Fear had put up a strong showing. But underneath the fear, curdling like milk left in sun, something else had set. "I'm not running, Master."

He studied me for a long moment. The lamp he'd finally lit threw his shadow huge against the wall behind him, and for once the shadow seemed the more honest portrait.

"Your mother said nearly the same thing once," he said at last. "Different words. Same spine." He rose, joints cracking, and went to the window, looking out at the dark street where no one walked. "If we stay, we stay as keepers, not as fugitives. We keep the roads open. We learn what's true about the court beneath the mountain. And we watch Greywater Span, because that's where they'll move him, and moving prisoners means carts, and carts mean routes, and routes—" he glanced back at me, and the ghost of the old dry humor surfaced in his face, "—routes, apprentice, are rather our area of expertise."

I looked down at the ribbon in my hand, blue once, grey now, ash-dusted at one end where my fingers had been less careful than my intentions.

Thirty days. Minus however long it took for someone to sell my name.

Outside, far off toward the river, a dog began to howl, and beneath the howl, faint but unmistakable, rising up through the foundations of the chandlery that wasn't a chandlery, the stones of Bellharrow started to sing — dozens of voices, the buried dead of the town, all of them at once, all of them saying the same thing, in the wordless way the dead say things:

*They're coming. They're coming. Count your days.*

I blew out the lamp, and in the dark, I started counting.
