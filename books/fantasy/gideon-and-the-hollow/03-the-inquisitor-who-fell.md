## 3. The Inquisitor Who Fell

I have burned forty-one people.

That is not a confession. It is a ledger entry. The Office of the Inquisition kept meticulous records — name, village, nature of the gift, method of proof, date of ash. I signed every one of them in my neat, upright hand, the same hand that once won a commendation for penmanship at the academy, which tells you something about the sort of young man I was: one who believed that if you wrote the horror beautifully enough, it became justice.

Forty-one. And then there was the forty-second, and her name was Wren Halloway, and she was nine years old, and she could make bread rise by singing to it.

I want to be clear about that last part, because the tribunal was not. They called it *unlicensed thaumaturgy of an agricultural character*, as though the crime were the absence of a permit rather than the presence of a child who hummed to loaves. Her mother had reported her herself — that is how thorough the fear had become by that winter, how deep the crown's proclamations had soaked into the mortar of Bellharrow's chimneys. A mother turned in her own daughter because she believed, God help her, that the Office would be gentler than the neighbors.

She was wrong about the neighbors. She was wrong about us, too.

* * *

The order came down from Commander Sera Kest's desk, though I knew the seal above hers was older and colder — the sovereign's own, pressed in grey wax that never quite looked like wax, more like skin that had been asked to hold still. *Subject to be purified at dawn. Soul intact for transport.* That phrase again. *Soul intact.* We were instructed, always, to burn them slowly enough, carefully enough, with the right salts in the right brazier, so that what came loose from the body would arrive beneath the mountain unspoiled. Like fruit packed for market. Like eggs nested in straw.

For six years I did not ask where the souls went. This is the sentence I would most like to strike from my history, and it is also the truest thing about me: for six years I did not ask, because asking is the first stair down out of a comfortable house, and I liked my house. I liked my rank, my grey coat with its silver flame, the way innkeepers comped my wine and farmers wouldn't meet my eye. Fear is a pension plan. I had invested heavily.

But Wren Halloway sang to bread. And when they bound her to the post she did not cry, which unsettled the men more than crying would have, and she looked at me — at me specifically, because I was the senior inquisitor present and children have an instinct for authority the way dogs have an instinct for thunder — and she said:

"You're the one who decides?"

"I'm the one who carries out the decision," I told her. A precise man, even then. Even now, God rot me.

"That's worse," she said. "Deciding you could refuse."

Dawn came up grey over the yard at Bellharrow garrison. I stood with the torch in my hand and forty-one names in my mouth like stones, and I thought about the salts, and the brazier, and the careful temperature, and somewhere beneath my feet — beneath all our feet — a court of something hungry waiting for its delivery, patient as rent.

I put the torch out in the trough.

It made a sound like a gasp. Forty men watched a senior inquisitor drown a fire, and the silence afterward was the loudest thing I have ever heard, louder than the mountain, louder than the screaming I'd stopped hearing years ago because a man can only carry so much before he sets some of it down by the road and walks on lighter and less human.

"Vale," Sera said. She wasn't angry yet. Anger comes later; first there is only the arithmetic, the recalculation of everything a person thought they knew about you. "Pick up the torch."

"No," I said.

Just that. No speech. I had composed speeches on the walk over — I am a precise man, I compose things — and every one of them died in my throat, because there is no oration that survives contact with a nine-year-old at a stake. There is only yes or no. I said no.

They took my coat off me right there in the yard, someone cutting the silver flame from the shoulder with a belt knife, unhurried, almost surgical, and the scrap of cloth went into the brazier I should have lit, and it burned greenish, the way treated thread burns, and I remember thinking: *that is the color my career makes when it dies. Greenish. Unimpressive.*

Wren Halloway lived another four days before the second order came through and a man named Doss, who had always envied me, carried it out while I was already running. Four days. I have done the arithmetic of those four days perhaps ten thousand times, and it never once comes out in my favor. If I had refused a year earlier. If I had refused a week earlier. If I had refused loudly instead of merely refusing, and been executed on the spot, and she had still died but at least the story would have been shorter.

This is the shame I carried into Thornfell. Not guilt — guilt is clean, guilt can be paid. Shame is a tenant. It pays no rent and it will not leave.

* * *

They branded me traitor on the third day of the chase, in absentia, which meant the papers went out to every garrison, every ferryman, every toll-keeper in three provinces: GIDEON VALE, FORMERLY FIRST INQUISITOR OF THE THORNFELL CIRCUIT, TO BE TAKEN ALIVE. *Alive.* You may draw your own conclusions about why a crown that burns witches wants one particular traitor breathing. I drew mine quickly. I had read the ledgers. I knew what intact meant.

So I ran north, into the hills, into the fog country, into the one place in the province where the crown's writ went to die: Thornfell proper, the massif that squats over the northern marches like a toad that has been thinking for a very long time. The lowlands call the mountain haunted. The mountain, in my professional assessment, is considerably worse than haunted. But haunted was precisely what I needed, because hauntings keep soldiers out, and soldiers were what I had behind me — two columns of them, hounds, and Sera Kest riding point with that expression she wore when the hunt stopped being duty and started being personal.

Here is what the average citizen knows about Thornfell: nothing reliable. Here is what the Inquisition knows: that the smuggler roads through it are real, that they move, that they are maintained by a guild so old and so reduced that most of our files listed its membership as *unknown, possibly extinct*. Three entries in thirty years. One of them, Aldric Vane, master of the Wayfarers' Guild, appeared in our records exactly twice — once as a name on a seized cargo manifest, once as a rumor attached to a road that had swallowed a tax patrol whole in 411.

I had spent my career learning how hunters think. Now I needed to learn how the hunted moved, and the hunted, in Thornfell, moved along roads that did not exist on any map the crown had ever confiscated, because the maps were not drawn in ink. They were drawn in memory. That much I had pieced together from interrogations, from a drunk excise officer in Lowmark, from a dying woman whose gift I had certified as fraudulent even as the dead spoke through her mouth and told me things about my father that no fraud could have known.

I did not believe in the gift. Let that stand in the record. Six years, forty-one burnings, and I maintained to the end — nearly to the end — that it was all trickery and hysteria and folk superstition dressed in shawls. It was easier to burn a fraud than a miracle. It was easier to burn anything than to admit the fire was the fraud.

And yet I ran toward the guild of the gifted like a drowning man runs toward a rope, believing in it exactly as much as I had to and no more, which is to say: with my legs, if not my heart.

* * *

The fog on the lower slopes of Thornfell does not behave like fog. I want to say that plainly, because everything else that happened to me in those first nights I might have explained away — exhaustion, terror, the mind's talent for decorating its own panic. But the fog. Fog should drift. This fog *waited*. It pooled in the passes at chest height and held its shape around me like a crowd deciding whether to let a stranger through, and when I walked into it the sounds of my pursuit — hounds, horns, Sera's voice cracking flat across the valley — simply stopped, cut off as cleanly as a door closing, and the silence on the other side had texture. It had *opinion*.

I walked for a day and a night by a compass that spun like a drunk, eating hardtack and frost, and on the second night I found the first marker: a cairn of stones no higher than my knee, arranged in a pattern I did not recognize, with a strip of blue cloth tied to the topmost rock. Blue. Guild color, if the excise files were honest, and the excise files were honest about exactly one thing: the Wayfarers used blue, and touching their markers was the fastest way in the provinces to become a story told to frighten other excise officers.

I touched it anyway. Desperation simplifies theology.

The stone was warm. Not sun-warm — there had been no sun for a day — warm the way a hand is warm, and under my palm I felt — I will write this down because I promised myself I would write everything down, the whole ledger of my unmaking — I felt a pulse. Slow. Patient. Like something enormous asleep just under the skin of the mountain, turning over.

"Roads that remember," the dying woman had said, in a voice that was not hers. "You can't map them, inquisitor. You can only be *known* by them."

Well. I intended to be known. It was either that or the hounds, and the hounds answered to Sera, and Sera answered to the seal that wasn't wax, and I had finished being an instrument three dawns ago in a muddy yard in Bellharrow, at the exact moment a child told me that refusing was possible. Deciding you could refuse. She had made it sound like a door. I had spent two days walking through it.

I followed the cairns. They led me down into a hollow where the fog parted — actually parted, drew back on both sides like curtains with manners — and revealed a path: a real path, flagged stone, old, worn concave by centuries of feet that had no business existing in a country the crown called impassable. Someone maintained this. Someone swept it, or the mountain did, and either answer frightened me equally.

I was three steps onto the flagstones when the knife found my throat from behind.

Not the edge. The flat, laid cold against my pulse, with the casual precision of someone who has done this many times and finds it boring. A voice beside my ear, low, neither male nor female in the dark, said:

"You're a long way from your fires, inquisitor."

They knew the coat. Of course they knew the coat — I still wore the damned thing, minus its shoulder, because I owned nothing else and vanity is the last luxury to die. I raised my hands, slow, the way you do when the blade is honest and you are trying to be.

"My name is Gideon Vale," I said. "Formerly of the Office of Inquisition. Currently of nowhere."

"Fancy title."

"Not anymore. They branded me traitor this morning." I turned my head as far as the knife allowed and saw, in the fog-glow, a figure small and sharp-eyed and mud-splashed, holding the knife the way a farmer holds a tool they were born to. Young. Tired. Watching me the way I had once watched suspects — cataloguing, pricing, weighing what I'd fetch alive against what I'd cost dead. "I need the guild," I said. "I need the roads. And I need to reach the court under the mountain before the solstice, because they have taken someone, and I know how the taking works from the inside, and I am done being the inside."

The knife did not move. The eyes did not blink. Somewhere above us, faint as a memory, a hound gave voice and was silenced — swallowed by the fog, or by the mountain, or by whatever keeps the guild's accounts.

"Why," the figure said at last, "should the last road in Thornfell open for a man who burned forty-one doors shut?"

I had no good answer. I had a true one, which is rarer and worth less.

"Because I'm the only person alive who knows where they're keeping the souls," I said. "And because I'm tired, and armed, and too stupid to die quietly. Choose whichever reason you find least insulting."

A long pause. The fog breathed. And then the knife came away from my throat, though it did not vanish — it lowered, the way a verdict lowers, and the figure stepped back into the blue-lit dark and said words I would spend the rest of this story earning:

"Walk ahead of me, inquisitor. Where I can see your hands. And pray your gods the guild is feeling merciful tonight, because I'm not sure I am."

I walked. Behind me, soft as a closing book, the fog sealed over the way I had come — and with it, the last road back to the man I had been.
