## 4. Knifepoint Negotiations

*Coral*

The fog came up out of the hollows at dusk the way it always did, slow and deliberate, like an old woman pulling a blanket over her feet. I stood on the third marker stone of the Shepherd's Cut and let it swallow me to the waist, because that was the trick of it — you didn't fight Thornfell's fog, you joined it. You became one more gray thing moving through gray, and the road forgot you were there.

The road remembered everything else, though. That was the problem.

I'd been walking the Cut since I was nine years old, back when Master Aldric still had two other apprentices and a guildhall with a roof that didn't sing in the wind. Now it was three of us — Aldric, old Marta who kept the ledgers and the bees, and me — and the guildhall roof sang like a widow at a graveside. But the roads were still ours. Every smuggler, every tinker, every soul-frightened runaway between Bellharrow and the coast paid the Wayfarers' toll or walked the king's high roads and took their chances with the hunters.

I was halfway down the Cut, thinking about nothing more dangerous than supper, when I heard the dead shepherd again.

*Cold,* said the stones under my boots, in his voice, thin as smoke through a keyhole. *It comes cold up from under, girl. Cold and counting.*

I stopped. Not because of the words — I'd been hearing Brammel's ghost mutter about the cold for three years now, ever since I'd first pressed my palm to his cairn and gotten an earful of the dead instead of the silence every honest person is promised. No, I stopped because of what came after the voice.

Footsteps. Wrong footsteps.

There's a grammar to footsteps on guild roads, the way there's a grammar to anything. Aldric walked heel-first, patient, like a man reading aloud. Marta shuffled, favoring her left knee and complaining about it in equal measure. Smugglers hurried but softly, weight on the balls of the feet, apologetic even when alone. The dead made no sound at all, which was its own kind of signature.

These footsteps were long-strided, even, confident, and placed exactly where a man who had studied maps would place them — which meant they were wrong, because nobody who had only studied maps knew that the fourth marker stone of the Shepherd's Cut was a lie. The real path bent left around the false hollow, and anyone who trusted the old survey charts would walk straight into the bramble sink and come out the far side scratched, furious, and exactly where I wanted trespassers.

The footsteps went right past the fourth stone without hesitating.

Well. I thought. Either we have a visitor who can read the land like a born Wayfarer, or we have a visitor who has stolen guild charts, and of the two, only one of them was going to be any fun to interrogate.

I slipped off the path and into the fog proper, letting it take me whole, and moved parallel through the trees. My knife came out of its sheath without my asking it to; that's the thing about a knife you've carried since you were twelve. It develops opinions.

He came around the bend below me — a big man, dark-haired, moving with that terrible economy that soldiers have, the kind of movement that isn't graceful so much as *expensive*, every gesture paid for in advance. He wore a traveler's cloak over what I'd have bet my mother's ring was army-issue leather underneath. And at his belt, half-hidden by the cloak's fall, hung something I recognized the way you recognize a snake by its rattle: the short black scabbard of an inquisitor's blade.

My mouth went dry and my hands went steady, which is how fear has always worked on me. Backwards.

A witch-hunter. On guild roads. Alone, unmarked, walking like he owned the fog.

I could have run. I want that recorded somewhere, in whatever ledger the dead keep of my stupid decisions: I *could* have run. Aldric would have told me to run. But the hunt had taken Tam Weller from Bellharrow not ten days past, and the neighbor woman with the herb-garden, and I had burned my mother's letters rather than let a single line of her handwriting fall into hunter hands, and somewhere in all that burning something in me had hardened into a shape I didn't much recognize.

So instead of running, I dropped out of the fog behind him and put my knife against the back of his knee, where the leather doesn't reach, and said, "Guild road. Toll's owed."

He didn't startle. That should have frightened me more than it did. He simply went still, the way a deer goes still, and said, "Name your price," in a voice like gravel settling.

"First the name. Then the price."

"Coral Nystrom." He said it flatly, no question in it, and the bottom dropped out of my stomach. "Last apprentice of the Wayfarers' Guild. Keeper of the hollow roads. And, if the crown's records are accurate, unregistered."

Unregistered. The word they used for people like me, people whose names belonged on the hunt's lists and didn't. People who were supposed to be found.

"You've got me at a disadvantage," I managed, and pressed the knife a little harder to make the point literal. "You know my name. Give me yours before I give you a limp."

"Gideon Vale." A pause, and then, almost sardonically: "Formerly Inquisitor Second Rank Vale. Currently just Vale, if it's all the same to you."

An inquisitor. Off the leash, in the fog, on my road, saying my name like he'd read it off a warrant. Every sensible thought I owned screamed *run* now, loudly, in chorus. Instead I did the arithmetic of the situation, which went roughly: he could have brought twenty hunters and burned the guildhall already, and hadn't; he was alone; and he had introduced himself as *formerly* an inquisitor, which was either the most elaborate trap ever baited or the truth.

"Turn around," I said. "Slowly. Hands where the fog can see them."

He turned. Slowly. Hands open at his sides, palms out, and I'll grant him this — he did it like a man who had done it before, many times, on both sides of the knife. His face was all hard planes and tired eyes, and there was a scar through one eyebrow that some knife had given him years ago, and none of it looked like a man enjoying a trap.

"Say it again," I said. "The formerly part."

"I refused an order." His jaw worked. "Three days ago. They wanted a fire built around a child, and I declined to build it, and the crown repaid eleven years of service with a traitor's brand." He pushed his collar aside, and there it was on his throat — a fresh brand, angry red, the crown's eye seared into him like livestock. "Now I'm told the only way through Thornfell that the hunt doesn't watch is a Wayfarer road. So here I am. Owing a toll."

I kept the knife where it was, because lowering it felt like a decision and I wasn't ready to make decisions yet. "Inquisitors don't get lost. Inquisitors don't get branded. Inquisitors especially don't wander into the fog alone unless they're leading everyone else to it."

"If I were leading the hunt here," he said, with the first flicker of something that wasn't exhaustion — something dry, almost amused, "you'd already be ash, and so would your guildhall, and I wouldn't be standing in a bog negotiating toll with a woman holding a knife to my leg. I'd be home, sleeping well, the way I used to." Something crossed his face then, quick and ugly. "I don't sleep well anymore."

"Why?" The word came out harder than I meant it. "Because of the child?"

"Because of the child," he agreed. "And because of what I learned when I asked why the crown wanted her alive until the burning. Which is a longer story, and one I'd rather tell sitting down, and preferably to someone who's decided not to cut me."

"I haven't decided anything."

"That's fair. Take your time. The midges are eating me either way."

And that — the sheer gall of it, the *wryness*, a branded traitor cracking jokes with a knife at his knee — that did more to lower my guard than any speech could have. I looked at him for a long moment, fog beading in his hair, midges, as advertised, swarming his neck above the brand, and I thought about Tam Weller dragged away in chains while the whole market watched and nobody breathed a word, myself included. I thought about my mother's letters curling into black lace in the brazier.

I lowered the knife. Not far. An inch.

"Talk," I said. "And Vale — if I hear one word that smells like a hunter's lie, the fog will swallow you and no one will ever find enough of you to bury."

"Understood," he said gravely. And then, glancing down at the ground between us: "Is this the part where you tell me the toll?"

"The toll," I said, "is everything."

*Gideon*

I have been threatened by better people than Coral Nystrom. Generals, mostly, and once a bishop, and once — memorably — a man I was actively hanging, who swore with such fluency and invention that I delayed the drop just to hear the end of the sentence. So being held at knifepoint on a haunted road by a smuggler half my size was, professionally speaking, a demotion.

And yet I found I minded it less than I should have. There was something restful about being threatened honestly, by someone whose reasons were plain. Coral wanted me gone or dead or both, and said so with the knife, and meant it. After eleven years of the hunt — of men smiling while they signed death warrants, of Sera Kest calling me *partner* with warmth in her voice while she loaded the crossbow — plainness was nearly a luxury.

We sat on a fallen beech trunk off the path, in a pocket where the fog thinned, and Coral sat sideways to me with the knife still loose in their grip — I never did settle whether Coral was man or woman, and eventually decided it was none of my business and possibly none of theirs either — and told me, in a voice stripped of everything but fact, what the crown's records said about me.

"They list you as the hunt's best," Coral said. "Fourteen years. Two hundred and six registered gifted taken alive. Forty-one burned." A pause. "You count them? Or does the ledger do it for you?"

"The ledger does it for me," I said. "I count them anyway. At night. It's faster than sleep."

Something shifted in Coral's face — not softening, exactly. Recalculation. The look of a merchant discovering the coin on the table is real.

"So talk," Coral said. "Why does the crown want the gifted alive until the burning? Because I've wondered. Everyone wonders. The official word is mercy — that the crown grants the condemned time to repent."

"The official word is a lie with good posture." I leaned forward, elbows on knees, and made myself say it plainly, because plainly was the only currency I had left. "Eleven years I believed the burnings were the point. Purification. Cleansing. All the pretty words. Then, three months ago, I was summoned to a closed session — no clerks, no record — and shown documents I was ordered to memorize and forget." I looked at the fog, because looking at Coral while I said it was beyond me. "The gifted aren't executed. They're *harvested*. There is a rite — old, older than the crown, older than the church that licenses the hunt — that takes the soul out of a living body intact. The body burns afterward for the crowds. The soul goes beneath Thornfell."

Coral had gone very still. Stillness, I was learning, was this one's version of shouting.

"Beneath Thornfell," Coral repeated. "Beneath the *mountain*."

"There's a court down there. The documents called it the Court Beneath, though I suspect that's a translation of something older and worse. The nobility of it — and it has nobility, ranks, heraldry, God help us — they don't age, or they age wrong, and they pay for that in souls. The crown sells to them. Has sold for two hundred years. Every witch-hunt, every proclamation, every pious bonfire — it's supply chain." I heard my own voice go flat and cold, the way it did when I was holding something down. "Two hundred and six souls, Coral. I delivered them personally. I checked their ropes myself."

The silence stretched. Somewhere off in the fog, water dripped from a branch with the patience of a metronome.

"My mentor," Coral said at last, and the voice was careful, controlled, a hand pressed hard over a wound, "Master Aldric. He's gifted. Unregistered, like me. If the hunt—"

"Then he's not on an execution list. He's on an *inventory* list." I hated the words as I said them. "Which is worse, if it helps to know that. Executions end. Inventory waits."

Coral stood up abruptly and walked three steps into the fog, and I let them go, because I understood needing a wall at your back and a moment where no one could see your face. When they came back, the knife was sheathed. Both hands were empty. Their eyes were wet and furious about being wet.

"Why tell me?" Coral demanded. "You don't know me. For all you know I sell information to Bellharrow magistrates. Why hand the crown's ugliest secret to a stranger with a knife?"

"Because you're the last of the Wayfarers' Guild, and the Wayfarers' Guild knows these roads better than the crown ever will, and I need to get under that mountain." I said it simply, because it was simple. "And because I spent fourteen years being lied to by people who smiled, and I find I'd rather trust a stranger who threatens me honestly than another partner who calls me friend."

That landed. I saw it land. Coral sat back down on the log, slowly, and for a while neither of us spoke, and the fog closed around us like a room.

"The roads under Thornfell," Coral said finally. "The hollow roads. We keep them secret because secrecy is the only armor a small thing has. You're asking me to spend the guild's last armor on you."

"I'm asking you to spend it on your master. And on two hundred and six others." I paused. "And, if it matters, on a child named Wren Halloway, who is currently in a cell under Bellharrow keep, scheduled for harvest at the solstice, who is seven years old and bites her jailers, which I respect enormously."

That got the ghost of a laugh out of Coral — startled out of them, unwelcome, quickly suppressed. Good. Laughter was a door, and I needed doors.

"The solstice," Coral said. "How long?"

"Nineteen days."

"And you know the way in."

"I know the hunt's route in, which is guarded by men I trained, including one who will shoot me on sight and be right to." I met Coral's eyes. "What I don't know is the way the Wayfarers know. The roads that remember. That's the toll I'm actually asking for. Not coin. Passage."

Coral looked at me for a long time. I have been weighed by judges and juries and once by a cardinal, and none of them weighed like this — this was a person deciding whether to bet the last of something irreplaceable on the word of a man wearing a brand.

"Here's what I believe," Coral said at last. "I believe you're telling the truth, because liars make it easier than this. I believe the crown harvests souls, because I've heard things in the stones lately that I couldn't explain any other way." A pause, and something flickered across their face — something they weren't ready to say, filed away for later. I noted it and let it go. "And I believe that if I take you to Aldric, he'll want to turn you away, and I'll argue with him, and probably lose."

"Probably," I agreed.

"But the solstice is nineteen days out, and I can't un-know what you've told me, and I was raised better than to sit on knowledge while a seven-year-old waits in a cell." Coral stood, and offered me — of all things — a hand up, which I took, because refusing seemed churlish and my knee had opinions about the log. "So. Provisional passage. Provisional trust. You walk where I say, stop when I say, and touch nothing that hums."

"Nothing that *hums*?"

"You'll know it when you hear it." Coral turned up the road, into the fog, and glanced back with an expression that was one part warning and, buried deep, one part something almost like hope. "Come on, Inquisitor. The guildhall's a mile north, and Marta will want to search you for charms, and Aldric will want to argue, and I—" a breath, "—I need to hear what my master says about the mountain. Because I think the stones have been trying to warn us about it for years, and I never knew what they were warning us *of*."

I followed. Of course I followed. The fog swallowed us both, and somewhere behind us, faint as memory, I could have sworn the road itself made a sound — low, resonant, almost like a chord struck on a distant instrument.

Like humming.

"Don't," I said.

"Told you," said Coral, not slowing down. "You'll know it when you hear it."
