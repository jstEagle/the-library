## 5. What the Dead Told Me

I want it on the record that I did not plan to tell him. Confession was not on the itinerary. The itinerary was: feed the traitor inquisitor, keep the knife within reach, and get him off guild roads before he got us all fitted for pyres.

But plans are for people whose lives don't keep rearranging themselves like furniture in a haunted house.

It started with breakfast. Or what passes for breakfast when your guild has dwindled to three members and a larder that echoes. I set out hard bread, smoked fish gone silver at the edges, and a pot of nettle tea that Gideon eyed the way a man eyes a sentence of exile.

"This is a punishment," he said.

"It's breakfast."

"It's punishment shaped like breakfast."

"Then you'll be pleased to know we're equal-opportunity punishers." I sat across from him at the guildhall's long table, the one Aldric had dragged up from the drowned chapel two generations back, its legs still carved with saints nobody believed in anymore. "Eat. You look like something the hunt dug up and regretted."

He ate. That was the thing about Gideon Vale — he had the manners of a wolf and the discipline of one too, which meant he never wasted energy complaining past the point of usefulness. He'd been with us four days now, sleeping in the map room because it had a door that locked from his side as well as ours, and every night I heard him moving through the stacks, reading our charts by candle stub. Learning us. Old habits.

"You're staring," he said.

"I'm assessing."

"For?"

"Whether you're worth what you're about to cost me."

That got his attention properly. He set down the bread. Somewhere above us, in the rafters where the swallows nested, the morning light came through the smoke-holes in long dusty columns, and I thought: *now or never, Coral, and probably never would have been smarter.*

Here is what nobody tells you about carrying a secret like mine: it gets heavy in a specific way. Not like a stone. Like water. It finds every crack. And Gideon Vale sitting at my table, having taken a crossbow bolt's worth of trouble to find me, having told me about souls being harvested under Thornfell like turnips — he'd paid in truth first. That was the currency between us now, whether I liked the exchange rate or not.

So I said, "There's something you need to hear before we go any further. And I mean *hear*. Literally."

His face did the thing faces do when they're deciding between disbelief and alarm. "If this is a test—"

"It's a confession. Try not to make it into an arrest."

* * *

The shepherd's stone sits in the low pasture above Bellharrow, a half-day's walk if you take the smuggler cut through the alder brake, which we did, because I wasn't about to walk my confession down the open road like a fool carrying a lit lantern. Gideon kept pace behind me without talking, which I'll grant him — most people fill silence like a leaking boat fills with water, but he could hold quiet the way he held everything else: tightly, and with visible effort.

The fog was doing its morning work in the hollows, thinning where the sun touched it, thickening everywhere else. Thornfell fog isn't weather. It's memory with nowhere to go. The guild's oldest maps mark it in blue ink instead of gray, and the margin notes say things like *do not answer if it calls your name* and *the third turning lies only on Tuesdays*, which sounds like madness until you've walked it.

We came up over the sheep-cropped rise and there it was: a standing stone no taller than a man, leaning like a tired elder, furred with lichen the color of old brass. Ordinary. Deliberately ordinary. That was the whole art of the Wayfarers — hiding the extraordinary inside the unremarkable until even we half forgot which was which.

"This is it?" Gideon asked. "A rock."

"A rock that remembers." I put my palm flat against the lichen. Cold, then colder, then that particular cold that isn't temperature at all. "Four days ago, on the run through here, I heard him. A shepherd. Dead maybe two hundred years. He sings to his flock when the fog comes in, because he lost sheep to the fog once and it broke him, and now he does it forever."

Gideon's expression went carefully blank — the professional blankness of a man who has spent fifteen years listening to confessions and cataloguing them under headings. *Delusion. Deception. Demon.* I watched him reach for the headings.

"Coral—"

"I'm not asking you to believe me. I'm asking you to listen. There's a difference, and it matters, and after this you can decide what to do with me. Report me. Run. Whatever your conscience can afford." My hand was steady on the stone. My voice wasn't, quite, but I've learned that steadiness is mostly a performance you keep giving until the audience believes it, including yourself. "The guild calls it the hearing. My mother had it. Her mother before her. It's why the Wayfarers' roads work — the dead remember every path they ever walked, and if you can ask politely, they'll walk you through."

"And if you ask impolitely?"

"They ignore you. They're dead, not stupid."

I closed my eyes and did what Aldric taught me in the year I turned twelve, when I'd come sobbing to him because the drowned chapel saints wouldn't stop humming hymns: breathe, ground, *open the door only as far as it needs to go.*

The world tilted the way it always does — like stepping off a stair that isn't there. Fog-light. Grass underfoot that isn't grass. And there he was, the way he always is: a broad shape in a hooded cloak, crook in hand, standing in a fold of mist that might have been a hillside two centuries ago, singing in a voice like wind through a keyhole.

*I found him singing,* I told Gideon, though my mouth wasn't moving, or maybe it was, time works strangely in the hearing. *He's been singing since the plague year. His flock scattered. He wants them counted.*

And then — because this was the confession, this was the whole terrible coin I was spending — I reached back through myself and took hold of Gideon Vale's hand, and opened the door wide enough for two.

* * *

People think the hearing works like sound. It doesn't. It works like grief. It comes up through whatever part of you is already hollow.

I felt it hit him. His hand clenched around mine hard enough to grind bone — I'd have bruises in the shape of his fingers for days, and I decided, in the distant administrative corner of my mind, that I wouldn't hold it against him. Then his knees went. I caught his weight against the stone, and we knelt together in the wet grass like two penitents at a shrine neither of us had planned to visit.

Because the shepherd, sensing a new listener — they always know, the dead, they crowd toward an open ear like moths to the one lamp left burning — turned his song toward Gideon.

And Gideon Vale, who built a career on the crown's official doctrine that the gifted hear nothing but their own madness, who had signed orders written in that doctrine, who had burned — I knew about the burning, he'd told me that much on the bridge at knifepoint, the parts he could say out loud — Gideon heard a dead man sing.

The song was simple. Counting. *One for the fog and two for the fen, three for the ewe that won't come home again...* An old counting rhyme shepherds used, the kind that exists in a hundred versions across a hundred valleys, except this one was worn smooth by two hundred years of a single voice repeating it into the mist, hoping.

Gideon made a sound I will never repeat to anyone. Small. Wounded. The sound of a load-bearing wall cracking.

"*That's not possible,*" he said. His lips were gray. "*That's not—*"

"He died in the sickness year," I said quietly, holding onto him, holding the door open because closing it now would be worse than cruelty, it would be proof of treachery. "Before your grandmother was born. Listen to the end of the verse. He changes it. He adds a line. He's been adding a line for two hundred years."

The song came around again, and there it was, the new line, the one that broke me the first time too:

*...and one for the boy who stayed to count.*

"That's him," Gideon whispered. "That's a man. That's—" He stopped. Started again, and his voice had gone somewhere young and frightened. "I wrote the doctrine. Did you know that? Not just enforced it. Wrote the revision. *Auditory phenomena reported by the gifted are consistent with shared delusional framework reinforced by isolation and folk practice.* I wrote that sentence. I stood in front of the Assembly and read it aloud and they gave me a commendation."

"I know."

"You don't—"

"I know," I said, "because you told me you built your career denying such things. And I brought you here anyway. Because I needed you to understand what you'd be reporting, if you report me. And because—" Here it was. The last coin. "Because you paid first. On the bridge. You told me about the harvest, about the court under the mountain, knowing I could hand you to the hunters and buy the guild's safety for a generation. Truth for truth. That's how trust works, Aldric says. Like a toll. Somebody has to pay going in."

The fog moved around us. The shepherd sang. Gideon knelt in the grass with a dead man's song in his ears and fifteen years of certainty dissolving like the fog itself, and I watched him do the bravest thing I ever saw him do, braver than the bolt he'd later take, braver than anything under the mountain:

He didn't look away.

"The child," he said finally. "The one I refused to burn. She heard voices too. Her village called them demons. I called them demons, right up until I stood there with the torch and she looked at me and said, *they're just grandmothers, sir. They're only lonely.*" His breath shuddered. "I thought she was repeating something adults had taught her. I thought — the doctrine gives you such clean explanations. Everything fits if you decide nothing real is happening."

"She was right, probably. The dead are mostly lonely. Mostly they want small things. A message carried. A name remembered. Someone to count the flock."

"What does he want?" Gideon nodded toward the stone, and I realized — absurdly, enormously — that he was asking sincerely. Asking the way you ask about a person, not a phenomenon.

"To finish the count. To have someone hear the last verse and stay for it." I hesitated. "You already did, actually. He knows. They always know when they've been truly heard. Listen."

The song was fading, folding itself away contented, and beneath it, faint as a footprint in snow, came something else — not words, just a warmth, a sense of *company*, of a cold room with a fire lit in it. Gideon heard it too. I felt him hear it. Felt the moment it landed somewhere in him that the doctrine had boarded shut for fifteen years, and the boards came loose.

"We should go back," he said at last, hoarse. "Your tea's getting cold."

"You hated the tea."

"I'm beginning to feel differently about things I was certain about."

* * *

We walked back through the alder brake in a silence that was different from the old silence. The old silence had been two people watching each other across a loaded table. This one was — I searched for the word while the fog thinned around our boots — *settled*. Not comfortable. Settled, like sediment after a flood. Things had come down out of suspension and were lying on the bottom where you could see them.

Halfway back, he said, "Why tell me? Truly. Not the toll-trust answer. The real one."

I considered lying. It would have been easy; I'm good at it; smuggling is ninety percent lying with a map in your hand. But the day had already cost me more than a lie was worth.

"Because I'm tired," I said. "Because the guild is three people and a hall full of ghosts, and I have spent my whole life being careful in ways that eat you from the inside, and you were going to find out eventually anyway, and I would rather it happened on a hillside with a friendly dead man than in a cellar with hot irons." I kicked a stone off the path, harder than necessary. "Also because if the court under the mountain is really doing what you say — harvesting souls, keeping them, *selling* them — then my gift isn't just a death sentence anymore. It might be the only tool we have that works down there. And tools need someone holding them who won't flinch. I needed to know if you'd flinch."

"And?"

"You knelt in sheep dung and cried a little and didn't call it madness." I glanced sideways at him. "That's more than I expected from the man who wrote the doctrine."

"I'm going to spend the rest of my life being less than that sentence," he said. There was no self-pity in it. Just accounting. He was, I was learning, very good at accounting — the grim kind, where you total up what you owe.

"Well," I said, "the guild takes apprentices of all ages. Aldric will be insufferable about it."

That earned me something rare: a short, startled laugh, quickly strangled, like a man catching himself whistling at a funeral. But it was real. I banked it like a coal.

We came out of the brake below the guildhall as the light went long and gold, and I smelled woodsmoke that shouldn't have been there — Aldric was baking, which meant either celebration or bad news, and with Aldric it was usually both. He was standing in the doorway before we reached the gate, flour to his elbows, and his face stopped me at forty paces the way a hand stops you in the dark.

"Coral," he said. "Come inside. Both of you. Now."

Behind him, through the open door, I could see the great map table, and on the map table, laid out neat as a place setting, something that glittered.

A hunter's badge. And beside it, a single black feather from a witch-finder's cap.

Someone had been in the guildhall. Someone had wanted us to know it.

Gideon's hand found mine in the darkening doorway — the same hand that had gripped mine on the hillside, though neither of us remarked on it — and squeezed once, hard, a warning or a promise or both.

"They know we're here," he said quietly. "They're already close."
