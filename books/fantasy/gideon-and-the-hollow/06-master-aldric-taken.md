## 6. Master Aldric Taken

The kettle was the first thing to betray us.

Not a whistle — Aldric had long ago charmed it into silence, one of the few small magics he permitted himself in his dotage, on the grounds that a man who had survived sixty years of smuggling deserved tea that arrived without announcement. No, the kettle simply *stopped*. The low murmur of water coming to boil cut off mid-breath, and the fire beneath it guttered flat as if pressed under a thumb.

I looked up from the ledger I was copying — tolls paid, favors owed, three names crossed out this season alone, which is what happens to a guild when the crown starts asking questions — and said, "Aldric."

"I know," he said from the other room.

He didn't know. Nobody knew. That was the whole problem with witch-hunters: they didn't rattle windows or kick doors like honest burglars. They arrived the way frost arrives, all at once and everywhere, and by the time you noticed your breath hanging white in the air, the garden was already dead.

I set down my pen. My gift stirred before I could stop it — it always did, an ear pressed to the floor of the world — and beneath the guildhall's flagstones I heard the stones themselves go quiet. Not silent. Quiet the way a field goes quiet when the crows lift off it all at once. Something was walking toward us that even the dead preferred not to watch.

"Aldric," I said again, louder. "The back way. Now."

He came into the kitchen wiping his hands on his apron, silver-haired and unhurried, with the expression of a man who has been told the ship is sinking and would like to see the hole for himself. "It's nearly dawn," he said. "They don't raid at dawn. Dawn is for poets and liars."

"They're here." I was already moving, pulling up the loose flag behind the flour barrel, the one that opened onto the Cold Stair. "I can feel them. Or feel where they aren't, which amounts to the same thing."

He watched me work for a moment, and something in his face softened in a way I distrusted profoundly. Aldric only got gentle when he'd already decided something terrible and was waiting for the rest of us to catch up.

"Coral," he said. "How many ways out of this hall do you know?"

"Eleven," I said. "Twelve if you count the chimney, which I don't, because I have dignity."

"And how many ways out for *two* people?"

My hands went still on the flagstone. The hall was very quiet around us. Somewhere above, on the ridge road, a bird started up and then thought better of it.

"That's not an answer," I said.

"It's the only answer there is." He came over and put his hand over mine on the cold stone, and his fingers were warm, infuriatingly warm, the hands of a living man who intended to stay one. "You're the last apprentice of the Wayfarers' Guild. Not the last member — the last *apprentice*. There's a difference. Members carry maps. Apprentices carry everything else." He smiled, and it was the smile he wore when correcting my knots, patient and merciless at once. "If they take me, they take an old man who knows roads that are already half-forgotten. If they take you, they take every road we ever kept, and every soul who'll need them after."

"You don't know they want you."

"I've been expecting them since Bellharrow burned those letters of yours' mother's." He tapped my sternum, right where the letters used to sit against my ribs before I fed them to the stove. "You think I didn't notice? You think I haven't been sleeping in my boots for a month? I'm old, girl, not oblivious. There's a difference."

"There's a difference," I agreed, because arguing with Aldric about phrasing was how he won arguments, and I wasn't going to give him the satisfaction. "So we both leave. Eleven ways, remember. We split up and meet at the Greywater marker by noon."

"Noon," he said, "is a lovely time to be ambushed."

* * *

We wasted four minutes arguing, which I will regret for the rest of my life, and I include in that estimate whatever comes after.

It might have gone differently if I hadn't hesitated at the top of the Cold Stair — if I hadn't done the thing apprentices are trained never to do, which is look back. But the guildhall had been my whole world since I was nine years old, all crooked beams and map-scented air, and some stubborn animal part of me wanted one more look at it before the dark swallowed it, the way you look back at a house you've sold.

That's when I saw the light in the front room. Not lamplight. Lamplight flickers. This light stood perfectly still, white and patient, like a held breath.

"Hunters," I breathed. "Front room. How—"

"The chimney," Aldric said grimly. "Thirteen ways. I forgot the chimney."

They came through the walls too, I learned later. Through the walls, through the floor, through places no honest soldier could fit — the hunters had their own gifts, ugly ones, borrowed ones, and the crown had never minded borrowing. One moment the kitchen was empty except for two smugglers and a dead kettle; the next it was full of men in grey-and-white, faces masked to the eyes, and the temperature dropped so fast my breath hung in ribbons.

Aldric moved faster than I have ever seen him move, before or since. Sixty years old and he moved like the roads themselves — sudden, sideways, impossible. His hand closed on my collar and he *threw* me, actually threw me, down the stairwell's throat, and I heard him shout — not my name, thank the hollow dark, he was too clever for that — but the old guild cant:

*"Road's yours! Walk it!"*

Then the trapdoor slammed above me and the bolts shot home from his side, and I was falling through the dark with his voice ringing in my ears and my heart trying to climb back up my throat the wrong way.

* * *

Here is the thing nobody tells you about hiding: it is the most active cowardice there is. Every muscle clenched, every sense straining, your whole body screaming *run* while your mind holds the leash and whispers *wait, wait, wait.* I crouched in the Cold Stair's third landing — a niche behind a fallen slab where the guild once cached brandy — with my knife drawn and my hand shaking so badly the blade chattered softly against stone, and I listened.

Through the floor, through the bones of the hall, my gift poured everything up to me whether I wanted it or not. Boots. Many boots, moving with the awful coordination of men who share one thought between them. The scrape of furniture. A crash — the map cabinet, my map cabinet, the one I'd spent six years drawing — and then a voice, muffled but clear enough, giving orders in the flat tone of a man reading a list.

And under all of it, faint as a coin at the bottom of a well: the dead. The guildhall had its ghosts, as any old building does — a smuggler lost in the flood year, a woman whose name we'd never learned, the ordinary sediment of the past. They were awake now, and agitated, and pressing against the walls of the world like moths at glass, and one of them kept saying, over and over, in a small dry voice:

*They're taking him. They're taking him alive.*

Alive. I clung to that. The crown wanted souls intact — Gideon Vale had told me that much across a knife's edge in the dark, and I had believed him precisely because it was the kind of truth a man hates admitting. They didn't burn everyone. They saved the strong ones. Saved them for the court beneath the mountain, where souls were currency, and Master Aldric Nystrom-adjacent, keeper of half the hidden ways in Thornfell, was worth more than any gold mine.

Which meant he wouldn't die today. Which meant there would be a tomorrow to fix this. Which meant —

Which meant I could stand here in a brandy cache with a shaking knife and call it strategy instead of what it was.

Above me, a door burst. Not the front door — the door to Aldric's study, the little room where he kept the guild's founding charter and a portrait of my mother and absolutely nothing valuable, because Aldric understood misdirection the way priests understand prayer. I heard his voice, calm, almost bored: *"Careful with that shelf, it's older than your order."*

Someone struck him. I heard it — the flat wet sound of a gauntlet across a face, carried clean through forty feet of stone as if the rock were doing me a cruelty on purpose. I was moving before I knew it, knife hand on the slab, ready to shift it, ready to climb up into eleven armed men with nothing but a blade and a fury—

*Don't.*

The dead shepherd. The one from the high pasture stones, the first voice I'd ever heard, the one who'd taught me that my curse had manners if I gave it patience. His voice came thin and urgent through the dark.

*Don't, girl. He bought you this chance. Spend it cheap and it buys nothing.*

"He's *alone*," I whispered.

*He's not alone,* said the shepherd. *We're with him. All of us. But we can't fight iron, and neither can you. Listen.*

And I listened, because what else does a last apprentice do, and what I heard broke me in a place I didn't know I had.

Aldric was talking. Not begging — Aldric would sooner beg the rain to stop — but negotiating, stalling, spinning words the way he spun them at tollhouses and customs gates, buying seconds with sentences. And threaded through his voice, underneath it, I could hear him doing the other thing. The guild thing. The thing masters do and never teach aloud because it can't be taught, only caught, like fever.

He was lying to them about the roads.

"...the Cold Stair collapsed in the spring floods, you'll find nothing down there but rubble and rats... the Hollow Road shifts with memory, useless without a guide, and the guide is dead... Bellharrow cellars, yes, those still run, but the tollhouse ghost has the passage sealed, ask anyone..."

Every word a false turn. Every sentence a dead end planted in their minds. He couldn't stop them taking him, so he was salted the earth behind him, poisoning the hunt's knowledge of my roads with his last free breath, and he was doing it *cheerfully*, with the same tone he used to complain about my handwriting.

I put my forehead against the cold stone and I laughed, or sobbed, or did whatever the thing is that sits between them and doesn't have a name. Because it was working. I could hear it working — confusion rippling through the hunters' flat voices, one of them snapping that the charts didn't match, another saying the old man was wasting their time, a third, colder voice cutting through:

"Doesn't matter. He's the last key we need. The court opens at solstice, and the mountain wants its roads. Bind him. Mind the soul — intact, I said. *Intact.*"

Bind him. There was a sound like chain, and a sound like Aldric exhaling — not fear, just the breath of a man settling a burden onto his shoulders one final time — and then his voice again, closer now, dragged toward the front door, and the last thing he said wasn't to the hunters at all. It was pitched low, thrown down through the floor like a coin tossed to a well, in plain guild cant, the old tongue of the roads that no hunter alive could parse:

*"Deep road, bright lamp. Don't follow. Lead."*

Don't follow. Lead.

Then the door, and the cold pouring in, and the boots fading down the ridge path, and the guildhall settling around me into a silence so complete I could hear my own blood.

* * *

I don't know how long I stayed in the dark. An hour. Three. Long enough for the shaking to stop and start and stop again. The dead kept me company the way weather keeps company — present, indifferent, occasionally unbearable. The shepherd sat with me, or stood with me; the dead don't sit, exactly, they just attend. After a while he said, gently:

*What will you do?*

"What I'm told," I said. My voice came out scraped raw. "He said lead. So I lead. Down the deep road, with the bright lamp, straight to the mountain's front teeth, and I get him back."

*They will expect grief,* the shepherd said. *Grief makes people stupid. Will you be stupid?*

"I'm going to be *furious*," I said. "Furious is smarter. Furious plans."

I climbed out of the Cold Stair at last and walked through the wreck of my home. They'd taken the charter, the ledgers, the maps — everything paper. They'd left the kettle. They'd left my mother's portrait, face-down on the floor, which felt less like mercy than contempt. In the study, the shelf Aldric had defended was splintered, and beneath the wreckage, tucked where only a Wayfarer would think to look — inside the hollow heel of the globe, our oldest joke — I found what he'd left me.

One page. Torn from his private atlas, the one he swore didn't exist. On it, in his crabbed hand, a single route marked in red: the deep road's true line, the one that ran not around the mountain but *under* it, past the falls, past the tollhouse, down to the doors the guild had sealed centuries ago and sworn never to open.

And beneath the route, three words:

*For the apprentice.*

I sat down on the floor of my ruined home, in the grey light of a morning that had taken everything, and I held that page, and I let myself grieve for exactly as long as it took the sun to clear the ridge.

Then I got up, packed the page, took the bright lamp from its hook by the door — the storm-glass lantern that burns on nothing, guild-craft, unquenchable — and went to find Gideon Vale.

Because if the crown wanted souls intact, then somewhere beneath Thornfell my master's soul was sitting in a vault with his name on it.

And I intended to collect.
