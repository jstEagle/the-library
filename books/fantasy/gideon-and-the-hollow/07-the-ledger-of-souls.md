## 7. The Ledger of Souls

The ledger was bound in calfskin the color of old bruises, and I had carried it for three days before I worked up the courage to open it.

That is not entirely honest. I had opened it twice — once to confirm it was what I thought it was, once to confirm I hadn't dreamed it — and then I had closed it again and gone to help Coral with the cookfire like a man fleeing a debt collector. There is a particular cowardice in bureaucracy. A sword at your throat you can fight. A ledger with your name in it, you can only read.

But on the third night, in the hollow beneath the split oak where the Wayfarers' Guild had once kept its stores — three crates of salted fish, a rusted lantern, and now two fugitives and a stolen book — I finally sat down with it under Coral's lamp and did the thing I had spent eleven years training other men to do.

I read.

* * *

You should understand something about ledgers, since everyone believes they are dull. Ledgers are confessions that have learned to keep their voices down. Every column is a sin someone decided was arithmetic. The hunt's ledgers were the worst kind, because we had been taught to write them beautifully — copperplate hands, ruled margins, wax seals pressed neat as communion wafers. Beauty was part of the machinery. If the record looked holy, no one asked whether the act was.

This one had belonged to Inquisitor Halvard Crane, my second master, dead these four years of a fever that I had privately suspected was his conscience finally catching up with him. Coral had lifted it from the guildhall ruins along with Aldric's maps while the smoke was still rising off the rafters, and had said nothing about it for three days, which told me Coral already knew what was in it. Or guessed. Coral Nystrom guesses at things the way rivers guess at the sea — inevitably, and by finding every crack.

I turned to the first page. Crane's hand, immaculate as ever.

*Inventory, Northern Circuit, Year of the Vigilant Crown, 3rd.*

*Item: souls harvested intact, per His Majesty's ordinance of quiet title.*

*Ordinance of quiet title.* That was the phrase. I remembered drafting the explanatory memorandum myself, at twenty-six, full of certainty and Crane's praise ringing in my ears. *Quiet title*, because you could hardly tell a village magistrate that the crown was seizing human souls to feed something under a mountain. So we called it settling property disputes. We called the gifted *holders of unregistered claims*. We called the taking *transfer*. Language is a wonderful place to hide a knife.

I read on. Names. Villages. Dates. And beside each name, in a column headed *Disposition*, entries in a shorthand I knew too well:

*Bellharrow — Maren Ostler, hedgewitch — transferred, spring court, lot 44.*

*Maren Ostler.* The neighbor they had dragged away in chains while criers read the proclamation. Coral had stood in that crowd with their fists hidden in their sleeves, and I had stood beside them doing the same, and here was Maren's soul reduced to a line item, a lot number, a destination.

I kept reading. It does not become easier. I want to be clear about that, because people imagine that inquisitors felt nothing, and the truth is worse: we felt everything and called it diligence. Page after page, year after year, hundreds of names in Crane's beautiful hand, each one a person who had heard something in the stones or dreamed true or healed a fever with a touch, each one taken, catalogued, and *transferred* — down. Always down. To the court beneath Thornfell, whose existence I had denied in three sworn reports and whose seal I had stamped on the transfer orders without ever once asking what happened at the bottom of the road the wagons took.

I had asked once. Early. My first year. I asked Crane where the transfers went, and he had looked at me with something almost like pity and said, "Vale, the crown protects you from questions the way a scabbard protects a blade. Be grateful." And I had been grateful. Gods forgive me, I had been *grateful*.

* * *

Coral came back from checking the snares somewhere past midnight and found me still reading, the lamp burned low, my hands cold enough that turning pages felt like handling river ice.

"You found your name yet?" they asked.

No greeting. No *how goes the reading*. Coral had the tact of a thrown knife, which I was learning to be grateful for, mostly.

"No," I said.

"It's near the back," they said, and went to see to the fire, giving me the mercy of not watching.

Near the back. Of course they'd looked. I would have looked too — I *had* looked, the first night, running my finger down the index of names like a man checking a casualty list for his own village. But the index only covered the completed harvests. What Coral meant was further on, past the dispositions, past the summaries, into the section Crane had headed — I saw it now, my stomach dropping ahead of my eyes like a stone down a well —

*Schedule of Future Claims.*

Because that was the thing I had never let myself understand, all those years. The hunt was never reactive. We told ourselves we were responding to reports, investigating rumors, answering the crown's call. But Crane's schedule listed names of people who had done nothing yet. People who had not been reported. People who, in some cases, were children. Entries dated years forward, in advance, with the serene confidence of a merchant booking cargo space on a ship that hadn't sailed.

*Thornfell district — projected yields, solstice court and following quarters.*

And there, halfway down the page, in Crane's hand, with a small notation mark I recognized from a hundred other schedules — the mark he used for *priority* —

*Vale, Gideon. Former Inquisitor, First Chair. Claim registered by order of the Lord Sovereign's court. Note: subject's denial of gift is itself irregular; recommend assessment upon recovery of subject. Value: exceptional.*

Value: exceptional.

I sat there for a long time. Long enough that the lamp guttered and Coral relit it without being asked, which was its own small kindness I didn't know how to acknowledge.

Here is the cold horror of it, laid out plain, because I have earned the right to say it plainly: I had built the machine that was coming for me. I had written the memoranda. I had trained the assessors. I had taught Sera Kest how to conduct an interview so gently the subject didn't realize it was an interrogation until the irons were on. And the machine, being a machine, did not care that I had turned. It had simply entered my name in its beautiful hand and moved on to the next line.

They thought I was gifted. That was the note — *subject's denial of gift is itself irregular*. Eleven years of denying, and the denial itself became evidence. There was no exit from their logic. That was the genius of it, if you want to honor the word. Confess and be harvested; deny and prove yourself a liar, which was worse, which required *assessment*. I had watched assessments. I had administered them. I had told myself the ones that went wrong were regrettable exceptions.

There are no exceptions in a ledger. Only lines.

"Read it to me," Coral said quietly, from across the fire.

"I don't need to—"

"Not for you. For me. I want to hear how they say it."

So I read it. My own name, in a dead man's copperplate, valued like a fat ox at market. Coral listened with their head tilted, the way they listened to everything — and I mean *everything*, I had seen them pause mid-step over bare stone as though eavesdropping — and when I finished they were silent a moment.

"Exceptional," they repeated. "What do you suppose makes you exceptional?"

"Coral."

"They priced Aldric high too," they said. "I found his line. *Aldric Vane, Master of the Wayfarers' Guild. Note: guild roads respond to holder's bloodline; value enhanced by continuity of line.* Continuity of line." Their voice stayed level, which is how I'd learned to tell when Coral was closest to breaking. "They're going to sell him *with the roads attached*. Like selling a house with the land."

"And you with it, if they catch you," I said. "Your bloodline is the same line."

"Yes," Coral said. "I did the arithmetic too."

* * *

I did not sleep that night. Instead I did what I had always done with dread, which was organize it.

Twenty days. That was the figure buried in the schedule — *solstice court, convening beneath Thornfell, twentieth day hence* — and everything else arranged itself around it like iron filings around a lodestone. Twenty days to cross country that wanted us dead. Twenty days to find a door the crown had spent a century hiding. Twenty days to learn enough of Coral's craft to move through roads that shifted with memory, carrying a stolen ledger and a name that made every hunter in the north salivate.

Against that: Aldric Vane, alive — the schedule confirmed it, *holder intact, sale pending* — but intact the way a pearl is intact inside an oyster you intend to pry open. And against that, further out, the whole shapeless mass of it: hundreds of lots, years of courts, a harvest conducted season upon season beneath the feet of a kingdom that sang hymns of gratitude for its protection.

I had spent my career believing the hunt was cruel but necessary, misguided in method but right in aim. Lying to oneself is a skilled trade; I had been a master of it. But a ledger doesn't lie. A ledger just waits. And this ledger said, in ink I had mixed myself in my first year — I recognized the brown-black tint, we cut it with oak gall to save coin — that the aim had never been protection. The aim had been *inventory*.

Somewhere toward dawn, Coral stirred across the ashes of the fire and said, without opening their eyes, "You've decided something. You get a line between your brows like a plow furrow."

"I've decided we're going to need more than knives," I said.

"We have knives."

"We have twenty days, a ledger, and knives. Against a court that has been buying souls for a hundred years." I turned the ledger closed under my palm, feeling its weight like a held breath. "We're going to need the one thing the crown never planned for."

"Which is?"

"A way in that isn't on any of their maps." I looked at Coral across the dead fire, at the last apprentice of a guild the crown believed it had already priced and sold. "Tell me about the Hollow Road."

Coral opened their eyes. And smiled — not the wary half-smile I'd been trading scraps of for days, but something older and sharper, the smile of someone who has just been handed a reason.

"The Hollow Road," they said, "doesn't belong to the crown. It belongs to the dead. And the dead, Gideon Vale, are owed."

---

Outside the split oak, the fog was coming up off Thornfell the way it always did, thick and patient, full of stones that remembered. Somewhere beneath it, in a hall I had certified as rumor, a clerk was entering the final lots for the solstice auction. He wrote in a fine hand. He used good ink.

He had no idea his inventory was about to walk in through a door that wasn't on his maps, carrying a grudge twenty years deep and a guide who could hear every soul in the walls singing.

Twenty days. I had been given death warrants with longer lead times. At least this one, I intended to answer in person.
