## 8. Roads That Remember

The Hollow Road does not begin anywhere in particular. That's the first thing I should have told Gideon before we stepped into it, but I was feeling contrary that morning, and there is a certain pleasure in watching a former witch-hunter stand at the mouth of a passage that isn't there and try very hard not to look foolish.

"It's behind the waterfall," he said.

"It's *behind* the waterfall," I agreed.

"Which one? There are three."

"Yes."

He looked at me the way he looks at ledgers full of lies — like the truth was in there somewhere and he intended to beat it out with patience. Then he picked the middle fall, which was wrong, and I let him walk into it, because I am not a saint and the spray down his collar was worth it.

"The left one," I said, when his dignity had suffered sufficiently. "The right one floods after rain. The middle one is just water. The guild has never seen the point of a door you can't close."

"And the left one?"

"The left one remembers being asked politely."

I won't pretend I didn't enjoy it. Gideon Vale had spent eleven years kicking doors in; there was poetry in making him knock.

* * *

The Hollow Road runs under Thornfell's eastern ribs, from the drowned chapel at Greywater to the old shepherd stairs above Bellharrow, and it is the only road I know that changes its mind. The Wayfarers built it three hundred years ago out of necessity and spite — smugglers need passages the crown can't map, so they made one out of the only material the crown couldn't confiscate: memory. The road shifts with what people remember of it. Walk it thinking of your grandmother's kitchen and it will smell of bread and take you past a hearth that never existed. Walk it afraid, and it narrows. Walk it angry, and I promise you will regret it.

Which meant my first real lesson for Gideon Vale was not knots or signals or how to read moss. It was this:

"You have to think about something kind," I told him, "or the road will eat us both."

He stopped walking. In the grey light that seeps through the Hollow Road — no torches needed, the walls give off a faint pearly glow, like moonlight soaked into stone — I watched him wrestle with the concept. He is not a man built for kindness on demand. He is built like a drawn crossbow: all tension, all aim.

"I don't—"

"You do. Everyone does. Think of something small. A meal. A morning. It doesn't have to be true anymore. It just has to have been."

We walked in silence for perhaps fifty paces while he searched himself. I felt the road listening — it always listens, the way a dog listens, head cocked — and I felt it grow uncertain, because Gideon's mind was giving it nothing to hold.

Then, quietly, without looking at me: "A courtyard. There was an orange tree in it. My mother grew them against every rule about climate and sense. She'd sit under it and read verdicts aloud to herself in different voices, like she was rehearsing a play." A pause. "She thought the trials were theater. She was probably right."

The road sighed. I felt it in my boots — a settling, a softening, the walls leaning back to give us room. Somewhere ahead, a stone arch bloomed into existence where there had been bare rock, carved with orange blossoms no mason ever cut.

"There," I said, and hated how pleased I sounded. "You're a natural."

"I recited a memory."

"You gave the road a gift. That's the whole craft, Gideon. Everything else is footwork." I tapped the wall as we passed under the arch, a courtesy — you thank a road that's made room for you, same as you'd thank a host. "The hunters think smuggling is about hiding. It isn't. It's about hospitality. You make friends with the dark places, and they carry you."

He was quiet for a long moment. When he spoke again, his voice had that flat careful quality I was learning to distrust. "My commander used to say the roads were tumors. Growths on the kingdom's body. That cutting them out was surgery."

"And now?"

"Now I'm standing inside one, and it grew me an orange tree." He touched the blossom-carving as we passed, mirroring my gesture without seeming to notice he'd done it. "So either the surgery was wrong, or—" He stopped.

"Or?" I prompted, because I am weak.

"Or everything I cut out was alive." He said it lightly, the way men say heavy things when they haven't decided yet whether to mean them. But the road heard him. Ahead of us, the passage widened by a full arm-span, generous as a held breath.

* * *

We made camp that night in a side chamber the road keeps for travelers — a low round room with a natural bench and a ceiling that glows faintly rose-gold, which Aldric always claimed was the memory of some ancient sunset nobody living remembered. I got a fire going in the stone ring, mostly for the comfort of it; nothing in the Hollow Road needs warmth, but travelers do.

Gideon sat across the fire cleaning his blade, which is what he does instead of praying. I've watched him do it every night since Greywater Span. The blade is beautiful in the way of dangerous things — inquisitor's steel, black-hilted, marked along the fuller with the crown's seal. I kept waiting for him to be ashamed of it. He never was. He just maintained it, the way you maintain anything you intend to use again.

"Teach me something," he said, out of nowhere.

"You've had lessons all day."

"Something useful. Something I'd need if you weren't there." He didn't look up from the whetstone. "If Kest finds us again, if we get separated — I need to know how to survive your roads alone. Not comfortably. Just alive."

It was, I had to admit, a fair request, and more honest than most requests I'd fielded. So I put down my tea and gave him the real curriculum.

"Rule one: the road takes its shape from the strongest memory in the party. If two of us walk it, it chooses between us. If you're alone, you're the only voice it hears, so keep your mind clean. Fear makes it narrow. Grief makes it loop — you'll pass the same arch forty times and think you're lost when really you're just sad. Anger—" I hesitated.

"Go on."

"Anger makes it open onto things that aren't roads anymore. Places underneath. The guild doesn't map those. We mark them and we go around."

His eyes came up. "Underneath what?"

"Underneath everything. Don't ask me what's down there. The dead who know won't say, and the living who found out didn't come back chatty." I fed the fire a stick. "Rule two: leave offerings. A coin, a button, a bite of bread, at any arch you pass through. The road remembers generosity longer than it remembers routes. Rule three: if the walls start showing you faces — actual faces, moving — stop walking, sit down, and wait for dawn. They're not ghosts. They're memories that got loose. They can't hurt you unless you follow them."

"And rule four?"

"There is no rule four. Three rules. The guild prizes brevity; it's why our manuals fit in a pocket."

Almost — almost — he smiled. It's a rare thing on him, and it changes his whole face, takes ten years off and adds a different ten years, ones with less blood in them. "Your mentor taught you this way? Jokes over doctrine?"

"Aldric says fear is loud and humor is quiet, and the road listens better to quiet." I wrapped my hands around my cup. "He also says a wayfarer who can't laugh on the road will eventually cry on it, and crying fogs the mind, and fogged minds end up in the underneath places. So. Jokes."

"Gideon Vale," he said, extending his hand across the fire with mock formality, "student of the three rules."

"Coral Nystrom," I said, shaking it, "instructor of brevity." His hand was warm and calloused in ways mine wasn't — sword calluses against rope and chalk. Neither of us let go particularly fast. Neither of us mentioned it.

* * *

The next day, he paid me back.

We were walking a stretch called the Long Gallery — half a mile of straight corridor where the road's memory is oldest and strongest, laid down by generations of wayfarers who all remembered it the same way, so it barely shifts at all. Safe ground, relatively speaking. Good ground for talking.

"So explain it to me," he said. "How does Sera hunt?"

"Sera Kest specifically, or hunters generally?"

"Generally first. Then her."

I shrugged. "Generally: they follow resonance. Every gifted person leaves a wake — the gift hums, even when you're not using it, especially when you're frightened. Your instruments read the hum. You triangulate. You corner. And then—" I gestured vaguely at the whole apparatus of everything.

"That's the field manual version. What do they actually rely on?"

I thought about it honestly, which took a moment. "Fear," I said finally. "They rely on the gifted being afraid, because fear makes people run in patterns. To family. To shrines. To the old hiding places everyone thinks are secret. A hunter doesn't chase you. A hunter stands where you'll arrive and waits."

Gideon nodded slowly, and there was something grim and satisfied in it, like a teacher watching a student land the answer. "Good. Now the part they don't put in the manual. Hunters are ranked by conviction, and conviction is legible. The true believers walk loudly — they want to be feared, they announce themselves, they burn effigies in market squares. You'll hear them coming for a week. The dangerous ones are the doubters. A doubter hunts quietly, because they're not performing, they're proving something to themselves. They check their work twice. They don't gloat." He glanced sideways at me. "Sera is a doubter. Has been for years. She reads every text twice, including the ones that contradict the doctrine. She keeps lists of discrepancies. If she's hunting us, she's already found three reasons not to, and she's hunting us anyway, which means someone above her is forcing the issue — or she's found one reason to that outweighs them."

"What reason could outweigh doubt?"

"Orders she believes protect something. Or a person she believes needs protecting." He said it simply, and then went quiet in a way that suggested the person in question might once have been him.

We walked another hundred paces. The Gallery's old stones held their shape around us, patient as scripture.

"Tell me how you'd catch us," I said. "Right now. If you were still wearing the seal."

He didn't answer immediately. I understood why — it cost him something, to open that machinery again and show me the gears. But he did it.

"You're heading east. The guildhall's burned, your mentor's taken south to the mountain, and grief walks in a straight line. So I wouldn't chase you at all. I'd seal the southern approaches — Greywater Span, the shepherd stairs, the ferry at Mirefoot — and let the geography do the work. Then I'd send one team, quietly, not to capture you. To frighten you. Because a frightened wayfarer stops thinking kindly, and if these roads run on memory—" he spread a hand at the glowing walls "—then terror is a weapon I can fire down any tunnel. I don't need to find you. I just need you scared enough that the road turns on you."

I stopped walking.

Because here's the thing: it was exactly right. It was so right that I'd never once articulated it myself, and hearing it spoken aloud in his low, level voice — hearing my own death explained as a procedure — did something strange to me. Not fear. Something adjacent to admiration, which is its own kind of frightening.

"That's why the ambush at the span worked," I said. "Not the bolt. Before the bolt. You were scared, and the bridge—"

"The bridge tried to drop you. Yes. I saw it happen. I've been meaning to apologize to the bridge."

Despite everything — despite the raid, the bolt, the fever, the ledger with Aldric's name entered in it like a line item — I laughed. Out loud, in the Long Gallery, where sound carries, and the road carried it gently, the way you'd carry something breakable.

* * *

That evening, trading watch shifts, we fell into the other kind of lesson — the kind neither of us named.

He asked me about the dead. Carefully, the way you ask about a wound. I told him about the shepherd whose voice lives in the stones near Bellharrow, how he sings counting songs to his sheep who are sixty years dust, how I'd known him since I was nine and he was the closest thing I had to a friend who never needed feeding. He asked if it hurt, hearing them. I said it hurt like weather hurts — not personally, but constantly.

And I asked him about the child. The one he wouldn't burn. He told me her name was Wren and she could make bread rise by singing to it and she was seven, and that the order had come in writing, with seals, and that he had read it twice because Sera had trained him to read everything twice, and that the second reading was the one where he'd understood what he'd become.

"She sang the whole time they held her," he said, staring into the fire. "Not a protest song. A rising song. For the bread that wasn't there." He turned the blade over in his hands, uncleaned now, just held. "I think about that bread sometimes. How it would have been the best loaf in the village. How no one will ever taste it."

The fire popped. Somewhere far off in the road's deep memory, water moved over stone.

"Gideon," I said. "The road wants to know something kind. You're drifting."

He looked up sharply — and then understood, and huffed something that was almost a laugh. "Is that true, or is that you being gentle?"

"Both. It's usually both. That's the trade." I nodded at the space beside me. "Sit. Tell the road about the orange tree again. Tell it about the bread. Give it something to hold that isn't a burning."

He sat. Closer than strictly necessary, though neither of us treated that as data. And he talked, low, into the fire — about his mother's impossible oranges, about a loaf that would never be eaten, about a partner named Sera who used to argue doctrine with him over cheap wine until dawn — and around us the Hollow Road settled and softened, and the rose-gold ceiling brightened by a shade, and I sat there listening to a hunted man remember goodness on purpose, and thought: *this is the craft too*. Nobody wrote it in the manual. Aldric would have said it anyway.

* * *

On the third morning the Gallery ended, and the road began to change under our feet in ways I didn't like.

It started subtly — a chill in the walls that had nothing to do with drafts. Then the pearl-light dimmed, and the arches we passed bore carvings I didn't recognize, and worse, carvings I *half*-recognized, as if the road were remembering things slightly wrong. A shepherd's crook etched in a style two centuries dead. A sigil on a lintel that made Gideon stop dead.

"I know that mark," he said. "That's a tollhouse seal. Crown charter, old issue." His finger hovered beneath it without touching. "There was a tollhouse on this stretch. On every map I ever seized from your guild, there's a tollhouse marker here."

"There was," I said slowly. "It's not on any guild map younger than eighty years. It was struck from the records. Aldric never said why. He only ever said—" and I stopped, because the words arrived in my mouth with a voice attached, faint and cold, rising out of the stones beneath my boots the way breath rises off a river at dawn.

*Coral.* The dead don't often speak all at once. But they were speaking now, dozens of them, murmuring up through the rock like a congregation finding its hymn. *Coral, come and count us. Come and count us. No one has counted us.*

Gideon saw my face change. He'd seen it before, at the shepherd's stone, and he didn't flinch this time — he stepped closer, hand hovering near my shoulder, not quite touching. Asking permission even now.

"How many?" he asked quietly.

"I don't know." My voice came out thin. "More than the road can hold politely. Gideon — there's a tollhouse ahead, and it isn't empty, and whatever happened there, the crown buried it deep enough that even my guild buried it too."

The murmur beneath us swelled, and for the first time since I'd known it, the Hollow Road seemed to be holding its breath — waiting to see whether we would walk around, the way eighty years of wayfarers had walked around, or whether we would finally, finally stop and listen.

Gideon looked at the seal on the lintel. Then at me.

"Whose orders?" he asked — and I knew, from the set of his jaw, that he'd already guessed the answer would wear a ring he recognized.
