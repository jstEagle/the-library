## 9. The Tollhouse Ghosts

The Hollow Road spat us out at dusk on the far side of Greywater's upper valley, and I will be honest with you: I was grateful. Three days in a passage that rearranges itself according to what you remember — and worse, according to what Gideon remembers, which turned out to include an alarming number of execution orders recited like bedtime prayers — had worn my nerves down to frayed twine.

"Is it always like that?" he asked, shaking river-silt off his boots. "The road."

"It's always like something," I said. "Yesterday it was my mother's kitchen for about half a mile. The day before, Aldric's lecture hall. Today it kept trying to be your old barracks, which I resented deeply, because I have no interest in seeing where you learned to fold blankets with military precision."

"I never folded blankets."

"You absolutely did. There was a whole corridor of them. Immaculate. It was the most frightening thing I've seen all week, and I once watched a man smuggle six live geese through a customs checkpoint in his coat."

Gideon made the sound that passes for his laugh — a short exhale through the nose, as if amusement were contraband he didn't want the border guards to find. We'd been traveling together long enough now that I could catalogue his entire emotional range on one hand, and I found, to my private irritation, that I'd started collecting these small emissions like a miser hoards clipped coins.

Ahead of us, where the valley narrowed and the old trade road climbed toward the pass, stood the tollhouse.

I should explain something about Thornfell before we go any further. The kingdom above is young; its roads are younger. But underneath everything runs the older country — the hollow ways, the corpse-roads, the paths people walked back when this land belonged to no crown at all. The Wayfarers' Guild didn't build those roads. We inherited them, the way you inherit a house with strange noises in the walls, and we learned to live with the noises.

Tollhouses were part of the inheritance. There used to be dozens strung along the high roads, back when tolls were paid in salt and iron instead of coin. Most burned or rotted. This one hadn't. It stood square and stubborn against the darkening sky, stone-built, slate-roofed, with its arched gateway still intact — the kind of structure that outlives its purpose out of sheer spite.

And from half a mile away, I could hear it.

Not with my ears. With the other thing. The gift I'd spent twenty-three years pretending I didn't have, the one I'd confessed to Gideon barely a week ago by pressing his hand against a standing stone and letting a dead shepherd's last words pour into him. He hadn't run. I still wasn't entirely sure whether that said something admirable about him or something broken.

"They're loud," I said, stopping.

He looked at me, then at the tollhouse, then back. He'd learned the signs fast — too fast, sometimes, the way a former hunter learns things. "How many?"

"That's not how it works." I rubbed my sternum, where the pressure always gathered first, like a hand pressing flat against my breastbone from the inside. "It's not a count. It's a weight. And this one is—" I searched for a word that wasn't *horrible*. "Heavy."

"We can go around," he said. "There'll be a shepherd's track over the shoulder of the hill."

"There won't. Not with our packs, not in the dark, not with hunters likely behind us." I said it flatly, because saying it any other way would have meant admitting I wanted to go around. "We take the road. We take the tollhouse. We sleep behind stone walls for one night, and in the morning we're two days closer to the mountain."

"You don't have to prove anything to me, Coral."

"I'm not proving anything." A lie, and both of us knew it. Since the confession at the standing stone, some contrary corner of me had been determined to demonstrate that the gift was manageable, domesticated, a tool rather than a wound. Pride is a stupid thing to carry into a haunted building, but I carried it anyway, the way my mother carried her rosary — automatically, and without much actual faith.

* * *

The tollhouse had two rooms: the gate-chamber, where travelers once stopped to pay, and a smaller room behind it where the toll-keeper lived. The great arch still stood open to the road. Someone had bricked up the inner doorway decades ago — badly, with mismatched stone — and ivy had done the rest of the sealing work.

The moment I crossed under the arch, the weight came down on me like a wet wool blanket thrown over my head.

Here is what hearing the dead is actually like, since Gideon asked once and I gave him a poet's answer instead of a true one. It isn't voices floating in the air. It's more like standing in a room where everyone is talking and you can't see anyone, and every voice is coming from directly beside your ear, and they've all been talking for years without pause, and they stop talking the instant you turn your head — no. They don't even stop. That's the polite version. They never stop. You just learn which voices to lean away from.

Most hauntings are thin. A place holds an echo the way a cup holds the smell of old wine. An old woman's humming. A child counting steps. Faint stuff, weather-worn, barely words anymore.

This was not faint.

"—they said surrender and we surrendered—"

"—the children went into the east room, we put them all in the east room because it had the thickest door—"

"—counted forty-one, I counted, I was the toll-keeper, counting is my work, forty-one souls paid no toll that morning—"

"—it was the third of Harvestmonth, the frost was early, remember the frost, someone has to remember the frost—"

I put my hand on the gatepost and breathed through my teeth. Beside me, Gideon had gone very still. He couldn't hear any of it — I'd learned that his senses were strictly mortal — but he could read me, and whatever he read made him reach out and steady my elbow.

"How bad?"

"Massacre." My own voice sounded far away. "Recent enough that the dead haven't worn smooth yet. They're... coherent. That's rare. That's very rare." I swallowed. "They're asking for something."

"The shepherd asked for his sheep found," Gideon said quietly. "What do they want?"

I didn't answer, because at that moment the dead answered for themselves.

The pressure changed. Anyone who hasn't felt it would call what happened next a draft — a cold current moving through a sealed room. But drafts don't have intention. Drafts don't gather. Every fragmentary voice in that stone shell bent toward me at once, the way iron filings bend to a lodestone, and for three heartbeats I heard nothing but my own blood, and then:

*Forty-one,* said a voice like a ledger being opened. *Counted. Counted and buried shallow. Who will carry the count?*

"Wayfarer," I said, because when the dead address you directly, you answer with what you are. It's courtesy. It's also armor. "Coral Nystrom, last apprentice of the Guild. Who asks?"

The cold gathered tighter. Behind me, Gideon's hand had gone to his knife — not threatening me, just needing the weight of it. Old habits.

*The toll-keeper,* said the ledger-voice. *Maren Ostrey, keeper of this house for thirty years. Show me your book, wayfarer. Show me who passed.*

"I don't have your book, Maren."

*Then you know nothing.* The bitterness in it could have stripped paint. *No one knows anything. Forty-one souls, and the crown wrote none of it down. Wrote it backwards, wrote it as plague, wrote it as bandits, wrote it as nothing at all. Thirty years I kept honest accounts, and the last account of my life was a lie written by other men.*

Gideon had gone pale. I felt him rather than saw him — the particular quality of stillness he got when something was detonating quietly inside him.

"What massacre?" he asked. His voice was carefully level, the levelness of a man walking across ice. "When? Coral, ask it when."

I asked. The tollhouse told us.

*Third of Harvestmonth, nineteen years past. Soldiers in crown grey, no colors on their collars. They came at dawn and said the tollhouse sheltered gifted folk — a hedge-healer who'd passed through in spring, a boy who dreamed true. They said surrender and we surrendered. They counted us. Forty-one, with the children in the east room. Then they fired the roof and held the doors.*

The east room. My eyes went to the bricked-up doorway, the mismatched stones, and understood with a lurch why it had been sealed — not by the killers, but by survivors, or by neighbors, or by guilt. The thick door. The children behind the thick door.

*Nineteen years,* Maren Ostrey said, *and no one has come to make the count right.*

* * *

Gideon sat down on a fallen roof beam like a man whose legs had been repossessed.

I'd seen him shaken before — at the standing stone, at the guildhall raid, at the ledger. But this was different. This was the look of a man finding his own signature on a document he didn't remember signing.

"Greywater Toll," he said. "Nineteen years ago. I was fourteen." He laughed, and there was nothing in it. "I was fourteen. I was still believing in the hunt then. Still writing essays about the righteousness of it."

"Gideon—"

"My commander." He was staring at the bricked-up doorway. "Voss. Commander Edran Voss. When I made full inquisitor, he gave me my oath myself. Put his hand on my head." His jaw worked. "There was a standing order in the archive. I filed papers next to it for eleven years. *Greywater Toll, containment action, records sealed by order of the Crown, Harvestmonth 3rd.* I thought it was a quarantine. Plague, maybe. Some village that resisted. You tell yourself stories about the sealed files. Everyone does. It's how you keep working."

"And the seal on the order?"

He looked up at me, and I watched him decide to say it, watched him choose truth the way you choose to step off a ledge.

"Voss's seal. I'd know it anywhere. The stag and the sword." He pulled off one glove, slowly, as if his hands belonged to someone else. "When I refused the burning of that child — the reason Voss signed my attainder personally, the reason he wanted me hunted rather than just dismissed — I told myself it was politics. That I'd embarrassed him. That he was protecting the hunt's reputation from a scandal." His voice dropped. "But that's not it, is it. He signed my ruin the week after I started pulling sealed files. He knew I was getting close to Greywater."

The dead had gone quiet around us — not gone, never gone, but listening. I could feel them the way you feel a crowd holding its breath.

"Maren," I said softly. "Your soldiers. The ones in grey. Did any of them wear a stag and sword?"

The cold stirred. Something was brought forward out of the crowd of voices — a memory handled like a relic.

*One. The tall one who gave the order. He wore it on his ring, and he took the toll-box himself, and he counted the coin aloud while the roof burned. Nineteen crowns, eight shillings, four pennies. He counted it twice because the first time he lost his place.*

Gideon closed his eyes. "That's him. That's exactly him. He counts everything twice. He made me recount evidence seizures until I dreamed in columns." He opened his eyes, and what was in them frightened me a little — not rage, exactly. Something colder and more durable than rage. "Nineteen years I served that man. I burned houses for him. I branded people for him. And the whole time, the proof of what he was sat in a file I walked past every single day."

"You couldn't have known."

"Don't." He said it sharply, then gentled, which was somehow worse. "Don't do the kind thing, Coral. Not yet. Let me sit in it for a minute." He rubbed his face with both hands. "You've carried this your whole life. Hearing them. Knowing what the crown buries. And they called *you* the abomination. They hunted people like you while men like Voss did—" he gestured at the stones, at the sealed door, at everything— "*this*, and sealed the file, and went to dinner."

"Yes," I said. "Welcome to my entire personality. Would you like to hear about the time I heard a drowned ferryman complain about his widow's second husband for six months straight? Because that's what my cursed gift mostly is. Gossip from the grave. Nuisance. Burden." I heard my own voice crack in the middle and hated it. "Nobody ever told me it could be *this*. Evidence. A witness that doesn't die."

Gideon looked at me for a long moment.

"Say that again," he said.

"What, the ferryman bit? Because honestly, the widow married the—"

"No. Before that."

I wrapped my arms around myself. The tollhouse was cold in a way that had nothing to do with temperature. "A witness that doesn't die," I repeated. "Forty-one people, and the crown wrote it down as nothing, and the only record left in the world is..." I gestured helplessly at my own head. "Me. Us. Whatever this is."

"Nineteen crowns, eight shillings, four pennies," Gideon said. "Counted twice. That's not testimony, Coral, that's forensic detail. No living witness remembers coin counts from nineteen years ago. No court dismisses that." He was on his feet now, pacing the length of the gate-chamber, and I recognized the stride — it was his inquisitor's walk, the one he used when a case broke open. Except this time he was walking the case toward his own commander, and there was a fierce, terrible joy in it that I understood completely, because I'd been feeling its twin since the moment Maren Ostrey spoke.

"You realize what this means," he said. "Every massacre. Every sealed file. Every village the hunt erased. If you can hear what the stones hold—"

"I'm not going to be your archive, Gideon."

The words came out harder than I intended, and he stopped mid-stride.

"I mean it," I said. "I've spent my whole life being told what my curse is for. The guild wanted maps. My mother wanted silence. The crown, if it ever caught me, would want a hunting instrument. Everyone looks at this thing in my head and sees a *tool*." I pressed my hand flat against my sternum again, where the dead pressed back. "So if I do this — if I start listening on purpose, if I start carrying counts and names and coin-tallies out of places like this — it has to be mine. My choice. My purpose. Not yours, not the guild's, not the dead's. Mine. Do you understand?"

To his credit, he didn't argue. He came and stood in front of me, close enough that I could see the exhaustion carved under his eyes, and said, "Then choose it. Or don't. Either way, I'm not the one asking. They are." He nodded at the stones, at the sealed door. "And for what it's worth — I don't think it's a curse. I think it's the only honest court this kingdom has left."

Something in my chest turned over. I looked away first.

* * *

We didn't sleep much, and when we did, it was in the smaller room, the toll-keeper's room, because Maren Ostrey had made it unmistakably clear that the east room was hers alone to tend.

Before that, though, I did a thing I had never done deliberately in my life.

I sat down in the middle of the gate-chamber, cross-legged, palms on the cold flagstones, and I listened on purpose. All of it. Not leaning away from any voice.

It nearly took me apart. Forty-one lives poured through me — the toll-keeper's thirty years of honest accounts, a carter's joke he'd been saving for the next traveler, a girl of maybe nine who'd hidden her cat under her coat and was still, after nineteen years, mostly worried about the cat. A hedge-healer who'd passed through in spring and never known what her passing cost. Two brothers who'd argued about whose turn it was to fetch water, and finished the argument, finally, in the fire.

Gideon sat against the wall the whole time and watched, and didn't flinch, and when I finally surfaced, gasping, he handed me his waterskin and said only, "Names. Give me every name they'll give. Ages, if they know them. Anything about the soldiers — height, accent, the color of their boots. We build the file properly or not at all."

So that's what we did, by candle stub, in a murdered tollhouse: a disgraced inquisitor taking dictation from the dead, writing the report the crown had spent nineteen years making sure no one would write. Forty-one names. The tally of coins. The stag-and-sword ring. The early frost, because someone had to remember the frost.

Near dawn, Maren Ostrey came to me one last time. The pressure of her was different now — lighter, the way a pack feels lighter on the last mile.

*You'll carry the count?*

"On my life, keeper."

*The mountain,* she said, and her voice dropped to almost nothing. *You're going to the mountain. Where they sell what we are.* A pause, and then, dry as her ledgers: *Mind the tolls, wayfarer. Nothing down there is free, and the price is always higher than posted.*

I woke Gideon with a hand on his shoulder, and told him what she'd said, and watched the words land in him like a stone dropped down a well — a long time falling, and then a depthless splash.

We packed in silence. At the arch, I paused and set my palm against the gatepost one final time.

"Rest," I told them. "Help is coming. I don't know what shape yet. But help is coming."

And from the stones, faint and many-voiced, forty-one people wished us luck on the road — and one of them, the little girl with the cat, told me to watch the bridge at Greywater Span, because the soldiers still used it, and soldiers never look up.

I was three steps past the arch when I remembered Sera Kest was hunting us, and that the only crossing into the deep hollows was a bridge.

"Gideon," I said. "How good are you at looking up?"
