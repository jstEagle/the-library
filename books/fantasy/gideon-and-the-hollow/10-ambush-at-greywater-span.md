## 10. Ambush at Greywater Span

I have been ambushed eleven times in my career. I know the arithmetic of it the way other men know psalms: how many archers a bridge requires, where they'll stand, which of them is nervous enough to loose early. Greywater Span was textbook. Two crossbows on the far tower, one in the willows to our left, and — because Sera Kest never did anything by halves — a fourth somewhere I couldn't find, which meant she'd saved her best shot for last.

The trouble with knowing an ambush is coming is that knowing it doesn't stop you from walking into it.

"Stop," I said.

Coral stopped. They'd learned that much about me in nine days on the Hollow Road: when my voice went flat as a ledger line, they obeyed it. They stood on the near end of the span with their satchel of maps hugged to their chest like a child's doll and looked at me with that expression I had come to dread more than any hunter's crossbow — the open one, the trusting one, the one that said *you've been here before, tell me what you see.*

What I saw was Sera Kest's work written all over the morning. The mist off the river lay wrong, pooled too neatly under the bridge instead of drifting. Cattle tracks on the near bank but no cattle. And the tollhouse bell — every span in Thornfell had one once — hung silent, its rope cut clean. Nobody cuts a bell rope unless they don't want it rung by accident.

"Sera," Coral said. Not a question. They'd heard enough about her over cookfires to assemble the shape of her: my partner for six years, the only person in the hunt who ever beat me at cards, the woman who'd held my hair back the night I drank myself stupid after my first burning. "The one from your ledger?"

"The one who taught me the ledger." I kept my eyes on the willows. "She'll have the whole file on us. My habits. Yours, if she's been reading guild intercepts."

"I don't have habits. I'm an apprentice. Apprentices are improvisation all the way down."

"You have three." I held up fingers. "You hum when you're mapping. You check your knife twice before sleeping. And you always, always look back at the person covering your retreat, which is generous of you and will get us both killed."

Coral opened their mouth — to argue, no doubt, or worse, to laugh — and that was when the voice came across the water.

"Gideon Vale."

Sera Kest stood up out of the reeds on the far bank as if the river had grown her overnight, grey cloak beaded with mist, crossbow held low and easy against her hip. Behind her, shapes resolved in the fog: four hunters, five, six, fanning along the parapet of the far tower. Textbook. She'd even brought young Pell with her, the boy whose hands shook so badly she made him carry the lanterns instead of the bolts. She remembered things like that about people. It was why she was better at this than I had ever been.

"Serah," I called back. "You're standing in the wet again. You'll catch your death."

"You always did joke at the wrong moment." Her voice carried flat across the water, stripped of everything we'd been to each other. That was its own kind of message. She'd decided already. This was not a conversation; it was a formality with arrows attached. "Gideon Vale, by order of the Crown's Commission, you are named traitor, apostate, and consorter with the gifted kind. Surrender the witch and yourself, and the Commission may yet let you die quick."

"And if we decline?"

"Then I put a bolt through the witch's throat and you watch."

There it was. No theater, no offer of redemption, none of the speeches the Commission trained into us. Just the arithmetic. Six years ago that bluntness would have wounded me. Now I found I admired it. It was the only honest thing on that bridge.

"Here's my counteroffer," I said, and stepped sideways, putting my body between the willows and Coral, between the parapet and Coral, between the whole grey morning and Coral, because apparently I'd become the sort of man who did that without deciding to. "You take your people home. You burn the orders. You remember that the brother you write letters to every solstice is going to end up in a jar under the mountain same as everyone else's, and you ask yourself who fills the jars."

Something moved behind Sera's eyes. I'd hoped it wouldn't. Hope is a liability in an ambush; I teach that, I taught *her* that.

"You don't get to say his name," she said quietly.

And the world went to pieces in the order I'd predicted, except for one piece I hadn't.

* * *

The willow bolt came first, low, aimed at Coral's legs — a disabling shot, Sera wanted them alive, souls intact, the crown's one merciful cruelty. I was already moving. Nine days of learning Coral's rhythm paid its dividend: I knew where they'd dodge before they knew, and I went the other way, into the space the bolt would cross, and took it high in the left shoulder instead of letting it find the meat of Coral's thigh.

It hurt exactly as much as you'd imagine. More, actually, because a hunting broadhead doesn't punch clean, it opens you like correspondence.

I heard Coral scream my name — heard it the way you hear thunder, felt it before I understood it — and then the road itself answered. That's the part I still can't make orderly in my memory. The stones of the near approach heaved up under the lead hunter's feet like a wave, and he went down hard, and Coral was shouting something in the old guild cadence, the road-speech Aldric had drilled into them, and the bridge — the bridge *listened*. A smuggler's road remembers who walks it kindly. Apparently it had decided I qualified.

"Off the span!" Coral hauled me upright by my good arm, and I have never in my life been so grateful for someone else's stubbornness. "The river caves — there's a mouth under the third pier — Gideon, *move*—"

We ran. Bolts chased us, two, three, one sparking off stone a hand's width from Coral's ear. Sera wasn't shooting at me anymore. I noted that even mid-stagger, even with my shoulder pouring warmth down inside my coat: she'd ordered her people to fire past me, around me, to wound the ground near me. Old habit. Or new doubt. File it away, Vale, if you live to read files again.

The third pier was black with old flood-stain, and beneath its lip, half-drowned in shadow, the cave mouth breathed cold air. Coral went in first — of course they did, it was their road, their country, the one place in Thornfell where a Nystrom outranked a Kest — and pulled me after by the collar, and the last thing I saw of the morning was Sera Kest standing at the far rail, crossbow lowered, watching us go with an expression I couldn't read and have spent every hour since trying to.

Then dark, and water-sound, and the pain arriving all at once like a debt collector.

* * *

The river caves under Greywater are guild property in every way that matters. Coral lit a stub of candle with hands that were not quite steady and got me sitting against the wall with the efficiency of someone who has patched smugglers before. Smugglers get stabbed; it's practically a guild benefit.

"Bolt's through the muscle," they said, probing with two fingers and a bedside manner I can only describe as meteorological. "No bone. You're lucky."

"Luck is a skill."

"Luck is a skill you used up catching that bolt with your *body*." Their voice cracked on the last word, and then they did something worse than crack: they stopped talking altogether, pressed their lips white, and worked. Tourniquet above the wound. Blade heated in the candle flame. They didn't ask permission. Guild apprentices don't, I suppose; you learn to act and apologize to the corpse later.

"You should have dodged," I said, because the fever hadn't taken my tongue yet, more's the pity. "Left. I told you your habits—"

"You stepped *in front of it*." The words came out low and shaking. "That was — nobody does that. Inquisitors don't do that. Hunters calculate. You *calculated*, didn't you, you absolute — you weighed it, didn't you, my leg against your shoulder, and you—" They stopped. The candle guttered. In its light their face was doing several things at once and none of them were steady.

"Coral."

"Don't." They tied off the bandage with a yank that I chose to interpret as affection. "Don't talk. Fever talks. Fever says stupid things and then in the morning everyone pretends it didn't happen, that's the law, that's older than the guild."

"Fever hasn't started yet."

"It starts now." But they sat back on their heels and didn't move away, and in the small gold circle of the candlelight, with the river muttering somewhere ahead of us in the dark and Sera's bolts a world away, they looked at me the way they'd looked at the Hollow Road the first day: like something vast and dangerous that they intended, against all sense, to map anyway.

I thought about saying it. The whole of it, plainly, the way I'd renounced the hunt — loudly, outside the law, no taking it back. *I took that bolt because a morning without you in it seemed like a country I refuse to enter.* I thought about how the dead shepherd's voice had sounded when Coral shared it with me, how hearing the dead had rearranged something permanent in my chest, and how loving Coral Nystrom was rearranging the same territory with less warning and considerably more blood loss.

I didn't say it. Some things you say aloud and they become facts, arguable, deniable, filed. Some things you let sit in the body where no inquisitor's ledger can reach them.

So I said, instead: "Your hands are shaking."

"Yours are blue. We're a matched set." They blew out the candle, and in the sudden dark I heard them settle beside me, close enough that their shoulder rested against my unwounded one, deliberate as a signature. "Sleep, Gideon. I'll take first watch. The road knows we're here — it told me. Nothing follows us tonight."

"The road told you."

"Don't start."

I slept. And if I dreamed, I dreamed of a bridge in the mist, and a woman I'd loved like a sister lowering her crossbow, and the weight of a body stepping into the space where mine should have been — and woke once, briefly, to Coral humming in the dark, the sound they make when they're mapping, and understood that they were drawing the way forward by heart, in the black, for both of us.

Somewhere below us, the river ran toward the mountain. Somewhere ahead, twenty days gone now, Aldric's soul waited in a ledger with my name printed beneath it in my own old handwriting.

But that was tomorrow's arithmetic. Tonight there was only the candle-smell, the cold stone, and Coral's shoulder warm against mine — a fact I kept where the crown couldn't tax it.

In the morning, Coral shook me awake with news that made the wound feel simple: the road-speech in the stones had changed overnight. Someone — something — was answering back.
