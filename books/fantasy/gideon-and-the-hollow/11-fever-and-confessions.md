## 11. Fever and Confessions

*Coral*

The river caves smell of wet stone and old iron, which is fitting, because everything in my life lately smells of something I'd rather not be smelling.

I've made a nest out of every dry thing we own — two bedrolls, Gideon's cloak, the oilcloth from my map case, which hurt me more than a little to sacrifice, but maps can be redrawn and inquisitors cannot, apparently, be allowed to die of a festering crossbow wound without me having said at least four things I've been swallowing for weeks. The bolt went through the meat of his shoulder just above the breastplate's edge. Sera Kest's fletching. Of course it was Sera Kest's fletching. The universe has a sense of humor like a boot has a sole: flat, heavy, and always coming down on you.

He's been burning since dusk. Not the dramatic kind of burning — no thrashing, no shouting names of old sins, though I half expected that from a man with his ledger of regrets. Just a quiet, terrible heat, like a stone left too long in the sun, and a sheen of sweat that keeps coming back no matter how many times I wipe it away. His body is doing what bodies do when they decide, on insufficient evidence, that everything is an enemy.

"You're a very poor patient," I tell him, pressing a cool cloth to his forehead. "No complaints, no demands, no biting. It's unsettling."

His eyes open a sliver. Gray as the fog off Thornfell, and about as easy to read. "Coral," he says, or possibly "coral," or possibly he is attempting to say *water* and his tongue has staged its own rebellion.

I give him water anyway. He drinks most of it; a good portion ends up on his chin and my wrist. I decide not to hold it against him. There's a short list of things I'm willing to hold against him these days, and it gets shorter every time he takes a crossbow bolt for me, which, for the record, was once, and once is frankly excessive.

"I told you to stay behind me," he murmurs.

"You did. And I did. And then you stepped in front of me anyway, which suggests either that your tactical genius has limits or that you've forgotten which of us knows this bridge." I wring the cloth out over the basin. My hands are steady. I've been keeping them steady by force of will for two days, the way you keep a door shut against wind. "The bolt was meant for me, Gideon."

"It was meant for whoever was standing there," he says, with the maddening precision of a fevered man who still thinks he's arguing a case before a tribunal. "You were standing there. Therefore—"

"Therefore nothing. You're not a wall. You're not even a very good door."

A ghost of a smile crosses his cracked lips. "You've seen my doors?"

"I've seen your guildhall," I say, and immediately wish I hadn't, because the guildhall is ash now, and so is the warrant office, and so is the whole tidy architecture of the life he burned behind him, and his smile dies the way a candle dies when someone opens a window.

* * *

Here is what I have learned about vigils: they are long, they are cold, and they are the only place in the world where honesty becomes affordable.

By day I am practical. I boil water over a shielded flame, I change bandages, I grind willow bark and the last of Aldric's feverfew into a paste that smells like a swamp's apology. I count his breaths. Sixteen a minute at rest, twenty-two when the fever climbs, and I have become a scholar of this arithmetic the way I was once a scholar of road-gradients and fog-density, because numbers are things I can hold, and the alternative — the soft, shapeless terror of watching someone burn and being unable to stop it — is not.

But at night, when the fire is banked low and the river mutters through its channels below us, and Gideon lies half-lucid with his eyes closed and his walls down, I talk.

I tell him things I would never say to his waking face. This is cowardice, I know. It is also, I would argue, a form of triage. A man with a fever does not need the full weight of Coral Nystrom's heart dropped on his chest alongside the infection. But the words have been building pressure in me since Greywater Span, since the moment I saw him sway with a bolt in his shoulder and thought — with a clarity that felt less like a thought than like a stone dropping through deep water — *oh. Oh, no. Not him.*

So I say them to the dark, where they can't do any harm.

"The dead don't lie," I tell him tonight, my voice pitched under the sound of the water. "That's the part nobody warns you about. Living people lie all day, kindly, usefully, constantly. We couldn't function otherwise. But the dead have nothing left to protect, so they just... say things. And for years I thought my gift was a curse precisely because of that. Because I heard true things and had to carry them alone."

His breathing hitches. I pause. He doesn't wake. His brow furrows, the way it does when he's dreaming about ledgers, which I suspect is most nights.

"Aldric used to say a gift is just a tool that hasn't found its job yet," I go on. "He said it about lockpicks, too, so I never knew if he was being profound or cheap. But I think he was right. I spent my whole life treating my ears like a disease. And then a massacre came walking out of a tollhouse and demanded justice, and I discovered that what I'd been calling a curse was actually—" I stop. Swallow. "Actually the only reason either of us is alive right now. The dead showed us the way past Kest's pickets. They liked you, you know. The tollhouse ghosts. One of them called you 'the tall one who listens.' Which is ironic, given your profession."

The fire pops. Somewhere deeper in the cave system, water falls with a sound like slow applause.

"And here's the other thing," I say, quieter, because this is the part I've been circling for three nights like a moth around a lamp it refuses to admit it loves. "When you fell, I didn't think *the mission is compromised.* I didn't think *we've lost our fighter.* I thought — and I want it noted that I thought this while running, which is when I do my best thinking — I thought *if he dies, I will have known him for six weeks and it will not have been enough.*"

Silence. Even the river seems to lean in.

"That's the confession," I finish. "Filed and witnessed by one unconscious inquisitor and several hundred gallons of underground water. You may rule on it when you're well. Or never. Never is also acceptable. Never is honestly my preference, but the dead taught me that unsaid things rot, and I've had enough rot for one lifetime."

And then, because the universe has a sense of humor like a boot has a sole:

"Coral," Gideon says, clearly, awake, eyes open and fixed on me in the firelight.

* * *

*Gideon*

There is a particular cruelty in fevers, which is that they loosen the tongue at exactly the moment the mind loses its veto power.

I have spent thirty years perfecting the art of saying precisely what serves the hunt and nothing else. I could testify before a magistrate with an arrow in my leg. I once interrogated a smuggler-queen for nine hours with a broken hand, and I never once raised my voice, because the voice is a tool and tools obey. What comes out of my mouth when the fever climbs, however, obeys nothing. It is a floodgate with the lock rusted off.

So when I surfaced from the red murk and heard Coral Nystrom confessing love to a basin of water — to *me*, technically, but addressed with the caution of a person defusing something explosive — my first coherent act as a conscious being was to say their name, thereby ruining everything, including, quite possibly, myself.

They went white. Then gray. Then a color I can only describe as *cornered.*

"You're awake," they said.

"I appear to be."

"How much did you hear?"

I considered lying. It is my native language. But the fever had already sold my secrets downriver, and it seemed poor sportsmanship to buy them back at a premium. "Enough," I said. "The dead don't lie. Your mentor was profound or cheap. One of the tollhouse ghosts approved of me. And you'd have preferred I sleep through the last part."

"Yes," Coral said. "All of that. Precisely. Now if you'll excuse me, I'm going to go stand in the river."

"Don't," I said, and then, because the floodgate was still open and my veto power was somewhere downstream: "Please. Stay. If you leave, I'll have to say my part to your back, and I've seen your back, and it's very distracting, and I'd rather do this properly."

Coral stopped halfway to the water's edge. Turned around slowly, the way people turn toward sounds they're afraid of. In the firelight their face did something complicated — hope and horror sharing a small room and neither willing to leave first.

"Properly," they repeated.

"As properly as I know how, which I grant is not very." I tried to sit up. My shoulder informed me, in no uncertain terms, that this had been vetoed. I settled for propping myself higher on the bedroll, which cost me a sweat and was worth it. "I need you to understand something about me first. About what I was."

"You were the hunt," Coral said. "I know. I've known since the knifepoint. I've had time to make peace with it."

"You haven't. Nobody makes peace with it. I haven't, and I *was* it." My voice came out rougher than I intended, scraped raw by fever and truth in equal measure. "Do you know what I did, before? I was good at finding the gifted. Not competent — *good*. I could read a hiding gift in a face like a watermark in paper. I found four hundred and eleven people. I wrote their names in my own hand. And then I handed the ledger up the chain and told myself the crown knew best, because the alternative was to know what I actually was, which was the most efficient soul-catcher the hunt ever produced."

The fire crackled. Coral sat back down, slowly, folding themselves onto the stone beside my bedroll with their knees drawn up, watching me the way they watch unstable tunnel ceilings.

"And here is the thing I have never said aloud," I continued, "to anyone, ever, because saying it makes it real and I am apparently done protecting myself from real things. When I refused to burn that child — Marren, her name was Marren, she was seven and she could make flowers grow out of season — when I refused, everyone assumed it was conscience. A sudden attack of morality. Even Sera assumed that. Even I assumed that." I laughed, and it hurt my shoulder, and I kept going. "It wasn't conscience. Conscience would have been cleaner. It was that I looked at her and I saw the ledger, and I saw my name in it — I'd already seen my name, Coral, I'd been seeing it for a year, the hunt harvests its own eventually, anyone whose talent for finding gifts starts looking too much like having one — and I understood, all at once, that I had built the machine that was going to eat me. Refusing to burn her wasn't mercy. It was the first honest act of a coward who'd finally read his own handwriting."

Coral was quiet for a long moment. Then: "You know what the dead would say about that?"

"I genuinely cannot imagine."

"They'd say motive doesn't matter to the saved." Their voice was gentle in a way that undid me more thoroughly than any condemnation could have. "Marren is alive because you refused. Whatever you were feeling when you refused — shame, fear, self-preservation dressed up as principle — the outcome is the same. She grows flowers out of season somewhere, because of you. The dead are very pragmatic about this. They don't care why you pulled them from the river. They care that they're breathing."

"That is suspiciously generous theology."

"I get it from ghosts. They have a lot of free time and strong opinions."

I looked at them — at this sharp, stubborn, impossible person who navigated roads that rearrange themselves out of spite, who hears the dead and calls it a curse, who has spent three nights keeping vigil over a man who hunted people like them — and the last of the floodgate gave way.

"I love you," I said.

Saying it felt like stepping off a ledge in the dark and discovering, mid-fall, that the Hollow Road had decided to be there.

"I have loved you since the tollhouse, when you stood in a room full of angry dead and refused to flinch, and instead asked them their names. I have loved you since Greywater, when you took my weight and dragged me into those caves and cursed me with such fluency that I briefly forgot I was dying. I love you against every rule I ever served and every rule you ever kept, and I am aware that duty says otherwise, and I have come to the professional conclusion that duty can go hang. That is my confession. Filed and witnessed by one former inquisitor and several hundred gallons of underground water."

Coral made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth.

"You remembered the water part."

"I remember everything you said. Fever notwithstanding. Especially the part where you said *never is honestly my preference.*"

"That was cowardice."

"It was triage," I said. "I know the difference. I invented the difference. And for the record — my preference was also never. But the dead taught you that unsaid things rot, and they taught me the same lesson, only slower, and at considerably greater cost to everyone around me."

Coral stared at me. The firelight moved on their face. And then they did the thing that I will remember on my deathbed, whenever and however that arrives: they reached out and took my unwounded hand, turned it over, and traced the old inquisitor's brand on my palm — the mark they burn into us at induction, a circle with a line through it, the sign of the sealed way.

"This means *the road is closed,*" they said quietly. "That's what it meant, originally. Before the hunt stole it. The Wayfarers used the same symbol for a road that's fallen in. Unusable. Do not enter."

"I know what it means."

"I know you do." Their thumb moved across the scar. "I'm saying it's wrong. It's been wrong since the day you met me. You are the most open road I have ever walked, Gideon Vale, and I have walked roads that rearrange themselves out of spite."

For a man who once interrogated a smuggler-queen for nine hours without raising his voice, I displayed truly disgraceful composure. I said nothing at all. I held their hand and let the fever have whatever it wanted, because for once the heat in me had nothing to do with infection.

* * *

*Coral*

We fall asleep like that — or I do, sitting up, my back against the cave wall, his hand in mine, which is a terrible position and I regret nothing. The fever breaks sometime in the small hours. I know because I wake to find him watching me with clear gray eyes and an expression of appalled clarity, the look of a man reviewing his own testimony in daylight and finding it accurate.

"So," he says. "That happened."

"Extensively."

"And you're not fleeing into the tunnels."

"I considered it. The dead advised against it. They said, and I quote, *you've finally found someone who listens, don't ruin it.*"

"Ghosts gossip."

"Constantly. It's their whole culture."

He laughs, and then winces, and then laughs again anyway, quieter, and I fetch him water and willow paste and pretend my hands aren't shaking, and he pretends not to notice, and this, I realize, is what tenderness looks like between two people trained in secrecy: a conspiracy of polite blindness, lovingly maintained.

We spend the day like convalescents, which he is, and co-conspirators, which we both are. He quizzes me on hunter protocols — signal mirrors, picket spacing, the specific cadence Sera Kest uses when she's about to flank — and I quiz him on the Hollow Road's memory-triggers, and somewhere between the third lesson and the fourth our shoulders end up touching and neither of us moves away, and the theme of the week, apparently, is *duty can go hang.*

It's near dusk when the dead arrive.

Not gradually, the way they usually do — a whisper first, a pressure at the edge of hearing. All at once, dozens of them, crowding the mouth of the cave passage like birds before a storm, and every one of them is saying the same word in overlapping voices, a chorus with one note:

*Hunter. Hunter. Hunter coming.*

I'm on my feet before the last voice lands. Gideon tries to rise, fails, swears with impressive creativity for a man on willow bark.

"Coral. How many?"

I close my eyes and let the voices sort themselves. Old ones, faint ones, the shepherd from the stones above Bellharrow who has apparently followed me all this way out of professional interest. They press images against my mind: boots, lantern-light, the smell of oiled crossbows.

"One," I say. "Alone. On foot. Coming down the eastern channel." I open my eyes. "Gideon. It's Kest."

His face goes through several calculations and lands somewhere grim. "She tracked us."

"She tracked *us*, or she tracked *you*. And either way—" I reach for my knives, and my hand is steady now, steadier than it's been in days, because fear has always organized me better than comfort does. "Either way, she's about to walk into a cave full of ghosts who remember exactly what the hunt did at the tollhouse. And I don't think they're going to wait politely this time."

Behind me, in the dark of the passage, the dead begin to sing.
