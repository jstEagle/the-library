## 12. Sera's Offer

I had tracked men through blizzards, through peat bogs that swallowed boots to the knee, through a festival in Marrowgate where every third person wore the same grey cloak I did. I was good at this. I had been good at this for eleven years, and the crown had paid me well for it, and I had never once — not once — lost a quarry and called it anything but a delay.

So it was galling, in a way I could not put into words without swearing, that I found Gideon Vale on the fourth day by accident.

Not by craft. Not by reading sign or setting snares of observation along the likely roads. By accident: I stopped at a shrine off the Greywater road to change the dressing on my heel — the river caves had chewed my boots into suggestions of footwear — and there he was, twenty paces downstream, kneeling at the water with his shirt off while Coral Nystrom stitched a wound in his shoulder by the light of a shuttered lantern.

The bolt had gone deep. I knew because I'd loosed it.

Here is what nobody tells you about shooting the man who trained you: your hands remember him. My fingers had spent years learning the exact set of Gideon Vale's shoulders, the way he turned slightly left when he thought himself unobserved, the hitch in his stride from an old break in the ankle. When I drew on him at the span, my body had known him so intimately that my aim went strange — a half-inch of grief I didn't have a name for yet, pulling the bolt out of his heart and into the meat below his collarbone.

A half-inch. I'd been telling myself for four days that the half-inch was the wind.

It hadn't been the wind.

* * *

I should have reported the sighting. That was procedure. Signal fire, then courier bird, then hold position until the detachment arrived. Sera Kest followed procedure the way other people followed religion — faithfully, publicly, and with a private suspicion that none of it meant anything.

Instead I sat down behind the shrine with my back against cold stone and watched them, and I told myself this was surveillance, which was technically true, which is the kind of true that careers are built on.

Coral Nystrom worked with their sleeves pushed up, small quick hands, needle moving in a rhythm that said *field medicine, done often, done alone*. They talked while they worked. I couldn't hear the words at that distance, but I could see Gideon's shoulders move — laughing, or something near it. Gideon Vale did not laugh. I had served under him through two winters and a purge, and I could count his laughs on one hand with fingers to spare.

And then Nystrom paused, mid-stitch, head tilting like a bird's — toward the stones behind me, actually, toward the shrine itself — and said something, and Gideon went still in a way I recognized. The stillness of a man listening to something he doesn't believe in.

I felt it before I understood it. A pressure in the ear that wasn't sound. Like standing near a door with voices on the other side.

*Toma.*

My brother's name arrived in my skull the way a fish arrives at the surface of a pond — no warning, just suddenly there, breaking the skin of the world.

Tomas Kest had been harvested six years ago. I had signed the order myself, because someone Kest-shaped had to, because if I refused they'd have sent Vane, and Vane broke things. Tomas had walked into the wagons with his chin up and told me not to make that face, and I had made the face anyway, and then I had gone home and drunk half a bottle and reported for duty the next morning, because that was procedure.

They told us the gifted souls went to the court beneath the mountain as tribute. They told us it was painless. They told us it was necessary, and I believed it the way you believe a bridge will hold — not because you've inspected it, but because everyone else keeps walking across.

Six years of silence. And now, crouched behind a roadside shrine with a blistered heel, I heard my dead brother say my name like he was calling me in for supper.

"Go away," I whispered.

*Sera. It's loud here. It's so loud, why is it so loud—*

"Go *away.*"

The voice thinned, frayed, went. What stayed was worse: the certainty that it had been real. You can dismiss a dream. You cannot dismiss the specific texture of your brother's impatience, the little downward slide at the end of *Sera* that he'd never outgrown, present tense, alive, *now*.

Above me, faintly, I heard Nystrom say something to Gideon, and Gideon answer, and I realized with a lurch that whatever had opened in my head had been opened by them — or by the stones around them, or by whatever Nystrom carried. The witch-hunters' manual had a word for people like Coral Nystrom. The word was *asset*, and after it came procedures.

There were no procedures for this.

* * *

I followed them for three more days, and I want it noted for the record that I did it competently, at range, with all my professional pride intact. What eroded over those three days was not my skill. It was everything the skill was for.

Because the voice came back. Not constantly — thank every indifferent god — but in fragments, at dusk, near old stone, near running water. Tomas had always been drawn to water. He'd have sat in a creek until his lips went blue if Mother let him.

*You're hunting them,* he said on the second night, and it wasn't a question, and I lay in wet bracken staring at the stars and couldn't answer him.

*I can feel it when you get close. It's like — you know when the house goes quiet and you know Mother's angry? Like that. Except it's the whole mountain.*

"Tomas." My voice cracked on it. Six years of composure, and my brother's ghost took it apart in a sentence. "I don't know what you are."

*Neither do I,* he said, with a ghost of his old shrug in it, and that nearly finished me. *But I'm still me. Whatever they took, they didn't take that. Yet. Sera — yet. There's a yet.*

There was a yet. I had read enough confiscated correspondence, enough smuggled pamphlets we burned by the sackful, to know what happened to souls held too long in the court's keeping. The brokers called it *settling*. The soul stopped struggling. Stopped being anyone. Became, in the ledger's tidy phrase, *fully integrated stock*.

My brother was not yet fully integrated stock.

On the third night I made my report to Captain Vane by courier bird, because procedure, and I lied in it, because survival. *Trail lost at the river caves. Quarry likely headed south. Request reassignment of search grid.* Vane would grumble. Vane would waste a week sweeping country Nystrom had already left behind. And somewhere in that week, I told myself, I would figure out what I was doing, because I had not figured it out yet, and a woman who shoots her mentor half an inch off-center and then lies to her captain needs to figure something out fast or come apart entirely.

* * *

I cornered them at dawn on the fifth day, at the mouth of a defile where the Hollow Road ran close beneath the ordinary one — I knew the guild's habits better than they'd like, from eleven years of hunting their couriers. I came down the slope with my crossbow leveled and my heart going like a drummer boy's, and Coral Nystrom looked up at me with an expression I will be thinking about for the rest of my life, which was: recognition, and then pity.

Pity. For *me*. Standing there armed.

"Inquisitor Kest," Nystrom said. "Or do we skip the title?"

"Sera," Gideon said quietly. "Coral. This is Sera."

"We've met," Nystrom said. "She shot you."

"She did," Gideon agreed. "Sloppy of me. I taught her that flank."

"Don't," I said. My voice came out wrong. "Don't stand there being fond of each other. I have a bolt aimed at your chest, Vale."

"You had one aimed at my heart four days ago," he said, "and it isn't in my heart. So either the wind's changed remarkably, or you want to talk."

God, I hated him. I hated how well he knew me. I hated that the hate had a crack down the middle of it, and light coming through.

"The detachment is a week behind me," I said. "I misdirected them. If you run south, you're clear."

Nystrom's eyes narrowed. "Why?"

"Because I want something."

"Everyone wants something."

"Not like this." The bolt wavered. I lowered it, because my arms were shaking and because pretending otherwise was insulting us both. "Six years ago I signed the harvest order for my brother. Tomas Kest. Gifted — weather-sense, minor, the kind the tribunal used to wave through before the quotas went up. I signed because refusing meant Vane would take him instead, and Vane breaks what he carries." I swallowed. "Last week I started hearing him."

Silence. Wind in the defile. Somewhere below us, in the hollow dark, water moved over stone.

"He calls me by name," I said. "He knows things he can't know. And he says he's still himself, and he says *yet*, and I looked up what *yet* means in your ledgers, Vale, because you kept copies even after you ran, and I know what settling is. So." I made myself look at both of them. "Here is my offer. There is a back way into the court. Not the gate under the falls — the hunters' entrance, the one we use to move stock. I know its approaches, its watch rotations, its blind hours. I'll give you all of it. In exchange, you bring me proof my brother's soul survives. Before it settles. Proof I can hold in my hand."

"And if we can't?" Nystrom asked. Not cruelly. Carefully, the way you approach a wounded animal, and I resented that and was grateful for it in equal measure.

"Then I've wasted a week of Vane's time and my own career, and I'll live with it," I said. "But you'll bring me proof, Nystrom, because I've heard the way you talk to the stones. You hear them. Which means somewhere under that mountain, in whatever dark room they keep them in, you can hear my brother. And you're the only person alive who can tell me if he's really in there — or if I've finally gone mad from guilt, which, frankly, is also on the table."

Nystrom glanced at Gideon. Something passed between them — that silent fluency people develop when they've stitched each other's flesh and confessed things in fevers. I tried not to notice it. I noticed it anyway.

"If we do this," Nystrom said slowly, "you're not just giving us a door. You're giving the hunt a traitor. Vane will burn you for it."

"I know what Vane does."

"And if your brother's soul is already settled? If there's nothing left to prove?"

"Then I'd rather know," I said, "than spend the rest of my life walking across a bridge everyone tells me will hold."

That landed. I saw it land — saw Nystrom's jaw tighten, saw Gideon look away toward the mountains with the expression of a man doing arithmetic he hates. Because that was the whole rotten architecture of our lives, wasn't it. We kept walking. We kept trusting the crown's bridges. And underneath, all along, the spans were hollow, and the river was full of our brothers.

Gideon spoke first. "The hunters' entrance has a ward-keeper. Dual seal — inquisitor's blade and hunter's brand. You can't open it alone."

"No," I agreed. "But you still carry your blade. And I still have my brand." I pulled my glove off and showed them the scar on my palm, the sigil seared into it at my oath, the thing that had seemed so clean at nineteen. "Between the two of us, disgraced together, we open it. Poetic, if nothing else."

"It's a trap risk," Gideon said to Nystrom. Flatly, honestly, laying it out in front of me like cards. "If she's compromised—"

"I'm not compromised."

"You're compromised in ways you don't have words for yet," he said. "So am I. That's rather the point of us." He looked at Nystrom again. "But she shot me once and missed my heart, and she's had five days alone with me bleeding and hasn't finished the job. In my professional experience, that's as close to trustworthy as this life gets."

"That's a terrible standard," Nystrom said.

"It's the only one I've got."

Nystrom looked at me for a long moment. Then they reached into their coat and drew out a scrap of paper — a corner torn from a map, covered in their cramped, precise hand — and held it out.

"Aldric first," they said. "My mentor. His soul goes on the block at solstice. We go for him, and while we're inside, I listen for your brother. If he's there — if there's anything of him there — I'll find a way to mark it. Something you can hold." Their eyes were steady and very tired and very young. "But understand what you're buying, Kest. Once you walk us to that door, there's no procedure that covers what happens next. Not for any of us."

"No," I said, taking the paper. "There isn't. That's rather the point of me."

* * *

We moved at dusk, three shadows where there should have been one, down toward the falls and the door beneath them. I walked last, the way I always had, covering rear — some habits are load-bearing.

And as the water grew louder and the mountain's shadow swallowed the trail, the pressure came back behind my ears, closer now, eager, like a face pressed to a window.

*Sera?* Tomas said. *Where are we going?*

"Somewhere loud," I whispered, and kept walking.
