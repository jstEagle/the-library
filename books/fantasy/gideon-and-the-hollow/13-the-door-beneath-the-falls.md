## 13. The Door Beneath the Falls

Sera Kest's directions were written like a woman who expected them to be intercepted: no names, no dates, just a sequence of images that read less like a map and more like a fever dream transcribed by a very disciplined hand.

*A heron with a broken wing. A stone that sweats. Where the water forgets to fall, go down.*

I'd spent three nights with the parchment spread across my knees, cross-referencing it against guild charts so old their ink had gone the color of dried blood, and I will confess — because Gideon has since made a habit of catching me in confessions — that I did not believe it would work. Not all of it. A hunter giving up the court's back door was either a trap or a miracle, and my experience of miracles was limited to the dead telling me where they'd mislaid their teeth.

But there it was, on the third morning out from Greywater: a heron, standing in the shallows of the Greywater's last descent, one wing hanging wrong, watching us with the flat patience of a bird that had been bribed by no one and owed us nothing.

"That's her broken-winged heron," I said.

Gideon looked at it the way he looked at most things I found significant — as though reserving judgment until the thing proved dangerous. "It's a bird."

"It's a *landmark*, Vale. Try to keep up."

He almost smiled. He'd been doing that more often since the fever, these small cracks in the granite of him, and each one did something to my chest that I refused to examine under proper light. We had not spoken of what I'd said over his sickbed. We had not spoken of what he'd answered. We simply walked side by side through a country trying to kill us, and every time our hands brushed on a scramble up scree, we both pretended the touch was accidental, and we were both lying, and the lie had become its own kind of tenderness.

The river narrowed into a gorge the guild charts called the Throat, walls close enough that I could have touched both sides with a walking staff. The water here ran green-black and quiet, which is the worst thing water can do. Loud water you can trust. Quiet water is thinking.

"Stone that sweats," Gideon read over my shoulder. His breath stirred the hair at my temple, and I pretended not to notice that too.

"Downstream. The charts mark a seep around the bend — the rock there's riddled with springs. The guild used to call it the Weeping Wall." I paused. "Aldric called it that. Said if you listened long enough it sounded like crying, and then you'd never unhear it."

"Cheerful man, your mentor."

"The cheeriest. He once told me the best way to remember a road is to imagine how you'd feel dying on it. Very memorable, he said."

Gideon snorted — an undignified sound I treasured and would deny treasuring to my grave. Then his face settled back into the gravity that had been growing on him mile by mile, the way frost grows on a window: slowly, then all at once. He'd been quieter since the tollhouse. Since the ghosts had shown him his old commander's seal on the massacre orders, and he'd stood very still for a long time afterward, and finally said only, "I trained under that seal. I saluted it every morning for six years."

You cannot comfort a man out of something like that. You can only walk beside him while he carries it.

* * *

The Weeping Wall was exactly as advertised. Water bled from the limestone in a hundred thin threads, darkening the stone in streaks that looked, in the grey light, like tear-tracks down a vast pale face. I stood before it longer than I meant to. Not because of Aldric's warning about unhearable sounds — though he'd been right, the seep did murmur, a low wet whispering that sat somewhere behind the ears — but because the dead were already gathering.

They did that near thresholds. I'd learned it young, mapping the hollow roads: doors, gates, crossings, any place where the world thinned enough to pass through, the dead collected like moths at a lamp. And this place — whatever waited behind this wall — had drawn them from miles around. I could feel them the way you feel weather coming, a pressure change in the blood.

"Coral." Gideon's voice, careful. He knew the signs now. He'd learned to read me the way I read roads. "How many?"

"I don't know. More than the tollhouse. Fewer than—" I stopped. There was no need to say *fewer than the mountain*. We both knew what waited beneath Thornfell. We'd heard the singing in the walls of Sera's descriptions, in Gideon's ledger, in every silence between us. Hundreds of souls. Thousands. Packed into the dark like grain in a silo, waiting to be measured out and sold.

"Tell me what they're saying," Gideon said. Six months ago he'd have said *prove it*. Two months ago he'd have said nothing at all, jaw set, refusing the whole premise of me. Now he asked the way you ask someone to share a burden, holding out both hands.

I closed my eyes and let the whispers resolve.

It was hard. It is always hard, but here it was like listening to a crowd through a waterfall — dozens of voices braided together, centuries deep. Farmers. Miners. A woman whose accent marked her as five hundred years dead, from a kingdom that didn't exist anymore. They weren't wailing. That was the terrible part. They were *warning*, in the flat urgent tone of people who have watched others walk into the same door they walked through.

"*Not the left-hand passage.*" "*Count your steps past the third pool; the floor lies.*" "*She counts the guests. She always knows when one more comes.*" "*Don't carry iron past the gate, she tastes it.*" "*Turn back, turn back, turn back—*"

"They say the sovereign counts her guests," I managed. My mouth had gone dry. "And that she'll know when we arrive. And that we shouldn't carry iron."

Gideon's hand went to the inquisitor's blade at his hip — reflex, pure reflex — and stopped. The blade that had struck down the hunt's own warrants, that had cut Sera's hunters loose at Greywater Span, that had taken a crossbow bolt's worth of consequence already in everything but flesh. His face did something complicated.

"I'm not leaving it," he said.

"I'm not asking you to. But we should know we're choosing to ignore five centuries of good advice."

"We ignored worse advice getting here."

That was true. We'd ignored the advice of every sensible instinct I owned, which had been screaming since Bellharrow that the correct response to witch-hunters was distance, and the correct response to the court beneath the mountain was a very fast horse in the opposite direction. Instead here I stood, apprentice of a murdered guild, in love with a traitor inquisitor, about to knock on the door of the place that ate souls.

Pride in mastery of the roads, my old guildmaster used to say, is the last sin a wayfarer surrenders. He'd been right. Because underneath the dread — and the dread was real, cold and total, a lake in my chest — underneath it was something else. Something that sounded disgracefully like *I am the last apprentice of the Wayfarers' Guild, and no door in Thornfell is closed to me.*

"Come on," I said. "The water forgets to fall somewhere ahead. Let's find where."

* * *

We found it half a mile on, where the gorge opened without warning into a bowl of green stone and the river simply — ended. It poured off a shelf of rock thirty feet high and vanished midair. No plunge pool, no spray, no sound of impact. The water fell and did not land.

Gideon stared at it for a long moment. "Where does it go?"

"Underground. There's a cavern system under the whole eastern shoulder of Thornfell — the guild charts show the river resurfacing two valleys south. Somewhere between here and there, it drops into the deep ways." I hesitated. "And according to Sera, somewhere between here and there is also the door."

"A door behind a waterfall that doesn't fall."

"A door behind a waterfall that doesn't *land*. Keep up, Vale."

He gave me a look that in another man would have been a laugh. Then he went to the shelf's edge and did the thing he did better than anyone I'd ever known: he thought like a hunter. He crouched, studied the wet rock, studied the angle of the falling water, studied the shadows behind it.

"There," he said. "Behind the curtain. There's a gap in the rock face — see where the water bends? It's falling past something. An overhang. A ledge." He glanced back at me. "Your maps?"

I unrolled the oldest chart I carried, the one Aldric had drawn the year I was born, and found the mark I'd been hunting for three days: a small symbol like a keyhole, inked at the headwaters of the lost river, annotated in his cramped hand. *Gate of the Underway. Sealed by the Guild, by treaty, in the Year of Ash. Do not open. Do not speak of it. Forget it kindly.*

"Do not open," I read aloud. "Do not speak of it. Forget it kindly."

"And yet," Gideon said.

"And yet." I folded the chart away. "The guild sealed it after the crown took the mountain. Aldric's master's generation. They thought if they forgot the road hard enough, the crown couldn't use it. They were wrong, obviously — Sera says the court's brokers come and go through here all the time. The guild forgot. The crown didn't."

"The crown is very good at remembering things other people want forgotten." He said it flatly, without self-pity, but I heard the edge in it. He'd been the crown's memory once. Its knife with a ledger.

I climbed to the ledge behind the water curtain, and there it was.

* * *

I have opened a great many doors in my life. Most of them were ordinary — oak and iron, hinges and rot. Some were extraordinary in craft: the guild's hidden ways, cut through living rock so smoothly you couldn't find the seams. None of them prepared me for this.

The Gate of the Underway was not built. It was *grown*, or perhaps grown was the wrong word — it was as if the mountain had been persuaded, long ago, to open a seam in itself and then think better of it. The stone around the doorway flowed in whorls like frozen water, and the door itself was a slab of black glassy material that showed reflections that were slightly, subtly wrong. In its surface I saw myself, and my reflection wore an expression I wasn't making. Fear? Longing? It was hard to say. It looked like a person who had already been inside.

Beside it, cut deep and worn shallow by centuries of rain, ran words in Old Guild Script. I translated aloud, because some things you need to hear in your own voice:

*"Here ends the sun's warrant. What passes below passes unblessed. The Guild seals this way in sorrow, not in safety, for the mountain keeps what the mountain takes, and no road runs home from the deep courts."*

"No road runs home," Gideon repeated quietly.

"That's the part the guild chose to engrave. Not *danger*. Not *death*. *No road home*." I touched the letters. The stone was warm. Everything else in the gorge was cold, and this door was warm as a sleeping animal. "They weren't warning us about the mountain, Vale. They were warning us about ourselves. About wanting something down there badly enough to stay."

He was silent a moment. When he spoke, his voice was rough. "You could still turn back. Take the south ways, be over the border in nine days. Start a new guild in a country with no crown. I'd—" He stopped. Started again. "I'd understand."

It was, I realized later, the closest he ever came to saying *don't go, I can't lose you too*, and he wrapped it in permission because wrapping things in permission was the only kindness his training had left him.

"No," I said. "Aldric's down there. And everyone else they've taken. And besides—" I laughed, and it came out shakier than I intended. "Besides, I'm the last apprentice of the Wayfarers' Guild. If I won't walk the last road, who will?"

"You," he said, "are going to be the death of me."

"Probably. But I'll make it memorable."

* * *

Here is what I have not told anyone, not even the pages of this account, until now: I nearly turned back at the threshold. Not from fear of the mountain. From fear of the dead.

Because they had come, all of them, the gathered centuries of them, and they pressed close against the door like faces against a window, and they were not warning me anymore. They were *begging*. And begging cuts deeper than warning, the way grief cuts deeper than fear.

"*Don't open it, don't open it, she'll know your name before you're through.*"

"*My daughter is still down there. Four hundred years and she is still down there. Don't let them add you to her.*"

"*The door remembers everyone who passes. It will remember you forever.*"

"*Little listener.*" That one was close, and old, and kind, and it stopped my breath. "*Little listener, you hear us. Tell them. Tell them what we paid. Tell them the price of the deep courts is not death — death would be mercy. The price is being kept.*"

I put my palm flat against the black door, and it was warm, and through the warmth I felt them — hundreds, thousands, the whole sediment of the stolen centuries, pressing toward me like light toward a crack. And underneath the begging, underneath the warning, so faint I might have invented it, I heard something else.

Hope. Thin as a wire. *Someone has come to the door who can hear us. Someone has come who might open more than a door.*

I took my hand back. My palm print stayed on the black surface a moment, glowing faintly, then faded.

"Coral?" Gideon's hand on my shoulder. Steady. Warm. Alive. "What did they say?"

I told him the truth, or most of it. "They said the price of the deep courts isn't death. It's being kept." I looked up at him, at this disgraced, stubborn, impossible man who had taken a bolt for me and confessed to me in a fever and never once, not once, told me to be less than I was. "And they said the door remembers everyone who passes."

"Then let's give it something worth remembering."

Gods help me, I smiled. At the threshold of hell, with the dead keening softly at my back, I smiled — because that was the moment the plan stopped being a plan and became a fact, irreversible as a stone dropped down a well, and the only thing left to do was drop.

I drew my knife and pried at the seam where the black slab met its frame. The door resisted — then sighed, the way old wood sighs, the way old grief sighs, and swung inward onto darkness and the smell of deep stone and, far below, faint as a memory of music, the sound of singing.

Hundreds of voices. Singing in the walls of the world.

Gideon lit the lantern. The light reached maybe ten feet into a passage that sloped down and down, smooth-worn, ancient, patient.

"After you, wayfarer," he said.

"After you, inquisitor," I said. "One of us should lead, and one of us should watch our backs, and I know which one of us the dead are talking to."

He didn't argue. That should have worried me more than it did.

I stepped through the Gate of the Underway, and the door closed behind us without a sound, and somewhere above, muffled by a hundred feet of stone, the river remembered how to fall.
