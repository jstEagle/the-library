## 14. Court of Borrowed Faces

*Coral*

The papers Sera gave us said we were brokers named Vess and Marrow, licensed under the seal of a counting-house that had burned down eleven years ago. The crown's bureaucracy, it turned out, was exactly as thorough as the guild's — which is to say, not at all where it mattered.

"You look like a man who sells souls," I told Gideon as we dressed in the antechamber beneath the falls.

"I was one for nine years. It should fit like an old boot."

"It fits like a noose," I said, straightening his collar with hands that were steadier than they had any right to be. "That's the point."

The gate itself had been the worst of it. Not the climbing, not the cold — the dead at the threshold, hundreds of them layered in the stone like sediment, whispering *turn back* and *go on* and *we were told to go on too*. I'd stood there with my palms flat against the rock and let them pour through me until my knees buckled, and Gideon had held me up without asking what they said, because by then he knew better than to ask what they said. He just waited until I could stand, and then he said, "Which way?" and I pointed, and we went.

Now we went down.

The road into the mountain was not a smuggler's road. That was the first thing I noticed, and it offended something deep in my professional soul. Smuggler roads are built to be forgotten — narrow, mean, apologetic. This was a processional. The walls were dressed stone veined with something pale and luminous, and the veins pulsed, slow, like breathing. Like a great many breaths, slightly out of rhythm.

"Do you feel that?" Gideon asked quietly.

"That's not feeling. That's hearing." I stopped walking. "Gideon. The walls are singing."

He looked at me the way he always did when I said things like this — half dread, half hunger, a man who had spent his life insisting the world was empty of ghosts and now couldn't stop looking for them. "Singing what?"

I pressed my ear to the stone. The song resolved the way fog resolves into shapes when you stare long enough. Not words. Names. Hundreds of names, thousands, sung over and over in voices that had been worn down to their last thread of self, the way a coin worn smooth stops being anyone's face and becomes only money.

"Names," I said. My voice came out wrong. "They're singing names."

* * *

*Gideon*

I have attended courts before. I have stood in the Hall of Judgments in my white-and-scarlet and watched men sentenced to fires they did not deserve, and I told myself for nine years that this was order, that order was worth the cost, that the alternative was chaos and the gifted running wild with power no mortal law could check.

Nothing prepared me for the court beneath Thornfell.

We came up out of the processional road into a cavern so vast the ceiling was a rumor, hung with chandeliers that burned a color I refuse to call light. Blue-white, cold, steady. Under that light everything looked slightly wrong, the way faces look wrong in water. And the court itself — the court was a masquerade with no masks.

They wore stolen identities openly. That was the horror of it. A woman in cloth-of-gold turned to greet us and her face was beautiful and forty years old and utterly vacant, and behind her eyes — Coral squeezed my arm, hard, once — behind her eyes was nothing of her own. She introduced herself as Lady Amaranth Voss, and Coral's fingers dug into my sleeve again, and later, in the shadow of a pillar, Coral whispered: "That name belongs to a shepherd's daughter from Bellharrow who died in the second purge. I heard her grandmother weeping about it through the market wall."

"She's wearing a dead girl's name like a coat."

"She's wearing a dead girl's *everything*. Look at her hands. Those aren't her gestures. Someone taught her those gestures."

I looked. I wished I hadn't. The woman moved with borrowed grace, laughed with borrowed warmth, and when she passed close enough, I felt what I can only describe as a draft — cold air moving where no air should move, the absence of a soul leaving room for something else to fill it.

"What fills them?" I asked. "If the soul is sold — what's left walking around?"

Coral didn't answer. Coral had gone very still, head tilted, listening to something I couldn't hear, and their expression was doing something I had learned to fear more than any ambush.

"Coral. What fills them?"

"The walls," Coral said. "The walls are full of the ones who weren't bought yet. And some of the ones who were bought are still in there too, singing, because—" Their voice cracked and reassembled itself. "Because the body walks around wearing the name but the name itself is still in the stone. They keep the names. The names are the merchandise. You can't sell a soul twice unless you keep the receipt."

I am an educated man. I have read every treatise on the metaphysics of the gifted ever written by the Office of Inquiry, most of them authored or approved by me. In none of them — not one — had anyone written down what happens after the fire, after the taking. We wrote about containment. We wrote about threat. We never once asked where the souls went, because asking would have made us accomplices rather than servants, and the crown preferred its servants innocent.

"We're getting Aldric," I said. "And then—"

"And then nothing. There is no 'and then.'" Coral turned to face me fully, and under the cold chandelier-light their eyes were wet and furious. "Gideon, I have been listening to these walls for ten minutes and I have counted past four hundred distinct voices. Four hundred. And that's just the ones strong enough to sing. Do you understand? This isn't a rescue anymore. This was never going to be a rescue. We walk out of here with one old man and leave four hundred people in the masonry, then what are we?"

I didn't have an answer. I had spent three weeks telling myself the mission was Aldric — clean, bounded, achievable. One soul, one extraction, one debt paid. It had kept me walking. It had kept me from thinking about the ledger, all those pages, all those entries, the neat columns of harvest dates stretching forward twenty years.

"Then we come back," I said finally.

"With what army?"

"With whatever we've got." I took their hand. It was cold. "You rouse the dead better than any army I've ever commanded. I've seen it."

"That was four ghosts in a tollhouse. This is a mountain."

"Yes," I said. "It is."

* * *

*Coral*

Here is what nobody tells you about hearing the dead at scale: it has a music to it. One ghost is a voice. Ten is a chorus. Four hundred is a weather system. I had to learn to stand inside it the way you stand inside rain — let it fall through you without drowning — or I was going to stand in the middle of the sovereign's court and start screaming names at nobles who would smile their borrowed smiles and have us harvested before dessert.

So I stood inside it. Rain of names. And I did what guild apprentices do with overwhelming information: I mapped it.

The song had geography. Stronger voices clustered toward the east wall, near a door guarded by things in gray livery whose faces I decided not to examine closely. Fainter threads ran downward, deeper, toward a heat I could feel in my teeth. And threading through everything, everywhere, one voice louder than the rest — not stronger, just *fresher*, still carrying the shape of a living throat.

Aldric.

He wasn't singing his own name. He was singing mine. Over and over, patient as a lesson, the way he used to repeat map coordinates until I had them in my bones: *Coral. Coral. Coral.* Not calling for help. Marking his location. Teaching me the one coordinate that mattered, trusting that if anything of him survived in there, I would find it the way I found everything — by listening.

I excused myself from Gideon's side, walked to a pillar, and put my hand flat against the stone where the voice was loudest.

"I hear you," I whispered, so quietly that even the thing wearing Lady Voss's face ten feet away couldn't have caught it. "Hold on, old man. I'm good at maps. Even broken ones."

The singing stuttered. Just once. Then, faintly, wryly, in the exact tone he used when I'd solved a problem he thought would take me all night:

*Took you long enough.*

I laughed. It came out as something between a laugh and a sob, and I covered it with a cough, and a passing noble in a stolen admiral's face nodded graciously at me as if grief were a perfectly acceptable court manner.

* * *

*Gideon*

The auction floor opened at dusk — though dusk underground is a courtesy, a dimming of the false lights, nothing more. We presented our papers at the broker's table, and a clerk with the gray, stretched look of someone long since emptied checked our seals against a ledger, and I watched Coral hold their terror behind a broker's bored mask and thought, not for the first time, that the Wayfarers' Guild trained spies far better than the Office of Inquiry ever trained inquisitors.

"Lot numbers post at moonrise," the clerk said. "Viewing of stock begins now, for licensed buyers only."

Stock. I signed the viewing register with Vess's flourish and felt the ink dry like a brand.

The viewing hall was a long gallery, and along it, in alcoves behind glass that hummed faintly with warding, sat the merchandise. Not bodies. Bodies were cheap; the crown burned those publicly and called it justice. What sat in the alcoves were vessels — crystal, ceramic, lead-sealed jars — each etched with a name, each holding a light that moved. Some of the lights were calm. Some beat against their glass like birds.

Coral stopped at one jar and did not move for a long time.

I came to stand beside them. Inside the glass, a small stubborn light, and etched beneath it in a clerk's careful hand: *ALDRIC OF THORNFELL, WAYFARER'S GUILD. APPRAISED: RARE. PROVENANCE: INTACT.*

Intact. As if he were a manuscript. As if fifty years of teaching a stubborn apprentice to read stone and fog and the silence between heartbeats could be entered in a ledger as provenance.

"He's in there," Coral said. Barely sound at all. "He's awake, Gideon. He knows we're looking at him."

"Then he also knows we found him," I said. "Twenty days early."

"No." Coral's hand rose and hovered over the glass without touching it. When they spoke again, their voice had changed — flattened, precise, the voice they used for coordinates. "No, Gideon. He knows something else. Listen to how the light moves. Steady. Deliberate. He's not panicking. He's *waiting*. He expected us." A pause. "And he's terrified anyway. Not for himself." Coral finally looked at me, and their face in the cold light was older than I had ever seen it. "For us. He can see the room. He can see the doors, the guards, the wards. He's spent fifty years mapping places like this and he already knows what I'm only starting to understand."

"Which is?"

"That we were never going to walk out of here the way we walked in." Coral turned back to the jar, and the small light inside flared once, gently, like a hand squeezing a shoulder. "The plan was Aldric. But Aldric is one jar in a gallery of hundreds, and every single one of them is somebody's mentor, somebody's mother, somebody's child the crown called a threat. He's trying to tell me to take him and run." Their jaw set. "And I'm trying to figure out how to tell him I've already decided to disobey him."

Above us, somewhere in the dark of the vast cavern, a bell began to toll — slow, silken, ceremonial. The clerk's voice carried across the gallery, pleasant as a market crier announcing fish:

"Honored brokers. The solstice court convenes. Lot one will enter bidding within the hour."

Coral's hand closed into a fist at their side.

"Lot one," they repeated softly. Then, to me, with a terrible calm: "Gideon. Go find out what lot one is. Because I think the mountain just started selling before we were ready — and I want to know exactly what we're about to lose."
