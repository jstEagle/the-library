## 15. Aldric, Sold

The holding cells beneath the court were not cells at all. They were shelves.

I understood that the moment Sera's directions delivered us past the last of the soul-broker checkpoints and into a long gallery carved from the mountain's living bone. Niches lined both walls, floor to ceiling, hundreds of them, each one just tall enough for a standing person and just deep enough to keep them. Some were occupied. Most were not — because most of what the crown harvested didn't need a body anymore. The bodies were storage for the ones still in use.

Gideon went ahead of me with his inquisitor's blade drawn low against his thigh, wearing the borrowed face of a broker named Vessick whose seal we'd lifted off a drunk man two galleries back. I wore the face of nobody in particular, which is a skill my guild taught me before it taught me maps: be unremarkable, be furniture, be a shadow with paperwork. I carried a ledger under my arm that Gideon had forged in three hours by candlelight, and if anyone asked, I was an appraiser come to verify inventory before the solstice auction.

Nobody asked. That was somehow worse. Nobody asked because down here, nobody expected the inventory to have friends.

"Coral." Gideon's voice came barely above breath. He'd stopped at a junction where the gallery split, and he was looking at the wall to his left with an expression I had learned to read as carefully as contour lines. It wasn't horror, exactly. Horror is a storm; this was weather. The flat, settled dread of a man who had helped build this place and was only now seeing it finished. "Third gallery. The one marked with the sun-and-scales."

"I see it."

"The niches there are for high-value lots. Souls with craft in them." His jaw worked. "Guild-trained gifts would qualify."

"Then let's go shopping," I said, and hated how steady my voice came out, because inside it nothing was steady at all. Inside it I could already hear them.

That's the thing about my gift that no witch-hunter's manual ever got right. They wrote about hearing the dead as though it were eavesdropping — voices, whispers, secrets pried loose. But the dead aren't only the dead. A soul that's been pulled out of its body isn't dead. It's *displaced*, and displacement has a sound like a bell struck underwater, like a name called from the far side of a valley, like — I don't know. Like home when you're a long way from it and someone else has moved in.

The third gallery rang with it. Dozens of bells, all underwater, all calling names I couldn't make out. I pressed my palm flat against the stone as we walked, the way I do on the hollow roads, letting the mountain tell me where things were. And there — faint, frayed, but unmistakable, worn thin as an old coin passed through too many hands —

*Aldric.*

* * *

He was in the eleventh niche.

I knew it before I saw him, the way you know a road by the feel of it under your boots. The gift reached ahead of me and found him and my whole body turned toward that niche like a compass needle, and then I rounded the corner and there he was, and every map I had ever drawn fell out of my head at once.

Master Aldric Thorne, last senior wayfinder of the Wayfarers' Guild, stood upright in a slot in the mountain with his hands folded in front of him like a man waiting politely for a coach that is very late.

They hadn't bound him. They hadn't needed to. His eyes were open and they were looking straight through the wall in front of him, and they were the color of river water in winter, and there was nothing behind them. Nothing looking out. I had spent nineteen years being scolded by those eyes, corrected by them, occasionally — on my finer days — approved of by them. Aldric's gaze had weight. It could pin you to a doorway mid-sneak-out like a specimen card. And now it was aimed at nothing, and the man inside it had been carried off somewhere to be weighed and lot-numbered and sold.

"Aldric." My voice cracked on the second syllable. I tried again. "Master Thorne. It's Coral. It's Coral Nystrom, you stubborn old goat, look at me."

Nothing. Not a blink. The torchlight guttered in its bracket and his shadow didn't move either, which should have been impossible, and I realized with a lurch that his chest was rising so slowly, so shallowly, that he was barely bothering to breathe. The body was running on its lowest setting. A lamp turned down to a spark so the oil lasts until the buyer arrives.

Behind me, Gideon made a sound I'd never heard him make. Low, and short, and furious in a way that had nowhere to go. "This is a preservation niche," he said. "I've read the specifications. They keep the vessel healthy for transfer. The soul's already been extracted?"

"Two days ago, maybe three." I could hear it even now, that underwater bell, except it wasn't ringing from inside him anymore. It was ringing from somewhere deeper in the mountain, down where the auction house kept its vault. "He's still alive in there. What's left of him."

"Coral—"

"Don't." I held up a hand without turning around. "Whatever sentence starts with *Coral* and ends with *we can't*, save it. I'm not in the mood for realism."

I stepped into the niche.

It was exactly as wide as it looked, which meant that standing in it beside him put our shoulders nearly touching, and I did that deliberately, because I wanted him to feel a body next to his body, warmth against warmth, in case some animal part of him was still home and taking notes. I took his hand. His fingers were cool and dry and they lay in mine like something borrowed from a statue.

"Listen to me," I said. "You covered our escape. You kicked me through the guildhall's back passage and told me — I quote — *run, you disaster, the roads remember you*. Well, the roads remembered me. I'm here. I walked every cursed mile of your precious Hollow Road with a disgraced inquisitor who snores, and I mapped two new branches on the way, and I am going to stand here and talk at you until one of us gets a reaction, and I have a very high tolerance for tedium. Ask anyone. Ask the dead. Half of them complain about it."

His hand didn't close around mine. But his breathing hitched. Once. A tiny stutter in the rhythm, like a cart wheel catching a rut.

"There you are," I whispered.

* * *

We stayed. There was no good tactical reason to stay. Every hour we lingered in the third gallery was an hour closer to the solstice, an hour in which some clerk might notice that Broker Vessick's itinerary didn't exist, an hour in which Sera Kest's diversion at the upper gate might fail or succeed or simply expire. Gideon laid all of this out for me in the flat, patient voice he uses when he thinks he's losing an argument, and I listened to all of it, and then I sat down on the cold floor of the niche with my back against Aldric's legs like I used to sit against the guildhall stove, and I said, "Then we'll be quick."

He crouched across from me. In the torchlight his face was doing several things at once, none of them simple. "Coral. If the extraction's complete, talking to him is — it's not reaching him. You know that better than anyone. You've heard what's left when they take everything."

"I've heard shells," I said. "Empty rooms with wind in them. This isn't that." I lifted Aldric's hand again, turned it over, showed Gideon the calluses. "Look. Look at his hands. When they pull a soul clean out, the body forgets its shape — I've felt it in the tollhouse ghosts, the ones who've been gone longest. The body stops being a body. It becomes a coat on a hook." I pressed my thumb into the thick scar across Aldric's palm, the one he got at Greywater Span forty years ago hauling a drowning apprentice out of the ice — the story he told so many times the whole guild could recite it in rounds. "But this hand still remembers. Something's still holding the shape. They didn't take all of him, Gideon. They couldn't. Guild souls are knotted too tight. He'd have fought the extraction, and fighting leaves snags."

"You're guessing."

"I'm *mapping*," I snapped. "There's a difference. Guessing is what you do when you haven't done the work. I have done the work. Nineteen years of listening to what souls sound like when they're whole, torn, frayed, buried, burned, and sold. And Master Aldric Thorne sounds frayed, not gone. So we are going to sit here, and I am going to talk to him, and you are going to stand watch, and if you say the word 'tactical' one more time I will feed you to the first ghost I meet and let them explain patience to you at length."

For a long moment he just looked at me. Then something shifted in his face — not surrender, exactly. Recognition. The look of a man watching someone do the thing he'd have done, once, before the hunt taught him to count bodies instead of people.

"All right," he said quietly. "Talk."

So I talked.

I told Aldric about the journey down — the Hollow Road shifting around us, showing Gideon the flooded chapel where Coral's mother had been married, the tollhouse full of angry dead who'd finally gotten their justice spoken aloud. I told him Sera Kest was working our side of the board now, and watched Gideon flinch slightly at how casually I said it, because neither of us had fully processed that yet. I told him the ledger listed his lot number — 214, solstice evening, reserve price undisclosed — and that Gideon had his own name inked among future harvests, and that we intended to ruin everyone involved.

And then, because the tactical part of my brain had finally gone quiet enough to let the honest part speak, I told him the rest.

"You told me once that the guild doesn't mourn its roads, it mourns its walkers. You said roads can always be cut again, but a walker who's lost—" My voice snagged. I pushed through it. "I thought that was wisdom. It's not. It's just what you say so you can sleep. Because I'm sitting here and I would trade every road we ever cut, every secret route from Thornfell to the sea, every map in the guild archive — I'd burn the lot — to see you scold me about my bootlaces one more time. And I think you'd tell me that's exactly why the crown wins. Because they make us choose between the small love and the large work. And I'm telling you now, to your face, while you're off being furniture in your own skull: I refuse. I refuse the choice. I'm going to get you back AND break their whole rotten system, and if that's greedy, then the guild raised me greedy."

Aldric's breathing stuttered again. Longer this time. And — I swear this on every map I've ever drawn — his little finger curled. Just slightly. Just enough to hook against mine.

Gideon saw it too. He went very still, the way he does when the world confirms something he'd decided not to hope for. "Coral."

"I know."

"He's in there."

"I *know*."

"And the auction is tomorrow night."

"I know that too," I said, and the words came out with edges on them. I looked up at Aldric's empty eyes, at the slow tide of his breathing, at the mountain holding him like a coin in a fist, and everything soft in me hardened into something with a plan attached. "Which means we have one day to get into that vault, find lot 214, and figure out how to put a soul back where it belongs. And since the crown's own inquisitor is sitting right here—"

"I was never trained in restoration," Gideon said. "The hunt burns. It doesn't mend."

"Then we find someone who mends." I stood, joints cracking, and slung the forged ledger back under my arm. "Because there are hundreds of souls singing in these walls, Gideon, and every single one of them was put here by someone who assumed they'd stay bought. Tomorrow night, the buyers come. The brokers come. The sovereign comes up from the deep court to gloat over the merchandise." I looked at the niche one more time — at Aldric, patient as stone, keeping his shape, keeping his snags, keeping whatever small stubborn ember of himself he'd refused to surrender.

"Hang on, old man," I told him. "Your disaster apprentice is coming back with a crowbar."

And from somewhere far below us — down past the galleries, past the vaults, down in the dark where the auction house kept its treasures — I heard the underwater bells all shift at once. Hundreds of displaced souls, turning together like birds, like a current, like a road remembering its walker.

They'd heard me. And they were waiting.
