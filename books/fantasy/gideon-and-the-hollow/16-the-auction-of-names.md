## 16. The Auction of Names

I had attended executions that were better organized.

The solstice court convened in a cavern so vast the architects had clearly given up on dignity somewhere around the third century and settled for awe. Stalactites hung from the ceiling like chandeliers nobody had bothered to light, and instead the glow came from below — veins of pale luminescence running through the floor itself, so that every noble in attendance appeared to be standing on a sheet of ice over something drowning. Which, I suppose, was accurate.

Coral stood beside me in the borrowed robes of a soul-broker, hood up, hands folded with a serenity I knew was performance because I'd watched them practice it in a river cave while muttering curses at the fit of the sleeves. We were positioned among two dozen other brokers near the base of the auction dais, close enough to see everything, far enough — we hoped — to be beneath notice.

Beneath notice. In a room full of people who wore stolen faces. The optimism of the plan still impressed me when I let myself think about it, which is why I mostly didn't.

Below us, sunk into the floor like a jewel in a setting, was the Vault: a ring of glassy stone through which the souls awaiting sale could be seen as faint lights, hundreds of them, drifting like plankton. And threaded through the walls, through the very rock, Coral said they could hear them singing. I couldn't hear it. But I could feel it now, faintly, the way you feel a choir through a cathedral floor — a pressure in the teeth, a wrongness in the air, as if the mountain itself were holding one long held breath.

"They're starting," Coral murmured.

* * *

The auctioneer was called Master Quillon, and he had the voice of a man who had spent forty years making cruelty sound like courtesy. He ascended the dais in silver-grey robes, bowed to the sovereign's empty throne — the sovereign, I learned, preferred to watch from a balcony shrouded in gauze, which told me either great power or great vanity, and in my experience those traveled together — and spread his arms over the Vault like a farmer surveying a particularly good harvest.

"Honored collectors. Honored custodians." His voice carried without effort, rolling into every corner of the cavern. "Another year closes. Another solstice comes. And once again, the crown demonstrates its generosity: taking what is dangerous, refining what is precious, and offering it back to you — purified, bound, and permanent."

Purified. I thought of the child I hadn't burned, three months and a lifetime ago, and the word sat in my chest like a swallowed coal.

"The catalogue this season is exceptional." Quillon gestured, and a clerk unrolled a scroll the length of a sail. "We begin, as tradition demands, with the oldest lots — souls held in trust across generations — before proceeding to this year's acquisitions."

Acquisitions. Aldric was an acquisition. Somewhere in that scroll was his name between a line and a price, and I felt Coral go rigid beside me, felt the moment their grief sharpened into something with an edge on it.

"Steady," I breathed.

"I'm steady," they whispered back. "I'm just deciding how much of this room deserves to keep its ceiling."

"That's the opposite of steady."

"It's adjacent to it."

I would have smiled if my mouth had been working properly. It wasn't. Because the clerk had begun reading names, and each name came with a provenance — *recovered from a hedge-coven in the eastern fens; taken from a storm-speaker off the Greywater coast; surrendered by the family of a gifted child in Bellharrow* — and each provenance was a life I had helped end or failed to save, read aloud in a pleasant monotone while nobles in borrowed faces nodded and sipped wine.

That was the thing about wearing an inquisitor's memories: they didn't stay where you put them.

* * *

The early lots sold quickly. A merchant bought a weather-singer's voice, apparently to protect his shipping lanes. A lady whose face I recognized — not her real face, her real face had been hanged in Ashmoor two years ago under my own warrant — purchased a healer's touch for her son, "the boy's constitution being delicate." She paid in letters of credit drawn on a bank whose founder's soul, I happened to know, had been lot eleven some decades past. The economy of it all was almost elegant. Almost. Elegance is just violence that's been taught manners.

Then the clerk reached the middle of the scroll, and Quillon's voice took on the particular warmth he must have reserved for premium stock.

"Lot sixty-one. A soul of unusual depth and discipline. A scholar of the old roads, keeper of ways and crossings, taken intact by the crown's hunters at considerable risk." A pause, expertly measured. "Aldric Vane, last master of the Wayfarers' Guild."

Beside me, Coral made a sound I will hear until I die — small, bitten-off, the sound of a person swallowing a scream whole and keeping it down.

On the dais, a globe of glass rose from the Vault on a column of pale light, and inside it something turned. Not a flame, exactly. More like a lamp seen through deep water, warm gold, patient, turning slowly as though it were looking for someone.

Looking for someone. I understood then that it was. That Aldric — whatever remained of him, whatever the binding had left lucid — was searching the crowd for the apprentice he'd sent running.

"Opening bid," Quillon said, "one thousand crowns."

What happened next, I did not decide so much as discover myself already doing it. There is a version of me — the man I was for eleven years — who would have stood in that crowd and appraised lot sixty-one professionally and gone home to sleep well. I had met him. I had *been* him. And the entire distance between him and me, the whole length of my fall and Coral's rise, compressed itself into the four steps it took to walk out of the broker's row, throw back my hood, and say, in the carrying voice they train into you at the Inquisitors' Hall:

"This proceeding is halted by authority of the Crown's Commission on Gifts."

The effect was immediate and deeply satisfying. Two hundred heads turned. Quillon's smile froze into something architectural. Somewhere in the gallery, a woman gasped, and a guard started forward and then stopped, because recognition travels fast in rooms full of guilty people, and there are faces the powerful learn to fear the way sailors learn weather.

"Gideon Vale," someone whispered. Then, louder, disbelieving: *Vale.*

"Inquisitor Vale is dead," Quillon said smoothly. "Struck from the rolls. A traitor."

"Then it's fortunate," I said, climbing the dais steps, "that I kept my credentials." I drew them out — the seal-ring, the commission papers, the black-and-silver warrant card I had carried for eleven years and had not been able to make myself throw into the river, thanks be to whatever god keeps sentimental men alive. "And that the Commission's authority, per its own charter, can only be revoked by the sovereign in person. Who is, I note, watching from behind a curtain." I turned to the gallery and raised my voice. "Unless our sovereign would care to show her face and revoke it herself? No? Then by law — *your* law, the law you all hide behind when the burning gets uncomfortable — these proceedings are suspended pending review."

"You have no review to offer," Quillon said. "You're a dead man playing dress-up."

"Probably." I let them see me agree with him. It unsettled people more than denial does. "But before I'm killed, you'll want to hear what a dead man found in your own ledger."

I held up the book.

* * *

I should explain about the ledger, briefly, because the moment deserves its context. Three weeks ago, in a tollhouse full of ghosts, Coral had shown me what my hunt had buried at Greywater. After that I went looking for the accounting of it — because hunters keep accounts, it's the first thing they teach you, every gift catalogued, every soul weighed — and I found it in Sera Kest's field copy, which she gave me later, at knifepoint, with tears she'd deny to her grave. Names. Dates. Provenances. Prices. Eleven years of my work, translated into inventory.

I opened it on the auctioneer's own dais and began to read.

"Aldric Vane, taken alive by order of the Commission, harvest authorized under Seal of the Solstice Court. Sera Kest's brother, Tomas Kest, harvested eight years ago, listed as *sold, private buyer, name withheld by buyer's request.* The massacre at Greywater Span — forty-one gifted dead — recorded not as a tragedy but as a *bulk acquisition*, discounted for condition." My voice didn't shake. I'd spent eleven years learning to read horror aloud without shaking. It was the one skill from my old life that transferred cleanly. "And here—" I turned the page slowly, letting the gallery lean in, letting them see me find it fresh, though I'd known for weeks— "here is a list of future harvests. Approved. Scheduled. Including—" I looked up. "Including Gideon Vale, former inquisitor, flagged *gifted, undiagnosed*, harvest authorized upon capture."

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight and temperature.

Because that was the poison in the page, and I made sure to pour it slow: *undiagnosed.* If the crown could list me — me, who built their hunt, who wrote half their protocols — as a future lot, merely on suspicion, then no one in this cavern was safe. Not the buyers. Not the brokers. Not the nobles in their borrowed faces, who had perhaps forgotten that the faces themselves had come through this same machinery, and that machinery did not distinguish between raw material and loyal customer forever.

"You see the arrangement," I said quietly. "You are not the crown's partners. You are its larder, temporarily granted table privileges."

Quillon moved then — quick, for an old vulture — signaling the guards with a flick of two fingers. I had counted them earlier. Fourteen. I'd also noticed that six of them stood too close to the exits, the way men stand when they've decided which way they'll run.

What I had not anticipated was Sera.

* * *

She came down the gallery stairs in full hunter's black, crossbow slung, five of her hunters behind her, and every instinct I owned screamed that this was the arrest, that the plan had gotten us exactly far enough to die publicly. Coral's hand found mine behind the fold of my robe — a grip like a promise and a farewell in one.

But Sera walked past the guards. Past Quillon. She stopped at the base of the dais and looked up at me with an expression I'd seen on her maybe twice in fifteen years: the face she wore when the mission and her conscience had finally finished their argument.

"Sera," I said. "Kest."

"Don't." Her voice cracked on it. "Don't do the formal thing, Gideon, I will put a bolt in you out of sheer embarrassment." She turned to the gallery instead, and pulled a folded paper from her breastplate — her own commission, hunter's seal, twenty years of service. "Sera Kest, First Hunter of the Thornfell Circuit," she announced, loud enough to reach the balconies. "And I am telling every soul in this room that the ledger that man holds is genuine. I verified it against the Commission's master copy. I checked my brother's name myself." Her jaw worked. "Tomas Kest. Harvested eight years ago. Sold. I hunted gifted folk for twenty years believing we were protecting the realm, and the realm was *shopping*."

She threw her commission on the floor. The clatter of it rang off the stalactites.

"Hunters!" she called, and her five stepped forward, and one by one they removed their badge-pins and dropped them beside her discarded commission, a small bright rain of renunciation. "The circuit stands down. Anyone wishing to arrest us may form a queue behind the gentleman with the ledger."

Quillon had gone the color of candle tallow. "This is sedacy—"

"It's arithmetic," Sera said. "You're outnumbered, the guards are calculating their pensions, and half your buyers just realized their collateral is evidence. Do the sums, Master Quillon."

He did the sums. I watched him do them — watched the exact moment the auctioneer's self-preservation overrode the sovereign's interests, the little collapse behind the eyes of a man realizing which side of the ledger he himself appeared on. He looked at the balcony, at the shrouded figure behind the gauze, and received nothing. No command. No protection. The sovereign, it seemed, was suddenly very interested in not being identifiable.

And that was when the singing started.

* * *

I heard it this time. Everyone did.

It began in the walls — a low thread of sound, then another, then dozens, harmonies rising out of the rock itself, out of the Vault, out of the glass globe where Aldric's soul turned faster now, brighter, like a lamp being fed. The imprisoned dead, the harvested thousands, centuries of them, singing through the stone of their prison. Later Coral would tell me what they'd done — how they'd stopped hiding their gift for the first time in their life and *called* to every soul in the mountain at once, an open hand offered in the dark — but in that moment I only knew that the room's entire architecture of denial had developed cracks, and through the cracks came voices.

Nobles recoiled. One woman tore at her own face — her borrowed face — screaming a name that wasn't hers. A broker dropped his case of binding-vials, and the glass sang too, high and thin, in sympathy.

"Lot sixty-one," I said, into the chaos, because someone had to, and because I am apparently incapable of leaving a stage without a flourish. "Is withdrawn. Permanently. As are all lots, all sales, all debts contracted against souls in this place. The market," I said, and set the ledger down on the auction block where every eye could see it, "is closed."

The globe of glass descended toward the Vault — the bindings reacting, the machinery trying to reclaim its inventory — and Coral was moving before I could shout a warning, hood back, climbing onto the dais rail, reaching for the light with both hands.

And the sovereign's balcony finally, finally stirred.

Gauze parted. A shape rose — tall, crowned in something that was not metal but looked like condensed shadow — and a voice came down into the cavern that made the singing falter, made the lights in the Vault dim, made my knees consider their options.

"*Little thief,*" it said, and though it looked at Coral, I felt the words land on me like a hand on the back of the neck. "*You bring broken things into my house and call it justice. Very well. Come and bargain, then. Or come and break.*"

Coral's fingers closed around the globe of light.

The mountain shook.

* * *

The last thing I saw clearly, before the floor tilted and the first hairline fracture raced across the Vault's glassy ring like lightning finding ground, was Sera planting herself between her hunters and the sovereign's descending guards, crossbow leveled, shouting something I couldn't hear over the rising song of several thousand freed souls.

The last thing I felt was Coral's hand finding mine again in the dark — not a farewell this time.

The mountain was coming down, and we had an appointment with the long way up.
