## 17. The Bargain Beneath

The sovereign's chamber smelled of cold iron and old honey.

I want you to understand that I noticed the honey first. That's the kind of person I am — the kind who walks into a trap and catalogues the décor. The walls were black stone veined with something that glowed faintly gold, like veins of sap in petrified wood, and the ceiling was so far above us that it vanished into a darkness that might have been distance or might have been nothing at all. Braziers burned without smoke. The floor was warm under my boots, which was somehow worse than if it had been cold.

Gideon stood at my left shoulder with his hands loose at his sides, which was his way of saying *I have a knife and I am choosing, for now, not to use it.* Behind us, the great doors of the court had sealed themselves shut without a sound, the way a mouth closes around something it intends to swallow.

And before us, on a throne that looked less like furniture and more like a geological event — black stone grown upward into a shape that suggested a seat only because it wanted to — sat the sovereign of the court beneath Thornfell.

I had expected a monster. I don't know why; habit, probably. Everything else down here had been monstrous in one register or another: the nobles wearing borrowed faces like ill-fitting coats, the auction hall with its rows of glass bells each holding a soul that hummed when you walked past, the corridors where the dead sang through the masonry in voices worn thin as river-glass. I had spent three days in this place listening to hundreds of imprisoned souls sing in the walls, and every note of it had been horror.

The sovereign was a woman who looked like someone's grandmother. Silver hair braided in a crown. A face full of laugh lines. Hands folded in her lap like she was waiting for bread to rise.

That was the first seduction, though I didn't know to call it that yet. She looked *safe*. She looked like the kind of person who would tell you where the clean well was and slip you an extra roll at market. My gift, which had been screaming since we entered the chamber, went suddenly, horribly quiet — not silent, but hushed, the way a room goes quiet when a very old person begins to speak.

"Coral Nystrom," she said, and my name in her mouth sounded like it had been mine longer than I'd owned it. "Last apprentice of the Wayfarers' Guild. Cartographer. Smuggler." A pause, gentle as a hand on the shoulder. "Listener."

"You've been reading my file," I said, because my mouth does that when my spine wants to run.

"I wrote your file," she said, "before you were born."

* * *

Here is what I knew going in, and I want it on the record that I knew it, because later — in the bad nights, in the accounting I'd eventually have to make — I needed to remember that I was never fooled. Not exactly. Seduction doesn't work by lying to you. It works by telling you the truth in a voice you can't resist.

What I knew: the court beneath the mountain ran on harvested souls. The crown above hunted the gifted not out of piety or fear but as husbandry — a cull, a crop, a supply chain with witch-hunters for farmhands and an auction house for a granary. The souls went into the walls, into the roads, into the workings that kept the court rich and hidden and old. Gideon had found his own name in the ledger of future harvests. Aldric — my master, my last family, the man who'd taught me to read stone the way other people read scripture — stood hollowed in a holding cell below us, alive, breathing, empty, his soul already entered into tomorrow's solstice auction as Lot Forty-One.

What I wanted: Lot Forty-One back. Everything else was negotiable.

The sovereign let me stand there for a long moment, letting the silence do her work. Then she rose from the throne — no ceremony, just an old woman getting up, joints audibly protesting, which I did not believe for a second — and came down the steps to stand an arm's length away.

"Your guild built my roads," she said.

"They built their own roads," I said. "You squatted in them."

"Mm." She smiled, and the laugh lines deepened, and somewhere behind my ribs the dead went quiet-quiet, the listening quiet. "Three hundred years ago, the Wayfarers cut passages through the hollow places because they believed no road should belong to any king. Noble sentiment. Expensive hobby. The guild dwindled because the world stopped paying for wonder, Coral, not because the crown destroyed it. And now the guild is three old maps and one frightened apprentice, and the roads are still here, still perfect, still *mine*, because I paid for them in the only coin that lasts."

"Souls."

"Souls," she agreed pleasantly. "Everything durable is made of souls. You know this. You hear it every time you put your palm on a milestone. The dead shepherd in the stones outside Bellharrow — Tomas, wasn't it? Died in the white winter, '61. He's been holding up that stretch of road for sixty years and he sings when you pass because you're the first person in six decades who could hear him." Her head tilted. "Doesn't that strike you as a waste? All that music, and one listener per generation?"

"Don't," Gideon said quietly. It was the first thing he'd spoken since the doors closed. "Don't do the thing where you make it sound reasonable. I invented that thing. I wrote the manual."

"I know you did, Inquisitor Vale." She didn't look at him. She kept looking at me, and her eyes were brown and warm and utterly bottomless. "That's rather why you're both still alive. But this conversation isn't for him."

* * *

She walked, and I followed, and I hate myself a little for how easily I followed — three steps, four, away from Gideon, toward a window I hadn't noticed, a tall slit in the black stone that opened onto —

Not sky. A map.

The whole of Thornfell spread beneath the mountain's skin, rendered in light: the fog-roads, the smuggler ways, the guild paths my mother's mother's mothers had cut and memorized and died keeping secret. And threaded through every road, visible now in a way no living cartographer had ever seen, were lines of gold. Soul-light. The dead in the stones, singing the roads into permanence.

"Do you know why your guild's roads never wash out?" the sovereign asked. "Never shift, never flood, never fall to rockslide, while every other passage in these mountains rewrites itself with the weather? Because your founders bound listeners into the foundations. Willingly, at first — the early Wayfarers gave themselves to the roads the way monks give themselves to cloisters. It was an honor. A vocation." Her finger traced a golden line northward, toward Bellharrow, toward home. "Then the willing ran out, and the guild got squeamish, and the roads began to forget. And I —" she turned that grandmother's smile on me — "I simply continued the tradition. With better bookkeeping."

"You harvest people," Gideon said from behind us. His voice had gone flat, which anyone who knew him understood to be the loudest sound he made.

"I *employ* people," the sovereign said. "Beyond death. Indefinitely. Their gifts persist. Their knowledge persists. The shepherd still knows the hill he watched over. The midwife still knows every door in the town where she worked. Nothing is lost, Coral. Nothing rots. Is that what you think happens otherwise — that death improves things? That the grave is some mercy?" She shook her head, sorrowful, patient. "I am the only reason anything down here remembers anything at all."

And here is the terrible part, the part I'll carry: she wasn't lying. I could hear it. My gift, which had been raised on the whispers of the half-dissolved dead, heard in her words the resonance of truth, the same harmonic a true bell strikes. The souls in her walls weren't screaming. Some of them were. But some of them were *singing*, genuinely singing, contented as bees, and I had no idea what to do with that except feel it land in me like a hook.

"So," I said, and my voice came out steadier than I deserved. "You didn't bring us here to gloat. Auction's tomorrow. You could have let us rot in a cell until Lot Forty-One comes up. This is a sales pitch. So pitch."

* * *

She told me about the bargain the way you'd tell a child about inheritance.

"Aldric's soul is entered in the auction," she said. "Lot Forty-One. A Wayfarer's soul, intact, trained, road-sung — do you know what that's worth? Your master spent forty years learning the hollow roads, and all of that knowing lives in the soul, not the meat. Tomorrow some noble will buy him and set him in a wall, and he will hold their corridor for eternity, and the last of your guild's craft will decorate a rich man's hallway." She let that sit. She was good at letting things sit. "Or."

"Or," I said.

"Or you trade me a better soul for him. Yours."

The chamber went very quiet. Even Gideon didn't move.

"A listener's soul," she said, "bound whole, given willingly, at the height of its power. Do you understand what that means for the roads? Not one corridor. Not one town's worth of milestones. *Every road the guild ever cut.* Every passage, every tollhouse stair, every smuggler's tunnel from the coast to the capital — sung permanent, unbreakable, forever, by the last apprentice who can hear them all. Your guild's life's work, completed. Perfected. Made immortal." She stepped closer, and her voice dropped into something almost tender. "Aldric walks out of here tomorrow morning. Whole. Diminished, perhaps — the years will come back to him, the body will remember what it's owed — but himself. Himself, Coral. And in exchange, you become what your founders dreamed of being. Not a smuggler skulking in fog. The road itself."

I want to be honest, since honesty is apparently the currency down here: I nearly said yes.

Not because I'm brave. Because I'm tired. Because I have been lonely since I was nine years old, hearing dead men in fence posts while the living talked over my head, and she was offering me a loneliness with *purpose*. She was offering me a version of my own life with the fear removed — no more hiding the gift, no more burning letters, no more flinching at criers in the square. Just the work, forever, the work I loved, the work I was born for, held safe inside stone that would never crumble and never ask anything of me again.

She offered me myself, perfected, and all it would cost was everything I hadn't done yet.

"The roads would be unbreakable," I said slowly.

"Unbreakable."

"And Aldric goes free."

"This morning, if you like."

"And everyone else in your walls?"

Her smile didn't falter, which was answer enough, but she gave it words anyway, soft and reasonable as a lullaby: "Everyone else is already home."

* * *

I felt Gideon move before I heard him — that particular tension in the air of a man deciding whether to tackle someone. "Coral."

"I'm thinking."

"Don't think. Listen to me—"

"She's offering him back, Gideon." My voice cracked on the second word and I hated it. "Forty-one years he's been — he taught me to walk in the dark. He covered our escape and they took him *alive* because his soul was worth more intact, and I have been listening to him not-singing for three days, and she is standing there saying she'll hand him back before breakfast—"

"And she's lying."

"Is she?" I turned on him. "Because I can hear her, Gideon. I can hear whether people lie. It's the one thing my cursed gift is actually *good* for, and she is not lying. She'd give him back. She'd love to give him back, because—" and then I heard it, mid-sentence, the trap closing with an audible click somewhere behind my sternum, "—because a willing trade binds deeper than a harvest. Doesn't it."

The sovereign's smile widened by a single degree.

"There it is," she said warmly. "The finest mind the guild has produced in a century. Yes, child. A coerced soul fights its binding forever — that's the flicker you hear in my older walls, the discord, the ones your tender heart counts as screams. But a soul given freely, in trade, for love?" She pressed her hand flat against the black stone, and the gold veins brightened beneath her palm like breath on embers. "A soul given freely *wants* to hold. It becomes the road completely. No discord. No scream. Just song." Her eyes found mine, and for one unguarded instant the grandmother fell away and I saw what looked out from behind the face: something old and patient and hungry as geology. "I don't want your suffering, Coral. Suffering is cheap. I want your *consent*."

"That's worse," Gideon said.

"It's better," she said. "For everyone. Ask her."

And God help me, I almost did. I almost asked. The image was right there, fully furnished: Aldric blinking awake in grey morning light above ground, grumbling about his knees, demanding tea, alive, *himself*, while I sang in the stones beneath his feet and kept every road he ever loved safe until the mountains wore down to gravel. It didn't even look like sacrifice. It looked like coming home.

"Coral." Gideon's hand closed around my wrist — not pulling, just anchoring, the way you anchor someone in a current. His voice was low and rough and absolutely steady. "Tomas. The shepherd in the stones outside Bellharrow. Sixty years in the road. What does he sing?"

I blinked. "What?"

"What does he *sing*. You told me, the night you showed me. Word for word. What was it?"

My mouth opened. Closed. Because I remembered — of course I remembered, I'd carried those words since I was nine — and the remembering hit me like cold water, because the song wasn't a hymn and it wasn't contentment. It was a counting song. An old drover's counting song, the kind shepherds sing to stay awake, and the verse Tomas had been singing into the dark for sixty years, over and over, to whoever might finally listen, was:

*One for the road and two for the gate,*
*three for the year I couldn't wait —*

"He's counting," I whispered. "He's been counting the years. Sixty years of *counting*—"

"Contented bees," Gideon said grimly, jerking his chin at the glowing walls. "Listen again. All of them. Right now. Tell me what you actually hear."

So I did what I had spent my whole life being afraid to do at full strength. I opened the gift all the way — no filters, no flinching, every wall and milestone and golden vein in that chamber pouring into me at once — and I listened past the harmony, past the sweet chord the sovereign had tuned this room to, down underneath it, the way my master had taught me to listen for the second voice in any song.

Underneath, the walls were counting too. Hundreds of voices, thousands, each one numbering its years in the dark. Each one waiting for someone who could hear them. Each one, every single one, hoping.

Hoping meant prisoner. Contentment doesn't hope.

The warmth drained out of the chamber so fast I felt it go. The sovereign's face didn't change, but the room's did — the honey-smell curdling toward iron, the gold veins dimming like a held breath.

"Well," she said, and the grandmother's voice was gone, and what remained was pleasant the way a landslide is gradual. "It seems the last apprentice requires more time to deliberate than anticipated. How unfortunate. The auction opens at dawn, and Lot Forty-One has drawn considerable interest." She turned back toward her throne, then paused, and glanced at me over her shoulder with something that was almost respect. "For what it's worth, Coral Nystrom — I wasn't lying. I would have returned him. I keep my bargains to the letter. It's why I win."

The doors unsealed. Guards appeared — or had always been there, wearing faces I hadn't troubled to see — and took my arms and Gideon's, gently, efficiently, the way you handle things you expect to need again.

"One night to reconsider," the sovereign said, settling onto her throne of grown stone, folding her grandmother's hands. "Do sleep well. And Coral —" that smile one last time, warm as a hearth, false as a painted door — "when you dream tonight, and you will dream of the roads, notice whose voice is singing in them. You may find you've already begun to miss me."

The doors closed behind us. Somewhere below, in a cell I couldn't reach yet, Aldric's hollow body breathed in and out, in and out, keeping time.

Dawn was seven hours away. I had a plan forming — half-formed, mad, built entirely on a counting song and a stolen ledger and an inquisitor's blade that had once struck seals before — and I turned to Gideon in the torchlit corridor, guards' hands still on my arms, and saw the same thought catching fire behind his eyes.

"Get me to the auction floor," I said, "before Lot Forty-One goes up. I'm done trading."
