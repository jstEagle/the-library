## 18. Break the Seal

*Coral*

The sovereign's offer hung in the air like smoke that refused to rise.

*Your gift for his soul,* it had said, in a voice made of every voice at once — the choir of stolen names wearing one crown of sound. *The roads would never break again. The guild would endure forever. You would endure forever.*

Forever. Spoken by a thing that kept souls in jars. I had to admire the salesmanship.

I stood on the dais of the auction hall with Aldric's hollowed body behind me and Gideon's blood still drying on his sleeve from where he'd torn through two guards to reach my side, and I thought about my mother's letters burning in a Bellharrow hearth. About the dead shepherd who'd taught me that stones remember. About every road I'd ever mapped, and how every single one of them had been built on the promise that some things stay hidden so other things can survive.

The sovereign waited. The whole court waited — three hundred nobles in borrowed faces, their masks of other people's lives slipping at the edges now that Gideon had read the ledger aloud. Somewhere in those walls, hundreds of souls were singing. They'd been singing since we came down the falls-road, and I'd been trying not to listen, because listening meant feeling, and feeling meant wanting to answer.

I was done not answering.

"Here's my counteroffer," I said.

Gideon made a small sound beside me — half warning, half prayer. I squeezed his wrist once. *Trust me.* He'd earned the right to be trusted back; he'd spent chapters of his life learning what trust cost.

"I'll give you my gift," I told the sovereign. "Every note of it. Every voice I've ever heard in stone or water or bone. But you have to let them go first." I turned, slowly, so the whole court could watch me point at the walls. "All of them. Every soul in this mountain, released before I sign anything. You get the last Wayfarer's hearing; they get their names back."

A ripple went through the borrowed faces. Fear is contagious even among people wearing someone else's courage.

*You lie,* the sovereign said, amused. *You would not trade. Your kind never trades. That is why your guild is nearly extinct.*

"You're right," I admitted. "I wouldn't trade." And I let the gift come up out of hiding for the first time in my life at full strength, no leash, no flinch, no pretending to be less than I was. "But you believed me for exactly as long as I needed you to."

I opened every door inside myself at once.

*Coral—* Gideon started, and then he understood, because of course he did; he'd spent twenty years hunting people like me and the last month learning what we actually were. "Coral, the toll it takes—"

"Let it take," I said.

I reached into the walls.

* * *

*Gideon*

I have heard souls scream before. It was my profession. I want that written plainly, because everything else I write tonight will be an attempt to balance the ledger: I have heard souls scream, and I made my career on the silence afterward.

What Coral did in the auction hall was not screaming.

It was singing.

They stood on the sovereign's dais with their eyes gone white as river-ice, arms spread, and the walls of the under-mountain answered. Not gradually. All at once, the way a frozen lake answers spring. Three hundred nobles clutched their borrowed faces as the faces began to weep — tears of the true owners, rising up through stolen skin. The great seal above the throne, a disc of black iron wide as a wagon wheel, groaned on its chains.

And Coral's voice — Coral's small, dry, stubborn voice — multiplied until it was everywhere, speaking with the dead in a language I didn't know and understood completely anyway.

*We hear you,* it said. *We hear you. We hear you.*

The singing in the walls stopped being a lament and became a demand.

I had one job. Coral had given it to me in the falls-cave in that flat way they had, the way that meant they'd already grieved over the possibility of failure and decided to proceed anyway: *When they rouse, the seal will be the only thing holding the court together. The sovereign can't stop a thousand souls. But it doesn't have to stop them — it just has to keep its anchor. Strike the seal with the inquisitor's blade while it's anchored, and the whole claim breaks. Every harvest, every ledger entry, every bargain going back four hundred years. Gone.*

"And if I miss?" I'd asked.

"Then don't miss."

Sera Kest had turned her hunters aside an hour ago — turned them, and then aimed them, which was better — and the hall doors were held against the guard by six former witch-hunters who had heard her brother's voice coming out of the walls and made their choice in about four seconds. The court's soldiers were otherwise engaged. Which left me, a disgraced inquisitor with a stolen ceremonial blade and one shot at a wheel of black iron forty feet above a throne, standing next to the person I loved while they burned themselves open to free everyone I'd ever helped imprison.

There was no version of this where I got to be brave quietly. Fine. I'd been loud before.

"CORAL!" I shouted over the rising chorus. "WHEN?"

Their head turned toward me, eyes still blind-white, and their mouth smiled with someone else's humor — the shepherd, maybe, or Aldric's rekindled shard riding close. "*Now* would be traditional," they said in a voice layered like sedimentary rock.

I ran.

* * *

*Coral*

Here is what nobody tells you about hearing the dead: they are not quiet, and they are not gone, and they are not patient. Four hundred years of harvested souls packed into the bones of a mountain, and when one person finally listened — really listened, without flinching, without bargaining, without asking what was in it for the living — they came through me like a river through a broken dam.

It should have destroyed me. I want to be honest about that too. There was a moment — I remember it precisely, the moment the first hundred voices hit — when I felt the shape of myself start to dissolve, all my edges going soft, my name starting to slide off me like wet ink.

And then something held.

Not Gideon, though he was there, running, shouting, being magnificently unsubtle. What held me was the dead themselves. They knew what it cost. They'd all been someone's Coral once — the gifted child dragged from a neighbor's kitchen, the hedge-witch, the shepherd who'd died on Thornfell and stayed anyway. A thousand hands I couldn't see caught the edges of me and held on.

*Steady, little mapmaker,* said the shepherd, in the tone of a man who'd been waiting decades to say something useful. *You hold the door. Let us do the pushing.*

So I held the door.

The sovereign finally understood what was happening. I felt its attention swing away from me — away from the bargain it thought it was winning — toward the seal, toward the anchor of its entire rotten enterprise. It screamed an order in that choir-voice, and the throne room's remaining guards moved, and Sera's hunters met them with a crash like the world ending in miniature.

None of it mattered. None of it could matter. The seal was the only door the sovereign truly owned, and I had already picked it.

*Gideon,* I said — aloud, with my ruined human mouth, because some things you say out loud even when a thousand dead are speaking through you. "Now."

* * *

*Gideon*

The throne dais had seven steps. I took them in two jumps and a fall, which is not a technique they teach at the Inquisition but ought to be.

The sovereign rose from its throne to meet me, and up close it was worse than the borrowed faces — it was a absence shaped like majesty, a hole wearing robes, and it looked at me with the expression of every authority figure who has ever genuinely believed the rules existed for other people.

"Inquisitor Vale," it said. My old title, in my old masters' voice. "You are sworn."

"I was," I agreed, and swung the blade.

It caught my wrist. Its grip was cold in the specific way of things that have never been warm — not cruel, just empty, the way a ledger is empty of mercy. "Four hundred years of order," it said. "Roads that hold. Names that keep. You would trade all of it for chaos?"

"I've read your order," I said. "It's in the ledger. Page after page. I wrote some of it." The blade trembled between us. "I know exactly what I'm trading. I'm trading it for them."

Above us, the seal shrieked on its chains. Below us, Coral stood rooted like a tree that had decided, after long consideration, to become a mountain. Around us, the dead sang with a borrowed living voice, and every note landed on the black iron like a hammer.

The sovereign's grip tightened. Bones creaked — mine.

"You cannot strike," it reasoned. "If you miss, the court closes. If you hesitate, the girl burns out. Your whole life you have followed procedure, Vale. Procedure is the only reason you survived the hunt."

"That's the thing about procedure," I said, and thought of Coral teaching me the Hollow Road, how the passage shifted with memory, how the only way through was to stop gripping the map and trust the ground. "I finally learned a better one."

I let go of the blade with my right hand, twisted free of its grip, and drove the inquisitor's steel upward with everything left in me — not a duelist's stroke, not a soldier's, but a smuggler's throw, learned three weeks ago on a fog-road from a person who'd laughed at my form and then made me practice until midnight.

The blade crossed forty feet of screaming air.

And struck the seal dead center.

* * *

*Coral*

Black iron does not shatter. I want to be clear about that, because the ballads will get it wrong. Black iron *unmakes* — it comes apart the way a lie comes apart, all at once and then in pieces, the truth underneath suddenly visible everywhere.

The seal broke, and the claim broke with it.

I felt it go — felt four hundred years of bargains snap like rotted rope, every entry in every ledger dissolving, every jar unsealing, every stolen name flying home. The walls of the mountain lit from within, a gold-white flood, and the singing stopped being a demand and became — there's no other word — joy. Souls rising through stone the way water rises through roots, pouring out of the under-mountain toward whatever waits for souls when they're finally allowed to arrive.

Some of them paused on the way out. I felt them pass through me, each one a brief warmth, a hand briefly squeezed. One of them pressed something into my keeping — small, bright, stubbornly itself.

*Aldric's shard,* the shepherd said. *He asked to stay near you a while longer. Sentimental old road-wolf. Take him home.*

The sovereign came apart more slowly than its seal. It had worn majesty so long it tried to keep wearing it while it unraveled, robes and crown and choir-voice streaming away into the rising light, and the last thing it said — in a single voice, finally, one thin frightened voice instead of a thousand — was:

*"Who will keep the roads?"*

"Everyone," I said. "That's rather the point."

Then it was gone, and the mountain convulsed.

* * *

*Gideon*

The under-mountain had been carved by people who assumed it would stand forever, which is the assumption mountains most enjoy punishing. When the seal broke, the whole court began to come down — pillars cracking, the great vaulted ceiling shedding stone like a dog shedding winter coat, the falls-gate three miles back already groaning under shifting weight.

"CORAL!" I caught them as they swayed — they weighed nothing, they'd burned everything, they were cold as river-stone and grinning like a drunk. "Can you walk?"

"Ask me tomorrow," they said. "Carry me today."

I carried them. Inquisitor's blade abandoned in a throne, dignity abandoned somewhere on the dais steps, the last apprentice of the Wayfarers' Guild over my shoulder like a sack of contraband, running through a collapsing empire while the dead — the free dead, the *saved* dead — shouted directions from the walls.

*Left at the third pillar! Left!* cried a woman who'd been harvested in my grandfather's time. *The old stair, the one they bricked up — we remember where it is, we were the ones who built it!*

The dead guided us out. Roads only ghosts remembered, opening ahead of us pillar by pillar, and behind us the Court of Borrowed Faces folded down into honest rubble, and somewhere in the crush Sera Kest was holding the gate with six hunters and a crossbow she'd aimed at her own past.

Coral stirred against my shoulder as we ran. Their voice was a thread, but it was theirs — only theirs, one voice, no choir.

"Gideon."

"Don't talk. Save it."

"The seal." A breath. "Did I ever thank you for the throwing lessons compliment? Because your form is still terrible."

I laughed, which in a collapsing mountain is either madness or grace, and possibly both. "Hold on," I said. "Long way up."

Ahead, through the dust, the first gray light of the surface world showed at the end of a ghost-road — and Aldric's shard pulsed warm against Coral's chest, like a small heart remembering how.
