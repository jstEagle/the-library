## 19. The Long Way Up

The mountain came apart the way a lie does: slowly at first, then all at once.

One moment Gideon's blade was buried in the sovereign's seal and the walls were singing — every stolen soul in Thornfell's deep court set loose at once, a chord so vast it wasn't sound anymore, just pressure, just *yes*. The next moment the ceiling of the auction hall decided it had other plans, and the floor beneath our feet remembered it was stone, and stone, when insulted, cracks.

"Run," I said. It seemed inadequate. "RUN," I said again, louder, because apparently I'm the sort of person who repeats herself during an earthquake.

Gideon didn't need telling. He had Aldric over his shoulder — my mentor, ninety pounds of hollowed-out man with a soul-shard cupped against his chest like a coal that had remembered how to burn — and he ran the way I imagine he used to run toward fires, except now he was running away from one, which I suspect felt novel.

I looked back exactly once. The sovereign's borrowed face was coming apart like wet paper, all those stolen identities peeling off him in ribbons of light, and underneath there was nothing. Not a monster. Not a man. Just a shape the crown had worn for so long it had forgotten it was ever empty. The souls poured out of him and through him and past him, upward, thousands of them, a river running the wrong way through the mountain's veins, and I thought: *this is what you were afraid of, Coral. This is what you spent your whole life hiding under your tongue.*

It sounded like my mother's kitchen on a festival morning. It sounded like forgiveness.

Then a pillar came down where I'd been standing, and I stopped being poetic and started being fast.

* * *

Here is the problem with escaping a collapsing underground court: the roads out are roads the crown built, and the crown built them to keep things *in*. Every corridor we knew — every route Sera had sold us, every passage from my maps — was either blocked, burning with released soul-light, or actively trying to kill us. The great stair had sheared off three landings up. The broker's tunnel was a river of loose scree. The gate beneath the falls, our way in, was behind us and buried under four hundred tons of newly enthusiastic geology.

"We're going to die in here," Gideon said. Not panicked. Just factual, the way he said most things, as though death were a line item in a ledger he was auditing.

"Noted," I said. "Counterpoint." I put my hand flat against the wall.

And the dead answered.

They were everywhere now — freed, unbound, no longer pressed into the mountain's service, and they had not left. They were lingering the way people linger after a funeral, unwilling to go, full of things unsaid. And when I touched the stone they turned toward me the way flowers turn toward a window.

*Coral Nystrom,* said a voice like dry leaves. A woman. She'd been in the walls since before the guild had maps of this place; I'd heard her humming on my first descent, years ago, and been too frightened to answer. *Little listener. You answered.*

"I'm answering now," I breathed. "I'm sorry it took me so long. We need a road."

*A road,* she said, amused. *You want a road out of a dying mountain.*

"I want the road only you remember. The one they sealed when they built the court on top of it. There's always an older road. That's the first thing the guild ever taught me."

Silence. Then, all around us, through the whole shuddering dark, a rustle — hundreds of voices waking, passing the request along like buckets in a fire-line. *She asks the old way. She asks the old way.* And something in the stone shifted, some pressure easing, and I felt it like a hand taking mine.

*Follow the cold draft,* the leaf-voice said. *Where the air tastes of rain. Walk where we walked, before they built their pretty cage. Hurry, little listener. The mountain has little patience left, and less roof.*

"Gideon," I said. "Left. Now."

He didn't ask how I knew. That's the thing about Gideon Vale — the man who'd spent fifteen years insisting the dead were silent — he'd stopped asking somewhere around the moment ten thousand souls sang the seal apart. He just shifted Aldric's weight and went, and trusted me, and I found I could run faster with that trust than I ever had without it.

* * *

The old road was everything the crown's halls weren't. Where the court had been marble and gold leaf and stolen faces, this was raw stone and root and dripping dark, a smuggler's passage older than any kingdom — cut, I realized with a jolt, by hands like mine. Guild hands. Wayfarers' hands, generations back, before the guild went small and secret and ashamed. The walls still bore the old marks: compass roses scratched by lantern-light, the triple-notch that meant *safe water*, a warning sign in a script so old I could only read half of it. (The half I could read said *the mountain listens*, which, given recent events, felt redundant.)

We ran through it with the dead calling turns ahead of us.

*Fork ahead — take the right, the left is throat.*
*Low bridge — duck, tall one, DUCK—*
*Water rising in the third gallery, do not stop, do not look at the lights in the water, they are not lights—*

"What did she say?" Gideon panted, as we splashed through a flooded stretch with something pale and patient drifting below the surface, watching us with the particular interest of the not-alive.

"She said don't look at the lights."

"And you looked."

"I glanced."

"Coral."

"It was *one glance*, Gideon, and I'm fine, mostly, there's just a voice in my ear now saying my name in my mother's handwriting, which — okay, that sentence got away from me, keep running."

Aldric stirred against his shoulder. His eyes opened — milky, unfocused, but open — and his lips moved. No voice. The shard against his chest flared instead, a pulse of warm gold light, and the dead nearest us made a sound I'd never heard from them before. Not a wail. Not a demand.

A cheer.

*The maker wakes,* someone whispered. *The mapmaker's line holds.*

"He can't speak yet," I said, and my voice cracked on it, because I'd found Aldric hollowed out in a cell and I'd held his hand and he hadn't known me, and now he almost knew me, and almost was its own kind of torture. "But he's there. He's climbing back in."

"Then we get him home before he finishes arriving," Gideon said, and there it was — that grim, stubborn tenderness he thought he hid. He didn't hide it. He'd never once hidden it from me. That was the whole trouble with us.

Behind us, deeper in the mountain, something enormous gave way. The sound rolled up the old road like thunder in a bottle, and dust sifted down through the dark, and the dead's voices sharpened.

*Faster,* said the leaf-woman. *Oh, children, FASTER.*

* * *

We hit the worst of it at the Gallery of Names.

I don't know what the guild had called it originally. The crown had repurposed it — a long vaulted hall where every harvested soul had been entered into the ledger, and the ledger, being what it was, had soaked into the stone. When the seal broke, the names woke up. As we crossed the hall they rose off the walls in slow spirals of light, thousands upon thousands of them, each one a person, each one pausing — I swear this, I will swear it until I die — each one pausing to look at us as it passed.

Some nodded. Some reached out, not to take, just to touch — a brush of light across Aldric's shard, across Gideon's branded cheek, across my hands, and every touch said the same thing in a different voice: *thank you, thank you, we heard you, thank you.*

I am not going to pretend I kept running gracefully. I ran and I sobbed and I ran, and Gideon caught my elbow and pulled me along, and if he was crying too neither of us mentioned it, because we are, both of us, champions at not mentioning things.

"The ledger," he choked out. "Coral — the ledger — it's *emptying*. Do you understand? Every name I ever decoded, every entry — they're not entries anymore. They're just—" He couldn't finish. Fifteen years of believing he'd been cataloguing criminals, and the truth was he'd been reading a menu aloud to monsters, and now the menu was walking off the page and out of the mountain and *free*.

"You'll carry it differently," I said. "That's all any of us can do. Carry it differently."

Ahead, the passage bent upward, steeply, and the air changed — cold, wet, tasting of rain and pine and *above*, and the dead's voices gathered close, urgent and bright.

*The last door. The last door. The tall one with the crossbow scar — she holds it.*

"Sera," I breathed.

*She would not leave,* the leaf-woman said, and there was wonder in it, old dead wonder. *We told her the mountain was falling and she planted her feet in the doorway like a tree. Go. GO.*

* * *

Sera Kest stood in the breach at the top of the old road with her boots braced against the frame and her crossbow slung and her arms out, holding back — I am not exaggerating — a slow avalanche of dust and falling rock with nothing but her body and her opinion of it. Her face was gray with grit. One sleeve was torn and bloody. She looked, in short, like a woman having the worst and best night of her life simultaneously.

"Took you long enough," she rasped.

"You held the gate," Gideon said, hauling Aldric through. It wasn't a question. It was closer to a confession.

"Somebody had to. You two were busy performing whatever that was back there — I heard the singing, Vale. I heard my brother." Her voice caught, hard, and she shoved past it. "He says hello. He says he's going where the others are going and I'm not to follow for fifty years minimum. He always was bossy." She grabbed my shoulder as I passed, and her grip was iron and her eyes were wet. "Nystrom. The proof you gave me. It was real. All of it."

"All of it," I said.

"Then go. I've got the door until everyone's through. The dead know the count better than I do — ask them." A ghost of her old commander's smirk. "Apparently you can just *ask them* now. Wonderful. Terrific. Get out."

We went. Behind us the mountain groaned, and Sera stood in the doorway of the end of everything, holding it open with her shoulders, letting the river of light pour past her into the rain.

* * *

The last hundred yards were the longest of my life.

The old road climbed through a fissure so narrow we had to turn sideways, Aldric passed hand to hand between us, the shard glowing brighter with every step as if it knew. Above us, impossibly far, a coin of gray daylight. Below us, the mountain's death-rattle, and threaded through it — still, even now — the dead, calling the path.

*Left foot on the red seam. Handhold at your shoulder. Don't trust the third one, it's a liar.*

"Almost," I gasped. "Almost, almost—"

*Little listener.* The leaf-woman's voice, right at my ear, gentle as banked embers. *When you come to the mouth of the cave — we cannot pass beyond it. The living own the morning. But hear us:* and around her, hundreds of voices joined, soft as snowfall, *you were never cursed. You were never alone. You were the door we waited for. Walk in the sun for all of us who can't.*

I put my hand on the stone one last time.

"Thank you," I whispered. "All of you. I'll remember the roads. I'll carve them true. Every one."

*We know,* they said, in ten thousand voices, in one voice. *That is why we chose you.*

Then daylight took us.

* * *

We came out of the mountainside halfway up Thornfell's shoulder, into rain and wind and a sky so wide it hurt, dawn breaking gray-gold over the valleys where I'd spent my whole life learning to be small. Gideon laid Aldric down on the wet grass. I knelt beside them both, shaking, laughing, crying, doing all three badly at once.

The shard in Aldric's hands pulsed once, twice — and sank. Into his chest. Home.

His eyes cleared. Not all the way — there were rooms in him that would stay dark a long while, maybe forever — but enough. Enough that when he looked up at me, he saw me.

"Nystrom," he croaked. His voice was gravel and rust. "You're... crying in the rain. Very inefficient. Nobody can tell."

"You absolute *bastard*," I said, and hugged him hard enough to bruise, and he patted my back with a hand that weighed nothing and meant everything.

Gideon sat down heavily beside us, bolt-wound reopened, face gray, grinning like a man who'd forgotten the machinery. "The mountain?"

"Gone," I said. "The court, the seal, the sovereign — gone. The roads under it are gone too. Our roads. The guild's last way home." I said it out loud and felt it land, the cost of it, real and permanent. Everything I'd mapped. Everything I'd mastered. Ash and rubble under a broken crown.

Aldric looked at me for a long moment. Then, weakly, wickedly:

"Well. I suppose you'll have to draw new ones."

And above us — faint, threading down through the rain, from everywhere and nowhere — the dead began to sing.

Not the trapped, pressed, desperate song of the walls. Something freer. Something that was already fading, note by note, as they went wherever the freed go. But the last verse carried clear across the morning, and I memorized it, because that's what I do. I'm a mapmaker. I keep the ways.

Down in the valley, at the treeline, torches were moving. Hunters — or hunters who used to be, drawn by the mountain's death, come to see. Sera was still under the mountain holding the door for the last of the souls, and Gideon's name was still treason in the capital, and Aldric couldn't walk, and the guild was three people sitting in the rain on a hillside with no roads left.

I squeezed Gideon's hand.

"Come on," I said. "We should probably go explain ourselves to the mob."
