## 20. What the Guild Owes

The sun was the first thing that tried to kill him.

We'd carried Aldric up through the ghost-roads for what felt like a season and was probably four days, and when we finally broke the surface at the old shepherd's cairn above Greywater, the morning light hit his face and he flinched like a man struck. His eyes opened — pale, unfocused, swimming — and his first word in the world of the living was a hoarse *no*, aimed at the sky.

"Too bright," I said. "I know. Give it a minute."

He gave it a minute. Then he gave it another. And then he looked at me — really looked, the way he used to when I'd botched a knot or misread a contour line, with that particular disappointment of a man who expected better and was rarely surprised — and he said, "Coral Nystrom. You look terrible."

"You've been dead-adjacent for three weeks," I said. "You don't get an opinion."

"I sold my soul to save you and you dragged me out anyway." He coughed, a dry rustle like paper. "Insubordinate."

"Learned from the best."

Gideon knelt on his other side, saying nothing, doing the quiet competent things Gideon did — checking pulse, wetting Aldric's lips from our last skin of water, positioning the cloak beneath his head so the stones wouldn't bite. Aldric watched him with the flat assessment of a guildmaster who had spent forty years deciding which strangers to trust and had been wrong exactly once.

"You're the one who fell," Aldric said.

"Inquisitor Gideon Vale," Gideon said. "Formerly."

"Mm." Aldric's gaze slid to me. "And this is the company you keep now."

"The best company I've ever kept," I said, and didn't look at Gideon when I said it, because some truths are easier to say sideways.

Aldric made a sound that might have been a laugh if laughter hadn't been carved out of him along with everything else the court took. "Then the guild is three again," he murmured. "Praise whatever gods are left."

*Plus two,* I thought, but I didn't say it yet. Some arithmetic takes time.

* * *

We made camp in the ruins of the tollhouse — the same one, though I couldn't have told you how we'd come back to it, because the roads below had folded themselves shut behind us like a hand closing, and every path I knew by heart had gone strange or silent. The dead had led us out through passages that existed only in their remembering, corridors of memory wearing the shape of stone, and when the last of them had whispered *go, go, little mapmaker, go* and released us into daylight, the Hollow Road had simply ceased to be. Not collapsed. *Unwritten.* As if the mountain, having lost its seal, had decided to forget us entirely.

Aldric slept. Gideon cleaned his blade by the fire with the methodical care of a man who needed his hands busy or they'd start shaking. Sera Kest sat apart, as she always did, sharpening nothing in particular, and her brother's name hung between her and the rest of us like weather.

I climbed to the tollhouse roof because I couldn't stand to be inside my own skin, and because roofs were where I did my thinking, and because from up there I could see the whole sweep of Thornfell spread out under the evening — the fog-roads silvering in the valleys, the ridgelines going purple, the world looking exactly as beautiful as it had before any of it happened, which felt like an insult.

The old roads were gone.

I want to be precise about what that means, because grief makes people vague and I refuse. The Wayfarers' Guild had maintained eleven hundred miles of hidden passage beneath Thornfell. Smuggler roads, yes — salt, tin, untaxed brandy, fugitives — but also the older ways underneath those: the guild's true inheritance, paths keyed to memory and oath, known only to three living people and however many dead cared to remember them. My mother had walked them. Her mother before her. I had spent my entire life learning them, and I had been good, and being good at something secret is its own kind of happiness, cold and clean as well-water.

Now the mountain had forgotten itself. Every route home was a rumor. Every map in my satchel was a portrait of a corpse.

And the worst part — the part I turned over and over like a stone with something alive under it — was that I had done it. Not alone; Gideon's blade had done the striking, and ten thousand souls had done the shouting, and the sovereign's own greed had built the pyre. But I had chosen. They'd offered me the trade: Aldric's soul for mine, my gift bound into the foundations, the roads made permanent and unbreakable forever. A guild that would never again dwindle. A road that would never again close.

I had said no, and broken the bargain, and freed everyone, and cost us everything.

I would do it again. That was the obscene part. I sat on that roof with my legs dangling over the drop and I searched myself for regret the way you probe a bruise, and there wasn't any. There was just the accounting.

* * *

"You're brooding," Gideon said from the ladder. "You brood loudly. It's a gift."

"It's a curse," I said. "Everything's a curse. Ask anyone."

He climbed up and settled beside me, close enough that our shoulders touched, which was as much declaration as either of us could manage in daylight. Below, Sera had taken first watch, a dark shape against the darkening sky, and Aldric's breathing came slow and even from inside.

"Talk to me," he said.

So I talked. About the roads. About the maps that were now fiction. About how I'd spent twenty years becoming the last apprentice of a dying guild so that I could be the last master of a dead one. About the trade I'd refused and why, and how the *why* was simple and enormous: because a soul is not a foundation stone, and a road built on a chained person is just a prettier prison, and I would rather walk free on no roads at all than safe on ones paved with the enslaved.

Gideon listened the way he listened — completely, without interruption, as if my words were evidence in a trial he intended to judge fairly.

"When I broke the hunt," he said finally, "I thought I was losing my purpose. Turns out I was losing my cage." He looked out at the fog-roads. "Maybe the guild's the same. Maybe what you owed the roads was never the roads."

"That's very neat," I said. "I hate it."

"You love it."

"I love *you*," I said, "which is worse," and he went still in that way of his, the way of a man who has heard a confession he intends to keep, and after a moment his hand found mine on the shingles between us, and neither of us said anything else, because we'd already said it, back in the fever-dark and the river-caves and every silence since, and some things bear repeating only once.

* * *

Aldric woke properly on the third day above ground, and asked for his ledger.

It was ruined — water-stained, ash-bitten, half the entries illegible — but he held it in his lap like a sleeping cat and read what remained, and I watched his face do the work of mourning without letting anything fall.

"Eleven hundred miles," he said. "Four hundred years of keeping. Gone in an afternoon."

"Not gone," I said. "Changed. The dead still know the ways. They walked us out, Master. If the guild learns to listen instead of memorize—"

"The guild is three people and a traitor's knife," he said, but gently, testing whether I'd bristle. I didn't. I'd done enough bristling for one lifetime.

"Three people," I agreed. "Plus two." I nodded toward Gideon at the fire, and toward Sera on watch. "An inquisitor who chose us, and a hunter who's choosing us still, one day at a time. Four hundred years ago the guild was probably three desperate people too. We're not ending anything. We're starting over."

Aldric was quiet a long time. When he spoke again his voice had that quality it got when he quoted the old oaths — worn smooth, almost tender.

"The guild owes three debts," he said. "To the road that sheltered us. To the dead who taught us. To the gifted who were never safe until someone made them safe." He closed the ledger. "Two of those debts we can still pay. Perhaps that's the accounting, Coral. Perhaps the roads were never the point. Perhaps the *owing* was."

I looked at him — diminished, hollowed, a man with maybe half his fire left and all of his spine — and I understood that he had already done this math himself, somewhere in the dark of the auction block, and had made his peace with it before I ever came for him.

"We'll need new maps," he said.

"I'll draw them," I said. "I hear the cartographer's market in Bellharrow is about to be very interested in open roads."

* * *

It was near midnight when Sera came down off watch and stood at the edge of the firelight, holding something out to me. A scrap of vellum. I recognized the hand immediately — the tight, official script of the crown's survey office.

"A dispatch rider came through Greywater yesterday," she said. "Heading for Bellharrow. Proclamations." She let me take the page. "They know the mountain fell. They know souls got loose. And they know who did it."

I read it twice. The words swam and then sharpened:

*By order of the Regency Council: the former inquisitor Gideon Vale, the wayfarer called Coral Nystrom, and all persons harboring them are declared enemies of the crown. Rewards offered. Hunters commissioned.*

"They're not calling it witchcraft anymore," Sera said. "They're calling it sedition. Which means it's not the Church coming now. It's the army."

Behind me, I heard Gideon rise quietly to his feet. Above us, faint and clear as a bell rung underwater, the dead in the old stones began — softly, steadily, unmistakably — to sing.

"Well," I said, folding the proclamation into my coat, next to my mother's ashes' worth of letters and every other thing I'd ever burned or saved. "Good thing we were planning to redraw the maps anyway."
