## 21. Hunters No More

The ledger weighed less than I expected.

I had carried it for eleven days — wrapped in oilcloth, strapped against my ribs beneath my coat, sleeping with it like a lover I hated. Four hundred pages of names. Not the gifted's names, mind you; those were in the front, tidy columns in my own old handwriting from the years when I still believed the work was righteous. It was the back half that mattered. The back half was the crown's arithmetic: what each soul fetched at auction, which lords held liens on which dead, and — in a hand I recognized as the Lord Inquisitor's own — a running tally of *future harvests*, dated out like crop rotations.

My name was there. Page three hundred ninety-one. *Sera Kest, Captain, Third Hunt. Gift latent (auditory). Scheduled upon retirement or injury.*

Latent auditory. That was the official phrase for what had been happening to me since Greywater Span. The crown's clerks had known before I did. They'd been listening to me listen to my dead brother and writing it down in their neat little book, patient as farmers.

So yes. I carried it to the capital myself, because if I handed it to anyone else they would either burn it or sell it, and I had spent enough of my life doing both.

* * *

The printing house of Aldous Merrin sat in the Tanners' Quarter, wedged between a tannery that stank like sin and a chandler that smelled like forgiveness, so that walking past it you got both at once and couldn't tell which was which. Merrin was sixty, ink-stained to the elbow, and had spent thirty years publishing pamphlets the crown called seditious and he called *corrections*. He'd been arrested twice. Both times the crowd outside his cell had grown so loud they let him go.

I came in through the back, because old habits, and put the ledger on his composing table between a tray of loose type and a cold cup of tea.

Merrin looked at it. Looked at me. Looked at the hunt captain's badge on my coat — I hadn't thought to take it off, which tells you something about how far I'd already fallen — and said, "You're either here to arrest me or to ruin us both."

"Ruin," I said. "Definitely ruin."

"Thank the saints." He pulled the oilcloth open with the reverence of a man unwrapping a relic, and then his face changed as he read, page by page, the way a face changes when a man discovers the water he's been drinking all his life has been poisoned upstream. He read for a long time. The chandler next door must have finished his batch, because the smell of forgiveness won.

"This is real," he said finally. "This is the actual crown ledger."

"It's the actual crown ledger."

"You understand what happens to you when this prints."

"I've had eleven days to understand." I set my badge down on top of it. "Print everything. Names, dates, prices. Don't soften the arithmetic. People need to see that their neighbors were priced by the pound."

He looked up at me over his spectacles. "And your name is in here too."

"Page three ninety-one. Feel free to print that as well. It'll save the tribunal some work."

Merrin took off his spectacles and polished them on his apron, which I would learn was what he did instead of weeping. "Thirty years I've written that the hunt was a lie," he said. "Thirty years, and half the city called me a crank, and the other half bought my pamphlets to line their pantries. And now a hunt captain walks in and hands me the proof in her own government's hand." He put the spectacles back on. "Why?"

That was the question, wasn't it. Eleven days of road gives you time to rehearse an answer, but every rehearsal had come out wrong, so I gave him the true one.

"Because my brother's voice woke me up," I said. "Tomas. Harvested four years ago. Sold to a lord whose name is in that book, page two hundred twelve, right under the price. I spent four years telling myself the silence after him was grief. Then I started hearing him in the stones near Thornfell, clear as a bell, and grief doesn't sound like that. Grief doesn't say *Sera, it's cold where they keep us.*" My voice did something I didn't authorize. I let it. "The hunt taught me that hearing the dead was sickness. Turns out it was testimony. And I'm the only witness who can testify without getting burned first — well. Without getting burned *much* first."

Merrin nodded slowly, and began setting type.

* * *

It took him six days. Six days of compositors working by candlelight, of apprentices running copy through the rain to three other presses so no single raid could kill it, of Merrin himself reading proofs until his eyes bled. I stayed in the back room and helped where a hunt captain can help in a print shop, which is nowhere, so mostly I sharpened knives and listened to Tomas.

He was quieter in the capital. Farther from Thornfell, I think, or maybe farther from wherever the court beneath the mountain had kept its stores before Coral Nystrom broke them open. But he came, in the small hours, when the press was still and the type trays hummed faintly with all those little metal letters dreaming of sentences.

*Sera. Did you do it yet?*

"Not yet."

*Do it soon. It's cold waiting.*

"Tomas, it's been four years. You never once mentioned being cold while you were alive."

*I was alive then. Different rules.*

I laughed, alone in a back room at three in the morning, and somewhere across the city a dead boy laughed with me, and that was the moment I stopped being afraid of the tribunal. Whatever they could take from me, they couldn't take the fact that my brother was still my brother, and still funnier than me.

* * *

The broadsheet hit the streets on a Tuesday, because Merrin said Tuesdays were when the taverns were fullest and the magistrates most hungover. Eight pages. THE LEDGER OF SOULS, the headline said, and beneath it, in letters tall enough to read from horseback: *Every Name. Every Price. By Order of the Crown.*

By noon, the Tanners' Quarter was a river of people. By evening, it was a flood. They didn't riot, exactly — the city was past rioting, past the animal stage of it. What they did was worse. They *read*. They stood in the squares and read aloud to crowds who couldn't, and every name spoken was a small bell tolling, and by the third day the bells had reached the palace steps.

They came for me on Thursday. Two tribunal officers and a clerk, very polite, very frightened. Politeness is what fear wears when it outranks you.

"Captain Kest. Or — former Captain Kest, I suppose. You're summoned before the Tribunal of Inquiry."

"I know," I said. "I wrote the summons myself. Well. Dictated it. To your clerk, who is standing behind you trying not to make eye contact."

The clerk went the color of tallow. The senior officer cleared his throat. "You're not... going to run?"

"Officer, I have walked into every haunted hole between here and Thornfell carrying nothing but a crossbow and a bad attitude. You think a courtroom frightens me?" I picked up my coat. "Besides. Someone has to tell them where the bodies are priced."

* * *

The Tribunal of Inquiry sat in the Assize Hall, seven magistrates behind a bench tall enough to require ladders, which I have always felt said something. The gallery was packed. Merrin sat in the front row with his ink-stained hands folded, looking like a man attending his own canonization.

They asked me to state my name and rank. I stated my name and my former rank. There was a small, delicious pause while everyone absorbed the word *former*.

Then they asked the question they'd clearly been told to ask: "Did you steal this ledger?"

"No."

"Then how did you come to possess it?"

"I decoded it." I let that sit. "With assistance. From the disgraced inquisitor Gideon Vale, who built the cipher originally, because the crown trusted him with its arithmetic back when the crown still trusted him. The cipher is reproduced on page eight of the broadsheet, gentlemen, so your own clerks can verify every figure. I'd recommend they start with page two hundred twelve."

"Why page two hundred twelve?"

"Because that's where the crown sold my brother." I heard my own voice go flat and even, the voice I'd used on interrogations, turned around and pointed home. "Tomas Kest. Harvested in the spring four years past. Purchased by Lord Aldery Vane for the sum listed directly beneath his name. I'd invite Lord Vane to dispute the entry, but I understand he's fled to the coast, which I believe is its own kind of answer."

The hall made a sound. Not a gasp — something lower, more collective, like a building settling.

For two hours they walked me through it. Where the ledger came from. Who else had copies. Whether I had acted alone. I answered everything, because I had decided in the back room of that print shop that I would hide nothing ever again; secrecy was the crown's whole trick, and I was done performing it for free.

Near the end, the eldest magistrate — a dry stick named Corvane who'd signed, I happened to know, at least forty hunt warrants — leaned forward and asked the real question.

"Captain Kest. You served the hunt for nine years. You delivered gifted persons to processing. Do you now repudiate that service entire?"

I had rehearsed this one too, on the road, and every rehearsal had come out wrong, so again I gave the truth.

"No," I said.

The gallery stirred. Corvane's eyebrows climbed.

"I don't repudiate it *entirely*, because that would be a lie, and you've had enough of those from my profession. For six of those nine years I believed the work protected people. I was wrong, and the wrongness wasn't innocent — I chose not to look at the arithmetic, and the arithmetic was always there. So no, I don't wash my hands of it. I keep my hands dirty and I use them to fix what they helped break. Repudiation is easy. Repair is the job." I looked at the seven of them, ladder-height and all. "Which brings me to what I actually came here to say, since we're all being honest. The hunt isn't legitimate anymore. It wasn't legitimate before the broadsheet; the paper just made it expensive to pretend. Every warrant your courts issued under it is void, every soul-price in that ledger is a debt the crown owes, and every person with a gift — manifest or latent — is owed an apology with interest. You can rule that way today, or you can rule the other way and find out what a city full of grieving readers does next. I recommend the first option. I've seen the second. It involves a mountain falling down."

Silence. Somewhere in the gallery, Merrin made a noise that might have been a laugh strangled by piety.

They deliberated for nine days. I spent them in a cell that was, I'll grant, the nicest room I'd occupied in months — clean straw, a window, no ghosts except the ones I'd invited. On the ninth day they returned a ruling: the hunt dissolved, its warrants void, its ledgers entered into public record, restitution proceedings opened against the estates named therein. And regarding me — Sera Kest, who had stolen state documents and published them — they ruled, in language so carefully worded you could hear the lawyers sweating, that my actions constituted *an irregular disclosure in the public interest*, for which no penalty would be assessed, *at this time*.

"At this time," Merrin repeated gleefully, reading it aloud to me on the courthouse steps. "That's magistrate for *we're terrified of you and hope you retire*."

"They can hope," I said.

"What will you do?"

I looked out over the square, where someone had already chalked Tomas's name on the cobbles, and beside it a dozen others, and beside those dozens more, spreading out from the tribunal steps like roots. "I'm going to learn to listen properly," I said. "Seems the least I owe."

* * *

Gideon found me a week later, at the chandler's, of all places, buying candles for a grave I meant to visit properly for the first time in four years.

He looked better than the last time I'd seen him, which had been through a crossbow scope, which is not how you want to see anyone. The fever had left him thin, and the bolt scar at his shoulder would ache in rain forever, but his eyes had settled into something I hadn't seen in them since the early days of the hunt — before the promotions, before the orders started arriving pre-written. Peace, or the neighborhood of it.

"Sera Kest," he said. "The most wanted woman in the capital."

"Formerly most wanted. Read the ruling. I've been demoted to *irregular*."

"So I heard." He bought a candle from the chandler without asking me which kind, and paid for mine too, which was either courtesy or a very old habit from the days we'd shared mess tables. We walked out together into the street. For a while neither of us spoke, which was its own kind of conversation; Gideon and I had always been fluent in silence.

Finally he said, "I owe you an apology."

"Do you? Let me check my ledger." I patted my coat theatrically. "Nope. Nothing there. Must have printed it."

"The bridge," he said, and stopped walking. "Greywater Span. I know what I asked of you. I know what order I gave, and what you'd have had to do, and I've had a lot of fevered nights to turn it over." His jaw worked. "You aimed at Coral and I stepped into the line, and I've told myself a hundred versions of why. Duty. Mercy. A sudden attack of conscience twenty years late. None of them are quite true. The true version is that I saw you about to become the thing that broke me, and I couldn't watch it. That's not an order I had any right to give. So. Apology."

I considered it. Gideon Vale, apologizing, in daylight, in the street, unprompted — somewhere the old Lord Inquisitor was spinning fast enough to power a mill.

"Apology noted," I said. "Partially accepted. The remainder converts to a debt, payable as follows: you tell me everything the hunt knew about the harvesting that you never reported. Everything. Names, sites, methods. Coral's guild is rebuilding, and the gifted coming out of the woodwork deserve to know what was done to their dead and where the pieces went. You're the only man alive with the full map in his head."

"That could take years."

"Good. Something to fill the retirement."

He almost smiled. It was strange, seeing his face attempt it, like watching a door open in a wall you'd assumed was solid. "And in exchange?"

"In exchange, I stop aiming crossbows at the people you love." I stuck out my hand. "Uneasy peace?"

He took it. His grip was careful, mindful of the shoulder, mindful of everything — Gideon Vale, the man who'd once broken a smuggler's wrist for a lesser offense, holding my hand like it might sing to him. "Uneasy peace," he agreed. "The easiest kind we're likely to get."

"One condition more." I nodded toward the chandler's window, where our two candles sat in the bag in my fist. "Come with me to the graveside. Tomas liked you, back when. He used to say you were the only hunter who checked whether the cells were warm."

Gideon went still. "Sera. If I stand at his grave, and he speaks—"

"He'll speak. He never stops. Mostly he complains about the temperature." I started walking. "Come meet my brother properly. It's the least the law owes either of us."

Behind us, the press-rooms were already running the second edition — restitution schedules, the dissolved hunt's full warrant archive, Merrin's editorial titled *WHAT WE OWE THE LISTENING DEAD*. Ahead of us, the road wound up toward the cemetery on the hill, where the stones were ordinary stones, granite and rain and moss, and where — for the first time in the history of this kingdom, as far as I knew — a living man was walking to a grave on purpose, in daylight, to listen.

Tomas was already talking before we reached the gate.

*Sera. Tell him — tell the gloomy one — that I forgive him. And tell him he still checks whether rooms are warm. I watched him do it just now, at the chandler's. Some habits are the good kind.*

I glanced sideways at Gideon, who was frowning slightly at nothing, head tilted, like a man hearing a bell a long way off.

"Gideon," I said. "There's someone here who wants a word with you."
