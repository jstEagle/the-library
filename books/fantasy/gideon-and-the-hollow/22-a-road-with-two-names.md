## 22. A Road With Two Names

*Coral*

The guildhall smells of woodsmoke and new paper, which is to say it smells like nothing it has ever smelled like before.

For three hundred years the Wayfarers' Guild kept its maps in code — ink that faded, vellum that burned on command, routes memorized and never written whole. A smuggler's map was a confession waiting to be confiscated. We drew our roads in pieces, scattered across a dozen documents, so that no single seizure could ever hand the crown the shape of us.

I am sitting at Aldric's old drafting table with a sheet of clean white calfskin, a pot of ordinary black ink, and I am about to write down everything.

"Your hands are shaking," Gideon says from the doorway. He has a mug of tea in each hand, because he has learned that I forget to eat when I'm working, and because he is constitutionally incapable of walking past a task without doing it. It's insufferable. I've grown fond of it, which is worse.

"My hands are not shaking."

"They're trembling like a plucked string."

"That's called *precision*, Vale."

He sets the mugs down and comes to stand behind my chair, reading over my shoulder the way he does when he wants me to know he's there without making a production of it. Six months ago a man like Gideon Vale standing at my back would have meant a knife between my ribs. Now it means warmth along my spine and the faint smell of cedar oil from his blade, which he still sharpens every morning out of habit, though the only thing he cuts these days is kindling.

On the calfskin, I have drawn the first road of the new map. It runs from Bellharrow market square — right through the middle of it, past the crier's stone where they used to read the names of the taken — up into the fog-roads of Thornfell, and it is marked in plain letters for anyone to read: **OPEN WAY. ALL WHO HEAR THE DEAD MAY PASS.**

No code. No key. No toll.

"You're sure about this," Gideon says. Not a question, really. He asks it anyway, most mornings, the way some people say grace.

"The dead are sure." I tap my ear. "They've been singing about it since the mountain came down. You'd think after four hundred years of being locked out of everything, they'd want their roads kept quiet too. But no. They want them *loud*. They want signs posted."

"What do they sing?"

I let him hear. That's all it takes now — a breath, an opening, his hand on my shoulder, and the stones give up their voices to him secondhand. The shepherd's song first, the one I heard on my very first run, the one that started all of this: a low, patient melody about green places and gates left unbarred. Underneath it, hundreds of others, the freed souls of the under-mountain who chose to stay near rather than drift on, keeping watch over the roads they died beside.

Gideon's grip tightens on my shoulder. He still can't quite bear the whole chorus at once. Neither can I, honestly, but I've had a lifetime of practice being the only one in the room who could hear the dead, and it turns out that practice holds even when the room is full of them.

"*Loud,*" he repeats, when the singing fades. "Signs posted. The girl who hid her gift her whole life wants to nail it to a post."

"The girl who hid her gift spent twenty years listening to dead people beg for help she couldn't legally offer." I dip my pen. "I'd like to try the other way for a while."

* * *

*Gideon*

Here is what I know about maps: I used to burn them.

That was inquisitor work, in the early years — you find a smuggler's cache, you burn the charts, you burn the ledger, sometimes you burn the smuggler if the writ allows it, and you report a clean sweep. I told myself it was order. I was good at telling myself things. I could tell myself a child's gift was corruption; I could tell myself Sera Kest was my friend; I could tell myself, for eleven years, that the souls we harvested went somewhere holy instead of somewhere sold.

Coral draws, and I sit across the table and catalog my sins, and then Coral kicks me under the table because apparently my face does something when I do that.

"Stop brooding," they say, not looking up from the calfskin. "You're disturbing the ink."

"I'm contributing gravity to the workspace."

"You're contributing a cloud. Brood on your own time. Here — hold this end flat."

So I hold the map flat while Coral inks the second road, and I will admit — privately, in the locked drawer of myself where I keep the things I don't say aloud — that watching them draw is the closest thing to prayer I have left. Their pen moves like it knows the ground better than the ground does. Every curve of the Hollow Road, every memory-shift where the passage bends toward grief or joy depending on who walks it, laid down in honest black lines. When I look at this map I see the night I nearly died on Greywater Span. When Coral looks at it, I suspect they see the same night, and neither of us has said so directly, because we are both people who confess best sideways.

Though we're getting better at direct. Fever will do that. Nearly dying will do that. Watching your beloved walk into a collapsing court carrying a soul-shard cupped in their hands like a candle flame will do that very thoroughly indeed.

"There," Coral says, and sits back. Two roads on the new map now. The old Thornfell ways, and one brand-new line, drawn in fresh ink, running south out of the mountains toward the capital itself. Toward the press-house where Sera delivered the ledger. Toward the families of everyone whose name appeared in it.

"That road doesn't exist yet," I point out.

"It exists as soon as someone walks it." Coral caps the ink. "That's the entire philosophy of the guild, Vale. Roads aren't found. They're *made*, by feet, repeatedly, until the world remembers them. Master Aldric taught me that when I was nine and sulking about having to memorize the Greywater approaches."

"And how is the master this morning?"

"Complaining about the porridge, which means he's well." Coral smiles, and it's the smile I like least and best — the one that costs them something. Aldric woke diminished. Everyone knows it; nobody says it in front of him. His gift came back thin, a whisper where it used to be a voice, and some mornings he stands at the window looking at the fog-roads like a man reading a letter in a language he's forgetting. But he complains about the porridge, and he bullies Coral about proper cartographic technique, and last week he caught me trying to fix the roof alone and lectured me for an hour on load-bearing beams, so he is himself. Diminished and himself. We'll take it.

* * *

*Coral*

We hang the finished map in the guildhall's front room, which used to be a storeroom for contraband and is now, after Sera's press campaign made the word respectable again, a registry office.

That's the other thing that changed while I wasn't paying attention: the gifted started coming. First a trickle — a hedge-witch from Bellharrow who'd been sleeping in her cellar since the proclamation, a boy from Greywater who heard his drowned father in the river. Then more. Word travels fast when the crown's own inquisitor publishes a ledger proving the hunt was soul-theft, and word travels faster still when the freed dead start appearing in graveyards and crossroads all over the north, singing, visible at dusk, unmistakably *real*. You cannot convince a population that gifts are corruption once their grandmother's ghost helps them find the lost family cow.

So they come up the mountain asking for the Wayfarers' Guild, and what they find is three members and a disgraced inquisitor and a map nailed to the wall with the toll crossed out.

This morning's arrival is a girl of maybe fourteen, mud to the knees, leading a goat. She stands in the doorway vibrating with terror and hope in equal measure, and I recognize the proportions exactly, because I wore that mixture for twenty years.

"I heard—" she starts, stops, starts again. "People say there's a place. Where you don't have to hide it."

"There's a place," I say. "Come in. Mind the goat, Gideon's fond of it already."

"I am not—"

"He'll name it by supper," I tell the girl, and she laughs, startled, and something in her shoulders comes down half an inch, and that — that small loosening — is worth more than every road I ever kept secret.

Her name is Wren. Of course it is. Half the gifted children born in the last decade seem to be named after birds, as if their parents knew something before they knew it. She can hear the dead too, faintly, and she has spent two years believing she was mad. I sit her down at the drafting table — my table now, though Aldric pretends otherwise — and I put the shepherd's song into the stones of the floorboards so she can hear it, and I watch her face do the thing my face did at nine years old when Aldric first showed me the fog-roads: the moment the secret becomes a shared thing and stops weighing what it weighed.

Gideon brings tea. He has learned that I forget to eat; he has also learned that frightened children trust a large scarred man with a teapot faster than they trust any amount of gentle words. He sits with Wren and tells her, plainly, that he spent eleven years hunting people like her and that he was wrong, and that wrongness can be carried and worked against, and that she should never let anyone tell her guilt is a life sentence. She listens the way you listen to a confession you didn't expect to receive.

After she leaves — with a copy of the open-ways map, free, the first of its kind — Gideon stands at the window watching her and her goat descend into the fog.

"She'll be back," he says. "With others. By midsummer this hall will be full."

"Good."

"It won't be quiet again. Ever. You understand that. The guild you grew up in — three keepers, silent roads, everything hidden — that guild is over. What replaces it will be louder, messier, harder to protect. People will misuse the roads. People will fear us anyway. Some days you'll miss the silence so badly it'll feel like grief."

I come stand beside him. Below, the fog is lifting off Thornfell the way it does at midmorning, reluctantly, in rags, and beneath it the roads run clear and open and *known*.

"I grieved the silence already," I say. "Back when the silence was all I had. This is different. This is the sound after."

* * *

*Gideon*

Evening. The others are abed — Aldric snoring with the dignity of a man who has earned it twice over, Coral upstairs pretending to sleep and actually talking to the dead, which I pretend not to know about, because everyone is entitled to one private conversation.

I take the last watch, which is mostly ceremonial now, and I do what I do every evening: I sharpen the inquisitor's blade, and then I set it on the table where the light catches it, and I look at it until I remember what it's for now.

It struck the sovereign's seal. That's its history. Its future is smaller and better: it cuts rope, kindling, the occasional stubborn hinge. Sera wrote to me last month — an actual letter, stiff as a legal document, because Sera communicates exclusively in formats that could survive a courtroom — informing me that the tribunal has formally dissolved the Office of the Hunt, that the harvested-soul restitution process is underway, and that she has declined the position they offered her overseeing it. *"Someone should build the new thing who isn't cleaning blood off their own hands,"* she wrote. *"That leaves you and Nystrom. Don't waste it."*

Underneath, in handwriting that had clearly fought her the whole way: *"Tell Vale the bridge at Greywater is rebuilt. Tell him I checked the span twice myself. Tell him—"* and then nothing, just a blot where the pen had pressed too hard, and I understood that the rest was for her brother, whose voice she hears now too, and whom she visits at the river every week, and who forgave her before she ever asked.

Sera Kest, holding the gate. In every sense.

The floorboard behind me sings — softly, the shepherd's tune, the dead saying *we're here, we're here* the way they do now, less demand than companionship. And above me, through the ceiling, I hear Coral's voice, low, mid-conversation with someone I can't see, and then a laugh, and I decide the dead must be telling jokes tonight.

Coral comes down barefoot, hair loose, and drops into the chair across from me without ceremony, which took months to earn and which I treasure like a medal.

"You're talking to the ceiling," I say.

"I'm talking *through* the ceiling. Different thing entirely." They nod at the blade. "And you're communing with your knife. We're a strange household."

"We're a strange *guild*."

"Mm. Guild of two and a half." They count on their fingers. "Aldric's mostly retired. Wren starts apprenticeship next week. Sera's an ally-at-distance. The dead are honorary. That makes you and me the whole active roster, Vale. The entire institution."

"Terrifying."

"Statistically doomed."

"Then we'd better enjoy it." I reach across the table, and Coral puts their hand in mine, calloused pen-calluses over sword-calluses, and outside the fog rolls in over Thornfell like a tide coming home, and in the stones the dead begin their evening song — the shepherd's melody braided now with hundreds of others, the freed and the staying and the passing-through, all of them singing the roads open.

Tomorrow we walk the southern road, the one that doesn't exist yet, the one that becomes real the moment our feet make it true. Coral has the map. I have the blade. Between us we have a calling with two names — theirs and mine — and for the first time in either of our lives, the road home and the road forward are the same road.

Coral squeezes my hand. "Ready, inquisitor?"

"Ready, wayfarer."

And we go, together, into the morning that's coming — loud, messy, unmapped, and ours.
