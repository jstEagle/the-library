## 1. The Valley That Refuses

There are two kinds of cartographers in the world: those who map what exists, and those who map what refuses to.

I am the second kind. It was not a career I chose so much as one I inherited, the way you inherit a crooked chimney or a grudge — something already in the house when you arrived, humming quietly and refusing to explain itself.

The valley I live in has no name that outsiders would recognize, because outsiders have never successfully written it down. Every surveyor who came before me left with blank pages and a nervous look. The trade roads bend around us like water around a stone. The old kings' atlases show a smudge where we should be, annotated in fading ink: *here be nothing worth the trouble.* Which is a lie, of course. There is plenty here worth the trouble. The trouble is simply that none of it will hold still for ink.

I have spent thirty-one years — twenty-nine of them working, if you count the childhood years I spent tracing my father's drafts by candlelight while he pretended not to notice — trying to make the valley hold still.

This morning I finished a map no one asked for.

* * *

Let me describe my workshop, because it matters, the way a surgeon's table matters. It sits at the eastern edge of Hollowmere, the largest village in the valley, above the chandler's shop, which means my floorboards smell faintly of tallow year-round and my windows fog with wax-smoke on cold days. Three tables. One for drafting, one for inking, one for the slow, humiliating work of correcting myself. Shelves of rolled maps, each labeled in my own cramped hand: *North Terraces, third attempt. Millbrook oxbow, abandoned. The Fen Road, honest version.*

And in the corner, under a cloth, the family piece: my grandfather's great survey of the valley, begun in his youth and finished by my father, and corrected by me twice over, because even a perfect map of an imperfect place decays like everything else.

The valley itself — well. Imagine a bowl cradled by hills on three sides and a wall of grey cliffs on the fourth, cliffs no one has climbed and no one discusses, the way you don't discuss the fact that your grandmother never speaks of her sister. A river runs through the middle, the Liss, unhurried and slightly smug. Eleven villages, forty-one hamlets, one town (Hollowmere, population stubborn), and roughly nine thousand people who have lived their whole lives inside lines that no one drew and everyone obeys.

Because here is the thing about the valley, the thing I have spent my life documenting without ever once being able to say aloud at dinner: magic does not work here.

Not weakened. Not sleeping. *Absent*, the way color is absent from a charcoal sketch. Beyond the cliffs and the passes, they say, prayers get answered. Curses stick. Blessings take root like wheat. Here, a prayer is just breath shaped hopefully, and a curse is just bad manners. We are a country of the mundane, tucked into the world like a coin lost between floorboards, and the world's gods — whichever of them are real, and the travelers' tales disagree cheerfully on this point — cannot reach us.

My father believed this was a blessing. My grandfather believed it was a wound. I believe it is a border, and borders are the only things I have ever truly loved, which tells you something sad about me that I'd rather you learned now than pieced together later.

* * *

The map I finished this morning took four months. It is a chart of the Liss from its source springs down to the weir below Tarnwick, every ford and flood-mark and fisherman's lie included, drawn at a scale fine enough to show individual willows. No one commissioned it. No one will buy it. The fishermen of the Liss have navigated that river since before my family kept records, and they do not need a boy with ink-stained cuffs to tell them where the snags are.

I made it anyway, because that is what I do instead of sleeping properly, and because there is a hunger in me that has never once been fed by commissions. People come to me for practical things: a chart of the new drainage ditches, a fair copy of the parish bounds for a land dispute, a wedding gift map of someone's farm rendered lovingly and inaccurately large. I do these jobs well and charge fairly and go home at night and draw the things I actually want to draw, the things with no market and no purpose except the one gnawing at me.

Which is this: I want to know what is outside.

There. Said. Thirty-one years of careful, contented, meticulous life, and underneath all of it runs a single ungrateful thread: *what's out there?* What does the world look like where the cliffs end? Do the mountains beyond really burn blue at dusk, the way a peddler once swore to me over watered wine? Are there cities — actual cities, with walls and gates and streets laid out by someone with opinions? Is it true that in some countries rivers run backward on holy days?

Every traveler who comes through the valley says the same maddening thing: *you're lucky, living somewhere simple, somewhere safe.* And I smile and agree, because they're right, and because being right makes it worse.

* * *

Here is how you map a place that refuses to be mapped. You do not fight it. Fighting it is what broke the early surveyors — old Coram Hale, who walked the northern terraces for a season and produced a map so wrong that two shepherds followed it into a ravine; my grandfather, who claimed in his private notes that distances in the valley "disagree with themselves" depending on which direction you measure. You do not impose a grid. You listen.

You talk to the shepherds, who know the high ground the way they know their own hands. You walk every road at every season, because the valley shifts — not dramatically, not the way a story would tell it, but subtly, the way a face shifts. The fen grows a finger wider some winters. The pass at Cold Saddle is half a mile longer going up than coming down, and I have measured it both ways eleven times to be sure I wasn't mad. You record the disagreements. You make the disagreement itself the subject of the map. You stop asking *where exactly is this* and start asking *why won't this hold still,* and only then does the valley let you close, the way a cat permits petting once it has established that you know your place.

It took me ten years to understand that. My father never did. He fought the valley his whole life, redrawing the same boundaries until the day he died with a pen still warm in his hand, and I loved him, and I think he died mildly disappointed that the valley had outlasted him.

I intend to die of something softer.

* * *

So: this morning, the river map. Final inking done at dawn, when the light through the wax-fogged windows goes the color of weak honey. I set the sheet out flat, weighted at the corners with the river stones I use for weights because paperweights from the mason are too uniform and uniformity offends me, and I looked at it the way you look at a child leaving home.

It was good. I'll say that plainly, because false modesty is a tax on the reader. It was the best thing I've made. Every line true, every disagreement noted honestly in the margins — *ford here widens after rain; locals dispute by as much as forty paces* — the whole river rendered not as it pretends to be but as it actually behaves, which is the only honesty available to a cartographer.

And then I saw the edges.

At the top of the sheet, where the source springs sit in their fold of hills, my hand had wandered. Not far. An inch, maybe two, past the springs, past the last surveyed ground, up toward the shoulder of the cliffs. Little suggestion marks. Contours guessed from things shepherds said, from the way the light fell at evening, from pure restless invention dressed up as deduction. The beginnings of terrain I have never seen, drawn in my own confident hand as though confidence were evidence.

The same at the bottom margin, near the weir: a few tentative strokes suggesting the road south out of Tarnwick, the road that leaves the valley, the road I have never taken, drawn as if I were the sort of man who takes roads.

I stood there a long time. Long enough for the tallow smell to give way to the morning smells drifting up from the street — bread, river damp, the chandler's first batch of the day. Somewhere below, Marta the chandler was arguing with her delivery boy in the cheerful, practiced way of people who love each other and enjoy having something to argue about. Ordinary sounds. Valley sounds. The soundtrack of a life I could live out comfortably to its end, respected and busy and quietly starving.

Then I took the map to the brazier.

Understand: I don't destroy my work lightly. Every scrap of draft paper in this workshop represents hours I can't bill anyone for. But there is a rule in my trade that I made for myself long ago, and it is this: a map may not claim knowledge it doesn't have. A map that guesses is a hypothesis. A map that *pretends* is a lie with good posture. Those edge-marks weren't hypotheses. They were longing wearing the costume of data. If I left them, some future buyer — some farmer framing it for his daughter, some traveler carrying it out of the valley — would trust those inches. Would believe I knew what lay past the springs, past the weir, past the boundary of everything I have ever actually seen.

And I don't. I have never known. That is the whole shape of my life: I don't know, and I draw anyway, and I burn the parts of the drawing that forget they don't know.

I held the top edge over the coals and watched the little invented contours curl and blacken and go. Then the bottom. The fire ate my guesses with a small, satisfied crackling, and I thought, as I always think at this moment: *one day I'll draw those edges for real. One day I'll stand on the other side of the cliffs and map my way back in, and the valley will finally appear in the world's atlases not as a smudge but as itself.*

One day. The refrain of the eternally departing. I have been singing it for twenty years the way other men hum work songs.

* * *

I spent the afternoon on honest work — the drainage charts for the fen reeve, who pays promptly and complains thoroughly, both of which I respect — and it was nearly dusk when the girl came.

She couldn't have been more than eight, one of the miller's grandchildren, barefoot despite the season, out of breath, standing in my doorway with the particular urgency children reserve for matters that adults will find trivial.

"Master Reyes," she panted. "You have to come. It's Tam."

"Is he hurt?"

"No." She frowned, wrestling with the words, aware they sounded wrong. "He was at the shrine. By the mill. He was praying — Auntie told him to pray for the ewes, on account of the lambing — and Ada—" she caught herself, flushed, corrected: "*Master* Reyes. The shrine *answered* him."

I put down my pen. "Shrines don't answer, love. They're shrines."

"I know," she said, with the dignity of the empirically certain. "But it did. It talked. All the stones went cold and it talked, and Tam's crying, and nobody will come near it, and they said fetch you because you know about — about places and things—"

I want to record, honestly, what I felt in that moment, because it would be tidy to say dread. It wasn't dread. It was worse than dread. It was *recognition* — the feeling of a map you've drawn your whole life suddenly showing a feature you never surveyed, a river bending the wrong way, a coastline where the interior should be. Something in the deep structure of my world had just moved, and some cartographer's instinct in my chest registered it before my mind could catch up, the way animals know about earthquakes first.

Magic did not work in the valley. That was not folklore. That was the load-bearing wall of everything — my trade, my family's history, the reason we were passed over by every war and wonder that had reshaped the world beyond the cliffs for a thousand years. It was the answer to the question *why here, why us, why nowhere else,* and I had built my entire quiet, meticulous, hungry life on top of it.

"The shrine spoke," I repeated.

"It said it was sorry," the girl said. "It said it hadn't heard anyone in ever such a long time. And then it said—" she hesitated, and lowered her voice as though the words might be heavy enough to drop, "—it said *the name was taken,* and then it stopped, and Tam ran home, and the ewes are fine, apparently."

Outside, the light was failing over the rooftops of Hollowmere, over the eleven villages and forty-one hamlets and one stubborn town, over the whole careful, unmappable, godless valley I had spent my life drawing. Somewhere beyond the grey cliffs, in the wide world I had only ever burned out of my margins, something had just noticed us.

I took my coat off the hook.

"Show me," I said.
