## 6. Leaving the Map

I had always assumed I would leave the valley the way sensible people left places: in daylight, with a packed bag, a farewell or two, and a plan drawn in ink that would hold.

Instead I left at the hour when even the roosters have given up on the day, through a pass I knew only from a drunk drover's description and my own stubborn guesswork, with a scroll that could start a war sewn into the lining of a coat I'd owned for eleven years, and with a plan that consisted entirely of the word *away*.

Let no one tell you cartographers are calm people. We are simply people who have learned to look calm while screaming internally at bad coastlines.

* * *

The soldiers arrived in Harrowmere two days after the messenger died in my workshop, and they did not arrive quietly.

I heard them before I saw them — boots on the mill road, too many boots, moving in the matched rhythm of men who had drilled until their own footsteps stopped belonging to them. From my upstairs window I counted eleven. Six wore the grey-and-gold of Vessan's host, tabards stitched with the upturned hand that meant *open palm, open sky*, which was Vessan's way of saying *everything is mine if you think about it correctly*. The other five wore ash-grey hoods and carried no sigil at all, which was its own kind of statement. The Ashen Choir didn't advertise. Advertising was for gods who wanted something.

They went first to the shrine. Of course they did. The shrine where a child's prayer had been answered by a voice like grinding stone — the first answered prayer inside the boundary in living memory, the crack in the world I had watched open with my own ears. They circled it the way you circle a wound, prodding, and one of the Choir knelt and pressed his palm flat to the altar stone and stayed there so long the Vessan men started shifting their weight like horses bothered by flies.

Then they began going door to door.

I watched them reach the cooper's house, then the weaver's, then old Tam Wicker's, where Tam — who had never in his life kept a secret larger than the location of his second-best pipe — gestured up the hill toward my workshop with the eager helpfulness of a man who has finally been asked a question he can answer.

Well. That settled the question of how much time I had.

Here is what nobody tells you about a life built on precision: when it collapses, it collapses all at once, and every careful habit becomes a liability. My workshop was full of maps. Maps of the valley, maps of the passes, maps with annotations in my own unmistakable hand — *here the river bends wrong, here the rockfall of '61, here be nothing, as far as anyone knows, nothing*. A soldier with half an eye would know in ten seconds that the man who drew these had walked every inch of the border country. Would know, further, that such a man was exactly the sort who might notice a stranger bleeding his way up from the south road. Might take him in. Might take *charge of him*.

I gave myself five minutes. I have never been so aware of minutes in my life; they ticked past like soldiers' boots.

Two minutes: clothes, bread, the good knife, flint. Three: ink, three pens, a stub of charcoal, and the small waterproof satchel I used for fieldwork, because whatever else happened, I refused to face the world beyond the boundary unarmed in the only way that mattered to me. Four: the oath-scroll, wrapped in oilcloth, and here I hesitated — because carrying it made me a target, and hiding it made me a coward, and leaving it made me either a fool or a corpse depending on which faction found it first.

In the end I did neither. I took it apart.

Not destroyed — *disassembled*. The scroll was long, a covenant written in a tight clerical hand, and I unrolled it and cut it, wincing the whole time the way you'd wince cutting a man's coat off a wound, into four sections. One went into the lining of my coat, over my heart, because I am sentimental under all the irony. One went into the false bottom of my map case. One I rolled tight and slid inside the hollow reed of a surveyor's measuring rod, which any soldier would toss aside as a stick and no cartographer would ever abandon. And the last — the section with the names, the true names, the part that mattered most —

I put that one inside a rolled map of the valley itself. Folded the valley around its own crime and sealed the case. If they searched me and found a mapmaker's maps, well. A fisherman carries fish.

Five minutes gone. Down the back stairs, out through the drying shed, across the yard where my neighbor's dog — bless her stupid loyal heart — barked once at me and then, recognizing me, sat down with an air of having discharged her duty.

And then I stood at the edge of my own garden and looked north, at the pass, and felt the strangest sensation of my life, which was fear and longing arriving in the same heartbeat and refusing to be separated.

* * *

The pass is called the Shepherd's Cut, and I want to be honest about how much I actually knew about it, because honesty is about to become very expensive for me.

What I knew: it existed. It ran north-northwest out of the valley between the Two Sisters peaks, and it was narrow enough that sheep went over it single file, and the drover who told me about it — a man named Brannoc who drank like a man watering a horse he didn't like — claimed you could walk it in a day if the weather held and in your whole life if it didn't. He also claimed there was a cairn halfway up where travelers left a stone "for luck," though luck, in the valley, was a word we used the way other people used *someday*: often, and without expectation.

What I did not know: everything else. Where the scree fields were. Whether the meltwater had cut the path out in some spring flood. How wide the boundary itself ran, whether magic ended at a line or faded like a tide, whether crossing it would feel like anything at all.

That last question had haunted me since childhood. Every child in the valley learns the catechism early: *the gods' war never reached us, the gods cannot follow us, we are the quiet place.* Our grandparents' grandparents had fled here when the sky burned, and the valley had closed around them like a hand making a fist, and for nine generations nothing had come in and — this was the part I'd always found suspicious — nothing had ever really gone out, either. Oh, traders went down to the low markets and came back. But nobody *left*. Nobody woke up one morning and said *I will go see the drowned archive at Lake Verre* or *I will walk the Road of Small Gods and pray at a shrine that answers*. The valley wasn't a refuge. It was a bowl, and bowls hold things.

I had spent thirty years mapping a place I could not leave, and I had told myself this was devotion. Standing in my garden with a stolen god's name against my chest, I admitted what it actually was: I had mapped the walls so carefully because I couldn't bear to admit they were walls.

Behind me, down the hill, voices. Boots on gravel. Someone pounding on the door of my workshop, which was empty, which would stop being evidence of my innocence the moment they climbed the stairs and saw forty years of my family's work staring back at them in ink.

I went north.

* * *

The first hour was almost easy, which should have warned me.

The valley floor gave way to pine slope, the pine slope to bare granite, and the Shepherd's Cut opened ahead of me exactly as Brannoc had slurred it: a seam in the mountains, steep but walkable, with the morning mist still pooled in it like milk someone had forgotten to stir. I climbed with the automatic rhythm of a man who has surveyed worse ground, counting paces without meaning to, noting landmarks without meaning to — *cairn at 400 paces, switchback above the waterfall, scree field east-facing, unstable* — building the map in my head because the map was the only prayer I knew.

By midmorning I was high enough to see the whole valley behind me, laid out like one of my own sheets: Harrowmere's smoke, the river's silver stitchwork, the fields in their patient squares. Nine generations of quiet. Somewhere down there, soldiers were pulling my maps apart looking for a secret I had folded into one of them, and children were asking their parents why the shrine had spoken, and the parents were saying *it didn't, it couldn't, the valley doesn't work that way,* and being wrong for the first time in living memory.

I should have felt grief. I noted that I should feel grief, the way you note a landmark. What I actually felt, climbing, was lighter than I had felt in years, and I hated myself a little for it, and kept climbing.

The trouble started at the cairn.

Brannoc had said *halfway*, and the cairn was there, a waist-high stack of stones each one carried up by some traveler's hope, and I did what the custom demanded: I picked up a stone to add mine. And as I bent, I saw, wedged in a crevice beneath the oldest stones, a boot.

A boot with a foot in it.

He had been dead a while — weeks, maybe, dried out by the altitude — and he lay curled behind the cairn like a man who had lain down for a moment's rest and been declined the chance to get up again. Grey-and-gold tabard, rotted to lace. Vessan's colors. An open-palm man who had made it this far and no farther, and I stood there holding my luck-stone and doing arithmetic I did not want to do.

Because the boundary was close now. I could feel it the way you feel a storm front — not in the air exactly, but in the air's *attention*. And a dead soldier at the cairn meant one of two things: either the boundary had killed him, in which case I was about to die too, or he had crossed it alive and something on the far side had finished him, in which case I was about to die slightly later.

Either way, the stone in my hand suddenly seemed like a very small offering.

I set it on the pile anyway. Then, because I am who I am, I knelt and searched the dead man's pack, and I am not proud of how quickly I got over the deadness of him. Rations, mostly gone. A letter home, unsent, which I read and then regretted reading and then read again anyway — *Mama, the captain says the valley is real, the quiet place is real, we will be heroes* — and a map.

His map. Military issue, stamped with Vessan's hand-sigil, showing the approaches to the valley from the north. And here is the thing that made my blood go strange: it was *wrong*. Not badly wrong — confidently wrong. Rivers running where rivers weren't. A village marked that had burned out two generations ago. And the boundary itself drawn as a clean dotted line, neat as a fence, with a notation in a clerk's tidy hand: *BARRIER CONFIRMED IMPASSABLE. DIVINE PRESENCE NULL WITHIN.*

I had spent my career furious at maps like this. Maps drawn from rumor and assumption, maps that treated a place as a problem instead of a home. I had mocked them in margins. And now I stood over one and understood, with a lurch, that this confident ignorance was a weapon — that somewhere, a god's general staff believed they understood my valley because a clerk had dotted a line, and that belief had sent this boy up a mountain to die of something he hadn't been briefed on.

I took the map. It seemed owed to him, somehow, that it get used properly.

Then I shouldered my pack, said — out loud, absurdly — "Wish me luck," to a dead man, and turned north, and took the first step toward the line.

* * *

People had asked me, my whole life, what it was like at the boundary. Pilgrims' descendants, scholars passing through, once — memorably — a very drunk theologian who insisted the edge of a godless land must taste of something. I had always answered honestly: it isn't a wall, there's no shimmer, you just walk and keep walking and the valley is behind you and you're still yourself.

I had never actually tested it. No one from inside ever had, not and come back to say.

So when the air changed, I did what any trained observer does: I doubted my own instruments.

It started as pressure. Not wind — pressure, the feeling of descending into deep water, except I was climbing. The light thickened. Sound went strange, my own footsteps arriving at my ears a half-beat late, and the hair on my arms rose, and every instinct I had honed in thirty years of fieldwork screamed that this was the moment to turn around, and underneath that scream, quieter, older, was another voice saying: *this is the door. This is the actual door. You have drawn its shadow your entire life.*

I remember thinking, with terrible clarity: *if I turn back now, I will spend the rest of my life drawing this exact spot.*

I stepped through.

Cold water. That is the only honest description. Magic — because it was magic, it was *magic*, the thing my valley had been built to exclude, the thing I had mapped around the way you map around a lake — came back into the world and into me like plunging into a winter river. It poured through the soles of my feet and up my spine and out through my fingertips, and every hair on my body stood, and my heart slammed once, hard, like a fist on a door, and then—

And then I could *hear* it.

Not a voice. Not yet. But a hum, a vast low chord underneath the world, the sound of ten thousand prayers rising off the countryside below like heat off summer stone — *please let the calf live, please let him write, please let the rain stop, please, please, please* — and every one of those prayers was *real*, was a weight, was a thread tied to something enormous and listening, and I stood on the threshold of the quiet place with my mouth open like a fool, weeping, absolutely weeping, because I had spent my whole life mapping silence and I had never once imagined what the noise would sound like.

I had thought leaving would feel like escape. It felt like being born into a room that was already on fire.

I staggered three steps past the line and turned and looked back, and I will record this precisely, because precision is all I have: the valley behind me was just a valley. Green, sheltered, ordinary. The boundary was invisible from either side. All that divided the world's noise from the world's quiet was a line no eye could find, and I had just crossed it carrying a stolen name in my coat lining, and both factions of heaven wanted what I carried, and the nearest help was a dead boy at a cairn, and the nearest danger was everything else.

Below me, far below, on the southern slopes — movement. Grey-and-gold. At least six, maybe eight, moving fast, moving *upward*, and at their head a figure in ash-grey who walked without appearing to touch the ground.

They had found my workshop. They had read my maps. And my maps, God forgive me, my beautiful meticulous maps showed every route out of the valley except the one I'd taken — and the one I'd taken, the Shepherd's Cut, was marked in my own hand in the corner of my master sheet with a note I had written years ago in a mood of professional frustration:

*Unverified. Probably passable. Probably.*

They would come up the Cut. They would find the cairn. And they would move faster than me, because they had not spent the morning grieving a valley and marveling at the sound of prayer.

I ran. North, along the ridgeline, into the noise of the world, a man leaving the map at last — with no map ahead of him at all.

Somewhere below, faint on the wind, I heard the ash-grey figure begin to sing.
