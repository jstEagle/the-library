## 8. Maren Catches Up

I had been chasing a dead man's route for eleven days, and I want it on record that I was not in a good mood.

The messenger — Corvin, his name had been Corvin, though nobody at Vessan's temple had bothered to tell me that until I dug it out of a stable ledger — had traveled light and fast and stupid. Light, because he'd carried nothing but the scroll sewn into his coat lining. Fast, because he'd changed horses like a man who knew what was behind him. Stupid, because he'd stopped to sleep in an inn with only one back door, and that door opened onto a courtyard where two of the Ashen Choir's people were already waiting.

He'd gone out through the front instead. Barely. And then he'd bled for three days across half the province before dying in a cartographer's workshop in a valley that wasn't supposed to exist on any god's map.

Which is how I came to be standing at the Greywater ferry crossing at dawn, wearing a fishwife's shawl over my courier's leathers, watching a thin man with ink-stained fingers count out his fare in exact copper.

Ada Reyes did not look like a man carrying the most dangerous document in the world. He looked like a man who alphabetized his spice jars. He had the particular hunched patience of someone who spends his life bent over a drafting table, and he kept glancing at the river the way I glance at unfamiliar terrain — measuring it, filing it away, deciding how it would look from above. A cartographer even in flight. There was something almost admirable about it, in the way there's something almost admirable about a mouse cataloguing the hawk.

The ferry was a flat-bottomed barge with a rope strung bank to bank, and the ferryman pulled it along hand over hand while passengers stood in the wet and pretended this was normal. Fourteen souls aboard: two wool merchants, a family of five going to a funeral, a pair of pilgrims heading toward some minor shrine or other, a drover, and me. The mist sat on the water thick as porridge. Halfway across, you couldn't see either bank.

That was when I made my move, because halfway across is the only place in the world where nobody runs.

"Master Reyes." I let the shawl slip off my head so he could see my face, and I put my hand inside my coat where he could see that too. "Don't."

He went very still. Then, to his credit, he didn't do anything theatrical. He just looked at my hand, then at my face, and said, quietly, "You're the one who's been following Corvin's route. You paid the toll-keeper at Harrow Ford double to remember him."

"I paid her triple," I said. "She remembered anyway because he tipped badly. People always remember bad tippers. It's the one reliable thing about them."

"You're Vessan's." Not a question. His eyes had gone to the sigil worked into my belt buckle, mostly hidden under the shawl, not hidden enough. "The courier. They said a woman would come."

"They say a lot of things." I stepped closer, keeping my voice under the creak of the rope and the slap of water against the hull. "Here's what happens now. You give me the scroll, quietly, and when we reach the far bank you walk off this ferry as a man who had an uneventful morning. Or you make noise, and I take it from you anyway, and you arrive at the far bank in considerably worse condition. Your choice. I'm told cartographers are good at choosing routes."

"And if I don't have it?"

"Then you're the worst liar I've ever cornered, because your left shoulder has been turned away from me since I spoke, which is where a man carries something flat and rectangular when he doesn't want it pressed."

His jaw tightened. Good. Honest fear. Fear I could work with.

"The scroll," I said again, and held out my free hand.

And that should have been the end of it. Eleven days of cold roads and worse inns, ending in thirty seconds of professional efficiency. I'd done this kind of retrieval four times before — heretical texts, forged indulgences, once a love letter that would have started a small war between two river lords. You don't think about the contents. Contents are not your department. You are a hand, and the temple is the arm, and the arm does not explain itself to the hand.

But Ada Reyes looked at my open palm and didn't put the scroll in it. Instead he said, "Do you know what's in it?"

"That's not—"

"Because I've read it." He said it fast, low, the words tumbling like he'd been holding them behind his teeth for days and they'd finally gotten loose. "I broke the seal. I know I shouldn't have. But I read it, and I need to know — do *you* know what it says? Has anyone actually told you? Or did they just tell you it was dangerous?"

"It's heresy," I said. The word came out smooth and practiced, and I hated how smooth it sounded, because smooth meant rehearsed, and rehearsed meant someone had given it to me whole. "A forgery. Old lies dressed up to look like covenant law. It was written to destabilize the truce and hand the valley factions a pretext for war. My orders are to recover it and return it for destruction. That's all I need to know."

"All you *need* to know," he repeated. "Yes. That's exactly what I'd want a courier to say, if I were the one giving the orders."

The ferryman hauled another arm's length of rope. The mist swallowed us whole. For a moment we were fourteen people standing in a gray nowhere, and I felt the peculiar vertigo of that — no banks, no witnesses, no ground truth. Just his voice and mine and the water underneath.

"Tell me what the forgery says," I said, because I am, despite everything, curious, and curiosity is the flaw they never train out of couriers because a courier without curiosity delivers things without asking questions, and questions are the entire job.

He hesitated. I watched him weigh it — and here's the thing I noticed, the thing that has stayed with me since: he wasn't weighing whether to tell me. He was weighing whether telling me would hurt me. Like I was a person whose harm mattered. Which is not an assumption people generally make about Vessan's hands.

"The oath says," he began, and his voice dropped to almost nothing, "that the peace of the valley — my valley, the place I've mapped my whole life, the one place the gods' war never touched — was purchased. Bought. With a true name. Stolen from a god during the last years of the war, by a mortal, and held in trust ever since by that mortal's bloodline. It names the thief. It names the line. It sets out the terms of the trust, witnessed and sealed, and it says the name must be returned only when—" He stopped. Swallowed. "When the line fails. When there's no heir left to hold it."

The rope groaned. Somewhere behind me, one of the funeral family was quietly crying, and the sound of it seemed suddenly obscene.

"That's the forgery," Ada said. "That's the old lie. Tell me: does it read like a lie to you?"

I wanted to say yes. I want that understood — I *wanted* to say yes. I had been Vessan's courier for six years. I had run messages through three provinces and two border skirmishes. I had been promised, at my induction, that I served order, that the temples held the world together after the war had nearly torn it apart, that every errand I ran was a stitch in the seam. I was good at my work precisely because I believed in it.

And the problem — the whole rotten problem — was that I had spent eleven days retracing Corvin's route, and Corvin had been afraid. Not courier-nervous. Not run-and-dodge afraid. I'd talked to the woman at Harrow Ford, and she said he'd asked her, twice, whether the valley really existed, whether the peace there was real, whether children really grew up without hearing a single god's voice. He'd been checking. A man carrying a forgery doesn't check whether the thing it contradicts is true. A man carrying a forgery knows what's true; he's manufacturing the alternative.

A man carrying proof checks whether the world still matches it.

"There's a detail," I heard myself say, and my mouth kept going while the rest of me stood there appalled. "In the induction liturgy. The part they make you memorize. 'The peace was wrought and the price was paid and the price is not spoken.' Every initiate asks what price. Every initiate is told it's a rhetorical formula. That it means nothing."

"And now?" Ada asked softly.

"Now it sounds like a receipt," I said.

We stood there in the mist, and I watched him decide something about me, and I realized I was letting him, which was its own kind of failure. A proper courier would have taken the scroll by now. A proper courier wouldn't have recited liturgy to a target. A proper courier especially wouldn't have noticed that the target's hands were shaking, or wondered why a man who'd committed theft and seal-breaking and flight looked less like a criminal than like a clerk who'd found an error in the ledgers and couldn't unsee it.

"Give me the scroll," I said, one more time, but the authority had gone out of it, and both of us heard it go.

"If I give it to you," he said, "what happens to it?"

"It goes back to the temple. It gets destroyed. The lie dies."

"And if it's not a lie? If it's the actual covenant, the actual terms, the actual crime — what happens to the truth?"

I opened my mouth. Closed it. Because the honest answer was *nothing happens to it*, because truths that inconvenience gods don't get destroyed, they get buried, and buried things have a way of surfacing at the worst possible moment with the maximum possible damage. And the more honest answer, the one I couldn't say out loud on a ferry full of mourners, was that Vessan hadn't sent me alone. There were others behind me. Harder ones. Retrieval was my task, but I'd seen the second wave muster at the temple gates, and they hadn't looked like retrievers. They'd looked like erasers.

"The Choir wants it too," I said instead. "You know that. Ashen Choir agents burned the waystation at Dell's Crossing looking for you. If they get it, they won't destroy it quietly — they'll read it aloud in every market square from here to the coast, and they'll use it to strip Vessan bare in front of the world, and whether it's true or false won't matter, because it will start the war anyway. Both sides want it. Neither side wants it *read*. Doesn't that tell you something?"

"It tells me everyone's afraid of the same piece of paper," Ada said. "Which is usually what paper looks like right before it changes everything."

The far bank materialized out of the mist, gray trees, gray dock, the ferryman calling out for passengers to mind the gap between hull and planking. Ordinary sounds. The world insisting on continuing.

I still had my hand near my knife. He still had the scroll against his ribs. And I understood, with the clarity that comes only when you're about to do something irrevocable, that I had maybe ten seconds to choose which kind of person I was going to be for the rest of my life.

Here is what decided it. Not the liturgy. Not Corvin's fear. What decided it was this: Ada Reyes, cornered, unarmed, hunted by two divine factions, looked at me — the woman sent to take everything from him — and his face held no hatred at all. Only a kind of terrible sympathy. As if I were the one being robbed.

Nobody had ever looked at me like that. In six years of service, I had been a tool, a shadow, a signature on delivery receipts. Nobody had ever thought to wonder whether the hand might also bleed.

"Don't go to the conclave," I said abruptly.

"What?"

"When you reach Greyford — and you will, everyone in this story eventually reaches Greyford — don't trust the arbiters. Don't trust the clerks. The conclave is neutral the way a fence is neutral between two dogs fighting over a bone." I pulled the shawl back up over my hair, hiding the buckle, hiding myself. "There's another option. There's a place where oaths are kept. All of them. The originals. The drowned archive, under Lake Serel. If your scroll is real, there's a copy in the archive, and more than a copy — provenance, witnesses, the whole chain. Proof that doesn't depend on trusting either faction. Or me."

"Why would you tell me that?" His eyes narrowed. "If this is a trap—"

"If it were a trap, I'd have taken the scroll twenty minutes ago and you'd be face-down on these planks wondering what happened." I sighed. "Call it professional doubt. My employers told me the scroll was heresy. Heresy gets destroyed. But they sent a second team behind me, and second teams aren't for documents, Master Reyes. Documents burn easily. *People* who've read documents are the problem. So either my employers are lying to me about what this is, or they're lying to themselves, and I find I can't work for someone I can't trust to know their own mind."

The ferry bumped the dock. The ferryman called out. The wool merchants shuffled forward, and the funeral family gathered their black-clad grief, and the ordinary world resumed its ordinary motions around the two of us standing frozen in our private earthquake.

"So what now?" Ada asked. "You arrest me? Take the scroll? Report that you lost me in the mist?"

I looked at him for a long moment. Mist was still curling off the water behind him like the world hadn't finished deciding what it was.

"What I report," I said, "is my business. What you do next is yours. But if you're going to Lake Serel — and you'd be a fool not to — you'll need someone who knows the archive's approach, because the drowned stacks don't open for just anyone, and the Choir watches the northern road, and Vessan's second team watches the southern one, and I'm the only person alive who knows the third way in."

"That's a very generous offer from someone who threatened me ten minutes ago."

"I threatened you nine minutes ago, and I was better at it then." I stepped past him onto the dock, into the gray morning, and did not look back, because looking back is for people who haven't already decided. "Third way in. Northern shore. Two days. Bring the scroll, bring your maps, and bring a very good reason why I shouldn't regret this."

Behind me, after a pause long enough that I almost smiled, I heard his boots hit the planks, following.

What neither of us knew — what I couldn't have known, standing there in the fishwife's shawl with my career already burning behind me like a bridge I'd crossed and cut — was that the third way in was watched too. And that the watcher had been waiting, specifically, for a courier of Vessan's to lead them somewhere worth leading.
