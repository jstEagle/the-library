## 9. The Archive Beneath the Lake

**Maren**

I had never told anyone about the lake. Not because it was a secret, exactly — Vessan's order kept its secrets the way a miser keeps coins, counted nightly and resented either way — but because the archive was the kind of thing you didn't mention unless you wanted to be assigned to it. And nobody wanted to be assigned to it. The archivists down there hadn't seen the sun in forty years, and it showed in their complexions and their tempers.

Now I was bringing an outsider. A cartographer, no less. A man who made his living putting things on paper, which in my experience was one step removed from making your living taking things off paper and running.

"You're quiet," Ada said.

"I'm deciding how much to tell you."

"That's reassuring."

"It shouldn't be." I adjusted the strap of my pack and kept walking. The road along the lake shore was rutted and pale with dust, and the water beside us lay flat as hammered tin. "The archive is called the Sunk Repository. It's where oaths go when they're old enough that nobody wants to look at them anymore. Every covenant, every binding, every sworn thing that shaped the realms before the war burned itself out. They're stored under the water because water keeps things honest. Ink doesn't fade down there. Neither do lies."

"And Vessan's people just let you walk in?"

"Vessan's people *are* the people who walk in. That's the problem." I stopped at the shore, where a stone jetty jutted out like a broken tooth. "The archive is supposed to be neutral. Kept by mortals, open to any party to an oath. But for three generations the only parties who've come asking have been ours or the Choir's, and both of us treat it like a weapon cache. The archivists have gotten very good at saying no. I'm hoping they say yes to me, because I've spent six years fetching and carrying for them and they owe me small kindnesses the way everyone owes everyone small kindnesses."

"And if they say no?"

"Then we drown politely and go home."

Ada looked at the water the way he'd probably looked at every border he'd ever drawn: measuring it, wanting it to make sense. "How deep?"

"Doesn't matter. You don't swim. There's a door." I pointed at the jetty's end, where a bronze ring lay flush against the stone, green with age. "The founders built it so that oaths could be consulted without being exposed. You descend inside a column of air. It's one of the last works of the old craft, from when mortals still built things gods needed."

He crouched by the ring and touched it, and I watched something move across his face — not fear exactly. Recognition. The look of a man reading handwriting he knows.

"Cartographer," I said, "we need to be quick. When they find out I didn't bring you in, this becomes the first place they look."

"Then let's not stand on the doorstep admiring the architecture." He took hold of the ring and pulled, and the stone beneath our feet shuddered and opened like a mouth.

* * *

**Ada**

I want to say I descended into the lake with dignity. What actually happened is that I gripped the ladder rungs until my knuckles went white and made a sound I will deny to my grave as the air column closed above us and the water rose around it on every side — walls of green-gold light, fish drifting past at eye level, the whole impossible weight of a lake held back by nothing but an old promise.

Maren climbed down ahead of me, unbothered, which I found deeply offensive.

"The founders' craft," she said over her shoulder. "Air held by covenant. As long as the archive stands, the water stands aside."

"As long as the archive *stands*," I repeated. "That's a specific way to phrase it."

"Everything down here is phrased specifically. That's rather the point."

The shaft ended in a hall of black stone, dry and cold, lit by lamps that burned without smoke. Shelves ran away into the dark in ranks, and on the shelves — not books, exactly. Cases. Long tubes of glass and lead, each sealed with wax stamped in sigils I half-recognized from the margins of maps I'd copied as an apprentice. Oaths. Hundreds of them. Thousands. Each one a promise someone had once thought important enough to make permanent.

A figure detached itself from between the shelves. Old, robed, with the bleached look of a mushroom grown in the dark, and eyes that had clearly seen everything and been impressed by none of it.

"Maren of Vessan's service," the archivist said. "You're late returning the Corvane deposition. Eleven years late."

"I've been busy."

"So I hear." The milky eyes slid to me. "And this?"

"A cartographer. He's found something that belongs here."

"Everything belongs here. That has never once been a reason for anyone to bring anything."

"He found an oath-scroll," Maren said. "Sealed. Carried by a messenger who died getting it out of the valley. The valley, Elder Praxa. The one place none of this touches."

Something changed in the old woman's face. Not much. A hairline crack in plaster. "Show me."

I unwrapped the scroll from its oilcloth, and Praxa looked at the seal for a long time without touching it. Then she laughed — a dry, terrible sound, like a door that hadn't been oiled in a century.

"Do you know what this seal is?" she asked me.

"No."

"No one does. That was the design." She turned and began walking into the stacks. "Come. If you're going to break a seal that old, you'll do it in the reading room, where the ceiling can take the argument."

* * *

**Maren**

Praxa led us deeper than I'd ever been — past the public shelves, past the restricted cases, down a stair cut into bedrock to a round chamber with a single table of grey stone. She lit the lamps herself, waving off the junior archivist who scuttled at the edges of the light.

"Sit," she said. "Both of you. Now. Before you open that, you'll hear the terms, because ignorance is the luxury of people who haven't yet read what's under the wax."

"We're listening," Ada said.

"This repository holds oaths. It does not enforce them, interpret them, or — despite what both your factions believe — weaponize them. But there are documents here whose contents are load-bearing. Read them aloud in the wrong company and realms shift. Understand? The words are not a record of the truce. In places, the words *are* the truce."

Ada's hand had gone still on the scroll. "And this is one of those?"

"If it's what I think it is, it's the keystone of all of them." Praxa drew a thin knife and set it on the table between us. "So. Last chance. Walk away, and I report that you came, looked, and left. The peace holds another generation, maybe two, and you both die comfortably."

I should have taken it. I want that recorded somewhere, by someone, in ink that doesn't fade: I knew the right answer and watched myself refuse it. Six years I'd carried Vessan's messages, and in six years I'd noticed a pattern — every question I asked about the war's ending got answered with a different story. Gods don't lie, exactly. They just keep so many true versions that the truth drowns among them.

But a dead man had crawled thirty leagues to put a scroll in a stranger's hands, and his last word had been *taken*, and I'd spent two hundred miles telling myself it meant nothing.

"Open it," I said.

Ada broke the seal.

* * *

**Ada**

The parchment didn't crackle. That was the first wrong thing. Sealed scrolls of that age should crumble like autumn leaves; this one unfolded soft as cloth, and the ink was black as the day it dried, and the script was a formal hand I knew from boundary stones — the founding script, the one they taught you to copy before they taught you letters, because it appeared on every marker of every settled realm.

Maren read over my shoulder. I felt her breath stop.

It began the way oaths begin, with names and witnesses, and then it went somewhere oaths are not supposed to go.

*"Let it be entered in the deep record, in the sight of water and stone, that the god called Vessan did not lose his name in battle, nor spend it in mercy, nor give it in love. His name was taken. Taken in the night at Greyford by the hand of Aldous Brightwater, first founder of the Valley Covenant, who carried it across the mountains and buried it in trust beneath the hearth-stone of his line, that no power might speak it while the war endured.*

*In payment for this theft, the valley was granted its silence. No prayer shall cross its borders; no god shall follow. This is the wage of the crime, and the crime shall not be named while the line of Brightwater holds the trust.*

*If the line fails, the name wakes. If the name wakes, the war resumes. So it was sworn by the assembled powers, and so it is witnessed by the water and the stone."*

I read it twice. Three times. The words didn't change. I kept waiting for them to change.

"Aldous Brightwater," Maren said quietly.

"My family name," I said. My voice sounded like it belonged to someone standing further away than I was. "The valley — the whole reason the valley exists —"

"Was bought with a stolen god." She said it flatly, the way you'd state a distance or a toll. But her hand had gone to her belt knife, and I noticed, and she noticed me noticing, and neither of us said anything about it.

I sat down on the bench because my legs had filed a formal complaint. All my life I'd mapped that valley. Every stream, every fold of hill, every farmstead where the shrine stood silent and the children grew up never hearing an answered prayer. I'd thought of it as a refuge. The one clean country. The place the gods' madness couldn't reach.

It wasn't a refuge. It was a *fence*. A stolen thing hidden behind a wall of silence, and my family the thieves who'd built it, generation after generation keeping the trust, keeping the *quiet*, and calling it peace.

"The messenger," I said. "He said 'the name was taken.' Those were almost his last words. He wasn't delivering a secret. He was quoting."

"He was quoting the covenant," Praxa agreed from the doorway. She'd come down without my noticing, which I suspected was a professional habit. "Which means someone up there has read this document recently enough to be frightened of it. Which means the trust is stirring. Which means—" she looked at me with those awful clear eyes— "the line of Brightwater is failing. Or being made to fail."

"I'm the last of it," I said. "As far as I know."

"Then as far as the oath is concerned, you're not a man, cartographer. You're a condition."

The lamp flames leaned, all together, though there was no draft. Somewhere far above us, through all that held-back water, I felt — or imagined I felt — the lake press closer against the covenant that kept it out.

Maren moved between me and the door. Not blocking it. Standing at it. Listening.

"What?" I asked.

"Boots," she said. "On the upper stairs. More than two. Archivists don't march."

Praxa's face went smooth and unreadable. "The Choir has a key," she said. "They've always had a key. I told your faction that years ago and nobody listened, because nobody listens down here, that's rather the point of the place."

"How long do we have?" Maren asked.

"Less than that."

She snuffed the lamps with a pinch of her fingers, and in the sudden dark, the only light in the world was the covenant on the table, its ink faintly, patiently glowing — the way, I understood then, it had glowed for nine hundred years, waiting for someone of my blood to finally read it.

"They'll want the scroll," Maren whispered. "The Choir destroys what it can't hold. If they find you holding it—"

"Then I'd better not be holding it." I rolled the parchment, fast, and pressed it into her hands. Her fingers closed around it before her mind caught up — I saw the exact moment she realized what I'd done.

"You're giving it to *me*? I work for Vessan."

"You worked for Vessan. Two hours ago you chose to stand between me and a door instead of taking this and running. I map people too, Maren. It's the same skill as mapping coastlines. You watch for where the land stops pretending."

For a moment she said nothing. Above us, the boots reached the landing.

"Storeroom," Praxa breathed. "Behind the third shelf. There's a drain that runs to the shore spring. It's narrow, it's foul, and it comes up in a sheep field. Go."

We went.

* * *

**Maren**

The drain was every bit as narrow and foul as promised, and Ada complained about it with the steady, meticulous grievance of a man cataloguing injustices for future reference. I let him. Complaining keeps panic in its pen.

We came up in mud and sheep droppings under a grey afternoon sky, a quarter mile around the shoreline from the jetty. Behind us, faint through the ground, I heard the lake give one long groan — stone shifting, water finding new room — and then settle.

"They have the archive," Ada said.

"They have the archive. They don't have the covenant." I patted the oilcloth bundle at my chest. "Though I'd feel better about carrying it if I understood why you trusted me."

"Because you're angry," he said simply. "Not at me. At the arithmetic. I watched your face when you read the payment clause. *The valley was granted its silence.* Everyone always talks about the stolen name. Nobody talks about what the theft purchased — a thousand years of children praying into silence and calling it safety. You did the sums faster than I did. People who do the sums fast are the ones worth following into trouble."

I looked at him — this soft-handed mapmaker with ink stains to the elbow, heir to the oldest crime in the world, handing the most dangerous document in existence to a woman sent to arrest him — and I felt the last load-bearing wall of my loyalty to Vessan come down without a sound.

"There's a copy of the founding registry in the archive," I said. "I've seen it. Aldous Brightwater had descendants who stayed north after the valley was settled. One branch held a ferry charter on the Vell. Another—" I hesitated.

"Another what?"

"Another took service with the Choir. Three generations back. If blood matters to that oath, Ada, then the Ashen Choir may have a claimant of their own. And they just took the archive."

Ada went very still, the way he did over a difficult contour line.

"Then we'd better get to them first," he said. "Before they decide the simplest way to settle a claim is to end the bloodline."

Somewhere beyond the hills, thunder gathered that had nothing to do with weather. We started walking.
