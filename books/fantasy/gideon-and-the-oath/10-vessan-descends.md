## 10. Vessan Descends

I have been called many things across the long centuries of my existence: the Justiciar, the Scale-Bearer, the God Who Counts. My faithful pray to me for fairness in contracts, for clarity in disputes, for the strength to hold a grudge with dignity. They imagine me as a figure of terrible patience, weighing every soul on golden scales, unmoved by passion.

The truth is less flattering and more interesting. I am not patient. I have simply had to *be*, because patience was the only virtue left to me after the war took everything else. There is a difference between stillness and serenity, and I embody the former while perpetually resenting the latter.

Consider my current situation. Somewhere in the mortal world — specifically in a valley so aggressively unremarkable that even magic forgot its address — a scroll exists that contains my stolen name. The name that was taken from me before the war's end. The name without which I cannot be whole, cannot exercise my full dominion, cannot do any of the ten thousand things a god ought to be able to do but instead must accomplish through intermediaries, proxies, and the divine equivalent of doing your own taxes by candlelight.

And the courier I trusted to retrieve it has just informed me, through the proper channels of prayer-and-response, that she has lost it.

*Lost* it.

Do you understand what it means when an immortal says they are trying very hard not to feel something? It means the feeling is already inside them, wearing its boots, tracking mud across the furniture of their composure.

* * *

Maren Reyes knelt in the receiving chamber of my temple at Vessanholt, which is built entirely of white stone and black iron, because I believe architecture should reflect the personality of its occupant and I contain multitudes, most of them load-bearing.

She knelt well, I will grant her that. Spine straight, head bowed at precisely the angle of contrition that suggests genuine remorse rather than performance. Twenty-two years old, sharp-eyed, sharper-tongued when she forgets herself, and the finest courier my faction has produced in three generations. I had picked her out myself from the orphan rows of the war-scholar houses. I had invested in her the way a miser invests in gold: carefully, completely, and with the expectation of eventual return.

"The messenger reached the valley," she said. "He died there. Of his wounds, or of something else — the reports conflict."

"And the scroll?"

"Gone. A local man took it. A cartographer." She hesitated, and I noted the hesitation the way a jeweler notes a flaw in an otherwise perfect stone. "Ada Reyes. He hid it before the search parties arrived. We tore apart his workshop. We found maps. Hundreds of them. No scroll."

"Maps," I repeated.

"He's a cartographer of the valley. It's apparently a whole discipline. Mapping a place where nothing happens."

There it was again — that flicker of dryness in her voice that I had once found charming and now found instructive. Maren did not fear me the way the others feared me. She respected me, which is different, and more useful, and more dangerous. Respect can be renegotiated. Fear only ever depreciates.

"You find the profession amusing," I said.

"I find it strange that anyone would spend their life drawing lines around nothing."

"Then you understand him better than you think. I spent nine hundred years drawing lines around a wound." I let that sit in the air between us. Mortals always underestimate how much silence a god can deploy when he wishes to. Silence is our native language; speech is the translation, and translations always lose something. "Tell me about this cartographer."

"Ordinary. Middle-aged. No factional ties, no military record, no prayers logged in the last decade. He lives alone above his workshop. His neighbors describe him as precise, quiet, and — " she consulted her memory, and I watched her decide whether to include the next part — "fond of burning things. Paper, mostly. Map edges. Scraps."

"Burning things," I said. "A man who destroys the parts of his work that exceed his authority. How very disciplined of him. And how very inconvenient."

"You believe he'll destroy the scroll?"

"I believe," I said, rising from the judgment seat and descending the seven steps toward her — gods should never loom casually; looming is a craft and deserves intention — "that a man who burns the edges of his own maps is a man who understands that some knowledge changes the shape of everything around it. Which means he either fears the scroll or reveres it. Both are manageable. What concerns me is the third possibility."

"Which is?"

"That he reads it. That he understands it. And that he decides the theft described within it was committed by his own blood." I stopped one step above her, close enough that she could feel the pressure of my attention, which mortals describe as a weight on the chest, like standing at the bottom of deep water. "Because here is what the archives confirm, Maren, and what you will now carry as certainty rather than suspicion: the thief of my name was a mortal. A founder of the valley. One Brightwater, of the line that still holds the trust."

Her breath caught. Small, controlled, immediately suppressed. But I heard it. I hear everything said in my temples; it is one of the few perks of the position.

"His name," she said slowly, "is Ada Reyes."

"Reyes is the valley-name. Assumed three generations back, when the family scattered the records. Before that, they were Brightwaters. Every one of them. The trust passes down the line like a debt no one told them they owed." I turned away, because watching a mortal process betrayal is either cruel or educational depending on how much you respect them, and I was still deciding about Maren. "So you see, our cartographer is not an obstacle. He is the heir. And heirs, unlike thieves, can be reasoned with. Or replaced."

* * *

Let me tell you about the war, since everyone else tells it wrong.

The histories — mortal histories, which are written by survivors, and divine histories, which are written by winners, and in this case those were the same people — say the war ended in victory. They say the Choir of Ash rose against the old order and was broken, that the factions settled into their territories, that peace descended like a benediction.

Here is what actually happened: we exhausted ourselves into a truce, and then we lied about it.

Nine hundred years ago, the gods fought over the question of names — true names, the deep syllables that constitute a god's actual substance, the difference between being worshipped and merely existing. I held mine. The Choir wanted all such names destroyed, on the principle that no power should be absolute, which is a beautiful philosophy if you enjoy losing wars. The fighting burned whole realms down to the bedrock. Prayers went unanswered for decades because the ones who might have answered were busy tearing each other's heavens open.

And then a mortal did something none of us anticipated. A man named Ede Brightwater — a keeper of oaths, a minor functionary in the covenant-houses, a person so insignificant that history recorded his crime before it recorded his face — stole my name.

Not copied it. Not borrowed it. *Stole* it, out of the vault of heaven itself, using a gap in the covenant law that I have spent nine centuries trying not to think about, because every time I do, I conclude that the gap was deliberate, and that someone among my own kind arranged it, and that way lies a paranoia even I am not prepared to cultivate professionally.

Brightwater carried my name into the valley — the one place the war could not reach, the neutral ground, the pocket of the world where divinity had been burned out entirely — and he sealed it into an oath. An oath of trust. Held by his bloodline, in perpetuity, until such time as the name could be restored "by right and proof."

By right and proof.

Do you see? Do you see why I have waited nine hundred years? The oath does not say the name belongs to the valley. It does not say the name must be destroyed. It says the name must be *restored by right and proof*. Ede Brightwater, that magnificent little clerk, wrote my claim into the theft itself. He stole from me and then notarized my receipt.

The Choir believes the scroll, if it surfaces, should be burned — that the name should die with it, that no god should ever wield what was once stolen. They call this justice. I call it the philosophy of people who would rather burn a house than admit the deed exists.

But the deed exists. The proof exists. And when I stand before the conclave at Greyford — and I will stand there, soon, with the scroll in hand and the covenant law on my side — the arbitration will be simple. The name returns to me. My full dominion returns to me. And with it, the legal warrant to resume the war against the Choir on terms they cannot refuse, because I will hold the one thing they spent nine centuries ensuring no one held.

They will call it vengeance. I have made my peace with that word. Vengeance is simply justice that arrives late and angry, and I have been late for nine hundred years, so I have had ample time to become angry properly. Thoroughly. With footnotes.

* * *

"Maren." I had returned to the judgment seat, because a god should deliver verdicts from elevation; it saves time on the looming. "You will return to the field. You will find the cartographer. You will not harm him."

Something moved behind her eyes. "The orders from the field command say—"

"I know what the field command says. The field command is composed of mortals who mistake enthusiasm for strategy. You will not harm him because a dead heir cannot sign anything, and there may come a moment when we need his signature more than his silence." I let my voice soften, which for me is a tactical decision, like sheathing a blade to make a point about how easily it could be unsheathed. "You will bring him to me alive, and you will bring the scroll, and you will do both because you are the best I have and because I chose you knowing that you ask questions."

"Is that a compliment?"

"It is a warning wearing a compliment's clothing. Ask your questions, Maren. Ask them of me, if you like. But when the answers trouble you — and they will — remember that the alternative to my justice is the Choir's erasure. They do not want the name returned. They want it annihilated, and everything it touches with it. Every covenant. Every contract. Every promise ever sworn under the old law, dissolved. Including yours." I leaned forward. "Your oath of service to me is written under that law. If the Choir wins, you are not freed, child. You are *unmade as a person who made promises*. Think about what that means before you decide whose side doubt serves."

She bowed. Lower than before. Whether from conviction or calculation, I genuinely could not tell, and I want it recorded that I noticed this and filed it away, because I notice everything, and noticing is the burden that comes with the scales.

She rose to leave. At the door she paused — mortals always pause at doors, as if thresholds require editorial comment — and said, without turning:

"If the theft was real. If the name was truly taken from you." Her voice was level, professional, and underneath it ran something I recognized from the war-scholar houses, from the orphan rows, from every mortal who has ever looked up at a god and asked the question that gets people reassigned. "What did you do to deserve it?"

"A fair question," I said. "Ask it again after Greyford, when the answer will be legally binding."

She left. The doors closed. And I sat alone in my white-and-black hall, holding the silence like a scale that refused to balance.

Here is the thing I did not tell her, the thing I tell no one, the thing I keep in the locked room of myself where I store the inventory of my own honesty: I do not know what Ede Brightwater saw, that night nine hundred years ago, when he reached into the vault of heaven and took my name. I know what I was before the war. I know what the histories say I did during it. And I know that when a mortal steals from a god, the mortal is almost always the one who ends up dead — except that Ede Brightwater lived out his whole natural life in the valley, peacefully, unmolested, protected by an oath that no god dared break.

Protected. By an oath. From *us*.

I have examined that fact from every angle for nine centuries, the way one examines a tooth that aches only in certain weather. And every examination ends at the same uncomfortable threshold: either the oath protected him because breaking it would have cost us too much —

— or because some part of us agreed with what he'd done.

No. Enough. Doubt is a luxury for beings with time to waste, and I have wasted quite enough of eternity already. The scroll exists. The heir exists. The proof will be delivered, by courier or by conquest, and at Greyford I will stand before the conclave and speak my own name aloud for the first time in nine hundred years, and the world will learn what justice sounds like when it finally stops being patient.

My scouts report movement in the valley. The cartographer has fled east, toward the river country, carrying a rolled map that weighs considerably more than paper should. Someone travels with him — the reports are unclear, but the description matches a missing courier of mine.

Maren. Clever girl. Asking her questions out in the world instead of in my hall.

I rose from the judgment seat, and the temple stones rang beneath me like a struck bell, and every priest in Vessanholt fell to their knees without knowing why.

Prepare the road, I told them, in the voice that is not a voice. Your god is going to collect what is owed.

And somewhere far to the east, I felt it — faint as a heartbeat through stone — my name, stirring in its wrappings, turning over in a stranger's hands.

It knew me. Even stolen, even buried, even renamed and refiled under a mortal's borrowed surname — it knew me.

Soon, I promised it. Soon.
