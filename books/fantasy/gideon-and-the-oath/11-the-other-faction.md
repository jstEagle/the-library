## 11. The Other Faction

*Ada*

I had always assumed that if I were ever captured, it would be by people who wanted something from me. Information. Ransom. An apology, perhaps, for my handwriting, which Maren once described as "a spider's suicide note." I had not anticipated being captured by people who wanted me to give something *up*, and who were prepared to be extremely polite about it.

The politeness was the unsettling part.

It began with the net — because of course it began with a net; the gods' servants have no imagination and a great deal of rope — and ended with us kneeling in a circle of grey-robed figures in a hollow beneath a hill that did not appear on any map I had ever drawn, which, given that I had drawn most maps within two hundred miles, said something either about their skill or about the hill's character.

"The Ashen Choir," Maren muttered beside me, her wrists bound with the same efficient cord as mine. "Wonderful."

"You know them?"

"I know *of* them. Vessan's clergy calls them heretics. The Choir calls Vessan's clergy 'the ones who will burn first when the accounting comes.'" She flexed her fingers against the bindings, testing. "They're the faction that wants the name destroyed."

"Both factions want the scroll," I said. "One wants to use it. One wants to unmake it. And here I am, holding neither, which apparently makes me the most dangerous man in the valley."

"You're not holding anything. You hid it in your boot like an amateur."

"I hid it in my boot like a *survivor*. There's a difference, and the difference is that I'm alive."

"Debatable," she said, as the grey robes closed around us.

Their leader came forward out of the circle the way smoke leaves a doused fire: slowly, reluctantly, leaving a shape behind. She was older than I expected — I had imagined fanatics as young men with burning eyes, but this woman had the face of someone who had spent decades being patient, which is its own kind of fanaticism. Her robe was ash-grey at the hem and darker at the shoulders, as though she had been standing in a fire that never quite reached her head.

"Ada Reyes," she said.

My stomach performed a small, cold maneuver. "You have the advantage of me."

"We have the advantage of everyone. That is rather the point of us." She crouched so that we were eye to eye, which I appreciated and distrusted in equal measure. "My name is Sereth Ondile. I keep the Choir's ledger of grievances. Yours is one of the shorter entries, I'm pleased to say. A cartographer. A keeper of boundaries. A man whose family kept a valley empty for nine generations without knowing why."

"Without knowing why," I repeated. "So you do know why."

"Better than you do, I suspect. Better than Vessan would like anyone to." She studied my face with the particular attention of someone reading a document for forgery. "Where is the oath?"

"Safe," I said.

"From whom?"

"That," I said, "is the question, isn't it."

Something moved at the corner of her mouth. Not quite a smile. The ancestor of a smile, fossilized. "Maren Voss. Courier of the House of Vessan, four years' service, commendations for discretion." She turned her head slightly. "Your discretion has brought you a long way from your employer's reach. How does that feel?"

Maren's jaw tightened. "Ask me again when my hands are free."

"Oh, they will be. That is the offer I've come to make." Sereth rose, and the circle of grey robes shifted with her like water rearranging itself around a stone. "Not to you, courier. To him."

* * *

*Maren*

I did not like the way she said *to him*, and I liked even less the way Ada straightened, the way a man does when he is about to be flattered into something stupid.

Sereth Ondile walked the perimeter of our little prison while she talked, and I noticed what she was careful not to show: the Choir's camp was small. Thirty, maybe forty. Grey robes mended at the elbows. Cook-fires banked low, as if they'd learned to be ashamed of smoke. This was not an army. This was a congregation that had been losing arguments for centuries and had decided, somewhere along the way, that losing cleanly was its own victory.

"The war between the gods ended in exhaustion," Sereth said, to the camp as much as to us. "Not in justice. Not in peace. In exhaustion. Two powers too broken to continue, and a truce built on a theft neither would confess. Nine generations of mortals have lived in that valley thinking themselves blessed with quiet. They were not blessed. They were *collateral*. Held in trust like coin in a miser's purse, until one god or the other grew strong enough to come collect."

"And your solution?" Ada asked. His voice was level. I had learned in the weeks since the ferry to hear the effort behind his levelness — he leveled himself the way other men braced against a wall.

"To destroy the name. Entirely. Unwritten, unspoken, unremembered. No god may wield what no longer exists." She stopped before him. "And no mortal line may hold it in trust, and be hunted for it, and die for it, generation after generation, for a crime committed before their grandmothers' grandmothers drew breath."

I watched that land on him. I watched it land hard. Because it was a good argument — that was the trouble with the Ashen Choir, everyone said so; they were wrong in the way that good arguments are wrong, by being almost right — and because it offered him exactly what he had been running from the lack of: safety. An ending. A fire to throw the thing into, and then a life afterward.

"You're offering him sanctuary," I said flatly.

"I'm offering him *amnesty*," Sereth corrected. "Sanctuary is for the hunted. He would be free. The oath burns tonight, in front of him, by his own hand if he wishes it. The Choir scatters to the winds with nothing left to carry or protect. Vessan's hunters lose their quarry. The valley keeps its quiet — honestly, this time, because there will be nothing underneath it. And Ada Reyes walks away a man with no inheritance but his own name." She tilted her head. "Which, I understand, is more than he had yesterday."

"That's not how he'll walk away," I said.

"No?"

"He'll walk away wondering. For the rest of his life. Wondering what was in those lines he burned. Wondering who his family stole from, and what they bought with it, and whether the peace he sold to protect was worth the ignorance he chose instead." I got to my feet, awkward with bound wrists, because some things are worth doing awkwardly. "You're not offering him freedom. You're offering him a very comfortable kind of blindness. I've carried messages for Vessan for four years. I know what sealed orders feel like from the outside. This feels exactly like that."

Sereth looked at me for a long moment. "You are arguing against your own faction's destruction of evidence, courier. Do you understand what you're saying? If the name survives, Vessan can claim it. If Vessan claims it, the war resumes, and the last war cracked mountains."

"If the name is destroyed," I said, "the crime is destroyed with it. The mortals who suffered it never learn they suffered. The thief's line stays clean in every record. And the gods go on being gods, just poorer by one name." I shook my head. "That's not peace. That's a cover-up with hymns."

* * *

*Ada*

There is a particular vertigo in hearing someone argue your secret thoughts back to you in public. Maren had put words to the exact temptation coiling in my chest since Sereth said *burn it* — the sweet, shameful pull of erasure. Burn the oath and I stop being the heir to a crime. Burn it and I go back to being a mapmaker of a quiet valley, a man with a small life and clean hands.

Clean hands. As if cleanliness could survive contact with what I now knew.

"I'd like to see it," I said.

Sereth raised an eyebrow. "See what?"

"The fire. Your ledger. Whatever you burn things in." I worked my bound hands up between us. "Because here is my counter-offer, and I'll only make it once. I don't surrender the scroll tonight. But I'll read you its terms — the parts that matter to your grievance — and you can tell me whether annihilation still looks like justice when you know exactly what you'd be erasing. If after that you still want it burned, I'll consider it. That's more consideration than Vessan gave me. He sent soldiers."

For a moment the whole camp went still, thirty-odd grey robes not breathing. Then Sereth laughed — a short, dry sound, like a page turning.

"A negotiator. The messenger died to bring that scroll to you, and you treat it like a contract clause."

"The messenger died to bring it to *someone*," I said. "He didn't say who. Maybe he was wrong about me. But I intend to find out what I'm wrong about before I let anyone — god, choir, or cartographer — decide what gets erased."

She considered me for a long time. Behind her, the firelight moved on faces that had gone carefully blank, the way congregations do when doctrine and curiosity collide.

"Untie them," Sereth said at last.

The cords fell away. My hands ached with returning blood. Maren stood beside me, rubbing her wrists, and did not look at me, which from Maren was a form of applause.

"You'll read the terms," Sereth said. "Here, by this fire, where my people can hear. And then we vote. The Choir decides by voice, not by decree. It is slow, and it has cost us battles, and I would not trade it." She gestured toward the largest of the cook-fires. "Bring the scroll, cartographer."

"It stays where it is until I've seen your ledger of grievances," I said. "Fair is fair. You want to know what you're burning. I want to know who I'm negotiating with."

Another pause. That fossilized smile again, thawing a degree.

"Nine generations of your family kept a secret well enough to fool gods," she said. "I begin to see where you got it."

* * *

*Maren*

They brought the ledger out wrapped in oilcloth, and it was — I will grant the heretics this — a beautiful piece of work. Not a book of complaints, as I'd imagined, but a census. Names. Thousands of names, in ink faded through shades of brown and black, each one a mortal who had died in the old war, or in its long grey aftermath, or simply waiting, generation after generation, for an accounting that never came.

"This is what we are," Sereth said, opening it across her knees. "Not an army. A memory with a membership. Every name in this book belongs to someone the gods spent and never repaid. We keep it so that when the accounting finally comes, someone can stand up and say: *these*. These are the ones. Not abstractions. Not collateral. People, with names, who are owed."

Ada looked down at the pages for a long moment. Then he reached into his boot.

I tensed. So did half the camp. But what he drew out was the scroll, yes — and also, folded small beside it, a scrap of his own paper, covered in his terrible handwriting.

"Before I read the terms," he said, "you should know I've been doing what I always do. Mapping. The oath has a structure. Terms, conditions, a keystone clause, a line of trustees. And structures can be redrawn." He held up the scrap. "This isn't the oath. It's a sketch of what it *could* say. I started it the night I first read the original, because I couldn't sleep, and drawing is how I don't sleep. I haven't finished it. I may never finish it. But before you vote to burn the original, you should know that a third option exists. Not Vessan's claim. Not your erasure. Something else."

Sereth stared at the scrap of paper as though it had spoken. Around the fire, the grey robes leaned in despite themselves — I saw it happen, doctrine losing to curiosity in real time, face by face.

"What could it possibly say," Sereth said slowly, "that neither a god nor a grave would want?"

Ada smiled, and it was the first time I'd seen him smile since the ferry, and it was not entirely a comforting sight. It was the smile of a man looking at a blank margin and seeing room.

"That," he said, "is what I've been trying to draw."

And then the night split open with light — white, gold-edged, coming down through the trees from the hilltop like a blade through canvas — and every grey robe in the camp was on their feet, and I knew before anyone spoke whose arrival that was, because I had spent four years learning the shape of Vessan's power, and it had just found us.

"They followed me," I breathed. "Gods forgive me. They followed *me*."
