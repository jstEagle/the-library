## 12. A Name of One's Own

They gave me a cell with a window, which I suppose was meant as a kindness. The Ashen Choir had a strange relationship with kindness — it came wrapped in ash-scented robes and always, always with a price. The window was high and narrow and looked out on nothing but sky, which I suspect was the point. Sky has no borders. It was either a taunt or a lesson, and knowing the Choir, probably both.

I'd been in the cell — I counted by meals, because they fed me at intervals that were almost certainly designed to make time feel like porridge — for what I judged to be four days when I finally stopped pacing and did the thing I should have done on the first night.

I drew.

There was no ink. There was no paper. There was me, my memory, and a floor of grey stone worn smooth by centuries of penitents who had presumably also paced. But a cartographer learns early that the map is not the territory, and more importantly, that the territory is not the map. You can draw anywhere. I had once charted the whole southern reach of the valley in the dirt of my workshop floor while waiting for a delivery of vellum, and I had drawn coastlines on fogged glass as a boy, and I had spent one feverish night as an apprentice mapping the inside of my own eyelids.

So I sat down cross-legged in the middle of the cell, closed my eyes, and began to draft.

* * *

Here is what I knew, laid out like survey points waiting to be connected:

Point one: the oath-scroll named my ancestor — Aldous Reyes, founder, revered, his face stamped on coins I had spent without a second thought my entire life — as the thief of a god's true name. Not a finder. Not a keeper. A thief. The archive beneath the lake had been unambiguous about this, in the way archives are unambiguous about everything except their own biases.

Point two: the valley where I was born, where magic could not follow, where prayers went unanswered for generations — its peace was purchased with that stolen name. The name was the keystone. Remove it, and the arch falls. Keep it, and every quiet morning in the valley, every child who grew up never fearing the sky, stood on a foundation of theft.

Point three: Vessan wanted the name restored to himself so he could restart the old war from a position of strength. The Ashen Choir wanted the name destroyed so no god could ever wield it again. And the conclave at Greyford wanted the whole matter buried under enough procedural language that it would take another thousand years to dig out.

Point four — and this was the one that kept catching in my chest like a fishhook — point four was me. Ada Reyes, cartographer, heir to a trust he had never known he held. The scroll had said *held in trust by a mortal line*, and there was exactly one mortal line it could mean, because the messenger had walked half-dead across a valley boundary specifically to put it in my hands.

Everything I was stood on a crime.

I turned that over the way you turn over a stone in a stream, expecting it to be heavy and finding instead all the crawling things underneath. My meticulousness — the trait I'd built my whole identity around, the patience to chart a valley inch by inch — where had that come from? From men who had needed to be meticulous, who had needed to keep a secret through generations, who had taught their children precision the way other families taught piety. My restlessness, the hunger to leave that I'd mistaken for wanderlust — maybe that was inherited too. Maybe some part of me had always known the map was wrong, the way a dog knows before an earthquake.

Even my name. Even *Reyes*. A name is a kind of map too — it tells the world where you come from, what line you belong to. And mine pointed back to a thief.

I laughed, alone in my cell, and it came out wrong, and I decided not to do that again.

* * *

On the fifth day — or the sixth; the porridge-time made liars of us all — a Choir acolyte brought my meal and lingered at the bars longer than usual.

"The leader will see you tomorrow," she said. She was young, with the ash-grey streaks in her hair that marked full initiation, though her streaks looked recent, the black roots still fighting through. "He'll want your answer."

"You already know my answer," I said. "You've heard me say it to the wall. The wall's very convinced."

Her mouth twitched. Then she made herself solemn again, because solemnity was currency here. "Sanctuary is not nothing, cartographer. There are perhaps three places in all the realms where Vessan's people cannot touch you. This is one."

"And the price is the scroll."

"The price is the *name*," she corrected, and there was something almost gentle in it. "The scroll is just paper. The name is a wound that's been bleeding for nine hundred years. We only want to cauterize it."

"By burning the page."

"By ending it. Yes." She set the tray down and paused. "May I ask you something? Off the record of your wall?"

"That depends how much the wall charges for consultations."

"Why did you become a cartographer? Of unmapped things, specifically. Most mapmakers want the world filled in. You seem to want the opposite."

I considered lying to her, then considered that she was the first person in days to treat me as anything other than a parcel containing a document. "Because blank space is honest," I said. "A blank space says: we don't know. Everything else on a map is a claim. Coastlines move. Mountains get measured wrong. Kingdoms redraw themselves. But a blank space never lies to you. It just waits."

She nodded slowly, as if I'd confirmed something she'd suspected. "And if the blank space turns out to have something in it? Something terrible?"

"Then you draw it," I said. "That's the job. You don't get to leave things out because they're terrible. That's how maps kill people."

She left. I sat with my cold meal and realized I had just answered my own question, several days ahead of schedule.

* * *

That night I didn't sleep. Instead I drafted.

The Choir's offer was safety through erasure. Burn the name, burn the crime, burn the debt — and with them, burn the truth. Nine hundred years of valley peace would stand on nothing at all, an arch with no keystone, held up by habit and forgetting. And the mortals who had suffered the war — the ones whose villages burned, whose names were never recorded because recording was a luxury of the victorious — they would lose the only proof that they had ever been owed anything.

Vessan's claim was restoration through theft continued. Give the name back to the god who claimed it was his, let him wield it, let him settle scores. The war would begin again on favorable terms — favorable to whom? To a being who had watched mortals burn for nine centuries and called it truce.

Deliver it, destroy it. Restore it, erase it. Two options, two factions, and me in the middle like a border river that both armies wanted to ford.

But here is the thing about borders, the thing I had spent my whole life learning: a border is not a fact. It is a *decision*. Someone drew it. Someone, at some point, stood on the ground and chose where the line went, and everyone afterward treated that choice as if it were bedrock. The valley's boundary — the one that defined my entire existence, the one magic could not cross — someone had *drawn* that. And the oath-scroll, I now understood, was the drawing. It was the original survey. Every border in the world of gods and men traced back to lines of text, and text can be amended.

I opened my eyes in the dark cell and stared at the ceiling, heart going hard.

An oath is a map of obligation. It marks who owes what to whom, where the lines of duty run, where the territories of claim begin and end. And I was — whatever else I was, whatever crimes my blood carried — the finest cartographer of obligations in any realm, because I had spent thirty years mapping the one place obligations had been deliberately erased. I knew what a boundary looked like from the inside. I knew how it felt to live within a line someone else had drawn.

So why was I letting them choose between their two lines?

* * *

I began work at what I judged to be midnight, because midnight felt right for heresy.

No ink, no paper. Memory and stone. I recited the original oath to myself first, all of it, every clause, until I could walk through it blind — and I could, because I had read it eleven times in Maren's ferry cabin and twice more since arriving here, and because a mind trained to hold ten thousand coastline details does not easily release six paragraphs of covenant. Then I took out my imaginary stylus and began to find the errors.

Because that's what they were, once you looked with a cartographer's eye. Errors. The original oath assumed the god's name was property — *let the name be held in trust until such time as its rightful owner claims it*. Rightful owner. As if a name were a field, fenced and titled. As if the mortals who bled in the war were merely terrain upon which divine disputes occurred.

Clause by clause, I redrew it.

*Let the name be held in trust* became *let the name be returned* — but not to Vessan. Returned to those from whom its theft had cost the most. Not the god who claimed ownership. Not the void the Choir wanted to consign it to. The mortals. All of them. The war's dead, whose names no archive kept because archives belong to victors. Let the name be broken apart, if breaking was required, and given to the living as inheritance — a name belonging to no god, wielded by no throne, held in common like a road, like a well, like the sky above my narrow window.

It was, I recognized with a certain dizzy clarity, the same principle I'd used on my valley maps. When a territory refuses to be owned, you don't force the ownership. You mark it as common. You write *here be no lords* in the margin and mean it.

My hands shook. Not with fear — or not only fear. With the specific tremor of a man who has been holding a compass his whole life and has finally understood what it was for.

* * *

Near dawn, the flaws arrived, as flaws do.

First flaw: I was not a lawyer, a priest, or a god. An amended oath drafted by a prisoner in his head might be worth exactly the parchment it wasn't written on. Oaths, presumably, required formalities. Witnesses. Conclave sanction. Things I did not have and could not conjure.

Second flaw: even if the words held power, I had no right to speak them. The trust ran through my bloodline, yes — but inheritance of a crime is not authority. Or was it? The scroll had named my line *trustees*, and trustees, in every legal tradition I'd ever mapped, held the power of disposition. You cannot hold a thing in trust without the ability to decide its fate. That was what trust *meant*. My family had held the name in trust for nine hundred years by doing nothing, which was itself a decision — the decision to preserve the status quo. If silence was a choice, then speech could be one too.

Third flaw, and this one I sat with longest: everything I was stood on the crime. If I rewrote the oath, if I gave the name away, if I dissolved the divine claim entirely — then I dissolved my own inheritance. Not just the secret. The *name*. Reyes. The line. The whole architecture of who I was, built on Aldous's theft, would come down the moment the theft was undone. I would be unmade. No lineage, no claim, no place in any record. Legally, magically, genealogically — no one.

I thought about the valley. About the map I'd finished and burned the edges of, the one nobody asked for, the one where my hand had strayed past the known borders into blankness. I had burned those edges because I was afraid — afraid of what lay outside, afraid of wanting it, afraid that admitting the blank space tempted me would crack open the careful life I'd built.

But I had also burned them because the edges weren't true yet. A mapmaker doesn't publish speculation. He publishes what he knows, and he keeps the drafts.

This was the draft.

I sat in the grey dawn light from my narrow window and understood, finally, what I had been my whole life without knowing it: not a keeper of secrets, not an heir to thieves, not a carrier of other people's wars. A draftsman. And a draftsman's whole purpose is that the first version is never the last.

They wanted me to choose between delivering the oath and destroying it. Between Vessan's restoration and the Choir's erasure. Between two borders, both drawn by others, both drawn in blood.

But I had a third option, and it fit in my hand like a stylus.

I would redraw the oath. Not deliver it, not destroy it — *amend* it. Take the instrument of my family's guilt and turn it into the instrument of everyone's release. Give the stolen name back to the people it had been stolen *from* — not the god, not the void, but the mortals who had paid for the gods' war with their homes and their children and their unnamed dead. Sever the divine claim entirely. Unbind every god from every ancient warrant. And let the map of obligation be redrawn by the people who actually lived on the land.

It would cost me everything. I saw that clearly now, the way you see the whole shape of a coastline only when you're far enough out to sea. My name, my line, my place in any record ever kept. The moment the amended oath took hold, Ada Reyes would become a man with no territory at all.

I found, examining myself for terror, that there was remarkably little. There was grief — real grief, for the family I'd imagined I had, the honest mapmakers I'd pictured in place of thieves. But underneath the grief was something steadier. Something that felt, unmistakably, like coming home to a country I'd never visited.

Because here was the truth I'd been circling my whole restless life: I had never belonged to the valley. I had belonged to the *blank spaces*. To the margins. To the parts of the map that waited, honest and unclaimed, for someone to draw them true.

Let them have my name. Let them have the valley, the line, the legacy of Aldous the founder, Aldous the revered, Aldous the thief. I would trade the whole inheritance for a stylus and a clean margin.

I got up off the cold floor, joints cracking, and pressed my palm flat against the stone wall — my drafting table, my vellum, my world. Somewhere in that stone, somewhere in the memory I'd built clause by clause through the sleepless dark, the new oath existed. Complete. Amended. Mine.

All I needed now was paper. Ink. Witnesses. And a conclave full of frightened arbiters willing to hear a prisoner read six sentences that would end the age of gods.

Heavy odds. But I had escaped a valley that refused to be left, crossed a boundary that refused to be crossed, and survived four days of porridge-time. I could manage paperwork.

I was still smiling at my own poor joke when the lock turned, and the young acolyte appeared at my door — and behind her, filling the corridor with a pressure I felt in my teeth, came a presence I knew instantly, from the scroll's descriptions, from Maren's nightmares, from the way every prayer in the world seemed to lean toward it.

Vessan had found the Choir's sanctuary.

And he was asking for me by name.
