## 13. Maren's Choice

The order came at dawn, written in Vessan's own hand, which was the first insult. In eleven years of service I had received perhaps four direct communications from the god himself, and three of those had been reprimands. The fourth had been a birthday blessing that read like a tax assessment.

*Retrieve the scroll. Silence the cartographer. Return by moonrise.*

*Silence the cartographer.* Not "kill." Not "execute." Vessan wrote like a man who kept his hands clean by keeping his vocabulary cleaner. I read the order twice in the grey light of my tent outside Greyford, then a third time, because the third reading is where the lies usually show themselves, and sure enough: there it was, in the way the ink sat slightly heavier on *silence*, as if the pen had hesitated there, or as if the god writing through his scribe had leaned harder on the word than he'd meant to.

Gods don't write their own letters. Everyone knows this. But they press their will through the scribes like thumbs through wet clay, and you can feel the pressure in the strokes if you know how to look. I knew how to look. It was, in fact, my entire job — reading what powerful people almost said.

I burned the order in the brazier and told myself the ash meant nothing.

* * *

Let me tell you about obedience, since I was raised on it the way other children are raised on bread. My mother served the Choir before she defected to Vessan, and she used to say that faith was just loyalty with better lighting. She taught me to read routes, seals, faces, and silences — all the small cartographies of trust. When I was sixteen, Vessan's provosts found me in a border town, orphaned and quick, and asked me one question: *Can you keep a secret?*

I said yes. What I should have said was: *For how long, and at what price, and whose?*

Eleven years later I could recite the answer without thinking. The price was everything, paid slowly, in installments so small I never felt them leave. A friendship here. A doubt there. The habit of asking questions only in the second person, never the first, because *why do they want this?* was safe but *what do I want?* was not.

And now Ada Reyes sat in a cell beneath the conclave's east tower, and I had orders to open his throat, and for the first time in eleven years the question arrived in the first person, uninvited, at three in the morning, wearing no clothes and no manners:

*What do I want?*

* * *

They let me see him because I was still, officially, the courier who had brought him in. Warden Pell didn't even check my authorization. That's the thing about being trusted — it becomes a key nobody thinks to steal back.

"He hasn't eaten," Pell said, unlocking the door. "Not since yesterday. Sits there drawing."

"Drawing?"

"Little maps. On the floor, in the dust, on his sleeve. Man's got ink in his blood instead of sense." Pell shrugged, a man who had guarded heretics and saints and could not reliably tell the difference, and had stopped trying. "You've got until the bell. After that I'm supposed to log your exit, and I'd rather not log anything strange."

The door closed behind me. The cell was a stone box with a straw pallet, a bucket, and a barred window too high to reach, through which morning light fell in a single dusty column. Ada sat cross-legged in the column of light like a cat that had decided the sun belonged to him.

He looked up. He had a bruise along his jaw from the arrest and ink on three fingers and the expression of a man who has been doing arithmetic and does not like the total.

"You," he said.

"Me."

"They sent you to kill me, didn't they?"

I hadn't planned to be honest. Honesty is a bad habit in my trade; it gets people hanged. But something about the flatness of his voice — no fear in it, just bookkeeping — made lying feel like an insult to us both.

"Yes."

"Hm." He looked down at the dust map he'd been drawing. It was the valley, I realized. His valley. He'd drawn it from memory, every contour, and around its edges he'd drawn the realms beyond it — the ones he'd crossed with me, the burnt country, the road of small gods — sketched rougher, less certain, but drawn anyway. "Do you want to?"

"That's not a relevant question."

"It's the only relevant question. Everything else is logistics." He brushed dust off his knee. "Vessan wants the scroll. You have it, or you know where it is. And I'm the loose end. Standard practice. I've read enough history to recognize the shape of it." He paused. "Although — and correct me if I'm wrong — you haven't done it yet. The bell rang twice while I was waiting. You could have come at any bell. You came at this one."

I said nothing.

"So the question stands," he said. "Do you want to?"

The column of light had moved while we talked. Dust turned in it, slow as snow. Somewhere above us, the conclave was waking up, clerks shuffling to their desks, arbiters putting on their chains of office, an entire apparatus of careful neutrality grinding toward its daily output of careful nothing.

"No," I said.

It came out quieter than I intended. Eleven years of service and my first act of rebellion was a whisper in a basement.

Ada nodded, unsurprised, which annoyed me more than gloating would have. "When did you know?"

"When Vessan interrogated me." I sat down across from him, on the cold stone, because my legs had decided the matter before I did. "He asked me what the messenger said when he died. Word for word. Three times, word for word, and each time he watched me the way a moneylender watches a signature. And I realized — he doesn't care about the oath. He cares about whether *I* believe it. Because if I believe it, then it's true, and if it's true, then his claim to the name is real, and if his claim is real—" I stopped.

"Then the war isn't a tragedy," Ada finished. "It's a lawsuit."

"He told me the scroll was heresy. A forgery. That the messenger was a Choir plant." I laughed, and it came out wrong, brittle. "I believed him for six years. I carried his sealed orders into nine provinces believing I was carrying truth."

"And now?"

"Now I've seen the archive under the lake. Now I've read the covenant in the founder's own hand. Now I know the revered First Arbiter — the man whose statue stands in every market square from here to the coast — stole a god's true name out of a dying god's mouth and built a peace on it like building a house on a grave." I looked at my hands. Courier's hands. They had carried a great many things I hadn't opened. "Your family held the name in trust for two hundred years, Ada. They didn't even know what they were holding. Your grandmother died guarding a crime she couldn't name."

"My grandfather," he said quietly. "But yes."

"The point stands."

"It usually does, with you." Almost a smile. Then it faded. "Maren. If you free me, you can't go back. You understand that. Vessan doesn't forgive; he repurposes. He'll spend years finding a use for your betrayal before he spends a minute punishing it. Every favor you've ever banked becomes leverage against you. Every person you've ever trusted becomes a door they'll knock on."

"I know how it works. I've been the door."

"Then why—"

"Because he lied to me." The words came out of me like something that had been swallowed wrong for years and finally came up. "Not about the big things. About the small things. About *why*. He sent me to trace the messenger's route and told me it was routine verification. He sent me to follow you and told me you were a witness, not a target. He let me stand in that archive and watch you find your ancestor's confession and he never once said *this changes things, doesn't it? Let's talk about it.* Because talking is for people whose plans survive contact with the truth." I stood up. My knees cracked. I was thirty-one years old and I felt every one of them. "I chose justice over obedience once already, Ada. Six years ago, when I decided to believe him instead of checking. This is just me correcting the error late."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about treason: it is mostly paperwork.

I couldn't walk him out the front gate. The conclave had doubled its guard after the breach attempt — everyone assumed the Choir would try again, and everyone was half right, though the threat wore Vessan's colors, not ash-grey. So the front gate was out, the postern was watched, and the river gate was locked pending an inspection that would last, according to the notice nailed beside it, three days.

That left the cistern.

Every fortress has a cistern. Every cistern has a maintenance channel, because water must come from somewhere, and the engineers who build fortresses are always so proud of the walls that they forget the plumbing. I had mapped Greyford's plumbing during my first week here, out of professional habit, and had filed the map in the back of my mind under *someday*, next to a dozen other maps of ways out of places I wasn't supposed to need ways out of.

We went up, not down — the cistern channel ran from the reservoir on the hill, and the grate at the reservoir end was rusted through in one corner, and the corner was behind a dead juniper bush, and I had checked it two days ago because checking exits is the only prayer I've ever been consistent about.

Ada climbed out of the grate, filthy, bleeding a little from one palm, blinking in the daylight like a man surfacing from deep water. The reservoir lay silver and still below us, and beyond it the hills rolled toward the burnt country, and beyond that, somewhere, the neutral road south.

"Now what?" he said.

"Now you run."

"*We* run."

I looked at him. He looked back, patient, insufferable, holding out his ink-stained hand.

"Maren. You burned your standing with Vessan the moment you opened that cell door. There is no version of tonight where you're drinking wine in the provosts' hall pretending this didn't happen. By moonrise there will be a description of you on every gate from here to the sea. You're coming with me because the alternative is standing alone in territory full of people who have orders concerning both of us." He tilted his head. "Also, I can't navigate the burnt country alone. You drew the route. I memorized it, but memory is a map with the margins torn off, and you *are* the margins."

It was, I would realize much later, the most romantic thing anyone had ever said to me: *you are the margins.* At the time I just sighed, because sighing was cheaper than admitting he was right.

"Fine," I said. "But we do this my way. We move at dusk, we sleep in drains, and if you die of something stupid I will be extremely annoyed, because I will have committed treason for nothing."

"A fair bargain." He shook my hand, formal as a treaty. Then his face changed — that look I'd learned to dread, the look of a man doing arithmetic again. "Maren. The scroll. Vessan's men took it at the gate."

"I know."

"But you've read it. All of it. Before they seized it."

"All of it," I said. "Twice. Why do you think I'm here?"

He smiled then, and it was not a comforting smile. It was the smile of a cartographer looking at blank space and seeing, instead of emptiness, room to draw.

"Then we're not running from Vessan," he said. "We're running ahead of him. There's a copy of the covenant terms in my head and apparently in yours, and between us we might be able to reconstruct every clause — except the clauses I've been changing. I've spent three days in that cell rewriting the oath, Maren. Not forging it. *Amending* it. And if we get to the conclave floor before the trial convenes—"

Above us, on the ridge, a horn sounded. Once, twice — the signal for a search forming. Down at the reservoir, torches were already moving, spreading out along the shore in a neat, disciplined fan. Vessan's people worked fast. They always had. It was one of the things I'd once admired about them.

Ada grabbed my hand. "Run now, amend later?"

"Run now," I said. "Amend forever."

We went down the hill together, two names being crossed off a list, toward the burnt country and whatever waited past it — and for the first time in eleven years, I noticed, I wasn't carrying anyone's sealed orders at all.

Just my own.
