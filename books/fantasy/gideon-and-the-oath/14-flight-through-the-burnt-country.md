## 14. Flight Through the Burnt Country

*Ada*

The Ashen Choir's sanctuary had lasted four days, which I suppose is longer than most gifts last.

We left before dawn on the fifth, because Maren woke me with a hand over my mouth and a whisper that the Choir's hospitality had an expiration date: their leader had sent riders east in the night, and riders going east from a place with only one road meant one thing — word to Vessan, or word to someone who sold to Vessan. Either way, our borrowed safety had become a leash.

"They were going to let us go," I said, lacing my boots in the dark.

"They were going to let us go as far as the next ambush." She was already shouldering her pack. "There's a difference between a door and a trapdoor, cartographer. Learn it."

So we went west, toward Greyford, through the one country no faction wanted to follow us into: the Burnt Country itself.

I had mapped it once, from hearsay, the way I mapped everything beyond the valley. My version showed a wasteland, a few ruined keeps, a river that ran red in old songs. The truth, when we crossed into it at first light, was worse and stranger than ink could hold. The ground was black for miles, not ash-black but *burned-through*-black, like a page written on so hard the pen went out the other side. And rising off it, thin as morning mist, came voices.

Prayers. Dead prayers, still echoing.

"Keep walking," Maren said, though she'd gone pale too. "They can't hurt you. They're finished."

A woman's voice drifted past us, pleading with some god for rain. A child's voice asked someone named Ellorin to make the fever stop. An old man thanked — actually *thanked* — whatever power had let him see his grandson born. They hung in the air like smoke that had forgotten which way was up.

"They never got answered," I said.

"No. The gods stopped listening here. Or ran out of strength. Depends which faction's history you read." Maren walked fast, eyes forward. "Vessan says the Choir broke the world here. The Choir says Vessan did. Both histories are written by winners, and neither side won."

I thought about the valley behind us, where prayers fell into silence like stones into deep water, and how I'd spent thirty years believing that silence was peace. It wasn't peace. It was a held breath. And here, on the other side of everything, was what the breath had been holding: a battlefield where the dying hadn't even been allowed to finish speaking.

"Ada." Maren caught my sleeve. "You're crying."

"I'm mapping," I said, and wiped my face, and kept walking.

* * *

*Maren*

Here is what nobody tells you about escorting a civilian through hell: they ask questions. Constantly. Good questions, which is worse.

By midday we'd reached the first of the ruined keeps — Corven's Hold, the maps called it, though there was nothing left to hold. Walls like broken teeth. Inside the courtyard, the dead prayers were thicker, layered, dozens of voices all asking the same thing: *let it end, let it end, let it end.*

Ada stood in the middle of them with his sketchbook out.

"We're being followed," I told him. "You can draw later."

"We're always being followed. That's the arrangement." He didn't look up. "But look at the pattern, Maren. The prayers cluster. They're densest along this line—" he traced a seam in the scorched earth, running straight as a surveyor's chain toward the western hills— "and thin everywhere else. That's not random. That's a road."

"It's a battlefield."

"It's a *retreat route.* Somebody marched an army down this line, and everybody along it prayed, and none of the prayers were answered because the gods had already turned away." He finally looked up, and his eyes had that flat bright quality I'd learned to distrust and rely on in equal measure. "Which means somebody was retreating *toward* something. West. The same direction we're going."

"Greyford."

"Greyford existed before the war ended. Neutral ground doesn't get built during a war; it gets built by people planning for the war to end." He closed the book. "Your Vessan wants the name back so he can restart the war on favorable terms. But maybe the war didn't end the way anyone claims. Maybe somebody *ended* it, deliberately, from a place like Greyford, and the oath was the instrument."

I stared at him. Four days ago I'd have called this heresy. Three days ago I'd have called it treason. Today I called it what it was: better intelligence than anything Vessan's priesthood had given me in six years of service.

"You're saying the conclave isn't just arbiters. You're saying they're the heirs of whoever wrote the original covenant."

"I'm saying it's a hypothesis. I'm a cartographer, not a prophet. Prophets don't check their work." He started walking again, along the prayer-road, west. "Come on. If I'm right, the fastest path to Greyford is also the path least likely to be watched. Nobody patrols a graveyard."

He was right about that. We made eight miles before dusk without seeing a single rider, and I hated how much I was starting to trust him, because trust is just another kind of debt, and I already owed enough of those.

* * *

*Ada*

We camped that night inside the shell of a wayshrine — one of the small gods' houses, its roof caved in, its little altar split down the middle. Maren built a fire small enough to hide in a cupped hand. I spread my sketches across a fallen lintel and tried to make the Burnt Country honest on paper.

"The voices are louder near you," Maren said suddenly.

I looked up. She was watching me with the expression she wore when she'd caught a lie in a supply ledger.

"That's nonsense."

"Is it? In the valley, magic couldn't touch you. Here—" she nodded at the air around my shoulders, where the dead prayers did seem to drift closer, curling like moths— "here you're soaked in it. Cold water, you said. Well, water finds cracks."

"I don't have cracks."

"Everyone has cracks. Yours are just very well surveyed." She fed a twig to the fire. "The scroll. When you read it, did it say why your family? Why a cartographer's line?"

I had been avoiding that question the way you avoid a loose tooth. I made myself answer it now, because she'd earned it, and because saying things aloud is how I test whether they're true.

"The oath names the trust-keepers as 'the line of the Measurer.' Not a noble house. Not priests. *Measurers.* People who measure things." I smoothed the edge of my sketch. "My family has kept records for nine generations. Field notes, boundary stones, genealogies. I always thought we were just... compulsive."

"And the stolen name was hidden where trust-keepers keep things."

"In a record. In a map, maybe. Somewhere in nine generations of my family's papers, sitting in plain sight, filed under something boring." I laughed, and it came out wrong. "It might be in my workshop back in the valley. It might have been under my nose my whole life."

Maren was quiet a while. Then: "When we get to Greyford, the conclave will want the scroll. They'll offer you protection for it. Peace, continuity, all the good words."

"You think I shouldn't give it to them."

"I think everyone who's wanted that scroll has wanted it for themselves. Vessan. The Choir. Possibly the conclave. I've worked for powerful people long enough to know that 'the fragile peace' is usually code for 'my position in it.'" She looked at me across the little fire. "What do *you* want, Ada Reyes?"

Nobody had asked me that. I'd spent the whole journey being the vessel, the heir, the carrier. I opened my mouth to say something dutiful and instead heard myself say:

"I want to redraw it."

She frowned. "Redraw what?"

"The oath. It's a document. Documents have terms. Terms were *chosen* by someone — chosen badly, selfishly, by my ancestor, whoever he was. But choices can be unmade and remade. I'm not a priest. I'm not a soldier. I'm a man who corrects bad maps for a living." The firelight moved on the ruin around us, and the dead prayers seemed to lean in. "Deliver it to Vessan and he starts a war. Give it to the Choir and they erase a name that belongs to someone. Bury it with the conclave and the theft stands forever. There's a fourth option. Nobody's tried it because nobody who held the scroll could *draw*."

Maren stared at me for a long moment. Then she did something I'd never seen her do: she smiled without checking who was watching.

"When we reach Greyford," she said, "remind me to introduce you properly. Not as my prisoner. Not as my cargo." She reached across the fire and took the corner of my sketchbook, turning it toward herself, studying my lines of the prayer-road with a courier's eye. "As my colleague."

Above the ruined shrine, the dead prayers rose like sparks and thinned into the dark, still unanswered, still waiting — and for the first time since the valley, I felt less like a man fleeing something and more like a man carrying something toward its destination.

* * *

*Maren*

On the third day in the Burnt Country, the prayers changed.

Until then they'd been old — voices worn smooth, like river stones, griefs so ancient they'd lost their edges. But near noon, crossing a ridge of fused glass where lightning had struck the same ground a thousand times, Ada stopped dead and held up a hand.

"Do you hear that one?"

I listened. Under the murmur of the ancients, a new voice. Sharper. Recent.

*"...if anyone can hear me, they're coming, they're on the road behind us, please, please—"*

"That's not a dead prayer," I said. "That's a live one."

Ada was already climbing the ridge. I cursed him with several oaths Vessan would have disapproved of and went after him. From the crest we looked down into a shallow valley of black earth, and saw them: three figures on the western road, moving fast, and behind them — perhaps two miles, kicking up dust — riders. Six, maybe seven. Vessan's colors, the grey-and-gold, though at this distance I couldn't swear to it and wouldn't have sworn to anything with those colors on it anymore.

"Refugees?" Ada said.

"Or bait." But even as I said it, I knew. Bait gets placed where you'll find it, not fleeing *away* from your pursuers. Those three were genuinely running, and the riders were genuinely hunting, and every instinct I'd built in six years of service screamed that this was exactly the kind of scene Vessan would stage to flush us out — and every instinct I'd grown in the last two weeks, the ones I trusted more, said it was real.

"Ada. We can't afford detours."

"We can't afford to watch either." He was already counting distances with a surveyor's eye. "The road bends north past that broken aqueduct. If we cut the angle, we reach the bend before they do. There's cover in the ruins."

"If we're seen, both factions know exactly where we are."

"If we're *not* seen, three people live, and nobody knows where we are, and we're still ahead of schedule." He looked at me. "You told me the difference between a door and a trapdoor, Maren. Teach me the difference between a risk and a calculation."

I wanted to hit him. I wanted to kiss him. I settled for grabbing my pack. "Stay low. Follow my hand signals. And if I signal *run*, you run and you don't argue, because unlike you I've done this professionally."

We cut the angle through the glass-fields, bent double, silent. The live prayer grew louder as we neared the road — a young woman's voice, cracked with running, praying to no god in particular because no god in particular was answering. Ada flinched at every repetition. I kept us moving.

We reached the aqueduct ruins as the fugitives did. They were a mother and two children, I saw now, the woman half-carrying the smaller one. The riders were closing. And I understood, with cold clarity, what this was: not bait, not coincidence. This was what Vessan's occupation looked like from the ground. This was Tuesday, in the country he claimed to be saving.

The lead rider raised a crossbow.

Ada stepped out of the ruins onto the open road before I could grab him.

"HEY!" he shouted, arms wide, a skinny cartographer silhouetted against the burnt sky. "Over here! Your warrant's for *me!*"

Every rider turned. Every crossbow swung. And I did the only professional thing left: I used the distraction to put an arrow through the lead rider's saddle-girth, dropping him into a plunging, screaming heap that scattered the rest, and then I was dragging Ada off the road by the collar while the mother and children bolted north into the ruins and freedom.

We ran. Behind us, horns sounded — pursuit horns, but confused, divided, some chasing ghosts north and some circling the fallen rider. Ahead of us, the prayer-road stretched west toward the hills, and somewhere past the hills, Greyford.

That night, miles on, gasping in the lee of a fallen boundary stone, Ada looked at me with soot on his face and said, "That was the stupidest brave thing I've ever done."

"It's in the top five I've witnessed, and I worked for a god." I checked the road behind us. Clear, for now. "Why did you do it?"

He was quiet a moment. "Those prayers in the keeps. All those people asking *let it end*. Nobody answered them. I keep thinking — I can't answer them either. But I can refuse to walk past one more voice asking for help." He shrugged, embarrassed. "It's not strategy. It's just... drafting. First line of the new oath, maybe."

And there it was, laid out plain between us on the black earth: what we were becoming. Not fugitive and handler. Not heretic and traitor. Two people drawing the same line, westward, toward a conclave that might save us or sell us — carrying between us a stolen name, a rewritten oath, and the beginning of something neither of our old masters would have recognized.

"Greyford's gates close at dusk tomorrow," I said. "If the clerk at the gate asks who we are—"

"We tell the truth," Ada said. "It's the one weapon nobody's trained for."

I laughed — actually laughed, out loud, in the Burnt Country with horns still faint on the wind — and I understood that I was lost, entirely and gladly, to whatever this was.

Then the boundary stone beside us flickered with light, and a voice like grinding stone rolled out of the empty air, and I knew Vessan had found a faster road than ours.

"*Cartographer,*" it said. "*We should talk before the gates.*"
