## 15. The Conclave of Greyford

The arbiters of Greyford received us in a hall built to make visitors feel small, which was a strange ambition for mortals who had spent three centuries insisting that no power greater than themselves existed.

It worked anyway. The Hall of Weighing was a long stone throat of a room, its ceiling lost in shadow above banks of candles that never guttered — I checked; I'm a cartographer, I notice things like candle behavior — and at the far end sat the Nine behind a crescent table of black oak polished so thoroughly it reflected our faces back at us, upside down and slightly distorted, like we were the ones being examined from beneath water.

Maren walked beside me with her hands loose at her sides and her eyes doing arithmetic. She'd been counting exits since the gate. I'd stopped counting them myself somewhere outside Vell's Crossing, on the theory that if I knew where all the doors were, I'd spend the whole conversation looking at them instead of at the people.

"State your names and your grievance," said the center arbiter. Her name, according to the clerk who'd admitted us, was Magistrate Ondrel, and she had the voice of a woman who had spent forty years telling rivers where to flood and being obeyed.

"Ada Reyes," I said. "Cartographer. And my grievance is roughly nine hundred years old."

A murmur went through the benches behind us, where perhaps two hundred Greyford citizens had come to watch, because justice is the best theater in any town that doesn't have a proper stage.

"The scroll," Ondrel said. "You claim it authentic."

"I claim it's old," I corrected. "Authenticity is what you're here to decide. That's rather the point of a conclave."

Someone in the benches laughed, quickly smothered. Ondrel did not smile, but something at the corner of her mouth acknowledged the hit.

"Present it."

I unrolled the oath-scroll on the black oak, weighting its corners with four smooth river stones the clerks provided, and for a moment the whole hall went quiet in the particular way rooms go quiet when they're trying not to be afraid. Two hundred mortals staring at a strip of vellum and a broken seal, as though it might sit up and bite.

I let them look. Then I read it aloud.

I won't reproduce the whole recitation here — my voice gave out twice and Maren finished one passage for me in a flat courier's cadence that somehow made it worse, the way bad news delivered evenly always does — but the substance is simple enough to say plainly. The valley where magic cannot follow, the valley I had spent my life mapping, the peace every realm around it took as bedrock: it was purchased. Not won, not granted, not natural. *Purchased.* With a god's true name, taken from him while he slept after the last battle, sealed into an oath by nine mortal signatories, and held in trust ever since by a single bloodline.

Mine.

When I finished, the silence had a texture to it, like felt. I could hear the candles burning. I am not being poetic; I could actually hear them, little pops of the wick, because no one else in the hall dared make a sound.

Magistrate Ondrel spoke first, which was her job and, I suspect, her nature.

"You are the heir of record?"

"Apparently."

"You did not know."

"I mapped valleys," I said. "Not my own family. It turns out those were different disciplines."

Another smothered laugh. Maren's shoulder pressed against mine for half a second — a courier's acknowledgment, nothing more, though I noticed my pulse didn't treat it as nothing.

Ondrel turned to her colleagues. There were nine of them, and I watched them do a thing I recognized from village councils and guild disputes: they looked at each other the way people look when everyone already knows the answer and no one wants to be the one who says it first.

Finally the arbiter at the far left — an old man with ink-stained fingers, a fellow professional — leaned forward. "Master Reyes. What do you want?"

And there it was. The question I'd been carrying across five hundred miles of burnt country like a stone in my boot, and now it was set on the table in front of two hundred strangers, and I found I still didn't have a clean answer.

"I want the name returned to the mortals it was stolen from," I said. "All of them. Every farmer who prayed into silence for three centuries because their gods were busy fighting over property—"

"That is not a remedy this conclave can grant," Ondrel cut in.

"It's the only remedy the scroll describes."

"The scroll describes a crime." She spread her hands, and I watched forty years of careful governance arrange themselves into patience. "Master Reyes. Sit, if you would. Hear us."

I sat. Maren stayed standing, which told me everything about how she expected this to go.

* * *

Here is what the Nine proposed, laid out over the better part of an hour with the smooth inevitability of a legal argument that has been rehearsed since before either faction knew the scroll survived:

The oath would be surrendered to the conclave. Not destroyed — the Ashen Choir would never permit mere destruction, and Vessan's people would smell erasure from a hundred leagues — but *buried*. Sealed in the deep vaults beneath Greyford, alongside the other documents too dangerous to read and too important to burn. The valley's boundary would stand. The truce would hold. Neither god would learn the scroll's contents, and the balance that had kept the realms from tearing each other apart for nine hundred years would continue, undisturbed, forever.

"In perpetuity," said the ink-stained arbiter, with evident satisfaction, as though perpetuity were a thing you could file.

"And the stolen name?" I asked.

"Remains where it is."

"Which is where?"

He hesitated. "The oath binds it into the valley itself. Into the land. That is why the valley refuses magic, Master Reyes. The name is the lock."

I thought about that. I thought about every map I had ever drawn of home, every contour line and every blank margin, and understood that I had been drafting, in obsessive detail, the shape of a prison cell. The valley wasn't unmappable because it defied magic. It was unmappable because it was made *of* a name — a single word, buried under the soil, holding the door shut on a war.

"And the mortals who suffered the war," I said slowly. "The ones whose prayers went unanswered. The ones who died on both sides while their gods fought. They get nothing."

"They get peace," Ondrel said.

"They get the *absence* of war. Those aren't the same thing. Ask anyone who's lived with both."

"Peace is the only thing this conclave can give," she said, and here her voice changed, dropped its magistrate's polish, and became briefly, terribly human. "Master Reyes, I was alive during the Sack of Corren. I was six. I remember the sky over the eastern hills glowing for eleven days. You are asking me to risk that again over a question of *ownership*."

"I'm asking you to risk it over a question of *justice*."

"From where I sit, they have looked identical for nine hundred years."

I couldn't answer that immediately, and I resented her for making me try. Because she wasn't wrong, exactly. That was the trap of it. Every argument I had — and I had good ones, I'd been sharpening them since the Ashen Choir's cells — rested on the assumption that the truth was worth more than the quiet. And I believed that. I believed it the way you believe in your own heartbeat, without evidence and without alternative.

But I also believed in maps. And a map, I have learned, is a promise you make to someone who will travel after you. Burying the oath wasn't preserving the peace. It was handing the next generation a map with a lie drawn on it and telling them to navigate by it.

"Magistrate," I said. "May I ask the Nine a question?"

"You may ask. We may not answer."

"What happens," I said, "when someone else finds it?"

The ink-stained arbiter frowned. "The vaults are secure."

"The vaults are secure against thieves. I'm not talking about thieves. I'm a cartographer. My entire profession exists because secrets about terrain do not stay secret. Someone redraws a boundary, someone's grandson inherits a key, some clerk in three hundred years gets curious. Or—" I gestured vaguely upward, at the ceiling, at the sky beyond it, at everything. "Or a god gets patient. Vessan has waited nine hundred years. Do you imagine he stops at nine hundred and one? You're not burying this scroll. You're scheduling it."

The hall had gone very quiet again. In the benches, I saw faces turning toward each other, and I recognized the expression — I'd seen it in my own mirror for weeks. The look of people doing arithmetic they didn't want to finish.

Ondrel looked at me for a long moment. "You have thought about this more than we have."

"I've had less to lose for longer," I said. "That changes recently."

* * *

They deliberated for two days.

Maren and I were given quarters in the conclave guesthouse — comfortable, well-guarded, and unmistakably a cell with better linens. On the second evening she sat across from me at the little table, cleaning her boots with a concentration the boots had done nothing to earn, and finally said:

"They're going to offer it again tomorrow. The burial. And they're going to add something, because Ondrel is smart. Watch for it."

"Add what?"

"Something for you. A pardon. A stipend. A position — conclave cartographer, maybe. Legitimacy." She set the boot down. "They'll buy you, Ada. And the terrible thing is, it'll be a fair price."

I looked at her. "Is that what you'd do? Take the fair price?"

She was quiet for a while. Outside, Greyford's evening bells rang the hour, and somewhere below a cart rattled over cobblestones, ordinary sounds from an ordinary town that had no idea its magistrates were deciding whether to keep lying to it.

"When I took service with Vessan's faction," she said, "they told me the scroll was heresy. A forgery cooked up by the Choir to destabilize the truce. I arrested two people over that forgery. One of them was a boy of nineteen." She turned the boot over in her hands. "So no. I don't take fair prices anymore. I check what they're made of."

* * *

On the third morning, the Nine received us again, and Ondrel did exactly what Maren predicted, right down to the position. Conclave Cartographer Royal, a title that hadn't existed in eighty years, revived for me specifically, with a salary, a suite, and access to the greatest map archive in the mortal realms. Every unmapped coast. Every rumored range. Everything I had ever ached to chart, handed to me on the condition that I help them put one document in a hole and pretend it had never been written.

I stood there in the Hall of Weighing, in front of two hundred citizens and nine magistrates and the upside-down reflections of my own face, and I will be honest with you, whoever finds this account: I wanted it. Not the title — the *archive*. The part of me that had burned the edges off my own maps for twenty years, the part that had pressed its face against the valley's boundary like a child against a window, that part sang at the offer like a struck bell.

And underneath the singing, quieter, my mother's voice, or maybe just my memory of it, saying the thing she said whenever I brought her a drawing: *What did you leave out, love?*

"Magistrate Ondrel," I said. "Before I answer. One question."

She inclined her head.

"If I accept — if we bury the oath and seal the vault and go on as before — whose name holds the valley shut?"

Silence.

"Because that's what the scroll says, isn't it? The trust passes through my line. If I surrender the scroll, the trust doesn't dissolve. It transfers. To the conclave. Which means the conclave becomes the thief's heir. You wouldn't be ending the crime. You'd be *inheriting* it. Signing for it. Taking delivery of a stolen name and calling yourselves custodians." I looked down the crescent of faces. "Nine hundred years ago, nine mortals signed that oath. You're nine mortals. The symmetry can't have escaped you."

"We would be custodians of *peace*," said the ink-stained arbiter.

"You'd be custodians of a *lock*. And locks don't care what they're keeping in. Only what they're keeping out. And someday — I promise you, sir, I've read enough old charts to know how these things go — someday someone comes with a better key. And then the question won't be whether the theft happened. It'll be whether you knew. Whether all of you knew, and chose the quiet anyway." I paused. "History doesn't record the people who kept secrets well. It records what the secrets cost when they got out."

Ondrel studied me for a long time. When she spoke, her voice was quiet enough that the hall leaned in to hear it.

"You understand what you refuse. Both factions know the scroll survives. Vessan's people tracked you across half the world. The Choir let you live, which frightens me more. Within a season, Master Reyes, possibly within a month, one of them will come to Greyford, and they will not debate."

"I know."

"And you refuse the only shelter we can offer."

"I'm not refusing shelter. I'm refusing to be the wall you hide behind." I rolled up the scroll, slowly, and the sound of it was very loud. "You asked what I want. Here it is, plainly. Convene a trial. A real one. Both factions, both claims, heard openly, before mortal arbiters, under mortal law. Put the oath on the table and let everyone read it. Because I've met both sides of this war, Magistrate, and I'll tell you what neither of them will: they are both terrified. Vessan is terrified of losing his name forever. The Choir is terrified of anyone holding it. Fear makes people honest, eventually. Let's hear them out while they still remember how."

"And if the trial fails?" Ondrel asked. "If the gods will not submit to mortal law?"

"Then at least the failure will be *on the record*," I said. "At least the next person who finds this won't start from nothing. That's all a map is, in the end. A letter to someone coming after you, saying: *here is what I learned, here is where I failed, don't fall in the same river.*"

Nobody laughed this time.

The Nine withdrew. The deliberation, we were told, would be brief.

* * *

It was brief. It was also not what any of us expected.

Ondrel returned alone, descended the steps from the council chamber, and stood before the assembly with a document in her hands, and I watched her read the opening lines and stop, and read them again, and I saw her knuckles whiten around the paper in a way I have seen exactly once before — on the face of a dying messenger in my workshop, holding a scroll he'd carried too far.

"By vote of six to three," she read, and her voice was steady, magistrates' voices are trained for this, but her eyes came up and found mine across the hall, "the Conclave of Greyford declines to convene a trial. The Conclave further declines to accept custody of the oath-scroll. The Conclave's finding is as follows: *this matter exceeds mortal jurisdiction, and the Conclave will not be party to its resolution by any means.*"

A roar went up from the benches — confusion, outrage, relief, all tangled together. Maren grabbed my arm. "What does that mean? They can't just—"

"It means they're abstaining," I said. My mouth had gone dry. "It means they're stepping off the map entirely."

Ondrel raised a hand for quiet, and got it, and read the final clause, the one that had whitened her knuckles:

"*Custody of the matter reverts to the heir of record. Ada Reyes, son of the Reyes line, is recognized by this Conclave as sole and lawful holder of the trust established by the Covenant of Nine. The Conclave grants him safe passage from Greyford and nothing else. May whatever gods remain have mercy on his judgment.*"

Two hundred people turned to look at me.

I stood in the Hall of Weighing, holding a scroll that had just become, by formal decree of the most powerful mortal institution in existence, *my problem*, and I thought: *you cowards. You magnificent, honest cowards.* Because it was cowardice, yes — six to three, refusing to touch it. But it was also, in its terrible way, the first honest thing any institution had done since the messenger died in my workshop. They wouldn't bury it. They wouldn't buy it. They wouldn't pretend. They simply handed the weight back to the one person who had never been able to put it down.

Maren leaned close. "Ada. Listen to me. The moment you walk out that gate, you're not a petitioner anymore. You're a target with a title. Both factions will move within days — Vessan's people are already in the city, I've seen the signs, and the Choir—"

The great doors of the hall boomed open.

Every head turned. In the doorway, backlit by grey morning light, stood a dozen figures in ash-grey robes, faces veiled, and at their center a tall woman whose veil was folded back, whose eyes were the pale grey of cold cinders, and whom I had hoped never to see again.

The leader of the Ashen Choir smiled at the assembled magistrates, and it was not a comforting sight.

"Forgive the interruption," she said. "We're told there's an heir in the building."
