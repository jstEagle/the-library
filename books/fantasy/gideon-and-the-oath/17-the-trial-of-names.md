## 17. The Trial of Names

I have been worshipped by emperors who wept when I entered a room. I have had my name carved into mountains that are now gravel. So forgive me if I found the Greyford conclave — forty mortals in grey wool, seated on benches in a drafty hall that smelled of tallow and damp stone — a modest venue for the recovery of everything I am.

They had cleared the great chamber for us. That was their word for it: *cleared*, as though a broom could sweep away what was coming. Benches on the north side for the Choir, robed in ash-grey, faces veiled so that only their mouths showed, which I have always thought an honest arrangement. Benches on the south for mine. And between us, at the far end, a long table behind which sat the Arbiters of Greyford: nine mortals elected by no one in particular, empowered by nothing except the fact that both factions had agreed, three centuries ago, to pretend they mattered.

Mortals love their pretending. It is the closest thing they have to divinity.

I arrived as rain. Not out of drama — well, not *only* out of drama — but because my true form does not fit in rooms built for grain disputes, and the rain at least let me be everywhere at once, which is how I prefer to conduct business. I pooled along the rafters. I ran down the walls. I stood in every puddle in the hall at once, and I watched.

The mortal crowd filled the gallery above. Word travels fast when gods come to town; half of Greyford had crammed itself into the balconies, and the other half was outside arguing about whether to flee. They should have fled. But mortals are like that. They will stand in the doorway of a burning house to get a better look.

And there, at the center of the floor, alone at a small table with a cup of water he had not touched, sat the cartographer.

Ada Reyes. He looked exactly as wrong as he had the day his messenger died — no, not *his* messenger. Mine. Kellin had carried my seal for eleven years, and then he had run the wrong way with it, or the right way, depending on whose account you believe, and died in a valley that should never have been able to hold him. The cartographer had inherited the scroll the way one inherits a debt: involuntarily, and without reading the terms.

He looked smaller than I remembered. Thin hands. Ink-stained fingers, always ink-stained, even now, even here, as though he might need to draw his way out of the room. He kept glancing at the woman beside him — Maren, my Maren, my courier, my traitor — and she kept looking straight ahead with the rigid posture of someone who has already decided to burn her life down and is waiting for the weather to cooperate.

Good. Let them sit together. It would make the ending tidier.

* * *

The senior Arbiter was a woman named Odile Vasse, and I will grant her this much: her voice did not shake. "This conclave is convened under the Accords of Exhaustion," she said, "to hear competing claims upon—" she consulted the paper before her, though she did not need to; she had memorized it, I could tell from the way her eyes moved without reading— "upon a certain name, held in trust, provenance disputed."

*Provenance disputed.* How lovely. A god's own name, the word that made me, the syllables that predate the mountains, filed under *disputed* like a fence line.

"The claimant for the southern faction," Odile continued, "is Vessan, called the Rainfather, called the—"

"Called many things," I said, from every puddle at once, and enjoyed the way four hundred mortals flinched in unison. "None of them accurate. That is rather the point."

"Claimant," Odile said, with the patience of a woman who had raised six children and buried two, "you will speak when recognized."

I could have unmade her bench. I want that recorded somewhere, in whatever ledger keeps track of gods' restraint: I *could* have. Instead I gathered myself into the center of the hall, condensed, and took the shape I wear for negotiations — tall, grey-robed, face like weather about to happen. The gallery gasped. Somewhere a child began to cry and was hastily hushed.

"I recognize myself," I said. "Proceed."

* * *

Here is what I said, and I said it plainly, because the truth needs no ornament. Only lies do.

"Three hundred years ago, a war ended. You call it the old war. Your grandparents' grandparents called it the Sorrowing, and their grandparents called it the Time of Falling Sky, and before that it had no name because naming things was how we killed each other." I let that settle. "It did not end in victory. It ended in exhaustion. Every god who fought it bled power into the ground until the ground itself refused to hold any more. And in that last terrible year, a covenant was struck — an oath, sealed and witnessed — by which the war's central prize would be held in trust by a mortal line, hidden where no god could reach it, until such time as the rightful owner came to reclaim it."

"That is one telling," said the mouth of the Choir's speaker. Her voice was dry as ash stirred by wind. All the Choir spoke through one mouth at a time; it saved argument.

"It is the *telling*," I said. "It is written. Your faction has read the same scroll I have read."

"We have read a forgery."

"A forgery that matches, word for word, the memory I carry in my own substance?" I turned to the arbiters. "Arbiters. When your grandfather's deed to this hall was lost in a fire, and a copy was found, did you tear up the copy because the original burned? Or did you accept that a thing can be recorded twice?"

Odile's jaw tightened. She knew a trap when she heard one, but she also knew the law, and the law was the only weapon mortals had ever forged that could nick a god. "The Choir will state its position," she said.

The Choir's speaker rose. Under the veil, her mouth barely moved. "There is no rightful owner. There is no trust. There is a wound, and the name is the knife still in it. The old war was fought over names — over who may speak them, who may bind them, who may *be* them. Mortals died in numbers you have forgotten, Rainfather, because you have chosen to forget. The only peace worth having is the peace in which no one holds the knife. We say: annihilate it. Unmake the name. Let it pass out of the world entirely, and let every god learn to live without the weapon it was."

"You would unmake *me*," I said.

"We would unmake a *word*," she answered. "You were a god before you were named. Become one again. Or don't. Either way, the war ends forever instead of pausing for another century."

And here is the thing I will admit, since I am admitting things tonight: for one moment — one — the argument was beautiful. There is a cleanliness to annihilation that restoration lacks. I felt the pull of it the way a drowning man feels the pull of the deep: not desire, exactly. Recognition.

Then I remembered what I was.

"If the name dies," I said quietly, "everything bound to it dies with it. And the oath binds more than me. Read the fourth clause sometime. Ask the cartographer. He's read it more carefully than either of us."

Every head in the hall turned toward Ada Reyes.

He did not flinch. I will give him that too. He sat with his ink-stained hands flat on the table, and he looked at the arbiters with the expression of a man who has spent his whole life measuring distances and has just been asked to become one.

* * *

Odile Vasse laid out the procedural bones, and I listened, because procedure was the cage they had built and I needed to know its dimensions precisely.

"The scroll recovered from the breach at the gate," she said, "has been authenticated against the Choir's copy and against testimony given under sanction. Its contents are accepted into record." A pause. "The question before this conclave is disposition. Restoration to the claimant Vessan, per the covenant's stated terms. Annihilation, per the Choir's demand. Or—" and here her voice went careful, the way voices go when they approach a cliff edge— "a third disposition, if one exists, within the authority of the trust."

"There is no third disposition," I said.

"That," said Odile, "will be determined by the holder of the trust."

Silence. The particular silence of four hundred people trying not to breathe.

"The covenant names a mortal line as trustee," Odile continued. "That line has been traced. Through the drowned archive, through the original covenant recovered from it, through parish records and a family bible with a very interesting flyleaf." She looked down the length of the hall. "Ada Reyes. Step forward."

He stepped forward. Of course he did. That was the whole tragedy of the man, the thing I had counted on from the beginning: he was incapable of leaving a blank space unfilled. Show a cartographer a gap in the map and he will walk into it.

He walked to the center of the floor, and the gallery leaned over him like a tide deciding whether to come in. Beside him, Maren half-rose — my courier, sworn to me in blood and wages, and I marked her face in that moment, the way it was set, the way her hand hovered near nothing at all. Traitors always hover. It is the hovering that gives them away, long before the knife.

"Ada Reyes," Odile said. "By the covenant's terms, the trust passes through your line. The scroll came to you willingly, from the hand of its carrier. Do you acknowledge the trust?"

I watched him take a breath. I watched him understand, in real time, the shape of the trap: that whichever answer he gave would hand the world to someone. Say yes, and he became the legal instrument of restoration or annihilation, a mortal holding a god's throat. Say no, and the trust defaulted — to whom? To the arbiters? To the strongest faction standing when the ink dried?

"Yes," he said.

One word. It landed in the hall like a dropped coin in a silent church.

"And the trust requires," Odile said, "a disposition. The covenant empowers the trustee to restore the name to its rightful owner. The Choir contends there is no rightful owner. The claimant contends the rightful owner stands before you in grey robes." She folded her hands. "So the conclave puts the question, as the Accords require us to put it, plainly, and without shelter: Ada Reyes, trustee of the stolen name — what is your disposition?"

* * *

Let me describe, for the record, what it is like to be a god waiting on a mortal's answer.

It is like drowning slowly in air. It is like being a map with one country left blank, and all your rivers running toward it. I had planned for this trial down to the angle of the light. I had bought clerks and bribed gatekeepers — the clerk who sold them out at the gate had cost me less than a good horse — and I had arranged for the scroll to be seized, because a seized scroll can be studied, and studied, and its weaknesses found. I had arranged everything except the possibility that the least powerful person in the room would be handed the only vote that mattered.

That was my error, and I have had three centuries to make errors, so I collect them carefully: I thought of Ada Reyes as a container. A vessel the scroll happened to be sitting in. I forgot that vessels can choose what they pour.

He looked at me. Across the whole length of that cold hall, past the arbiters and the veiled mouths and the gallery full of mortals who had come to watch the sky argue with itself, the little cartographer looked directly at me — at the thing the rain was pretending to be — and something passed over his face that I have seen only twice before in my long existence, and both times it preceded catastrophe.

It was pity.

"Before I answer," Ada Reyes said, and his voice was steady, steady as a drawn border, "I'd ask the conclave to hear one thing. The covenant's first line. The line everyone quotes." He reached into his coat, and half the hall tensed, and Maren's hand finally closed around something — I saw it, I marked it, I let it be, because the moment required it — but what he drew out was only a strip of paper, worn soft at the edges. A fragment. The scrap the messenger had torn free, or that he himself had cut away; the record does not agree, and neither, anymore, do I.

"'The name was taken,'" he read. His voice did not waver on *taken*. "Everyone hears those words and assumes they know the rest of the sentence. Who took it. From whom. Why." He folded the scrap closed. "I've read the whole oath. All of it, including the clauses no faction wants read aloud. And I'll give my disposition — the trust demands it, and I won't hide behind procedure the way the rest of this war has hidden behind everything else."

"Reyes," I said, and my voice came out of the walls, out of the floor, out of the rain beginning again against the high windows, "consider carefully what you are. Whatever you think you hold, you hold it *in trust*. Trusts return what belongs to others. That is their entire purpose. You were never meant to keep it. You were meant to *carry* it."

"I know," he said. "I'm a cartographer. Carrying things is most of the job." He glanced back once — at Maren, at the arbiters, at the gallery where a child watched from between an adult's knees, the same way children had watched from doorways during the Sorrowing, I would have bet anything on it — and then he faced forward.

"But a map isn't the territory," Ada Reyes said. "And an oath isn't the truth. And before I dispose of a name that four hundred million dead paid for, this conclave is going to hear what the covenant actually says — every clause, including the ones your factions burned, buried, and drowned to keep quiet. Starting with the name of the one who took it."

The hall went absolutely still.

Because that was the clause. That was the clause I had bribed archivists to lose and generals to forget and three hundred years to bury: the covenant did not merely record the theft. It recorded the thief. And the thief's name was written in the founder's own hand, witnessed and sealed, preserved beneath a lake while empires rose and rotted on top of it.

"He doesn't know what he's saying," I said, and for the first time in the trial, my voice cracked like ice on a spring river. "Arbiters, this is a mortal playing at history with a scrap of—"

"Odile Vasse," said the senior Arbiter, cutting across a god, which took either courage or a death wish, and I suspected, watching her knuckles whiten on the table, that she could no longer tell the difference herself, "the conclave will hear the covenant read in full."

Ada Reyes unfolded the scrap of paper. And beside him, quiet as a closing door, Maren finally drew her hand out of her coat — empty, empty, I noted it, I *noted* it — and stepped forward to stand at his shoulder, facing me, her employer, her god, with the expression of a courier delivering news she knows will end her.

"Read it," Maren said.

And the cartographer, heir to the theft, keeper of the trust, least powerful soul in that entire hall, lifted the covenant's first page, and began.
