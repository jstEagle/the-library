## 20. Signing

The sky over Greyford had torn open like wet paper, and through the tear came a light that did not behave like light. It fell sideways. It pooled in gutters. It made shadows of things that had no business casting them — including me.

I was crouched behind the covenant stone with a stylus in one hand and Maren's blood on my sleeve, and I remember thinking, with the peculiar clarity that terror grants to cartographers: *the scale is wrong.* All my life I had drawn maps where gods were marked as features — a shrine here, a haunted ford there, small annotations in a careful hand. Nobody tells you that when a god actually arrives, he does not fit on any map. He *is* the scale.

Vessan came down out of the torn sky like a verdict.

He was not the golden figure from the chapel friezes. Those artists had flattered him, or perhaps they had simply never looked closely, because looking closely at Vessan was like looking closely at the sun — an act you survived exactly once. What I saw was a shape of storm-light and old grievance wearing the suggestion of a man, and beneath it, faintly, something like a wound. The stolen name had left a hole in him the way a pulled tooth leaves a hole in a jaw. He had spent three thousand years talking around it.

"Cartographer," he said, and his voice did not so much travel through the air as *replace* it. "You are writing on my stone."

"It's not your stone." My voice cracked on the second word, which was not the delivery I'd rehearsed. "That's rather the entire point of the document."

Behind me, the Choir's counter-song rose — a sound like a thousand voices exhaling ash — and above us the two factions met in earnest. I will not describe the battle properly because I cannot. Mortal wars have geometry. This had none. Prayers detonated like artillery. A river changed its mind about which way it flowed. Somewhere to the east, a hill that had stood since before the valley existed simply sat down.

Maren grabbed my shoulder. Her hand was shaking and her voice was not, which told me everything about what the last hour had cost her.

"Ada. Write."

* * *

Here is what nobody warns you about signing away a god's inheritance: the ink fights back.

The covenant stone was older than Greyford, older than the conclave, older than the lie it recorded. Its surface was dense with the original oath in script so old it predated the alphabet I'd been taught, and between those ancient lines ran grooves — channels worn by centuries of oaths being sworn and unsworn, promises poured in like molten metal and cooled into law. To amend it, I had to write into those grooves. And the grooves remembered who they belonged to.

My first attempt slid off the stone entirely. The stylus skated sideways and scored a long useless line across the granite, and Vessan laughed — a sound like a glacier deciding something.

"You cannot rewrite an oath with a mortal hand," he said, striding closer across a battlefield that rearranged itself to suit his stride. "The covenant was cut by gods. It will only be cut again by gods."

"He's lying," Maren said. "He's terrified. Look at his hands."

I looked. The storm-shape of them flickered. Not with power — with *uncertainty*, the tremor of a thing whose whole architecture depended on a name it did not possess, watching a mortal walk toward the deed to that absence with a stylus worth four coppers.

"The name was taken," I said aloud, and the stone shivered. "That's the first line. Your own covenant admits it, Vessan. You built your claim on a confession."

"I built an *empire* on it—"

"You built a truce on it. There's a difference, and I've spent my life learning to tell them apart." I set the stylus back to the groove. "Borders drawn in fear hold until someone stops being afraid. I stopped about an hour ago. It's very freeing. You should try it."

The second attempt held. Whether it was the words, or the blood — mine, from a split knuckle; Maren's, from her cheek; the messenger's, still dried under my nails from a week ago — whether it was grief or stubbornness or simply that the stone had waited three thousand years for someone to tell it the truth, the stylus bit. Ink sank into the groove and the groove drank it, and the old letters around my new ones began to glow like coals remembering fire.

And Vessan began to run.

Not walk. *Run.* A god crossing a battlefield at a dead sprint, scattering the Choir's singers like leaves, and every step he took the ground forgot how to be ground. I heard Maren swear in three languages. I kept writing.

*Let it be recorded,* I wrote, my hand moving faster than my fear, *that the name called [here the stone supplied it, unbidden, vast, a word like a mountain range] was taken, not won; stolen, not traded; and that its theft was committed by Aldous Reyes, called Founder, called Blessed, called Thief.*

My own ancestor's name. My family's founding crime, in my family's hand, at last. I had expected shame. What came instead was stranger — a kind of terrible relief, the feeling of a map finally matching the territory. For generations we had drawn the valley's borders and never asked why the valley needed them. Now the ink knew. Now everyone would.

*Therefore let the name be returned —*

Vessan arrived.

* * *

I want to be honest about this part, because later they will tell it wrong. They will say I stood unbowed while a god bore down on me. Here is the truth: when Vessan reached the stone, I fell over. Sideways, behind the granite, stylus still clutched like a knife I had no idea how to use. The shockwave of his arrival turned the air to hammer-blows. My ears filled with a ringing like a struck bell the size of the sky. Maren landed on top of me — she had thrown herself down too, and I choose to believe tactically rather than that she also fell over.

"Finish it," she shouted into my ear, over the roar of two theologies colliding overhead.

"My arm's asleep—"

"*Finish it.*"

I rolled back up against the stone. Vessan loomed over us both, and up close the wound in him was all I could see — the hollow where his true name should have lived, three thousand years of borrowed authority stretched over nothing. He raised a hand and the light in it was the light of the old war, the light that had burned the country we'd crossed to get here, the light that had taught my ancestors to dig a hole in the world and hide in it.

"You understand what you unmake," he said. Almost gently. That was the worst part. He wasn't raging. He was grieving, and he thought the grief gave him the right. "Without the name, there is no warrant. Without warrant, no war. But also no peace as you have known it. No valley. No sanctuary. Everything your family bought with their crime — gone. Is that justice?"

I thought of the valley. Of the shrine that had answered a child wrongly after generations of silence. Of the map I'd burned the edges of because my hand wanted more world than I was allowed. Of a dying messenger whispering *the name was taken* with his last breath, choosing truth over safety with the little time he had left.

"No," I said. "It's better than justice. It's an amendment."

And I wrote the rest:

*—returned not to the god who claims it nor to the void that would erase it, but to the mortals who bled beneath the war it ended. Let the name belong to the living. Let no divine claim bind it. Let the oath stand amended by the hand of Ada Reyes, cartographer, heir of the thief, who signs not in triumph but in payment.*

Then I signed it. My actual name. The one thing I had left that was truly mine, and the last thing I ever would.

The stone took it the way dry earth takes rain.

* * *

What happened next, I felt before I saw. Every hair on my body stood up. The ringing in my ears resolved into a single sustained note — the note the stone had been holding, silently, for three millennia, waiting for someone to finish the sentence. The grooves lit gold from within, all of them, the old oath and the new, thief's confession and cartographer's correction, and the light climbed off the stone and into the air and wrote itself across the torn sky in letters taller than cathedrals.

Vessan staggered.

I watched a god discover gravity. Not the falling kind — the other kind. Weight. Limit. The specific humiliation of a boundary. The storm-shape of him guttered like a lamp running low, and where the wound in him had been, something closed — not healed, but *closed*, sealed, the way a border closes. The stolen name passed out of reach of every claim, divine or otherwise, and settled where I had sent it: into the crowd. Into the mortals pressed against Greyford's walls, the soldiers of both factions frozen mid-strike, the clerks and cooks and ferry-men, thousands of ordinary people who suddenly carried, each of them, one syllable of a god's true name and didn't know it yet.

They would know soon. That was chapter twenty-one's problem.

"You have made yourself unmappable," Vessan said. His voice was smaller now. Still vast, still terrible, but small the way a cathedral is small once you've seen the mountains. "No realm will hold you. No god will know you. No prayer will find you. Do you understand what you have given up?"

I got to my feet. My legs worked, mostly. Maren stood beside me with her sword drawn and pointed at nothing, breathing hard, alive, which was more than I'd honestly budgeted for.

"A cartographer's job," I said, "is to know the difference between the map and the place. You never learned it. You thought the oath *was* the world." I looked up at what remained of him. "It isn't. It never was. Go home, Vessan. Wherever that is, now."

For a long moment — a moment I will be turning over for the rest of my life, however long the rest of it turns out to be — Vessan looked at me. Not with hatred. With something worse and better: recognition. The look of one maker of boundaries acknowledging another.

Then the Ashen Choir's song crested, the torn sky began — slowly, grudgingly, like a wound deciding to close — to knit, and Vessan withdrew. Not fled. *Withdrew*, the way an army withdraws, the way a tide withdraws: in good order, with dignity, leaving the beach transformed. His light drained upward through the closing tear. The last thing to go was his voice, and it did not curse me.

It said, quietly, almost kindly: "*Well drawn.*"

Then the sky was a sky. An ordinary, intact, slightly smoke-damaged sky, going grey at the edges toward evening, and underneath it lay a battlefield full of kneeling soldiers and drifting ash and one cracked covenant stone glowing softly gold, cooling, becoming just a rock with very important graffiti.

* * *

Afterward there was silence, the specific stunned silence of ten thousand people waiting to find out if they were allowed to make noise.

A soldier of the Choir broke it. He was young, ash-smeared, and he was staring at his own hands with an expression I recognized from mirrors — the look of a man whose world has just been redrawn without his consent. Then he laughed. Just once, a short bark of it, and said, to no one, to everyone: "It's quiet. In here. It's been loud my whole life and it's *quiet*."

The silence broke into something else. Not cheering, not at first — more a rising murmur, thousands of people discovering syllables in their mouths that hadn't been there that morning, testing them, passing them along. Somewhere a bell started ringing in the wrong rhythm, and nobody corrected it.

Maren lowered her sword. She looked at the stone, then at me, then at my right hand, which had begun — now that adrenaline was departing and taking my strength with it as rent — to shake uncontrollably.

"You signed it," she said.

"I signed it."

"With your name."

"That was the currency. It's a long story about trust instruments." I sat down, abruptly and without dignity, against the warm granite. "Also I may have just erased myself from every registry in existence. Divine, legal, possibly cartographic. We'll see."

Maren sat down beside me. She didn't say anything for a while. Overhead the last of the Choir's singers drifted down out of the ruined air like ash settling, and among the kneeling soldiers people were beginning to stand, and touch each other's shoulders, and cry, and laugh, and somewhere in the middle distance a child was saying a word over and over that made the older people flinch and the younger ones stare.

"Ada," Maren said finally. "Your hand's bleeding on the stone."

"So is the rest of me, probably."

"No — look."

I looked. The blood from my split knuckle, smeared where I'd braced myself against the granite, was sinking in. Being taken. The stone was drinking it the way it had drunk the ink, and where it soaked, new lines were surfacing — fine, faint, fresh-cut grooves spelling themselves out in a script I didn't know and somehow could read.

*Received,* the stone said, in the voice of all law everywhere, patient and absolute. *One name, freely given. One signer, accordingly released. Let the record show: Ada Reyes, son of a thief, heir of no one, citizen of nowhere, debtor to all — discharged.*

The glow faded. The stone went cold. Ordinary. Done with me.

"Well," I said, after a while. "That's that, then."

Maren was quiet a moment longer. Then she stood, brushed ash off her knees, and offered me her hand — the same hand, I noticed, that had hauled me bodily out of a burning archive, dragged me across a burnt country, and shoved me behind this very stone when a god came down like weather.

"Come on, then," she said. "Nobody. Let's get you fed."

Beyond the walls of Greyford, in the gathering dark, the world was already changing — borders softening, old wards failing, valleys that had refused maps for three thousand years opening like fists. Somewhere out there, my valley was dissolving its boundary, and I would never be able to draw it the same way again.

I took her hand, and we walked out through the gate, past the stone, past the kneeling and the singing and the weeping, toward the largest blank space either of us had ever seen.
