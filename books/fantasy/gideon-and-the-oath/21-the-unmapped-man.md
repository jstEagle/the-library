## 21. The Unmapped Man

I have served a god for eleven years, and I will tell you the strangest thing about the morning after his defeat: it was quiet.

Not the quiet of peace. I'd never actually known that one, so I couldn't have said at the time which it was. It was the quiet of a room after an argument ends — that ringing, emptied-out quality, where everyone stands around not looking at each other and someone has to start sweeping up the broken crockery.

In Greyford, the crockery was mostly buildings.

I stood in the conclave square with ash in my hair and Vessan's sigil still inked on my wrist — a commission mark that no longer answered when I pressed it, like knocking on a door in a house where everyone had moved out. Around me, mortals were doing the thing mortals do best, which is survive loudly. Someone had already set up a soup stall against a shattered colonnade. Two children were arguing over a scrap of blue sky visible through the torn clouds, as if they could claim it by shouting hardest.

The gods were gone. Not dead — I want to be precise about this, because precision was my job and I've grown attached to it. Vessan was diminished, folded down into something small enough to fit inside a temple again, sulking somewhere east with what remained of his choir. The Ashen Choir's masters had withdrawn too, their great project of annihilation rendered suddenly pointless: you cannot destroy a name that no longer belongs to anyone. They'd gone quiet the way a debt collector goes quiet when the debtor dies.

And the name itself — the stolen name, the keystone, the whole rotten foundation of two thousand years of truce — belonged to the mortals now. All of them. Which meant, practically speaking, it belonged to no one, the way a public well belongs to everyone who drinks from it and therefore to nobody at all.

Ada Reyes had done that with a pen.

I kept thinking about that. I'd spent eleven years believing power was something you carried — orders, seals, the weight of a god's attention on the back of your neck. And this cartographer with ink-stained fingers and a habit of talking to his own maps had unmade the entire divine order by *amending a document*.

There's a lesson there. I've been avoiding learning it ever since.

* * *

I found him three days later, or rather I found the crowd and assumed he'd be at the center of it, because that's where trouble congregates around Ada Reyes like iron filings around a lodestone.

It wasn't a crowd, exactly. It was a clerk. A Greyford registrar, a small dry man with spectacles and a ledger, standing in front of Ada with the expression of a man forced to deliver news he finds personally distasteful.

"—there's simply no provision for it," the clerk was saying. "The rolls list every freeborn soul by lineage and holding. Your family line held the trust of the valley under covenant law. With the covenant dissolved, the holdings revert to the conclave, and the name Reyes is struck from—"

"Struck," Ada repeated.

"Struck from the rolls." The clerk said it gently, the way you'd tell someone their house had burned down. "You understand, it isn't punitive. There is simply nothing left for the name to *be*. You gave it away."

"I gave the *name* away," Ada said. "My name. The other one. The god's one."

"The god's name was returned to its heirs, per your amendment. The mortal name Reyes was the instrument of trust, and instruments of dissolved trusts are—" the clerk consulted his ledger with the enthusiasm of a man reading his own eulogy, "—extinguished."

I watched Ada take that in. He didn't argue. That was the thing about him that unnerved people who expected argument — he went quiet instead, and his eyes did that thing where they unfocused slightly, like he was drafting something on the back of the world.

"So I'm not anyone," he said finally. "Legally."

"You are a person present in Greyford without registration," the clerk agreed. "Which is technically a misdemeanor."

"I saved the world."

"The world," said the clerk, "is not a registered entity either. Good day."

He packed up his ledger and left, and Ada stood there in the wreckage of his legal existence, and I swear to every silent god, the first thing out of his mouth was:

"Do you think they'll strike me off the maps too?"

"That depends," I said, walking over, "on whether the maps were drawn by the conclave or by you."

"Both. Mostly me." He looked at his hands. Ink in every crease, permanent as sin. "I drew the valley. Every stream, every fence line, every place old Tam fell off his roof. If the valley's boundary is dissolving — and it is, travelers say the mist wall came down Tuesday — then my map is wrong now. My life's work is wrong. And I'm not legally real enough to file a correction."

There was a grief in his voice, but underneath it — and I heard it clearly, because I'd spent eleven years listening for what people said underneath what they said, that being most of my job — underneath it was something else. Something almost like relief.

"Maren," he said. "What am I?"

I considered several answers. *A misdemeanor.* *A hero.* *A man who owes me a very long explanation about why he didn't tell me he was rewriting the oath while I was holding off an Ashen Choir zealot with a borrowed sword.*

"A blank margin," I said.

He blinked.

"That's what you used to call them. The places you left undrawn. You told me once — on the road, the night before the burnt country — that a map with no blank spaces wasn't finished, it was closed. Dead. That the blanks were gifts." I shrugged, and tried to make the shrug do less work than it needed to, because my voice was threatening to do something embarrassing. "Congratulations. You're a gift now."

* * *

We walked out to the valley together a week later. I don't think either of us planned it; we just found ourselves on the north road, and the north road only goes one interesting place.

The boundary was gone. I'd known that intellectually — reports, rumors, the general cessation of divine screaming — but knowing and seeing are different professions, and I've always been a seeing sort of woman.

Where the mist wall had stood for as long as anyone remembered, there was just... country. Rolling green hills running right up against the valley's edge with no seam, no scar, no shimmer. As if the valley had been a drawing all along and someone had finally lifted the frame. Wildflowers grew in the exact spot where, two months ago, stepping would have gotten you erased from causality. A shepherd's dog was sniffing at the old boundary line with profound suspicion, having clearly been raised on stories.

Ada crouched and put his palm flat on the grass, the way he did with maps, reading the ground.

"It doesn't feel different," he said. "That's what gets me. I spent thirty years thinking the edge of the world would feel like something. Cold water. A held breath. And it's just grass."

"What did it feel like when you crossed it? Leaving. Back then."

His hand stilled. "Like drowning in reverse. Like everything I'd never been allowed to notice came flooding in at once." He looked up at me, and there was that expression I'd learned to distrust and then rely on and then — somewhere on the road through the burnt country, somewhere between sharing water and splitting the last of the bread — come to need. "You're asking because you felt it too. When you crossed in."

"I felt like a courier delivering herself," I said. "Vessan's leash went tight the moment I passed the line. I could feel him noticing me. Cataloguing me. I'd been his for eleven years and I only felt owned the moment I crossed into the one place his ownership didn't reach." I looked out at the flowered hills. "Funny, isn't it. The valley was the only free ground in the world, and neither of us could stand it."

"Speak for yourself. I loved it. I just also wanted to set it on fire and leave." He stood, brushing off his knees. "Do you feel owned now? With him diminished?"

I checked. Honestly checked — pressed the sigil scar on my wrist, listened for that grinding-stone attention I'd lived under so long I'd stopped hearing it the way you stop hearing a river.

Nothing. Silence with a shape where a god used to be.

"No," I said, and my voice came out strange, and I decided not to examine why. "No, I don't."

* * *

Here is what happened to the valley, since someone should record it and the clerks are useless:

The people stayed. That surprised everyone except the people. Scholars from three realms had predicted mass exodus — why remain in a backwater once the walls come down? But the valley folk had spent generations perfecting the art of living without gods, and it turned out that art traveled beautifully. They planted. They argued. Old Tam, who falls off roofs, immediately climbed onto a new roof to celebrate the boundary's dissolution and fell off it, and swore — with feeling — that no god had pushed him.

Some of them resented Ada. I won't pretend otherwise. A delegation came to his workshop door — the workshop survived; the workshop will outlast civilization — and asked him, with the particular politeness of the deeply angry, whether he understood that he'd ended the world's longest peace. That the war might come back. That their grandparents' grandparents had built lives on the certainty that the gods would never, ever come here.

Ada told them the truth, which is his worst habit: that the peace had been built on a theft, that the theft had a victim whose name they'd never even known, and that a peace you can't look at directly isn't peace, it's a debt accruing interest.

Half the delegation spat at his feet. The other half came back the next week to ask him to teach them to read maps.

As for the rest of the realms — the small gods were stirring everywhere, cautious as animals after a fire, testing whether prayers still worked and finding that they did, but differently. Looser. Nobody's prayer got answered by right anymore; it got answered by *attention*, which is a different contract entirely, and mortals were learning to haggle. I heard a miller in the borderlands had negotiated his local river-spirit down from first-born sons to an annual cheese tribute, and honestly, good for him.

The old order was dead. What grew in its place was going to be messy, unfair, occasionally magnificent, and completely unmapped.

Ada Reyes — formerly of nowhere, currently a misdemeanor — wanted to draw all of it.

* * *

He asked me about it at dusk, at the top of the pass where I'd once cornered him at the ferry with an arrest warrant and a bad conscience. We'd walked up there for no reason except that it was high and quiet and the light did something generous to the ruined country below.

"I've been drafting," he said.

"Dangerous word, coming from you."

"Hush. I mean maps. Actual maps. Of the newly open realms — the deep forests where the Choir's saints supposedly sleep, the drowned archive, the burnt country, all of it. Nobody's charted any of it properly. Everyone's too busy rebuilding or praying or suing each other over cheese tributes." He turned the page toward me. His draft work was beautiful even unfinished, lines confident where he knew the ground and deliberately soft where he didn't. "But I can't survey alone. Some of these places eat solo travelers. And I'm—" he gestured at himself, the whole legal void of himself, "—uncredentialed. No guild will stamp my work. No conclave will register me. Anything I draw will be officially fiction."

"So don't ask permission," I said. "You're the man who amended a divine oath with a pen. Credential yourself."

"It's not quite—" He stopped. Started again, which he does when something matters. "Maren. Come with me."

I had prepared for this. I want that noted. I'd rehearsed answers on the walk up — the gracious refusal, the deflection, the joke about how someone had to keep him from getting eaten by whatever slept in the drowned archive's deeper shelves. Eleven years as a god's courier teaches you to have your exits drafted before the conversation starts.

"Yes," I said.

All the drafts, wasted.

He looked startled, which was fair, because so was I. "Just like that?"

"You're a blank margin, Ada. Blank margins need someone to guard them until the future fills them in." I took the draft page from his hands and studied it so I wouldn't have to study him. "Also, you cannot navigate, you cannot fight, you cannot lie to innkeepers, and you will absolutely walk into something's den because it 'looked geographically interesting.' You need a professional."

"And you've resigned from professionalism."

"I've resigned from divinity. Different department." I handed the page back. "Besides. Somebody has to witness this. The man who isn't anyone, mapping everything. Future historians will need a source, and historians are worse than clerks."

He laughed — really laughed, the sound still new enough to both of us that we paused afterward, slightly surprised, like people who'd heard a bird sing somewhere birds shouldn't.

Then he looked west, where the sun was going down over country no accurate map had ever recorded, and his eyes did the drafting thing again, and he said:

"There's a rumor about a city past the burnt country. A city that moves. Merchants swear it's in a different valley every season, and every map that's ever marked it has been wrong within the year."

I felt the old professional reflex rise — *rumor, unverified, likely heresy* — and let it die quietly. It had earned its retirement.

"How far?" I said.

"Nobody knows. That's rather the point." He rolled up the draft, and tucked it into the satchel where a sealed oath had once ridden against his spine and changed the shape of the world. "We could find out."

Below us, the valley lamps were coming on one by one, in a place that was no longer a refuge or a prison or a secret — just home, for whoever chose it. Above us, the sky was empty of gods and full of stars, and the stars, at least, had never once demanded worship.

"We leave at dawn," I said, because someone responsible had to say it, and between the two of us, I was apparently still pretending to be her.

Ada smiled and began, naturally, to sketch the sunset.
