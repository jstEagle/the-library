## 22. Blank Margins

There is a particular kind of silence that comes from a map that knows it's finished. I spent thirty years chasing that silence and never once caught it. Every map I ever completed in the valley had the same flaw: the moment I signed my name in the corner, the world went on being itself without my permission. Rivers wandered. Roads grew elbows. A hill I'd drawn with a modest little hump would spend the winter growing ambitions.

So when people ask me now — and they do ask, usually over ale, usually with the slightly reverent tone of someone who has heard the stories and wants to know if they're true — whether I consider myself the man who ended the war of the gods, I tell them the truth, which is that I am the man who left a lot of blank spaces on purpose. It sounds like false modesty. It isn't. It's cartography.

Maren says I'm insufferable about it. Maren says a great many things about me before breakfast, most of them accurate.

* * *

Our camp this morning sits on a ridge above what the old maps called the Weeping Steppes, which the war had scoured down to grey glass and bone-dust, and which — eleven years after Greyford — has decided, against all reasonable expectation, to be green again. Not the tidy green of farmland. The reckless green of a place that was told it was dead and took it personally. From up here it looks like someone spilled a bucket of spring across the horizon and let it run wherever it liked.

Which is precisely the problem, professionally speaking.

"Ada." Maren's voice from below the ridge, carrying that flat courier's patience she uses when she's about to be right at me. "The river moved."

"It did not move."

"The river moved."

I climbed down to where she stood at the bank with her boots off and her trousers rolled to the knee, glaring at the water as though it owed her an apology. She held our working copy of the steppe survey open one-handed, and I will grant that the map showed the Aldiss running a good half-mile east of where it was currently making a nuisance of itself.

"Huh," I said.

"'Huh,' he says. Eleven years. Eleven years of mapping the reachable realms, and every spring the whole world rearranges itself out of spite."

"That's not spite, that's recovery. The land's still settling. The war broke its bones; the bones are knitting crooked." I crouched and put my fingers in the cold water, an old habit, checking for the faint hum that used to live in everything touched by divine weather. Nothing. Just water. Just cold, ordinary, gloriously unclaimed water. "Besides, you knew this when you signed on."

"I signed on for adventure. Nobody said adventure was redrawing the same river annually like some kind of aquatic penance."

"You love it."

She looked at me over the top of the map, and the corner of her mouth did the thing it does — the small, grudging tectonic shift that on anyone else would be a full smile but on Maren constitutes a formal declaration. "I tolerate it," she said, "at an extremely high standard of quality. Now get your ink out. If we're wrong about the Aldiss, we're wrong about the ford, and if we're wrong about the ford, the settlers coming up from Greyford next month are going to drive their wagons into a bog."

So we worked. That's the shape of our days now, and I want to be honest about how unheroic it is, because the ballads — and there are ballads, gods help us all, there are three, and two of them give me a beard — the ballads have no interest in the truth, which is that the great rewriting of the oath was followed by eleven years of measuring fords and arguing about contour lines and leaving blank spaces.

Especially the blank spaces. People think that's modesty too, or worse, laziness. Here is what it actually is.

* * *

When the amended oath took hold on the covenant stone — when the stolen name came loose from Vessan's throat like a fishhook from a lip, when both factions felt their warrant for war dissolve into something thin and embarrassing — the realms didn't just open. They *unclenched*. Places that magic had never been able to touch because the valley's peace depended on a theft, places that had been locked behind the old gods' claims like rooms in a house whose owner died intestate — all of it became mappable. Reachable. Ours, in the only sense that matters: nobody's, and therefore everybody's.

And the first thing I learned, out in those newly opened lands with Maren at my shoulder and both factions' surviving agents watching us with expressions ranging from grief to grudging respect, was that I didn't know anything. Thirty years of meticulous valley work, and the moment the borders dissolved I was a child with a stick at the edge of an ocean.

The old maps had lied, of course. Not maliciously — the valley's isolation meant everything beyond it was rumor dressed up as fact. There were mountain ranges on the pre-war charts that turned out to be clouds. There were seas that were lakes, kingdoms that were one stubborn village, and at least one vast desert that proved to be a very committed forest with poor signage. The Ashen Choir's archives, once we were granted access — Maren negotiated that, in the aftermath, with the particular steel she reserves for institutions — contained maps so confident and so wrong that I framed one and hung it in our workshop as a warning against certainty.

But here is the thing I decided early, and have never regretted: I stopped filling in the edges.

Every map we publish now — and we publish them through Greyford, under the conclave's seal, though the conclave mostly stays out of our way since the clerk who betrayed us was quietly retired into a life of auditing grain tariffs — every map carries deliberate blanks. White spaces at the margins. Unsurveyed valleys, unnamed rivers, whole coastlines that trail off into nothing with a single line of text beneath: *Here the map ends. The world does not.*

We got letters about it, at first. Angry ones. A cartographer's guild in the eastern cities sent a formal complaint accusing me of abdicating my professional duty, which, given that my previous professional act was rewriting a divine covenant in front of two warring god-factions while the sky tore open, struck me as a somewhat selective reading of my career.

Maren wrote the reply. It said, in full: *A finished map is a closed door. We prefer hinges.* The guild framed it, apparently. People are strange.

But travelers understood immediately. That's the part that still gets me, eleven years on. Within a season, the blank margins started coming back to us *filled*. A shepherd's sketch of a pass we'd left white. A trader's account of a lake town that named itself after reading our blank space aloud — they call it Margin's Rest, and yes, there's an inn, and yes, the ale is mediocre, and yes, I cried a little into it. Children send drawings. Whole communities treat the white spaces as invitations, which is exactly what they are. The oath gave the name back to mortals; the blank margins give the *world* back, one unfinished edge at a time.

Peace, I've come to believe, is not a thing you finish. It's a thing you keep drawing.

* * *

In the evening, Maren cooked — she cooks the way she does everything, efficiently and with visible contempt for inefficiency — and I sat by the fire with the case I never travel without, even though it weighs more than my tent.

Inside, under glass, mounted on board that Maren stained herself because she said the original frame made it look like a relic and relics attract pilgrims and pilgrims attract con artists: a strip of vellum, scorched at one edge, bearing six words in a dead messenger's failing hand.

*the name was taken*

That's all that survives of the original oath. When the amended version took hold on the covenant stone, the old scroll didn't burn or shatter or do any of the dramatic things the ballads claim. It simply became paper. Ordinary, mortal, unsigned paper — because the trust it recorded had been discharged, and a discharged oath is just ink remembering. But the first line stayed legible, and I wanted it legible forever, for reasons I've explained to approximately four people without success.

It's not a trophy. I want to be clear about that. I'm not proud of those words. My family wrote them in blood and theft three hundred years before I was born; my inheritance was guilt with good penmanship. Some nights I look at the strip of vellum and feel the vertigo come back, the old night-question from the workshop in the valley: *who are you, if everything you are stands on a crime?*

But I keep it framed because it's the truest sentence I know. Every peace I've seen since — the conclave's treaties, the settler charters, the careful diplomacy between villages that used to pray to rival gods — every one of them rests on something somebody took from somebody else, and the honest thing, the only cartographically honest thing, is to write that down. To mark it on the map. *Here be the wound. Here be the debt. Walk carefully, and leave room for repair.*

The blank margins are my apology and my amendment, rolled into one. You can't give back a name that's been returned already. But you can refuse to claim the territory of certainty. You can leave the edges open so the people who come after can draw themselves in.

"Ada." Maren, across the fire, holding out a bowl. "You're doing the face."

"What face?"

"The vellum face. The one where you're having a conversation with a dead messenger." She settled beside me, shoulder warm against mine, and looked at the framed line without touching it — she never touches it, which is its own kind of respect. "He'd be glad, I think. The messenger. That it ended up meaning something instead of rotting in a god's vault."

"He'd be glad it wasn't for nothing," I said. "That's different. Gladness needs leisure. He was running on fumes and faith when he reached my door."

"Fumes and faith." She snorted. "You should put that on the guild's next complaint response."

We ate. The fire cracked. Somewhere downslope, the relocated Aldiss ran over stones it hadn't been running over last spring, cheerful and lawless, and I thought about adding a note to tomorrow's survey — *river subject to revision; see also: everything* — and decided the blank margin could say it better.

"Maren," I said, after a while. "The letter from Greyford. The one that came with the supply wagon. You read it before I did."

"I always read it before you do. You'd answer a summons from a burning building with 'hmm, let me check my sources.'"

"What did it actually say? Past the pleasantries."

She was quiet long enough that I knew it wasn't nothing. She reached into her pack — her courier's pack, scarred and patched, older than our partnership — and drew out a folded sheet with the conclave's seal broken neatly along one edge, because Maren breaks seals the way she does everything: precisely, and with intent.

"They've found another one," she said.

My stomach did the old drop, the Greyford drop, the *oh no* drop. "Another what."

"A trust. An old arrangement, predating the oath — predating the *war*, maybe. Out past the Saltreach, in country none of our maps cover because none of our maps cover it." She tapped the blank western edge of our working survey, the white space we'd left there two seasons ago with the standard line beneath it. "There are people living in it, Ada. People who've been keeping something safe for three centuries on the strength of a promise nobody remembers making. And now that the gods are unbound, the thing they're keeping has started—" she paused, choosing the word with a courier's care, "—*answering*."

"Answering what?"

"Prayers." She met my eyes across the firelight, and I saw it in her face — not fear, exactly. Maren doesn't do fear. Recognition. The look of a woman who once traced a dying messenger's route into a silent valley and found the world cracking open. "They're praying to it, Ada. And it's answering *wrong*."

I looked at the fire. At the vellum in its frame, six words in a dead man's hand. At the white space on the western edge of our map, waiting, patient, the size of everything we don't know yet.

"Well," I said, and reached for my ink.
