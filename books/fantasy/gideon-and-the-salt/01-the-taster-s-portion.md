## 1. The Taster's Portion

The seventh dish was quail in a dark sauce, and Quentin Whitlock tasted it the way she tasted everything: slowly, with her whole attention, like a woman reading a letter that might contain either love or a death sentence.

She held the portion on her tongue for a count of ten. The sauce was wine and blackcurrant and something earthier underneath — dried morels, she decided, and beneath them the faint mineral ghost of a stock made from bones too old to be honest about their animal. No bitterness at the back edge where poison liked to hide. No sweetness that didn't belong. She swallowed, set down the silver spoon, and waited.

The kitchen steward watched her with the particular patience of a man who had learned not to hope for anything interesting. "Well?"

"Clean," Quentin said.

He exhaled through his nose — relief, but also a small disappointment. In nine years of service, Quentin had never once spat into the basin. The other tasters, when there had been others, treated each dish like a lottery ticket: spit, gasp, clutch the throat, enjoy the brief theater of survival. Quentin simply ate, announced, and moved on. The court had decided this was composure. It was actually arithmetic.

Seven dishes tonight. The king's birthday feast was three weeks off, which meant the kitchens were already testing courses, and which meant Quentin's body was already absorbing a season's worth of slow accumulation. She could feel it beginning now, right on schedule — the numbness. It started at the edges of things. The rim of her ears went distant. Her fingertips reported the tablecloth as something remembered rather than felt. By midnight her tongue would be a stranger in her mouth, and by morning it would come home again, slightly less itself than before.

That was the trade. Nobody said it aloud, because saying it aloud would make it a grievance, and grievances required answers. But every taster in the palace understood: you did not die of one poisoned dish. You died of ten thousand clean ones. The king's food was safe because it was tested on someone, and *someone* was a career measured in dulling nerve-ends and a tongue that would spend its last years tasting nothing at all.

Quentin was twenty-six years old and could no longer reliably distinguish between honey and sugar syrup. She had made peace with this the way you make peace with weather.

"Send up the eighth," she said.

"There is no eighth."

"Then I'm finished."

"You're finished." The steward gathered his ledger of menus and paused, as he always paused, at the door. "Her Majesty asked whether you'd dine at the high table for the feast. As an honor."

"I'll consider it."

"She'd like an answer before—"

"I'll consider it," Quentin said again, in the tone that ended conversations, and the steward left her alone in the small stone room that the palace called the Tasting Chamber and that Quentin privately called the mouth.

It was a fitting name. The room had a long table, a basin, a fire that never quite warmed the corners, and a single high window through which the evening came down gray and thin. Everything in it existed to pass through something else. Food passed through her. Verdicts passed through her. She herself passed through the palace's attention without leaving any impression at all, which was precisely the point, and precisely the loneliest fact of her life.

She sat for a while after the steward left, hands flat on the table, letting the numbness spread at its own pace. This was the part of the evening she never described to anyone, because describing it would require admitting she noticed it, and noticing it would require admitting it mattered. A taster who minded the cost was a taster looking for sympathy, and sympathy made people ask questions, and questions were how tasters ended up retired to some damp cottage with a pension and a tongue that couldn't tell bread from bark.

Instead she counted. Seven dishes. Fourteen minutes of contact per dish, average. Nine years, four months. She had done the full sum once, early on, and arrived at a number of hours she refused to repeat even to herself. Some arithmetic you do once and then live inside.

The bell above the door rang twice — the signal that the chamber was cleared for the night — and Quentin rose, folded her napkin into a precise square, and went belowstairs.

* * *

Belowstairs in the palace meant cellars, and cellars meant coal, and coal meant a corridor that smelled of black dust and old water where nobody with rank had ever had reason to walk. Quentin knew the route by feel now, in the dark, counting turns: left at the third arch, down the stair with the broken fifth step, along the wall where the mortar had been patched with something that glittered faintly when you weren't looking directly at it.

At the end of the corridor stood a door that opened onto a coal store. Behind the coal store stood another door, and behind that door sat Marrow.

Marrow was the guild's fence, its bookkeeper, its memory of itself, and possibly the ugliest man in the capital, though he'd have contested the title on grounds of craftsmanship. He had a face like a dropped pie and hands so fine-boned they looked borrowed from a smaller, more elegant person. He kept a desk, a lamp, two chairs, and a wall of pigeonholes that stretched back farther than the cellar had any right to be deep. Each pigeonhole held a ledger. Each ledger held a week.

"You're late," Marrow said, without looking up.

"The king ate slowly."

"The king always eats slowly. That's why he needs you." He slid a fresh ledger across the desk. "Week forty-one. You know the form."

Quentin sat in the second chair, which creaked in a way she found comforting, and uncapped her pen.

Here is what the world believed about Quentin Whitlock: that she was the palace taster, orphaned young, unambitious, unremarkable, and quietly poisoning herself toward an early retirement. All of this was true, which was what made it such good cover. What the world did not believe — what the world had been carefully prevented from believing — was that the palace taster was also the Guild of Small Mercies' most reliable courier of records, and that every week she descended into a coal cellar and wrote down, in a small neat hand, exactly what impossible cargo had moved through the capital while the Crown wasn't looking.

The guild traded in things the law said could not be owned. Not jewels, not weapons — those were merely illegal, and illegality was just a tax with worse manners. The guild dealt in the other category. Memories. Names. Songs that had been outlawed with their singers. Deeds to houses in provinces that had been dissolved from the maps twenty years ago, along with the provinces themselves. When Old Vessany was struck from the country — its borders unwritten, its towns renamed, its dead reclassified as having never lived anywhere in particular — everything the Vessani had been became contraband overnight. And contraband, as any fence will tell you, does not disappear. It just changes hands.

Quentin recorded the changes of hands.

"Week forty-one," Marrow prompted. "Start with the lullaby."

"A stolen lullaby." She wrote as she spoke, the words coming out in her ledger-voice, flat and exact. "Out of the House of the Sisters, Bellows Lane. Seller claims provenance from before the Dissolution. Buyer paid in salt-weight. Cargo description: melody, four lines, minor key, sung to children of the drowned coast. Condition: intact, though the seller wept during transfer, which may have altered the tempo."

"Tempo alterations are noted in the margin," Marrow said. "You know this."

"It's noted in the margin."

"And the widow?"

Quentin turned the page. "A widow's legal name. Sold by her late husband's family, who considered it theirs by marriage. She bought it back through us at considerable loss. Entry reads: name of Marguerite Ostrow, restored to owner, witnessed by myself, condition good, slight wear at the edges where it was used by other people for eleven years."

Marrow grunted. "Wear at the edges. That's good. Write things like that and the ledgers read like they were kept by a human being."

"They were kept by a human being."

"Debatable. Next."

"A jar of sea-salt." Quentin hesitated over this one, pen hovering. "From a drowned province. The seller wouldn't say which province. Said the jar had been raised off the harbor road by divers working without lights. Buyer is a chandler on Cordwain Street who wants it for — " she checked her notes — "for reasons he declined to specify."

"Chandlers always want salt for reasons they decline to specify. It's practically the definition of the trade." Marrow held out his hand, palm up, and Quentin placed the week's tokens in it: small lead seals, each stamped with the guild's mark, each tied to a transaction she had witnessed but never examined. "Anything unusual?"

"No."

"Look again."

Quentin looked at her own entries. Neat rows. Flat voice. Nothing unusual, nothing strange, nothing that reached out of the page and — 

"No," she said.

"Then we're done." Marrow began shelving the ledger, then stopped, as if remembering something trivial. "Your mother used to write her margins fuller. Little comments. Weather, moods, whether the buyer had kind eyes. I told her she was sentimentalizing state property. She told me records without feeling were just graves with better handwriting." He slid the ledger home into its slot. "I still don't know which of us was right."

Quentin's pen had gone still against the desk. "You keep bringing her up lately."

"Do I?"

"Twice this month. Three times if you count the thing about the margins."

Marrow closed the pigeonhole with a soft wooden click. "Old men repeat themselves. It's how we haunt people politely." He looked at her then, directly, with an expression she couldn't file under anything. "Same time next week. Eat something sweet on your way up — it helps the tongue find its way home."

It didn't help. They both knew it didn't help. But she took the twist of honey-candy he offered anyway, because refusing it would have been admitting the numbness was real, and some fictions are load-bearing.

* * *

The rule came to her, as it came every night on the climb back up, in her mother's voice, though her mother had been dead or dissolved or whatever happened to arrested persons of Old Vessani descent for nineteen years now:

*Never read what you record.*

It was the first rule of the guild and the only one enforced absolutely. A courier who knows the cargo can be tortured into telling it. A courier who only knows the shape of the entries — weight, condition, provenance, price — has nothing in her head worth burning anyone for. Quentin had obeyed the rule for nine years with the devotion most people reserve for gods. She wrote down stolen names without learning them. She catalogued smuggled memories without wondering whose they were. She had recorded, over the years, enough erased country to rebuild Old Vessany in paperwork alone, and she could not have answered a single question about any of it, because she had trained herself not to hear her own hand moving across the page.

Tonight, climbing past the broken fifth step, she caught herself doing the thing she never did: turning one entry over in her mind. *A jar of sea-salt from a drowned province.* The seller wouldn't say which province. Divers working without lights. And somewhere in her chest, in the space where the numbness hadn't reached yet, a feeling she declined to name was insisting that she knew which province. Was insisting that the taste of that salt — if she had been fool enough to taste it — would be the taste of a coastline she had walked before she could write.

She stopped on the stairs and put the thought down the way you put down something hot.

*Never read what you record.*

But rules, she had begun to notice lately, were like the numbness. They worked at the edges first, and then they worked inward, and one morning you woke and couldn't feel them at all.

She reached the top of the stair. The corridor was empty, the torches burned low, and the palace settled around her with the vast indifferent breathing of a building that owned her completely and had never once thought about her.

In her room, she ate the honey-candy. It tasted, as always, of almost nothing.

And it was only later — lying awake in the dark, listening to the numbness arrive in her fingertips like a tide coming in — that she registered the small wrongness she had carried out of the cellar without noticing, the way you carry a splinter:

One entry in week forty-one had not been written in her hand.

She had written every line herself. She knew this. And yet one entry — the jar of sea-salt, the drowned province, the divers working without lights — had been written in a hand she almost recognized, a hand that slanted left where hers slanted right, a hand she had spent nineteen years forbidding herself to remember.

Tomorrow, she told herself, she would go down and look again.

She fell asleep before she could decide whether looking again counted as breaking the rule.
