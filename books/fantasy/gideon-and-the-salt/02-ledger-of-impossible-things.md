## 2. Ledger of Impossible Things

The ledgers lived in a room that did not exist.

Officially, the cellar beneath the Tallowman's Rest was a root store, and the door to it was bricked over, and anyone who claimed otherwise was describing a dream. Quentin had learned early that the best hiding place for a thing was not darkness but paperwork — a room so thoroughly recorded as nothing that no one thought to look. The Crown kept meticulous lists of what it had seized, what it had burned, what it had dissolved. It had never once made a list of its own omissions. That was the guild's whole trade: we are the list of what you said wasn't there.

She came down at half past two in the morning, still wearing her court grey, still tasting nutmeg at the back of her throat from the seventh dish. The numbness was settling in nicely, that familiar cotton-wool quiet spreading from her tongue down into her jaw, and she let it. Numbness was the day's wage. The ledger work was the night's.

Three candles. Ink already blacked with a drop of ash so it couldn't be read back against her by lamplight color. She sat on the stool with one leg folded under her, the way Serle had scolded her for at fourteen — *a taster sits square, girl, your spine is an instrument* — and opened the week's book.

Her rule was simple and she had never broken it: **never read what you record.** She wrote entries; she did not read them. She copied cargo descriptions from the couriers' chits into the master ledger, assigned each item a cipher-number, initialed, closed the line. She knew the shape of the words without their meaning, the way a taster knows the weight of a spoon without the flavor. Meaning was dangerous. Meaning was how inquisitors got hooks into people. A woman who only ever handled paper as paper could be tortured for a long time before she gave up anything true.

So she worked by rule, and the rule held, and the routine of it was the closest thing she had to a warm coat.

*Item forty-one.* A lullaby. Stolen — or rather, *rescued*, though the ledger didn't care about verbs — from a nurse in the Duke of Harrow's household who had been singing it to the duke's daughter, not knowing it was Vessani, not knowing the melody itself was contraband. Twenty years since the dissolution, and the law still held that certain songs were illegal in any mouth. The entry ran four lines: provenance, condition (intact), destination (a choir in exile, across the water), fee. Quentin's pen moved through it like a hand moving through familiar dark.

*Item forty-two.* A widow's legal name. This was delicate work even by cipher. The widow had been born in Old Vessany, which meant that when the country was dissolved, her name was dissolved with it — struck from parish rolls, from deeds, from the great Index of Persons that the Crown maintained with such loving thoroughness. Legally speaking, she had never existed, which meant her late husband's property had escheated to the Crown, which meant she was now a squatter in her own house. The guild's service: they carried names out of the country and re-entered them under foreign registries, so that somewhere, in some clerk's careful hand, the woman existed again. Quentin wrote the cipher, the registry, the fee. She did not read the name. She never read the names. Reading a name was how you started remembering it, and remembering was the first symptom of the disease the inquisitors burned people for.

*Item forty-three.* A jar of sea-salt, provenance: the drowned province. That one made her pause, quill hovering.

There were nine provinces in Old Vessany once. Charts now showed eight, and the ninth showed as open water — the Sallow Reach, the cartographers called it, deep and unremarkable, as if the sea had always been there. But salt from the drowned province still turned up sometimes, in old cellars, in smugglers' holds, in the pockets of the mad. The guild treated it as cargo of the highest class. Quentin didn't know why. Rule: never read what you record. She wrote *one jar, sea-salt, provenance per chit, sealed, do not open*, initialed it, and moved on.

Outside, above the cellar stairs, the Tallowman's Rest went about its business of being ordinary. Someone racked mugs. A dog sighed. Rain had started again, that fine coastal rain that sounded less like weather than like something being whispered at the windows.

Quentin worked. The candles guttered and were replaced. Her hands were steady now — the shaking from the evening had passed, as it always passed, and she chose not to think about what it meant that a twenty-six-year-old woman's hands shook after seven dishes, or what Serle would say if he saw, or what Serle already knew. Serle knew everything. That was his function. She fed herself to the king nightly and pretended this was employment rather than devotion, and Serle watched her do it with those pale patient eyes, and between them stood thirty years of poisons in graduated doses, and neither of them named the arrangement.

*Item forty-four* was where it went wrong.

It was a small entry. Small entries were common — a pressed flower, a child's milk tooth, a sentence overheard and written down before the speaker could be made to forget saying it. Quentin took the courier's chit, set it beside the master ledger, and began copying.

And stopped.

Not because of the content. She hadn't read the content. She'd stopped because of the *hand*.

The chits were supposed to be written by couriers. Couriers had hands like everyone else — hurried, idiosyncratic, alive. This hand was not hurried. It was small and upright and ruthlessly even, each letter standing at attention, the crossbars of the t's drawn in single clean strokes without flourish, because flourishes wasted ink and ink cost money and money was owed to someone, always. Quentin knew that hand the way you know a scar on your own body. She had learned her letters under it. She had been made to practice it, page after page, until her own writing came out half its shape.

Her mother had been dead for nineteen years.

Quentin sat very still. The candle flame leaned and righted itself. Somewhere overhead the dog barked twice at nothing and gave up.

She should burn the chit. That was procedure — anomalous material was destroyed unread, reported to Marrow, forgotten. Anomalous material was how inquisitors traced supply lines. Anomalous material had gotten three guild members hanged in Corvane's time, before her own, in the bad years she'd only heard about in fragments.

She did not burn it.

She told herself she was examining it for forgery, which was technically true and completely false. She tilted it toward the candlelight. The ink was fresh — days old, not decades. The paper was current guild stock, watermarked with the little fish that only chapter-house stations used. Whoever had written this had sat at a guild desk within the month, with guild materials, in a dead woman's handwriting, writing a dead woman's even, patient letters.

*Never read what you record.*

Her own rule, her own armor, and here was the flaw in it shining up at her like a coin at the bottom of a well: the rule protected her from the *contents*. Nothing protected her from the hand. You could refuse to know a thing and still know the hand that wrote it. Some knowledge lived below reading, in the wrist, in the eye, in the place where a child learns what safety looks like in ink.

Four lines. She let herself see only the first word of the first line, because the first word was a cipher-marker, standard guild notation, harmless: *Received.*

Received. From whom? Delivered when? By whose living hand? Her mother's handwriting on current paper meant one of three things, and Quentin made herself count them like dishes at a tasting, cold and orderly, because orderliness was all she had: either someone in the guild could forge a hand perfectly enough to fool the person who'd learned her letters beneath it — possible, unlikely, and if so, why? Or the chit was old and the paper new, which was alchemy, which was impossible. Or —

Or her mother's hand existed somewhere outside her mother's body, the way the lullaby existed outside the nurse's mouth, the way the widow's name existed in a foreign registry, the way everything the guild carried was a thing surviving the death of its owner. Cargo. If a song could be smuggled out of a silenced country, if a name could be carried past the Index — then a hand. Then a hand could be carried too.

Quentin put the quill down because her fingers had gone strange on it.

This was the trouble with the trade, Marrow always said. Everything we move is a haunting with a bill of lading.

She finished the entry. She made herself. Cipher-number, description (*documents, four pages, sealed*), fee, initial. Her initials looked wrong next to that hand — Q.W., blunt and modern, a stranger countersigning a ghost. She blotted the line, closed the master ledger, and then, breaking another rule she'd kept for eleven years, she slid the chit out of the weekly file and into her sleeve instead of the archive box.

The stairs up were narrow and the rain had thickened. In the common room the last drinkers were leaving, and the innkeeper gave her the nod he always gave her — the nod that said *I see no one come up from nowhere at half past three* — and Quentin climbed to her rented room with the chit against her wrist like a second pulse.

She locked the door. She lit no candle. In the grey light off the harbor she unrolled the chit on the table and looked at it properly for the first time, all four lines, reading what she had recorded, and the contents were worse than the hand.

It was a delivery confirmation. Routine. *Received, one item, sealed, condition intact, fee paid.* Dated six days ago. And beneath the confirmation, in the same even hand, a note in the margin — margin notes were against guild protocol, margins were where mistakes lived — a single line:

*Tell my daughter the tide is coming in.*

Quentin stood in the dark room with the harbor breathing below the window and felt the floor tilt very slightly, the way it did after a bad tasting, the way it did when the numbness crept past the tongue and into the blood.

Six days ago. Six days ago she had tasted seven dishes at the king's table and gone home and slept, and somewhere in the world her mother's handwriting had signed for a parcel and left her a message in a margin, and every instinct she owned — the taster's instincts, the ledger-keeper's instincts, the orphan's instincts, all of them trained to a fine edge by Serle and by hunger and by fear — every one of those instincts now said the same thing:

Someone knows who I am. Someone is using her to reach me. And whatever is coming in on that tide, it is coming for me.

She folded the chit along its old creases and hid it inside the binding of her tasting ledger — the one book no inquisitor would ever seize, because seizing a royal taster's records was tantamount to confessing the king feared poison, and the Crown would dissolve a province sooner than admit that.

Outside, the rain kept whispering at the glass. Down at the Fish Gate, the tide was turning, though she couldn't have said how she knew.

Dawn was two hours off. Quentin sat down at the table, took out a fresh sheet, and began — for the first time in her career — to write an entry no courier had asked for and no rule permitted:

*Query. One chit, anomalous hand. Requesting provenance.*

She addressed it to Marrow, sealed it, and set it where the morning pickup would find it. Then she waited for daylight, listening to the tide come in, and did not sleep at all.
