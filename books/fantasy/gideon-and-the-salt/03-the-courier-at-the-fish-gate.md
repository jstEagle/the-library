## 3. The Courier at the Fish Gate

Dawn at the Fish Gate smelled the way it always did: brine and blood and the sweet rot of yesterday's catch, all of it cut through with woodsmoke from the ovens that fed the palace before the palace woke. Quentin had learned to read a city by its smells, the way other people read faces. This one said: *nothing has gone wrong yet.*

She came down to the fish market every morning she could manage it, though she had no need of fish. The palace kitchens bought what they bought, and she tasted what they bought, and her opinion was neither requested nor required. She came because it was the one hour of the day when nobody watched her eat.

That was the joke of her life, if she let herself think about it as a joke, which she mostly didn't. A woman whose profession was putting things in her mouth, who treasured the single half-hour when no one required her to swallow anything at all. She would stand at the long stone counter where the gutters worked, buy a cup of hot barley water she barely drank, and watch the boats come in with their silver-bellied cargo, and for twenty minutes she was no one's instrument. Not the Crown's. Not the guild's. Just a thin woman in a plain grey coat, standing in the cold, holding a warm cup.

The rule was never to come at the same time twice. Serle had taught her that without ever meaning to — *a taster is a creature of habit, and creatures of habit are creatures of schedule, and schedules can be poisoned* — so she varied it, arriving sometimes before the first boat, sometimes after the third, letting the market's own chaos disguise her. This morning she had come late, drawn by a sleeplessness she didn't want to name, and the light was already up, gilding the gulls, turning the harbor water the color of old pewter shot through with gold.

She was paying for the barley water when the man fell against her.

Not stumbled. Fell — deliberately, she understood even as it happened, the way a falling tree falls toward the thing it means to crush or shield. His hand closed on her wrist with a strength that surprised her, given what the rest of him was doing, which was dying. She felt the wet spread against her coat before she saw it: dark, soaking through his shirt at the side, too much of it, the kind of bleeding that doesn't wait for anyone's permission.

"Help," someone said, and it took her a moment to realize it was her own voice, thin and useless above the market noise. "Somebody help him."

But the market had already begun to do what markets do, which is to flow around trouble like water around a stone. The gutters looked away with the practiced skill of people who have learned that looking is expensive. A woman selling eels pulled her child behind her stall. Somewhere down the quay, boots were striking cobbles in unison, and everyone heard them, and everyone decided not to hear them.

The man dragged her down with him, into the lee of the counter, into the smell of fish blood and ice melt, and pressed something into her hands.

It was wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff with salt. Crusts of it, white and crystalline, had grown across the folds like frost on a window, and when she took it, some of those crystals broke away and settled on her palms, and her skin prickled where they touched — not painfully, but with a strange specificity, as if each grain knew exactly where it wanted to land.

"The door knows your blood."

His voice was wrecked, wet, barely more than breath shaped into words. Up close she could see him properly now: young, younger than her, maybe, with a courier's lean face and salt-white burn scars on his fingers, the kind you got from carrying certain kinds of cargo too long. His eyes were fixed on hers with the terrible focus of a man spending his last coins.

"You're the taster," he said. "Whitlock's girl."

"I don't—" The lie rose automatically, smooth as trained. "I don't know what you mean."

He smiled. It cracked blood at the corner of his mouth. "Nobody knows what anybody means. That's why we write things down." He pushed the package harder against her chest. "Three keys. The tide won't wait. Tell them—"

The boots arrived.

They came around the fish stalls four abreast, grey coats, grey hoods, the pale leather gloves that marked the Crown's inquisitors apart from common soldiery, and the market went silent in the way markets do — not quiet, but *performing* quiet, every voice suddenly bent on innocent commerce. Quentin knew the choreography intimately. She had watched it from the safety of the servants' hall a dozen times: the way a street died when the grey coats entered it, the way everyone became very busy being nobody.

There were three of them. The lead one was tall, with a face like a folded document, and he stopped at the edge of the spreading blood as if measuring it.

"Anyone see what happened?" His voice carried without effort. It was a mild voice. That was the worst thing about inquisitors; the mild ones were the ones who enjoyed their work.

Silence, performed beautifully.

"He came off a boat," offered the eel-seller, not looking up. "Looked bad. Fevered, maybe."

"Fevered." The inquisitor considered this. He crouched, unhurried, and turned the body's chin toward the light with two gloved fingers. Whatever he saw there made him nod slowly, as if confirming an appointment. "Search the stalls. Gently. We're looking for nothing in particular."

Which meant they were looking for everything.

Quentin had perhaps four seconds. She spent them well. The oilcloth was stiff and awkward and would show under her coat like a second spine, and there was nowhere to put it, nowhere, except—

Her tasting ledger.

It lived in an inner pocket sewn into her left sleeve, a slim bound book with a worn leather cover, and it went everywhere she went because Serle's first law was *the ledger never leaves your skin*. Every dish, every hour, every sensation, recorded in her small neat hand. No inquisitor in the world would look twice at a taster's log. It was the most boring document in the kingdom, page after page of *roast fowl, hour six, no bitterness; wine, second pour, clean; soup, faint metallic note, reported.*

She opened it against her forearm, slid the salt-crusted packet between the pages near the back where the entries thinned out, and closed it. The book bulged. Anyone holding it would feel the bulge. But no one would hold it, because no one would want to.

The tall inquisitor straightened and turned, and his gaze crossed the market like a slow net being drawn.

"You," he said.

For one vertiginous moment she thought he meant her, and her heart performed a maneuver she would later record in the ledger as *palpitation, cause unknown.* But his gloved finger had settled on the gutter-boy nearest the body, a child of perhaps ten with fish scales in his hair.

"Did he say anything? Before?"

The boy's eyes went wide and blank, the perfect emptiness of the very poor and the very smart. "Said the fish were bad today, m'lord."

"Did he." The inquisitor smiled without warmth. "Bad fish. At the Fish Gate. Someone should write a ballad." He stood, and now his gaze did find Quentin, and stopped there, and she felt it the way she felt poison on her tongue before she swallowed: a precise, professional attention. "You were standing closest."

"I was buying barley water." She held up the cup as evidence. Her hand, thank every dead god her mother had ever prayed to, was steady. Twenty years of poisons will teach a body stillness. "He fell against me. I tried to help."

"Did he give you anything?"

"A great deal of blood," Quentin said. "I'll be needing a new coat."

Something flickered in the inquisitor's face — not suspicion exactly, but the memory of suspicion, the way a scar remembers pain. He crossed to her, unhurried, and she stood her ground while every instinct screamed at her to run, because running was confession, and confession at the Fish Gate ended at the question house on Tanner's Row, where people went in with names and came out with lists.

"Your coat," he said mildly. "May I?"

"It's soaked through. You'll ruin the gloves."

"I have many gloves."

He searched her with a thoroughness that was almost impersonal — sleeves, waistband, the false seam in her collar that held her guild token, which he found and examined and returned without comment, because a tasting-token was a license, and licenses were untouchable, and they both knew it. Then his hand paused at her left sleeve, over the pocket where the ledger sat, and Quentin felt the whole world narrow to the width of a book.

"This is?"

"My tasting ledger. Palace service." She produced the words flatly, boredly, the tone of a woman asked about her work a hundred times too often. "You're welcome to read it. Page forty has my notes on Tuesday's consommé. I suspect the sous-chef of using copper pots again."

He took it. He weighed it in his palm — she watched him weigh it, watched his fingers register the slight extra thickness, and her pulse counted the seconds like a condemned woman counting steps — and then he opened it, and leafed through it, and his expression performed the exact journey she had predicted: interest, then puzzlement, then the glaze of profound tedium.

"*Herring, hour two, clean,*" he read aloud. "*Barley water, no bitterness. Faint note of tin in the third course, reported to under-cook.*" He closed it. "You people write everything down."

"Everything," Quentin agreed. "Poison is patient. So are we."

He handed it back. And here was the thing she would turn over in her mind for years afterward, in the dark, in the space between one heartbeat and the next: as he passed the ledger back, his eyes lifted to hers, and held there for one moment longer than procedure required, and in that moment there was nothing mild at all. There was a question. Not spoken. Filed.

Then he turned away, and gestured to his men, and the body was taken up on a canvas stretcher with the efficiency of routine, and the grey coats withdrew the way weather withdraws, leaving the market to exhale and resume its noise as if nothing had happened, which is what markets believe, because believing it keeps them alive.

Quentin stood very still until they were gone. Then she walked, at a perfectly ordinary pace, out of the Fish Gate, up Cooper's Lane, through the tanners' quarter where the stink covered all other stinks, and only when she reached the narrow stair behind the chandlery where she kept her rooms did she allow her knees to inform her that they had opinions.

She sat down on the stairs and unwrapped the oilcloth with shaking hands.

Inside was a map.

Or the ghost of one. It was drawn on something that wasn't quite paper and wasn't quite skin, and the ink had been mixed with salt — she could taste it in the air off the page, that same prickle she'd felt on her palms — and it showed a coastline she had never seen and yet somehow recognized, the way you recognize a face in a dream after waking. Bays and inlets and a long curling spit of land, and along the margin, in a cramped hand, a single line of text:

*The border is not a line. It is a memory, and memories must be inherited.*

And beneath the coastline, smaller, almost hidden in the cartouche's scrollwork, a word she had not seen written since she was nine years old, since the night men in grey coats came for her parents and a neighbor pressed her face into a pillow so she wouldn't cry loud enough to be remembered:

*Vessany.*

A country that did not exist. A country that had been dissolved, decreed, struck from maps and rolls and prayers twenty years ago, so thoroughly that saying its name aloud in public was worth a fine the first time and your tongue the third. Quentin had built her whole self on top of that absence, the way a city builds on landfill — carefully, knowingly, over something buried.

She should burn it. That was the sane arithmetic. A dead courier, three inquisitors, one filed question, and a package that could cost her everything: the sensible move was fire, ash, and a return to the numbing comfort of seven dishes a night.

Instead she sat on the cold stair with the map open on her knees, and her eyes kept returning to the handwriting in the margins, and the longer she looked at it the harder her heart beat, because she knew that hand. She had known it before she'd known letters. She had watched it move across shopping lists and recipe cards and the flyleaves of forbidden books, in a kitchen that smelled of dill and burnt sugar.

It was her mother's writing.

The tide of terror came back, but it came back changed, fused now with something older and less survivable, and Quentin understood, sitting in the dark behind the chandlery with salt drying white on her fingertips, that inheritance does not ask permission. It simply arrives, bleeding, at whatever gate you happen to be standing at, and puts itself in your hands.

She opened her tasting ledger to the back pages, where the entries thinned, and pressed the map flat between the records of harmless meals. Then, beneath the last entry — *barley water, dawn, no bitterness* — she wrote, in her smallest hand:

*One delivery received. Sender deceased. Contents: inherited.*

Belowstairs, the palace would be waking. Serle would expect her at the noon tasting, punctual, steady-handed, bored. She closed the ledger, buttoned it into her sleeve against her skin, and began to climb the stairs toward the longest ordinary day of her life.

Behind her, on the stair, one grain of salt remained where the map had rested, catching the light like a tiny star — and if she had looked back, she would have seen it trembling, very slightly, in time with her heartbeat.
