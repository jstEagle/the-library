## 4. What the King Eats

The shaking started at the second dish and would not stop.

Quentin held her hands low, folded into the shadow of her apron, and let them tremble where no one could see. Above the table, the king of a country that had eaten three of its neighbors in her lifetime was laughing at something his brother-in-law had said, and the laughter shook the candle flames, and the candles threw light on seven silver covers waiting in a row like small domed hills.

She tasted them in order. That was the discipline. Never skip, never guess, never let your face tell you what your tongue already knew.

The first was a cold sorrel soup, sharp enough to make her eyes water, harmless. The second — the one that started the shaking — was quail in a dark sauce, and under the wine and the fat and the burnt sugar she found it: a thread of bitterness so fine it might have been nothing, might have been the sauce's own character, might have been the fourth time this month someone had tried.

She swallowed anyway. That was also the discipline. You did not spit at the king's table. You swallowed, and you smiled, and you counted your own pulse until it told you whether you were going to die in front of the dessert course or live to wash the taste out with barley water.

Her pulse climbed, steadied, climbed again. Not hemlock. Not quicklime. Something slower, something the kitchens bought in small paper packets from a man who came on Thursdays. She filed the bitterness away the way she filed everything — precisely, without comment — and moved to the third dish.

*You are the wall between him and the world,* Serle had told her when she was nine years old, standing on a stool so she could reach the tasting bench. *Walls don't flinch.*

The third dish was eel. The fourth was bread so white it was nearly a sin. By the fifth, a braised shoulder glazed with honey and something resinous, the numbness had arrived — the familiar gray tide that crept up from her fingertips after every court dinner, turning her mouth to felt and her thoughts to wool. It was not poison. It was the accumulated cost of twenty years of swallowing small deaths for another man's appetite. Serle called it immunity. Quentin knew better. Immunity was what they called it when the damage became useful to them.

The sixth dish she barely tasted at all. The seventh was a custard, and she took her portion of it with steady hands because by then she had made them be steady, which was its own kind of poison, self-administered, and worked just as well.

The king ate everything. He always did. He was sixty-one years old and pink as a ham and he would outlive them all, because men like him were served by people like her, and the ledger of that arrangement never balanced.

* * *

Belowstairs, the palace kept its other life.

Quentin walked it the way she walked all corridors — at the pace of a woman with an errand, eyes forward, hands quiet. Past the scullery where two boys were fighting over a copper pot. Past the laundry, exhaling steam and lye. Down the stair that smelled of tallow and old stone, to the narrow room where the tasters kept their records.

It was really two rooms pretending to be one. On the long bench nearest the door lay the Crown's ledger: dishes, dates, portions, initials. Boring as gravel, and meant to be. But behind the second shelf, behind the false back Serle himself had built thirty years ago for reasons he had never explained, stood the guild's ledger — the true one, the impossible one — and Quentin sat down before it with the numbness still humming in her jaw and opened it to the week's page.

She did not read what she recorded. That was the rule, hers before it was anyone's. *Never read what you record.* A taster who reads becomes curious. A curious taster becomes careless. A careless taster becomes a name in someone else's book. So she wrote in shorthand of her own devising, entered cargo by weight and seal and route and nothing else, and if a line described something that could not exist — a song folded flat enough to post, a name peeled off a living woman and shipped north in oilcloth — she wrote it the way she wrote everything: cleanly, quickly, without letting the words mean anything to her.

Tonight the entries went fast. Three lines, four, five. Her hand knew the shapes. Her hand had done this since she was fourteen, since the night Serle first turned his back for exactly the right number of minutes, and she had understood — without being told, without anything ever being said — that the false back existed for her, and that her whole apprenticeship had been a corridor leading here.

Then she reached the sixth entry and stopped.

The seal was right. The weight was right. The route notation was in her own shorthand, correct to the letter. But the description line — the one field she permitted herself to fill in plain, because the fence required it legible — was written in a hand she knew better than her own.

Round-bellied letters. The *g* that looped too far and came home late. The little crossbar on the *t* slanted like a bird in wind.

Her mother had been dead for nineteen years. Officially erased for nineteen years — no grave, no record, no name the law would admit to having known. And here was her handwriting, fresh-inked, describing a jar of sea-salt from a province that had been drowned so thoroughly the maps now showed open water.

Quentin sat very still. Somewhere above her, the palace went on digesting its dinner. A pipe knocked in the wall. Water ran somewhere far off, patient as tide.

She should have struck the line. She should have blotted it, rewritten it in her own hand, buried the anomaly the way she buried everything. Instead she looked at it for a long time — long enough to break her own rule twice over, because she read it, and then she read it again — and the words said only: *Salt, sea-gathered, from Vessany shore. Keep for the heir.*

Keep for the heir.

The numbness in her jaw chose that moment to spread to her chest, or perhaps that was something else entirely. She closed the ledger. She pressed her palm flat against its cover, the way you'd press a hand to a wound, or a mouth.

Above her, faint through the floor, the king laughed again at something.

* * *

Serle found her at the washing bench, as she'd known he would.

He had a way of arriving that was almost tender — no sudden appearances, no shadow falling across your work. He simply came into existence beside you, the way weather does, and by the time you noticed him he had already been watching for a while. He was a big man gone soft at the edges, gray at the temples, with the careful hands of someone who had spent forty years handling things that could kill him. He set a cup of barley water next to her elbow without being asked.

"You favored the quail," he said.

"I didn't favor anything."

"Your left hand dropped half an inch on the swallow." He picked up a drying cloth and began working through the tasting spoons, one by one, laying them in their felt slots. "Half an inch, Whitlock. I've watched you swallow death for eleven years and I've seen your hand drop maybe four times. So either the quail was worse than I judged, or something else is shaking you."

"The quail."

"Mm." He laid another spoon in its slot. The clink of metal on wool was very loud in the small room. "There's a promotion," he said. "I've been sitting on it, waiting for the right night. This is the right night, I think. Or you'll tell me it isn't."

Quentin rinsed her mouth with the barley water and did not answer. Promotions, in her experience, were things that happened to other people. Tasters did not get promoted; they got retired, which was a word with two meanings in the palace, and only one of them involved a cottage.

"Permanent immunity work," Serle said. "The Privy Seal's office wants a dedicated body. Someone who takes the preventive doses daily — full regimen, not the court dribble — and eats at every sitting, every meal, state functions, private suppers, all of it. No more sharing the burden across the bench. One throat. Yours." He said it lightly, the way he announced everything, but his eyes stayed on the spoons. "It comes with rooms in the east range. Your own key. A stipend that would embarrass your current one. And—" he finally looked at her, and his face was doing something complicated that he was trying to make simple— "it means you'd never taste on the street again. No more market samples. No more errands below the hill. The palace would be your whole world, Quentin. Safe walls. Known food. Nothing in your life unaccounted for."

He said *nothing unaccounted for* the way other men said *I love you*, and she understood, with a clarity that felt like cold water, that he meant it kindly. That was the worst part. Every word of it was kindness, measured out in the exact doses he'd been administering to her since she was nine years old — enough warmth to keep her alive, never quite enough to let her grow anywhere he couldn't see.

"And if I refuse?" she asked.

"Nobody refuses the Privy Seal." He smiled. "But you wouldn't refuse. You're the most dutiful creature I've ever raised."

*I'm not yours to raise.* The thought arrived fully formed and surprised her with its heat. She kept it behind her teeth. Outside, rain had begun against the high windows, a sound like many small fingers.

Permanent immunity work. Daily full-regimen doses. She did the arithmetic without wanting to: the preventive regimen at full strength would burn through what remained of her tolerance within the year, and after that there would be no more trembling hands to hide, because there would be no more hands that answered her. She had seen the old dedicated taster, decades back, a woman named Odile who by the end could eat arsenic with jam and weep without knowing why. They had carried Odile out of the east range in a chair, smiling.

And the rooms. The key. The walls. A life with nothing unaccounted for — meaning a life in which every hour was accounted for by someone else. He was offering her a cage, and gilding it, and calling the gilding honor, and the terrible thing, the thing that made her want to put her fist through the washing bench, was that some tired animal part of her wanted to say yes. To lie down in the warm straw of it. To stop running routes at dawn and keeping double books and swallowing bitterness in the dark. To be safe, if safety was what this was.

"You've been shaking since the quail," Serle said quietly. "And you went belowstairs after service instead of going straight to your bunk, which you never do on heavy-tasting nights, because I taught you better. So." He set down the last spoon. "Something happened tonight. And whatever it was, the palace can hold it. Whatever it is, I can hold it. That's what the offer means, underneath the language. Let me carry it."

For one insane moment she nearly did. Nineteen years of orphanhood leaned toward him like a plant toward a window. *My mother's handwriting is in my ledger. My mother has been dead since before I could write. Tell me how that can be. Tell me what you know about salt from a drowned shore.*

Instead she said: "When do they want an answer?"

"A week. The Seal's office is patient." He gathered his cloth, folded it into thirds the way he folded everything. At the door he paused, and did not turn around, and said: "Whatever you swallowed tonight — I'd have caught it. You know that. There's nothing at that table that gets past me to you. There never has been."

He left. The rain kept up its small blind drumming. Quentin stood at the bench with the barley water going cold in the cup, and understood that he had just told her, in the only language he trusted, that he had been watching her longer than she had ever known — that even her poisons were supervised, even her dangers curated, even her fear administered.

A cage dressed as honor. A kindness with bars in it.

She tipped the barley water out into the sluice and watched it run, and thought about a jar of salt from a shore that no longer existed, marked *keep for the heir*, in a dead woman's hand.

One week. The Seal's office was patient. But dawn was only a few hours off, and dawn was when the fish gate smelled of brine and the watch changed over and nobody looked hard at a woman in a taster's apron walking fast with her collar up.

Whatever was happening to her had started reaching through paperwork. She intended to be at the fish gate before it reached any further.
