## 7. Serle's Ledger

The inquisitors kept their offices where the palace kept its shame: below the waterline, in the old cisterns that had fed the fountains of a dynasty nobody would name anymore. Serle descended the wet stairs with his ledger under one arm and his hands folded behind his back, because a man who carried poison for a living learned early that hands should always be visible or always empty, and he had chosen empty.

The room at the bottom smelled of lamp oil and cold stone. Three of them sat waiting behind a table too long for three, which was a trick — make the furniture outnumber you and the visitor feels audited before anyone speaks. The middle one was called Inquisitor Halden, though Serle privately thought of him as the Clerk, because everything about him was ruled lines: the part in his hair, the crease of his collar, the way he squared a sheet of paper to the table's edge before looking up.

"Taster-Master Serle." The Clerk did not rise. "You asked for this meeting."

"I did."

"Unusual. Most people wait to be summoned."

"Most people," Serle said, "have something to hide."

He set the ledger on the table. Not the guild's ledger — never that; he had never seen it and had spent twenty years making sure no one could prove otherwise. This was his own book, bound in grey calfskin, its corners worn round from a pocket. Twenty years of entries in a small, disciplined hand. He opened it to the first marked page and turned it to face them.

The Clerk read. His colleagues leaned in. For a while there was only the sound of paper and the drip of water somewhere back in the dark of the cisterns.

"This is a record of dosages," the Clerk said at last.

"It is a record of a child," Serle said. "Dosage by dosage. Year by year."

* * *

He had started when she was six.

That was the fact the ledger began with, and it was the fact he could not soften, so he had never tried. A six-year-old girl brought to him by the Crown's wardens after her parents were taken, thin as a reed, with her mother's dark eyes and her father's stubborn jaw, and an order on warden's paper that said, in the flat language such orders used: *assess suitability.*

Suitability. As if she were soil.

Serle had been taster-master for nine years by then. He knew what the order meant. The Crown had arrested two of Old Vessany's most notorious memory-smugglers and discovered they had a daughter, and the daughter was either a liability to be quietly drowned in some workhouse cistern or an asset to be grown like a plant toward the light the Crown preferred. Someone upstairs had decided to see which.

So he had begun.

The first entry, in his own hand, dated the third of Thaw-month: *0.25 grain, bitterleaf extract, in honeyed milk. Subject reports headache. No other effect.* He had watched her drink it. He had held her hair back when she vomited afterward, and told her it was a medicine, and hated himself with a thoroughness that surprised him, and done it again the next week.

"You poisoned a child," said the inquisitor on the Clerk's left. A heavy man, Vosse, with rings that clicked against the table when he moved his hands. "For the Crown."

"I tested a child," Serle said. "For the Crown. The poisoning came later, and it was hers before it was mine." He turned the pages. "Week four. Week nine. Month six. I increased the doses as her body learned each one. By age eight she could take enough bitterleaf to stop a grown man's heart for a full minute and walk away from it. By ten, salts. By twelve, the compounds we don't name in front of junior staff. She has never once been sick at the king's table. Do you know why?"

"Immunity," said the Clerk.

"Discipline," said Serle. "The immunity is just what discipline looks like from the outside. Every dose I gave her, I also taught her to take — how to breathe through it, how to eat around the taste, how to lie with her face while her stomach screamed. I built her the way you build a wall. Stone by stone. And every stone was a crime I committed against a child who trusted me, and I have written down every single one, because a man who won't write down his crimes is a man planning to commit them again."

Vosse grunted. "And why bring us this confession now? You've had twenty years to burn that book."

"Because the book isn't the confession." Serle reached across and turned to the last written page. "This is."

* * *

The last page was recent. Four days old. It recorded a single line: *Subject's hands unsteady at evening tasting. Cause unknown. Watch.*

"The courier died at the Fish Gate four days ago," Serle said. "You know this. Your own men found the body. What your men did not find was whatever the courier was carrying, because the courier found my taster first, and my taster hid it, and my taster has been lying to me ever since with a steadiness of face I should be proud of if it didn't frighten me."

"You believe she took something from the body," the Clerk said.

"I believe she took a map." He watched their faces. Good. They hadn't known. "A salt-crusted map, hidden inside her tasting ledger, which is the one place in this palace no one searches, because everyone knows the taster's ledger is sacred. Clever girl. I taught her that too — that a ledger is a kind of armor. I just never taught her to use mine against me."

The third inquisitor, a woman named Ashe who had not yet spoken, finally lifted her eyes from the ledger. "How do you know it's a map?"

"Because of what she did next." Serle took out a second paper, folded small. "My network of kitchen spies costs the Crown forty crowns a month and is worth every one. On the second day after the courier died, she went to Marrow. The fence. The one who moves cargo through the fish market."

"We know Marrow."

"Then you know he deals in things that don't officially exist. And you know what kind of map a dying smuggler bleeds on." He unfolded the paper and laid it beside the ledger. It was a rubbing — charcoal on cloth — of a fragment of wall in the fish market, where someone had scratched a coastline into the stone. "Marrow sells information twice, same as everyone. He sold it to her, and he sold it to me, and I paid more, so I got the detail. That coastline appears on no chart in this kingdom. It matches nothing. Except—" he tapped the rubbing— "except the drowned provinces. The ones that stopped existing twenty years ago. The ones whose names your office strikes from documents for a living."

Silence. Water dripped in the dark.

"She is the daughter of Vessany smugglers," Serle said. "Her parents are dead. A courier dies at her feet and gives her a map to a border that officially cannot exist. You do the arithmetic. I've done it every night for four days and it comes out the same every time. She is heir to something. And whatever it is, the door to it opens soon, because these things open on tides, and the tide-tables say the great salt-tide comes in eleven days."

Ashe leaned forward. "You want us to arrest her."

"No." The word came out harder than he intended, and he let it stay hard. "I want you to do exactly what I tell you, in exactly the order I tell you, and nothing else. Because I have spent twenty years building the only person alive who can survive what's coming, and I will not watch three clerks with wet boots ruin her in an afternoon."

* * *

They let him talk for another hour. That was the thing about inquisitors — they were patient the way drains are patient. He walked them through it.

She would run. Of course she would run; he'd have run. But she would run along guild roads, and the guild was already broken — his spies confirmed the chapter-house emptied two nights ago, arrests made, and he noted, without inflection, that the arrests had produced ledgers, and that the ledgers were in the Crown's hands now, and that if anyone in this room thought about what those ledgers contained, they would understand why Quentin Whitlock had perhaps eleven days of freedom left and no more.

"The map is the key to finding her," Serle said. "Not the man. Not the boy. The map. She'll guard it past all sense. People die for maps to doors; it's the oldest story there is. So we don't chase her. We let her lead us to the door, and then we close the door, and then—" he paused— "and then she comes home."

"And if she doesn't come home?" Vosse asked.

Serle looked at him for a long moment. "Then the country gets a new taster-master," he said, "and I get a grave, and neither of us will have deserved it."

The Clerk squared his papers. "You're asking us to trust you with her."

"I'm asking you to trust twenty years of results. She has tasted for the king since she was fourteen. Seven hundred meals. Not one incident. Not one tremor until four days ago. Whatever else I am, gentlemen, madam, I am the man who built the safest instrument this Crown owns, and I am telling you the instrument has picked up a signal." He closed his ledger. "Follow the signal. Leave the instrument to me."

* * *

They agreed, in the way that offices agree — which is to say they agreed to let him try, and reserved the right to drown him later if he failed. Serle climbed the wet stairs feeling the familiar weight settle onto his shoulders, the weight he had carried so long it had become part of his skeleton: the weight of loving something you were also holding prisoner.

Because that was the truth under all of it, the truth the ledger recorded page by page without ever once writing it down. He had tested poisons on a six-year-old because the Crown ordered it, yes. But he had volunteered to keep testing them. He had requested guardianship. He had raised her in the rooms above the tasting-kitchen, taught her letters, taught her wines, taught her which courtiers smiled with their teeth. He had watched her grow up in the exact shape of the parents the Crown destroyed, and he had told himself every year that the doses were protection — build her immunity, build her armor, keep her safe the only way a taster knows how — and some nights, most nights, he almost believed it.

But he had also taught her never to leave the palace. Had arranged the promotion offer, the permanent post, the honor that was a lock. Had reported her childhood friendships to wardens until she stopped making them. Had built her a life so narrow and so safe that it fit inside a cage, and called the cage a career.

He loved her. That was the worst of it. Love that had nowhere to go, so it went into control, the way floodwater goes into cracks.

At the top of the stairs, in the gray light of morning, he stopped and took out the ledger and wrote one more line, because the ledger demanded honesty even when he couldn't afford it:

*Day 4. Subject believes herself free. Subject is wrong. I am sorry. Proceeding anyway.*

He blotted it, pocketed the book, and went to find out which gate she would leave by — because a girl carrying a salt-map would need a fence, and a fence needed a buyer, and Serle had already bought the only road out of the city that mattered.

By nightfall he would know her direction. By tomorrow, the inquisitors would too.

And somewhere ahead of her, on a road through country that did not exist, the trap was already patient, already quiet, already shaped exactly like kindness.
