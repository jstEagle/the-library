## 8. A Country That No Longer Exists

The road out of the capital was called the King's Spine, and it ran straight as a needle for forty miles before remembering that the land it crossed had never agreed to be ruled.

Quentin walked it with Finch a half-step behind her, the way he'd walked since the chapter-house, since the debt-token warm in her pocket. He didn't ask where they were going. She hadn't told him. She wasn't sure herself — only that the map's first clue had pulled them west, toward hills that appeared on no survey she'd ever tasted in the palace archives, and that every mile put more distance between her and Serle's kind, worried eyes.

"You keep saying words under your breath," Finch said around midday. "I can't make them out."

"I'm not saying anything."

"You are. You've been doing it since the fish gate." He kicked a stone off the road with unnecessary violence. "My mam used to do that. Talk to herself when she thought no one could hear. She said it kept the quiet from settling."

Quentin realized she'd been mouthing *Vessany* over and over, like a prayer or an antidote. She closed her mouth. The word sat behind her teeth anyway, patient as poison.

They ate hard bread and harder cheese at a milestone that read, in worn imperial script, MILE 61 — VESSANY PROVINCE BOUNDARY. Someone had chiseled the second line out decades ago, but stone keeps its scars, and Quentin could read the ghost of the letters the way she read ghosts in everything now: by what was missing.

"Here," she said, and stopped.

"Here what?"

She looked down the road ahead. It bent through low grey hills, gorse and bracken, a shepherd's hut with its roof caved in. Ordinary country. Forgettable country. The kind of land cartographers skimmed because there was nothing to steal from it.

Then she said, aloud, deliberately: "Old Vessany."

The world *twitched*.

It was not dramatic. That was the horror of it. A hillside that had been bare grew, for one blink, a line of drystone wall. A track that wasn't there became briefly a lane, hedged with elder. In the cleft between two hills she saw — she would swear on any ledger — a chimney smoking, a thread of woodsmoke rising from a house that did not exist, and then did not exist again.

Finch made a sound like he'd been struck. "What — did you see—"

"The smoke." Her heart was slamming. "You saw it too?"

"There was a—" He pressed his fists against his eyes. "There was a village. Down there. I could hear someone singing. Then it went." He lowered his hands and stared at her with something between fear and awe. "It happens when you say it. When you said the name."

*The door knows your blood.* The courier's last words, salt-crusted into her memory. And now this: a country that existed only in the mouths of people willing to speak it.

"It flickers," she said slowly, working it through the way she worked through a dish with an unfamiliar bitterness — isolate the note, name it, decide if it kills. "The land remembers. But nobody's left to hold the memory steady. So it only shows when someone says the name. We're — we're the candle. Speaking it is lighting it."

Finch looked at the empty hills a long moment. "Then why does everyone burn for remembering it?"

"Because a candle in a dark room is either a comfort or a threat," Quentin said, "depending on who wants the room dark."

They walked until the light went copper, speaking the name at intervals like taking a medicine at prescribed hours. Each time, the country surfaced: a well with a rope still swinging, a field of barley that rippled though no wind reached it, a road-sign in a hand older than the empire reading WELCOME TO VESSANY, TRAVELLER, MIND THE TIDE. Each time it sank again, and each time the sinking hurt worse, like watching someone drown in slow motion, repeatedly, and being unable to dive after them.

By dusk Finch had stopped flinching. By full dark he was doing it himself, whispering *Vessany* at the landscape like feeding crumbs to something shy.

"It likes it," he said.

"It's starving," Quentin corrected. "Liking comes later. If it survives."

The woman's cottage found them rather than the reverse — which, Quentin would think later, should have told her everything. One moment they were stumbling through gorse in the dark; the next there was a lit window where no lit window had been, and a door opening, and a voice saying:

"You'll be wanting shelter, and I'll be wanting news. Come in before the tide turns."

Inside was a single room, immaculate, hung with dried herbs Quentin could not identify in any of the empire's four pharmacopoeias. A woman stood at the hearth, sixty perhaps, with iron-grey hair braided crownwise in a style Quentin had seen only once before — on the map, in the marginal drawing of a woman holding a key.

"You knew we were coming," Quentin said.

"I knew *someone* was coming. The walls have been humming since noon." The woman ladled something from a pot — nettle and smoked fish, Quentin's tongue reported automatically, safe, safe, a trace of wild garlic, nothing meant to harm — and set two bowls down. "Sit. Eat. I'm Brannoc. I was a citizen of nowhere for twenty years, and I've gotten good at knowing when nowhere sends visitors."

"Citizen of nowhere?"

"That's what the edict made us." Brannoc sat, unhurried, as if her joints had all the time in the world. "Dissolution Edict, year one. They didn't just erase the border. They erased the *citizenship*. Every soul born inside Old Vessany's lines woke up legally belonging to no country at all. Not exiles — exiles belong somewhere else, painfully. We belonged nowhere, painlessly, on paper. Which meant no law protected us and no law could see us." She smiled without warmth. "You'd be amazed how many things can be done to a person no law can see."

Quentin tasted the stew. It was good. It was also, faintly, unmistakably, seasoned with salt she recognized — the same mineral ghost she'd caught in the king's seventh dish, the same salt from the drowned province. Her stomach turned.

"Where do you get your salt?" she asked.

Brannoc's eyes sharpened. "Trade question or heir question?"

The word landed like a dropped knife. "*Heir?*"

"Oh, sit properly, girl, you'll spill." Brannoc waved her spoon. "A bleeding courier dies at the fish gate pressing a map into your hands. Within a day, the guild chapter-house is emptied. Within two, you're walking west along the old boundary saying a dead country's name to the hills, and the hills are answering. Who else would I conclude you are?" She leaned forward. "Your mother had your chin. Less suspicion in it, back then. More hope."

"You knew my mother."

"I bought salt from her." Something moved behind Brannoc's face, quick as the flickering hills. "Everyone in the trade knew Odile Whitlock. Best courier the guild ever had. Carried cargo no one else could even lift — memories, mostly. Heavy things." She stirred her stew without eating. "She came here the winter before they took her. Said she was hiding something so large she had to split it into pieces and hide the pieces in different kinds of forgetting. A map in salt. A key in a debt. A bell in water. I told her she was mad." Brannoc looked up. "I was wrong. She was thorough."

The fire cracked. Outside, very faintly, Quentin heard — or imagined she heard — the sea, though they were three days from any coast.

"She hid them," Quentin said carefully, "in different kinds of forgetting."

"That's how you hide something from an empire that confiscates memory, girl. You don't bury it deeper. You bury it in a *kind* of forgetting they don't police." Brannoc counted on rough fingers. "Salt forgets upward — it rises, it seasons, it gets eaten. Debt forgets sideways — it passes hand to hand till no one recalls whose it was. Water forgets downward — it drowns, it keeps, it gives back only to those who grieve properly." She pointed the spoon at Quentin. "Your mother built a lock with three tumblers, and each tumbler is a way of losing something. And she gave the map to a dying man to carry to the one person alive who could taste her handwriting in it."

Quentin set down her bowl. Her hands were steady. Twenty years of training. "Why tell me this?"

"Because you'll need to know what you're carrying before Corvane finds you."

The name went through the room like cold air through a broken pane. Finch's head came up sharply. "You know Corvane?"

"Everyone in the trade knows Corvane." Brannoc's voice flattened. "And everyone in the trade knows what he sold. Your father didn't betray the guild, whatever that man tells you. Aldric Whitlock died refusing to sell the door. Corvane was the one holding the contract." She rose, took a candle stub from a shelf, and pressed it into Quentin's hand along with a twist of paper. "Salt from the drowned harbor. Keep it dry. When the tide comes — and it's coming soon, girl, the walls have been humming tide-songs for a week — you'll want a taste of home in your pocket."

"Why help us?" Finch asked. "You don't know us."

Brannoc looked at him, then at Quentin, and her face did something complicated and old.

"Twenty years ago I woke up a citizen of nowhere," she said. "No law saw me. No map held me. Do you know what kept me *existing*, boy? Not food. Not shelter. Once a month, sometimes, a stranger would look at me — actually look — and say something like *you were from the coast road, weren't you, near the white chimneys*. And for a day afterward I'd be real. Being remembered is the only citizenship some of us ever got back." She turned to the fire. "So no, I don't know you. But you're carrying my country in your pocket, and I'd sooner it survive than me."

*

They slept in shifts. Quentin took first watch by the window, the candle stub and salt twist in her pocket, the map against her ribs.

Near midnight, Finch spoke from his blanket, half-asleep. "Quentin. When we open the door. What's on the other side?"

She had an answer prepared — escape, exile, the guild rebuilt beyond the border, all the careful arithmetic of survival. She looked out the window instead, at the hills, and said the name softly, testing:

"Old Vessany."

Below the window, in the dark, a whole street lit up. Lamps in windows. A dog barking. A woman's laugh, cut off mid-note as the vision sank, and the silence after it was the worst thing Quentin had ever heard.

"I don't know," she said honestly. "But I think it's less a door out than a door *back*."

Finch mumbled something and slept. Quentin watched the hills and did not watch them, and somewhere past the deepest hour of night she saw, at the far edge of the flickering fields, a rider on the ridge line — motionless, patient, watching the cottage the way a man watches a pot he knows will boil.

He wore no colors. He needed none.

Corvane had found them, and he was in no hurry at all.
