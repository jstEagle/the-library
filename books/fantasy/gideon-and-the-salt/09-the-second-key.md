## 9. The Second Key

The chapel had drowned a century ago and never learned it was dead.

Quentin stood at the tree line with Finch breathing too fast beside her and looked down at what the flood had left: a roofline breaking the water like a whale's spine, a bell tower leaning at an angle that defied argument, its crown just above the surface. The water was the color of old pewter. Nothing moved on it. Not a bird, not an insect, not even the wind's reflection.

"That's it?" Finch said. "That's the famous flooded chapel?"

"It's on the map."

"The map is salt. The map is *crackers*." He said it loudly, the way boys do when they want the world to hear them and disagree. "We walked three days for a church at the bottom of a pond."

"Keep your voice down."

"Why? There's no one here. That's the whole problem with here." But he lowered his voice anyway, because he was learning her, and she hated how pleased that made her feel.

She took out the map — Marrow's copy of the courier's map, drawn in ink that shimmered faintly, like oil on water — and held it toward the tower. The lines of ink crawled, rearranged themselves, and settled into new instructions: *Descend where the bell once hung. The bell remembers what the law forgot.*

"The bell once hung," Quentin read aloud, "in the tower."

"So we climb the tower."

"We descend the tower." She folded the map away before it could change again. It did that sometimes now, since Salt on the Tongue; it seemed to be deciding things about her. "The flood came up through the crypt. The chapel was built over an older chapel, which was built over something older still. The bell didn't fall. It sank with everything else."

Finch looked at the water for a long moment. "How deep?"

"Deep enough."

"You're not going to say anything useful, are you. Ever. For the rest of this journey."

"I'm going to say *swim*."

* * *

The water took her in stages. First her boots, then her hips, then the shock of it closing over her shoulders — cold enough to make her ribs feel like a fist around her lungs. She'd tasted worse. That was the strange arithmetic of her life: she had swallowed things designed to kill her so many times that cold water felt like a courtesy.

Below the surface, the world went green and silent. The chapel's windows were still intact, somehow — glass thick with silt, glowing like the eyes of drowned animals. Pews drifted in loose formation, soft with rot. A saint's statue stood in a niche with its hands broken off, its face worn to a suggestion, and Quentin thought: *that's what erasure looks like up close.* Not fire. Water. Patience. The country that no longer exists hadn't been burned out of the maps so much as soaked out, dissolved, allowed to drift apart until no one could say where one piece ended.

The bell rope was still there.

It hung from the darkness above the tower's open heart, furred with weed, swaying very slightly in a current she couldn't feel from outside. She followed it down. The tower's interior was a stone throat, and she descended through it hand over hand, the rope slimy against her palms, her ears aching, the light behind her shrinking to a coin.

At the bottom, the rope ended at a wooden platform — a belfry floor, submerged but sound, tilted a few degrees like a ship settling. And on the platform stood the bell.

It was smaller than she expected. Chapel bells were supposed to be enormous, bronze-throated, loud enough to call a village. This one would have fit in a market basket. It was dark iron, unornamented, and it sat upside down on the platform like a thing asleep, or playing dead.

Quentin surfaced beside the platform and hauled herself onto it, gasping, hair plastered to her face. Above her, the tower shaft rose thirty feet to a disc of grey daylight. The air trapped under the belfry roof was stale and sweet, mushroom-sweet.

"Finch," she called up. Her voice bounced strangely off the water. "Stay at the top. Watch the shore."

A pause, then his voice, thin and echoing: "Watch it for *what*?"

"Inquisitors. Corvane. Anything that moves."

"What am I supposed to do if anything moves?"

"*Run*, Finch."

Another pause. "Right," he said, in the tone of someone who has decided not to argue with a drowning woman. His footsteps faded along the ruined gallery above.

She turned back to the bell.

Up close, she could see writing on it — a band of script circling the crown, fine as engraving, in a language she shouldn't have been able to read and found, with a lurch, that she could. Old Vessanic. The court script of a dissolved country, the alphabet the inquisitors scraped off gravestones. Her mother had written grocery lists in it, humming.

*This bell rings only for those the law forgot,* the band read. *To lift it, leave a memory the law cannot take.*

"Well," Quentin said to the bell. "You're a cheerful object."

The bell did not answer. Bells rarely do, though she was beginning to lose confidence in her categories.

She gripped it by the lip and pulled.

It didn't move. It weighed nothing and everything at once — her hands slid on wet iron as though the bell were actively declining to be lifted, the way a cat goes boneless. She braced her boots against the platform and pulled until her shoulders screamed, and the bell sat there, patient, upside down, immovable as a fact.

Then she understood, and stopped pulling, and knelt in the shallow water on the platform with her heart going hard.

*A memory the law cannot take.*

The first key had cost Finch his debt-token — cheap, in the end, because Finch had already decided to burn it. The second key wanted something of hers. Something true.

She sat back on her heels and let the silence of the drowned chapel press in, and she made herself think about her mother, because there was nowhere else to look.

Here was the problem: Quentin Whitlock had built her whole self out of discipline. Never read what you record. Never taste without cataloguing. Never love what you carry. Twenty years of rules, each one mortared onto the last, and underneath them all, one load-bearing memory she had never once examined because examining it might crack it.

Her mother, in the kitchen of the house in Old Vessany that officially had never existed, teaching her the difference between salts.

Not poison. Salts. Quentin had been six, maybe seven, standing on a stool so she could reach the table, and her mother had laid out little dishes like a jeweler: grey salt from the marshes, pink salt from the cliffs, black salt that wasn't really salt at all but ash and trickery. And her mother — who had a laugh like a door opening — had said, *taste, little taster, and tell me which one is lying.*

And Quentin had tasted, and the black one had lied, and her mother had laughed that door-opening laugh and said, *good, good, you'll survive them all someday,* and Quentin had not known what she meant and had laughed too, because her mother was laughing, and that was the whole of the memory: salt dishes, a stool, a laugh, a sentence that only made sense twenty years later when a courier died at her feet saying *the door knows your blood.*

Her mother had known. Had been preparing her. Had looked at a six-year-old and seen a survivor of poisons not yet invented.

Quentin knelt in the flooded belfry and cried the way she did everything — quietly, efficiently, with an eye on the exits. The tears mixed with the floodwater, which didn't care, which had already taken everything else in this building and would take this too if she let it pool.

Because that was the price. She saw it now, clear as the script on the iron. To lift the bell she had to give up a true memory, and the law couldn't take this one because the law had already tried — had erased the house, the village, the province, the name — and failed, because it lived in her. It was the last copy. The bell wanted the last copy.

"There are others," she told the bell, reasonably. "I remember her face. I remember her hands. I remember—"

But even as she said it, she knew those were different memories. The face was a portrait; this was a morning. You can lose a portrait and keep the person. Lose the morning and you lose the proof that the person ever turned toward you and laughed on purpose.

The bell waited. It was very good at waiting. It had been waiting under the water for a hundred years.

"I hate you," Quentin told it, with feeling.

Somewhere above, distantly, Finch sneezed.

She put her hands on the bell one more time — not to lift it, just to hold it, the way you hold a hand before letting go — and she spoke the memory aloud, because it seemed right that it should go somewhere other than just *out*. She described the kitchen. The stool. The seven dishes of salt arranged in a crescent. The grey one honest, the pink one shy, the black one lying through its crystals. Her mother's sleeves rolled to the elbow. The laugh. *Taste, little taster, and tell me which one is lying.* The word *someday*, hanging in the air like a promise neither of them had read carefully.

"And I choose to give it," she said. "Nobody takes it. Understand that. If anyone asks — if *you* ask, later, whatever you are — I gave it. That's the entry. Witnessed."

The bell grew warm under her hands.

Not hot. Warm, the way a stone is warm after a body leaves it. And then it rang — once, silently, a note she felt in her teeth and sternum rather than heard, a note that passed through the water and the stone and the drowned pews and out into the country that no longer existed, and for one moment the whole flooded valley *answered*. Every sunken thing hummed. The dead glass in the windows lit briefly from inside, like lanterns behind skin. Somewhere far below the platform, in the crypt beneath the crypt, voices — dozens, hundreds, a congregation's worth — sighed all together, the way a crowd sighs when a door finally opens.

The bell came up off the platform as easily as lifting a cup.

Quentin held it and felt the shape of the hole where the morning had been. It was a clean hole, oddly. Not painful, exactly. Just absent — a room in her she could walk into and find empty, and know something had lived there, and not know what. She tested the edges of it, probed it the way her tongue probes a missing tooth, and found she could remember *that* her mother had taught her salts, but not the teaching. Could remember the laugh existed, but not hear it.

"Gone," she said aloud. Her voice sounded wrong in the tower, flattened, like a coin rubbed smooth. "Good. Fine. One key left."

She slung the bell inside her coat, against her chest, where it lay warm as a sleeping animal, and started up the bell rope.

* * *

Finch was waiting at the waterline, soaked to the waist, furious with relief.

"You were down there forever. I counted to four thousand. I ran out of numbers I trust."

"Did anything move?"

"A fish moved. I nearly died of it." He squinted at her coat, at the bulge over her heart. "Got it, then?"

"Yes."

"What'd it cost?"

She looked at him. He was fourteen and sharp-eyed and had already lost a guild, a city, and any reasonable expectation of safety, and he asked questions like a ledger clerk because that was the trade they shared.

"A memory," she said. "Of my mother."

His face did something complicated. "Is it — I mean. Do you miss it?"

"I don't know yet. That's the trick of it. You can't miss what you can't find." She wrung out her hair, watching the water run dark onto the mud. "I remember that I loved her. I remember that she taught me things. I just can't remember the afternoon anymore. There was an afternoon. I know there was an afternoon."

Finch was quiet for a while. Then, awkwardly, the way boys give gifts: "I'll remember you had it. The afternoon. Somebody should keep the record, right? That's the whole point of us."

Quentin felt something crack slightly in her chest, in a good way, which was worse. "Yes," she said. "Somebody should keep the record."

They walked inland, away from the drowned chapel, toward the high ground where Marrow's notes said the third key slept — and Quentin stopped so suddenly that Finch walked into her back.

On the path ahead, sitting on a milestone as if he'd been there for hours, was Corvane.

He was smiling. He had a fire going, small and neat, and something cooking over it that smelled appallingly good, and he said, in the warm voice of a man offering shelter:

"You look hungry. Both of you. Sit down, and let's talk about your father."
