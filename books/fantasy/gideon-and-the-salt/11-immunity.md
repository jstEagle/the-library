## 11. Immunity

They took her at the fish gate, which she supposed was fitting. Everything in her life had begun to smell of brine and endings.

There were six of them, gray-cloaked, moving with the unhurried confidence of men who had never once been told no by a city. Quentin had time to set down the basket of eels she'd been carrying — a prop, a cover, an ordinary woman's errand — before the first one reached her. She even had time to notice that the youngest of them hesitated, just slightly, when he saw her face.

"You're the taster," he said. Not a question. "Whitlock."

"I'm a woman buying eels."

"You're the king's taster." He said it louder, for the benefit of the market square, and the word *taster* did its work: people looked away. Nobody wanted to be remembered having seen the poison-woman taken. "Serle vouches for you. That's the only reason you're walking instead of carried."

*Serle.* The name landed in her chest like a swallowed stone. Of course it was Serle. He had been watching her hands shake over the salt map's clue; he had smelled the sea on her that wasn't the harbor's sea. He had raised her from a child who couldn't hold a spoon without spilling, taught her palate every bitterness in the capital's pharmacopoeia, and he had sold her to the gray cloaks with what she was certain was a heavy heart.

That was the thing about Serle she could never get around. His betrayals were always tender.

They walked her through streets she knew better than her own pulse, past the guild-house where Finch might or might not still be hiding, past Marrow's shuttered stall, up the long hill to the Inquisitory, which everyone called the Lantern because of the light that burned in its topmost window all night, every night, and had done since the year Old Vessany was struck from the maps. They fed her bread and water in a cell with no straw and left her alone with her own heartbeat for what felt like most of a day.

She used the time the way Serle had taught her, back when she was nine and crying after her first dose: *Breathe low, girl. Fear is a flavor too. Learn it and it can't surprise you.*

So she learned this fear. She turned it on her tongue. It tasted, she decided, faintly of copper and cold iron, with a finish like unwritten paper.

Then they came for her with the serum.

* * *

The interrogation room was round, which she hadn't expected. She had imagined the Lantern's rooms as angular, all corners and edges, but this one was a perfect circle with a domed ceiling painted with clouds, so that a man strapped to the chair beneath it might look up and believe, briefly, that he was outdoors. Someone had thought about that. Someone had thought about it carefully, and that frightened her more than the instruments laid out on their velvet — the hooks, the pincers, the little brass funnel.

The instruments were theater. She understood theater. The serum was the play's whole plot.

It sat in a glass vial on a wheeled table, and it was the color of weak tea, and Quentin knew it by sight the way a priest knows his god: *veritas nigrum*, black-truth, distilled from moonshade root and something they never wrote down. One drop loosened the tongue. Three drops made liars weep out their secrets in detail. Five drops stopped the heart of anyone whose body hadn't learned to fight it.

Inquisitor Dane — she learned his name because he introduced himself, courteously, the way a headsman does — held the vial up to the lamplight and smiled at her.

"Quentin Whitlock. Twenty-six years old. Taster to His Majesty's table for eleven years, apprentice before that under Master Serle." He recited it like grace before meat. "You understand why you're here."

"Someone thinks I know something."

"Someone knows you have something." He set the vial down. "A map. Salt-crusted. Taken from a dying courier at the fish gate four days ago. We've been patient, Mistress Whitlock. Patient is not the same as kind."

"I don't have any map."

"Of course not." He drew a chair opposite hers and sat, knees nearly touching hers, and leaned in with the intimacy of a confessor. "Here is what will happen. I will give you five drops. Your body will tell me everything your mouth won't. Where the map is. Who gave it to you. What the guild has been carrying through my city for twenty years. And then, if you're fortunate, I'll write down only some of it." He paused. "Master Serle has asked to be present. He says you'll be frightened. He says you trust him."

Her stomach went cold and stayed cold. "And you let him?"

"He raised you. A familiar face calms the subject." Dane shrugged. "Also, he begged. It was almost moving."

They brought Serle in ten minutes later, and he would not look at her. That was how she knew how bad it was. Serle looked at everything — at her posture, her pupils, the tremor in her eyelids, the sweat at her hairline; reading bodies was his religion and she was his best-read text. Now he fixed his gaze on the middle distance above her head and stood by the wall with his hands folded, an old man attending a funeral he had organized himself.

"Quentin," he said quietly.

"Don't." Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "Don't do the voice you use when I'm sick."

He flinched. Dane watched them both with the pleasure of a man watching two clocks agree.

The straps went on her wrists and ankles. The funnel was brass and cold and shaped like a lily, and Dane tilted her head back with a gentleness that was worse than violence, and the first drop hit her tongue and slid down her throat like winter.

"Breathe low," Serle murmured from the wall. "Girl. Breathe low."

She breathed low.

* * *

Here is what no one in the room understood, because no one in the room had spent twenty years being poisoned on purpose:

Quentin's blood was a garrison.

Every morning of her childhood, Serle had come to her cot with a spoon and a smile and a new small horror — hemlock water, arsenic dust, the grey mold from bad rye, the leaf that numbs, the berry that stops breath. He had called it training. He had called it duty. He had held her hair while she vomited and dosed her again the next dawn, building in her, drop by drop, a body that no longer recognized poison as an event. Her liver had grown into something a physician would pay to study. Her blood carried antidotes to toxins that didn't officially exist. When the court physicians tested her annually, they pronounced her a marvel, a natural, a gift.

*A gift.* Chosen, ordered, commissioned — though she wouldn't learn by whom until much later, and wouldn't believe it until later still.

Black-truth was a poison. That was its whole mechanism: it poisoned the mind's doorkeeper, the little sentry that stands between what you know and what you say. And Quentin's body had spent two decades learning to refuse exactly such visitors.

The first drop died somewhere behind her sternum. She felt it go — a brief cold bloom, then nothing, her blood rising up around it like a city closing its gates.

Dane waited. Counted. Tilted the second drop between her lips.

Nothing.

By the fourth drop, Dane's courtesy had curdled. By the fifth, he was holding the empty vial upside down as if the failure lived in the glass, and Serle had finally, finally looked at her — and what was on his face was not disappointment.

It was recognition. It was the look of a man seeing, for the first time, the full shape of the thing he had built.

"She's immune," Dane said flatly. "How is she immune? There's no immunity to black-truth. There's tolerance, there's resistance, there is no—"

"There is," Serle said softly, "if you start young enough."

The silence that followed had weight. Dane turned slowly. "Start young enough. You mean—"

"I mean what I said." Serle's hands were shaking. Quentin watched them shake and thought: *he dosed me for twenty years and his hands are steady as a surgeon's, and now they shake.* "I mean I built her. Drop by drop. Year by year. Under orders, after her parents were taken. I mean that whatever she is, whatever she can survive, I made her able to survive it, and I will answer for that before any tribunal you like. But you will not put another drop in her mouth tonight, because there is nothing in her mouth for you to find, and because I am asking."

"You're begging, you said."

"Yes," Serle said. "I'm begging."

Dane looked at Quentin for a long moment. Then he laughed — short, humorless, a man recalculating. "Fine. The serum fails. The instruments don't." He gestured at the velvet. "We have time, Mistress Whitlock. The Lantern burns all night."

* * *

What happened next, Quentin would spend the rest of her unnamed life turning over, and she would never fully decide whether it was courage or arithmetic or simply the oldest instinct there is.

Dane unstrapped her right hand first, to work the buckle, because he was a careful man and careful men do things in order. Her left wrist stayed bound. Her ankles stayed bound. And Serle stepped forward from the wall — old, slow, devoted — to help, as he had helped with every strap and spoon and cup of her childhood.

Their eyes met. Just for a heartbeat. And Quentin saw it there, plain as print in a ledger: *I cannot undo what I did. But I can stand where I am standing.*

She moved.

Her right hand closed on the brass funnel — cold, solid, heavier than it looked — and swung it in the arc Serle had trained into her arm across a thousand hours of tasting-spoon drills. It caught Dane across the temple with a sound like a bell dropped on stone. He went down sideways, taking the instrument table with him, hooks and pincers ringing across the floor tiles.

Serle did not step aside. He stepped *in* — into her swing's path, into the space between her and the door, arms half-raised, and whether he meant to stop her or shield her or simply stand in front of the consequences of his life, she never learned, because the funnel was already moving and his shoulder took the blow meant for his skull. He cried out — a small sound, terribly human — and went down against the wall, and slid, and sat there with his hand pressed to his collarbone and tears running freely down the seamed old face that had never once wept over her vomit, her fevers, her nine-year-old screaming.

"I'm sorry," he said. "I'm sorry. I'm sorry. Go, girl. *Go.*"

She went. Her ankles came free with a jerk that would bruise for weeks. The door was oak and locked, but the key hung in it on the inside — Dane's carefulness again, a man who feared assassins more than prisoners — and then she was in the corridor, barefoot, in her shift, running past cells where voices rose up around her like startled birds.

Behind her, she heard Dane groan back toward consciousness. She heard Serle's voice, thick and wet: "She overpowered us both. Do you hear me? Both of us. Write it down exactly that way."

Buying her a story. Spending his last credibility on it. The tenderest betrayal, reversed.

* * *

The Lantern's lower floors were a maze and its upper floors were a legend, but every building in the capital leaked, and Quentin had spent eleven years learning where the palace leaked, and the Inquisitory had been built by the same masons with the same bribed foremen. There was a laundry chute that served the third floor and emptied into a cart-court behind the tanneries; there was a gap in the cart-court wall where boys had squeezed through for three generations; there was a tanner's boy, asleep on a pile of hides, whose boots fit her well enough.

She ran. Through the tannery stink, through the fish gate where a courier had died pressing salt into her hands, down to the river stairs where the fog came off the water thick as wool. Only when the Lantern's topmost window was a smudge behind her did she stop, doubled over, hands on knees, breathing low.

And there, in the fog, the thought arrived that she had been outrunning since the cell:

*They built me for this.*

Not the escaping. Not the swinging of funnels. But the surviving. Every morning spoonful of horror, every held-back hair, every fever — none of it had been cruelty, or none of it had been *only* cruelty. Someone, twenty years ago, had looked at the orphan daughter of arrested traitors and given orders: *make her unable to be poisoned. Make her unable to be made to talk. Make her a lock that doesn't open.*

Her curse was armor. Her whole ruined childhood had been a smithy, and she was the blade, and someone had forged her for a door she hadn't yet seen.

She laughed, alone in the fog, and it came out closer to sobbing, and she let it, because there was no one to taste it.

Then she straightened, and counted what she had: one stolen map hidden in a ledger in a safehouse, one boy named Finch who might still be alive, one fence named Marrow who owed her, two keys of remembrance and one still unfound, and eleven days — no. Fewer now. Days bleeding away like the tide going out.

And one debt she hadn't expected to owe: an old man sitting weeping on the floor of a round room, having chosen, at the end, to stand in front of her.

"I'll come back for you," she said to the fog, to the smudge of light on the hill, to the man who had poisoned her loyal and loved her badly. "Whatever you did. Whatever you were ordered to do. I'll remember you. That's the only payment I have left."

The fog didn't answer. The tide was going out.

Somewhere south of the river, a bell began to ring — not the alarm bells, not yet, but the chapel bell by the flooded quarter, the one that rang only for those the law forgot, tolling slow and patient, as if it had been waiting for her all along.

Quentin pulled the dead boy's boots tighter and started walking toward the sound.
