## 12. Finch's Debt

Finch had stolen things since he was six years old. Bread, mostly, at first. Then buttons, then knives, then names — because in the trade of small mercies, a name was worth more than any knife, and easier to lift off a sleeping man than his purse.

He had never once been paid to steal something he couldn't carry.

The debt-token sat in his pocket like a coal that wouldn't cool. It was a flat disc of grey tin, stamped with the guild's mark — a hand holding an open eye — and beneath it, the number of what he owed: *four hundred crowns*, which was to say, everything. His mother's medical debts. His sister's indenture fee. The cost of the roof over their heads for three more winters, all of it called in and bought up by Corvane's people in a single afternoon, then handed back to Finch as a weight on a string.

*Lead her to the coast road past Harrow Fen,* they'd said. *Corvane will do the rest. You won't even have to watch.*

He'd told himself it wasn't betrayal. She was going to die anyway — everyone who carried heir-maps died, everyone who remembered Old Vessany died, the whole guild had gone into wagons overnight and only he'd slipped the net because he was small and quick and nobody important. So what difference did it make if she walked into Corvane's arms instead of the inquisitors'? Same end. Different hands.

But she'd shared her bread with him at Harrow Fen without counting it first.

That was the thing nobody warned you about Quentin Whitlock. Everyone said tasters were cold, that years of poison made you nothing but a body that could be trusted with other people's deaths. And she was careful, yes — she counted coins like a woman defusing them, she slept facing doors, she spoke the name *Vessany* only in whispers as if the word might bite. But when she broke the loaf at the fen crossing she gave him the bigger half, and she did it without looking, the way you'd do it for family, and Finch had sat there chewing and understanding, with terrible clarity, that he was going to betray a person who fed people without looking.

They made camp that night in the lee of a fallen boundary stone — one of the old Vessany markers, its carved words worn to ghosts, though when Quentin touched it in passing she'd flinched like the stone had spoken. Finch built a small fire, because she let him, because she said the dark hid inquisitors better than it hid two tired travelers.

She was asleep. Or near enough. Her breathing had gone long and even, one hand curled near the knife she never fully put away.

Finch took out the token.

It caught the firelight. Four hundred crowns. He turned it over. On the back, in tiny stamped letters, the terms: *REDEEMABLE UPON COMPLETION OF SERVICE.* Service. That was the word they used. As if leading a woman to her death were a chore, like hauling water.

He thought about his sister, Wren, indentured to the dye-works in Casterhale until the debt cleared. Four hundred crowns was Wren free. Four hundred crowns was his mother's lungs seen by a real physician instead of a street-corner humbug. Four hundred crowns was every good thing Finch had ever wanted for the people he loved, pressed into a disc of tin no heavier than a coin.

And across the fire, Quentin shifted in her sleep and murmured something — a name, half-formed, maybe her mother's — and her face in the firelight looked so young. Younger than him, almost, though she had to be ten years older. Poison aged you slow and strange, Marrow used to say. But right now she just looked like a tired woman dreaming about someone dead.

*You won't even have to watch.*

He watched anyway. Every day since the chapter-house, he watched. He watched her taste the map grain and go white as whey. He watched her give up a true memory of her mother to lift the bell from the flooded chapel — he'd heard her describe the memory aloud, halting, loving, and then heard it *leave* her, heard her try afterward to recall what she'd surrendered and find only smooth blankness where a mother should be. She paid and paid and paid, this woman, and kept walking north.

And tomorrow, if he did nothing, the road would bend at the split oak above Gullswater, and Corvane's men would be waiting behind the rocks, and Finch would walk her straight into it, and collect.

His hands were shaking. He noticed distantly, the way you notice rain starting.

"Finch."

He nearly swallowed the token. She was awake, propped on one elbow, watching him with those flat grey taster's eyes that saw everything and admitted nothing.

"You're holding it wrong," she said.

"What?"

"The token." She sat up. "You're gripping it like it burns. If you're going to keep a thing, keep it easy. If you're going to throw it away, throw it clean." She held out her hand. "Which is it?"

He looked at her palm. Steady. Scarred at the fingertips from twenty years of other people's poison. A hand that had fed him twice now without counting.

"I was paid," Finch said. The words came out fast, tumbling, the way stolen things fall out of a torn coat. "Before I ever met you. Corvane bought my mother's debts and my sister's paper and he gave me this and told me to lead you to the split oak at Gullswater and there's men there, there's *six* of them, and I was supposed to bring you tomorrow—"

He stopped because she hadn't moved. Hadn't reached for the knife. Just sat there in the firelight, listening, the way you'd listen to a ledger entry.

"Go on," she said quietly.

"That's all. That's — that's all of it. I swear it on the guild mark, that's everything they gave me." He was crying now, furious about it, swiping his sleeve across his face. "I'm not going to do it. I already decided, I decided at the fen, I just didn't know how to tell you, because telling you means you'll send me away or worse, and I don't — I don't have anywhere else to *be*, Quentin. The guild was the place. The guild is wagons and chains now, and you're the last — you're the whole rest of it, and I sold you before I ever knew you, and I'm sorry, I'm sorry—"

"Stop." She said it without heat. "Breathe. Taster's habit — panic ruins the palate."

A wet, hiccuping laugh escaped him despite everything. She waited while he got his breath back, and then she held out her hand again, palm up, patient as stone.

"Give it to me."

"You can't redeem it. Only Corvane's office can—"

"*Give it to me.*"

He gave it to her.

She turned it over once, reading the terms the way she read everything — completely, in silence, once. Then she stood, walked to the fire, and crouched beside it, the token balanced on her scarred fingertips.

"A debt-token," she said, "is a contract. And a contract is only as strong as the thing it's written on." She glanced back at him. "Guild law. First lesson. Marrow taught you that?"

"Marrow taught me everything."

"Then Marrow would want you to know what happens next." She held the token over the flames. "This isn't your debt anymore. Do you understand? Whatever they buy with your service, they haven't bought it yet. And a contract uncompleted—" the tin began to glow at its edges, the stamped eye softening in the heat, "—is a contract *void*. Their claim dies with the metal. Not yours. *Theirs.*"

"But Wren," Finch whispered. "My mother. Four hundred crowns—"

"Is a number." Quentin's voice was very steady. "I've spent my life around numbers. Numbers are the easiest things in the world to change, Finch. Names are harder. People are hardest of all." She looked at him over her shoulder, firelight moving on her face. "You chose. Tonight, sitting there turning that thing over like a hot coal, you already chose. Burning it just makes it official."

He came and knelt beside her at the fire. Together, without speaking of it, they both pushed — her fingers and his, stacked on the little disc — and the debt-token slid off their hands and into the coals.

It went up in a spit of orange sparks. The stamped hand-and-eye bubbled, ran, became nothing. Somewhere in Casterhale, Finch thought, some clerk's ledger had just developed a smudge where four hundred crowns used to be. Let them chase the smoke.

He felt lighter. He also felt terrified. Both at once, which he was learning was simply what choosing felt like.

"They'll still come," Quentin said, watching the metal sink into ash. "Six men at the split oak, expecting you tomorrow. Which means we know exactly where they'll be, and when, and how many." Something moved through her face then — not a smile, nothing so warm, but the cold bright edge of one. "Corvane bought an ambush. We may as well get some use out of it."

"You want to *walk into it*?"

"I want to know what Corvane knows. And I want him to think his bait still works." She rose, brushing ash from her knees. "Sleep, Finch. You've earned it. You did the hardest thing tonight, and it gets easier from here — that's the one lie I'll allow myself to tell you."

He lay back down. The fire ticked. Above the fallen boundary stone the stars came out over country that officially did not exist, and Finch found, to his surprise, that he wasn't afraid of the morning.

Because whatever waited at the split oak, he wouldn't be walking toward it as a boy who'd been bought.

He'd be walking beside the woman who burned the price.

* * *

They reached the split oak an hour past noon, and Finch understood immediately why Corvane had chosen it: the road narrowed between the tree and a rockfall, single file, nowhere to run. Six men, Quentin had said, and Finch counted five visible — slouched among the boulders with the studied idleness of men pretending not to be waiting.

The sixth was Corvane himself, stepping out from behind the oak as if he'd grown from its roots.

He was smaller than Finch expected. Neat. Grey-gloved, pleasant-faced, with the manner of a man about to discuss freight rates. His eyes found Finch first, and warmed.

"There's my boy," Corvane said. "Knew you had it in you."

Quentin stepped forward, and Finch watched Corvane's attention slide to her — appraising, cataloguing, the way a fence looks at cargo. "The map," Corvane said. "And the keys, if you've gathered any. In exchange, the boy walks free, debts cleared, and you get passage to the coast that doesn't end in an inquisitor's wagon. I'm told you've had a difficult week. This is the gentle option."

"And the other option?" Quentin asked.

Corvane sighed, genuinely regretful. "The other option involves the gentlemen behind the rocks and a very long afternoon."

Finch's heart was hammering. Beside him, Quentin was silent a moment too long — and then she laughed. Short, dry, utterly without fear, and Finch realized with a lurch that she'd done it on purpose, that everything about her stillness was a performance, that the taster who had survived twenty years at court knew exactly how to hold a room.

"Gentle option," she repeated. "Let me offer you mine. Your men are behind the rocks. My friend here—" she tipped her head at Finch, "—counted them before you ever stepped out. And the inquisitors who emptied our chapter-house have been following *your* trail for three days, Master Corvane, because a man who sells doors makes noise. So the question isn't whether I hand anything over. The question is whether you want to be standing here when the Crown arrives to collect *you*."

Corvane's pleasant face didn't move. But his gloved fingers flexed, once, and Finch — who had spent his life reading pockets and pauses — saw it plain as print.

*He believes her.*

"You're bluffing," Corvane said.

"Taster's habit," Quentin said. "We never bluff. We count." She smiled, thin as a blade. "Count with me."

And down the road, faint but unmistakable, came the sound of hooves.

What happened next happened fast. Corvane's men broke cover; Quentin pulled Finch off the road into the bracken; the riders swept past — grey cloaks, crown badges, inquisitors after all, though whether drawn by her trail or Corvane's, Finch never learned. Shouts. A scuffle. Corvane's voice rising, bargaining, then cutting off sharp.

By the time the road went quiet, the inquisitors were riding away with a neat grey-gloved prisoner bound across a saddle, and Quentin and Finch were crouched in the ferns, unhurt, unmoved, and richer by one enemy removed.

"He'll talk," Finch whispered. "Corvane. He knows everything. He'll sell you to them in a heartbeat."

"Yes," Quentin agreed calmly, already moving back toward the road. "Which is why we're not going to be where he says we are." She paused, looking south, toward the distant glitter of the sea, and her jaw set in a way Finch was beginning to recognize — the look she wore when arithmetic became resolve.

"Eleven days," she said softly. "Marrow's notes said eleven days to the great salt-tide. And the third key is in the palace vault, Finch. In the one place in the world they'll never expect me to walk back into."

Finch stared at her. "The palace. *Your* palace. Where they know your face."

"Where they know my face," she corrected, "and trust my tongue." She started walking, quick and certain, and after a moment Finch ran to catch up.

Somewhere behind them, in an inquisitor's wagon, Corvane was already talking. Ahead lay the capital, the court, the king's poisoned table — and a door that only opened once, forever.

Finch had been paid to lead her to death. Instead, he was going to lead her home.
