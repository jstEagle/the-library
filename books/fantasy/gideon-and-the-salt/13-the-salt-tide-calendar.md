## 13. The Salt-Tide Calendar

Marrow's notes smelled of smoke and vinegar.

I found them where he'd told me to look — sewn into the lining of a dead man's coat, hanging in a pawnshop window in the Tanners' Row, priced at four coppers because grief makes bad merchants of us all. I bought the coat. The pawnbroker didn't ask why a woman in a fine court cloak wanted a threadbare thing with burn holes down one sleeve. He was counting my coppers before I'd finished paying them.

Finch waited outside with his hood up, looking like exactly what he was: a boy too young to be this frightened, trying very hard not to show it.

"Well?" he said.

"Inside."

We walked. That's the first rule Marrow ever taught me, back when I was still allowed to think of him as only a fence and not the spine of everything: *when you've got something worth killing for, walk like it's nothing.* So we walked past the chandlers and the tallow-stinks and the shrine to some saint whose name had been sanded off his own statue, and we didn't stop until we reached the flooded cellar under the old rope-walk, where the guild used to dry its ink.

Finch lit one candle. One. His hands were steady doing it, which surprised me. Mine hadn't been steady in days.

The coat's lining gave up its secret grudgingly, stitch by stitch, and what came out was a sheaf of papers no bigger than prayer cards, written in Marrow's cramped leftward slant, the handwriting of a man who'd learned to write small so he could write fast and hide the evidence faster.

The top card said: **SALT-TIDE CALENDAR — READ ALONE OR NOT AT ALL.**

"I'll check the street," Finch said, and went up the stairs without being asked twice. Good boy. Better than good. I'd stopped thinking of him as a debt I owed and started thinking of him as the last piece of the guild that still answered when I knocked.

I read alone.

* * *

Here is what Marrow knew, set down in his ugly little hand sometime before they came for him:

The door out of Old Vessany is not a door the way a door is a door. It does not stand in a wall. It stands in a tide. Once every nineteen years, the great salt-tide comes up the drowned harbor higher than any water has a right to come, and for the length of that tide — one hour, maybe less — the sea remembers being a border, and the border remembers being open.

Nineteen years since the last one. Nineteen years since my parents were arrested, though I didn't know those two facts were the same fact until I read it there in Marrow's slanted script: *the last opening was the night the Crown moved on the Vessany families. They timed it that way on purpose. Let the country try to leave while the door stood open, and catch everyone who tried.*

Eleven days from tonight, the tide would come again.

And it would be the last. Marrow had done the arithmetic the way he did everything — coldly, completely, with no room for comfort. The coastline that appears on no chart was dissolving. Not metaphorically. The salt-memory that held the shape of Old Vessany's shore was thinning generation by generation, as the people who remembered it died or were made to forget, and each great tide took more of it than it gave back. This one, he wrote, would be the last time the water rose high enough. After that, the door wouldn't be locked. It simply wouldn't be anywhere.

One hour. Eleven days. Three keys.

I turned to the next card and my stomach did something complicated and cold.

**THE KEYS.** Two struck through — the debt-token Finch had given me, the bell from the flooded chapel, both marked with a single word in Marrow's hand: *held?* And beneath them, unstruck, waiting:

**THE THIRD LIES WHERE THE CROWN KEEPS WHAT IT TAKES. THE VAULT OF CONFISCATED THINGS. UNDER THE PALACE.**

I sat in the dark of the rope-walk cellar and laughed, once, without any humor in it at all. Of course. Of course it was in the palace. Where else would the Crown keep a country's door-knocker but in the same building where I'd spent ten years learning to poison myself politely?

They say God has a sense of humor. In my experience it's more of an administrative irony.

* * *

Finch came back down when I called him, and I laid the cards out on the damp stone between us, weighted at the corners with four pebbles, like a little paper kingdom.

"Tell me what you know about the vault," I said.

He shrugged, then thought better of the shrug. "Only stories. Everyone in the guild had stories. They say it's three floors down under the east wing, behind a door that opens to a song instead of a key. They say the inquisitors bring things there in boxes lined with lead so the things can't be remembered through the wood." He paused. "They say nobody who's gone in carrying anything true has come out the same."

"Do you believe the stories?"

"I believe the guild believed them," he said, "and the guild is in a cell somewhere now, so." He looked at the cards instead of at me. "Eleven days."

"Ten and a half. The tide turns at moonrise on the eleventh night."

"And you're going to walk back into the palace."

It wasn't a question, but I answered it anyway. "Yes."

"They're hunting you. Serle reported you. Half the city watch has your face memorized by now — I've seen the sketches, and whoever drew them flattered you, which is somehow worse." His voice cracked in the middle and he hated it, I could see him hating it. "You can't just walk in."

"No," I agreed. "I can't *just* walk in. I have to walk in the way only I can."

He waited. He'd gotten good at waiting. Prisoners and apprentices learn it young.

"The palace knows me," I said. "Not Quentin Whitlock, fugitive. The taster. Ten years at the king's table, ten years of never missing a service, never missing a dish, never giving them anything to remember except my usefulness. Fear fades. Habit doesn't. Every clerk in that building knows my schedule better than the king's. If I come back through the servants' gate at my usual hour, in my usual clothes, with my usual face—" I tapped the card with the vault written on it. "—then for a few days, I'm not a hunted woman. I'm furniture. And furniture gets to go places people can't."

"And if they've already struck you from the rolls?"

"Then the rolls are wrong," I said, "and I intend to make them righter."

That got half a laugh out of him, strangled though it was. Then his face went serious again, the way it did when he was about to say something he'd been carrying a while.

"My mother used to say the palace eats people," he said. "Not kills them. Eats them. Slowly. You go in whole and come out in pieces, and the pieces all still work, they just don't belong to you anymore." He looked at me with those too-old eyes. "You've been in there ten years. How much of you is left to eat?"

I thought about lying to him. I decided against it; he'd earned the truth, and besides, lies are heavy cargo and I was traveling light.

"Enough," I said. "Barely enough. But enough."

* * *

We spent the next two days preparing, and I want to record honestly how those days felt, because later — if there is a later — someone should know that courage is mostly bookkeeping.

Marrow's notes gave me the tide-tables, and the tide-tables gave me the shape of the eleven nights. Moonrise on the eleventh, at the drowned harbor, three keys joined. Which meant I had ten days to get into a vault that opened to a song, extract a door-knocker I'd never seen, and get out again with the entire apparatus of the Crown between me and the sea.

Finch worked the streets. He was better at it than I was — a boy is invisible in a way a woman never quite manages — and he came back each evening with pieces: the guard rotations on the east wing, the name of a laundry-maid who owed the guild a favor and could get a cart through the service court, the rumor that the Chief Inquisitor herself had taken rooms in the palace for the season, which meant the hunt for me had been elevated from police work to ceremony.

"They're not looking for a criminal," Finch reported, the second night. "They're looking for a *relic*. Corvane's been telling anyone who'll listen that you're carrying an heir-map, and the inquisitors have started treating it like scripture. There's a reward now. Not money." He swallowed. "A pardon. Full immunity, for life, for whoever brings them the map and the person holding it."

Immunity. The word landed strangely, the way words do when they've been used against you before. Serle had offered me immunity once — permanent immunity work, a cage dressed as an honor. Now the Crown was offering it to strangers in exchange for my body. Everyone, it seemed, was eager to make me safe. Nobody was eager to ask me what safety was for.

"What about Corvane?" I asked. "Where is he?"

"Gone to ground. Since the chapel, nobody's seen him. Marrow's people think he's waiting for us to do the hard part." Finch's jaw tightened. "Let the heir dig up the keys, then take them off her at the end. Why risk the vault yourself when you can rob a graveyard?"

"He thinks like a merchant," I said.

"He thinks like a jackal."

"Those are often the same profession." I folded the tide-tables away. "Which means we finish the job before he finishes waiting. Speed is our only armor he can't buy."

* * *

On the third night I did something I hadn't done since the fish gate. I took out my tasting ledger — the real one, the guild's ledger, the one I'd hidden inside the false one — and I read it.

Not the entries. Never the entries; that rule had kept me alive through ten years of double bookkeeping, and I wasn't going to break it now. But I read the ledger itself. The object. The weight of it, the wear of it, the way the cover had gone soft at the corners from being carried against my ribs through a thousand services and a thousand smuggled names.

Because here was the thing I'd understood somewhere between the flooded chapel and the pawnshop coat, and hadn't yet said aloud, even to myself:

The third key was in the vault. But the vault was full of confiscated things — seized memory-objects, lead-lined boxes, everything the Crown had spent twenty years taking from a country it had erased. And my ledger was full of confiscated things too. A stolen lullaby. A widow's legal name. A jar of sea-salt from a drowned province. Weeks and months and years of impossible cargo, recorded faithfully, never read, never understood — until now.

I had been carrying a second vault the whole time. A portable one. Made of paper and obedience and a rule I'd never questioned: *never read what you record.*

What if the rule was the lock? What if reading was the keyhole, and everything I'd catalogued in numbness was waiting to mean something?

I didn't read it that night. Not yet. Reading it might cost me memories I'd need, and I had eleven days and no margin for waste. But I held the ledger in my lap in the dark of the rope-walk cellar and I understood, finally, what kind of thief I was.

Not a thief of things. A thief of keeping. Everything the Crown confiscates, someone, somewhere, has to refuse to lose.

That someone had been me all along. I just hadn't been told.

* * *

The fourth day, Finch brought me a dress.

Court grey, servant-cut, with the palace laundry mark still stitched inside the collar — he'd traded three favors and a lie to get it, and refused to tell me which three. I put it on in the cellar's dark and looked down at myself and felt the strangest sensation: the fabric remembered me. Ten years of that uniform, ten years of walking those corridors, and the cloth fit like a sentence I'd already served.

"You don't have to do this," Finch said. He'd said it every day, in different words, and every day I'd given him the same answer in different words, and we both knew the ritual was really for him, not me. "There has to be another way into the vault."

"There isn't."

"There could be. We could find one. We have ten days—"

"Nine and a half." I straightened the collar. "And every way in except the front door costs more time than we have. Finch. Look at me."

He looked.

"In eleven days the sea opens for one hour, and after that it never opens again. Everything Old Vessany was — every name, every lullaby, every legal identity the Crown burned — goes out with the tide, permanently, unless someone holds the door. I am the last living heir. The keys answer to my blood. There is no version of this story where I stay safely underground and it ends well." I picked up the ledger and slid it into the dress's inner pocket, where it had lived for ten years, against my ribs, over my heart, like a second heartbeat. "So the question isn't whether I walk back in. The question is what I carry out."

"And if you don't come out?"

"Then you take the ledger and the two keys and you find Marrow's other contacts, and you finish it without me." I said it steadily, and I meant it, and meaning it was the hardest bookkeeping I'd done yet. "The door matters more than the heir. Write that down somewhere, in case I'm not around to insist on it later."

"I'm not writing that down."

"Finch—"

"I'm *not*." His voice broke clean through this time and he let it. "Everyone keeps telling me what to remember and what to forget. The guild. The inquisitors. Corvane. You." He wiped his face with his sleeve, furious at himself, magnificent anyway. "I decide what I keep. That's the one thing they haven't figured out how to confiscate. So no. I'm not writing your death down. I'm keeping the space empty until you fill it yourself."

I didn't have an answer for that. I stood there in my dead woman's dress with my living heart in my pocket, and for the first time since the fish gate, since the map, since the word *heir* entered my life and rearranged all its furniture — for the first time, the dread in my chest changed shape.

It didn't get smaller. Dread doesn't. But it sharpened, the way fear does when it finally finds its purpose — from a fog into a blade, from *something is coming* into *something is required.*

Eleven days. One hour of tide. Three keys, two of them mine, the third sitting three floors under the palace behind a door that opened to a song.

I knew a lot of songs. I'd tasted seven dishes a night for ten years in a building full of singers.

"Finch," I said, and my voice came out level, and level was its own kind of resolve. "Get me the laundry-maid's name. And a cart."

He looked up. "When?"

"Tomorrow at dusk. The servants' gate opens for the evening delivery, and the guards change at the same bell, and for ninety seconds nobody looks at faces because everybody's watching hands." I pulled the hood up over my hair and felt the old routine settle over me like armor — the walk, the posture, the taster's blank and useful face. "I'm going home."

Outside, far off toward the harbor, the gulls were screaming over water that didn't know yet what it would soon remember.

Nine and a half days. Then the sea would rise, and either I'd be standing at the door with all three keys —

— or the country would close forever, with me on the wrong side of it, and my name in every ledger but the one that mattered.
