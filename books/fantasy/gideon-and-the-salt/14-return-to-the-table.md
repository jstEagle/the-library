## 14. Return to the Table

The palace gates had never looked like a mouth before. Now they did — arched, wet with morning fog, and open, waiting to be fed.

Quentin walked through them with her hands folded and her face arranged into its court expression: mild, attentive, faintly bored. The guards knew her. One of them nodded. That was the worst part, how easily they let her back in, how little she had ever mattered here except as a pair of working taste buds attached to a body that could be discarded if it convulsed.

*You are walking back into the cage,* said a voice in her head that sounded like Marrow's notes read aloud. *Voluntarily.*

But the third key was in the vault of confiscated things, three floors below the king's dining hall, and there was no other way to it. She had spent eleven days learning this. Eleven days since the salt-tide calendar, since Finch had pressed his burned debt-token's ash into her palm like a charm, since Corvane had taken the map and left them alive on the coast road because a map without keys was only expensive paper.

Eleven days of sleeping in ditches and remembering, deliberately, on purpose, what Serle had taught her about poisons: *the body keeps accounts you don't know you're keeping.* Her body was keeping an account now. It remembered every corridor of this palace the way her tongue remembered bitterness.

She passed the fish gate. Someone had scrubbed the stones where the courier had died. The stain was gone. The stain was not gone; she could still see it, the way she could still see everything the Crown wanted forgotten. That was apparently her inheritance: perfect recall of erased things.

"Whitlock." A steward, hurrying, relieved. "Thank the saints. We were told you'd run."

"I took ill," Quentin said. "Family matter. It's resolved."

"Serle will want to know—"

"Serle," she said carefully, "is in the vaults. He'll be some time."

The steward blinked at that but wrote nothing down, which told her the vaults were not a place stewards asked questions about. Good. Serle had stayed behind in the dark with the door-knocker in his hands, taking her place, buying her hours with whatever mercy had arrived from the wrong direction. She did not let herself think about what would happen when they found him there. She filed it in the same ledger-room where she kept everything else she refused to read.

*Never read what you record.* Her own rule, turned inward.

* * *

The tasting hall smelled of beeswax and roasted quail, and beneath both, faintly, something wrong.

Quentin stood at her station while the kitchen sent up the evening's first service. Seven dishes, as always. The numbness came on schedule after the third — the slow silver spreading across her tongue, her body filing each poison under its familiar headings: *alkaloid, heavy metal, compound I have no name for but recognize by shape.* Twenty years of being poisoned for a living had made her palate a ledger too.

Dish four arrived under a silver dome, and the wrongness sharpened.

It was a simple thing. Braised eel, river-caught, glazed with honey and something dark. The kind of dish kings ate when they wanted to feel humble. Quentin took her portion, chewed, swallowed, waited the regulation count.

And then she tasted the salt.

Not sea-salt. Not mine-salt. It was older than both, grainier, with a mineral undertone like iron left out in rain, and underneath it — she closed her eyes — underneath it was a coastline. Not a memory of one. A coastline itself, compressed into crystal, the way the heir-map had held a shore that appeared on no chart. She had tasted that exact flavor once before, off a crumb of paper in Marrow's back room, and it had shown her drowned harbors and a border that existed only in inherited memory.

Old Vessany. Salted into the king's dinner.

Her hands did not shake. She had trained them not to, years ago, for exactly this reason: a taster whose hands shook got pulled from service, and a taster pulled from service stopped hearing things. But inside, something went very cold and very quiet, the way water goes before it freezes.

She finished the portion. She pronounced it clean. Her voice came out level.

Then she stood at her station through five more courses and listened.

The king's table was a market, if you knew how to listen. Ministers traded favors in the pauses between courses. The Lord Treasurer complained about the cost of garrisoning a border that officially did not exist — "twenty years of paying soldiers to stand in fog, gentlemen, and for what?" The Master of Seizures, a soft man with ink-stained fingers, mentioned almost casually that the royal vault had received "a remarkable acquisition" last month, recovered from a smuggler's cache, "an artifact of the Vessany question."

The table laughed politely at the phrase *the Vessany question*, the way people laugh at anything said with enough confidence.

Quentin recorded all of it. Names, tones, the order of courses, the vintage of wine. Somewhere above the fear, above even the fury beginning to bank itself in her chest like a coal, the guild-trained part of her mind was doing what it always did: cataloguing. *Entry: the country is not only remembered. It is being consumed.*

Because that was what the salt meant. She understood it now, standing numb-tongued beside the king's chair while he tore bread and discussed tariffs. Old Vessany hadn't just been dissolved. Its borders, its names, its dead — they were being broken down and served, course by course, to the people who had erased it. The salt from the drowned province wasn't contraband. It was *cuisine*. Someone in this building was eating a country and finding it well-seasoned.

The king himself lifted a spoonful of the eel glaze, savored it, and said to the Master of Seizures:

"There it is again. That note at the end. Like the sea, but older. Where do they get it?"

"A provincial source, Majesty. Complicated supply chain."

"Mm. Tell them to secure it. Whatever it costs." He licked the spoon. "A flavor like that shouldn't be allowed to die out."

Quentin looked at the floor and thought about drowning him.

* * *

After service, she asked permission to inspect the kitchens — standard procedure, tasters' prerogative, a formality so old no one questioned it. The under-cooks parted around her like water around a knife.

The spice room was locked, which was normal. The lock was new, which was not. And through the door's gap she saw, on a shelf reserved for imports, a row of small glass jars, each sealed with black wax, each labeled in a clerk's hand: *V. Province 3. V. Province 7. V. Coastline, undivided.*

Jars of salt. Dozens of them. Enough to season a kingdom's memory for years.

Beside them, a manifest. She didn't touch it — never read what you record, and besides, her eyes were enough. The manifest listed deliveries going back months. Monthly. Regular as tide. Which meant someone upstairs had a supplier, and a supplier meant a route, and a route meant—

A route meant the door was already known. Or half-known. Or being mined.

The cold thing in her chest finished freezing.

She had come back for a key. She had told herself it was strategy, cover, necessity. But standing in the spice-smelling dark with the jars gleaming like teeth, Quentin admitted the other truth: she had come back angry, and anger was a currency the palace paid better than wages.

Finch was waiting in the laundry yard, disguised in a scullion's smock two sizes too big, looking like exactly what he was — a boy pretending very hard not to be afraid.

"Well?" he whispered.

"They're eating it," she said.

"Eating—"

"The salt. The country. They've got jars, Finch. Labeled by province. The king asked for more." She heard her own voice and didn't recognize it: flat, precise, each word laid down like a stone. "Twenty years we thought the Crown wanted Old Vessany forgotten. Forgotten things don't get harvested. You don't put a lullaby in a jar. This isn't erasure anymore. It's *farming*."

Finch was quiet. Then: "The vault key. Tonight?"

"Tonight. While the salt-tide is still rising toward full." She looked back at the lit windows of the dining hall, where the king was finishing his eel. "And Finch — if anyone asks, the taster found nothing wrong with any dish tonight. The food was excellent. The king is safe."

"You sound like you mean the opposite."

"I mean both," Quentin said. "That's what immunity is. Learning to hold poison and truth in the same mouth without swallowing either."

She turned toward the servants' stair, toward the vaults, toward the door-knocker that had hung on her family's house before the house existed on any map worth keeping. Behind her, faintly, through seven doors and a corridor, she could almost hear the king asking for seconds.

The tide was coming in. So was she.
