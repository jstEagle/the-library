## 17. Corvane's Bargain

The sea came back to us an hour before Corvane did.

We heard it first — a long exhale under the dunes, the sound of water remembering itself — and then the smell, salt and rot and something older underneath, like a cellar that had been shut for twenty years. Finch stopped on the crest of the last hill and pointed down at the valley beyond, and for a moment neither of us said anything at all.

The drowned harbor lay below us in the gray light of late afternoon, half-swallowed by dunes that had crept over it like a blanket drawn up by a careful hand. Rooftops broke the sand in places, blackened ribs. A church spire leaned at an angle no honest builder would have chosen. And out past all of it, where the harbor mouth should have been, the sea stood in a long low wall, waiting. Not advancing. Waiting.

"That's it," Finch said. "That's Old Vessany."

"Don't say it here."

He glanced at me. "You just did."

"I'm allowed." I didn't explain. I wasn't sure I could. Since the chapel, since I'd paid the bell its price and felt my mother's face go soft and featureless in my memory like wet paper, the name had lived in my mouth differently. It was mine now in a way nothing else was. The land heard it when I spoke it. The land had been listening ever since.

Finch shrugged and started down the dune path, and I followed, one hand flat against the map inside my coat. It had gone quiet since the second key joined the first — two small weights in my breast pocket, the debt-token and the bell, warm as coins held too long in a fist. Warm as if they were getting ready for something.

The path wound down through marram grass and gorse, past a fence post still standing after two decades of weather, its paint flaked to bare wood. Someone had nailed a horseshoe to it, upside down, so the luck would pour out. Vessany superstition. My hand knew it before my mind did; I'd seen a hundred fences like it in memories I couldn't fully hold anymore.

"Quentin." Finch had stopped halfway down. "There's a man on the beach."

I saw him then. He was sitting on an overturned fishing boat near the tideline, patient as a heron, and even at this distance there was no mistaking the set of his shoulders or the way he'd placed himself — squarely between us and the water wall. Between us and the door.

"Corvane," I said.

"You know him?"

"I've read his entries." I kept walking. "Every smuggler's cargo has a middleman. He's ours."

"Is he going to help us?"

"No," I said, and kept walking anyway, because there was nowhere else to go. The tide would not wait. The keys would not wait. And a man sitting between you and the only door out of your life is a man you must eventually walk toward, whatever he intends.

* * *

Corvane rose when we were ten paces off. He was smaller than I'd imagined from his ledger hand — a compact, tidy man in a fisherman's oilskin, gray at the temples, with the kind of face you forget while looking at it. His hands were clean. That was the detail I fixed on. Two days' walk from the capital, on a dead coast, and his hands were clean as a clerk's.

"Mistress Whitlock." He inclined his head. "And the boy. Finch, isn't it? Guild roll number eleven hundred and six. Apprentice, third year. Debts outstanding: one."

Finch went very still beside me. I felt him do it, the way you feel a rope go taut.

"You've been reading our rolls," I said.

"I've been *keeping* them, these last three weeks. Since your chapter-house burned." He said it mildly, the way Serle might announce the seventh dish. "Someone has to. The guild doesn't survive on sentiment."

"The guild survives on ledgers," I said. "I know. I wrote enough of them."

"Yes." Something moved behind his forgettable face — pleasure, maybe, or appetite. "You did. Remarkable work, actually. The Whitlock system. Never read what you record. Record everything. It made confiscating your chapter-house almost trivial — every entry cross-referenced, every route documented, every member named in cipher so simple a child could break it." He looked at Finch. "A child did break it, in fact. They were very proud of him."

Finch made a small sound. I put my arm across his chest without looking, the way you'd steady a door in wind.

"What do you want, Corvane?"

"Straight to business. Good. I've always admired that in tasters — you people don't waste time on flavor." He stepped aside, gesturing at the boat, at the stretch of shingle before it. Two packs sat there, provisioned, watered, roped for travel. "I want what everyone wants. Passage. And you're the only person alive who can give it."

"The map opens nothing without the keys."

"I know. Which is why I'm not asking for the map." He smiled, and it was the smile of a man laying down a winning hand with genuine regret. "I'm asking for the keys. And in exchange, I'll let the boy walk away."

The sea filled the silence that followed. Not waves — there were no waves, not yet — just that vast held breath, the sound of a country inhaling under water.

"Let me be precise," Corvane continued, because men like him are always precise, always fair, always *reasonable*, and that is how you know the knife is coming. "Behind me, at this moment, are four inquisitors who believe I am scouting the coastline ahead of them. They will arrive within the hour. When they do, they will find either two fugitives and three relics of a dissolved nation — or one fugitive, one cooperative guildsman, and a story about a boy who ran. I have already written both reports. I need only decide which to send."

"You're guild," Finch said hoarsely. "You took oaths."

"I took wages." Corvane turned to him, and for a moment something almost gentle crossed his face. "Oaths are for people who can afford them, boy. Your guildmaster took wages too, in the end. So did your father, mistress. Everyone sells something. The only question is the price."

"And yours?" I asked. "What's the Crown paying for the keys?"

"Nothing." He said it simply, and I believed him, which frightened me more than any sum could have. "The Crown doesn't know about the keys. The Crown knows about a map and a dead courier and a taster who's gone missing from her post. No — this purchase is private. There are collectors, mistress. People who survived the dissolution with their fortunes intact and their nostalgia intact, and who will pay extraordinary sums for a piece of the country they helped erase. A bell that rings for the forgotten. A token that remembers a debt. A door-knocker, if you've found it yet." His eyes flicked to my pocket, quick as a pickpocket's. "Have you?"

I said nothing. He nodded as if I had.

"There it is, then. Three keys, one door, one tide. You want to go through it. I want to sell it. Neither of us gets what we want while the other is standing here." He spread his clean hands. "So. Arithmetic."

* * *

Here is what I knew about arithmetic, having learned it at court, seven dishes a night, poison measured in grains:

Everything costs. Nothing costs fairly. The trick is never the price — the trick is knowing which thing you can afford to lose, and lying to yourself about it afterward.

I looked at Finch. He was fourteen years old and had been paid, once, to lead me into a trap, and had chosen instead to burn his own debt-token in a tavern fire rather than see me sold. He was shivering now, though the evening wasn't cold. He was looking at me the way you look at the last lit window in a burning street.

"Don't," he said.

"You don't know what I'm going to—"

"Don't." His voice cracked on it. "Whatever it is. Don't."

"Boy," Corvane said softly, "she hasn't asked your opinion."

"She's not going to give you the keys."

"No," I agreed. "She isn't."

And I watched Corvane's face do the small, fatal thing I'd been waiting for it to do — the flicker of recalculation, the merchant hearing a number he hadn't priced in. Because he thought he had me cornered. He thought the trade was keys-for-boy, and that I'd pay it, because everyone pays for the ones they love, that was the whole architecture of his bargain, the load-bearing wall of it.

But Corvane had spent three weeks reading my ledgers and had learned, apparently, nothing about the woman who wrote them.

"The keys stay with me," I said. "All of them. Through the door and gone, tonight, and you'll never see them again, and neither will your collectors. That part isn't negotiable, so don't waste breath."

"Then the boy—"

"Is worth more than your collectors' money." I pulled the map from my coat.

It had changed since the fish gate. The salt crust had spread, whitening the parchment like frost climbing a window, and where the coastline had been there was now something else — lines that moved if you didn't look directly at them, a shore that existed only in inherited memory, drawn in a medium no inquisitor could seize and no collector could display. The heir-map. The last living drawing of a border that had been officially dissolved twenty years ago.

Corvane's composure slipped. Just a hairline crack, but I saw it, and I pressed.

"This is what your collectors actually want. Not the keys — anyone can melt a bell. This. The map itself. Proof the door exists, proof of where it stands, the one object in the world that says Old Vessany was real." I held it out, just short of his reach. "You can't sell what you can't show. And you can't show this to anyone who isn't of the blood — Marrow told me, and Marrow was many things but never wrong about cargo. To anyone else it's salt-stained waste paper. But to the right buyer — the right *believer* — it's the relic of relics. Worth more than the keys, because the keys are useless without it, and worthless to anyone but me besides."

"You're bluffing."

"Am I?" I let the wind take the map's edge, let it flutter, let him watch the lines move. "Test me. Take it by force and spend the rest of your life trying to find a buyer who can see anything but blank vellum. Or take the trade."

His jaw worked. Behind him the sea-wall of water seemed, very slightly, to lean inland.

"And the boy walks free," I said. "Both reports burned. The inquisitors find nothing. You get rich, I get gone, Finch gets old. That's the arithmetic, Corvane. Check it."

For a long moment the only sound was the sea holding its breath.

Then Corvane laughed — a short, genuine, ugly sound, the laugh of a man who has just watched someone beat him at his own game and cannot help admiring the technique.

"You're your father's daughter," he said.

The words landed harder than he could have known. Harder than he intended, I think, because he saw my face change and something in his own changed with it, some professional detachment failing at the seam.

"You knew him," I said.

"I killed him."

The sea went very loud and very far away, all at once, the way sound does when your body decides the world is too much and turns the volume down. I heard Finch say my name — my actual name, the one I still owned, the one I had left to lose — and it came to me through cotton.

"On whose contract?" My voice sounded like someone else's. Calm. Taster-calm. The voice I used for the seventh dish.

"The Crown's. Twenty years ago. Before the dissolution was even finished — they were erasing the country in stages, you understand, first the laws, then the names, then the maps, and finally the people who remembered. Your father was stage four." Corvane's hands stayed loose at his sides, clean, empty, terrible. "He was offered what I'm offering you. Sell the door's location, keep your family, live. He refused. Not bravely — I want to be honest with you, since honesty is the currency of this transaction — not nobly. He simply didn't believe the door was his to sell. Said it belonged to whoever remembered it next." He paused. "He was right. Here you are."

"You killed him for refusing a sale," I said, "and now you're making the same offer to his daughter."

"I'm making a better one. He got nothing for his refusal. You're walking away with your boy alive and your bloodline's door intact." He tilted his head. "In the ledger of this family, mistress, I'm the kindest entry in twenty years."

I wanted to kill him. I want to be honest, since honesty is the currency of this transaction. I stood on the shingle of my drowned country with my father's killer breathing the same wind, and every part of me that had survived Serle's poisons and the inquisitors' cells and the bell's price wanted to close the distance and find out what a man like Corvane kept in his body that could stop a heart.

But Finch was shaking beside me. And the tide was coming. And the arithmetic does not care what you want.

"The map," I said. "Now. Then the boy goes, and you burn both reports, and we never meet again."

"Agreed." He held out his hand.

I did not shake it. I laid the map across his palm the way you'd set down something dead, and stepped back, and put my body between him and Finch, and waited while he examined it — turning it over, tilting it against the fading light, watching the coastline breathe. Watching a country move under salt.

"It's beautiful," he said quietly. Almost to himself. "God help me, it's beautiful. Do you know what they'll pay for—"

"Reports," I said. "Burn them."

"They're already ash." He tucked the map away inside his oilskin, and just like that it was gone, twenty years of inheritance folded into a fisherman's coat, and I felt the loss go through me like a hook — and then felt something else, stranger, underneath it. Lightness. The map had been a weight since the fish gate. Now it was his weight. Let it drag him wherever he swam.

"Go," Corvane said. He jerked his chin at the water wall, and for one unguarded instant his forgettable face looked almost young. "The tide won't wait and neither will the inquisitors. And mistress—" I was already turning, Finch's wrist in my hand, pulling him toward the harbor ruins. "Mistress Whitlock. Your father's last words were a name. I never learned whose. Perhaps the door will tell you."

I did not answer. There was no answer that cost less than everything.

* * *

We ran.

Up the shingle, through the ribs of the fish market, past a customs house whose sign still hung by one chain — VESSANY PORT AUTHORITY, the letters half-eaten by rust, the name surviving on wood when it could not survive on paper. Finch ran beside me, breath ragged, and did not ask where we were going, because there was only one place left in the world to go.

At the harbor's edge the stones sloped down into water that was not moving and yet was rising, inch by inch, silent as a held note. The church spire cast a long shadow across it. In my pocket the two keys had grown hot enough to feel through cloth, and beneath my feet, faint as a pulse through a floor, I felt the harbor begin to hum.

"Quentin." Finch caught my sleeve. His eyes were wet and furious. "You gave him the map. You gave him *everything*, and for me, and I was *paid* to—"

"I know what you were paid to do." I gripped his shoulder. "I also know what you did instead. That's the whole ledger, Finch. That's the entire entry. We're square."

"But the map—"

"Was a picture of a door," I said. "The keys are the door. And the door—" I looked out at the rising water, at the shape gathering in it, at the humming that was becoming, note by note, unmistakably, a song. "The door wants something else entirely. Come on. We're out of time."

Because the tide had begun.
