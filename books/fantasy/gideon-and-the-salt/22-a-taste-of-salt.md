## 22. A Taste of Salt

The guild had a new rule, and it was written on the door in Finch's own hand, in paint that hadn't quite dried when the first customer tried to come through it: **NOTHING IMPOSSIBLE GETS TRADED. EVERYTHING REMEMBERED GETS KEPT.**

He'd painted it twice, because the first time he'd spelled *remembered* wrong, and Marrow — who was not dead after all, only arrested and then released into a country that had no idea what to do with him — had stood behind him on the ladder and said, "If you're going to found something, boy, spell it right."

So now the letters were right, and the paint was dry, and the guild of Small Mercies had a chapter-house again: a narrow building on a street that smelled of tar and frying fish, with a cellar for the keeping-things and a kitchen above for the feeding-people. Finch ran both. He was seventeen now, or thereabouts; nobody had ever told him his birthday, so he'd picked one, the day the tide closed, and every year he would eat an extra portion and call it a feast.

The rules were simple, and he enforced them like scripture.

Nothing impossible got traded. No stolen lullabies, no widows' names, no jars of salt from drowned provinces. If someone came to the door holding a memory-object, they didn't sell it — they *deposited* it, and the guild kept it safe, and if the owner ever wanted it back, it went home free. The cellar shelves filled slowly, respectfully, like a library where every book was somebody's grief.

Everything remembered got kept. That meant ledgers — but honest ones, open ones, readable by anyone who asked. Finch kept three. One listed what the guild held. One listed who had given it. And the third, the thickest, bound in grey leather with salt-stains still ghosting its cover, listed names.

It was the third ledger people came to read.

They came at all hours. A woman whose brother had been struck from the rolls of a dissolved country twenty years ago, looking for proof he'd existed. A man who'd forgotten his own grandmother's face and wanted to know if anyone had written down her name before the forgetting took him too. Children, sometimes, sent by parents who remembered just enough to be frightened of it.

Finch never turned anyone away. He'd sit them at the kitchen table, pour them tea he'd learned to make properly — bitter, strong, with a spoonful of honey because Marrow said sweetness made truth go down easier — and he'd open the grey ledger and read until they wept or smiled or both.

The names had come from everywhere. Some from Marrow's surviving notes. Some from the exiles who drifted to the new chapter-house like iron filings to a lodestone, each carrying a scrap, a fragment, a half-remembered cousin. And some — most — from a single source.

A blank ledger, carried out of the surf by a boy with bleeding hands, its pages empty except for the faintest impression, as if words had been pressed into them once and then lifted away.

Finch had spent months reconstructing it. Not from memory — memory wouldn't serve; whatever she'd paid, she'd paid completely, and when he tried to recall her face he got a shape like light through water. But the impressions remained. He'd held page after page to candlelight at angles, breathing on the paper, coaxing up the ghosts of ink. *Vessany. Corvane. Serle.* Line after line in a hand he didn't recognize and couldn't forget, and beneath each entry, in smaller script, a name.

He read them aloud, one by one, into the grey ledger. It took him the whole winter. When he finished, on the morning of the tide's anniversary, he'd gone down to the harbor alone and stood in the shallows with his boots off and said thank you to nobody, to everybody, to the water.

Now it was spring, and the guild was quiet in the way Finch had learned to love: not empty-quiet but full-quiet, the hush of a place doing its work.

He was in the kitchen, cooking.

Marrow had taught him this too — "a guild that feeds people is a guild that lasts" — and Finch had discovered he had a talent for it, or at least a devotion, which amounted to the same thing over years. Today it was a fish stew, white wine and fennel and a broth he'd built since dawn, and he was seasoning it when the bell above the door rang.

"One moment," he called. "Sit anywhere."

He heard her come in. Footsteps soft, unhurried. He heard her take the table by the window — everyone took the table by the window; it caught the afternoon light and the smell of the harbor — and he heard her set something small on the wood, a click of glass or ceramic.

He finished the stew. Ladled it into a bowl, laid bread beside it, carried it out.

She was maybe thirty, though it was hard to say. Brown hair, ordinary face, the kind of face you forgot while looking at it — except that Finch's chest went tight and strange, the way it always did around certain strangers, the way it had done since the tide. She wore a plain coat and her hands, wrapped around a cup of the tea he'd poured without asking, were steady as bedrock.

"Stew," he said. "It's good. I don't say that about my own cooking, so you can trust it."

"I trust it," she said.

Her voice did something to him. Not recognition — there was nothing there to recognize, he'd checked that wound a hundred times — but a kind of echo, a note struck in a room he'd once lived in. He set the bowl down carefully.

"We feed anyone," he said. "That's the rule. But if you're here about the other thing—" he nodded toward the cellar door, "—the reading room's open till dark. Names are free. Everything's free. That's the other rule."

"I know your rules." She smiled, small and private, and looked down at the bowl. "I've read the door."

"The door's badly painted."

"The door's the truest thing on this street." She broke the bread, dragged it through the broth, ate. Then she stopped. Went very still, the way people do when a taste reaches back further than they expected.

Finch watched her. His heart was hammering and he couldn't have said why.

"Too much fennel?" he asked.

"No." She took another bite, slower. Then another. And then she looked up at him, and her eyes were wet, and she was smiling — smiling at the salt, at the steam, at the whole improbable fact of a hot meal in a warm room — and she said:

"It tastes like somewhere that doesn't exist anymore."

And Finch, who had spent a winter reading dead women's handwriting by candlelight, who had burned a debt-token in the surf and called it a birthday, who kept a blank ledger under his pillow because some debts aren't paid but carried — Finch felt the echo resolve into something almost like knowing, and heard himself answer:

"That's the salt. Comes from a coast that isn't on any chart. A friend of mine used to taste for a living." He swallowed. "She taught me that everything worth remembering has salt in it somewhere."

The woman set down her spoon. From her coat pocket she brought out the thing she'd set on the table when she came in — a small glass jar, sealed with wax, holding a few grains of coarse grey salt.

"For the keeping-shelves," she said. "A deposit. Not a trade."

"What do we write in the record?"

She thought about it. Outside, gulls argued over the harbor; inside, the stew steamed between them like a small weather.

"Write that it came from a taster," she said. "Who tasted seven dishes a night for years and never once let the poison reach the king. Write that she kept ledgers no one ever read, and that every word in them was true. And write—" her voice caught, steadied, "—write that she's alive, and fed, and that the food is good."

Finch took the jar with both hands, the way Marrow had taught him to take anything that mattered. He fetched the deposit ledger and wrote it all down, word for word, and when he got to the last line his pen hovered.

"And the depositor's name?" he asked. "We record everyone. That's the rule."

The woman looked out the window at the water, at the light, at the country that had forgotten her on purpose and was better for having been made to remember. She smiled again — at the salt, at the boy, at nothing, at everything.

"No name," she said. "Just write: *she was here.*"

He wrote it. She finished her stew, left coins enough for ten meals, and went out into the spring afternoon, and Finch sat a long while at the table by the window, turning the little jar in his hands, watching the grains shift like a coastline deciding where to be.

Then he carried it down to the cellar, found an empty shelf in the row marked *SALT*, and set it beside the others — a jar from a drowned province, a jar from a chartless coast, a jar from a woman with no name — and climbed back up to the kitchen, where the pot was still warm, and put more water on to boil, because someone else would be hungry soon, and the door was open, and the rule was simple:

Everything remembered gets kept.
