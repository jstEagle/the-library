## 1. The Valley That Forgot Magic

The road out of Hollowmere smelled of wet stone and woodsmoke, and Emrys Delacroix counted both as good omens.

Rain had come in the night — a soft, gray rain that left the cobbles dark and the gutters running — and by dawn it had passed on eastward toward the border, leaving the valley rinsed and shining under a sky so clean it looked newly made. Three hundred years of sky, more or less. The valley's oldest families liked to say their grandmothers' grandmothers had seen it change, though what change they meant no one could quite say, and the saying was less a memory than a habit, like touching iron before you spoke a stranger's name.

Emrys touched the iron buckle at their belt now, out of that same habit, as they led the mule cart through the south gate of Hollowmere. The gate guard, a boy named Tam with a jaw still soft from childhood, raised two fingers in the old salute — palm flat, then closed into a fist, held over the heart where a god might listen if gods listened.

"Morning, Delacroix."

"Tam." Emrys returned the salute without slowing. "Gate's latched?"

"Latched and blessed. Priest Aldren came by at first bell."

"Then we're twice protected." Emrys said it dryly, but not unkindly, and Tam grinned, because everyone in the valley knew the joke and told it anyway: *blessed twice, answered never.* Gideon was the silent god. Had been silent for three hundred years, since the founding, since the Wards went up along the valley's rim like a ring of gray teeth. No prayers answered. No omens sent. No voice in the temple's hollow, no warmth in the cold altar-stone. The priests kept the rituals anyway, all nine of them, from the Kindling at midwinter to the Closing of Hands at year's end, and the people kept them too, because a ritual was a rope thrown across a chasm, and even if nothing caught it on the far side, the throwing itself held something together.

Emrys had stopped praying at fourteen and started taking contracts at sixteen, and in the eight years between those two facts they had worked out the only theology that ever made sense to them: you did the work in front of you, you kept your blade sharp and your word sharp, and if the god wanted to notice, the god would notice. So far the god had not noticed. Emrys had decided this was fine. Mostly fine. Fine in the way that a room is fine when the window is painted shut — you stop feeling the draft after a while, or you tell yourself you do.

The mule, a bad-tempered gray named Sorrel, blew out her lips and plodded on. Behind her, the cart carried salt pork, lamp oil, two crates of nails, a bolt of good wool, and a locked strongbox with the miller's quarterly wages, which was the actual reason anyone had hired an escort at all. The wool was for the shepherds at Coldbrook. The rest was for the ward-station at Graywatch, the last outpost before the border stones.

The border stones. Everyone in the valley called them the wards, and everyone in the valley lived inside them the way a fish lives inside water — without thinking about it, without being able to imagine anything else. They ran the whole rim of the valley, forty miles of standing stones, each one taller than three men, each one carved over every inch with lines and loops and characters that no one living could read. The scholars at the archive in Underhill had spent generations on the script and gotten nowhere; the priests said the letters were Gideon's own hand, written in the language he'd spoken before he fell silent; the farmers just called them the god's stitching and left offerings of bread and thread at their bases, because bread fed travelers and thread mended things, and it seemed polite to cover both possibilities.

What the stones *did* was simpler than what they were. Nothing came in that shouldn't. Nothing went out that shouldn't. There were no wolves in the valley worth fearing, no fevers that spread, no blights on the barley, no storms that turned. And outside — well. Outside was outside. Traders came up the pass twice a season from the lowlands, and they were strange folk with strange coin and strange accents, and they never stayed long, and when children asked what the world beyond the wards was like, the adults said *loud*, and said it the way you'd say *sick*.

Emrys had stood at the ward-line exactly once, at nineteen, on a dare from a girl who'd died since, drowned in the Coldbrook flood, and had put one hand against the nearest stone. It had been warm. Not sun-warm. Warm like a sleeping animal, warm like something breathing very slowly underneath, and Emrys had snatched their hand back and laughed and said the dare was paid, and had not gone back, and had not told anyone about the warmth, mostly because there didn't seem to be anything to tell. Stones were warm in summer. It had been summer.

They thought about it sometimes anyway. Usually at night. Usually for no reason.

* * *

Graywatch station sat on a shelf of granite where the valley floor began its long climb toward the western rim, and it looked exactly like what it was: a squat stone house, a stable, a signal tower with a brazier platform, and a low wall built from the rubble of a fourth thing that had once been there and wasn't anymore. The garrison was six soldiers in a valley that needed perhaps thirty, spread thin across four stations, and their captain was Halvard Crane, who had been Emrys's friend since before either of them had been old enough to hold a real sword.

Halvard met them in the yard, wiping grease off his hands with a rag that had clearly lost the fight. He was a big man gone quietly thick around the middle, with a beard going iron-gray at the chin and forearms scarred in pale bands from a lifetime of rope and haft-work. He looked at the cart, then at Emrys, then at the sky, in the order of a man checking off a list.

"You're late."

"Road's washed out at Miller's Bend. Took the ford."

"The ford. In spring melt." Halvard shook his head with the weariness of long practice. "One day you'll drown in six inches of water and I'll have to write the report, and I'll write *cause of death: stubbornness*, and the archivist will make me change it to something dignified."

"Put *heroic stubbornness*. I earned the adjective."

Halvard grunted, which from him was laughter, and helped walk the cart into the stable while two of his soldiers — Ferro, who never talked, and young Wren, who never stopped — unloaded the crates. The strongbox went inside under Halvard's arm, and the miller's wages were signed for, and the contract was done, and that was the whole shape of Emrys's week: carry things safely from one place to another inside a ring of silent stones, get paid, sleep, do it again.

It was a small life. Emrys knew it was a small life the way you know your own hands — completely, and without much commentary. There had been a time, around seventeen, when the smallness had felt like a cage, and Emrys had walked the whole rim road in eleven days just to see whether the valley was really as big as it felt, and had come back with blistered feet and the uncomfortable discovery that the valley was exactly as big as it felt, and that the feeling of smallness hadn't come from the valley at all. It had come from inside, from some room in the chest with a painted-shut window. Eight years later, Emrys had furnished that room as best they could and learned to live in it. Restless contentment, Halvard had called it once, drunk enough to be honest. *You're a man pacing a room he refuses to leave.* Emrys had thrown a pinecone at him.

Supper at Graywatch was barley stew and the last of the autumn apples, and talk of nothing: a cow that had gone lame, a wedding at Underhill, the price of nails. Wren asked Emrys, for the third time that month, what the lowland traders were like, and Emrys gave the third version of the same answer — *tall, loud, they haggle like it's a religion* — and Wren nodded like he'd been handed scripture.

"They say the stars are different out there," Wren said. "Past the wards. More of them."

"More stars is just more distance," Ferro said, to everyone's surprise, and then returned to his silence as if embarrassed by it.

After supper Emrys climbed the signal tower out of habit, because the view from the platform was the one indulgence they allowed themselves. The valley unrolled below in the last blue light: Hollowmere's lamplight in the middle distance, the fields a darker patchwork, the river a ribbon of tarnished silver, smoke rising straight up from a hundred chimneys into air with no wind in it. And above it all, the stars coming out one by one, steady and familiar, the same stars the valley had watched for three hundred years. The Shepherd. The Loom. The Two Sisters, close together, almost touching.

Emrys leaned on the parapet and let the quiet settle in. This was the part nobody paid for and everybody needed: the ten minutes at the top of the day when the world held still. Below, Halvard's voice drifted up from the yard, giving Wren some instruction about the brazier, patient and gravel-rough. Somewhere a dog barked itself out. The valley breathed.

And beneath it all — so faint Emrys might have imagined it, might have been imagining it for years — the silence. Not the absence of sound. The other kind. The kind that sat under everything like a floor under a house, that made the valley feel *held*, the way a cupped hand holds water. Emrys had grown up inside that silence the way they'd grown up inside their own skin, and had never once thought to question it, because who questions the floor?

They noticed it most up here, actually. Or noticed the edges of it. Like a note held so long you forget it's playing until someone else stops humming.

"Delacroix." Halvard's head appeared at the top of the ladder. "You've got that look."

"What look?"

"The one where you're listening for something." He came up onto the platform, joints cracking, and leaned beside them on the parapet. For a while neither of them said anything, which was the way they'd always talked best. Then: "Contract's done. You heading back tomorrow, or staying the night?"

"Staying. Sorrel's earned a dry stall and I've earned your terrible beer."

"It's not terrible. It's honest."

"That's what terrible men say about themselves too."

Halvard huffed. Down in the yard, Wren lit the brazier — the evening watch-fire, lit every night for three hundred years, never once allowed to go out, though no one could have said precisely what it signaled or to whom. Just another rope thrown across the chasm. Emrys watched the flame catch and climb, orange against the deepening blue, and felt the day close around them like a book.

"Eight years you've been doing this," Halvard said suddenly. "Cart work. Escort work. You could take a garrison posting, you know. I'd sponsor you. Steady pay, a roof, a place in the line."

"I'd die of boredom inside a month."

"You'd die of boredom inside a month *here*, guarding a wall nothing has ever attacked."

"Nothing's attacked *yet*," Emrys said, and heard how it sounded even as they said it — the little joke that wasn't entirely a joke, the pebble in the boot. Halvard looked at them sideways, and for a moment his face did something complicated, something older than the conversation, and Emrys got the distinct feeling they'd stepped on a thought he'd been having for years and had never said aloud.

"Aldren says the wards are as strong as the day they were cut," Halvard said at last. "Three centuries, and not one crack. You know what he told me last Closing? He said the silence isn't absence. He said it's the god *holding his breath*." He shook his head. "Priests. They can't leave a mystery alone. Got to dress it up."

"And what do you say?"

Halvard was quiet long enough that Emrys turned to look at him. In the brazier light his face was half gold, half shadow, and the shadow side looked tired in a way that had nothing to do with age.

"I say a man can hold his breath a long time," he said. "But not forever."

He clapped Emrys on the shoulder — his hand heavy and warm, an anchor of a hand — and went down the ladder, leaving Emrys alone on the platform with the fire and the stars and the held breath of the world.

* * *

Emrys woke past midnight to a silence of a different shape.

Not the valley's silence — that was still there, the floor under the house, the cupped hand. But threaded through it, wrong, was a new quiet, the way a room goes quiet when someone stops speaking mid-word. Emrys lay in the guest bunk listening to it the way an animal listens, ears before mind, body knowing first.

Sorrel screamed in the stable.

Emrys was up and moving before thought caught up, knife in hand, out into the yard where the night stood strange and cold and utterly still. No wind. No insects. The brazier on the tower burned exactly as it had, but its flame stood straight up, unmoving, like a painting of a flame. Above the rim of the western ridge, where the ward-line ran, the darkness looked wrong — thicker, somehow, as if a piece of the night had been cut out and replaced with a deeper piece.

And the stars. Emrys's eyes went up and their stomach dropped, slow and total, like a floor giving way.

Over the western ridge, where the Shepherd should have been hanging low and golden, there was nothing. Not cloud-cover — the rest of the sky was clear, the Loom still turning, the Two Sisters still close together, almost touching. But the west was black. A hole in the heaven, growing.

One by one, moving eastward, the stars began to go out.

Emrys stood in the yard of Graywatch station with a knife in their hand and no enemy to point it at, watching three hundred years of certainty extinguish itself star by star, and felt — distantly, absurdly — the painted window in their chest shudder in its frame. Around them the station was waking: doors banging, Halvard's voice roaring for lanterns, Wren's high frightened questions. The mule kicked her stall to splinters.

At the rim of the valley, faint as a heartbeat heard through a wall, something that had held its breath for three hundred years finally exhaled.
