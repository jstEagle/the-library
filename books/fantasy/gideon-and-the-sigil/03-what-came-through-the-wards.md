## 3. What Came Through the Wards

They rode out at first light, and the valley pretended nothing had happened.

That was the thing about Ashvale — it had three hundred years of practice at pretending. The farmers were already in their fields, backs bent to the barley as if the sky hadn't gone black as a closed eye overhead. The smiths of Lowmark hammered away at plow-blades and hinges, and the sound carried up the slopes like a heartbeat that refused to acknowledge anything was wrong. Even the dogs had stopped howling sometime before dawn, as if they'd all agreed together to forget.

Emrys didn't forget. Their hands still remembered the shape of the silence breaking — the way it had felt like a floor tilting underfoot, like a door opening in a house they'd lived in all their life without knowing it had doors.

"You're riding like a woman going to her own funeral," Halvard said beside them. The captain sat his gray mare the way he did everything: solidly, without flourish, as though the horse were simply an extension of his own stubborn mass. "Loosen your shoulders. Whatever's at the stones, it'll still be there when we arrive."

"That's what worries me."

Halvard grunted. He'd said little since moonrise, when the stars had gone out one by one like candles snuffed by a passing hand, and little since dawn, when they'd come back — every one of them, in their proper places, as if apologizing for the inconvenience. The valley council had wanted to send forty men. Halvard had argued them down to six. *If something came through those wards,* he'd told the council chamber, his voice flat as slate, *forty men won't matter more than six. And if nothing came through, six is fewer mouths to feed on the road.* It wasn't logic anyone could argue with, which was Halvard's particular gift.

The other five rode behind them: Bren and Osla, twins who'd served under Halvard since before Emrys joined the company; young Piett, whose hands wouldn't stop fidgeting with his reins; old Maren, who'd walked the border longer than anyone alive and claimed to remember the last time the wards had even flickered; and Tam, quiet Tam, who watched everything and said less than Emrys did, which was saying something.

The border road ran north along the ridge, following the line of ward-stones the way a scar follows a wound. From here Emrys could see them at intervals down the slope — standing stones taller than two men, grey as old teeth, each carved over its whole surface with characters no one in the valley could read. The Silent Script, the elders called it. Gideon's own hand, some said. Others said the stones had been there before Gideon, and the god had merely signed work already done. In three hundred years, no one had settled the question, because in three hundred years the stones had never needed answering. They stood. They held. That was the whole of theology in Ashvale.

Except now.

Emrys saw the first wrongness from half a mile out: a smudge of color against the grey stone, like a bruise on an old man's arm. Then the wind shifted and brought the smell — copper and rot and something sweeter underneath, something that made the back of the throat close.

"Blood," Osla said quietly. She'd smelled it too.

"Halvard." Maren reined in hard, and her voice had gone strange — thin, stretched. "Captain. Look at the stones."

Emrys looked, and felt the world tilt again, the way it had at moonrise.

The ward-stone nearest the road was *open*.

There was no better word for it. The carvings that covered the stone from base to crown — dense, interlocking, a lattice of lines so fine they might have been woven rather than cut — had parted. Along a single seam running top to bottom, the script had drawn back from itself like flesh parting around a blade, and through the gap shone a light that wasn't light at all: a darkness with depth to it, a darkness you could fall into. Not broken. Not shattered. *Unlocked*, the way a door is unlocked by someone who has the key.

And the stranger lay crumpled at its foot.

* * *

Halvard took command the way he always did — not with shouting but with arrangement. "Bren, Osla, high ground on the flanks. Piett, hold the horses. Maren—" he hesitated, because what he wanted to say was *tell me this has happened before*, and they both knew she couldn't. "Maren, with me. Tam, Emrys — on my left."

They approached the stone in a loose crescent, weapons drawn, boots crunching on frost that shouldn't have been there. Frost, in late summer, in a ring ten feet wide around the ward-stone, glittering in the morning sun like spilled salt. Emrys felt it through their boots — a cold coming up out of the ground, or out of the stone, or out of the dark seam in the stone, cold with intention behind it.

The stranger was a man, or had been. Middle-aged, gaunt, dressed in travel-ruined robes of a cut Emrys had never seen — deep blue, embroidered at the cuffs and collar with silver thread in patterns that hurt to look at directly, the way the sun hurts after too long indoors. His right hand was clenched against his chest, fingers locked rigid around something. His left arm ended at the elbow in a ruin of dried blood. He was breathing in long, rattling pulls, each one sounding like it cost him something he couldn't afford.

"He's dying," Maren said. It wasn't a guess. She'd sat with enough dying men to know the sound of the last chapter.

"Can he be moved?" Halvard asked.

"Not far. Not fast."

"Then we hear what he has to say here." Halvard crouched at the man's side, careful, unhurried. "Friend. You're among soldiers of Ashvale. Can you hear me?"

The man's eyes opened. They were pale — not blind-pale, but washed-out, the blue bleached almost to white, and they went past Halvard immediately, scanning the crescent of faces, counting, dismissing, until—

Until they found Emrys.

Emrys felt it like a physical blow. The man's whole body changed — the rattle in his breath caught, his ruined hand spasmed tighter around whatever it held, and his mouth worked around words that wouldn't come. When they finally did, they came in a language Emrys had never heard, syllables like water over stones — and then, with visible effort, he dragged himself across into the valley tongue, accented so heavily it took a moment to resolve into sense.

"You. You're— " A cough took him, wet and deep. "The mark. Show me the mark."

"I don't know what you mean," Emrys said.

"You do." The pale eyes burned. "You've hidden it your whole life. Under the collar, or wrapped, or inked over — I know how your kind hides. But you *know*. You've always known you were different. You've dreamed of the stones. Haven't you. Haven't you dreamed of the stones."

Emrys hadn't answered, because there was nothing to answer with. Because the man had spoken the truth so plainly and so precisely that denial died in their throat. They *had* dreamed of the stones — all their life, dreams of walking a line of grey teeth under a black sky, dreams they'd never told another living soul, not even in confession, because the valley had no priests and the silent god accepted no confessions.

"Emrys." Halvard's voice, low. A question in it. *What is he talking about?*

Emrys didn't look at him. Couldn't.

The man struggled upright — Maren moved to stop him, thought better of it — and dragged his clenched fist away from his chest. The fingers uncurled one by one, stiff as a dead man's, and in his palm lay a disc of dark metal the size of a coin, strung on a broken chain.

A sigil. Cut into the metal with the same fine, interlocking lines as the ward-stones, the same script, the same impossible lattice — but at its center, one mark stood alone, larger than the rest, a character like a flame seen upside-down, like a root growing toward the sky.

Emrys knew that mark.

Emrys had been born with it.

It sat below their left shoulder blade, in skin the color of old tea, and their mother had traced it with one finger when they were small enough to be held and said, *the midwife says it's just a birthmark, love, just a stain, everyone has one somewhere* — and Emrys had believed her for years, until they'd grown enough to see it in a mirror and understand that no one else's stains looked like writing.

Their hand moved without permission. Fingers found the hem of their shirt, dragged it up and aside, twisting to bare the skin below the shoulder. The morning sun fell on it. The sigil in the dying man's palm and the mark on Emrys's body were the same mark, line for line, curve for curve, down to the smallest stroke.

Nobody spoke. Somewhere above, a crow called once and thought better of it.

"It's true," the man whispered. He began to laugh, and the laugh became coughing, and the coughing bent him nearly double, and when he straightened there was blood on his lips and something like joy in his ruined face. "Three years I searched. Three years, and half my order dead on the road, and it's *true* — the keystone lives. The seal has a heart, and it breathes, and it's *standing right in front of me*."

"Keystone," Halvard repeated. His voice had gone very quiet, which anyone who knew him understood was worse than shouting. "Seal. Order. You'll explain yourself, friend, slowly, and you'll start with who cut you open."

"The Order of the Unbound." The man spat the name like a bad taste. "My order. My *former* order. We are — we were — scholars. Seekers. We believe the Silence is a theft. That magic belongs to the world, to everyone, and that your god stole it and buried it here and called the grave a blessing." His eyes went to the opened stone behind him, to the seam of patient darkness. "We were right. Gods, we were right, it *can* be opened — but not broken, never broken, it must be *unlocked*, and the key—" he raised the sigil-disc with trembling fingers, "—the key answers only to the bloodline. To the bearer. To *you*."

"I'm a mercenary," Emrys said. Their voice sounded distant to their own ears. "I escort grain carts and collect debts. I was born in Lowmark. My mother mended nets."

"And my mother sang lullabies," the man said, "and none of it matters. Blood doesn't care what you do with your days. Blood doesn't care about anything except continuing." He swayed. Maren caught his shoulder and eased him back against the ward-stone, and where his skin touched the stone the frost crept up around him like a living thing, like something curious. "Listen to me. Listen, because I don't have long, and what I know dies with me unless you take it. There are others coming. Corvane is coming. Vess Corvane, First Voice of the Unbound, and she doesn't want to study the seal, she wants to *end* it, and she will burn every farm and drown every child in this valley to reach the heart of it. And the heart of it—"

His hand rose. One finger, shaking, pointed at Emrys's chest.

"—is you."

* * *

He lasted another quarter hour. Emrys would remember all of it, in fragments, for the rest of their life.

He gave them names — Corvane; a man called Serevan who led the hunters; a place called the Charnel House, far beyond the borders, where the Unbound kept what he called their *proofs*. He gave them warnings — *don't trust the refugees, when they come, and they will come; don't trust the quiet ones; Corvane wins people, she doesn't conquer them*. He tried to give them instructions and failed — *the seal can be held, it can be remade, the rites are in the temple, the old temple beyond the pass, find the murals, find the murals* — and then the sentence broke apart in his mouth like wet bread, and he never finished it.

Maren asked him, gently, near the end, whether he regretted it. Three years searching. Half his order dead. Dying in the frost with his life's work pointing at a stranger.

The man smiled. There was blood in his teeth.

"I spent my whole life," he said, "in a world with the lights turned off. And now I've seen the proof that they can come back on." His pale eyes found Emrys one last time. "Whatever you choose — break it, keep it, run from it — just *choose*. Don't be like your god. Don't spend eternity refusing to answer."

He died with the sigil-disc still clutched in his fist, and it took both Halvard and Bren working finger by finger to pry it loose.

* * *

They burned him at noon, downwind, according to the old custom for strangers who died on the border — though Emrys suspected the custom had never anticipated a stranger quite like this one. Halvard read the words of committal, the ones every soldier of Ashvale learned: *Go quiet, go unknown, go where the god does not look.* The smoke went up straight in the windless air, and the ward-stone behind them hummed — actually hummed, low and constant, a sound Emrys realized they'd been hearing since dawn without registering it, the way you don't register your own pulse.

The others had withdrawn to the horses. Halvard and Emrys remained at the fire, alone, which Emrys understood was deliberate.

"Show me again," Halvard said.

Emrys pulled up the shirt and turned. Halvard studied the mark for a long time, his face doing nothing at all, which was its own kind of eloquence.

"How long have you known?"

"Known *what*? That I have a birthmark? All my life. That it means—" Emrys gestured at the opened stone, the frost, the ash, the whole unraveling world, "—*this*? Since an hour ago. Same as you."

"That's not what I asked." Halvard crouched, fed a stick to the fire. "I asked how long you've known you were different. Because I've commanded you eight years, Emrys, and I'll tell you what I've seen. I've seen you walk a night watch in country you've never crossed and never once take a wrong turn. I've seen you stand at the ward-line during the Rite of Vigil while grown soldiers fainted from the pressure of it, and you stood there like it was a warm bath. I've seen things, and I filed them away, because you were the best soldier I had and the valley doesn't pay me to ask questions." He looked up. Firelight moved in his eyes. "So. How long?"

Emrys opened their mouth. Closed it. The truth was a door in front of them, and through it they could see everything waiting on the other side — the dreams, the stranger's dying certainty, the word *keystone*, the word *heart*, the word *bloodline* — and they understood, with a dread that sat in the stomach like swallowed stone, that once they stepped through, there would be no stepping back. For them, or for Halvard. Or for the valley.

"The dreams started when I was six," Emrys said finally. "I never told anyone. I didn't know what they meant. I still don't."

Halvard nodded slowly. He didn't say *you should have told me*. He didn't say *I trust you*. What he said was:

"Council sits tomorrow. They'll want to send riders to the capital, petition the Crown, beg for scholars and soldiers. And maybe that's right. But before they do—" he stood, joints cracking, an old man's sounds in a strong man's frame, "—I need to know whose side you're on. Because that dying man called you the heart of our seal, Emrys. And hearts can be defended." He picked up his helmet from the grass. "Or they can be cut out."

He walked away toward the horses, leaving Emrys alone with the fire, the ash, and the humming stone.

Behind them, in the parted seam of the ancient script, the darkness waited — patient, deep, and cold as the space between stars. And Emrys stood before it with a dead man's sigil in their pocket and their own name burning unreadable beneath their shirt, and understood, for the first time in their life, that they had never been standing outside the story at all.

They had been written into it before they drew breath.

And someone named Corvane was already coming to read them.
