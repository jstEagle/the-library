## 4. The Dying Man's Secret

The stranger was still breathing when the sky finished dying.

Emrys had watched enough men die to know the difference between a wound that killed quickly and one that took its time. This one was taking its time. The bolt had gone through him below the collarbone, angled down and back, and the blood pooling beneath him had stopped spreading — which meant the heart was slowing, or the cold had done what the steel couldn't quite finish. His lips had gone the color of slate. But his eyes were open, and they were fixed on Emrys with an intensity that had nothing to do with dying and everything to do with recognition.

"You," he said again. It was the third time he'd said it.

"Me," Emrys said. They crouched at arm's length, sword drawn but low, watching the tree line where Halvard had gone to check the second ward-stone. The fallen monolith lay behind them like a felled giant, its carved sigils dark for the first time in three hundred years, and above it the sky gaped starless and wrong, a black so complete it felt less like night than like a held breath. "You came through my valley's border with a crossbow bolt in you and a dead man's grip on a piece of metal. Start talking, or I start assuming you're the reason it's dark."

The man laughed. It cost him; the laugh turned into a wet cough, and when he wiped his mouth his fingers came away black in the starless light. "Assume whatever you like. You'll do it either way." He tried to prop himself against the ward-stone's flank and failed, sliding down until his shoulder met the frozen ground. He didn't seem to mind. "My name is Aldous Veyne. I was — am — a reader of the Order of the Unbound. Third chair of the Athenaeum at Coriel. I spent eleven years proving a heresy, and the Order spent six months killing me for it."

"The Order of the Unbound." Emrys turned the words over. They'd never heard them. That alone was strange; the valley remembered everything, kept ledgers of births and debts and grudges going back generations. A whole order, unnamed? "Never heard of you."

"No," Veyne said, and something in his face softened into something almost like pity. "No, you wouldn't have. That was rather the point of your valley."

*Your valley.* The phrase landed strangely, like a coin dropped down a well that never hit water. Emrys shifted their weight. "Halvard will be back soon. He's captain of the watch. If you've got last words, say them to me now or save them for him."

"I don't have last words." Veyne's hand crept along the ground, slow, deliberate, non-threatening in the way of a man who knew exactly how fast mercenary reflexes were. "I have first ones. There's a difference, and you're standing on top of it."

Emrys didn't lower the sword. But they didn't step back either, and Veyne seemed to take that as permission, because he kept talking, his voice gaining a scholar's cadence even as it thinned, the lecture reflex outliving the lungs that fed it.

"Do you know what a sigil is, warden?"

"It's a mark. Carved on stones. Keeps things out." Emrys nodded at the monolith looming over them both. "Or it did."

"A mark is a *copy*. A sigil is an *act*." Veyne's eyes glittered. "Every rune on every stone in this valley is a fragment of one great working — the largest ever cut into the world. Three centuries ago there was a war. Not a war of kingdoms. A war of *magic*, unbound, each school and covenant and cabal reaching for more, and the world burning under it. Crops that grew teeth. Rain that remembered being fire. Children born speaking languages no one taught them, languages that spoke back." His breath caught. "And then it ended. Not won. *Ended*. Overnight, everywhere, all at once — magic simply stopped. And in this valley, they told you a god did it. Gideon the Silent. Gideon who asks nothing and answers nothing. Gideon whose silence you mistake for holiness."

"Gideon is real," Emrys said flatly. "The wards are real. Whatever else—"

"The wards are real because someone *made* them." Veyne pushed himself up another inch, grimacing. "Gideon was not a god. Gideon was a person. The first sigil-bearer — the first human being to learn that magic could be written rather than merely wielded. And when the war had burned long enough, Gideon did the only thing left to do. Didn't win it. *Sealed* it. Wrote magic itself out of the world — or thought they had. Bound it beneath a working so vast it needed anchors. Three keystones. Places where the seal grips the world and holds." He looked up at the dark, dark sky. "This valley isn't beside the seal, warden. This valley *is* the seal. Every farm, every fence, every prayer your people whisper to a god who never answers — you've been living inside the lock your whole life."

The words went through Emrys like cold water through a cracked wall. They thought of the valley's rituals: the hour of silence at dusk, the way no one sang above a certain pitch near the border, the old law that no child was told the shape of the sigils until they swore the watch. Three hundred years of careful, reverent ignorance. They'd always assumed the reverence was for Gideon.

It had been for the walls.

"And the stars?" Their voice came out steadier than they felt. "What did the stars ever do to anyone?"

"The stars are the oldest sigil-work there is. The first map of the binding. When the keystones hold, the heavens hold their pattern. Tonight two of the three failed." Veyne's gaze dropped to the object in his other hand — the thing he'd been clutching since Emrys pried him from the mud. A disc of dull metal, palm-sized, etched with a spiral of interlocking marks. Even dark, even dead, it made Emrys's teeth ache to look at, the way a sound just below hearing does. "The Order broke the second keystone tonight. We broke the first a month ago, in the Ashen Reaches. No one noticed there. Everyone will notice here."

"*We*," Emrys repeated. "You said the Order killed you for your heresy."

"They did. Slowly. A knife here, a lie there, until I understood I had two choices: die quiet, or die loud carrying the truth to the one place it mattered." Veyne smiled, and it was a terrible smile, the smile of a man who had run the numbers on his own death and found them acceptable. "So I stole the third keystone's key and ran. And the Order followed. The bolt in me is theirs. The rest—" he lifted the disc a fraction, "—is mine. My life's work. Proof."

"Proof of what?"

Veyne didn't answer with words. With the last of his strength he dragged the disc toward the light of Emrys's lantern, tilting it, and the flame caught the spiral of marks — and Emrys stopped breathing.

Because Emrys knew those marks.

Had known them since before knowing anything, since childhood baths and the private shame of a body marked differently than everyone else's, since the midwife's frown and the mother's careful silence and the years of keeping a sleeve pulled down. Since the birthmark that curled over their left ribs and up toward the shoulder, a spiral of interlocking lines that no healer could name and no priest would bless.

The mark on the disc was the same mark. Line for line. Curve for curve.

"That's not possible," Emrys said. Their voice sounded far away.

"Everything about you is impossible," Veyne said gently. "That's what I'm trying to tell you. The seal needed a keystone that couldn't be stolen, couldn't be moved, couldn't be broken by any force the Order could raise. So Gideon did the cruelest, most brilliant thing imaginable. Gideon hid the final keystone in the one place no one would think to look." His bloody hand rose, trembling, and pointed — not at the ward-stone, not at the valley, but at Emrys's chest. At the ribs beneath the wool and leather. "In a person. In a bloodline. Sealed into flesh and passed down, generation to generation, thinned and hidden, until the night it was needed. You aren't standing inside the lock, warden. You *are* the lock. The living keystone. The last piece of Gideon's working still walking around with a heartbeat."

The world tilted. Emrys heard themselves speak from a great distance. "You're lying. You're dying, and you want company, and you picked—"

"I picked the only person in this valley whose skin carries the master-sigil." Veyne's voice was fraying now, each word costing more than the last. "I tracked the bloodline for nine years. I know what you are better than you do. Better than your mother knew, gods forgive her. Listen to me. *Listen.* The Order doesn't want the valley. They don't want the stones. They want *you*. Break the living keystone and the seal dies everywhere, all at once, and magic comes home to the world — free, wild, and hungry. Corvane thinks that's liberation. Corvane has never watched rain catch fire." His hand seized Emrys's wrist, grip shockingly strong for a dying man, and pulled them close. His breath smelled of copper and cold. "There is a way to hold it. There has to be. The texts are incomplete, but the seal was *written*, and anything written can be—"

He stopped.

Not because he chose to. Because something behind his eyes went out, the way the stars had gone out, one flicker and then darkness, and his grip on Emrys's wrist loosened finger by finger until it was only the cold holding his hand there.

Emrys knelt in the snow beside the fallen ward-stone, holding the wrist of a dead man, under a sky with no stars, wearing a birthmark that was apparently the last lock on the world.

They stayed that way for a long time.

* * *

Halvard found them there when the lantern had burned down to a stub.

She came through the trees at a jog, sword drawn, and stopped dead at the edge of the ward-circle. Emrys saw her take it in piece by piece, the way she read any battlefield: the dead man, the dark disc in Emrys's hand, the way Emrys was sitting — not crouched ready, not standing guard, just *sitting*, knees drawn up, like someone who'd forgotten what their body was for.

"Report," she said, because Halvard always said report, because ritual was how she kept the world bolted together.

"He's called Aldous Veyne. Scholar. He was running from the people who shot him." Emrys's voice came out sanded flat. "Halvard. The stars are out because the wards are down. All of them. Both borders."

"I can count, Emrys."

"There's more." Emrys looked down at the disc. In the lantern light the spiral of marks sat there, patient, familiar, obscene. "And I can't give you the more. Not yet. Maybe not—"

"Don't." Halvard's voice cracked like a whip, and then, immediately, quieter: "Don't do that. Twenty-two years. You've slept across the fire from me since we were fifteen, and you've lied to me exactly twice, and both times I let it go because you came back and stood your watch anyway. Don't make it a third."

Emrys opened their mouth. Closed it. What was the sentence? *I am the last piece of a god's machinery. I am the thing everyone in this valley has been praying around without knowing it. If I die wrong, the world ends.* There was no way to say it that didn't sound like madness or confession, and Emrys wasn't sure yet which it was.

"Halvard," they said finally, "if I tell you, you'll have to decide what to do about it. And I need time before anyone decides anything about me. One day. Give me one day."

Something moved behind Halvard's eyes — hurt, mostly, banked hard under discipline. She looked at the dead man for a long moment. Then she sheathed her sword, knelt, and began going through his pack with the brisk efficiency of twenty years' habit, not looking at Emrys while she did it.

"One day," she said. "Then you tell me everything, or I tell the council what I saw here tonight. Dead stranger. Dark stone. And you sitting in the snow looking like a man reading his own death warrant." Her hands paused over a bundle of letters sealed with a spiral sigil — the Order's mark, though neither of them knew it yet. "We should move. Whoever shot him might come back for the body. Or for us."

Emrys rose. Their ribs ached under the bindings where the birthmark sat, and for the first time in their life they wondered if it had always ached, faintly, and they'd simply mistaken it for the ordinary wear of being alive.

They took the disc from Veyne's stiffening hand. It was warm. That was the worst part — the metal was warm, as if something inside it were still working, still waiting, patient as a heart.

As they turned toward the tree line, Emrys caught sight of the letters in Halvard's pack, the wax seals catching the lantern flame — and froze. Because stamped into the wax, plain as daylight, was the same spiral. The Order's mark. The mark on the disc. The mark on Emrys's own skin.

Whoever these people were, they had already been writing to each other about Emrys for a very long time.

"Halvard," Emrys said quietly. "We need to talk about those letters."
