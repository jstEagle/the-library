## 5. Sworn to Silence

The council chamber smelled of tallow smoke and old cedar, the way it had smelled every market-day of Emrys's life. Twenty-three chairs in a half-circle, twenty-three faces lit by a fire that had burned in that hearth since before anyone living was born. The Speaker's chair at the center was empty — it had been empty for three hundred years, kept for a god who never came — and so the deliberations fell, as they always did, to whoever spoke loudest and longest.

Tonight that was Aldous Merrin, the grain-factor, whose voice had the particular carrying power of a man who had never once doubted himself.

"—riders to the capital. That is the whole of it. We send riders, we ask the Crown what has happened to the sky, and we ask them to send soldiers or priests or whatever else they keep for emergencies." He struck the arm of his chair with a flat palm. "The wards are down. The stars are *out*. We do not sit in this room and debate while the world outside our border decides what it wants to be."

Emrys stood at the back wall with the other off-duty swords, arms crossed, watching the fire. They had been summoned an hour after dawn, still smelling of the ward-stones, still with the dead man's blood dried brown under their fingernails no matter how many times they had scrubbed. Halvard stood beside them, solid as ever, but Emrys had felt the captain's eyes on them twice already — once when Aldous mentioned the fallen stones, and once when he mentioned the stranger found among them.

*Unlocked,* Emrys thought. *Not broken. Unlocked.* And the sigil in the dying man's fist, the same mark that sat beneath Emrys's own skin like a second heartbeat. Those words had not yet been spoken aloud in this room. Emrys intended to keep it that way.

"The capital is nine days' ride," said Maren Ostrey, the weaver, in her dry way. "If there is still a capital. If there is still a road."

"There is a road."

"There was a road. The last carter through told us the toll-houses were empty. Empty houses don't collect tolls, Aldous."

"Then we send riders who can fight."

"We have perhaps forty fighters in the whole valley," Maren said, "and you want to spend eight of them on a road that may not exist anymore."

Aldous turned to the fire, to the line of swords along the back wall. "And what would the mercenaries say? You walked the border. You saw the stones. Tell them."

Every face turned. Emrys felt the attention settle on them like weather.

They stepped forward into the firelight. "The stones weren't toppled," they said. Their voice came out level, which surprised them. "They were opened. There's a mechanism in the bases — old work, older than anything in the valley. Someone unlocked them from the outside. Deliberately."

Murmurs moved around the half-circle like wind through wheat.

"And the man?" asked old Sera Voss, who kept the shrine candles and had buried more of the valley's dead than anyone. Her eyes were milky but missed nothing. "The one you carried down from the pass. What did he die of?"

"Wounds," Emrys said. It was true. It was also a lie, because of everything else it left out. "He'd been hurt before he reached the stones. He didn't last the night."

"What did he say?" Sera pressed. "Men who crawl to a border to die usually want something said over them."

*Gideon was no god.* The scholar's blood-wet whisper rose in Emrys's memory with terrible clarity. *The valley is the seal. You are the keystone. Find the temple before—*

"He said a name," Emrys said. "I couldn't make it out."

Sera looked at them for a long moment. Blind eyes, seeing everything. Then she nodded slowly, and let it go, and Emrys learned in that moment what it cost to lie to someone who had known you since you were small enough to sit in her lap during candle-lighting: it cost exactly as much as you would have paid to tell the truth, and you paid it anyway, silently, forever.

Halvard shifted beside them. Said nothing. Which was its own kind of testimony.

* * *

The vote, when it finally came near midnight, was fourteen to seven for sending riders. Four volunteers stepped forward within the hour — Tam Weller, who was young and eager; the brothers Ansel and Rook; and a scarred woman named Ivetta who had come up from the lowlands fifteen years ago and never spoken of why. They would leave at first light, armed, provisioned, and carrying a letter sealed with the valley's plain wax stamp: *The wards have failed. The stars are dark. Send help.*

Emrys watched them saddle up in the grey cold from the doorway of the stable, and felt something in their chest pull tight and strange.

It wasn't fear for the riders, though there was that too. It was the shape of the letter itself. *Send help.* As if help were a thing that could arrive from outside and fix this. As if the problem were out there, beyond the border, rather than standing in the stable door wearing a sword and carrying a secret the size of the sky.

Because here was the arithmetic Emrys had been doing all night, in the gaps between the council's voices, in the dark behind their eyes when sleep wouldn't take: if the scholar had told the truth — and every instinct Emrys owned, every scarred and pragmatic instinct honed by thirty years of mercenary work, said he had — then the seal holding magic out of the world ran through the valley like a spine. And the keystone of that seal, the piece that held the whole architecture together, was not a stone or a shrine or a god's forgotten name.

It was a person. It was *them*. Written into their blood before they could walk, before they could choose, by ancestors who had apparently decided that one line of the family would carry the weight of the world and never be told.

And if Emrys said that aloud — in that chamber, to those people — what happened next?

They knew these people. Loved some of them. Had bled for most of them. And they knew, with the same certainty, exactly how it would go. First there would be silence, the particular stunned silence of people reordering their entire understanding of a person they thought they knew. Then Aldous Merrin would start talking, because Aldous always started talking, and what he would say was: *we must protect them.* And protection, in a valley suddenly ringed by a dark and hungry world, meant walls, and guards, and decisions made about where Emrys could go and what Emrys could touch and whom Emrys could speak to — for their own good, of course. For everyone's good.

Or worse. Or the other thing, the thing Emrys couldn't quite look at directly but which lived in the back of the skull like a splinter: if the seal needed a keystone, and the seal was failing, then somewhere down the road — maybe soon, maybe desperate — someone in that chamber would begin to wonder whether the keystone could be *used*. Whether blood could be spilled on a ward-stone in the right pattern, whether a sacrifice freely given might hold where three centuries of quiet had failed. Whether Emrys Fairweather, sworn sword, orphan of no family anyone remembered, might be worth more to the valley dead on an altar than alive in a barracks.

They wouldn't say it. Not one of them would ever say it. But Emrys had spent their life reading rooms full of frightened people, and frightened people did not need to say things aloud for those things to become real.

So: silence. Sworn to it now, though no oath had been spoken. Keeper of a truth that grew heavier every hour, like a stone carried in a pack that refilled itself each dawn.

"You didn't tell them about the sigil."

Halvard's voice, from the shadows of the stable. Emrys hadn't heard him approach, which was rare enough to be alarming; Halvard moved like a barn door.

"It wouldn't have helped," Emrys said.

"That's not the same as it being right to leave out."

"No," Emrys admitted. "It isn't."

Halvard came forward into the grey light. He looked tired in a way Emrys had never seen — not the bone-tiredness of a long patrol but something deeper, a man whose map of the world had been redrawn overnight without his consent. "You've been strange since the pass," he said. "Since you knelt over that body. I've commanded you eleven years, Emrys. I know your silences the way I know my own horse's. This one has teeth."

Emrys looked at him — at the broad, honest face, the grey coming into his beard, the man who had taught them how to hold a shield-line and how to drink without weeping and how to bury friends — and felt the truth press against the backs of their teeth like a fever.

Tell him. Tell *him*, at least. One person. Split the weight.

But Halvard was a soldier of the valley, sworn to its council, bound by its laws. And the law said every finding went to the council. Tell Halvard, and either he lied too — and Emrys would have made him complicit, bent him into the same isolation — or he did his duty, and the truth went into that chamber, and the arithmetic began.

"I'll tell you when I can," Emrys said. "That's all I've got. When I can."

Something flickered across Halvard's face — hurt, quickly mastered, and beneath the hurt a stubborn, muleish loyalty that refused, even now, to fully close the door. "See that you do," he said gruffly. "Before the not-being-able-to becomes a habit." He turned toward the stable, then paused. "The riders will pass the third ward-stone on the road out. You checked it?"

"This morning. Holding. For now."

"For now," Halvard repeated, and the two words hung in the cold air between them like a prophecy neither wanted to claim. He went inside, and Emrys stood alone in the doorway and watched the eastern sky, where the sun was rising into a sky that still had no stars waiting beyond it, only a blackness like a held breath.

* * *

Three days later, the first of them arrived.

Not riders. Not soldiers. Refugees — a ragged column of eleven souls coming down the old north road, dusty and hollow-eyed, carrying bundles and children and the particular exhausted silence of people who had walked away from something. The gate-watch brought word to the barracks, and Emrys went with Halvard to see them processed, because that was procedure, because procedure was a comfort, because doing the ordinary thing felt like holding a plank in floodwater.

They were ordinary. That was the first thing Emrys noticed, and the second, and the third. A miller with burned forearms. Two sisters who might have been fisherwomen. A boy asleep against his mother's shoulder. An old man with a walking staff and a scholar's stoop. They gave their names in low voices — from Harrowmere, from the river towns, from places Emrys knew only as names on maps that had seemed decorative until last week. The wards had fallen everywhere, one of them said. Everything past the border was... changed. They'd walked nine days.

Emrys stood at the edge of the registration table and did what they always did with strangers: read them. Hands, boots, eyes, the wear-patterns on gear. It was habit, trade-craft, nothing personal.

Which is why it stopped their heart.

The miller — big man, burned arms, humble manner — wore a belt-knife. Common enough. But the scabbard's throat was bound with cord in a pattern Emrys had seen exactly once before: wound three times, crossed twice, knotted in a loop that had no beginning. The same binding had held the scholar's satchel shut on the night the stars went out. And on the old man's staff, half-hidden by a gnarled hand, a small brass ferrule stamped with a mark that Emrys's fingers remembered better than their eyes: the shape burned into the fallen ward-stone's base. The unlock-shape. The keyhole-signature of whatever had opened the valley's defenses.

Eleven refugees. Three marks, if you counted carefully. Maybe more, if you counted the way Emrys was learning to count.

They said nothing. They signed the register as witness, welcomed the newcomers in the valley's ritual words — *be safe, be fed, be counted among us* — and walked away with their skin crawling.

That evening they climbed the watchtower above the east gate alone and looked out at the dark horizon, and made themselves think it through like a campaign, because thinking like a campaign was the only discipline keeping the fear from becoming a scream.

The Order — if that was what the scholar's killers called themselves, if that was what these marked ones served — had not come as an army. Armies were visible. Armies triggered walls and militias and the valley's forty swords. No: they had come as the weak, the wounded, the weary, wrapped in the valley's own oldest virtue, its duty of shelter. Every gate in the valley would open for people like these. Every kitchen would feed them. Every bed would warm them. And they would spread outward, patient as ink in water, until the day their masters gave the word and the friendly faces turned.

Unless the word had already been given. Unless the unlocking of the ward-stones had been only the first move, the prying of the lid, and what came next required hands inside the valley. Hands that needed a place to sleep and bread to eat and time to search.

Search for what?

Emrys's hand drifted, without permission, to the birthmark under their collarbone. The sigil. The keystone-mark. The thing that made them, according to a dead man's last breath, the linchpin of the world.

*They're not looking for the seal,* Emrys thought, with a certainty that settled into the bones like cold water. *They're looking for me.*

Below, the valley lay in its lamplit evening peace — cook-smoke rising, the shrine candles kindling one by one, children being called in from lanes that had known no darkness deeper than a lamp's for three hundred years. Somewhere in that lamplight, three strangers with knotted cords and brass ferrules were being folded into the community's warmth, given beds, given trust.

And Emrys stood above it all, sworn to a silence that was starting to feel less like discretion and more like a noose, knowing that the next move belonged to the enemy — and that the only person in the valley who understood the game was the one person who could not explain it without ending the world they were trying to protect.

Far out past the border, where the stars should have been, something flickered. Once. Twice. Like a signal answered.

Like a count beginning.
