## 6. The Order of the Unbound

The refugees came up the north road on the third morning after the stars went out, and the valley took them in because that was what the valley did.

Emrys watched them from the wall above the Northgate, arms folded against the cold that had crept into a season that should have been turning warm. There were perhaps forty of them — families mostly, carters and shepherds by their hands, driving thin oxen and carrying children too young to understand why the sky had gone wrong. They wore the dust of the borderlands and the particular hollowness of people who had walked away from everything and not yet decided where they were walking to.

"Forty-one," said the gate-sergeant beside Emrys, marking her tally-slate. "Council says quarter them in the tithe-barns. Feed them from the autumn stores."

"The stores won't last," Emrys said.

"The stars went out, Delacroix. Nothing's going to last." The sergeant spat over the parapet. "But we don't turn people away at the gate. Gideon's law."

Gideon's law. Emrys bit down on the reply — *the wards are down, and you're letting strangers walk straight to the heart of the valley* — and said nothing. That was the trouble with a god who never answered: everyone got to decide for themselves what he would have wanted.

They descended to the gateyard. Halvard was already there, running the intake with the brisk kindness he wore like armor, pointing families toward the barns, ordering water drawn, pausing to lift a child off a cart and set her on her feet. He looked better than he had since the night of the fall — purpose suited him, filled in the hollows under his eyes. He'd barely slept either. None of them had. But Halvard slept badly out loud, and Emrys had learned to sleep badly quietly, and between them they made something that looked almost like composure.

"You should be resting," Halvard said when Emrys came near. "You were on watch until dawn."

"So were you."

"I'm old. It's expected." He glanced past Emrys at the line of newcomers shuffling through the gate. "They came fast. Borderlands are three days' hard walk, and these folk look like they ran most of it."

"They're scared," Emrys said. "Scared people run."

"Mm." Halvard's gaze lingered on the column a moment longer than it needed to. Then a carter shouted about a lame ox, and he was pulled away, and the moment passed the way moments did now — quickly, uneasily, like a fish slipping a hook.

Emrys stayed in the gateyard through the afternoon, ostensibly helping, actually watching.

It was the gear that caught the eye, once you knew to look. Most of the refugees carried what refugees carried: bundles, bedrolls, cooking pots lashed to packs. But six of them — scattered through the column, never together, never speaking to one another — carried their goods differently. Their straps were doubled and cross-lashed in the manner of soldiers who expected to drop a pack and fight. Their boots were wrong for farmers: oiled leather, high-cuffed, worn at the ball of the foot in the pattern of long road-marching rather than field work. And each of the six wore some small thing of worked metal — a buckle, a clasp, a knife-pommel — stamped with a mark.

A circle, broken by a vertical stroke, wrapped around a smaller closed ring.

Emrys knew that mark. Had traced it in ash and blood on the chest of a dying man among the fallen ward-stones, while his mouth shaped words his lungs could no longer push out. The scholar's satchel had been covered in it, embossed and inked and burned into the leather — the sigil of whatever order had sent him to die at the border with a warning half-delivered.

*The valley is the seal. You are the keystone.*

None of the six looked at Emrys. That was the first thing that truly frightened them into stillness. Refugees looked at everyone — at the walls, the guards, the granaries, anything that meant safety or its absence. These six looked at nothing. They had catalogued the gateyard before entering it and now performed the looking without doing it, the way Emrys themselves had done on a hundred jobs, moving through a place while measuring it for exits and threats.

Hunters dressed as quarry. And only Emrys saw it.

* * *

The council met at dusk and voted, as Emrys had known they would, to send riders south to the capital — four men and a wagon of petitions, asking the Crown to send ward-smiths, soldiers, anyone who remembered how the world outside worked. Three hundred years of isolation had left the valley rich in grain and poor in memory. They believed problems could be petitioned. They believed someone, somewhere, was minding things.

Emrys sat through the session with their hands flat on the table and their silence like a stone in their throat. Twice the archivist, old Maren, glanced their way — she had that quality of looking through people to the shape of what they hid — but no one asked Emrys to speak, and Emrys did not volunteer. What would they say? *Six of your refugees are soldiers of an order that wants the seal broken, and I know this because a dying man told me I'm the lock.* They would be locked in the temple cellar for their own protection, or worse, given to the valley as a talisman, a thing to bargain with. Emrys had spent thirty years being useful and unremarkable. It was the safest shape a life could take, and they were not going to surrender it in a single evening.

Afterward, in the corridor, Maren fell into step beside them.

"You saw something at the stones," she said. Not a question.

"I saw a man die."

"People see men die all the time. They don't come back from it looking like you do." She stopped. Her eyes were pale and quick, a hawk's eyes in a grandmother's face. "Whatever you're carrying, child, carry it carefully. Secrets are heavy, and they bend the spine toward the ground exactly when you need your head up."

Then she walked on, leaving Emrys standing in the lamplight with their spine very straight and their stomach very cold.

* * *

Emrys began following the six the next morning.

Not all at once — that was how amateurs got caught. They learned the rhythm of the tithe-barns first, volunteering for guard rotation the way they always did, becoming furniture. The six moved separately through the refugee camp: a grey-bearded man called Ostrec who mended harnesses with a soldier's economy; two sisters, dark-haired, quiet, who drew water and watched the well-heads; a young one, barely twenty, with a burn scar on his jaw who asked too many questions about the valley's history and too few about its food; a broad woman named Sefa who laughed easily and whose laughter never reached her eyes; and the sixth, a lean, silver-tongued man called Corvane — Vess Corvane — who had appointed himself the refugees' spokesman within a day and was very good at it.

Corvane was the reason Emrys stopped sleeping properly, such as sleep was.

He stood in the barn-yard in the evenings and told stories, and the displaced gathered to him like heat gathers to a hearth. He told them the stars would return. He told them the Crown would help, and if it didn't, then the valley's neighbors would, and if they didn't, then the world itself would remember its debts. He told them suffering had a purpose, though he never said what purpose, and people nodded because they wanted so badly for their suffering to mean something. He had a voice built for doorways and thresholds, warm and resonant, and a face that seemed assembled to be trusted — open brow, steady gaze, a scar across one cheekbone that suggested a life honestly risked.

And every night, when the fires burned low, Vess Corvane found a reason to walk the perimeter of the camp. Alone. Counting.

Emrys counted him counting.

On the fourth night, Emrys followed him out.

* * *

The camp lay east of the town, in the fallow fields below the terraces, and beyond it the land rose into orchards gone wild at the valley's edge, and beyond those, the foothills where the broken ward-line ran. Corvane walked the perimeter first, unhurried, nodding to the sentries, exchanging pleasantries. Then, when the fires had guttered to coals, he stepped over the low stone wall at the camp's eastern edge and climbed into the orchards, and his gait changed. No more refugee's shuffle. He moved like Emrys moved — weight low, steps placed, breath metered.

Emrys followed at forty paces, downwind, matching him shadow for shadow through the apple trees. The moonless dark helped; the stars were still gone, and the nights were blacker than any Emrys had known, as if the whole sky had been shuttered. Underfoot, frost crackled where no frost should be in late spring. The cold was wrong. Everything since moonrise on that first night had been wrong, and the wrongness was spreading inward from the borders like watercolor bleeding through paper.

Corvane stopped in a clearing where an old boundary-marker stood — one of the minor waystones, not a true ward-stone but kin to them, a finger of granite carved with Gideon's closed-eye sign. He crouched beside it and drew back his sleeve.

Even at forty paces, even in the dark, Emrys saw the light.

It came from the metal band on his forearm — the same broken-circle mark, but here it was not a stamp. It was cut into his flesh, fine lines of silvered scarring, and it was glowing, faintly, the way embers glow. As Emrys watched, Corvane pressed his palm flat against the waystone and whispered something too soft to carry, and the stone answered.

It shouldn't have. Nothing in the valley answered. That was the whole covenant, the whole three-hundred-year bargain: the silent god kept magic out, and silence was the price of safety. Stones did not glow. Prayers did not echo. But the waystone's carved eye flickered — once, twice — like a lamp guttering in wind, and Corvane exhaled with something that sounded horribly like satisfaction.

"Well," he murmured, to the stone or to himself. "Still alive in there. Good."

He rose, and Emrys pressed deeper into the tree-shadow, heart hammering — and that was when the hand closed on their shoulder from behind.

Emrys moved on instinct, years of it: pivot, break the grip, elbow back, blade clearing the sheath — and stopped, because the blade's edge rested against a throat that belonged to the grey-bearded man, Ostrec, who had somehow gotten inside Emrys's reach without a sound, and whose own knife was already at the join of Emrys's neck and shoulder. Old man's hands, soldier's speed. A stalemate in the dark, both blades trembling with restraint.

"Easy," Ostrec breathed. "Easy. If I wanted you dead, you'd never have heard me coming."

"You didn't," Emrys whispered back.

"No." Something like respect crossed the seamed old face. "Nobody does. That's rather the point of you, isn't it?"

Before Emrys could parse that, a voice spoke from the clearing, warm as a banked fire.

"That'll do, Ostrec. Let our guest come and sit."

Ostrec withdrew his knife and stepped back, and Emrys — because there was nothing else to do, because running would confirm everything and answer nothing — turned and walked into the clearing with their blade still bare and their mind racing through escape routes and finding none worth the name.

Vess Corvane stood by the waystone with his sleeves rolled down, the silvered sigil hidden again, smiling the smile from the firelight. Up close, in the starless dark, the warmth of it didn't quite reach his eyes. Those were patient eyes. Eyes that had waited a long time for something and were pleased to find it early.

"Emrys Delacroix," he said. "Sworn of Gideon. Best blade on the border watch, if the talk in the taverns means anything. Four days in the valley and already I've heard three songs about you." He gestured to the waystone. "You've been following my people since the gate. Don't insult us both by denying it — you're good, but you count your steps when you think, and you think constantly. So. Let's skip to the part where we're honest with each other."

"What's in the stone?" Emrys said.

Corvane's smile widened a fraction. "Direct. I like that too." He laid his palm on the granite again, gently, almost fondly. "Magic. A thread of it. Every stone in this valley has a thread of it, spun through the rock like wire through wax. Your god didn't banish magic, mercenary. He *bottled* it. Sealed it into the stones and the soil and the bloodlines, and built your pretty valley on top of the cork." He tapped the stone twice. "Three centuries, it's been screaming in there. Quietly. The way things scream when no one's allowed to hear."

"Gideon ended the war," Emrys said. "That's the teaching."

"Gideon *started* the war," Corvane said pleasantly, "or finished it, depending which corpse you ask — and then, rather than heal what was broken, he buried it alive and told the survivors it was peace. Your valley isn't holy ground, Delacroix. It's a lid." He stepped closer, and his voice dropped into something softer, more intimate, more dangerous. "We're the Order of the Unbound. We intend to take the lid off."

"And kill everything in the valley when it comes off?"

"Kill? No." Genuine surprise, genuine hurt, theatrically perfect. "We want to *free* it. Magic belongs in the world — in everyone, not just in stones and not just—" he paused, delicately, "—not just locked in one unlucky bloodline. The age of hiding is over. The stars went out because the seal is failing, and failing seals don't negotiate. Either we open it carefully, on our terms, or it tears on its own and takes the valley with it. We're offering mercy. We're the only ones offering anything at all."

"You killed a scholar at the ward-stones," Emrys said. "Four nights ago. Left him dying in the dark."

Something flickered behind Corvane's eyes — calculation, quick and cold, there and smoothed away. "Did we," he said. "How sad. The borderlands are full of sad things lately." He spread his hands. "Here's what I know, Emrys Delacroix. You've felt it since the night the stars died — that pull, that itch under the skin. You patrol a border you've never once crossed. You serve a god who has never once spoken. You are the most awake person in a valley of sleepwalkers, and you are *miserable*, and you are *alone*, and you are very, very good at pretending otherwise." His voice gentled further, became almost kind. "I'm offering you a place where pretending isn't necessary."

For one treacherous heartbeat, Emrys felt the offer land — felt it slide into the hollow that thirty years of competent solitude had never quite filled. *The valley never truly wanted you. It tolerated you. It used you.* Corvane watched that heartbeat happen, and smiled, because he had clearly watched it happen in a hundred faces before.

Then Emrys thought of the dying scholar's blood on the frost. Of Halvard's face at the gate, lifting a stranger's child down from a cart. Of Maren's pale hawk-eyes. Of forty-one refugees fed from stores the valley could not spare, because that was what the valley did, lid or no lid.

"No," Emrys said.

Corvane sighed, theatrical regret. "No. Of course. Not tonight." He stepped back, and Ostrec melted forward to flank him, and somewhere in the orchard darkness Emrys sensed the other four — the sisters, the scarred boy, Sefa — closing positions they had held all along. They had let Emrys follow. They had led Emrys here. *Hunter becoming hunted,* sang the cold voice in the back of their skull, *and you walked into it whistling.*

"Then understand me plainly," Corvane said, and the warmth left his voice entirely, and what remained was worse — reasonable, measured, final. "The seal breaks within the season whether you help or not. The keystones are falling, one by one, and no petition to the capital will stop it. When it breaks, I would rather it broke gently. Stand in the way, and I will be sorry — briefly." He inclined his head, courteous as a duelist. "Go home, warden. Kiss the valley goodnight. And when you lie awake listening to the silence — listen closely. It's thinner than it used to be."

Emrys backed out of the clearing with their blade bare until the trees swallowed them, and then ran — not from fear, or not only fear, but because every second in that orchard was a second Corvane could study them, and they had shown him far too much already. Shown him the doubt. Shown him the loneliness. Shown him the hook, so he'd know precisely what bait to use next time.

Behind them, faint through the black branches, Emrys heard Corvane speaking softly to the waystone again — and heard, unmistakably now, the stone answering. A low hum, rising, like a voice remembering how.

At the orchard's edge Emrys stopped, bent double, breath steaming in air far too cold for spring, and made themselves look back at the sky.

The stars were still gone. All of them. But along the northern horizon, where the broken ward-line ran, there was a new light — faint, silver, wrong — pulsing in slow rhythm, like a heartbeat under the skin of the world.

Counting down.

Emrys turned and ran for the town, for Halvard, for the truth they could no longer afford to keep — knowing, with sick certainty, that they were already too late, and that somewhere behind them in the dark, Vess Corvane was smiling.
