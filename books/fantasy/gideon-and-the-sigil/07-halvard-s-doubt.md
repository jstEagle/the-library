## 7. Halvard's Doubt

The lie had been small. That was what Emrys kept telling themselves as they mucked out the stables at dawn, three days after the wards fell and the stars went dark. *A small lie. A necessary one.* The stranger's sigil was buried in a leather pouch beneath the floorboards of their quarters, wrapped in oilcloth like something already dead. The dying man's words were buried deeper, in the place where Emrys kept everything they'd never said aloud.

*The valley is the seal. You are its keystone.*

They shoveled hay and told themselves the stink of it was honest work, honest sweat, and if they kept their hands busy enough, the rest of them might stay quiet too.

Halvard found them before the second stall was done.

He didn't announce himself — he never did, a habit from twenty years of soldiering that Emrys had inherited along with his training, his scarred practice sword, and half his vocabulary. He just leaned against the stable door with his arms crossed, gray-bearded, gray-eyed, watching Emrys work with an expression that had no weather in it at all. Flat. Waiting.

"You missed muster," he said.

"I was on watch until moonset."

"You were relieved at moonset. Muster was after." He let that sit. "You've missed two musters since the wards fell, Emrys. You've eaten alone twice. And yesterday I watched you walk past Marta's forge without going in, which even her dead husband wouldn't have done."

Emrys pitched another forkful of straw. "I'm tired."

"You're lying."

The word landed like a dropped stone in still water. Emrys kept their back to him, kept shoveling, but their hands had gone clumsy, and Halvard had taught them to read hands the way other people read faces.

"Three nights ago," Halvard said, conversationally, terribly, "when we walked down to the ward-stones together, you knew things you shouldn't have known. You went straight to the fourth stone. Not the first, not the nearest — the fourth, half a mile through the dark, like you'd already been told where to look. And when we found the man dying there, you looked at his hand before you looked at his face. At the thing in his hand." He paused. "And then you lied to me about it. In front of the council. You said you'd never seen the mark before."

"Halvard—"

"Don't." His voice didn't rise. It never rose; that was the frightening thing about it. "I've carried you off two battlefields. I taught you how to hold a shield so a spear doesn't take your arm off. I stood at your mother's grave beside you because she had no one else and neither did you. So don't stand there shoveling shit and tell me you're tired."

Emrys set the fork down slowly. The stable was dim, smelling of straw and horse and the cold coming through the gaps in the boards. Somewhere outside, the valley was waking up to its third morning under a starless sky, and the whole settlement wore the look of people waiting for a fever to break.

"The man was dying," Emrys said finally. "There was nothing he could tell us."

"That's not what I asked."

"He was Order of the Unbound. Or he'd been running from them. It amounts to the same thing."

Halvard's jaw tightened. The Order. Even the name made people flinch now, though most of the valley couldn't have said why — only that the scholars who'd come asking questions after the wards fell spoke of it in whispers, and the whispers had teeth. "You knew that the night it happened. And you let the council send riders to the capital without saying a word."

"The council would have done nothing different."

"The council would have doubled the watches. Would have sealed the passes. Would have—" He stopped himself, breathed through his nose. "You don't know that. You decided for all of us, Emrys. Again. Like you always do. Lone wolf, lone blade, lone grave — that's not a philosophy, it's a wound, and you keep bleeding it on the rest of us."

That one found bone. Emrys turned away, gripped the stall rail, and for a moment the wood under their hands was the ward-stone's cold granite, and the dying man's blood was warm on their fingers again, and the sigil in his fist burned its shape into the air behind their eyes — the same shape, mark for mark, line for line, as the birthmark over their own heart.

"Show me," Halvard said quietly.

"What?"

"Whatever you took from him. Whatever he gave you. Show me, or tell me why you can't. One or the other. Those are the only doors out of this room."

* * *

They ended up walking. It was Halvard who suggested it — "Walls make liars comfortable" — and so they took the east path out of the settlement, past the tanneries and the terraced fields, up toward the ridge where the border wards ran like a spine of gray stones through the hills. Morning fog clung to the valleys below. Above, the sky sat empty and wrong, a ceiling instead of a heaven, and every time Emrys looked up at it their stomach did the same slow lurch it had done the first night, when the stars had gone out one by one like lamps being snuffed by an unseen hand.

They walked in silence for a mile. It was the old silence, the companionable kind, and Emrys hated how much they wanted to hide inside it.

"The man's name was Aldous Vey," Emrys said at last. "He was a scholar. He'd spent thirty years studying the wards, the valley, Gideon — all of it. He wasn't Unbound. He'd left them. They killed him for it, or they chased him until the crossing did."

Halvard said nothing. He was good at that — making silence a question.

"He told me the wards weren't broken. They were opened. From outside, yes — but opened, like a lock, like someone finally found the key. And he told me..." Emrys stopped at the crest of the ridge, where the first ward-stone stood, its carved sigils dark and dead as burnt coals. Three hundred years of silence, and now the silence had a hole in it. "He told me Gideon isn't a god."

The words came out smaller than Emrys intended. Smaller and heavier at once.

Halvard frowned. "Blasphemy from a dying heretic. The valley will be shocked."

"Halvard." Emrys turned to face him fully. "He said Gideon was a person. The first sigil-bearer. That the war that ended the old world — the one our grandmothers' grandmothers fled from — didn't end because a god silenced magic. It ended because a *man* sealed it away, and the valley is the seal. The wards aren't walls around us. They're the lock itself. We've been living inside the mechanism for three centuries and calling it holy ground."

The wind came up the ridge, carrying the smell of wet grass and, faintly, something else — something scorched and sweet that hadn't been here a week ago. Magic's residue, maybe. The world leaking in.

"And you believed him," Halvard said.

"I saw the proof."

"Which is?"

Emrys's hand moved before their courage did — up, to the collar of their shirt, pulling the fabric aside enough to show the birthmark over their heart. The interlocking lines of it, the seven-pointed geometry that every healer in the valley had called unusual and nothing more. Halvard stared at it. Emrys watched his face go through recognition the way a field goes through fire — fast, total, leaving nothing standing.

"That's the ward-mark," he said hoarsely. "That's the sigil on the stones. On the temple doors. On—" He stopped. Started again. "That's Gideon's mark."

"It's mine. It's always been mine. And it was his." Emrys let the shirt fall. "Vey called me a keystone. The last living piece of the seal. He died trying to tell me how to hold it, and he didn't finish, and now the only person who knows is ash in a cairn I built myself at four in the morning while the rest of you slept."

For a long moment Halvard just looked at them. And Emrys braced for the anger, the accusation, the *you should have told the council* — all the things they deserved. Instead Halvard sat down on the flat top of the fallen boundary marker, slow, like a man whose legs had been cut out from under him, and put his face in his hands.

"Gods below," he murmured into his palms. "You've known for three days."

"Yes."

"Three days, and you've been carrying it alone. Sleeping alone. Lying at muster. Letting me—" His voice caught, and he mastered it the way he mastered everything, brutally and a beat too late. "Do you know what I thought? When you started lying? I thought you'd gone over. I thought the Unbound had gotten to you, bought you, marked you somehow. I had Sera shadowing you yesterday. Did you know that?"

Emrys blinked. "No."

"She reported you visited the cairn. Alone. Twice. Talked to it, she said." He lifted his head, and his eyes were red-rimmed and furious and more afraid than Emrys had ever seen them, including the night the stars went out. "I was deciding whether to report you to the council as a traitor, Emrys. I was losing sleep over whether my own — whether *you* —"

"I'm not a traitor."

"No. You're worse. You're loyal to everyone except the people who love you." He stood, and the gentleness in it was worse than shouting. "You think keeping secrets protects us. You think you're a wall between the valley and whatever's coming. But walls don't get to decide who they protect. And you don't get to decide what we can bear. That's not strength. That's just loneliness wearing armor."

The words went in deep, past the armor, into the soft place underneath where Emrys kept the truth they'd never said: that the valley had raised them orphaned and strange, marked and quiet, and that belonging had always felt like a door left slightly ajar — open enough to see the warmth, never quite open enough to enter. That secrecy wasn't strategy. It was habit. It was the only home they'd ever trusted.

"I was going to tell you," Emrys said, and heard how thin it sounded.

"When?"

"...Soon."

Halvard laughed once, without any humor in it at all. "You know what the worst part is? I believe you. I believe you meant to. And I know it would've been too late anyway, because you'd have waited until telling me cost you nothing." He turned and looked out over the valley — the smoke rising from chimneys, the fields, the tiny figures of people going about their morning under a sky with no stars behind its blue. "My loyalty is to these people. All of them. If it comes down to the valley or your secret, Emrys, I need you to understand, right now, on this hill, which way I'll fall."

Emrys held his gaze. "I know."

"Do you? Because three days ago I'd have sworn you'd never lie to me either."

It was the kindest cruelty anyone had ever done them, and Emrys took it standing, the way they took everything. Below them, the second ward-stone caught the light — and Emrys's breath stopped, because the light was moving. Flickering. Faint threads of color crawling across the dead sigils like frost forming in reverse, like veins filling with blood.

"Halvard," they said quietly. "Look at the stone."

He followed their gaze. The flicker strengthened — a pulse, then another, rhythmic, deliberate. Not random. Not decay.

*A signal.*

And down in the valley, near the eastern gate where the refugee wagons had been admitted two days ago, Emrys saw figures pause among the tents. Saw one of them raise a hand, casually, toward the ridge. Saw the faint gleam of sigil-work stitched into a travel cloak catching the sun.

"They're not refugees," Emrys breathed. "Some of them never were. They're answering it."

Halvard's hand went to his sword out of pure habit, useless and human. "Answering what?"

Emrys watched the pulse in the ward-stone quicken, watched the cloaked figure below turn and begin, unhurried, to climb the very path they stood on.

"The seal," Emrys said. "Someone's knocking."
