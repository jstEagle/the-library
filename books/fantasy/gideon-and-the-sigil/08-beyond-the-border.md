## 8. Beyond the Border

Emrys had spent their whole life within sight of the ward-stones, and until three weeks ago they had never once wondered what lay on the other side.

That was a lie, and they knew it. Every child in the valley wondered. They were told the same thing their parents had been told: beyond the line was nothing, or worse than nothing — the old world, drowned and dead, where magic had burned itself out and taken everything lovely with it. The wards didn't keep danger out so much as keep the valley from looking too long at its own grave.

But the stars had gone out, and graves did not go out. Graves stayed dark and quiet. Whatever was out there now was awake.

Emrys left at dusk, alone, because asking for company meant explaining, and explaining meant confessing, and confessing meant becoming either a saint or a sacrifice depending on which way the council's fear leaned that morning. They told Halvard only that they were scouting the fallen stones along the western reach. Halvard had looked at them for a long moment — that flat, patient look of a man who had buried friends and knew what lies smelled like on a person's breath — and said, "Take the gray mare. She's the only one who won't spook."

The gray mare's name was Sorrel, which made no sense, and she carried Emrys up the shepherd's track through the last of the valley's pines as the light died behind them. The air changed before the trees thinned. It was subtle at first, like walking into a room where someone had recently been crying — a pressure, a wrongness, a taste of copper at the back of the throat. Then it wasn't subtle at all.

The pine needles underfoot began to glow.

Faintly, fitfully, like embers breathing. Each needle wore a hairline seam of pale blue light along its spine, and when the wind moved them they shed tiny motes that drifted upward instead of down. Emrys reined in and sat very still, watching fire fall upward, and felt the first true vertigo of their life. Not fear. Fear they knew. This was the ground itself refusing to be trusted.

*Magic*, said the dying scholar's voice in their memory. *It isn't gone, mercenary. It was never gone. It was locked away, and you are the lock.*

"Shut up," Emrys told the dead man quietly, and rode on.

* * *

The ward-line announced itself long before they reached it.

The old stones stood in a broken procession across the shoulder of the mountain pass — twelve of them once, the archivist's maps said, though Emrys could count only nine still upright, and those leaned like drunkards at a funeral. In the valley's stories the ward-stones were sacred things, smooth and white as riverbone. Up close they were scarred granite furred with lichen, and every one of them bore the same mark: a circle crossed by seven lines, worn nearly to nothing by three centuries of weather.

The same mark that sat under Emrys's collarbone, birthed into their skin, and had never once been explained to them.

They dismounted and walked the line on foot, leading Sorrel, because some instinct older than sense said you did not ride through a door; you walked, and you acknowledged it. The pressure in the air thickened with every step until it felt like wading upstream. The glowing pine gave way to bare black rock, then to grass that glowed in patches, whole constellations of it mapped across the hillside in cold blue-green fire. Somewhere below, a stream ran silver-bright, lit from within.

And above it all, where the sky should have held stars, there was nothing. Not cloud-cover — Emrys knew cloud-cover, had slept under it half their life. This was a darkness with texture, a darkness that pressed down like a lid, and if Emrys stared at it too long they began to feel watched by it, the way a mouse feels watched by the shape of an owl against the moon.

They stopped at the seventh stone. The gap between it and its neighbor was wide enough to drive a cart through — this was where the wards had failed, where the stranger had crawled through to die with a sigil clutched in his fist. The air in the gap shimmered faintly, like heat off summer stone, except it was autumn and freezing and the shimmer bent the starless dark behind it into slow, oily spirals.

Emrys stood at the threshold of the only world they had ever known and understood, finally, why the valley had needed a god who never answered. A god who never answered asked nothing of you. A god who never answered let you believe the silence was peace.

Beyond this line, the silence had ended.

"Well," they said aloud, because saying it aloud made it real and real things could be fought. "Nothing for it."

They stepped through.

* * *

Crossing felt like nothing and everything. There was no wall, no flash, no thunderclap. One stride they were in the valley's thin cold air; the next, the world simply opened — sound came flooding in, actual sound, wind with a voice in it, and beneath the wind a hum so low Emrys felt it in their teeth, in the roots of their teeth, in the marrow behind them. The land on the far side of the line sloped away into a country Emrys had no map for: rolling moorland stitched with rivers of light, forests standing black and dense in the middle distance, and far off, on the horizon, mountains that were not mountains but the ruins of something enormous, broken towers or broken fingers, silhouetted against a sky that held no stars at all.

Sorrel shuddered the whole length of her body but did not bolt. Good mare. Emrys mounted, because distance suddenly seemed like the only safety worth having, and rode north-northeast, toward the temple marked on the scholar's map — the map they had taken from his body after the council saw him buried, because the council had seen only a madman's scribbles and Emrys had seen a route.

The scholar's notes, copied out in Emrys's own careful hand, said: *Sanctum of Gideon, forty leagues past the line, in the hills above the Ashwater. The Order holds it is defiled. The Order is wrong. Nothing there is defiled. Everything there is waiting.*

Waiting. Emrys had turned that word over for three weeks and liked it less each time.

They rode through the night because stopping felt worse than tiredness. The moorland was beautiful in a way that made their skin crawl — that was the honest truth of it, and they made themselves hold the thought rather than flinch from it. Rivers of light. Trees whose leaves chimed faintly against each other like distant bells. A herd of something grazing on a far slope, shapes too tall and too jointed to be deer, moving with a grace that no animal in the valley had ever managed. Once, something vast passed overhead without wings or sound, a shadow darker than the starless sky, and Sorrel screamed — actually screamed, a sound Emrys had never heard a horse make — and bolted half a mile before Emrys got her back under control, both of them shaking.

The world out here was not dead. That was the second truth, and it was worse than the first. The valley's stories said magic had burned the old world to ash and cinders. But ash did not sing. Cinders did not graze on far hillsides under a blind sky. Magic hadn't destroyed this place. Something had *caged* it here, badly, and for three hundred years it had been chewing at the bars.

At the bars was where Emrys lived.

* * *

Dawn, if it could be called that, came as a graying of the dark rather than any kind of light. Under the lidless sky the sun was a rumor, a brightening behind haze, and shadows fell wrong — soft-edged, directionless, like shadows underwater. Emrys watered Sorrel at a stream that ran clear and cold and utterly unlit, and was grateful for one ordinary thing, and ate hard bread and dried mutton with their back against a boulder, watching the treeline.

That was when they found the village.

Or what had been a village. It sat in a fold of the moor a mile off their route, and Emrys might have ridden past it, but the light coming off it was wrong — not the clean blue of the streams and grass but a sickly amber, flickering, and flickering meant burning, and burning meant people, or had once.

They told themselves they would only look. They were lying when they told themselves that, and they knew it, and went anyway, because whatever else Emrys Delacroix was, they had never learned how to walk past a thing that was bleeding.

The village had not burned. That was the horror of it. Every roof stood. Every door hung closed. The amber light came from the well at the village center, which glowed like a furnace throat, and from the windows, each of which poured the same dull gold onto the ground as though hearth-fires burned within. But when Emrys dismounted and pushed open the first door, there was no fire. There was no furniture either. There was a table, yes, and chairs, and a bed, and a cradle — but all of them grown, not built, wood and grain fused seamless as bone, as though the house had decided one morning what a home should be and grown itself one overnight.

In the cradle lay a bundle of woven light, shaped like a swaddled infant, and it breathed.

Emrys backed out of that house faster than they had ever done anything, and stood in the street with their heart slamming, and forced themselves to think. Not haunted. Not cursed, exactly. *Remembered.* The houses remembered being homes. The cradle remembered a child. And whatever had lived here had either fled across the ward-line into the valley's ancestors' path three centuries ago, or —

Or it was still here, somewhere, folded into the light, and Emrys decided firmly not to finish that thought.

They did check the well, because a mercenary's discipline dies hard. Peering over the lip, down into the amber glow, they saw coins — hundreds of them, old coinage stamped with the seven-lined sigil, a wishing-well paid fat with prayers. And around the well's rim, cut deep and fresh, days old at most, someone had carved words in the old tongue that the scholar's notes had taught Emrys to read:

*THE SLEEPER STIRS. THE KEY WALKS. COME HOME.*

Emrys stood there a long time, reading it, rereading it, while the amber light breathed up out of the earth and gilded their face like a benediction or a warning.

Then they got on the mare and rode, and did not stop again until full dark.

* * *

They smelled the temple before they saw it.

Not incense — the valley's temples had smelled of incense, tallow and myrrh and the honest stink of too many bodies at midwinter service. This was different. This was a smell like rain about to fall, like the air before lightning, sharp and green and electric, and it grew stronger as the road climbed into the hills above a river the map called the Ashwater, though no ash colored it now; it ran bright as molten glass.

The temple crowned the highest hill, and Emrys reined in below it and simply looked for a while.

It was vast. Vaster than anything the valley had ever built, vaster than anything the valley could have imagined building — a crescent of pale stone open to the east, its walls fifty feet high even where they had fallen, its columns thick as valley oaks. Half of it was ruin. The other half was not, and the half that was not had been *kept*: the moss scraped from its steps, its shattered statuary dragged into orderly rows, its great doors — black wood banded in silver, twenty feet tall — standing closed but conspicuously unbroken. Someone tended this place. Someone had tended it for a long time.

And carved across the entire eastern facade, above the doors, larger than a barn door, worn by weather but unmistakable, was the sigil. Seven lines crossing a circle. The mark on the ward-stones. The mark on the scholar's scrap of metal. The mark under Emrys's collarbone, which had begun, in the last mile, to warm — a slow spreading heat like a hand laid flat against their skin from the inside.

Emrys sat the sweating mare in the shadowless gray light and made themselves take inventory, because that was what kept mercenaries alive: count your blades, count your exits, count your fears and name them so they can't name you first. Blades: two, plus a knife in the boot. Exits: the road, the riverbank, the ridgeline. Fears: too many to count, so they named only the largest.

*I am afraid that whatever is in there has been waiting for me specifically, and I am afraid that it will be right.*

The heat under their collarbone pulsed once, gently, almost patiently. Like a knock.

Like an answer.

Emrys swung down from Sorrel, tied her to a leaning column at the base of the hill where the grass still grew merely green, and drew one knife — not both; they weren't fool enough to think steel would matter, and weren't coward enough to climb unarmed — and started up the steps.

Halfway up, they stopped.

Because the doors, which had stood shut when they looked from below, now stood open. Just a crack. Just enough to show a sliver of the interior, and in that sliver, impossibly, warmly, impossibly — light. Candlelight. The soft gold of living flame in a world where the sun itself had become a rumor.

And from somewhere deep inside the temple, unhurried, echoing off stone that had waited three hundred years for footsteps, came the sound of someone setting out a second cup.
