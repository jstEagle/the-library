## 9. The Temple of the Silent God

The temple had no door.

Emrys stood at the threshold where a door should have been, boots on cracked flagstone, and understood that the absence was deliberate. Three centuries of wind and rain had chewed the rest of the ruin — the colonnade was broken teeth, the roof long since fallen into the nave like a ribcage caving in — but the doorway itself remained a clean-edged void, as if the stone had simply declined to grow there. As if something had once passed through it so often that the wall gave up trying to be a wall.

Beyond it, dark.

Not the ordinary dark of an abandoned building. Emrys had spent their whole life in a valley that forgot magic, and they knew silence the way farmers knew weather. But this was different. The silence inside the temple wasn't empty. It was *attending*.

"You don't have to go in," Halvard said behind them.

They'd left the horses two miles back, where the blighted borderlands gave way to older ground — black soil that drank the rain without puddling, grass growing in spirals instead of rows. The company of three had become a company of one at the treeline; Halvard and the scout, Brenna, were to hold position until moonrise, then come looking. That had been the plan. Emrys had agreed to it easily, which should have told them both something about how much plans were worth out here.

"I do," Emrys said. "That's rather the point."

"Emrys." Halvard's voice dropped into the register they used for dying men and frightened children, low and level. "You crossed the ward-line yesterday. You've slept maybe four hours. Whatever's in there—"

"Answers."

"Or a trap."

"Both, probably." Emrys turned. In the grey half-light under the dead stars, Halvard's face was all hard planes and worry worn smooth by years of practice. "Halvard. The scholar died with my birthmark in his fist. There are murals in there — I can feel them from here, the way you feel a fire in the next room. If I walk away now, I'll spend the rest of my life wondering, and wondering is worse than anything waiting inside."

Halvard held their gaze for a long moment. Then, grudgingly: "Brenna keeps watch at the threshold. You shout, we come running."

"It's a temple to a god who never speaks. I doubt shouting will help."

"Then we come running anyway." Halvard's jaw worked. "Three days ago I'd have said nothing in this world could make me let you walk into a ruin alone. Then the stars went out." A breath. "Go. And Emrys — whatever you find in there, whoever you find yourself to be when you come out — you come out. That's not a request."

Emrys almost smiled. "Yes, Captain."

* * *

Inside, the air was cold in the way water is cold — total, enveloping, indifferent to skin. Emrys moved down the nave with a shuttered lantern held high, and the light behaved strangely: it fell on the flagstones ahead of them but seemed reluctant to fall on the walls, pooling and sliding, as though the darkness had texture and the light kept losing its grip on it.

The floor was mosaic. Emrys realized it only after several steps, because the tiles were so old and so vast in design that from ground level they read as mere color — a river of blue-grey running the length of the nave toward the altar, fed by tributaries of white and gold. Figures stood along its banks. Thousands of them, each no bigger than Emrys's thumbnail, rendered in chips of glass and stone so fine the work must have taken lifetimes.

They were all facing the same direction. Toward the altar. Toward whatever stood — or had stood — at the end of the river.

Emrys walked the river.

Their footsteps made no echo. That was the first wrongness they noticed consciously, though their body had been cataloguing wrongnesses since the threshold: the way their heartbeat sounded too loud, the way their own breathing arrived at their ears a half-beat late, as if the temple were considering whether to permit sound at all. In the valley, silence had always been a shared thing — the whole valley held it together, three hundred years of people not speaking of magic, not asking questions, keeping the quiet the way you keep a grave. Companionable silence. Chosen silence.

This was not chosen. This was silence as a held breath. Silence as a hand pressed over a mouth.

At the altar, the river of mosaic ended in a pool of gold, and above the pool, where any other faith would have set a statue, the wall was blank. Perfectly, deliberately blank — a rectangle of bare stone perhaps ten feet tall, framed in carved laurels, polished smooth. An empty frame the size of a god.

Emrys stood before it and felt the silence lean closer.

"Gideon," they said aloud, and their voice came out small and flattened, swallowed instantly.

Nothing answered. Of course nothing answered. Gideon had never answered — not to prayers, not to sacrifices, not to the three centuries of valley children taught to fold their hands at dusk and address the silent one. Emrys had made peace with that long ago, or thought they had. A god who does not answer is easy to carry. You fill the silence with your own meanings and call it faith.

But this was different. Standing here, Emrys understood — with a certainty that bypassed thought entirely — that the silence was not absence.

It was refusal.

Something was present in this room. Something enormous and patient and *withholding*, holding itself back from every sense Emrys owned the way a speaker holds back words, the way a hand hovers over a door it has decided not to knock on. It was not dead. Dead things did not press against the world like this. Dead things did not *notice*.

"You're listening," Emrys whispered. The lantern flame bent toward the blank wall, just slightly, like a plant toward sun. "Three hundred years. My whole valley prayed to you and you never — you never said a word. Were you even there? Or did you leave us the silence and call it protection?"

The pressure in the room deepened. Not anger. Not grief. Something more terrible: *attention*. The sensation of being read, thoroughly, from the outside in — and of the reader finding what it expected.

Emrys's birthmark woke.

It had been quiet since the ward-line — a faint warmth along the left side of their ribs, the shape of a closed eye, the mark every midwife in the valley's history had called a blessing and every soldier had called a target waiting to happen. Now it burned. Not painfully. *Insistently.* Like a word rising to the tip of the tongue.

And beneath their feet, the mosaic began to move.

Not physically. The tiles stayed fixed. But the light from the lantern slid across them and the figures along the riverbank seemed to shift their weight, turn their heads, and Emrys — heart slamming, telling themselves it was exhaustion, it was shadow-play, it was anything — followed the river backward, away from the altar, to where the tributaries began.

That was where the murals started.

* * *

They climbed the crumbling stair to the gallery level with the lantern held out before them like a talisman, and the first mural stopped them so completely that for a moment they forgot to breathe.

It covered the entire north wall, forty feet of it, and it depicted a war.

Two armies met in the center of the composition, and Emrys understood within moments that these were not armies of men — or not only. On the left, figures wreathed in painted sigils, light pouring off them in rivers of gold leaf that still, after three centuries, caught the lantern and threw it back. On the right, figures wreathed in different sigils, darker ones, worked in iron-oxide red, and between the two hosts the ground itself was painted as torn open, the world split like a seam.

Sigil-bearers. The scholar's dying words came back with the taste of blood still attached: *Gideon was no god. He was the first of us.*

Emrys walked the length of the war. They found Gideon in the third panel — unmistakably, though the face had been effaced, chiseled away with reverent violence so that only a smooth hollow remained. But the figure's posture survived: standing alone between the armies, arms raised, and around the raised hands the artist had painted a sigil so large it spanned the panel's full height. A closed eye.

Emrys put their hand against their own ribs.

The mark matched. Stroke for stroke, curve for curve — the sweeping lid, the lash-line that doubled as a blade, the small spiral at the corner that Emrys had spent a childhood tracing in pond-water and thinking nothing of. It matched the way a key matches a lock it was cut for.

"No," they said quietly, to no one, to the painters long dead. "No, that's coincidence. Marks like that run in families. Half the valley has moles shaped like something if you squint—"

But the next panel took the argument away from them.

It showed the sealing. Gideon — faceless, hollowed — kneeling at the center of a ring of ward-stones, and pouring out of the figure's chest a thread of gold that wound outward through the stones, through the landscape, through a valley rendered in loving detail: the river bend, the twin peaks, the terraced fields. Emrys knew every landmark. They had grown up inside this painting without knowing it.

And threaded through the gold, distributed through the valley like seed through furrows, were small human figures. Each one marked on the chest or ribs with a tiny version of the closed eye. Each one connected to the central kneeling figure by a hair-fine line of gold.

A lattice. A web. A seal made not of stone but of *people* — scattered through the valley across generations, each carrying a fragment, each unaware.

Emrys counted the marked figures in the panel. Eleven. Their mouth went dry. The archivist's oldest rolls listed eleven bloodlines granted founding rights in the valley, eleven families whose names the council spoke at warding festivals. Eleven lines, three hundred years, and somewhere in the unrolling generations the fragments had thinned and gathered and concentrated, the way silt gathers at a riverbend —

— until there was one.

The final panel was smaller than the others, tucked into the wall's end as if the painters had hesitated to paint it at all. It showed a single figure standing at the border wards at night, under a sky of extinguished stars. The figure's face was not effaced. It was simply unfinished — sketched in outline, features left blank, awaiting a face.

Around the figure's ribs, the closed eye. And beneath the figure's feet, inscribed in letters older than the valley's founding script, older than anything, a single line of text that Emrys could not read and yet understood perfectly, the way you understand a blow:

*THE LAST WARD WILL CHOOSE.*

Emrys stepped back. Their heel struck the gallery rail and they caught it, and for a moment the whole temple tilted — the vertigo not of heights but of selfhood, the floor of their identity giving way beneath them. Because this was the thing the scholar had tried to tell them and died before he could finish, the thing they had been circling for days without letting it land:

This was not prophecy. Prophecy was prediction. This was *design*. Someone had stood in this gallery three hundred years ago and painted a person who did not yet exist, had given them a birthmark and a role and a sky of dead stars, and then — patiently, generation by generation, bloodline by bloodline — had arranged the world until that person walked up these stairs with a lantern in their hand.

Every choice Emrys had ever made about their own life — taking the escort work, staying single, keeping to the borders, the restless edge that had always made the valley feel slightly too small — how much of it had been theirs? How much had been a lattice pulling taut?

"I'm not a person," they heard themselves say. Their voice didn't shake, which somehow made it worse. "I'm a component. I'm the last piece of someone else's machine."

The silence leaned in. And for the first time, Emrys felt it clearly for what it was: not the silence of a god who could not speak.

The silence of a god deciding what to say.

The lantern flame stretched toward the blank wall below — toward the empty frame — and in the gold pool of the mosaic at the altar, the light caught something it hadn't caught before. Fresh scratches in the ancient tile. New. Days old at most.

Letters, cut by a careful hand, in the valley's own script:

*WE KNOW WHERE YOU ARE NOW.*

Emrys snuffed the lantern and ran.
