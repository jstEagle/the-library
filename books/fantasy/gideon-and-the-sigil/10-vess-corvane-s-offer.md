## 10. Vess Corvane's Offer

The temple road wound up through the hills like a scar that had never quite healed, and Emrys walked it alone because alone was the only way they knew how to do anything that mattered.

They had told Halvard they were scouting. It wasn't even a lie, not entirely — the Unbound agents had slipped into the valley three days ago wearing refugees' rags and pilgrims' dust, and someone had to learn what they wanted before the council's riders came back from the capital with nothing but bad news and worse advice. But the truth underneath the truth was simpler and uglier: Emrys could not sit in the barracks one more night, listening to the valley pray to a god who had never once answered, knowing what they now knew about who that god had been and what Emrys's own blood was doing at the bottom of it.

So they followed the strangers instead. And on the third day of following, the strangers stopped following their own path and turned to face them.

Emrys knew it before they saw it — felt it in the place behind their sternum where the birthmark sat, a warmth like a coal stirred back to life. The ward-sigils stitched into the travelers' gear were not hidden well enough to fool someone who had spent two nights memorizing the dying scholar's sketches. The tall woman with the gray braid wore hers under her collarbone. The boy with the burned hands had his inked along his forearm, half-covered by a sleeve he kept tugging down.

And the woman in front — the one who had stepped out from behind the ruined milestone as if she'd been waiting there all morning, which she probably had — wore hers openly, a silver thread worked into the leather of her bracer, coiled like a sleeping serpent.

"You've been very patient," the woman said. "Three days is a long time to walk behind someone without asking them anything. Most people would have confronted us by now. Or run."

Emrys's hand went to the sword at their hip out of habit more than intent. "Most people would be right to."

"Would they?" The woman smiled. She was perhaps fifty, with laugh lines carved deep around eyes the color of river ice, and she carried no weapon that Emrys could see — which, given everything else, meant nothing at all. "You're Emrys Delacroix. Sworn to Gideon since you were fourteen. Escort work, border patrol, the occasional job for the council when they need something done quietly and don't want to ask too many questions." She tilted her head. "Did I get any of it wrong?"

"Who told you all that?"

"You did. Every time you checked a sightline, every time you chose the high ground, every time you put yourself between us and the farms below." She said it lightly, almost fondly. "I'm Vess Corvane."

The name landed like a stone dropped into still water. Even in the valley, even in a place that had spent three centuries pretending the rest of the world didn't exist, Emrys had heard whispers of the Order of the Unbound — heretics, the priests called them, though the priests called everyone heretics. A name spoken in the same breath as the fallen wards of the outer kingdoms, the burning of the Athenaeum at Vel Sarras, the disappearance of an entire archipelago that maps no longer bothered to draw.

"You're supposed to be a story," Emrys said.

"I'm supposed to be a great many things." Corvane gestured up the slope, where the temple road climbed toward a ridgeline Emrys had never crossed. Beyond it, faintly, the air itself seemed darker — the sky beyond the old ward-line, where the stars had gone out. "Walk with me. Not far. Just past the last farmstead, where we can speak without some goodwife reporting your movements to the council by supper."

"I'm not going anywhere with you."

"No," Corvane agreed pleasantly. "But you're going to stand there and listen, because you've spent your whole life listening to silence, and I am the first person who has ever offered you an answer." She began walking, unhurried, along the verge of the road, and after a moment — hating themselves for it, or telling themselves they hated it — Emrys fell into step several paces behind, hand never far from the sword.

* * *

They walked past the last terraced fields, where winter wheat stood in pale rows and a farmer Emrys had known all their life raised a hand in greeting without breaking his stride. Past the shrine at the crossroads, its stone worn smooth by three hundred years of touching hands, its alcove empty because Gideon had no face and never had. Past the boundary marker where the valley's care ended and the wild country began.

Corvane stopped there, at the line, and looked out at the darkened horizon the way other people looked at sunsets.

"Do you know what it's like," she said, "to live in a world where the stars went out? Where magic bleeds through the cracks in the seal like light under a door, and every kingdom it touches tears itself apart fighting over the scraps?" She didn't wait for an answer. "Of course you don't. You grew up here, in the one quiet room left in the house. Your whole life, the worst thing that ever happened to anyone you loved was weather."

"The valley isn't a room," Emrys said. "It's a prison. You just described it kindly."

Corvane turned, and something in her expression shifted — the charm settling away like a mask loosening, revealing something older and wearier beneath. "There it is," she said softly. "I wondered how long you'd make me dig for it."

"Dig for what?"

"For the truth you already know. That they never wanted you." She said it without cruelty, which somehow made it worse. "Foundling, weren't you? Left at the temple steps in the dead of winter. Raised by the order because nobody else claimed you. Sworn to a god who never spoke to you, in a valley where every family has held the same land for ten generations and every name in the parish rolls goes back to the founding — except yours." She took a step closer. Emrys did not move, though every instinct screamed to. "I've read the histories, Emrys Delacroix. There are no Delacroixes in them. You are a word that appears nowhere in the book, standing in a valley that is nothing but book."

The words found the bruise precisely, the way a blade finds the gap in armor. Emrys thought of the council chamber, of the way Elder Maren's gaze slid off them during votes; thought of the children who'd called them ward-ghost behind the schoolhouse; thought of Halvard, the single exception, and how even Halvard had needed years to stop watching them sideways.

"You know nothing about my life," Emrys said.

"I know how it ends if you stay on this road." Corvane's voice gentled further. "The seal is failing. Not because of us — we're only hastening what was always coming. When it breaks, the valley will drown in everything it locked out, and the people inside will look for someone to blame. And there will be one person whose blood is written into the wards. One person who could have warned them and didn't. One stranger with no family and no history." She let the silence stretch. "You've already started lying to them. I can see it in how carefully you hold your face. How does Halvard look at you these days?"

Emrys's jaw tightened. They said nothing, and the nothing was answer enough, and Corvane nodded slowly as if Emrys had spoken volumes.

* * *

"So here is what I came to say." She unslung a waterskin, drank, offered it; Emrys refused. "The Order doesn't want to destroy the valley. Whatever the priests have told you, whatever the dying man you cradled in the ward-stones managed to gasp out before the end — we are not vandals. We are surgeons. The seal Gideon made was a tourniquet tied around the world's throat three centuries ago, and the world has been suffocating ever since. Magic cannot be imprisoned forever. It can only be dammed, and dams break, and the difference between us and your silent god is that we'd rather break it on our terms than let it burst and bury everyone downstream."

"And your terms involve what, exactly? The stars going out?"

"The stars going out is the seal dying, not us. We didn't darken them. Gideon did, the night he sealed the sky so no one could navigate by the old lights, so no mage could find the ley-lines again." Corvane shook her head. "He didn't end the war, Emrys. He ended the world that fought it. Everything since has been the long aftermath."

"You sound like you admired him."

"I did. I do. That's rather the point." She smiled again, and this time it reached her eyes, and it was terrible. "Gideon was the greatest of us. The first sigil-bearer. He understood better than anyone what magic cost, because he paid more of it than anyone alive. And when he chose to seal it away, he knew a seal that strong needs a keystone made of living will — not stone, not scripture. A person. Someone bound into the working so deeply that the seal becomes part of their blood, passed down, generation to generation, until—" she spread her hands, "—until it comes down to one."

The coal behind Emrys's sternum pulsed. They pressed a fist against it without meaning to.

"You're saying I'm him. His heir. His—"

"I'm saying you're the last page of a book he started writing before your grandmother's grandmother was born. Yes." Corvane watched them steadily. "And I am offering you the only choice anyone has ever offered a keystone: what shape the ending takes."

"Which is?"

"Break it willingly. Walk out of the valley with us, stand at the final ward-stone, and open the seal with your own hand — awake, consenting, unafraid. In exchange, the Order protects you. Not just your body. Your name." She leaned forward. "Because understand what happens otherwise. If the seal shatters around you — if we take it, or if it simply fails, and it will fail — the backlash passes through the keystone first. Through you. What remains afterward won't be enough to bury. But if you choose it, if you open it deliberately, the power flows through you and out, and you live. You keep your name, your mind, your life. You become the person who freed the world instead of the corpse the valley buries under its collapsed wards."

The wind moved through the wheat behind them, and somewhere downslope a dog barked twice and went quiet, and Emrys stood very still and let the offer settle over them like cold water.

It was a lie. It had to be a lie. Everything Corvane said was shaped too perfectly, every fear named too accurately, every promise sized exactly to the hole in Emrys's chest. They knew this. They had survived twenty-six years in a valley full of soft-spoken people by knowing when a kind voice was selling something.

And yet.

*Yet.*

The valley had never wanted them. That was not bitterness speaking; it was arithmetic. Foundlings were tolerated, not treasured. Their contracts came to them because they were good and because they asked no questions, not because anyone trusted them. When the plague year came, the quartermaster had listed them last for medicine, and no one had apologized, and Emrys had understood, in the wordless way they understood most things, that this was simply where they stood in the ledger of other people's hearts. Second to last. Above the livestock, below the dogs.

And here was a woman who had read that ledger over their shoulder and offered, for the first time in Emrys's life, a place at the top of someone's list.

"What happens to the valley," Emrys said quietly, "when the seal opens? Even willingly."

Corvane paused. It was a small pause, a hair's breadth, but Emrys had spent a lifetime reading silences, and this one had weight.

"The valley adapts," she said. "Like everywhere else. Like everyone must, eventually. Change is not cruelty, Emrys. Keeping a world chained so that one village can sleep soundly — *that* is cruelty. We're offering the world back to itself."

"And the people who die adapting?"

"People die," Corvane said, and for a moment the charm dropped entirely, and what looked out of her face was old grief, genuine and fossilized. "People died in the war Gideon ended. Thousands a day. Children. He couldn't bear it, so he stopped the world instead of stopping the killing, and called it mercy. I have read his journals. I know his handwriting better than I know my mother's. He regretted it every day of his long, long life." Her voice steadied. "We are finishing his regret. That's all the Order is. An apology three centuries late."

Emrys looked down the road, back toward the valley. From here they could see the rooftops of the town, the smoke rising straight in the windless afternoon, the shrine at the crossroads small as a knuckle of bone. Somewhere down there Halvard was drilling the garrison and lying to the council about where their best scout had gone. Somewhere down there was a grave plot already picked out for Emrys, in the row reserved for those with no family — they'd seen the sexton's map once, years ago, and never mentioned it to anyone.

"They'll fight you," Emrys said. "When you come. And they'll lose."

"Some of them. Perhaps." Corvane didn't flinch from it. "I won't insult you by promising otherwise. But fewer than will die when the seal bursts on its own, with no one holding the pen. That is the whole of my argument. Not that no one suffers. That less suffering is still worth choosing."

The horrible thing — the thing Emrys would carry into every hour that followed — was that it almost worked. That for one long breath, standing at the edge of the only home they'd ever had, Emrys genuinely weighed it: the road out, the name kept, the belonging bought at the price of a border. To walk away from the valley before it could reject them one final time. To be, for once, the one leaving.

Then they thought of the farmer at the terraces, raising his hand. Of the children who'd called them ward-ghost, grown now, one of them a mother who waved to Emrys across the market every week. Of Halvard, who had chosen them, stubbornly, against the grain of the whole valley's indifference — proof that the ledger was not the whole of the world.

And beneath all of it, warm and insistent as a second heartbeat: the sigil. The mark that had been theirs since before they had a name. Whatever Gideon had done, whatever purpose had been written into their blood without their consent — it was *theirs* now. No one else got to decide what it meant. Not the council. Not the Order.

Not even the silent god.

"No," Emrys said.

Corvane exhaled, slow, and something in her shoulders settled — not disappointment, Emrys realized. Readiness. As if this, too, had been a path she'd walked before, and knew the turns of.

"I thought not," she said. "But I want you to understand something before we part. I did not come here to recruit you. Recruitment was possible; I took the chance; it failed. I came here because I needed you to hear it from me, face to face, before the end." She stepped back, and the gray-braided woman and the burned-handed boy emerged from the rocks behind her, moving to flank her without a word. "The seal comes down within the month, Emrys. With you or without you. And when it does, I need you to remember that I stood on this road and told you the truth, and offered you a way through it, and that you were free."

"Free," Emrys repeated. "That's what you call this."

"I call it honest." Corvane turned to go, then paused, glancing back over her shoulder with the ghost of the smile returning. "One more thing, freely given, because I respect you and you'll need it soon. Ask yourself why the wards fell the night the stars went out — why *then*, after three hundred years. Seals don't fail on their own schedule, keystone. Something called to them. Something on the inside." Her ice-colored eyes held Emrys's. "Your god is waking up. And I promise you, whatever you think Gideon wants — you do not want to be standing between a god and his unfinished work."

She walked away down the wild side of the ridge, into the darkened country, and the two others folded in behind her, and the grass closed over their passing as if they had never been.

Emrys stood a long time at the boundary marker, until the light went gold and then gray, turning Corvane's words over like a blade looking for the flaw in it. *Something on the inside.* The dying scholar's last breath. The murals they hadn't yet seen. The silence behind the silence.

Then, far below, a bell began to ring — not the watch bell's steady measure but the alarm, fast and ragged, the sound the valley made maybe once in a generation.

Smoke was rising from the direction of the ward-stones.

Emrys ran.
