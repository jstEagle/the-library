## 11. The Valley Bleeds

Emrys smelled the valley before they saw it.

They had crossed the border at dusk, moving fast through country that had once been meadow and was now something else — grass gone grey as old ash, hedgerows standing like the skeletons of hedgerows, a stream running clear but tasting of iron and cold. The blight spread outward from the ward-line in slow rings, and Emrys had walked through its outermost edge for two days telling themselves it was nothing, that the land had always been lean here in autumn, that Corvane's words were still ringing in their ears and coloring everything.

But this was not autumn. This was death moving inward like a tide, and by the second morning the smell reached them: rot sweetened by something floral and wrong, the scent of flowers forced to bloom out of season on things already dying. Wild sigils, blooming where the seal was thinning. The Unbound's work, or the world's own fever — either way, it meant the same thing.

The valley was bleeding.

Emrys crested the last ridge above the border farms an hour past dawn and stopped so suddenly that their horse bumped their shoulder.

The first ward-stone was visible from here, the great grey monolith at Millbrook Ford that every child in the valley learned to touch for luck. It had stood for three hundred years, older than any building, older than any family name. Farmers swore oaths on it. Lovers carved initials into its base and the carvings never weathered.

It was black now. Not shadowed — *black*, the way a burned log is black, all its grey gone to cinder, and around its base the ground lay in a perfect circle of dead earth thirty feet across, precise as if drawn with a compass. And rising from the stone, faint even at this distance, threads of light — pale, silverish, shivering — being pulled upward and away toward the north, toward the dark stars, like smoke from a fire that had already consumed everything else.

"No," Emrys said, and heard how small their voice sounded in all that open country.

They rode down into the valley hard enough to tear their cloak on a dead branch and did not care.

* * *

Millbrook was empty.

Not destroyed — empty. Doors stood open. A cradle rocked in a doorway with no wind to rock it. A pot of porridge had boiled dry on a hearth and split, and no one had come to scrape it out. Emrys walked the main street with their sword drawn and their heart hammering against their ribs, calling names — Marta, old Tam, the Aldern boys — and hearing only their own voice come back off the stone walls.

At the well in the square, someone had chalked a message in a shaking hand:

*GONE TO THE STONE AT ASHFORD CROSS. ALL WHO READ THIS, COME ARMED.*

Ashford Cross. The second ward-stone, the one at the valley's heart, three hours' ride south. Emrys did the arithmetic of dates — two days since they'd left Corvane's camp, half a day crossing the blight — and felt the numbers close around their throat like a fist.

While they had been sitting across a fire from Vess Corvane, listening to her talk about belonging, about freedom, about a valley that had never wanted them — while they had been *tempted* — her people had been here.

They remounted and rode south.

* * *

They heard Ashford Cross before they saw it: the sound of many voices, and under the voices a low hum that Emrys felt in their teeth, in the roots of their molars, in the marrow of every bone the way they had felt the silence of Gideon their whole life. But this was the silence *breaking*. This was the sound of a held note finally failing.

The second ward-stone stood on a low rise above the crossroads, and the valley had gathered beneath it. Hundreds of them — farmers with scythes and billhooks, smiths with hammers, a handful of the border watch in mismatched mail, children too young to fight carrying water and bandages up the slope. They had built barricades of wagons and felled trees across the road approaches. They had done everything people without magic could do against what was coming.

And coming it was. On the far side of the crossroads, maybe four hundred yards off, the Unbound had formed ranks. Emrys counted banners — six, seven — and between the banners moved figures whose outlines shimmered wrong, wrapped in workings Emrys could see now that the wards were dying, could see the way a man born blind sees light for the first time and wishes he hadn't.

Above it all, the ward-stone hummed its failing note, and threads of silver light streamed from it northward, thicker than at Millbrook, and where the light passed overhead the sky seemed to bruise.

Emrys was still fifty yards from the barricades when a sentry spotted them and shouted, and then a voice Emrys knew better than their own cut across the slope:

"Emrys!" Halvard came down through the crowd at a run, sword belted, arm bound at the bicep with a field dressing gone brown at the edges. "Where in the silent god's name have you *been*—"

"Halvard." Emrys caught his shoulders. He looked ten years older than he had six days ago. "What happened? When?"

"Night before last." His eyes were red-rimmed and ferocious. "They came out of the dark like they'd stepped through a wall. Hit Millbrook first, then here. Not to burn — to *drain*. They put things on the stones, Emrys. Sigil-anchors, black iron, they drink the light right out of them. We drove them off twice." He swallowed. "We won't drive them off a third time."

"Casualties?"

"Eleven dead. Twenty wounded, some bad." Halvard's jaw worked. "Denna Sorel is dead. You remember Denna."

Emrys remembered. Denna Sorel, who had taught them to fish the Millbrook shallows when they were nine, who had laughed at their terrible casting for a whole summer. Eleven dead. The number went into Emrys like a blade finding the gap in armor, because Emrys knew — had known for days — and had told no one, and had spent one long evening listening to the enemy explain why the valley deserved it.

"I have to tell you something," Emrys started. "Halvard, I—"

A horn blew from the Unbound lines. Long, low, almost gentle. The enemy ranks began to move.

"Later," Halvard said, and shoved them toward the barricade. "Whatever it is — later. Right now I need your sword."

* * *

The third assault came at midafternoon, when the light slanted long and gold across the crossroads and made the Unbound's workings glitter like oil on water.

It was not a battle so much as a grinding. The valley folk held the barricades because there was nowhere behind them to fall back to — only the stone, only each other. Emrys fought on the left flank beside Tam Aldern's sons, cutting down a robed Unbound caster whose shield-sigil flared white where Emrys's blade touched it, and learned in three exchanges of steel that the workings could be broken — that a sigil was a sentence, and sentences could be interrupted. They killed a second caster by driving a dagger through the anchor-tattoo on his wrist before he finished speaking his working, and the knowledge settled into them cold and useful: *this is what I am for. This is why my blood matches the stones.*

By the time the Unbound withdrew at dusk, the barricades held, barely, and the cost was written in bodies laid in rows by the roadside. Fourteen more. Emrys walked the rows because it seemed like the least they owed, and stopped at the fourth body, because it was Halvard.

He was alive. Barely. A caster's working had caught him across the chest and side — not a cut, something worse, a wound that smoked faintly and would not close, the flesh around it greyed like the blighted fields. Two women were binding it with poultices and prayer in equal measure, and neither was working.

"He took the blow meant for the boy," one of them said. Her hands were shaking. "Pushed Tam's youngest out of the way. It went through him instead."

Halvard's eyes found Emrys. They were clouded with pain but they focused, and his mouth shaped words with no breath behind them, and Emrys leaned close enough to hear.

"The stone," he whispered. "It's failing faster. Listen."

Emrys listened. Under the moans of the wounded and the crackle of cookfires, the ward-stone's hum had dropped since morning — lower, slower, a bell rung underwater. The threads of light streaming from it had thinned to filaments.

"How long?" Emrys asked the healers.

"Ask the archivist," one said bitterly. "She says days. She says she doesn't know how many."

Emrys knelt and took Halvard's hand. It was cold. "You're going to live," they said. "You're going to lie there and be insufferable about it for forty years."

Halvard's cracked lips pulled toward a smile. Then his grip tightened, sudden, desperate, and he dragged Emrys down until their foreheads nearly touched.

"You left," he breathed. "Right after the stranger died. You knew something and you *left*, and I covered for you, and now Denna's dead and Sorel's boys are dead and I don't—" A cough racked him; blood showed at the corner of his mouth. "I don't care what it is anymore, Emrys. Whatever you're carrying. Tell me or don't. But if keeping it is what brought them here—"

"It is," Emrys said.

The word came out of them before they could weigh it, and once it was out they could not take it back, and did not want to. Halvard stared up at them. Around them the camp noise went on — someone weeping, someone singing the low mourning-hymn over the dead, the stone humming its dying note — and none of it mattered.

"They came for me," Emrys said. "Or for what I am. The stranger at the ward-line — he wasn't lost, Halvard, he was running, and what he told me before he died is the reason the Order exists and the reason they're draining our stones. It's about Gideon. It's about the seal. And it's about me."

Halvard's eyes closed. For a horrible moment Emrys thought he had slipped away mid-truth, and then his hand squeezed again, weaker.

"Then tomorrow," he rasped, "you tell the council. All of it. In front of everyone. Because I'm not able to stand between you and them anymore, and secrets are what got us these rows of dead." His breath hitched. "Promise me."

"I promise," Emrys said, and meant it, and hated how much it cost, and understood that whatever it cost was less than it should have.

Halvard slept. Emrys sat beside him a long time, watching the silver filaments stream from the stone into the starless dark, and let the guilt settle into their bones where it would live from now on, permanent as the birthmark on their wrist. Corvane had offered them a place among people who would never ask them to be less than what they were. The valley had just paid fourteen lives for the secret Emrys kept from it.

Tomorrow they would tell the truth. And Emrys already knew, with the certainty of the birthmark itself, that the truth would frighten these people more than the enemy at the gates — because the truth was that the keystone of everything they had ever believed was sitting among them, wearing a mercenary's sword, and had known for weeks.

Beyond the barricades, in the deepening dark, the drained ward-stone gave a sound no one had ever heard it make: a single, cracking note, like ice giving way underfoot.

Every head in the camp turned. The archivist came running down from the rise with a lantern and an old book clutched to her chest, and her face, when she reached the fires, was the face of someone reading their own death warrant.

"There are three keystones," she said, loud enough for the whole camp to hear. "I've found it in the oldest records. Three keystones hold the seal — and the third one isn't a stone at all."

Her lantern-light swept the crowd and stopped, inevitably, on Emrys.

"It's a person. And I think it's standing right there."
