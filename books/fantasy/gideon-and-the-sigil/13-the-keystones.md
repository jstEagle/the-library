## 13. The Keystones

The council chamber smelled of tallow smoke and old arguments. Emrys stood at the edge of it, where the lamplight thinned, and watched forty faces decide what to do with a truth they still didn't fully possess.

They had confessed at dawn — everything. The dying scholar among the ward-stones, the sigil matching their birthmark, Gideon's true nature, the blood in Emrys's veins that was not merely blood but architecture. They had said it all in a flat voice, the way you'd read a supply ledger, because any other voice would have broken.

Now the valley's elders argued about riders and roads and whether the capital would send soldiers or priests, and Emrys understood, with a tired clarity, that none of it mattered. You could not ride to the capital for help against something that had already been inside your walls for weeks.

Halvard sat near the front, arm bound and slung, face gray from blood loss but spine straight as a spear shaft. When the talk circled back to *how many refugees to turn away*, Halvard's voice cut through like an axe through kindling.

"You're counting wagons while the house burns. Ask the one person in this room who actually knows what we're fighting."

Forty heads turned. Emrys had spent three days being looked at like a stranger; they were getting used to it, which was its own kind of grief.

"The Order of the Unbound doesn't want the valley," Emrys said. "It wants the seal. And the seal isn't the wards. The wards are just — the fence around the thing itself." They hesitated. This was the part they'd held back even in confession, because saying it aloud made it real. "I don't know what the seal *is*. Not yet. But I know someone who might."

Every eye went to the corner of the chamber where Maerlin Ostreth sat behind a table stacked with books no one else in the valley could read.

* * *

Maerlin had been the archivist for longer than Emrys had been alive, which everyone knew, and older than she appeared, which fewer people suspected. She was a small woman made mostly of wool shawls and disapproval, with ink stains on her fingers so old they seemed grown into the skin rather than spilled upon it. Her archive occupied the cellar beneath the meetinghouse: three rooms of shelves carved into the valley stone, holding the accumulated paper of three hundred years of a people sworn to silence.

She let Emrys and Halvard down the stairs alone, then barred the door behind them.

"Three hundred years," she said, without preamble, "my predecessors kept records. Births, harvests, ward inspections, the usual tedium of a town that believes nothing will ever happen to it. But the first archivist — the woman who came into this valley with the first settlers — she kept something else." Maerlin lifted a lantern and led them past the orderly shelves into the deepest room, where the air was cold enough to see breath. "A private record. Sealed. My grandmother opened it once, when she was archivist, read half of it, and resealed it. She told my mother it was heresy. She told me, on her deathbed, that it was the only true book in the valley."

The chest she opened was iron-banded oak, and inside lay a single volume wrapped in oilcloth, its leather gone dark as dried blood.

Maerlin unwrapped it on the reading table with the care of someone handling a wound. The pages were dense with two hands of writing — a flowing early script and a cramped later one, as if the book had begun as one person's testimony and become another's commentary.

"Read the beginning," Maerlin said, and turned the book toward Emrys.

Emrys read aloud, slowly, because the old script fought them:

*"I was there when he did it. I want that written plainly, at the start, so that whoever finds this understands these are not the dreams of a madwoman or the inventions of a loyalist. I stood on the field at Sorrel's End, and I watched Gideon — not a god, a man, tall and burned half to bone — drive the first sigil into the earth with his own hand, and I watched the magic die in the world like a fire starved of air.*

*He told us it was mercy. Perhaps it was. The war had eaten thirty years and every army twice over. But mercy is not the same as truth, and the truth is this: a seal is not a wall. A wall stands on its own. A seal must be* held."

The lamp flame bent in a draft that shouldn't have reached this deep underground. Halvard shifted beside Emrys, their good hand finding Emrys's shoulder — not comfort exactly, more like anchoring.

"Turn further," Maerlin said. "The second hand. That's where it becomes relevant to your particular misfortune."

Emrys turned pages. The later script was tighter, angrier:

*"The seal has three keystones. This the founder wrote plainly, though she buried it in metaphor, as if ashamed of the engineering of the thing. Three anchors, set at three points, and between them the valley itself — the land warded, the magic pressed out, the silence maintained. Destroy a keystone and the seal does not break all at once. It sags. It leaks. It dies by degrees, the way a man dies of wounds rather than a blow.*

*The first keystone is the Heart Stone beneath the valley's center — the oldest ward, the one the founders called the Anchor of Names.*

*The second is the Watch Stone in the eastern hills, where the border runs highest.*

*The third—"*

Emrys stopped.

The lamp hissed. Somewhere above them, muffled through three floors of stone, the council was still arguing about wagons.

"Keep reading," Halvard said quietly.

*"The third keystone is not a stone. The founder was explicit about this, and explicit about why she hid it: a stone can be guarded, but a stone cannot be moved to safety, cannot be warned, cannot run. A living keystone can do all those things, which is precisely why the founder despaired of it. The third anchor of the seal is a person. Born within the valley, marked at birth with the founder's own sigil, carrying the seal in their blood as surely as the stones carry it in their substance. While they live inside the borders, the seal holds. If they die outside it — or are unmade, or give themselves willingly to the Unbound — the last anchor fails, and everything Gideon built comes down in a single night.*

*I write this so that some future archivist will understand the duty before them: find the marked child. Guard them. Tell them nothing until they are strong enough to bear it, and then tell them everything, because a keystone that does not know what it is will walk blindly toward the cliff edge.*

*I failed in my generation. The marked child died of fever at age six, and for eleven years the wards sagged, and we told ourselves the storms at the border were weather. May the next archivist fail better than I did."*

Emrys closed the book. Their hands were steady, which surprised them. Everything else felt unsteady — the floor, the lamplight, the whole architecture of their life — but the hands were calm, the way hands go calm in the worst moments, saving their trembling for later.

"The marked child," Emrys said. "That's me."

"It's you," Maerlin said. "Born in the valley, marked with the sigil. There hasn't been another in three hundred years. The founder's line ran thin and then ran out, and the seal held anyway, weakened, waiting. And then you were born, and my mother — archivist before me — entered your name in the registry with a small symbol beside it that I never understood until this week." She tapped the margin of the old book, where the same symbol appeared beside a list of names. "We were supposed to tell you years ago. I convinced myself there was time. That telling you would only paint a target on you. That ignorance was a kind of armor." Her voice frayed. "You see how well that reasoning served us."

"How long," Emrys said. "How long until the seal fails completely. If the other keystones fall."

Maerlin didn't answer immediately. She took the lamp and led them deeper into the cold room, to a section of wall where the valley stone showed bare, unshelved — and carved into it, worn nearly smooth by centuries of touching, was a map of the valley and its borders, with three points marked by sigils Emrys now recognized from the dying scholar's effects, from Corvane's gear, from their own skin.

"The Heart Stone here, at the valley's center — under the old shrine, if the texts are right. The Watch Stone here, east, in the high pass." She touched each mark. "And you feel the third one, don't you? You've felt it your whole life. Every time you crossed the border and felt the silence press close. Every time the wards hummed under your palm and no one else noticed. You're not standing *near* the seal, Emrys Delacroix. You're standing *in* it."

"And the others?"

"The Order struck the borderlands four nights ago. Drained the outer ward-stones." Maerlin's jaw tightened. "That wasn't the seal breaking. That was them clearing the approaches — cutting the fence so they can reach the keystones. The first keystone, the Heart Stone, sits under our feet right now, in the middle of a valley full of farmers who think they're safe. The second is a week's hard ride east." She met Emrys's eyes. "If I were Vess Corvane, I would take the far one first. Kill the distant anchor while the defenders are still deciding whether to believe in the war. Then come for the heart when the valley is already bleeding."

"So we have days," Halvard said. "Not weeks."

"We have however long it takes Corvane to march," Maerlin said. "Which is a countdown, not a promise."

* * *

They climbed back into the lamplight of the upper rooms, and Emrys stood for a moment in the doorway of the council chamber, listening to the argument roll on — grain stores, mounted patrols, whether to appeal to the capital — and felt the strange double vision of a person who has just learned the shape of their own death.

Because that was what it was, wasn't it. Not a secret anymore. A schedule.

All their life, Emrys had thought of themselves as surplus. The mercenary who took jobs no one else wanted, who ate alone, whose oath to a silent god was a formality because the god had never once answered. They had assumed the valley tolerated them the way it tolerated bad weather — with endurance, without affection. And now it turned out the valley had been *standing on them* the whole time. Every harvest, every wedding, every quiet night by the fire — all of it resting on the fact that Emrys Delacroix drew breath inside the border.

It should have felt like belonging. It felt like being a load-bearing wall in a house that never asked your name.

"Emrys." Halvard's voice, low, at their elbow. "Say it. Whatever you just figured out in there. Say it out loud, because you've got the face you get before you do something stupid alone."

Emrys almost laughed. Almost. "The second keystone is a week's ride east. Corvane will hit it first. And if it falls, the seal starts dying in earnest — and the third keystone is me, Halvard. I'm not the person who knows about the seal. I'm the last piece of it. When they come for the heart of the valley, they won't be coming for the shrine stone." They looked at their own forearm, where the birthmark sat pale under the lamplight, the sigil that matched a dead scholar's amulet and a silent god's murals and, now, a three-hundred-year-old archivist's warning. "They'll be coming for me."

For a moment Halvard said nothing. Then, in the tone of a soldier accepting a report that changes the entire campaign: "Then we don't wait for them to march east. We get there first."

"Halvard—"

"No. Listen to me." Halvard stepped in front of them, blocking the view of the arguing council, forcing Emrys to look at nothing but that scarred, stubborn face. "Three days ago I caught you lying to me and I wanted to put you in irons. Yesterday I learned you've been carrying the weight of the whole world since the night the stars went out, alone, because you thought telling us would make things worse. Maybe you were wrong. Probably you were wrong. But you're not doing the next part alone either. That's not forgiveness. Forgiveness comes later, after you stop almost-dying on me. Right now it's just arithmetic: the valley needs its keystone alive, and its keystone needs an escort, and I'm the best damned escort this valley has ever produced."

Behind them, the council droned on. Beyond the walls, the valley lay under a sky still missing its stars, the dark pressing closer every night. And somewhere east, past the dead ward-line, an army of the Unbound was already moving toward a stone that held up the sky.

Emrys looked at the map in their mind — heart, watch, self — and felt the countdown begin ticking somewhere behind their ribs, steady as a second pulse.

"Get me six riders," they said. "Fast ones. We leave before dawn."

* * *

They gathered in the yard behind the meetinghouse while the valley slept its last easy night: Halvard, arm bound but riding anyway; Sera Voss, the youngest scout, who had lost family at the borderlands and volunteered before Emrys finished asking; Dain and Orla, brothers who ran the ferry and knew the eastern fords better than any map; Tam Broadfoot, who complained about everything and had never once failed to show up; and young Pell, sixteen, who lied about his age and whom nobody believed and everybody let come.

Maerlin came out into the cold to see them off, wrapped in her shawls, the sealed book heavy under her arm. She pressed a folded page into Emrys's hand — a copy of the keystone passage, in case.

"A keystone that knows what it is," she said, "is harder to kill than one that doesn't. Remember that. Fear is honest, but it's a bad strategist."

Emrys swung into the saddle. Above the eastern hills, where the Watch Stone waited in its high pass, the darkness had a texture to it now — a faint wrongness, like a bruise showing through skin. The magic leaking through the failing wards, maybe. Or the shadow of what was coming.

Either way, they would outride it or die trying.

"Ride!" Halvard called, and six horses broke into the dark, eastward, toward the second stone — carrying, whether they fully knew it or not, the last living piece of the seal, and whatever remained of the world's quiet.
