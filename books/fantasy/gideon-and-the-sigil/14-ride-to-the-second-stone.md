## 14. Ride to the Second Stone

They left before dawn, six riders and a cart of provisions, through the western gate where the ward-stones still stood but no longer sang. Emrys had grown up believing the stones hummed at night — a lullaby every child in the valley knew without knowing it, the way you knew the sound of your own house settling. Now they stood mute as grave-markers, and the silence they made was not Gideon's silence at all. It was just absence.

Halvard rode point. She had not spoken to Emrys since the confession — three days of drills, supply counts, and pointedly assigned duties, all conducted with the flat courtesy of a soldier addressing a stranger. But when the company formed up in the grey dark, she had looked down from her saddle and said only: "You ride second. Behind me."

It was not forgiveness. It was something more deliberate than that, and Emrys understood it for what it was: Halvard was choosing to keep them close enough to watch.

The borderlands beyond the gate had been green once. Emrys knew this the way you know things from stories — the old songs spoke of meadows past the wards, of pilgrims' roads lined with rowan trees, of a river called the Senn that ran silver and cold. What lay ahead now was a country drained of color, grass gone the grey of old ash, trees standing leafless though it was high summer. The blight spread outward from the fallen first keystone like a stain through wet cloth, and everywhere it touched, the world forgot what it was supposed to be.

A bird flew overhead — or something shaped like one, its wings beating slightly out of rhythm with itself, leaving faint afterimages in the air. No one commented. They had all stopped commenting on such things by the second day.

"Company holds formation," Halvard said, loud enough for all six. "We make the ford at Senn's Crossing by nightfall. The second keystone sits in the shrine above it — the Old Chapel, if the archivist's maps are true. We defend it until we can't, then we fall back to the valley and warn them." A pause. "Nobody dies for a rock. Is that understood?"

"Understood," said Bren, the youngest, too quickly, the way young soldiers say things they don't believe.

Emrys rode behind Halvard and watched the back of her head, the iron-grey braid, the set of her shoulders, and thought about all the years those shoulders had been between Emrys and whatever came over the wall. Twenty-two years of service together. Eleven of them riding patrol side by side. And in all that time Emrys had never once told her the truth about anything that mattered.

*You could tell her now,* said some small voice. *Out here, with the world coming apart, what's one more secret?*

But the truth was a stone in Emrys's chest, and stones do not move because you want them to.

* * *

They found the first bodies at midday.

Unbound scouts, four of them, dead among the grey grass in a way that suggested their own sigil-work had turned on them — skin marked with blackened lines that crawled up their throats like ivy, eyes open on a sky that gave nothing back. One of them still clutched a length of chalk-white cord knotted in patterns Emrys recognized from the dying scholar's effects: binding-craft, the Order's signature.

"They were hunting us," Bren said, dismounting to check the corpses. "Or scouting the road."

"Hunting," Emrys said quietly. They crouched beside the nearest body and turned it half over. Beneath the dead man's collar, inked into the skin: a sigil Emrys knew intimately, because an imperfect copy of it sat under their own left shoulder blade, written there in birthmark rather than ink. "This one was tracking me specifically. See the mark? It's keyed. A compass-ward. It points."

"To you," Halvard said. Not a question.

"To me."

She looked at Emrys for a long moment, and the wind moved the dead grass around them both, and somewhere far off the misshapen bird cried out in a voice like a hinge. Then Halvard turned away and remounted.

"Then we change the plan," she said. "They expect you on this road. So we don't take the road. Senn's Crossing has a goat-track along the north ridge — steep, ugly, slow. We take it anyway. Bren, you carry Emrys's cloak and ride point wearing it. If anyone's watching, let them watch the wrong rider."

Bren swallowed. "And if they shoot?"

"Then duck," Halvard said, "and be flattered."

It was such an ordinary joke, so entirely Halvard, that Emrys felt something crack open behind their ribs — grief and gratitude in the same breath. She was protecting them. Not because she'd forgiven them, but because the mission needed them alive, and maybe — maybe — because underneath the anger there was still eleven years of shared fires and shared watches, and Halvard did not abandon people she had bled beside. Even people who lied to her.

Even people who *were* lies, in a sense. Who had been authored by a silent god and never known it.

They cut north.

* * *

The ridge track was everything promised: narrow as a knife's edge in places, the blighted hillside falling away into valleys where the corruption pooled visibly, a grey fog that moved against the wind. Twice they passed shrines — little roadside cairns the valley folk had raised in the generations after the wards went up, offerings to a god who never answered. On each one, someone had recently scratched a new mark: the Unbound's spiral-and-broken-line, their sigil of severance. Vandalism, or a claim staked. Either way it made the hair on Emrys's arms rise.

Near dusk, the attack came.

Not from the front — from below, from the fog-pooled hollow, three figures rising out of the grey on foot with the unnatural speed of sigil-fed muscle. Unbound hunters, hooded, their gear stitched with glowing thread. The lead one threw something that unspooled in the air — a net woven of light and chalk-lines, expanding toward the column like a thrown shawl.

"Down!" Halvard roared, and the company scattered off the track as the net caught empty air and one shrieking horse, dragging it screaming over the edge into the fog. The sound it made going down was something Emrys would hear for the rest of their life, however long that turned out to be.

Then it was chaos and close work. Emrys rolled, came up with sword drawn, and met the second hunter blade to blade — and felt the wrongness immediately, the way the hunter's strikes came fractionally faster than a human arm should move, sigil-work burning along their forearms like veins of ember. Fighting the Unbound was fighting a clock; every exchange cost them power they couldn't replenish, and they spent it gladly because they believed they were spending it on freedom.

Emrys did not believe in freedom that cost other people's valleys. They feinted low, took a gash across the forearm doing it, and drove their sword up under the hunter's guard, through the sigil-lit throat. The light in the man's arms guttered out like a lamp pinched between fingers. He fell looking almost relieved.

Across the track, Halvard had closed with the leader — a woman, tall, moving like water — and was losing. Emrys saw it in two heartbeats: Halvard's parries a half-step late, her boot sliding on scree, the hunter's curved knife drawing a line of red across her jaw. Emrys was already running when the third hunter stepped out of the fog directly into their path, hands raised, chalk-lines crawling up their wrists.

Emrys didn't break stride. They reached instead for the thing inside themselves they had been afraid of since the night the stars went out — the pressure behind their sternum, the hum that lived in their blood, the piece of the seal that was *them*. They didn't know how to use it. They only knew how to *let go of the dam*, a little, the way you loosen a fist.

The world went white and silent — Gideon's silence, but theirs — and the hunter's chalk-lines simply stopped, frozen mid-crawl, and the hunter dropped like a puppet with cut strings.

Emrys staggered, nose bleeding freely, ears ringing. Across the track, the leader-hunter froze too — feeling the same pressure, recognizing it — and then did the sensible thing: broke off, threw a smoke-pellet of grey fire, and vanished downslope with the speed of the sigil-fed.

Silence again. Real silence. Bren was coughing in the smoke. Two horses gone, one over the edge. Renna had a broken wrist from her fall. And Halvard stood with blood on her face, staring at Emrys across twenty feet of ruined track.

"What," she said slowly, "was that."

"That," Emrys said, wiping their nose, "is what I've been trying to tell you since the council hall. I'm not carrying a secret, Halvard. I *am* the secret. I'm the last keystone. Whatever I just did — that's the seal, leaking through me. That's why the Unbound are hunting me. That's why—" Their voice cracked, and they hated it. "That's why I never belonged anywhere. I wasn't a person who failed to fit. I was a lock pretending to be a woman."

The smoke thinned. Somewhere below, the fog churned. Halvard crossed the distance between them, took Emrys's chin in a grip hard as a smith's tongs, and turned their face side to side, inspecting — the blood, the exhaustion, the terror underneath.

"You idiot," she said at last, and her voice was rough. "Eleven years. You could have been bleeding magic all over my patrol routes for eleven years and you said *nothing*."

"I was afraid—"

"You were afraid of the wrong thing." She released them, and something in her stance shifted — not softening exactly, but settling, the way a wall settles after an earthquake, finding which cracks it can live with. "Here's what happens now. You don't hide anything from me on this ride. Not a vision, not a hunch, not a bad dream. If your blood starts humming, you tell me. If you start hearing Gideon, you tell me. Because I can't protect a keystone I don't understand, and I have decided — against all evidence and better judgment — that protecting you is still my job." She mounted, wincing at her jaw. "I'm not saying I forgive you. I'm saying we'll settle accounts after the world stops ending. Understood?"

Emrys found they could not speak for a moment. They nodded.

"Good." Halvard wheeled her horse north. "Ride second. Behind me."

The same words as before. But they meant something different now, and they both knew it.

* * *

They reached Senn's Crossing an hour past full dark, and the river was still there — the one thing in the blighted lands that remembered itself. It ran black and cold and honest, and the sound of it was the first clean sound Emrys had heard in days. Above the ford, on the far bank, the Old Chapel stood against the sky: a squat stone building older than the valley's memory, its single tower crowned with a pale shape that even in darkness seemed to hold light the way the moon used to.

The second keystone. Still whole. Still singing — very faintly, a note Emrys felt in their teeth rather than heard.

They made camp in the chapel's shadow, cold-fire only, no blaze to draw eyes. Halvard set watches. Emrys took first, walking the perimeter while the others slept, and stopped at the chapel door, where the archivist's map said the keystone rested beneath the altar stone.

Through the crack of the door, in the starless dark, Emrys could see it: a pillar of white stone no taller than a child, carved edge to edge with script that predated every language Emrys knew — except that they *did* know it, suddenly, the way you know a face in a crowd. The same script crawled across their own skin in dreams. The same script had been inked into the dying scholar's palms.

And beneath the altar, fresh-chalked at the base of the keystone, someone had already begun the Unbound's severance-spiral. Half-finished. Abandoned — or paused.

Emrys stood in the doorway with their hand on the cold wood and understood, with perfect clarity, that the hunters on the ridge hadn't been scouting the road at all.

They'd been buying time. The real work had already started here — and whoever had chalked that spiral was coming back to finish it.

Behind them, in the camp, Halvard stirred in her bedroll, and Emrys opened their mouth to call out — to tell her everything, starting now, the way they'd promised —

— and from inside the chapel, soft as a breath, something answered the keystone's faint song in a voice Emrys recognized.

Their own.
