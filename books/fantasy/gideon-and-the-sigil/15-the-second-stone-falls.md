## 15. The Second Stone Falls

They smelled it before they saw it.

For three days they had ridden through borderlands that were dying in ways Emrys had no words for — grass silvered and stiff as wire, streams running clear but tasting of iron and something sweeter beneath, like rotting fruit. The blight moved in patches now, not creeping from the ward-line inward as it had after the first stone fell, but blooming outward in rings, as if the land itself had been struck by stones dropped into still water.

Now there was a new smell on the wind. Ozone and honey. The smell of a struck bell that never stopped ringing.

"Magic," Halvard said, reining in at the crest of the ridge. She said the word carefully, the way you'd handle a snake you'd found in your boot. "That's what magic smells like?"

"That's what *broken* magic smells like," Emrys said.

Below them, in a shallow bowl of hills where two rivers met, stood the second keystone.

It was nothing like the ward-stones along the valley border, which were waist-high and humble as fence posts. This one had been raised when the world was younger and crueler — a monolith of black granite taller than the temple spire in the capital, carved end to end with sigils so old that half of them predated the script the archivist had shown them. It should have been visible for miles. Instead it was barely visible at all, because the air around it *moved*, thick and shimmering, like heat off summer stone except that the morning was cold enough to frost their breath.

And it was cracked. Even from the ridge, even through the distortion, Emrys could see the wound down its face — a seam of white light splitting the monolith from crown to base, wide as a wagon, bleeding radiance into the sky.

The sky above it was wrong. Not dark the way the stars had gone out over the valley — dark the way a bruise is dark. Colors bled upward from the crack in bands of green and violet, and where they touched the clouds, the clouds *remembered being other shapes*: faces, hands, a horse mid-gallop frozen for an hour and then gone.

"We're too late," said Tam Vessik quietly. He was the youngest of them, a ward-rider who'd volunteered before anyone asked, because someone always did. "Look at it. Whatever they did, they already did it."

"No." Halvard's voice was flat and hard, the voice she used on green soldiers and on herself. "The archivist said the keystones don't break all at once. The first one took the Unbound a full night to shatter, and Corvane wasn't there for the finish — our scouts confirmed she'd pulled back. They're doing this *carefully*. Ritual work takes time, and ritual work can be interrupted."

Emrys looked at the crack, at the light pouring out of it, and felt the thing in their blood — the thing they were beginning to think of as the seal, the thing that had been quiet their whole life and was now a constant low pressure behind their sternum — *pull* toward the monolith like a hooked fish feeling the line go taut.

"It's still holding," Emrys said. "Barely. I can feel it fighting."

Halvard glanced at her. In the weeks since the confession at the ward-stone, since the council's fear and the long silence afterward, Halvard had chosen trust again without ever once saying so out loud. She showed it in other ways: riding at Emrys's flank, watching her back in the skirmishes with Unbound hunters, asking her opinion first in every council of war. But her eyes still went careful sometimes, and this was one of those times. "Can you feel how much longer it holds?"

"No," Emrys admitted. "But I can feel that it's *worse* today than yesterday. And I can feel—" She hesitated. Saying it aloud made it real, and real things had a way of becoming true. "I can feel it calling me. The keystone. Like it knows what I am."

Tam shifted in his saddle. Old Maren, their tracker, spat over the side of her horse. Nobody else said anything, which was its own kind of answer. They had all heard the story by now — the whole valley had heard it, told in the council hall with the doors open because Emrys had insisted. *The last keystone is a person.* They rode with her anyway. Emrys still hadn't decided whether that made them brave or foolish or simply people who understood that if the last keystone broke, there would be nothing left to be afraid *for*.

"Then we ride now and ask questions later," Halvard said. "Maren, find us a path down the north slope — we come at them from the river side, out of the sun. Tam, you're on me. Emrys—"

"I know," Emrys said. "I'm the weapon."

"You're the *last resort*," Halvard corrected. "There's a difference, and I'd like very much to keep it."

They came down through the birch trees at a canter, hooves silent in needle-fall, and the smell of ozone and honey grew until it coated the back of the throat. Emrys counted the enemy camp as they descended: forty, maybe fifty, in white-and-unbleached-linen robes that had been armor-plated since the Order stopped pretending to be refugees. Sigil-work crawled up their vambraces and spear-hafts, glowing faintly blue-white, drinking the light that leaked from the wounded keystone. At the center of the camp, ringed by a dozen chanters kneeling in a crescent, stood the ritual scaffold — a lattice of iron and bone-white wood built against the monolith's cracked face, hung with bells that made no sound and mirrors that reflected nothing in the camp at all.

No Corvane. Emrys searched for the tall silhouette, the silver-streaked hair, the unhurried way she moved through battle like a woman strolling a garden, and found nothing. A lieutenant commanded here — a broad man with a scarred jaw whose sigils burned brighter than anyone else's.

"They've split their force," Emrys breathed. "She's taken most of them somewhere else. Toward the valley?"

"Doesn't matter," Halvard said. "What matters is that there are fifty of them and nine of us, and the thing we need to protect is already bleeding out." She drew her sword, and the blade caught the green-violet light and threw it back wrong, colors sliding across the steel like oil on water. "We don't win this. We survive it, and we buy the keystone another hour, and maybe the hour matters. Everyone understand? We are not heroes today. We are a splinter in the foot."

They hit the camp's western edge out of the low sun, and for ninety seconds it worked exactly as Halvard had drawn it in the dirt.

The pickets died fast and quiet. Maren's bow put down the nearest chanter before the crescent knew anything was happening. Then Tam cut the first ritual-bell free of the scaffold and the sound it made coming loose was like a scream played backward, and every head in the camp turned, and the ninety seconds ended.

After that it was chaos of the kind Emrys knew well — the close, grinding, ugly arithmetic of melee, sword against sigil-lit spear, the particular horror of fighting people whose weapons glowed. The wild light leaking from the keystone made everything worse. Where it pooled, the ground *misbehaved*: grass grew ankle-high in the space of a breath and then crumbled to gray ash; a dead man's shadow got up and walked away from him; Emrys swung at a robed swordsman and for half a heartbeat saw her own face under the hood, older, weeping, and nearly died for the hesitation.

And through all of it, the pull in her chest grew stronger. The keystone wasn't just calling anymore. It was *leaning* on her, the whole weight of a failing seal settling onto the one living thing designed to bear it, and with every minute the crack widened she could feel the pressure climbing — a headache behind the eyes, a ringing in the teeth, the sense of a door straining against a hand too small to hold it.

"Halvard!" she shouted across the melee. "I have to get to the stone!"

"Then get there! Tam, Maren, on me — we hold the crescent!"

Emrys fought her way inward. It cost her — a gash along the forearm she didn't remember taking, a bruise blooming across her ribs where a shield boss caught her — but the Unbound near the scaffold were chanters, not soldiers, and they broke around her like water around a prow. She reached the base of the monolith and put her palm flat against the black granite, and the world *opened*.

Not sight. Not sound. Something older than either. She felt the keystone the way you feel your own heartbeat — from inside. Three keystones, Gideon's great work, the lock that had ended the ancient war by locking magic away from the world entire. One already dust. This one cracking, its sigils guttering like lamps in a draft, and beneath the guttering, vast and slow and terribly tired, the awareness of the seal itself turning toward her touch like a sick man turning toward a nurse.

*I know you,* the stone said, without words. *Little ward. Last ward. Hold.*

"I'm here," Emrys whispered, and meant it, and did not know what holding meant.

She poured everything into the contact — the strange blood-logic the stranger had died trying to explain, the pressure behind her sternum, the nameless thing that was her inheritance. And the crack slowed. Did not close. Slowed, the way a hemorrhage slows under a pressed palm.

Which was when the corruption found Tam Vessik.

* * *

Later, Emrys would try to reconstruct those minutes and fail. Memory itself was unreliable inside the keystone's bleed — she would learn that the hard way, that the wild magic warping the land warped recollection too, so that everyone who survived the second stone would remember the ending differently, and none of them would be lying.

Here is what Maren remembered: Tam cutting down the last chanter, standing victorious over the broken crescent, laughing — actually laughing, young and alive — and then the light from the crack washing over him like a wave, and his laughter changing key.

Here is what Halvard remembered: Tam turning toward the monolith with his sword still drawn, saying, in a voice that was his and yet arranged strangely, syllables balanced like stones in a cairn, "*It wants to be finished.*"

Here is what Emrys remembered, palms against the granite, feeling it through the seal rather than seeing it: the wild sigils in the bleed-light finding the boy the way floodwater finds a gap under a door. He had no wards. None of them did; the valley had spent three centuries teaching its children that wards were superstition. The sigil-light poured into that unguarded space and began, with the blind enthusiasm of all wild magic, to *write*.

On his skin. In his blood. Through his memory.

"TAM!" Halvard's voice, raw, closer than Emrys had ever heard it break. "TAM, LOOK AT ME!"

He turned. His eyes had sigils in them — small, spinning, beautiful, glowing softly in whites of his eyes like stars drowned in milk. He smiled at Halvard, and it was Tam's smile, and it was not, and he raised his sword not at her but at himself, laying the flat of the blade against his own forearm, and where the steel touched, characters bloomed up through the skin like bruises flowering in fast motion.

"It's so *loud*," he said wonderingly. "Captain, it's been so quiet my whole life, and it's so *loud*, why did they keep it from us, why did *Gideon* keep it from us—"

"Tam." Halvard had dropped her sword. Emrys had never seen Halvard drop her sword. She crossed the ground between them in four strides and took the boy by the shoulders, and the sigil-light crawled up her gauntlets where she touched him, writing on her too, and she did not let go. "Listen to my voice. You're a ward-rider of the valley. You swore to the silent god. You're *mine*, do you hear me, you're one of mine—"

"He's not yours," said the scarred-jawed lieutenant, and Emrys realized distantly that the fighting had stopped, that both sides were standing in a ragged ring watching, because some things outranked war. "Nothing is anyone's anymore. That's rather the point."

"*Hold him down!*" Emrys screamed, tearing her hands from the granite, and the crack leapt wider the moment she let go, and she did not care. "Maren, rope, NOW — Halvard, get away from him, the writing will take you too—"

"He's sixteen," Halvard said, not moving. "He's sixteen years old and he is *afraid*, and I am not letting go."

The sigils reached his heart.

Emrys saw it happen — saw the glow in his chest brighten past bearing, saw the characters on his arms begin to move, rearranging themselves with terrible purpose, the wild magic completing a sentence it had started at random. She saw Tam look down at his own glowing body with the expression of a boy reading his own death warrant written in a language almost like his own.

"Captain," he said, quite calmly. "It doesn't hurt. You should tell them it doesn't hurt."

Then the sentence finished, and Tam Vessik came apart into light.

Not fire. Not blood. Light — a soft, green-violet bloom, silent, total, and where he had stood there was nothing but a scorch-shadow on the grass in the shape of a boy, arms slightly out, and a sword falling end over end through the place where he had been, ringing on the stones like a struck bell that finally, finally stopped.

The silence afterward was absolute. Even the keystone's hum seemed to hold its breath.

Halvard stood in the center of it, hands still open, still held the way she'd been holding his shoulders, holding nothing. She did not fall. Emrys wished she would fall. Falling would have been easier to watch than the way she stood there, straight-backed, soldier-perfect, while something behind her eyes went out with a thoroughness that no scream could have matched.

The lieutenant cleared his throat. "You see," he said gently, horribly, "this is what the seal has cost you. Three hundred years of ignorance, and when the power finally touches one of yours, he burns like a wick. The Order offers training. Understanding. *Names* for the namesless. Come with us, warden, and no one else needs to—"

Emrys hit him.

She did not remember crossing the distance. She remembered the impact, his guard coming up a half-second too slow, her fist and then her knee and then her sword-hilt, and she remembered that she was screaming words she couldn't later reconstruct, and she remembered Halvard's arms locking around her from behind, dragging her back before the killing stroke landed, Halvard's voice in her ear saying *not like this, not like him, not like this* over and over until the words got through.

The lieutenant lay in the trampled grass, breathing wetly, and did not get up. Around him the Unbound withdrew, orderly, unhurried, falling back from the monolith in good order — because they had won, and they knew it, and cruelty did not require haste.

"The ritual resumes at moonrise," the lieutenant said from the ground, smiling through bloody teeth. "Our lady sends her regards. She says the third stone is lovely this time of year."

* * *

They buried what there was to bury at dusk, which was a sword, a boot, and a scorch-shadow they covered with stones because leaving it bare was unthinkable and digging it up was worse. Maren spoke the old words, the ones for soldiers who die far from home, and her voice did not shake, which somehow made it unbearable. Tam had no family in the company. His mother kept a smithy two valleys east. Someone would have to ride to her. No one volunteered, and no one was ordered, because some errands cannot be assigned.

Halvard sat apart, on a fallen birch, cleaning a sword that was already clean.

Emrys went to her and stood, because sitting felt presumptuous, and for a long time neither of them spoke. Above the trees, the bruised sky was doing its slow trickery — clouds remembering shapes, a green aurora crawling where no aurora had any business being. The keystone's light pulsed behind them, steady now that Emrys had gone back to it, palm to granite, holding the crack the only way she knew how: by being there.

"He laughed," Halvard said finally. "Right before. He was winning, and he laughed, and then it took him. Thirty seconds, Emrys. From laughing to gone." She set the sword down with exaggerated care, the way you set down something you might otherwise throw. "I've buried a lot of riders. I always had a speech. *Died holding the line. Died well. Died for the valley.* What speech is there for this? He didn't die. He got *rewritten*."

"He chose to stand," Emrys said. "That's not nothing."

"Don't." Halvard's voice cracked like ice underfoot. "Don't give me the consolation. I watched the seal's great war reach out and eat a child, and you want to tell me it *means* something?" She looked up then, and her eyes were red-rimmed and absolutely level. "You felt it, didn't you. When you touched the stone. It knows you. It called you *little ward*."

"Yes."

"Then answer me honestly, because I have lost my patience with honest things arriving late." She stood. "If the only way to save that stone is to pour more of yourself into it — the way you did today, the way you've been doing since the first night — how much of you does it take? And when it's done taking, what's left?"

Emrys opened her mouth. Closed it. Because the true answer was *I don't know*, and the truer answer was *some part of me suspects the answer is all of it, and I have known since the temple, and I have not said it aloud.*

Before she could decide which truth to offer, the keystone spoke.

Not through her palms. Not through the blood-sense. Out loud, in the air, in a voice like dry leaves and deep water, in a silence so complete it functioned as sound — the first words the silent god had spoken in three hundred years, and every soul in the ruined camp, Unbound and valley-born alike, heard them at once:

*THE SECOND STONE WAS NEVER THE TARGET. YOU WERE MEANT TO COME. YOU WERE MEANT TO TOUCH IT. AND NOW IT KNOWS EXACTLY WHAT YOU ARE, LITTLE WARD — AND SO DO THEY.*

In the treeline, soft as falling snow, the Unbound began to laugh.

Emrys's hand was still on the granite. The crack beneath her palm had stopped widening hours ago.

It had started, very slowly, to trace a new shape — and the shape was a name, and the name was hers.
