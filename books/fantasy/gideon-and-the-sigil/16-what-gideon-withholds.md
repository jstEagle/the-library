## 16. What Gideon Withholds

They buried Sera at the edge of the blight line, where the grass still remembered how to be green.

Emrys dug the grave themselves. No one argued. In the valley you did not let another carry your dead if you could help it — an old custom from the years of plague, when grief was thought to be contagious and shared burdens were the only kind worth having. The earth came up in dark clods, smelling of rain that hadn't fallen yet. Halvard stood at the rim of the pit with his arm bound to his chest, too proud to sit, too broken to pretend otherwise, and said nothing while Emrys worked.

Sera had been twenty-three. She'd joined the company two winters ago because her father's farm failed and mercenary work paid in coin instead of hope. She could whistle through her teeth. She'd once talked Emrys into dancing at the harvest fire, badly, laughing the whole time. None of that belonged on a headstone, so there wasn't one. Just a cairn of river stones, and above it, hanging in the sky like a wound that wouldn't close, the absence of stars.

The blight had reached the valley's western fields overnight. Where the Unbound had drained the second keystone, the land didn't die — it *changed*. Wheat grew silver and soft as fur. A pond near the border had turned to standing glass, and things moved beneath its surface that no one could name. Old Marta Corren had woken three mornings running convinced she was nine years old again, weeping for a mother forty years dead. Memory ran downhill now, like water finding cracks, and magic pooled in the low places of people's minds.

Three days since the stone fell. Two keystones gone. One left, and it was Emrys.

*You are the last ward,* the dying scholar had said, blood on his teeth, months ago at the border. *The valley is the seal, and you are what holds it shut.*

Emrys had spent every day since trying not to believe him. Now belief was no longer optional. It was arithmetic.

When the last spadeful of earth was tamped down, Emrys washed their hands in the stream and made a decision they should have made weeks ago.

"I'm going back to the temple," they said.

Halvard turned. His face was gray with pain he refused to name, and older than his years in a way that had nothing to do with wounds. "To pray?"

"To demand answers."

* * *

The temple of Gideon sat a hard day's ride beyond the old ward-line, up a switchback trail through forest that had begun to whisper. That was the only word for it. The trees murmured in voices just below hearing, and twice Emrys caught their own name in the rustle of leaves and could not swear it was the wind.

They rode alone. Halvard had wanted to come; Emrys had refused, and for once Halvard hadn't fought it. He'd gripped Emrys's shoulder with his good hand instead — the grip of a man sealing something, a promise or a farewell — and said, "Come back. Whatever you find out there, whatever it tells you, come back and tell *me*." And then, quieter: "No more carrying it alone. I meant what I said at the ward-stone. You don't get to protect me by lying to me. Not anymore."

"No more," Emrys had promised, and meant it, and hated how easily promises came now that everything else had burned away.

The temple looked worse than it had in autumn. Or perhaps Emrys saw it more clearly now. The colonnade had shed another wall; moss climbed the silent god's shattered statue like green fingers prying at a secret. Inside, the great nave still held its impossible hush — that pressed-felt quiet, deeper than silence, the silence of a mouth held shut — but beneath it Emrys felt something new. A tremor. Like a held breath beginning to shake.

They built no fire. They ate cold bread and harder cheese, and then they walked the length of the nave to the mural wall they'd found on their first visit, and they looked at it until the torchlight guttered and their eyes ached.

The figure with the birthmark. Their birthmark — the coiled spiral-and-bar at the base of their throat, the mark every midwife in the valley had called Gideon's Kiss and every child had teased them about and every adult had quietly, superstitiously avoided touching. On the mural, the marked figure knelt before a greater figure whose face had been chiseled away centuries ago, receiving something: a light, a chain, a burden rendered in faded pigment as a ring of small burning symbols passing from one hand to another.

On their first visit, Emrys had read it as benediction. A god choosing a servant.

Tonight, hollowed out by Sera's grave and the silver wheat and Marta Corren weeping for her childhood, Emrys read it differently. The kneeling figure's painted face was turned up, and the painter — some forgotten hand with a genius for misery — had given it an expression Emrys knew from mirrors. Not joy. Not awe.

*Please don't.*

"You knew," Emrys said aloud. Their voice fell flat in the nave, swallowed whole. "You've known since before I was born. Since before the valley was founded. You put me in the world like a nail driven into a door, and you never said a word."

The silence answered with silence. It always had. Three hundred years of prayers rising from the valley like smoke into a sealed room, and not one answered. Emrys had grown up inside that silence the way fish grow up inside water. They had defended it to skeptics, explained it to children: *Gideon is a silent god. Silence is how he loves us. He took all the noise of magic out of the world so we could live in peace, and he asks nothing back but our quiet faith.*

It had been a comfort once. Tonight it felt like the silence of a parent watching a child walk toward a cliff with their hands folded, doing nothing.

"Say something." Emrys's voice cracked on the last word, and they were too tired to be ashamed. "You owe me that much. You owe the valley that much. Sera is dead. Halvard nearly died. The Correns' boy woke up screaming in a language nobody speaks. If any of this was ever about protecting us — if any of it was ever real — then *say something*."

Nothing.

Emrys laughed, a short ugly sound, and pressed their palm flat against the mural, against the painted face of the figure who wore their mark. The plaster was cold and gritty under their hand.

"Fine," they whispered. "Then I'll say it for you. I know what you are. The scholar told me before he died, and I've spent half a year calling him a liar because the truth was unbearable. You're not a god. You're a man — the first sigil-bearer — who ended a war by locking magic behind a seal and letting the world forget you did it. You let us build temples. You let us invent prayers. You let my grandmother die believing, and her mother before her, and—" Their throat closed. They pushed through it. "You let me be born into this without my consent, and you watched me bleed for it, and you never once—"

The torch went out.

Not guttered. Not blown by draft. It simply ceased, flame and smoke and heat together, as though fire had been unmade rather than extinguished. Darkness poured into the nave like water into a hull.

And the silence broke.

* * *

It did not come as a voice. Emrys would spend the rest of their life failing to describe what it actually was, and every attempt would fall short, but the closest they ever managed was this: it was as if the temple itself remembered speech. As if the stones, the air, the dark, all the dumb matter of the world had been holding a word in their mouths for three hundred years and finally, painfully, let it go.

The word arrived everywhere at once — behind Emrys's eyes, in the marrow of their arms, in the scar tissue of old wounds — and it was not kind, and it was not cruel, and it was not what they expected.

*I DID NOT SEAL MAGIC TO END THE WAR.*

Emrys stood very still in the dark. "Then why?"

*I SEALED IT BECAUSE I COULD NOT STOP USING IT.*

The words kept coming, each one landing like a stone dropped into a well, the echoes getting darker as they fell.

*I WAS THE WAR'S GREATEST WEAPON BEFORE I WAS ITS END. EVERY SIGIL I INSCRIBED MADE ME MORE, AND LESS. I COULD RAISE THE DEAD AND I COULD NOT RAISE MY HAND AGAINST MY OWN WORK. THE OTHERS FOUGHT FOR LAND OR GODS OR VENGEANCE. I FOUGHT BECAUSE THE POWER WANTED TO BE USED, AND I HAD FORGOTTEN HOW TO REFUSE IT.*

"So you locked it away." Emrys's voice sounded small and far away, someone else's voice. "Including in yourself."

*THE SEAL WAS NOT A WALL I BUILT. IT WAS A DOOR I CLOSED WITH MY OWN BODY ON THE FAR SIDE. WHAT THE WORLD REMEMBERS AS ASCENSION WAS AMPUTATION. I CUT MAGIC OUT OF MYSELF AND OUT OF THE WORLD IN THE SAME ACT, AND I TOLD MYSELF IT WAS MERCY.*

"And was it?"

A pause. Long enough that Emrys understood, with a lurch, that a god — a man who had become a god-shaped hole in the world — was hesitating.

*FOR TWO HUNDRED YEARS, YES. CHILDREN GREW UP WHO HAD NEVER SEEN A VILLAGE BURNED BY SIGIL-FIRE. THEN THE POWER BEGAN TO LEAK. SEALS ARE NOT STONE, EMISSARY. THEY ARE WOUNDS, AND WOUNDS HEAL WRONG WHEN NO ONE TENDS THEM. I COULD HOLD THE CLOSURE FROM WITHIN, BUT I COULD NOT REPAIR IT. I HAD MADE MYSELF PART OF THE KNOT. A HAND CANNOT BIND ITSELF.*

The dark pressed close. Emrys became aware of their own heartbeat, absurdly loud, mortal, insistent.

"So you needed hands on the other side of the door."

*I NEEDED A KEYSTONE THAT COULD CHOOSE. STONES CAN ONLY BE BROKEN. A PERSON CAN HOLD. I POURED WHAT REMAINED OF MY WILL INTO THE VALLEY'S FOUNDING — INTO THE WARDS, INTO THE BLOODLINE, INTO THE MARK. YOUR ANCESTORS WERE WARDENS. YOU ARE THE LAST DISTILLATION OF THREE CENTURIES OF INTENT. I WATCHED YOU BE BORN. I WATCHED YOU TAKE YOUR FIRST OATH. I HAVE WATCHED EVERY DAY OF YOUR LIFE AND SAID NOTHING, BECAUSE A KEYSTONE THAT KNOWS IT IS A KEYSTONE CAN BE PERSUADED, AND CORVANE HAS SPENT DECADES LEARNING HOW TO PERSUADE.*

"That's not protection." The words tore out of Emrys raw. "That's a cage with better paint. You hid the truth from me so I'd be harder to steal. You used my ignorance as a lock."

*YES.*

No excuse followed. No apology. The single syllable hung in the dark, absolute, and somehow that was worse than any justification. Gideon did not lie to soften it. Gideon had apparently never learned how.

Emrys's knees gave. They sat down hard on the cold mosaic floor, in the dark, in the temple of the god who had made them, and they wept the way they hadn't wept since childhood — gracelessly, furiously, with their fists against the floor. For Sera. For the valley that had prayed to a liar. For the child with the coiled mark at their throat who had grown up believing the silence was love.

"You let them worship you," they said when they could speak. "You let them name their children after you. You let them believe you were listening."

*I LET THEM BELIEVE WHAT KEPT THEM WHOLE. IT IS THE SAME SIN YOU ACCUSE ME OF. I HAVE HAD THREE CENTURIES TO REPENT IT AND I HAVE NOT, BECAUSE THE ALTERNATIVE WAS THIS CONVERSATION, AND I KNEW WHAT IT WOULD COST YOU.*

"What would it cost?" Emrys wiped their face with the heel of their hand. "Tell me the rest. All of it. What happens when the last keystone falls?"

The darkness gathered itself. When the answer came, it came slower, weighted, each word laid down like a man setting down something breakable.

*WHEN THE THIRD STONE FALLS, THE SEAL DOES NOT BREAK. IT INVERTS. EVERYTHING I IMPRISONED — NOT MERELY MAGIC, BUT THE WAR ITSELF, THE RAGE OF IT, THE THINGS THE OLD BEARERS MADE AND COULD NOT UNMAKE — COMES THROUGH AT ONCE, SEEDED IN THE FIRST SOUL TOUCHING THE SEAL. THE UNBOUND BELIEVE THEY WILL INHERIT WONDER. THEY WILL INHERIT THE WAR. AND IT WILL WEAR THEM LIKE ARMOR.*

"But if I hold. If I stand at the stone and hold—"

*THEN YOU HOLD IT UNTIL YOU DIE, AND THE SEAL FAILS ANYWAY, ONE GENERATION LATER, BECAUSE A MORTAL KEYSTONE IS A DELAY, NOT A SOLUTION. I DESIGNED YOU TO BUY TIME. I NEVER DESIGNED A WAY OUT. I COULD NOT FIND ONE. I LOOKED FOR THREE HUNDRED YEARS, EMISSARY, AND THERE IS NO WAY OUT THAT I COULD SEE FROM INSIDE THIS SILENCE.*

Emrys stared into the black. "But you're telling me anyway. Why tell me now, if there's no way out?"

Another hesitation. Longer. And when Gideon spoke again, the voice — the memory-of-speech, the world remembering language — was different. Quieter. Almost human.

*BECAUSE YOU ARE NOT ME.*

"What?"

*I MADE THE SEAL ALONE, IN PRIDE AND SHAME, AND I MADE IT PERFECT AND UNCHANGEABLE, AND ITS PERFECTION IS ITS FLAW. YOU HAVE SOMETHING I NEVER HAD. YOU HAVE PEOPLE WHO WOULD DIE FOR YOU WITHOUT BEING DESIGNED TO. YOU HAVE A CAPTAIN WHO FORGAVE YOU A LIE. YOU HAVE A VALLEY THAT BURIED A GIRL YESTERDAY AND DID NOT BLAME YOU FOR IT, THOUGH THEY HAD CAUSE. I BUILT A DOOR. YOU MIGHT BUILD SOMETHING ELSE. I CANNOT SEE HOW. I WAS NEVER ABLE TO IMAGINE A SEAL THAT WAS ALSO A BRIDGE. BUT I HAVE WATCHED YOU FOR TWENTY-SIX YEARS, AND YOU IMAGINE THINGS I CANNOT.*

The silence began to close again, the pressure easing, the world forgetting speech. Emrys scrambled upright.

"Wait. Wait — is that it? You drop all of this on me and go back to *silence*? How do I rewrite a seal I don't understand? Who taught the first bearers? Where do I even—"

*ASK THE ARCHIVIST,* the dark said, already receding, already becoming ordinary silence again. *THE ORDER BURNED THE LIBRARIES. SHE HID ONE. THE FIRST BEARERS LEFT RECORDS, EMISSARY. I COULD NOT READ THEM FROM WHERE I AM. THEY WERE WRITTEN TO BE READ BY SOMEONE WHO CHOSE THE WORK FREELY.*

*THAT WAS ALWAYS THE DIFFERENCE BETWEEN US.*

And then the temple was only a temple — cold, dark, ordinary, the torch a dead stick in Emrys's hand — and the presence that had filled it for three hundred years withdrew like a tide, leaving Emrys alone on the mosaic floor with the wreckage of everything they'd believed.

They sat for a long time. Outside, through the broken roof, the starless sky wheeled on, vast and blind. Somewhere south, Corvane was marching. Somewhere west, silver wheat grew over graves. Somewhere in the valley, an archivist named Odile Fen was cataloguing salvage and had no idea what she was about to be asked.

Emrys stood. Their legs shook; they let them. They spoke once, quietly, to the empty nave, to the chipped statue, to the man who had amputated himself to save the world and then hidden inside the wound.

"I'm not forgiving you yet."

The silence, for the first time in Emrys's life, felt less like absence than like someone listening.

They walked out into the dark, mounted up, and rode for home — for the valley, for Halvard, for the hidden library and the last keystone beating in their own chest — while behind them the ruined temple settled back into its long watch, and ahead of them, at the bottom of the world, the stars stayed dark, and waited to see what a mortal would dare to write.
