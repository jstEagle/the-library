## 17. The Choice of Names

The archive beneath the council hall smelled of dust and tallow and the particular sweetness of old vellum going soft at the edges. Emrys had walked past its door a hundred times in their life and never once thought to open it. In the valley, the past was a settled thing — three centuries of quiet, folded and put away like winter wool. Nobody dug through it. Nobody needed to.

Now Sela Thorn, the archivist, had every lamp in the room burning, and the past lay spread across three tables like a patient opened on a surgeon's slab.

"Here." Sela's finger trembled slightly as it traced a line of faded script. She was older than Emrys's mother would have been, if Emrys's mother had lived, with ink-stained fingers and the kind of eyes that had spent decades reading by bad light. "The *Codex of the First Warding*. We always assumed it was liturgy. Prayers. The kind of thing priests copy out when they have nothing better to do." She laughed — a thin, unsteady sound. "It's not liturgy. It's an instruction manual."

Emrys stood very still. They had learned, in the weeks since the confession at the ward-stone, that stillness was the only posture the valley's fear permitted them. Fear looked different on different faces — Halvard's was grim patience, the council's was calculation, the farmers' was the flat watchfulness of people deciding how close to stand to someone who might be a weapon. But all of it asked the same thing of Emrys: *be still. Don't give us a reason.*

"And what does it instruct?" they asked.

Sela turned the page with both hands, careful as a midwife. The vellum crackled. Beneath the script, half-hidden by centuries of marginalia, was a diagram Emrys knew intimately — the sigil from the dying scholar's hand, the sigil burned into their own skin below the left collarbone since birth. But this version was whole, unbroken, drawn in a circle of small figures whose faces had been worn away by thumbs and time.

"The seal has three keystones," Sela said. "You know that part. Two stones, one blood. The Unbound have broken one stone and are hunting the second. And everyone — Gideon's silence, the Order, the council — everyone has been talking about the seal as though it were a wall. Something you hold up or let fall." She shook her head. "But walls don't need names, Emrys. Seals do. A seal is a promise, written into the world, and a promise is only as strong as the one who keeps it."

The lamps guttered, all of them at once, though there was no draft. Emrys felt the hair rise along their arms. That happened sometimes now — the valley's dead air twitching, the way a sleeping man twitches before waking.

"Gideon wrote the first promise," Emrys said slowly. "Three hundred years ago. With his own name."

"With his own *self*," Sela corrected. "Look here." Her nail found a passage near the diagram's edge, the letters cramped, hurried, as if the writer had been running out of something — time, light, courage. "*The bearer gives not ink but identity. What is inscribed must be surrendered entire. No name may serve twice. No hand may write another's name but its own.*"

The words seemed to sink through Emrys's skin rather than into their ears. They read them again. Then again. The room felt smaller.

"No name may serve twice," they repeated. "Meaning—"

"Meaning Gideon's name is spent." Sela closed her eyes briefly. "He gave himself to the seal completely. That's why he's silent, child. There's almost nothing left of him to speak. He's not withholding out of cruelty or mystery — he's a candle burned down to the wick, holding a flame that isn't his anymore. When the second keystone falls, his portion of the seal goes with it. And then—"

"Then the seal fails," Emrys said. "Unless someone writes a new name into it."

"*Their own* name," Sela said. "That's the condition no one can cheat. The codex is explicit. You cannot carve your name and walk away; you cannot delegate it; you cannot trick it with a false name or a borrowed one. The seal reads truth the way blood reads blood. Whatever you write, you must *become*. Your old self — your history, your name as the world knows it, everything you were before the writing — it doesn't survive the inscription. Not destroyed, exactly. More like... dissolved. Poured in." She looked up, and her eyes were wet and steady at once. "The person who rewrites the seal ends. The guardian begins."

The silence that followed was not the valley's silence. It was Emrys's own, the one they had carried since the night the stars went out — the silence of standing at the edge of a certainty and seeing nothing solid beneath it.

They thought of the murals in the ruined temple. The figure with their birthmark, kneeling at the base of the first ward-stone, hands pressed flat against the stone as if listening for a heartbeat inside it. They had stood before that image and felt the vertigo of learning their identity had been authored by someone else — that Gideon had made them, shaped generations of blood toward this single purpose, a keystone cut without consent. Every choice they had ever believed theirs — taking up the mercenary's sword, taking the escort jobs along the border, even the restless habit of walking the ward-line at night — had been a river following a channel dug long before they were born.

And now the channel ended here, at a table covered in dead men's handwriting, with a choice that was no choice at all.

"If I refuse," Emrys said, "the seal breaks. Magic comes back unbounded. The war starts again."

"Yes."

"If I restore the seal — try to restore it, Gideon's way — I'd have to give my name anyway, and it wouldn't hold, because his is already spent. The codex says so."

"It says a seal written by a spent name is a door painted on a wall. It looks right until the first wind."

"So there are only two doors." Emrys's voice came out flat, mercenary-flat, the tone they used for pricing a job. "Break it and become the Order's herald, or rewrite it and stop being myself. Either way, Emrys Delacroix dies in this room."

Sela flinched. "I didn't—"

"No. You did what archivists do. You found the truth." Emrys touched the diagram, the circle of faceless figures. "Tell me about them. The ones around the edge."

"Witnesses," Sela said softly. "The codex calls them the Circle of Witness. When the seal was first written, twelve people stood at the boundary and watched, so that the sacrifice would not be done in darkness, unseen. So that someone would remember who Gideon had been — not the god, the *man* — after the name was gone." She hesitated. "There's a final passage. I haven't been able to translate all of it, but the sense is clear enough. It says the witnesses owe the new guardian two things: memory and company. Memory, so the old name isn't lost. Company—" she stopped.

"Company," Emrys finished. "So the guardian isn't alone inside it."

"The seal is not a tomb," Sela said. "Read the last line yourself."

She turned the vellum toward the light. The final line was larger than the rest, written in a steadier hand, as if the author had found their resolve at the end:

*A seal kept alone is a prison. A seal kept among the living is a watch. Choose your watchers well, for they will speak your name when you can no longer say it.*

Emrys read it four times. On the fourth reading, something in their chest cracked — not painfully, more the way ice cracks on a river in early spring, the first sign that the water underneath is moving.

All their life they had understood themselves as a thing inherited. The birthmark. The bloodline. The valley's oldest family, sworn to a god who never answered, carrying a purpose nobody had explained because nobody remembered it. Even their rebellion had been inherited — the restlessness, the border-walking, the refusal to settle into farm or forge. They had thought of themselves as a lock waiting for a key, a wound waiting for a knife. When the dying scholar told them what they were, it had confirmed the oldest fear of their life: that they had never once chosen anything, that choice itself was an illusion granted to keep the livestock calm.

But this — this was different. This was not a purpose imposed from outside. This was a door, and behind it a shape only they could make.

Gideon had written his name into the seal and vanished into it, becoming a silence, a withholding, a god-shaped absence that three centuries of worshippers mistook for divinity. He had chosen anonymity and called it holiness. The codex, in its dry archival way, was telling Emrys that this had been a mistake — that a seal kept in silence curdles, that worship grows around absence like moss around a stone, and that the whole rotting edifice of the Order of the Unbound had grown from that one error: a god who stopped answering, a promise kept in the dark.

Emrys could write their name and disappear the same way. Become the new silence. The new absence. The new god the next age would misread.

Or—

"You said the guardian is answerable," Emrys said. "If the seal is rewritten with a living name. If the guardian stays... present."

"That's what the codex implies, yes. A living keystone can hear. Can adjust. The seal becomes less a wall and more a—" Sela searched for the word. "A conversation. Bounded magic, witnessed magic, magic that answers to a person rather than a silence. It's never been done. Gideon didn't dare. He thought presence would corrupt the seal — that a living mind would bend the promise toward its own fears and wants." She smiled faintly. "He may have been right. Or he may simply have been afraid. Gods and men are rarely distinguishable at that altitude."

Outside, the afternoon light was failing toward the grey that had become the valley's permanent weather since the wards began to fail. Through the archive's narrow window, Emrys could see the second ward-stone's distant silhouette on the western ridge, and beyond it the blighted borderlands where the land warped and memory leaked — where a shepherd had come back from the high pasture last week insisting he was his own grandfather, weeping, unable to be persuaded otherwise.

Time was not abundant. Somewhere across those hills, Vess Corvane was gathering her force, and the countdown Sela's texts had revealed was ticking down in shattered stone.

"I need to think," Emrys said.

"You need to decide," Sela answered gently. "Thinking is what we do instead of deciding, when we're lucky enough to have the luxury. I'm not sure you have it." She gathered the codex pages with careful hands. "But take tonight. Take what's left of it."

* * *

Halvard was awake when Emrys came to the infirmary, which was itself a small victory. The wound across his ribs — taken holding the line at the drained ward-stones, a blade of raw sigil-light that had cauterized as it cut — had kept him feverish and half-lucid for days. Now he sat propped against pillows, grey-faced but present, watching the door as if he'd been counting Emrys's footsteps from the hall.

"You look," he rasped, "like a man who's been reading."

"I've been reading."

"Bad news?"

"The worst kind." Emrys dragged a stool to the bedside and sat. For a moment neither spoke. The fracture between them — the lie about the night the wards fell, the confrontation, the weeks of careful, wounded distance — had been mended in motion, on the ride to the second stone, in the failure there and the loss of Brenna to the wild sigils' corruption. Mended, but scarred. Some things you did not say aloud anymore; you just sat on the same side of them.

"Sela found something in the codex," Emrys said finally. "About the seal. About how it ends."

Halvard listened without interrupting, which was his gift and his weapon both. When Emrys finished — the spent name, the rewriting, the dissolution of the old self — he was silent for a long moment, working it through the way he worked through battle plans, turning each piece over.

"So it's not restore or break," he said at last. "It's rewrite or lose."

"That's the shape of it."

"And the price is you. All of you. The name, the history, the—" he gestured vaguely, "—the whole stubborn mule of a person I've known for twenty years."

"Something like that."

Halvard looked at the ceiling. His jaw worked. When he spoke again, his voice was lower, rougher. "When you confessed to the council, I was angry. Not about the secret — well. Also about the secret. But mostly because you carried it alone, and carrying alone is the one thing you've always done, and I thought—" He stopped. Started again. "I thought you'd decided you weren't really one of us. That the valley was just a post you were stationed at."

"It was," Emrys said quietly. "For most of my life. I stood apart. You all swore to a god together and I swore the same oath and somehow mine never took. I told myself it was doubt. I think it was just — I never believed I belonged in the vow."

"And now?"

"Now I've learned the vow was a cage built around me before I was born." Emrys turned their hands over, looking at the palms as if the answer might be written there. "And the only way out is through a door that erases me. Except—" They stopped. Breathed. "Except Sela's book says the seal needs witnesses. Watchers. People who remember the name and speak it when the guardian can't. It says a seal kept alone is a prison."

Halvard's eyes came back to them, sharp despite the fever's residue.

"I spent my whole life thinking I was unchosen," Emrys went on. "Marked by blood I didn't ask for, aimed at a purpose I never agreed to. And every road out of that led somewhere worse. Corvane offered me belonging — her kind of belonging, the Order's kind — and it was almost enough, because the valley never wanted me either. But this..." They tapped their own chest, over the birthmark. "This is the first thing anyone's ever offered me that I get to *choose*. Not the burden. The burden's fixed. But the manner of it. Whether I vanish into the silence like Gideon did, or stay. Whether I write my name and die, or write my name and remain — mortal, flawed, answerable, *here*. Nobody's ever handed me a real choice before, Halvard. I don't intend to waste it feeling noble about the cost."

Halvard was quiet again. Then, slowly, painfully, he reached out and gripped Emrys's forearm — the soldier's grip, the one that meant *oath*, the one that meant *counted*.

"Then choose your watchers," he said. "Because I'm volunteering, and I don't accept refusals from lieutenants."

Emrys's throat tightened. "You can barely sit up."

"I'll be sitting up when it matters." His grip firmed. "Listen to me. Whatever you decide — vanish or remain — someone has to remember who you were. That's not sentiment. That's tactics. A guardian nobody remembers is a god, and gods are how we got into this mess. A guardian somebody remembers is a neighbor." A ghost of his old crooked smile. "Besides, someone has to tell the story of the idiot who waited twenty years to make a decision and then made the biggest one in three centuries."

Emrys laughed — a short, cracked sound, surprised out of them. It felt strange in their own throat, laughter in this season of the valley. Good-strange.

They stayed until the lamp burned low, talking through the mechanics of it — who would stand witness, how many, whether the council could be trusted with the full truth of the rite or only its edges. Halvard argued for telling the council everything; Emrys argued for telling them enough. They compromised the way soldiers compromise, which is to say each side conceded exactly as much as it could afford to lose.

It was near midnight when Emrys stepped out into the cold and saw the sky.

The stars were still dark — they had been dark since the wards began to fail, a blackness overhead that no amount of cloud could explain. But tonight the darkness was moving. Faint smudges of wrong-colored light crawled along the northern horizon, like auroras seen through dirty glass, and beneath them, far off, a sound rolled across the blighted lands: not thunder, not quite. A rhythm. Drums, maybe. Or boots. Thousands of them, keeping time.

From the watchtower on the ridge, a horn sounded — one long note, then two short. The valley's oldest signal, taught to every child: *they are coming.*

Emrys stood in the infirmary doorway with the codex's last line burning behind their eyes — *choose your watchers well* — and understood, with the terrible clarity of the moment before a battle, that the choice of names would not be made at leisure in an archive after all.

It would be made at the last ward-stone, with the Order of the Unbound breaking against the valley's farmers and smiths, and whatever name Emrys chose to write, they would write it in blood and firelight, with no time left to be anyone else.

Behind them, Halvard's voice came hoarse through the door: "Emrys. Tell me that's not what I think it is."

Emrys turned back toward the north, where the false lights crawled, and the birthmark beneath their collarbone pulsed once, warm as a struck match.

"It's exactly what you think it is," they said. "Get your boots."
