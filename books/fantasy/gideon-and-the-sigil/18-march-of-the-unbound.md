## 18. March of the Unbound

The horn sounded at dawn, and it was wrong.

Emrys had grown up on the valley's horns — the low moan that called shepherds in from the high pasture, the double-blast that meant fire, the three rising notes that meant flood water in the Alden. This was none of those. It came from the border watch-towers, a single sustained note that did not rise or fall or end, held so long that Emrys's chest ached in sympathy with it, and they understood before anyone told them: this was a horn no one had blown in three hundred years, because there had been nothing to blow it for.

*They are coming.*

They stood on the steps of the council hall when the second horn answered from the south tower, then a third from the east, until the whole valley rang like a struck bell and would not stop ringing. Smoke rose from the borderlands where the blight had taken hold — not the gray smoke of burning timber but something darker, violet-tinged, that climbed against the wind. The wild sigils were still feeding on the land out there, and now the Unbound were coming through the wound they had made.

"They'll reach the heart-stone by nightfall," Halvard said.

Emrys turned. They hadn't heard him come up the steps; they weren't sure he'd made any sound at all. He was dressed for war — the old parade harness the valley kept for ceremonies, polished and absurd, over his field leathers — and his left arm was bound tight beneath it, the wound from the ward-line still knitting. He should have been abed. He looked like a man who had decided sleep was a luxury for people with more time.

"You shouldn't be standing," Emrys said.

"And you shouldn't be alive," Halvard said. "We've both stopped pretending the world runs on what it should do." He nodded toward the square below, where the bells had begun to toll and people were spilling out of their houses into the cold morning light. "Look at them."

Emrys looked.

The square was filling. Not soldiers — the valley had perhaps two hundred of those, and half were already dead or wounded from the strike on the borderlands. Farmers. Smiths. The cooper whose name Emrys had never learned despite twenty years of passing his shop. Old Maren from the mill, who had once thrown Emrys out of her yard for trespassing as a child, standing now with her husband's boar spear in her hands like she'd been born holding it. Children being herded up into the hills by grandmothers who refused to go with them.

"They can't win," Emrys said. It wasn't despair. It was arithmetic. Corvane had marched through the broken border with six hundred veterans and enough captured sigil-work to level the valley twice over. Against farmers with boar spears.

"No," Halvard agreed. "But they're arming anyway." He was quiet a moment. "I've been thinking about why."

"Because it's their home."

"That's part of it. That's most of it, maybe." He turned to face Emrys fully, and there was something in his expression Emrys had rarely seen there — an openness he usually guarded like coin. "But I watched you, these last weeks. Standing apart. Ready to walk out to that stone alone and die on it, and let us all keep our comfortable ignorance while you paid the bill." His jaw worked. "You were going to do it again. Weren't you? Last night. When you left the infirmary."

Emrys said nothing, which was answer enough.

"I thought so." Halvard exhaled slowly. "So here's what I've decided, and you don't get a vote. You are not going to die alone on that stone. If the valley stands, it stands *with* you. All of us. Because—" he gestured, abruptly, at the square, at Maren and the cooper and the whole armed congregation of ordinary lives, "—because they deserve to know what they're fighting for. And so do you."

"Halvard—"

"The council meets within the hour. You're going to tell them everything. Again, if you must. And then you're going to stand in front of these people and let them choose." His voice roughened. "You spent your whole life believing you stood apart from them. Let them prove you wrong. It's the least they can do, and the most."

* * *

The council chamber had never held so many people.

Word had moved faster than any crier could carry it — the horns, the smoke, the simple animal telegraphy of fear — and by the time Archivist Senn opened the session, the hall and its galleries were packed shoulder to shoulder, and more stood in the doorway and the yard beyond. The council sat at their long table looking suddenly small: seven faces Emrys had known all their life, seven faces that had voted to send riders to a capital that no longer answered, seven faces now turned toward the door as Emrys entered with Halvard at their side.

Senn stood. She was the oldest person in the room and possibly the valley, a small bent woman with ink-stained fingers and eyes that had read more years than she'd lived. When she spoke, her voice carried the way quiet things sometimes do.

"Emrys Delacroix. The council has questions. But I think—" she glanced down the table, and no one objected, "—I think the valley has them too. So we will do this in the open. Tell us what is coming."

Emrys had rehearsed this confession once already, at the ward-stone, and it had gone badly — fear, recrimination, a mother pulling her child closer. They had expected the same here. They braced for it, drew breath, and began.

They told it all. The dying scholar among the unlocked stones. The truth about Gideon — not god but bearer, not savior but jailer, the first to carve the sigils that sealed magic away and ended the old war at the cost of ending the old world. The valley itself as the seal's heart, three centuries of silence built on a lie of omission rather than malice. Their own blood. Gideon's final ward. The last living keystone.

And then the part they had not planned to say, the part that came out of them raw and unpolished because Halvard's eyes were on their back:

"I was going to go to the stone tonight. Alone. Corvane needs me to break the seal willingly — my consent, my hand, my blood given freely. If I refuse, if I die refusing, the seal holds. I thought if I stood there and gave them nothing, the valley would be safe, and none of you would ever have to know what I am." They stopped. Their voice had gone hoarse. "That was cowardice wearing sacrifice's clothes. I'm done with it. Ask me anything."

The silence afterward was enormous.

It was broken, unexpectedly, by the cooper — Tam Voss, that was his name, Tam Voss, and Emrys wondered at themselves for finally learning it on the day everyone might die. He rose from his bench, a big slow man with burn-scarred forearms, and he did not look at the council at all. He looked at Emrys.

"My daughter was born the year the stars went dark," he said. "She's never seen one. None of the children have. We tell them stories about stars like they're grandmother tales." He turned his hands over, examining them as if seeing them new. "I always thought the silence was holy. Sworn to Gideon, silent god, silent valley. I thought our whole lives were a kind of prayer." He looked up. "Now you're telling me it was a wall. That we've been living inside a locked room and calling it heaven."

"Yes," Emrys said. "I'm sorry."

"Don't be sorry. Be straight with me." Tam's voice didn't rise; it just got heavier. "If the seal breaks — if Corvane gets what she wants — what happens to us?"

"Magic comes back. All of it. Unbounded, unwitnessed, the way it was before the war. The blight in the borderlands is a taste of it — land warping, memory warping. Some people would take to it. Some would burn. The Order believes that's freedom." Emrys met his eyes. "I believe it's the war starting again, with worse weapons."

"And if the seal holds?"

"It holds only while I live and refuse. Corvane knows it. Every day I stay alive is a day she has to come to me instead of waiting me out."

Tam nodded slowly. Then he did something Emrys would remember for the rest of their life, however long that proved to be. He picked up his smith's hammer from beside his bench — he had brought it to a council meeting, because he had known, the way everyone had known, what the horns meant — and he set it down on the table in front of the council with a knock that echoed through the chamber.

"Then we put ourselves between you and her," he said. "Not because you're a keystone. Because you're ours. You've walked our borders twenty years and taken our coin and kept our roads, and you never once asked to be anything more than that. Well." He sat back down. "More found you. That's not your doing. Standing with you is ours."

The gallery did not erupt. It was quieter than that, and heavier — a sound like a tide turning, benches shifting, boots on stone, the low murmur of a community arriving at a decision it had already made somewhere deeper than speech. Old Maren stood and laid her boar spear across the table next to Tam's hammer. Then another weapon. Then another, passed hand to hand down the rows, until the council table was a hedge of iron and ash wood and the chamber smelled of oil and honest sweat.

Senn raised her hand for quiet, though she was smiling — a small, fierce, ancient smile. "The council will vote," she said. "All in favor of standing at the heart-stone, whatever comes, say aye."

The word came back from two hundred throats like a landslide.

* * *

They worked through the morning, and the valley became something Emrys had never seen it be.

The smithies ran without pause, hammers falling in overlapping rhythm, re-edging scythes and hammering pikeheads from wagon springs. The fletchers' row was a forest of half-finished arrows. Women who had never held a blade in their lives learned spear-drill in the market square from veterans of the border watch, and learned it badly and laughed while they learned it, and the laughter was its own kind of armor. The chandlers made torches by the hundred. The tanners cut leather into crude brigandines. Someone dragged the old festival brazier up to the hill above the heart-stone and filled it with pitch-soaked wood, so that when night fell there would be at least one star in the sky, man-made and defiant.

Emrys walked among all of it and did not know how to hold it.

They had spent a lifetime learning the exact dimensions of their apartness — the mercenary who took the jobs no one else wanted, who ate alone, whose birthmark made grandmothers touch iron and mothers draw children close. They had built a whole self out of that distance, and now the distance was gone, dismantled in a single morning by a cooper's hammer on a council table, and Emrys kept catching themselves waiting for the mistake. Waiting for someone to look at them and see the keystone instead of the neighbor.

It happened once. A young woman at the well — Emrys knew her face, Dara something, Dara Ilves, who had lost her brother at the borderlands — stared as Emrys passed, and her grip tightened on the bucket rope, and Emrys braced.

"You're really it?" Dara said quietly. "The thing the seal hangs on?"

"I'm a person the seal hangs on," Emrys said. "There's a difference. I'm trying to learn it myself."

Dara studied them a long moment. Then she dipped her cup in the well and held it out. "Drink," she said. "You'll want your strength. My brother used to say the valley doesn't love anybody easy, but when it loves somebody, it's forever." Her voice cracked on the last word, and she pressed the cup into Emrys's hand and walked away before either of them had to say anything else.

Emrys drank. The water was cold and tasted of stone and snowmelt and home.

* * *

The scouts came back at midafternoon, riding hard, and the news was worse than the arithmetic had promised.

Corvane had not divided her force. Six hundred marched in a single column up the river road, unhurried, disciplined, with captured ward-stone fragments lashed to wagons and Unbound sigil-bearers walking at the flanks like shepherds around a flock. And ahead of the column, ranging wide, the hunters — the ones who had chased Emrys across the blighted borderlands, the ones with the wild sigils scarred into their skin.

And something else. The scout, a boy named Perrin who could not have seen seventeen winters, delivered his report in a flat rapid voice with his hands shaking: the column was preceded by a fog. Not weather-fog. Sigil-fog, violet-gray, eating the color out of the fields it crossed. Where it passed, the grass stood up gray and brittle, and the cattle in the outlying pastures had gone silent — not dead, the scout insisted, just *silent*, standing motionless in the blighted grass with their shadows lying the wrong way.

"They mean to unmake the ground as they come," Halvard said grimly, in the war council gathered in the guardhouse. Maps lay on the table, weighted with daggers. "Take the land's memory first. By the time they reach the stone, there'll be nothing left to fight for except the stone itself."

"Then we meet them before the fog does," said Captain Orla Fen, who commanded what remained of the border watch. She was a broad, gray-haired woman with a voice like a rockslide, and she had spent the afternoon turning farmers into something almost resembling a line. "Here." Her finger came down on the map at the narrows where the river road squeezed between the millpond and the alder slope. "One road, water on one side, thick trees on the other. Numbers stop mattering. We make them come to us in a throat, and we make the throat cost."

"They'll burn the alders with sigil-fire," someone said.

"Then they waste an hour doing it. An hour is an hour." Orla looked around the table. "We are not winning this. Everyone understand me? There is no clever plan that wins this. There is only time. We buy hours. One hour, two, three — every hour we bleed them for is an hour Emrys needs at that stone."

Every eye in the room turned to Emrys.

They had been dreading this moment since morning — the moment when the whole plan narrowed to a single point, and the point was them. They had imagined flinching. Instead, strangely, they felt calm. The calm of a bowstring drawn to full draw, when the archer's part is done and only the release remains.

"What do you need?" Orla asked simply.

"An hour," Emrys said. "Maybe less. When I reach the stone, I'll know. The sigil will... tell me. Or I'll tell it. Either way, once I start, no one touches me and no one calls me off. Whatever happens around me. Whatever happens *to* me." They looked at Halvard. "Swear it. All of you. If I falter, you hold the line anyway. The seal breaks or it doesn't, but the valley goes on either way. Promise me you'll make it go on."

Halvard held their gaze for a long moment. Then he put his fist, gently, against Emrys's breastbone — the old gesture, soldier to soldier, the one that meant *my life rides behind yours*.

"Aye," he said. And around the table, one by one, in voices rough and steady and afraid, the others said it too.

* * *

Dusk came early, because the fog ate it.

From the ridge above the heart-stone, Emrys watched the southern sky bruise violet, and the first torches of the column appear along the river road like embers blowing upstream. Below, in the failing light, the valley's line was forming at the narrows — two hundred and forty souls, by Orla's count, farmers and smiths and soldiers, spears and scythes and hunting bows, standing in the throat of the road with the millpond black at their backs. Torches guttered along the line. Nobody sang. Nobody prayed aloud, though Emrys saw lips moving everywhere, and understood that after three centuries of a god who never answered, some habits were older than theology.

Halvard found them at the ridge. He had put on his helmet, which Emrys had never seen him do outside of ceremony, and it made him look like a figure from one of Senn's old manuscripts — some hero out of a story nobody believed anymore.

"The hour's bought or it isn't," he said. "Go now, while they're still forming. The narrows will hold long enough. I'll make certain of it."

"Halvard—"

"Don't." He smiled under the helmet's shadow. "Don't waste the hour saying things. Go write your name on that stone. Come back and say them after." He paused. "Both of us. After."

Emrys gripped his forearm, hard, feeling the pulse beneath the bandage, alive, alive, alive. Then they turned and went down the north slope alone, toward the heart-stone, where the old sigil of Gideon waited in the dark like a held breath.

Behind them, far to the south, the first war-horn of the Unbound sounded across the fields — and was answered, instantly, by every horn and bell and hammer and human voice in the valley, a ragged roaring chord that rolled up out of the dark to meet the marching fog.

The battle for the last ward had begun.
