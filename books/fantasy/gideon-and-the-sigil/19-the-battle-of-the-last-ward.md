## 19. The Battle of the Last Ward

The horn sounded twice before dawn — once from the eastern ridge, once from the mill at the river bend — and by the time the second note died, every soul in the valley knew what it meant.

Emrys stood on the low hill above the last ward-stone and watched the dark come down the mountainside like water finding a crack. Not soldiers in ranks. Not a line. The Unbound came as a flood of small lights, hundreds of them, each one a sigil burning cold white against the starless sky, and beneath those lights moved shapes that had been people once and were something else now, their shadows moving wrong, lagging behind their bodies or running ahead of them.

"They're not even trying to hide," Halvard said beside them. The captain's armor was old parade steel, polished for the first time in years because there had been no time to do anything but polish it. "No scouts, no feints. They think we're already dead."

"Are we?"

Halvard smiled, and it was the same crooked smile they'd shared around a hundred watchfires, the one that meant *yes, probably, but not yet.* "The smiths finished the pikes at midnight. Farmer's iron on farmer's ash handles. Three hundred of them." A pause. "Corvane has three thousand."

"Then we'd better be worth ten each."

"We were always worth ten each. You told me that when you were sixteen and I caught you brawling with the tanners' boys." Halvard's hand found Emrys's shoulder, gripped once, hard. "I'm glad I was wrong about you, Emrys Delacroix. All these years, I thought you were restless because you didn't belong here. Turns out you were the only one who belonged here more than any of us."

"Don't," Emrys said. Their voice cracked on the word. "Don't say it like a farewell."

"I'll say whatever I like. I'm your captain." Halvard turned to the line below — three hundred farmers and smiths and soldiers drawn up in a crescent around the ward-stone, the stone itself rising black and ancient from the valley's heart, its sigils guttering like candles in wind. "Positions. Everyone to positions. We hold until Emrys reaches the stone, and then we keep holding anyway, because holding is what we do."

The line murmured assent. No cheering. The valley had never been a place for cheering; it was a place for work done quietly and well, and this morning's work was dying if it came to that.

Emrys looked east, where the sky should have been lightening. It wasn't. The stars had been out for weeks now, and without them dawn came grudgingly, gray as dishwater, as if the world itself had forgotten how mornings worked. Somewhere beyond that gray, the second keystone's ruin was still bleeding its corruption into the rivers, and somewhere south of that, the temple of Gideon stood open and empty, its god silent, its murals staring.

*You were made to finish what I could not.*

Gideon's voice still lived under Emrys's skin like a splinter. They flexed their hands and felt the sigil in their blood answer, faint and warm, the way a scar answers before rain.

"Halvard." They made themselves say it plainly, because Halvard deserved plain words. "If this goes wrong—"

"It won't."

"If it does. If I don't come back down from that stone. Burn my name into the ward-line with the others. Don't put *ward of Gideon*. Put just my name. Just Emrys. That's all I ever was, and it's enough."

Halvard studied them for a long moment, and then did something they had never done in fifteen years of soldiering together: saluted. Fist to heart, the old gesture, the one sworn only to the valley itself.

"Just Emrys," the captain said. "Now go. You've an hour to buy, and I've a line to hold."

* * *

The Unbound hit the western slope first, and the sound was nothing like any battle Emrys had ever imagined.

They had expected shouting, steel, the animal noise of men killing men. What they got instead was quiet — a terrible, spreading hush, as if the flood of white sigils drank sound the way they drank everything else. The front rank of the valley's militia loosed their arrows, and the arrows flew silently, and where they struck the Unbound's shield-wall the barbs simply unraveled, fletching and shaft and head dissolving into threads of pale light that drifted upward and went out.

Then the lines met, and the silence broke all at once into screaming.

Emrys did not go with the line. Their path ran straight through the middle of it, down the hill, across the trampled barley field, toward the ward-stone — and between them and the stone stood everything Corvane had brought.

They drew the old mercenary sword in their right hand. In their left, they let the sigil rise.

It came easier now than it ever had. Weeks ago it had been a birthmark, a curiosity, a thing hidden under sleeve and shame. Now it was a language they were learning to speak, and the first word they had learned was *no*. Light crawled up their left arm in branching lines, silver-white shot through with something darker, older — Gideon's geometry rewritten at the edges by Emrys's own hand, their own name threaded through the pattern like new stitching in old cloth.

The first Unbound warrior reached them — a big man whose shadow moved a half-second behind him, his blade sheathed in hungry white fire. Emrys caught the blow on the flat, felt their teeth click with the impact, and answered with the sigil: a short, brutal stroke of counter-light that took the man's borrowed magic apart at the seams. He dropped like cut rope, breathing, alive, his shadow snapping back to him with a look of stunned gratitude on his face.

*Not all of them chose this,* Emrys remembered. *Most of them didn't choose anything at all.*

So they fought the way they'd learned to fight in the valley: economically, without cruelty, breaking the magic and sparing the mage wherever breaking was enough. It was not always enough. The third one who came at them had sigils burned into her eyes, and when Emrys unmade her workings she screamed as though he'd unmade something in her, and swung anyway, and Emrys's sword took her under the arm because mercy could not wait forever.

The barley field became a charnel house of light. Everywhere the valley's line bent and buckled and — impossibly, stubbornly — held. Farmers who had never held anything heavier than a scythe learned in an hour what a pike wall could do when everyone in it was too frightened to run. The smiths' iron sang. And over it all, above the din, Emrys heard Halvard's voice, hoarse and steady, calling the wheel of the line like a bell tolling:

*Left! Close left! Tanners, hold — hold — now wheel!*

An hour. Halvard had promised them an hour.

Emrys cut the last guardian down at the base of the hill and started up it, and that was when the sky tore open.

* * *

Vess Corvane descended from the torn air like a woman stepping off a ship, unhurried, her boots finding the scorched earth as though it had been laid down for her. Behind her, high above the battlefield, the wound in the sky pulsed with white light, and through it Emrys could see — briefly, unbearably — the shape of the world as the Unbound wanted it remade: a world with no borders, no wards, no walls, every secret laid open, every power loose, everything touching everything.

It would have been beautiful, Emrys thought, if it hadn't also been the end of every quiet thing.

"You're late," Corvane called down the hill. She sounded almost fond. Her coat was scorched; a line of blood traced her jaw; she looked like a woman who had walked through her own army's slaughter and found it invigorating. "I expected you an hour ago. I've been keeping your stone warm."

"The hour isn't up."

"No?" She glanced back at the valley's line, and Emrys followed her glance, and their stomach turned.

The line was folding. Not breaking — folding, deliberately, the way cloth folds, the way Halvard had drilled them to fold when a flank collapsed rather than let the whole wall shatter. But the flank *hadn't* collapsed. Halvard had folded it on purpose, pulling the center back around the ward-stone's hill, trading ground for time, giving up the field inch by inch so that every inch cost the Unbound another minute. It was the maneuver of a commander who has already decided the field is lost and the day is not.

And at the hinge of the fold, where the pressure was worst, stood Halvard, parade steel gone gray with other people's blood, roaring the wheel-call in a voice that was starting to fray.

"Your captain fights well," Corvane said. "Better than my people expected. They're disappointed. They were promised the valley would scatter." She turned back to Emrys, and her expression shifted — the fondness draining away into something colder and more honest. "I'm going to offer you one last kindness, keystone. Walk away. Walk west, past the wards, past the ruins, keep walking until the world runs out. I'll let you live. I'll even mean it. You've earned a life that isn't this one — gods know no one in this valley ever gave you one."

"And the valley?"

"The valley is the seal's body. The seal dies tonight. I'm sorry it hurts." She said it gently, and that was the worst part — she genuinely was sorry, the way a surgeon is sorry, the way a storm is sorry. "You've seen what's coming, Emrys. You've felt the magic bleeding in since the second stone fell. You know it can't be stopped, only shaped. Break it willingly and we shape it together. Make me kill you for it, and it breaks anyway — uglier, crueler, with your people's deaths written into the working. Either way the stars come back. Either way the world wakes up. The only question is whether you die believing you mattered."

Emrys stood very still. Below them, the line folded again, and Halvard's voice cracked mid-call, and picked itself up, and called again.

Every word Corvane said was true. That was the trap of her, the thing Emrys had understood since the temple road: she never lied. She only showed you the truth arranged in the shape of surrender.

"You're right about one thing," Emrys said. "The seal can't be restored. Gideon's dead wrong about that, and so are you. It can't be broken either." They began to climb the hill, sigil-light climbing with them, arm and shoulder and throat. "There's a third thing, Vess. I found it in the temple. I found it in the archivist's books. And I'm going to write it into that stone if it's the last thing I do."

Corvane's eyes widened — just slightly, just for a heartbeat — and Emrys saw, for the first time, fear in the woman who feared nothing.

"*Rewritten?*" she breathed. Then she laughed, sharp and disbelieving. "By *you*? You're a mercenary with a birthmark. You're a valley rat who never—"

"I never belonged anywhere," Emrys agreed. "That's exactly why it has to be me."

They broke into a run.

* * *

Corvane met them halfway up the hill, and the duel that followed was not a duel; it was two philosophies trying to kill each other.

Her sigils were the old ones, Gideon's own grammar, learned in the Order's archives and sharpened for war — vast, precise, inhumanly elegant, each working a cathedral of light. Emrys's were the same alphabet misspelled, stitched over, corrected in a living hand: smaller, faster, wrong in ways that were suddenly right. Where Corvane built, Emrys edited. Where she struck with a hammer of force that would have shattered the old Emrys like glass, the new Emrys stepped *through* it, rewriting the strike's own edge a half-inch wide of their body, spending themselves like a spendthrift, blood running freely from their nose, their ears, the corners of their eyes, because the seal's keystone was paying for every word of the new language out of their own flesh.

Below them, the battle roared. Above them, the tear in the sky widened.

And below them — Emrys saw it in a gap between exchanges, a half-second they would pay for with a burn across the ribs — the Unbound's reserve finally crashed into the folded line at the hill's foot, and the line, three hundred strong at dawn, was forty, and the forty were falling back up the slope toward the stone, and at their center, holding the hinge alone, stood Halvard.

*Hold the line. Buy the hour.*

Halvard bought it. Emrys would learn later, from survivors, exactly how: how the captain planted the valley's banner in the earth and anchored the last wheel of pikemen around it; how the smiths' iron held against sigil-fire long past any reason it should have; how Halvard took a blade of white light through the chest and did not fall, but caught the shaft of it in both gauntleted hands and *held it*, the way you hold a door shut, the way you hold a line, snarling through bloodied teeth at the Unbound adept who wielded it — *not this ground, not today, not while my people are still on it* — and held, and held, and held.

Emrys saw none of that. What Emrys saw, in the terrible clarity of the moment between Corvane's strikes, was simpler: Halvard going down. Slowly, the way an old tree goes down, groaning, taking branches of the enemy with it, refusing to the last to fall backward — falling forward, toward the enemy, toward the stone, toward Emrys, so that even in death the captain's body would be a wall between the Unbound and the thing they'd come to break.

The scream that tore out of Emrys was not a word. It was the sigil itself, spoken aloud, the whole new language at once, every correction and every stitch and every letter of their own name flung down the hill in a single wave of silver-dark light that blew the Unbound off Halvard's body like leaves off a grave.

The silence after it rang.

Corvane lay ten feet away where the wave had thrown her, coat smoking, one arm hanging wrong, propped on her good elbow and staring at Emrys with an expression that had finally, finally shed its certainty. Around them both, the battle stuttered — Unbound and valley-folk alike frozen, staring up the hill at the figure standing over the ward-stone with blood sheeting down their face and light pouring off them like heat off summer iron.

Emrys did not look at Corvane. They looked at Halvard, small and still at the bottom of the hill, the banner fallen beside them, the line around them shattered and reforming and shattering again. Fifteen years. A hundred watchfires. One crooked smile.

The hour was bought. The price was paid.

Emrys turned to the ward-stone, raised their bleeding hand, and pressed their palm against Gideon's ancient sigil — and felt, beneath the stone's dead surface, something vast turn its attention toward them, attentive and afraid.

Beneath their palm, the old sigil began, at last, to move.
