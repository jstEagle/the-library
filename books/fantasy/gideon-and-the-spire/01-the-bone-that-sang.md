## 1. The Bone That Sang

The smuggler's name was Wren Halloway, and he was dying the way old river ice dies—slowly, then all at once.

Theo Iversen had known him for eleven years, which in the Thornfell mountains was long enough to call a man a friend without ever saying so out loud. Wren ran goods along the low passes: salt, iron nails, tallow, occasionally a crate of something that had never seen a tax stamp and never would. He paid Theo in coin when he had it and in stories when he didn't, and once a year, on the first hard frost, he showed up at Theo's door with a jug of plum brandy and the claim that this year's batch was better than last year's, which it never was.

Now he lay on the cot in Theo's workroom with his boots still on, because he'd refused to take them off—"I might need to run," he'd said, and laughed, and the laugh had cost him something—and his hands were gray as old ash.

"Carve me something," Wren said. His voice came out thin, scraped down to the grain. "Something for my girl. Marta. She'll want to hold onto something that isn't a memory of me coughing."

Theo set down the knife they'd been using to trim candle wicks and looked at him for a long moment. "You know what I carve."

"I know exactly what you carve." Wren's mouth pulled sideways. It wasn't quite a smile. "That's why I'm asking."

* * *

Everyone in the high valleys knew what the bone-carver did, or thought they did. They brought Theo their dead—or rather, they brought pieces of them, a knuckle bone from a father, a rib sliver from a drowned daughter—and asked for relics. A pendant. A ring-stone. Something small enough to wear against the skin, carved fine, polished smooth. And when you held it, the carvings would warm, and if you were quiet, you could hear the dead person inside it. Not a ghost. Not a voice, exactly. More like a room you could walk into. The shape of a life pressed into bone the way flowers are pressed into books.

That was the story the villages told about Theo Iversen, anyway, and Theo had spent years making sure the story stayed vague. *The bone-carver hears things,* people said, and left it there, because the truth was stranger and the truth invited questions, and questions invited visitors, and visitors were how a person stopped being invisible.

Invisibility was not a flaw in Theo's life. It was the architecture of it.

Theo worked alone in a stone cottage two hours' climb above the village of Harrowgate, at the treeline where the pines gave up and the scree began. No family name worth speaking. No trade anyone official cared about. The Crown's tithe-collectors came through twice a year and marked Theo down as *artisan, minor*, which was the most useful lie ever told by accident. The inquisitors hadn't come into the Thornfells in a generation. The old gods' shrines up here had gone quiet so long ago that children used them as wind-shelters and nothing more.

And the dead—the dead were quiet too, mostly. That was the part no one understood. People imagined hearing the dead must be like living in a market square, all that clamor. But bones held only what the person had put into them while alive, and most people put in very little. A good relic carried an hour of feeling, maybe two. A mother's lullaby worn smooth by repetition. The specific terror of a rockfall, brief and total. Love, sometimes, if the person had been generous with it.

Wren Halloway had been generous with almost everything except money.

"All right," Theo said. "But I choose the bone."

"Knew you'd say that." Wren fumbled at his neck and drew out a cord. On it hung a small finger-bone, yellowed, wrapped in waxed thread. He'd been wearing it for years; Theo had noticed and never asked, because asking was how conversations became confessions. "Left hand. Index. My own."

Theo took it carefully. "You cut off your own finger?"

"Winter crossing, twenty years back. Frostbite got to the second knuckle before I felt anything." He flexed the nine remaining fingers of that hand, a little flourish, showing off the scar. "Kept the bone. Seemed wasteful not to." His eyes were wet now, though his voice stayed level. "It's mine. All of it's mine. Whatever she hears in it, I want her to know it's true."

*Marta will hear.* So he knew. Of course he knew. Half the mountain knew and said nothing, the way mountain people protect anything useful and strange: silently, completely, like snow protecting seedlings.

"I'll need a day," Theo said. "Maybe two."

Wren nodded, and then, because he was dying and dying men don't waste breath on transitions, he closed his eyes and slept.

* * *

Theo worked at night, by lamplight, the way they always did.

The workroom smelled of linseed oil and marrow-dust and the particular cold-stone smell of a house built into a hillside. Tools hung in their places along the wall: rasps and burnishers, a dozen knives graded by fineness, three drills with bits no blacksmith in the lowlands could have made. On the shelf above the bench sat the failures—relics that had come out wrong, carrying stutters of feeling instead of whole memories, or worse, carrying nothing at all. Theo kept them as a mason keeps cracked stones. Proof that the work was real work, done by hands and not by miracle.

The truth of the gift—which Theo had never told anyone entire, not even Wren—was this: bone remembers pressure. Every year of a life presses on the skeleton the way water presses on a riverbed, and a carver with the knack can read those pressures, follow the channels, find where the deepest currents ran. Carving a relic was not putting a soul into bone. It was cutting away everything that wasn't the soul, until what remained was pure enough to hold its shape outside the body. Like releasing a knot. Like opening a fist.

It had been Theo's whole life since childhood, since the day in the plague summer when they'd picked up grandmother's knucklebone from the death-cart and heard her humming, clear as morning, and screamed loud enough to bring the neighbors running. Since then: decades of careful, quiet work. Decades of staying small.

Wren's finger-bone sat on the bench under the lamp, and Theo looked at it for a long time before touching it.

A self-carved relic was rare. Most people couldn't do it—couldn't bear the intimacy of it, or couldn't get the angle right one-handed, or simply couldn't think of themselves as finished, as material. But Wren had carried this bone against his throat for twenty years, and bone that lives close to a person drinks deep. There would be a lot in it. Theo braced the way they always braced, shoulders loose, breathing slow, and picked up the finest knife.

The first cuts went easy. That was always the sign of good material—bone that wanted to open, that had been waiting. Theo pared away the outer layer, the dull mineral shell, and felt the first warmth rise under the blade. Familiar territory. Wren's childhood: a kitchen with a leaky roof, the taste of burnt porridge, a woman singing off-key. Then the frostbite winter, sharp and white, the astonishing calm of sawing through one's own flesh because panic would kill faster than cold. Then Marta—a tavern, rain on the windows, her laugh doing something to his chest that he'd never managed to describe to anyone—

Theo paused. Set the knife down. Pressed the heels of both hands against their eyes until colors bloomed.

This was the part of the work no one warned you about, because no one outside the craft believed it: you didn't just read the memories. You *felt* them. Secondhand, diluted, but real. Twenty years of loving someone poured through Theo's chest in the space of ten heartbeats, and it was like drinking a whole cask of wine in one swallow. They sat there shaking slightly, grinning foolishly at nothing, until the feeling settled into something they could carry.

Then they picked the knife back up, and the bone sang wrong.

There was no other word for it. Bone made sounds when it opened—not audible ones, not to the ear, but to whatever it was behind Theo's sternum that did the hearing. Good bone hummed. This *shrieked*, one high discordant note, like a wire snapping under tension, and Theo's hand jerked and the blade skated across the bench.

They sat very still.

Bones didn't do that. In thirty years of work, through hundreds of relics, through the worst deaths the mountains could devise, bone had never shrieked. Grief rang. Terror clanged. But this was neither. This was the sound of something that should not be inside the bone at all.

Theo waited until their hands steadied, then went back in. Carefully now. Cautiously, the way you'd approach a snare you'd half-seen.

Below Wren's memories—beneath the kitchen and the frostbite and Marta's laugh, beneath even the oldest layer, the boy running ridgelines barefoot—there was another channel in the bone. Thin. Recent. A hairline crack of foreign pressure, the kind of mark left when bone is near something that presses on it from outside. And threaded through that crack, faint as smoke, was a voice.

Not Wren's.

Theo had heard a thousand voices out of bone and knew every texture the dead could have: the mumbling, the looping, the fierce clarity of the newly dead. This was none of these. This voice was *far away*. That was the only way to hold the thought—it came up the channel in Wren's bone like sound coming up a well, attenuated, distorted, but enormous, the way thunder is enormous even heard through a wall.

And it was screaming.

Theo's hands were steady—they were always steady, that was the joke of it, the bone-carver's hands never shook—but everything behind the hands had turned to ice water. Because the scream had words. Screams usually didn't. This one did, over and over, worn smooth as a litany, as if whatever was screaming had been screaming it for a very long time:

*the crown is hollow*

*the crown is hollow*

*the crown is hollow—*

Theo dropped the knife.

It clattered against the bench and rolled off and hit the floor, and the sound was absurdly ordinary, and Theo was grateful for it, grateful for anything ordinary. The lamp flame stood straight. The wind worried at the shutters. Somewhere below, Wren coughed in his sleep, twice, and was silent again.

*The crown is hollow.*

There was one crown that mattered. Everyone in the Nine Valleys knew whose head wore it, even here, even at the top of the world where the king's face appeared only on coins and proclamations nobody could read. King Aldric the Peacemaker. Twenty-two years on the throne. The wars ended, the roads safe, the shrines of the old gods closed and shuttered and forgotten. A good king, the lowlanders said. A quiet reign.

*Hollow.*

Theo looked at the finger-bone lying on the bench, small and yellowed and utterly unremarkable, and understood, in the slow horrible way one understands things at night, that Wren had no idea what he'd been carrying. Twenty years against his throat. Whatever had pressed that voice into the bone had done it recently—within the season, within the month—and Wren had walked through half the mountain passes since, oblivious, delivering salt and untaxed brandy and, apparently, messages from somewhere no message should come from.

From the crown itself. From inside it. From whatever was left inside it, screaming.

"No," Theo said aloud, to the empty workroom, because saying it aloud made it smaller. "No, that's not—that's fever-talk. That's the bone settling. That's—"

The voice came again, and this time it wasn't screaming. It had noticed them. Theo felt the attention turn, the way you feel eyes on your back on a dark road—a pressure shift, a prickle, the sense of something vast redirecting its vastness toward one small point of light in one small room at the top of the world.

It spoke once, quietly, and the quietness was infinitely worse than the screaming.

*you hear me*

Not a question. A statement, delighted and terrible, the way a hunter is delighted.

Theo did the only thing thirty years of discipline had trained them to do. They wrapped the finger-bone in wool, placed it in the iron box on the high shelf—the box lined with lead, bought years ago from a superstitious smelter for reasons Theo had never fully examined until now—and closed the lid, and sat down on the floor with their back against the bench, and breathed.

The box did nothing, of course. Lead stops radiation, not voices. But the voice didn't come again. Perhaps distance helped. Perhaps the thing at the bottom of the well had lost interest, or been called elsewhere, or had gotten what it wanted, which was worse: confirmation. *Someone hears.*

Dawn was still hours off. Below, Wren coughed again, longer this time, and Theo thought about Marta, who would want something true to hold, and about the fact that the truest thing in Wren Halloway's finger-bone was not Wren's at all.

They got up. They cleaned the knife. They put fresh oil on the whetstone, and trimmed the lamp, and did every small orderly task that stood between them and the one large disorderly fact sitting in an iron box on the shelf.

Because here was the problem, laid out plain as a ledger: Theo Iversen had survived by being no one. By carving quietly, charging fairly, telling the villagers their grandmother's lullaby sounded lovely, and never, ever asking questions about where bones came from or what was happening in the lowlands. Invisibility had kept them alive through plague winters and border skirmishes and the slow tightening of the Crown's grip on everything from taxes to prayer. Invisibility was the whole design.

And invisibility had just cracked open like river ice, because a dying smuggler had carried home a scream from the capital, and the scream knew Theo's name.

Well. Not the name. But the *hearing*. The thing that made Theo Theo.

Somewhere south, past the passes, something wearing a king's crown had reached out through twenty years of bone and found the one pair of ears in the mountains that worked.

Theo banked the lamp and went down to check on Wren, who was sleeping badly, muttering, his gray hands twitching at nothing. Theo sat beside the cot until the muttering stilled, watching the man's chest rise and fall, rise and fall, and tried to think of a single course of action that didn't end in disaster.

Stay hidden and hope the voice forgets? Things like that didn't forget. Go south and look? Look for *what*—a carver with a knife and a pack of relics, walking into the capital to investigate the king?

Warn someone? Who? Who would believe it? Who would *repeat* it, safely, without the wrong ears catching the words?

Outside, the sky was going gray at the edges. Theo stepped out the door into the cold, and the wind came down off the high snowfields carrying the smell of pine and stone, and beneath it—so faint Theo might have imagined it, except that they had never once imagined anything in thirty years of hearing—came a sound from the direction of the valley. From the village of Lowmark, two days' walk east, where Theo delivered relics twice a year.

It was the old shrine on Lowmark's hill. Silent for sixty years. Stone and moss and bird droppings and nothing else.

It was humming.

* * *

Wren died on the third evening, between one cough and the next, mid-sentence, complaining about the price of lamp oil. It was, Theo reflected later, exactly the death he would have chosen: quick, cheap, and mildly aggrieved.

By then the relic was finished. Theo had gone back to the finger-bone on the second night, after a full day of arguing with themselves and losing, and carved it properly—cut around the foreign channel, sealed it off behind clean walls of polished bone, and left only Wren inside. The kitchen with the leaky roof. The frostbite and the terrible calm. Marta laughing in a tavern while rain wrote on the glass. Everything true, everything his.

When Marta came for the funeral, Theo put it in her hands and watched her face do what faces always did—confusion, then recognition, then the breaking-open—and said the words they always said: "He's in there. All of him that matters."

She thanked them through tears. She never knew what it had cost.

And that night, alone, Theo packed a travel bag they hadn't touched in six years, and took down the iron box, and did not open it, and stood holding it in both hands like a man holding a live coal.

Two days east, a dead shrine was humming. South, a hollow crown was screaming. And Theo Iversen, who had spent a lifetime learning to be unheard, understood at last that the hearing had never been theirs to switch off.

The road went down. Down was where all of it was waiting.
