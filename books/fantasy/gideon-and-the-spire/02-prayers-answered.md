## 2. Prayers Answered

The trouble with miracles, Theo's mother used to say, is that they never come free. She'd said it the way she said everything—half to herself, hands busy with the curing salts, not looking up. Theo had been nine, maybe ten, and hadn't understood what she meant until years later, when they'd held their first relic and heard a dead woman's last thought: *the price was too high, tell them the price was too high.*

They thought about her now, standing in the mud of Lowmark's market square with a sack of carved bone trinkets that had stopped selling an hour ago.

Because nobody in Lowmark was buying funeral relics anymore.

That was wrong on its face. Lowmark was a dying village—the young ones left for the river towns, the old ones stayed, and death was the one trade that never dried up. Theo had been coming here twice a year for six years, selling wards against restless dead, keepsakes cut from elk bone so a widow could hear her husband's voice one more time. Good business. Quiet business.

Today the square had gone strange. People watched Theo from doorways with a look they usually saved for tax collectors and weather. An old man spat when Theo passed his stall. A woman pulled her daughter indoors hard enough to make the girl stumble.

"You're the carver." The voice came from behind—a boy, twelve at most, all elbows and suspicion, blocking the lane like he'd been assigned to it. "The one who talks to bones."

"I carve them," Theo said carefully. "Talking's extra."

"Ma says you're a fraud." The boy's chin came up. "Says if anyone could really hear the dead, they'd have heard what took Da's memory."

Theo went still. "Took his memory?"

The boy faltered, suddenly aware he'd said more than he meant to. But there was something under the hostility—something raw and young and desperate—and it won out over his mother's warnings. "The shrine," he said. "Up past the mill. It's been dead my whole life. Moss on it, birds nesting in the god's eye-hole. Then three weeks back, my sister prayed at it. She's seven. She just wanted—" His voice cracked and he hated himself for it. "Da's been sick since autumn. Coughing blood. She prayed for him to get better."

"And?"

"He got better." The boy looked at Theo with something close to hatred, which Theo recognized as grief wearing armor. "He got out of bed in four days. He's hauling timber again. And Ma can't remember his name."

* * *

The house sat where houses sit in dying villages—leaning into its neighbor, roof patched with mismatched slate, smoke rising from a chimney that worked harder than it should. The boy, whose name was Piet, led Theo there without another word, and his mother met them at the door with a carving knife in her hand and no apology in her stance.

"Get off my step, bone-witch."

"Annet," Piet said. "Just—listen."

"There's nothing to listen to. My husband's well. Praise whatever heard us." Her eyes were red-rimmed and furious, and she gripped the knife like she'd practiced. "Whatever you're selling, we don't want it."

"I'm not selling anything." Theo kept their voice low, level, the voice they used on frightened animals and grieving widows. "I want to help your husband remember."

Something flickered across her face—hope, quickly murdered. "You can't. The physicker from Harrowgate came. Said it's a wasting of the mind, happens sometimes, nothing to be done." She laughed, and it was an awful sound. "Nothing to be done. That's what they say when they mean *we don't care.*"

"May I see him? I won't charge you. I won't touch anything. I'll just look."

She stood there a long moment, knife trembling slightly, and then stepped aside because hope is a thing people can't quite bring themselves to kill, even when they know better.

Bram Iversen—not related to Theo, though half this mountain range seemed to share the surname—sat by the fire in a chair built for a bigger man. He was broad-shouldered, timber-muscled, maybe forty, and he smiled at Theo with the open, unguarded warmth of someone meeting a stranger for the first time in his own home.

"Visitors! Annet, we have visitors. Is it market day?" He turned to his wife. "This is market day, isn't it? You'll stay for supper, friend. Annet makes a stew—what do you make, love? You make a stew."

"Yes, Bram." Her voice was flat as slate.

"She makes a stew," Bram confided to Theo, delighted. "Best in Lowmark. Isn't that right, Piet? Best stew." He patted the arm of his chair, frowning faintly. "I'd stand up to greet you properly, but I've got this feeling I'm forgetting something important. Been having that feeling for weeks now. Like walking into a room and the room's empty." He chuckled. "Doctor says it's nothing."

Theo knelt beside the chair. "May I hold your hand, Bram? Just for a moment."

"Course you may. Friendly sort, aren't you."

His hand was calloused, warm, alive. Theo closed their eyes and reached—not for the dead, for once, but for the living, which was harder, like trying to hear a whisper in a storm. Living minds roared. Dead ones whispered clearly. But memory leaves residue everywhere, even in the living, even in the blood and bone, and Theo had spent twenty years learning to read the faint script of it.

What they found made their stomach drop.

It wasn't emptiness. Emptiness would have been kinder. It was *edges*—memories with the centers scooped out, like apples after wasps. Bram remembered that he loved his wife; the shape of the love remained, warm and solid. But every specific memory of her had been eaten clean. He knew he'd married her. He could not recall her face, her voice, the wedding, the first time he'd seen her, the fight they'd had about the mill taxes, the night Piet was born. All of it—gone, and not faded. *Removed.* The edges of each absence were smooth, deliberate, surgical.

And at the center of every hollowed memory, where the missing piece should connect to what remained, Theo felt something else. A texture. A signature. Something vast and patient and *hungry*, like the mark a mouth leaves on fruit.

Theo opened their eyes. Bram was watching them with mild curiosity, waiting politely for the stranger to finish holding his hand.

"Well?" Annet demanded from the doorway. "Well?"

Theo stood. Their legs felt unsteady. "How long has the shrine been active?"

Her face shut like a door. "I don't know what you mean."

"The shrine above the mill. Your daughter prayed there. Your husband was healed. Now he doesn't know your name." Theo held her gaze. "I'm not here to take anything from you, Annet. I'm telling you that whatever answered her prayer ate nineteen years of your marriage out of your husband's head. And I need to know if yours is the only family it's fed on."

The knife-hand dropped slowly to her side. When she spoke again, her voice was small in a way it hadn't been before. "The Verrins' boy. He prayed for rain for their crops. Got his rain." She swallowed. "Old Marta. Prayed her rheumatism away. Her son says she looks at him now like he's a lodger. Doesn't know him. Doesn't know she raised him alone after his father drowned." Annet's eyes filled, and she blinked the tears down with visible fury. "Nobody remembers anything. Nobody remembers *anything*, and everyone's so grateful they don't ask why."

* * *

The shrine sat on a shelf of granite above the millpond, exactly as Piet had described: moss-furred, bird-nested, ancient beyond any living memory. It was older than the kingdom, older than the roads, one of the thousand nameless wayshrines scattered through the Thornfells that the Crown tolerated because tearing them down had never seemed worth the effort. A squat stone figure, features worn to suggestion, a hollow where its eyes should have been.

Except the hollow wasn't empty anymore.

As Theo climbed the last stretch of path, they felt it before they saw it—that pressure behind the ears, the taste of copper at the back of the throat. Twenty years of hearing the dead had taught Theo every flavor of silence: the silence of empty graves, the silence of well-rested bones, the silence of things that had moved on. This was none of those.

This was the silence of something listening.

The shrine's eye-hollows glowed. Faintly, like coals banked under ash—but glowing, unmistakably, in daylight. And around the shrine, the moss had died in a perfect circle, brown and crisp, as though the ground itself had been drained.

Theo stopped ten feet away and did what they always did, what they couldn't stop doing: listened.

The dead of Lowmark were all around—generations of them sleeping in the churchyard below, in the barrows on the far slope, in the unmarked plots where the plague years had overflowed. Normally they murmured like wind through grass, formless, content. Now they were pressed flat against the far side of their rest, silent in the specific way of animals hiding from a predator.

But underneath that terrified hush, threading through it like a root through soil, Theo caught something else. Not a voice. A *pull*. Slow, rhythmic, patient as tide. Somewhere far away—farther than Lowmark, farther than the mountains—something enormous was breathing in, and everything loose in the world drifted toward it. Memories. Prayers. Names.

Every prayer answered here was paid for here, but the payment traveled somewhere else.

Theo backed down the path without turning around, the way you leave a room with something dangerous in it.

* * *

That evening, in the taproom of the Drowned Ox, Theo spread a map of the Thornfell trade routes across a corner table and bought enough ale to keep the landlord friendly and incurious.

The logic was simple and ugly. Shrines didn't wake on their own. Whatever had stirred the Lowmark shrine had to reach it somehow—or rather, Lowmark's prayer had to travel *somewhere*, along some channel, and channels followed roads. If one shrine had woken, others might have. If others had, there would be a pattern. Patterns had centers.

Theo marked Lowmark with a bit of charcoal. Then, working from six years of delivery routes, they marked every wayshrine they knew of within three days' walk: the twin stones at Harrowgate Ford. The cairn-shrine at Miller's Rest. The broken arch at Sallow Cross. The one in the sea cave near Greywater that smugglers swore by. Eleven shrines, all told, scattered along the old drover roads like beads on a fraying string.

Then Theo did something they'd never done before. They took out the oldest relic in their pack—a wrist-bone from a drover dead two hundred years, carved with route-marks by his own grandson so the man wouldn't lose his way even in death—and asked it a question no relic had ever been asked.

*Where are the shrines singing?*

The bone grew cold. The drover's ghost surfaced slow and confused, a man-shaped ripple in deep water, and then—instead of his own memories—he turned, in the manner of ghosts, toward something outside himself. Toward the north. Toward the capital road.

And he began, very softly, to hum.

Not a tune. A vibration. The same pull Theo had felt at the shrine, translated into sound the dead could make: a long, low note that Theo realized, with a lurch, was the same note they'd felt in Bram's hollowed memories. The signature. The hunger. The breathing thing beneath the world.

The bone sang it for eleven heartbeats. Then the drover's ghost fled back into his relic, and the bone went ordinary and cold in Theo's palm, and the taproom noise rushed back in like water into a footprint.

Theo looked down at the map. Eleven shrines. They spent the next hour doing what they did best—quiet, invisible work—and by the time the candles burned low, they had their answer.

Every shrine within a day's walk of the capital road: awake. Glowing. Fed.

Every shrine deeper in the mountains, off the old pilgrim ways: still asleep, moss-covered, safe.

The pattern wasn't random. It was a *network*. Someone—or something—had woken the shrines nearest the great roads first, the ones travelers passed, the ones children could find. Building something. Feeding something. A chain of little mouths all swallowing memories and prayers and names and passing them down the line, south, toward the capital.

Toward the Spire.

Theo had heard the word only in fragments—in the mutterings of dead kings' servants, in the half-remembered complaints of masons whose bones Theo had read years ago. *The Spire, the Spire, mind the Spire, we dug too deep and the king said dig deeper.* They'd always assumed it was a tower, some royal folly in the capital. Now, looking at the map, at the neat fan of awakened shrines all pointing south like compass needles, Theo understood it was neither folly nor tower.

It was a drain. And everything in the Thornfells was flowing toward it.

A shadow fell across the table. Theo's hand closed around the wrist-bone on reflex.

"Carver." The landlord, refilling a mug Theo hadn't touched. He nodded at the map without pretending not to look at it. "Word of advice, friend. There's a rider come through yesterday asking after you. Crown man. Inquisitor's badge, grey cloak. Asking which road the bone-carver took." He set the mug down. "Folk here are grateful for what you told Annet. But grateful only stretches so far when there's a badge involved."

Theo's blood went cold. "Which way did he ride?"

"North. Toward Harrowgate." The landlord hesitated. "He had a list. Long one. Names of villages. Names of shrines." He met Theo's eyes. "Names of carvers."

Theo packed in the dark, left coin on the table for the map's worth of ale, and slipped out the back of the Drowned Ox into a night full of stars and nothing else. Behind them, Lowmark glowed faintly at its shrine-lit edges, feeding. Ahead, the road ran north toward Harrowgate, toward the inquisitor, toward the capital and the thing that breathed beneath it.

Eleven shrines humming. One rider hunting. And somewhere south, behind the capital's walls, something ancient opening its mouth.

Theo pulled their hood up and walked into the dark, and for the first time in six years, invisibility felt less like safety and more like being already caught.
