## 3. The Justiciar's Fall

They took her sword first.

That was the order of things—Serah Vane had learned the ceremony of degradation the way she'd learned everything else in twelve years of service: by watching it done to someone else and memorizing the mistakes. They always took the sword first, because the sword was the office made visible. Then the badge of the twin scales. Then the cloak, folded by a clerk who couldn't meet her eyes while he did it, his hands shaking so badly the white fabric slithered off the table twice before he got it into a square.

The sword they kept. The badge went into a ledger drawer with a receipt. The cloak they burned, because that was doctrine—a justiciar's cloak was consecrated, and a disgraced one could not be allowed to carry the Crown's protection out into a world that might mistake her for something she no longer was.

*You are no longer protected,* Lord Chancellor Merrick said, in the dry voice he used for reading sentences. *Which means you are no longer bound. Remember which of those frightens you more.*

She remembered. She thought about it every hour since.

The hearing had lasted forty minutes. Forty minutes for twelve years. The charges were read in a monotone: refusal of lawful execution, contempt of tribunal, obstruction of the Inquisition's work in the matter of one Maren Ostwick, shrine-keeper, of Lowmark. Serah had stood at attention through all of it because sitting felt like begging, and she had never begged in her life and did not intend to learn the habit at the end.

"You were shown the evidence," Merrick said. "Confession, witnesses, the shrine itself warm with old-god residue. Inquisitor Cole performed the rite of verification himself."

"I was shown a confession extracted over three days without sleep," Serah said. "I was shown witnesses who recanted under my questioning and then un-recanted when Cole's clerks spoke to them alone. And I was shown a woman with burn scars on her wrists from ropes, not fire, who told me she'd do anything to make it stop." She let that sit in the silent chamber. "I asked for two weeks to investigate. I was given until dawn."

"And instead you cut her bonds and put her on a smuggler's cart east."

"Instead I did my job."

"Your job," Merrick said, and here his voice changed—not crueler, but quieter, which from Merrick was worse—"was to execute the sentence of the tribunal. Justice is not your private possession, Vane. It belongs to the Crown. That is what the badge means. That is what it has *always* meant."

And there it was. Twelve years of believing the opposite, laid bare as heresy in front of six men who had never once knelt in a burned village and counted the bodies.

"She was innocent," Serah said. "We both know it now. The real keeper confessed before Cole's pyre finished cooling. A boy. Seventeen years old. He turned himself in to save her and Cole burned him anyway, because the tribunal's sentence had already been pronounced and unpronouncing it would have been an admission."

Merrick's face did not move. That was the thing about him; nothing ever moved. "An admission," he repeated, "that the Inquisition does not make errors. Yes. I'm aware of the arithmetic."

"So you knew."

"I know many things." He closed the ledger in front of him with a soft, final sound. "Serah Vane. You are stripped of office, rank, and protection. You are exiled from the capital for a period of no less than five years. Return before your term ends and you will be tried as a common criminal. May the Twins weigh you kindly."

The Twins hadn't weighed anyone kindly in years. But she bowed her head, because the form demanded it, and walked out of the chamber past the six men who would not look at her, and down the long marble hall where her boots sounded like someone else's footsteps entirely.

* * *

They gave her until sundown to leave the city. It was, she supposed, a courtesy. Some part of the machinery still remembered she'd been useful once—the Thornfell pacification, the Saltmarsh cult, the eleven years of warrants served clean and cold without a single appeal upheld against her. Useful tools got courtesies. Broken ones got pyres.

She spent the hours doing three things.

First she went to the archive annex on Tanner's Row, where a junior records-keeper named Pell owed her his life from the Saltmarsh affair, and she stood in the doorway of his office while he pretended to file things, and she said, "I need copies. Everything the Inquisition holds on the shrines along the Thornfell roads. Survey reports, incident logs, tithes collected, personnel assigned. Ten years back minimum."

Pell went gray. "Serah—Lady Vane—I can't. If Cole finds out—"

"He won't find out if you're careful, and if he does find out, you'll tell him I stole them and you fought me. He'll believe it. Everyone believes I'm exactly the kind of woman who'd do that." She held out her hand. "Pell. Eleven years ago I pulled you out of a cellar in Saltmarsh while a man with a knife was deciding whether to open your throat. I've never asked you for anything since."

He looked at her hand for a long moment. Then he looked past her, at the empty corridor, and shut the door.

Second, she went to the temple district and stood outside the Hall of the Twins for a while, not going in. She'd been raised devout—her mother had been a lay sister, her father a carter who tithed even in bad winters—and somewhere in the last decade the faith had quietly become a costume she wore over the actual work, which was this: finding out what was true, and making the truth cost less than the lie. The Twins were supposed to be about that. Weighing. Measuring. *Justice should outlive the law*, her mother used to say, and young Serah had thought it was piety. Now she understood it was a warning. Laws were written by men who could be frightened, bribed, or dead. Justice had to survive them.

She didn't go in. The gods of the capital had stopped answering prayers years ago—or so everyone said—and lately, according to the reports Pell would soon be copying for her, they had started again. That frightened her more than Cole did. Things that resumed after silence were never resuming as the same thing.

Third, she went home. Home was two rooms above a farrier's shop in the Coppersmith Ward, spartan as a barracks, because she'd never seen the point of accumulating things a posting could take away. She packed in twenty minutes: clothes, whetstone, the short blade she'd bought privately at nineteen because a justiciar's sword belonged to the Crown but her life belonged to her, a coil of good rope, flint, dried meat, a heavy traveling cloak the color of wet slate. Her mother's book of hours went in last, wrapped in oilcloth. She wasn't sure why. She wasn't sure of much anymore except the direction she was riding.

At the bottom of her campaign chest, under fifteen years of folded orders and commendations, she found the letter she'd been carrying since the tribunal. Not Merrick's sentence—that was public record. This was older. A warrant, issued eight months ago, signed by the king's own hand, ordering the quiet removal of "irregular devotional activity" along the mountain roads. Removal. Not investigation. *Removal.* She'd filed it, then, because filing was procedure and procedure was safety, and she'd told herself the word meant demolition permits or tax seizures or any of the hundred dull instruments by which crowns ground people down.

It meant what it said. It had always meant what it said. The shrines weren't being taxed. They were being erased, root and branch, and the king had ordered it personally, and now the king's inquisitors were burning keepers on confessions that wouldn't survive a candle's scrutiny, and when one justiciar in the whole rotten apparatus said *wait, look, this is wrong*, the apparatus had turned on her within a day like a body rejecting a splinter.

That speed was the thing she kept turning over on the ride north. Institutions were slow. Bureaucracy moved like a glacier—it had taken four months to approve her request for two extra clerks in Saltmarsh. But from her objection to Maren Ostwick's pyre to her own degradation had been nine days. Nine days. Someone high up had been waiting for her to step wrong. Someone had already decided what she was, and the tribunal had merely performed the paperwork.

Or—she made herself consider it, riding up through the river country with the capital's smudge of smoke sinking behind her—someone above the tribunal. Someone whose decrees needed signing these days more often than they used to, and whose signature had changed. The clerks gossiped. Clerks always gossiped. Pell had mentioned it once, laughing: the king's hand had gotten cramped, cramped and small, like a different man wrote it.

She rode north, and she let the thought grow teeth.

* * *

The Thornfell mountains announced themselves a day before she reached them—a bruise-colored wall across the horizon, snow still lying in the high passes though it was nearly midsummer. She'd served a winter tour here early in her career, hunting bandits for a garrison commander who drank, and she remembered the country the way you remember a bad wound: the roads that weren't on maps, the villages that watched you pass without a single door opening, the way sound behaved strangely, carrying for miles in the cold air or dying a foot from your ear depending on the mountain's mood.

The first shrine she came to was three days out of the lowlands, in a fold of hills above a village called Bracken Hollow. She found it because Pell's copies—delivered to a waystation in a package marked as survey documents—included an incident log from two years prior noting its demolition. *Shrine of the Door-Below, structure razed, stones scattered, site salted per protocol.*

The stones were not scattered. They were stacked. Neatly, recently, by hands that had carried each one back up the hill and rebuilt the little ring wall and set the lintel stone across the top, and someone had carved fresh marks into the lintel—a spiral, worn deep, the grooves dark with grease or blood or both.

Serah stood in front of it for a long time with her hand on her sword hilt, feeling foolish, because there was nothing there to fight. Just a circle of stones and the wind and the enormous indifferent silence of the mountains.

Then the silence changed.

She had no better word for it. The wind didn't stop. The birds didn't stop. But underneath everything, the way you feel a storm coming in your bad knee, she felt the air acquire a pressure, a *leaning*, as if the whole valley had tilted slightly toward the shrine. Toward her. The hair rose on her forearms. The spiral on the lintel seemed—for one heartbeat she would deny for the rest of her life—to turn.

And a voice that was not a voice said, inside her skull rather than her ears, in a tone of vast and ancient relief:

*At last. One who weighs.*

She drew her sword. There was nothing to point it at. The pressure eased, drained away like water through sand, and the valley was only a valley again, and she stood alone in the grass with a naked blade and her heart slamming against her ribs like a prisoner demanding release.

After a while she sheathed it. After a longer while she sat down on the fallen lintel stone, because her legs had opinions of their own, and she laughed—one short, ugly bark of a laugh with nothing funny anywhere near it.

*One who weighs.* The Twins' oldest title. The words her mother had whispered over her cradle. Either the old god-stories were true and something in the mountains had just noticed her, or she was going mad in exile, and she genuinely could not say which explanation she preferred.

What she could say—what she wrote in her journal that night by firelight, in the flat unadorned hand of a woman trained to record evidence—was this:

*Day 4 north. Shrine at Bracken Hollow rebuilt against direct order, within two years of demolition. Fresh offerings. Anomalous acoustic/psychological event witnessed personally. Conclusion: the Inquisition's suppression has failed completely, and whatever is waking in these mountains knows the difference between a soldier and a justiciar.*

*Also concluded: I am not going home in five years. I am going to find out what answered that child's prayer in Lowmark, and what burned for it, and why the king's hand has changed, and I am going to write it all down somewhere Cole's fire cannot reach. Let them call it justice without a badge. A badge is tin. The weighing is the work.*

She banked the fire and slept badly, dreaming of scales tipping.

* * *

Two days later, in a narrow pass where the map showed nothing but a goat track, she heard hoofbeats behind her and turned her horse sideways in the trail to block it, hand on her short blade, because in the Thornfell the things that followed you were rarely friendly and never accidental.

The rider came around the bend at a canter, hooded, leading a second packhorse loaded with panniers that clinked. Smuggler, she thought. Cult courier, she corrected, remembering the spiral. Either way: a witness, a lead, or a threat, and she had learned in twelve years that those three categories were usually the same person wearing different weather.

"Halt," she said, stepping down into the trail, badge-less, lawless, armed, and absolutely certain of one thing only: whoever this was, they were riding toward the same answers she was, and she intended to reach them first.

The rider reined in. Beneath the hood, a thin face looked down at her—sharp-boned, wary, younger than she'd expected—and the packhorse shifted, and from somewhere inside its panniers came a sound that stopped Serah's breath.

A sound like bone clicking against bone.

And beneath it, faint as a dying ember, unmistakable as a name:

A voice. Whispering. *Begging.*

The rider's eyes widened—they'd heard it too, or felt her hear it—and for a moment the two of them stared at each other across the narrow trail, outlaw and outlaw, while the dead in the panniers murmured their secrets to the mountain air.

"Well," Serah said slowly, drawing her blade anyway, because caution was the one virtue that had never yet betrayed her. "I think you and I need to talk."
