## 4. Smuggler's Road

The pass had no name, which was the point of it.

Serah Vane had found it the way she found most things worth finding—by following what powerful people wanted hidden. Three days out of Lowmark, two days behind a rumor of a courier carrying relics for the cult of the Open Door, she'd climbed past the last shepherd's hut and into country where the mapmakers gave up. The trail narrowed to a shelf of stone above a drop that would kill a man twice on the way down. Pine wind. Snowmelt ticking off the rocks. A road that existed only for people with something to hide.

She crouched behind a fallen slab of granite and did the arithmetic again. The courier would come from the north, moving light and fast, probably alone. Cult couriers traveled alone because what they carried couldn't be explained to companions. She'd seen the pattern in the capital before her fall: seizures of Open Door correspondence, letters written in a cipher that referenced *hearers*—people who could pull truth out of the dead the way a well pulls water. The Crown called them witches. The cult called them keys.

Her hand rested on the hilt of her sword, and she noticed, not for the first time, that it didn't feel like hers anymore. A justiciar's blade was an extension of a warrant. Without the warrant it was just steel, and steel without law was just murder with better posture.

*You're not a justiciar anymore,* she reminded herself. *You're a woman with a sword and a grudge, hiding behind a rock.*

The morning wore on. She ate half a heel of bread and let the cold sharpen her. And then, near midday, she heard footsteps on the trail below—unhurried, careful, the walk of someone who knew every loose stone on this road and stepped around them out of habit rather than fear.

Serah drew her sword and waited.

* * *

Theo Iversen smelled the ambush before they saw it.

Not the person—the person smelled of nothing but cold iron and old leather—but the *intention*. Ten years of smuggling through the Thornfell taught you that a pass this good went unused for one of two reasons: nobody knew about it, or somebody was waiting in it. Theo had been counting on the first. The silence of the stones around them said otherwise. Rocks didn't gossip like people did, but they held a kind of memory, and the memory of this particular ledge had a recent human weight in it, patient and armed.

Theo stopped walking and spoke to the empty air ahead.

"You should know I can hear you breathing."

Nothing. Then, from behind the granite slab, a voice like a door closing: "Then you know I've been patient. Come out slow. Hands where I can see them."

Theo came out slow. Hands where she could see them. The woman who rose from behind the rock was tall, sunburned, with the cropped hair and hard-worn travel clothes of someone who'd stopped performing femininity somewhere around the same time she'd stopped believing in institutions. Her sword was drawn but low. Her eyes did a quick, professional inventory: the pack, the knife at Theo's belt, the way Theo stood—weight back, ready to run.

"Cult courier," she said. It wasn't a question.

"Smuggler," Theo said. "Which is a much less exciting job."

"The pack."

"Is full of my work."

"The pack," she repeated, "or I open it myself and we find out together."

There were ways out of this. Theo catalogued them automatically: the scree slope to the left, the blind corner thirty paces back, the knife. All of them ended with this woman's sword in play, and sword-people were unpredictable in exactly the way smugglers hated. So Theo did the thing that had kept them alive through every bad checkpoint and suspicious toll-keeper since childhood.

They set the pack down slowly and opened it.

"Look," they said. "But gently. Some of these are older than your grandmother."

Inside, nested in wool and cedar shavings, lay the bones. Not grisly ones—a carver's collection, each piece worked smooth and bright, etched with fine lines of script that caught the light like frost. A knucklebone the size of a plum. A rib curved into a crescent. A skull no bigger than a fist, polished to ivory.

The woman stared at them. Something moved across her face—not disgust. Recognition of a different kind.

"These are relics," she said. "Funeral bones. You're a bone-carver."

"I'm a lot of things today, apparently." Theo sat back on their heels. "Who are you? Inquisitors don't usually work alone, and you don't burn like one. You look like someone who used to have paperwork."

The sword tip dipped an inch. "Serah Vane."

Theo knew the name. Everyone in the Thornfell trade knew the name by now—the justiciar who wouldn't light the pyre, stripped of office in front of the garrison at Harrowgate, gone into the mountains like a coin dropped down a well. Smugglers told stories about her. None of them agreed.

"They say you burned three shrines before you found your conscience," Theo said.

"They say a lot of things about me. Most of them were written by the people who fired me." Serah crouched, keeping the sword across her knees, and looked at the relics properly now. "I'm looking for a cult courier. Carrying relics to the Open Door. You fit the description."

"Do I?"

"You're on their road, carrying their cargo, alone."

"I'm on *my* road. This pass has been mine for nine years. And the cargo is going to grieving families, not cultists." Theo turned one of the bones over with a fingertip—a small vertebra, carved with a spiral. "This goes to a widow in Coldbrook whose husband died in a rockfall. When she holds it, she'll hear him. His voice. His actual memories, the last year of his life, as long as the carving lasts. That's not cult work. That's grief work."

Serah was quiet for a moment. "And the Crown says that's witchcraft."

"The Crown says a lot of things." Theo closed the pack. "You burned shrines, Justiciar. Did any of them talk back?"

Something flickered in her expression—there and gone, fast, but Theo had spent a lifetime reading faces that were trying not to speak.

"One did," she said finally. "After. Too late. I keep—" She stopped herself, jaw tightening. "That's not why I'm here."

"Everyone says that too."

* * *

It might have ended there—an armed truce, two travelers passing in opposite directions on a road built for neither of them—if the pack hadn't spoken.

It happened when Serah reached out, almost involuntarily, to touch the crescent-shaped rib. Her fingers had barely brushed the carved surface when Theo saw her go rigid. Her breath caught. Her eyes lost focus, fixed on some middle distance where nothing was.

"What—" she started.

"Don't fight it," Theo said quietly. "It only hurts if you fight it."

For three heartbeats, Serah Vane heard the dead.

What came through wasn't words, exactly. It was a man's last afternoon, compressed into feeling: the smell of pine resin and woodsmoke, the weight of an axe handle worn smooth by his father's hands, a woman's laugh from inside a house he was building plank by plank. And underneath all of it, threaded through everything, a low grinding dread that had nothing to do with rockfalls or axes or wives laughing in unfinished houses.

*A shrine on the Thornfell road. A shrine that answered.*

He'd prayed there once, desperate, when the money ran out and the winter looked hungry. He'd asked for work. He'd gotten work—and woken the next morning unable to remember his mother's face. He'd told everyone who'd listen. Nobody believed him. Then the inquisitors came asking questions about shrines, and he'd learned very quickly which questions got honest men burned, and he'd stopped talking, and the dread had settled into him like a stone in a boot, and he'd carried it for eleven years until the mountain took him.

The vision released her. Serah rocked back on her heels, breathing hard, one hand pressed flat against the stone.

"That man," she said. "The one who owned that bone. He knew about the shrines. He *prayed* at one, and it answered, and it took something from him."

"A memory," Theo said. "His mother's face. He never got it back."

Serah looked up, and Theo watched something rearrange itself behind her eyes—the expression of a woman whose evidence had just grown a voice.

"I have documents," she said slowly. "Seized correspondence. Crown cipher-keys. Records of payments made to shrine-keepers who were actually Crown agents provocateurs, seeding the revival so the inquisitors would have heretics to burn. I've spent four months trying to prove the chain of custody. Trying to prove the shrines' revival isn't natural—that it's being *managed*. And every court in the capital would call my evidence forged, because I'm disgraced, because I'm nothing, because a warrantless woman's word is worth less than the paper she used to carry." Her voice had gone flat and precise, the tone of someone reciting a grievance they'd rehearsed too many times. "But a dead man's memory—"

"Can't be cross-examined," Theo finished. "Or dismissed. Or bribed."

"Can it be shown? To others?"

"With effort. With the right relic, the right reading, witnesses willing to swear they felt what they felt." Theo hesitated. "Why? What do you want with it?"

Serah stood. She sheathed her sword—a deliberate motion, performed slowly, the way you'd lower a shield between yourself and someone you'd decided, provisionally, not to kill.

"I want the truth entered into a record that survives," she said. "I want the men who seeded those shrines named in front of witnesses who can't be bought. I want justice done somewhere other than a pyre." A pause. "And I want to know why the shrines started answering in the first place, because my documents explain the *agents*, but they don't explain the *answers*."

Theo looked at this woman—this exiled, stubborn, dangerous instrument of a law that had chewed her up and spat her out—and felt the familiar instinct toward invisibility war with something newer.

Because here was the thing. Theo had heard the dead screaming about a hollow crown for weeks now. Had heard the shrines humming along every road in the Thornfell like struck bells that wouldn't stop ringing. Had spent those weeks telling no one, because telling people was how bone-carvers became pyres.

But this woman had already been burned. There was nothing left to take from her, and she was still standing in the road asking questions anyway.

"The shrines aren't being managed," Theo said. "Not entirely. Something under the mountains woke up, and it's listening through them. And the dead—" They stopped. Started again. "The dead have been saying things. About the king."

Serah went very still. "What things?"

"*The crown is hollow.*" Theo met her eyes. "Every relic I've carved this season. Every death I've attended. The dead are screaming it, Serah Vane. The crown is hollow, and something else is wearing it."

The wind moved through the pass. Somewhere below, snowmelt dripped its patient count.

Serah Vane, who had lost her title, her order, her reputation, and nearly her faith, looked at the bone-carver for a long moment. Then she laughed—one short, disbelieving bark with no humor in it at all.

"I rode three days to catch a cult courier," she said, "and instead I find the only witness in the mountains whose testimony comes pre-deceased." She extended a hand—sword-hand, callused, still trembling faintly from the dead man's memories. "Alliance. Provisional. You read your bones; I'll read my documents. We compare notes and see whose conspiracy is worse."

Theo took the hand. It was warm, and steady despite the tremor, and it pulled them both upright like an oath neither had quite sworn yet.

"Provisional," Theo agreed. "But if we're doing this, there's a relic you need to see. Back at my camp, half a day south. It's a century old. It belonged to a man who helped build something beneath the capital—and what he remembered about it will make your documents look like a child's letter home."

Serah's eyes narrowed. "Build what?"

Theo shouldered the pack and started down the pass, toward the lowlands, toward the capital they'd both been avoiding for different reasons.

"The Spire," they said. "Come on. The dead don't like to be kept waiting—and lately, they don't wait at all."
