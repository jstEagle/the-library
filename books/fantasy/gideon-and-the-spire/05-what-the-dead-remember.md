## 5. What the Dead Remember

The relic arrived wrapped in oilcloth and lies.

Marta the ferry-woman brought it up from Lowmark three days after Serah Vane rode out of my camp, and she brought it with the particular carefulness smugglers reserve for things that might be worth killing over. "Found it in a dead man's cache," she said, which was true in the way that all her statements were true—technically, narrowly, with the interesting parts amputated. "Cache was old. Older than the cache had any right to be."

She set it on my workbench like a woman setting down a sleeping snake.

It was a rib bone. Human, or close enough to human that the difference didn't matter to anyone but a priest. A century of dark had gone into it—not rot, because bone doesn't rot so much as forget itself slowly—and someone had carved it with a patience I recognized and envied. Fine lines. Geometric at first glance, then not: the geometry resolved into script as you turned it, an alphabet I'd never seen, looping and interlocking like knotted rope. And beneath the script, worked into the very grain, a shape repeated over and over down the length of the rib.

A tower. Or a spire. The same shape, again and again, smaller than a fingernail each time, descending toward the bone's narrow end like steps going down.

"Where," I said.

"That's the part I figured you'd want to hear." Marta leaned against the doorframe of my workshop, arms crossed, watching me the way people always watched me when they thought I didn't notice. "The cache was under the old waystation at Grayfell Pass. You know the one—the Crown sealed it eighty years back, said the road was unstable. Well. The road's fine. The waystation's fine. What's under the waystation is what got sealed." She shrugged. "Smugglers don't ask why the Crown hides things. We just dig."

"And the dead man?"

"What dead man?"

"The one whose cache it was. Bones don't sit in caches, Marta. Someone put this there. Someone died putting it there, or running from whatever made them put it there."

She looked at me for a long moment, and I watched her decide how much truth I could afford. It was a kindness, in its way. Most people just lied outright.

"There were bones in the cache," she admitted. "Old ones. And newer ones on top. Two sets. The older set was wearing robes that crumbled when we touched them. The newer set—" her jaw tightened— "the newer set still had boot leather. Inquisitor boots. Somebody went down there after the sealing, found the robed man, and killed him. Then somebody else came along later and did the same courtesy to the first somebody."

"A chain of silences."

"A ladder of them." She pushed off the doorframe. "Read it or don't. But Theo—if the Crown buried this deep enough to kill over twice, maybe think hard about who you tell what you find."

"I live alone in a cave and talk to dead people. My list is short."

"It wasn't short last week." She let that land, because it deserved to, and left.

I sat with the rib for a long while before I touched it.

Here is what most people don't understand about hearing the dead: it isn't like listening. There's no voice waiting politely for your attention. It's more like pressing your ear to a wall and finding that the wall has been dreaming this whole time, and now it knows you're there, and it wants to tell you everything. Every relic carries its carver—that's the first law of my trade, the one that pays for bread. A funeral piece holds the maker's last intentions, their grief, their love, sometimes their guilt. Carve well and honestly, and the dead speak through your hands for generations.

Carve something else, though—

The old texts my mother burned rather than let the inquisitors find them had a phrase for relics carved under compulsion, or in terror, or as testimony meant to survive the carver's death. *Witness-bones*, they called them. And they carried a warning I'd never had occasion to test: *a witness-bone does not remember. It insists.*

I lit every lamp I owned. I ate nothing—I've learned the hard way that reading on an empty stomach is safer, though no one has ever explained to me why, and I suspect the explanation would have been worse than hunger. Then I took the rib in both hands, laid it across my knees, and closed my eyes.

And the wall began to dream out loud.

* * *

His name was Aldous Ren, and he was an architect, and he was afraid in the specific, exhausted way of a man who has been afraid so long that fear has become a kind of furniture.

He was carving the rib by lamplight in a room I could feel around me—stone, vast, cold, the air tasting of iron and deep water. His hands were steady. That was the horror of it, right at the start: his hands were perfectly steady, because he had trained them to be, because a shaking hand makes a bad line, and bad lines in this place got people erased.

*I am Aldous Ren,* the bone said, in the wordless way bones say things, meaning poured directly into the marrow. *First architect of the Binding Works. If you are hearing this, then the works have held longer than I dared hope, and you are either a fool or a friend. Possibly both. The two overlap more than the College admits.*

Images came with the words, the way memories do when the dead share them—half-seen, half-felt, all of it true.

I saw the Spire being built.

Not the tower the capital knows—the pretty white needle above ground, the monument kings point to in paintings. I mean what was underneath. I saw the shaft going down past the water table, past the roots of the mountains themselves, down into a hollow in the world's bones where something had been sleeping since before sleep had a name. I saw the binding-stone at the heart of it, a single mass of black granite the size of a cathedral, quarried whole and lowered on chains, and I felt Aldous Ren's hands shake for the only time in his life as he watched it descend.

Because the stone wasn't empty. Nothing is ever empty. Stone remembers pressure, heat, the long patience of mountains—and this stone had been chosen precisely because it remembered *everything*. It was the densest archive in creation. And they were lowering it onto a god like a lid onto a well.

*We did not come to conquer it,* Aldous insisted, and I believed him, because the dead can lie but witness-bones cannot—it was carved into his hands whether he wanted it or not. *We came because it was waking, and a waking god does not distinguish between worship and noise. It would have taken us the way the sea takes a sandcastle. Not cruelly. Completely.*

*So we bound it. Nine hundred masons, forty years, and a rite so old that half of it predates language. And it worked. That is the part no one tells you about great crimes—they work. They work beautifully.*

Then the images turned, and the beauty curdled.

*A binding needs feeding.* His thoughts moved carefully here, the way a man walks on ice he has already heard crack. *Not blood—we tried blood, in the early years, and it only made it hungry in new directions. No. A god sleeps on stories. On souls. Not the soul entire—that would be murder, and murder echoes—but the memory of a life. The years of it. Give the prison enough human living to dream on, and it stays under. Starve it, and it starts reaching.*

*Every king since the Founding has known this. It is the actual crown duty, the one behind all the other ones. Once a year, on the Long Night, the king descends—or sends a surrogate, in the lazy reigns—and gives the Spire a tithe of memory. Volunteers, at first. Criminals condemned to die anyway, later, which everyone agreed was tidy. The condemned surrender their lives' remembering, and the god sleeps another year, and the kingdom goes on forgetting that it is standing on a mouth.*

I opened my eyes. My workshop was exactly as I'd left it—lamps, tools, the rain starting against the shutters—and none of it helped. I could still feel the cold of that shaft. I pressed the rib harder against my knees and went back under, because Aldous wasn't finished, and because some part of me had already done the arithmetic he was walking toward, and I needed to hear him say it.

*There is a flaw in the design. Mine. I will not pretend otherwise.* His steadiness cracked here, just once, a hairline fracture running through a century of control. *The tithe must be given freely. Willingly surrendered. Coerced memory is thin gruel; the god tolerates it the way a man tolerates drinking seawater. For four hundred years the kings understood this, and found volunteers, and paid them honor, and the arrangement held.*

*But volunteers are expensive. And kings are what they are.*

*In the reign of Maric the Second, the rite was changed. Quietly. The condemned stopped being asked. They were simply taken, their memories stripped by force, their bodies released to die of emptiness in the alms-houses where no one counts the vacant-eyed. And it worked—thin gruel is still gruel—for a while. But every forced tithe taught the Spire something. Every stolen year showed it the shape of the lock from the inside. It learned us, gentlemen of the College. It learned our greed, because we fed it ours.*

*And now it has learned patience too.*

The lamplight in Aldous's memory guttered. He was writing faster now, carving faster, the lines of the spire-shape descending the rib like a countdown.

*I am dying. They know I know, which is why I am doing this in a broom-closet off the ninth gallery instead of my rooms. Hear the last of it, whoever you are, and then decide quickly what kind of fool you are:*

*The tithe has failed. The god no longer waits to be fed. It has learned to feed itself—to reach up through the dreams of the desperate, the grieving, the praying, and take payment directly. Every shrine in the kingdom is a straw stuck into the same wound. Pray at one, and if your need is sharp enough, it will answer—and the answer costs you years of your own living, siphoned down the old channels to the thing below. The priests call it grace. The accountants of the College call it maintenance. It is neither. It is a god learning to eat without permission.*

*And the king—listen, the king—*

The memory shuddered. Boots, somewhere above. Lamplight swinging shadows across the broom-closet door. Aldous Ren wrapped the rib in oilcloth with hands that were, even now, perfectly steady, and slid it into a gap in the stones, and his final thought came through clear and cold as well-water:

*If the crown ever stops tithing entirely—if some king decides the god is safely asleep and saves himself the annual descent—then the starving ends and the hunting begins. It will eat upward. Through the shrines first. Then the prayers. Then the prayers' pray-ers. And at the top of that chain, closest to the Spire, fattest and easiest to reach—*

*—it will eat the king.*

*Watch the crown. If the man wearing it begins to feel hollow—if his decrees grow strange, if his eyes go distant, if the court whispers that he has become serene beyond reason—then it is already feeding, and you have less time than I did.*

Boots at the door. Steel, unhurried. Aldous Ren picked up his chisel one last time, and his last feeling—not a thought, just a feeling, the residue a century later of a man's final heartbeat—was not fear.

It was relief. He had gotten the message out. Whatever happened next happened to him alone.

The bone went quiet in my hands.

* * *

I don't know how long I sat there. The lamps had burned down to stubs; the rain had stopped; somewhere outside, real birds were making real morning sounds, and it all seemed obscene, like music played at a funeral by someone who hadn't been told.

I did the arithmetic again, awake this time, hoping the numbers would come out differently.

Every shrine along the Thornfell roads, humming again after a century of silence. A child's prayer answered in Lowmark, and a mother's memory taken as the price. The dead screaming a stranger's scream into a funeral relic: *the crown is hollow.* King Aldric, whom I had seen once from a distance at a border muster—a big, calm man, famously calm, serenely calm, the court chroniclers falling over themselves about his unshakable peace.

Serene beyond reason.

And the thing Aldous had described, the mechanism of it, the slow hydraulic horror of the whole arrangement: a kingdom built on top of a mouth, fed by consent for four hundred years, then fed by theft for four hundred more, and now—now that it had learned patience, now that it had learned to reach—feeding itself. Upward. Through every answering shrine. Through every desperate prayer. Through the king himself, year by year, memory by memory, until what remained on the throne was a shape with a crown on it and nothing underneath.

The dead hadn't been screaming randomly. They'd been screaming the news. The oldest news there is: something below is hungry, and the walls are thin.

I looked down at the rib in my hands—at the little spires descending its length like steps going down—and I understood, finally, why the Crown had sealed the waystation, why two men had died in the dark over this, why Marta had watched me with pity when she said my list used to be short.

This wasn't a secret. Secrets can be kept. This was a debt, a century overdue, and debts collect.

I should have run. Any sensible invisible man would have run—burned the rib, walked north, found some coast where nobody prayed at anything older than a lighthouse. I have survived the Thornfells my whole life by knowing exactly when to leave.

Instead I sat in my workshop in the gray morning light and did what I always do when the world gets too large: I reached for the dead. My pack sat by the door, full of the week's commissions—funeral pieces, small honest griefs, the work that keeps me human. I pulled out the nearest one, a locket-carved-from-a-rib for a shepherd's daughter, wanting—needing—one clean voice, one simple mourning, one reminder that the dead were mostly just people who missed things.

The locket spoke. And underneath the shepherd's daughter's gentle grief, faint as a stain showing through whitewash, something else said:

*The door is almost open.*

I dropped it. Scrambled back from my own workbench like a child from a spider. Every relic in the pack—every piece, finished and unfinished, every scrap of carved bone I owned—was humming now, a chord of whispers, dozens of voices braided into one sentence, patient as dripping water:

*The door is almost open. The door is almost open. The door is almost—*

Outside, far down the valley, a bell began to ring. Not the village chapel's bell—this was deeper, older, coming from the direction of the ruined shrine on the ridge, the one that had been silent for a hundred years and had started, three weeks ago, to hum.

It rang nine times. Then it stopped.

And in the silence after, every bone in my workshop said, together, in the flat voice of a fact already decided:

*He is coming to see the carver.*
