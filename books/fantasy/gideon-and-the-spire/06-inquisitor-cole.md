## 6. Inquisitor Cole

The smoke reached Lowmark before the riders did.

Theo smelled it from the ridge above the village—woodsmoke, yes, but under it something sweeter and fouler, like fat rendered in a hot pan, like old prayer-soaked timber finally giving up what it had soaked in for two hundred years. They stood on the road with their pack half-open, the relics inside humming against each other like struck tuning forks, and knew.

They had known since dawn, if they were honest. Since the crows lifted off the shrine roof all at once, without reason. Since the dead in the bones went quiet—not sleeping-quiet but listening-quiet, the way a room goes quiet when someone dangerous walks in.

Now the smoke confirmed it.

Theo did not go down into Lowmark. That was the first rule of invisibility: you do not walk toward the fire. They turned instead onto the drover's path that cut behind the village, the one that would let them see without being seen, because some part of them—the part that had spent thirty years pretending to be nothing—needed to look. Needed to know what their name on a warrant cost.

From the bend above the millpond, they could see the whole of it.

The shrine of Lowmark was not large. It never had been—a squat stone circle older than the kingdom, its standing stones worn smooth as river pebbles, its little iron bell green with age. Children left bread there. Farmers left milk. For generations it had answered nothing, done nothing, been nothing more than a place where people set down their hopes and picked them back up again unchanged. Theo had passed it a hundred times and heard only the faintest murmur from the stones, the way you hear wind through a wall.

Now it burned.

Not just the offerings—the stones themselves, ringed with pitch-soaked faggots stacked to twice a man's height, black flames climbing the weathered faces where old prayers were carved so deep no chisel could erase them. The fire didn't seem to want to take the stone; it clung anyway, fed by something that wasn't wood. And around the pyre, at a careful distance, stood the villagers of Lowmark in their market clothes, herded into a silent crescent by six men in the grey-and-gold of the Crown's Inquisition.

And in front of them, on a white horse that seemed offended by the mud, sat Inquisitor Cole.

Theo had never seen him. Everyone in the Thornfell trade roads had seen him, or knew someone who had. He was not what rumor promised. Rumor said tall, gaunt, terrible. In truth he was a broad, comfortable-looking man, pink-faced, with the build of a butcher and the mild eyes of a schoolmaster. He wore no armor. His voice, when he spoke, carried to the millpond without effort, warm as a hearth.

"—and I want to be clear," he was saying, "that no one here is accused. The Crown does not punish grief. The Crown does not punish hope." He smiled at the crescent of villagers, and several of them flinched. "What the Crown punishes is *traffic*. Someone has been moving relics through your valley. Someone has been teaching your dead to speak again. And speech, friends, is how rebellion begins."

He gestured, and one of his men brought forward a woman Theo recognized with a lurch: Maren Ostley, the shrine-keeper's daughter, who swept the stone circle every week and had once paid Theo three copper bits to carve a memorial for her father's dog. She was maybe nineteen. Her hands were tied in front of her with red cord—the color of testimony, the color of verdicts already written.

"She kept this shrine," Cole said gently. "She tended it. And when it began to answer prayers—when it began to *take payment*—she did not report it. She let a child lose her mother's face to a hungry stone." He leaned down from the saddle. "Did you know what it was doing, Maren?"

"I swept it," Maren said. Her voice was steady. Theo thought: *she has decided already.* "That's all I ever did. I swept it."

"That's all anyone ever does," Cole agreed sadly. "That's what everyone says, everywhere I go. I sweep. I carry. I carve. I only listened." He straightened. "But someone carved the bones that taught these stones to wake. Someone wrote the song. And until that someone stands in front of me, the Crown must assume the keeper of the instrument knows the player."

He did not make a speech about it. He did not even raise his voice. He simply nodded to a man with a torch, and the man touched it to the base of the pyre where Maren stood tied among the offerings, and the flames came up around her with a sound like a held breath released.

She did not scream. Theo would remember that longer than anything else about that day—that she did not scream, that she looked out over the heads of her neighbors toward the mountains, toward the passes, toward the drover's path where a bone-carver stood frozen with a pack full of singing dead—and she pressed her lips together and burned in silence, as if she knew exactly who was watching and wanted to spare them the sound.

The dead in Theo's pack screamed for her.

All of them at once, every relic, every carved remnant, a chord of anguish that shot up Theo's arms and locked their jaw shut so they wouldn't answer aloud. *She swept us,* the stones' dead wailed through the bones. *She swept us every week.* And underneath, fainter, from the burning shrine itself, something vast and drowsy turning over in its sleep, tasting the smoke, murmuring a word Theo couldn't catch but felt in their teeth:

*...closer...*

Theo ran.

* * *

They ran the drover's path until it became the shepherd's stair, until the stair became goat track, until the village was a smudge of black air behind them and their lungs burned like the shrine had burned. Then they stopped in a hollow of scree, hands on knees, and made themselves think.

Warrants. There would be warrants. Cole did nothing without paper; that was his reputation too—he was the Inquisition's clerk-turned-butcher, a man who believed the world was a ledger and every soul a line item. By nightfall every waystation, toll house, and smuggler's cache from Thornfell to the capital would carry the same notice: *THE BONE-WITCH. Carves false relics. Wakes forbidden shrines. Answers to no name. Reward sufficient to retire on.*

Reward sufficient to retire on. That was the knife of it. Not a number—a promise. Cole understood poverty better than he understood mercy. He hadn't posted a price; he'd posted an escape, and every hungry soul on the mountain roads would now be hunting Theo with the fervor of people hunting their own salvation.

Which meant the network was dead. Which meant—

"No," Theo said aloud, to the scree, to the wind. "No, we get ahead of it."

Because the network wasn't just routes and caches. It was people. It was Hesk with his lame leg who ran salt over the Col. It was the twins at Graywater ferry who never asked what was in the oilcloth. It was old Tam Voss, seventy years a courier, who had taught Theo which birds went quiet before soldiers and which went quiet before worse things. Thirty years invisible, and invisibility, Theo realized with a nausea that had nothing to do with the climb, had never actually been free. It had been paid for, all along, in other people's willingness to not see. And now the Crown was offering those people a fortune to see.

Theo unslung the pack and took out the bones.

There were eleven relics in the oilcloth—no, ten, and the eleventh wrapped separately, the screaming stranger's finger-bone from the smuggler's funeral, the one that had started everything. Theo set it apart and addressed the others the way they'd learned to address the dead: palm flat, breath slow, attention open like a door.

"I need runners," they said. "I need warnings carried faster than paper. Who can move tonight?"

The answers came up through their hand like cold water rising. Hesk was at the Col, three days gone, unreachable. The twins were near enough—one flicker of assent, sharp as a snapped twig. Tam Voss was closest of all, camped at the Drover's Rest inn below the pass, and his reply came with the particular dry warmth that was somehow entirely him even through bone: *Boy, I've been waiting thirty years for you to ask me to do something reckless.*

"Tam—" Theo began, and didn't know how to finish it. *Stay away from me. I'm worth a fortune now. You're old.* None of it would matter to him. It hadn't mattered when his wife died and he'd asked Theo to carve her knucklebone so he could keep hearing her argue about his smoking. Some debts don't run in the direction you'd expect.

*Go to the Waymeet cave,* Tam's dead voice said. *Above the falls. You know it. Send your warnings from there and stay off the roads yourself. I'll rouse the twins and Hesk's boy. We'll scatter the caches before the greycoats sniff them out.*

"It's too dangerous—"

*Everything worth doing is,* said Tam Voss, who had once walked forty miles through a blizzard carrying a dead woman's femur because Theo had been too sick with fever to travel. *Now stop wasting my afterlife and write your letters.*

* * *

The Waymeet cave was a smuggler's institution: a hollow behind the falls where messages could be left in a split in the rock and retrieved by anyone who knew the knock. Theo reached it at dusk, soaked in spray, and by lantern-stub wrote four notes in the cramped cipher Tam had invented decades ago—*greycoats south, burn nothing, bury deep, lie low till frost*—and sealed them in wax with the sign of the low road, a simple horizontal line. No name. Never a name.

Then they sat against the cold stone and listened to the water fall and tried not to do arithmetic.

One death already. Maren Ostley, who had swept the stones. Whose only crime, whose *actual* crime in the eyes of the ledger, was knowing Theo's face well enough to have paid them three coppers for a dog's memorial. If Theo had never taken the commission—if Theo had stayed invisible down to the smallest transaction—would Cole have found a thread to pull? Or would he have pulled some other thread, because the shrines were waking whether Theo carved or not, because the thing beneath the capital was feeding itself now and the waking was bigger than any one carver?

Both things were true. Theo knew both things were true. And neither one made the smoke smell any sweeter.

"You're doing the thing," they said to themselves, in their mother's accent, the way she used to say it. "You're doing the thing where you make it your fault so it feels like something you can carry. Fault isn't the same as weight, little bird."

But it was, though. That was the lesson of the Thornfell, the lesson nobody wrote down: fault and weight weighed exactly the same in the dark.

The dead stirred in the pack. Not the strangers—the familiar ones, the ones Theo had carried for years, the smuggler Brannic whose finger-bone had screamed *the crown is hollow*, and old Sefa, and the nameless child whose relic Theo had never managed to put down. They pressed close against the walls of their bone houses, and what they sent up wasn't words but a feeling, the way a hand rests on a shoulder without gripping. *We're here. Keep moving.*

So Theo kept moving.

* * *

They were two miles north of the falls, high on the sheep paths where the stars came down close enough to bite, when the bells started.

Not church bells. The Lowmark shrine bell—green iron, two hundred years silent—ringing. Ringing from a shrine that was ash. Ringing in a rhythm Theo's body understood before their mind caught up: alarm, alarm, alarm, the old signal the mountain folk had used before the Crown banned signal fires, three strikes and a rest, three strikes and a rest.

The dead in the pack went rigid.

*Tam,* said every relic at once, and Theo was already running, sliding down scree in the dark, taking the switchbacks at a speed that should have broken an ankle, because the bell meant the network's oldest code and the network's oldest code meant *they've taken one of ours.*

The Drover's Rest sat in the pass mouth like a barnacle on a whale's back, all its windows lit, horses in the yard—too many horses, grey caparisons, the Inquisition's colors gone dull in the lamplight. Theo flattened into the gorse on the slope above and watched through the gaps.

Two greycoats dragged a figure out the inn door. Old. Lame in the left leg—no, not Hesk, wrong build, thinner, taller, and then the lantern swung and Theo saw the white beard and the crooked smile even in pain, and the world went very quiet, quieter than the silence that was coming for them all.

Tam Voss. Caught with one of Theo's letters still in his fist. Seventy years a courier, and he'd gone down holding the line.

An officer Theo couldn't see said something. Tam laughed—Theo heard it clear across the yard, that wheezing, unrepentant laugh, the laugh he'd laughed at blizzards and bailiffs and death itself—and said something back that made one of the greycoats step away from him sharply, superstitious.

Then Inquisitor Cole came out of the inn.

Even at distance, even in bad light, Theo felt him the way you feel a change in pressure before a storm. He walked to Tam unhurried. He bent and spoke quietly, at length, the schoolmaster's patience, the butcher's hands folded behind his back. Whatever he offered, Tam's answer was short.

Cole sighed—the sigh of a man disappointed in a student—and nodded, and the greycoat's blade did its work in the lantern light, quick and businesslike, and Tam Voss died in the mud of the yard he'd crossed ten thousand times, and the last thing Theo's gift brought them across that dark distance was not fear and not pain but a single transmitted thought, aimed like an arrow up the slope, straight into the bones in the pack, straight into the carver who had made them:

*Run, boy. Don't you dare watch. Run.*

Theo ran.

They ran until the pass was gone, until the stars wheeled, until their legs gave out in a hollow full of bracken and they lay gasping with the pack clutched to their chest while the dead keened softly for the man who had carried them, and somewhere below, faint and patient, the green iron bell rang on—three strikes and a rest, three strikes and a rest—as if the shrine's ghost were counting out the cost, keeping the ledger now, marking each name.

Maren Ostley. Tam Voss.

Invisibility had a body count, and Theo Iversen knew both numbers by heart.

Toward dawn, wrung hollow, they dug out the architect's fragment Serah Vane had helped them win—the scrap of ancient bone that pointed, in lines no living cartographer drew, down and east, toward the capital, toward the Spire. Toward the thing that was wearing King Aldric's crown and drinking the kingdom through a straw.

The dead had stopped whispering *the crown is hollow*. Now, all ten of them together, in a voice like wind through a keyhole, they whispered something new:

*It knows your name now, carver. It told the fire. Come find out why.*

Theo looked east, where the sky was going the color of ash, and shouldered the pack, and started walking—not away from the hunt anymore, but into the teeth of it.
