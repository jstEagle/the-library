## 7. The Cult of the Open Door

The invitation came on a Thursday, tucked into the hollow of a goose egg.

Theo found it at the bottom of their pack, which was the first wrong thing. The second was the egg itself—unbroken, cold as river stone, though Theo had walked all day through the high passes where nothing laid anything. They turned it in the firelight, turning it and turning it, and the dead in Theo's bones said nothing at all, which was the third wrong thing. The dead always said something. Bone remembered. But this egg had no bone in it, no history, no ghost humming in the shell like a struck bell. It was just an object, mute and patient, waiting to be opened the way a trap waits.

Inside: a slip of vellum, soft as skin, with seven words inked in brown so dark it might have been blood dried slow.

*The door is open. Come home, hearer.*

Theo read it four times. Then they fed it to the fire and watched it curl, and told themselves the shaking in their hands was only the cold.

They did not go home. That was the point of the mountains—you could walk away from any road, any village, any face that had started looking too long. Cole's warrants were nailed to every post from Lowmark to the river gorges now, *THE BONE-WITCH*, and beneath the word a woodcut of a figure so generic it could have been anyone, which meant it could have been everyone, which meant Theo had stopped sleeping in the same place twice. The smugglers who'd carried them for years had scattered like startled birds. Marta was dead. Theo carried her name the way they carried everything else: quietly, constantly, like a stone sewn into the lining.

So when the second egg appeared—inside their boot, inside Serah's camp, three days later, while Serah stood watch twenty feet away with her sword across her knees—the shaking got worse.

"They're mocking you," Serah said, when Theo showed her. She held the vellum up to the light as if she could read the hand behind it. "Or testing you. Cults love a test."

"You know what this is?"

"I know what it smells like." She dropped it into the fire without ceremony. "Open Door. My old commander called them a rumor with candles. Supposed to have died out forty years ago." Her jaw tightened. "Supposed to."

"And now they're leaving me mail in my boots."

"Now they're telling you they can reach you anywhere." Serah looked at Theo across the fire, and her voice went flat and careful, the way it did when she was about to say something true. "That's not an invitation, Iversen. That's a leash being measured."

But the third egg came anyway, and the fourth, and by the sixth Theo had stopped burning them, because each one carried something new. A pressed sprig of rowan from the tree outside the house where Theo was born. A child's milk tooth. Once, unbearably, a sliver of carved bone Theo recognized with a lurch that went through them like a blade: their own work, their first relic ever, cut for a girl named Annet who had died of fever eleven years ago in a village two hundred miles south. Theo had buried that relic with her. Theo had *watched* it go into the ground.

The vellum inside that one said only: *We keep what is given.*

Serah wanted to run. Theo understood why. Running had kept them alive for thirty years, invisible, unremarkable, a carver of funeral trinkets passing through lives like weather. But invisibility was over—that had ended the day the shrines woke and the dead began screaming about a hollow crown—and a person who cannot be invisible must choose between hiding and standing somewhere on purpose.

On the ninth day, Theo chose.

"The map says there's a valley past the Grey Stair," they said. "Old terraces. Nobody farms them anymore because the soil's wrong. Serah—I'm going."

"Then I'm going."

"No."

"Yes." She said it the way she said most things, like a door closing. "You don't get to walk into a cult alone because you're trying to protect me. I've been exiled, Iversen. Burned my own life down already. There's very little left you can take from me, and none of it is yours to guard." She paused, and something moved under the flatness. "Besides. You talk to the dead. Somebody should be there who talks back to the living."

* * *

The valley opened below them near dusk, green and impossible.

Everything around it was grey—the scree slopes, the dead pines, the frost-bitten heather of the high Thornfell—but the valley floor lay lush as lowland pasture, grass to the knee, wildflowers in drifts of white and gold, and the air smelled of honey and warm bread. Terraced walls climbed the slopes in old green steps, and among them moved people. Dozens. Maybe a hundred. Robed in undyed wool, faces open, unhurried, carrying baskets of late harvest as if winter were a rumor from another country.

"It's autumn," Serah said slowly. "It's been frost here for a month."

"I know."

"This valley shouldn't exist."

"I know, Serah."

At the valley's mouth stood a shrine—one of the old ones, pre-Crown, a waist-high cairn of stacked stones worn smooth by centuries of weather—and it was *humming*. Theo felt it before they saw it, a vibration in the teeth, in the marrow, in the fine bones of the inner ear where the dead lived when they lived anywhere. Every shrine since Thornfell had woken with that hum, faint and wrong, like a voice heard through a wall. This one didn't hum.

This one *sang*. Clear and sweet and wordless, a melody with no source, and the people passing it touched the stones as they went by the way you'd touch a loved one's shoulder.

A woman met them on the path before they'd taken ten steps into the green. She was perhaps sixty, silver-haired, plain-faced, with the kind of eyes that made you feel catalogued. She wore the same undyed wool as the others, and her hands were a farmer's hands, cracked and capable, and she smiled at Theo with such unguarded warmth that Theo's whole body flinched.

"Hearer," she said. "You came."

"Theo," Theo said. "My name is Theo."

"Of course it is." She said it gently, as if correcting nothing at all. "I am Cassia. We've been expecting you since the first shrine woke. Longer, actually. Since the night a smuggler died screaming about a hollow crown, and a bone-carver in the Thornfell heard a stranger's grief through someone else's relic." Her smile deepened. "Word travels strangely along the old roads. The dead are terrible gossips."

Behind Theo, Serah's hand had gone to her sword. Cassia looked at her without fear, without even much interest.

"Justiciar Vane. Or former Justiciar Vane. You burned the letter, by the way. The pardon. We know about that too—we know almost everything that happens within a day's ride of a waking shrine, and there are more waking every week." She spread her hands, encompassing the impossible valley, the singing stones, the harvest that defied the calendar. "You're both tired. You're both hunted. Come eat. Come sleep under a roof that the Crown doesn't know exists. Ask your questions. Leave whenever you like." A beat, and her warmth acquired an edge so fine it was almost invisible. "Though I'll say plainly: no one has ever wanted to leave."

* * *

Dinner was bread and stew and honey-cakes, served at long trestle tables in a hall built of living trees—branches trained and woven over decades into walls and roof, leaves still growing through the rafters. Theo sat among the faithful and tried to eat and mostly failed. Not because the food was bad—it was the best thing Theo had tasted in a year—but because of the children.

There were children here. Born in the valley, maybe raised in it, running between the tables, laughing, and every one of them healthy, rosy, *fed* in some way that had nothing to do with stew. And every time one of them passed close, Theo's gift flared like a struck match. Not words. Not memories. Just—a pressure. A presence underneath the small warm bodies, vast and slow and patient, like a whale surfacing beneath a swimmer.

Something was in this valley. Something enormous. And it liked the children.

"Theo." Cassia settled across from them, pressing a cup of cider into their hands. "You're listening. Good. That's what you're for." She said it without cruelty, the way you'd say *that's what hammers are for*. "Ask me what you're actually wondering."

"What's under the valley?"

"A threshold." She drank, unhurried. "Not the Spire—that's far away, under the capital, and we'll come to it. Here there's only a crack, a seam where the old gods still press close to the world. When the Crown sealed the Spire a thousand years ago, it didn't seal just one god. It sealed the *doorway*, and everything divine beyond it went deaf and dumb and starving. Your whole life, Theo, humanity has been praying into a locked room." Her eyes caught the lamplight. "We're opening the room."

"The shrines. The answered prayers."

"First light through the crack. Yes, they answer now. Yes, they take payment—memory, mostly; divinity has never learned to ask politely. The mother in Lowmark who forgot her daughter's name paid a fair price for a miracle, whatever the inquisitors say." Cassia leaned forward. "But a crack lets through whispers. A door lets through *gods*. And when the Spire's binding breaks—when the last lock fails—everything changes. No more abandonment. No more prayers screamed into silence. The old gods return, the world remakes itself according to older and kinder laws, and death—" she paused, savoring it, watching Theo's face with those cataloguing eyes, "—death becomes negotiable."

The hall seemed to quiet around the word. Even the children stilled.

"You believe that," Theo said.

"I've given my life to believing it. As you gave yours to hearing the dead." Cassia reached out and, before Theo could withdraw, took their hand—turned it palm-up, examined the calluses, the old knife scars, the fine tremor that never fully left. "Do you know what you are, hearer? Do you know why the gift landed on you and not on the thousand other children born that year? Because you're not just sensitive to the dead. You're a *keyhole*. A shape the old gods cut into the world before they were sealed, so that someday, when the door needed opening, there would be a hand that fit." Her thumb traced the largest scar, feather-light. "Every hearer for a thousand years has been a failed attempt. Too weak. Too frightened. Too dead too soon. But you—" she smiled, and for one flickering moment the warmth fell away entirely and Theo saw what lived underneath it, something old and hungry and *attentive*, "—you heard a stranger scream through a stranger's bone. You're the strongest keyhole born since the sealing. Mother Cassia knows her scripture, Theo. And her scripture says you're the last one we'll ever need."

Theo pulled their hand back. The hall's warmth suddenly felt like a mouth.

"That's not sanctuary," they said. "That's ownership."

"It's *purpose*." For the first time, Cassia's voice sharpened, and around the hall the faithful went still, watching, and Theo realized with a cold drop that this was doctrine, this was liturgy, this was the sermon and they were standing in the pulpit. "You think you have purpose out there? Carving trinkets for peasants, running from warrants, burying your gift so deep you nearly drowned in your own silence? We offer you a home, hearer. A family. A *reason*. All we ask is what you were made for."

"All I was made for," Theo said quietly, "was carving names into bone so the grieving could sleep. That's the whole of it. That's enough."

Cassia studied them for a long moment. Then the warmth returned, seamless, total, as if it had never left. "Sleep on it," she said. "Everyone deserves one night of doubt before faith."

* * *

They gave Theo a cell in the tree-hall, small and clean, with a bed of woven branches and a window looking out on the singing shrine. Serah took the room next door and wedged a chair under the door handle, which fooled no one but satisfied her.

Theo didn't sleep. They lay in the dark and listened.

The dead were loud here—not the dead of the valley, which had none, which was itself a horror Theo only registered at midnight (a hundred people, generations of harvests, and not one grave, not one ghost, not one echo; where did these people *go*?)—but the dead beyond the valley, the great slow tide of them Theo had carried all their life. And here, tonight, for the first time, the tide sounded different. Closer. Strained. Like voices calling through a door from the other side, palms pressed flat against wood.

And beneath them, deeper, the vast patient pressure turned over in its sleep, and something that was not quite a voice moved against the inside of Theo's skull like a thumb against silk:

*Soon, little keyhole. Soon.*

Theo sat up in the dark, heart slamming, and made their decision the way they made all their decisions: fast, terrified, and final.

Because here was the thing Cassia hadn't counted on, the flaw in her perfect scripture. Theo had spent their whole life hearing the dead, and the dead did not lie—they couldn't; memory was memory—but the *living* lied constantly, beautifully, and Theo had learned to hear lies the way musicians heard false notes. And every warm word Cassia had spoken today had been true except one. One small phrase, offered early, meant to put them at ease:

*Leave whenever you like.*

No one had left. She'd said it herself, smiling. And the valley's soil grew things it shouldn't, and the valley held no graves, and the children glowed with borrowed divinity, and the door in the deep was already whispering *soon*—

This wasn't a home that wanted nothing. This was a home that wanted *everything*, and had simply learned to ask nicely.

Near dawn, Theo slipped from the cell. In the hall below, Cassia's map-chest sat where she'd left it, unlocked, unguarded—because who would steal from paradise? Inside, beneath rolls of valley charts Theo didn't need, lay what Theo did: a survey of the capital's undercrofts, drawn in a precise architect's hand centuries dead, with the Spire marked at its heart and the binding-wards annotated in a cipher Theo couldn't read yet but recognized the *shape* of, the way you recognize a language you studied once, long ago.

Theo took it. Left, in its place, the only payment they had: the sliver of Annet's relic, their first work, the bone they'd watched buried eleven years ago.

*We keep what is given,* the notes had said.

Then let them keep that, and choke on it.

Theo woke Serah with a hand over her mouth, pressed the map into her hands, and pointed at the window, where the first grey light was touching the singing shrine. Below on the terraces, the faithful were already rising for morning prayer—and every one of them, every single one, had stopped mid-motion and turned their faces toward the tree-hall.

Toward Theo's window.

Cassia stood among them, silver hair bright in the dawn, and she was not angry. She was smiling, wide and serene, and her lips moved, and though a hundred yards of morning air lay between them, Theo heard her perfectly, heard her the way they heard the dead:

*"Run, little keyhole. Doors are for opening either way."*
