## 8. Duty's Weight

The letter came with the morning post, which was itself a miracle of sorts. Nothing official had found Serah Vane in four months—not since she'd ridden out of the capital with her seal broken and her name struck from the rolls of justiciars. Official things did not follow disgraced women into the Thornfell.

She knew what it was before she broke the wax. She knew by the weight of it, by the crispness of the vellum, by the way the courier—a boy of maybe fifteen with a Crown badge polished too bright for a mountain road—wouldn't meet her eyes. Warrants were thin things, single sheets. Pardons came thick as prayer books, all their conditions folded inside like knives in cloth.

She read it standing in the yard of the waystation inn, because she refused to sit down for it. *By order of the Lord Chancellor, acting under the authority of His Majesty King Aldric the Second of that name—*

Aldric's name. Signed with his seal. She looked at it a long moment, the great crowned A pressed into red wax, and thought: *you are not him.* She had known Aldric when she was a cadet, had stood honor guard at his coronation, had heard him speak at length about justice being the spine of a kingdom. The man who signed this letter was a shape wearing his memory.

*—the charges of insubordination and obstruction having been reviewed, and the circumstances of your dismissal reconsidered—*

Reconsidered. As if they hadn't dragged her before the tribunal in full view of the court, as if Magister Corven hadn't said the words *you have brought shame upon the office* in a voice pitched to carry to the galleries. As if she hadn't walked out of those doors knowing that every eye on her back was writing the ending of her story for her: *the justiciar who loved an innocent more than her oath.*

They had wanted her to burn Maren Cottle. That was the whole of it, stripped of its ceremony. Maren Cottle, shrine-keeper, sixty years old, who had tended a roadside altar for forty years and never asked anything of anyone—and when the inquisitors decided the old shrines were feeding sedition, Maren had been first on the list because she was nearest and weakest and would not fight. Serah had examined the evidence herself, all three days of it, and found it was ash. Fabricated testimony, a witness bought with remission of debts, an accusation built on nothing but fear of things the Crown no longer controlled.

She had said so. Loudly. In writing. With her seal.

And now here was the letter, thick as a psalter, offering it all back.

*—full restoration of office, rank, and privileges, contingent upon the following conditions:*

There were five. She counted them twice, because her hands were not quite steady and she wanted to be sure she wasn't inventing the worst one.

First: return to the capital within thirty days.

Second: submit to a hearing of reinstatement before the Lord Chancellor's bench.

Third: make formal public renunciation of her prior statements regarding shrine-keepers and the old cults.

Fourth: provide testimony regarding the fugitive known as the bone-witch—her whereabouts, methods, associates, and crimes.

Fifth: *participate actively in the apprehension of said fugitive.*

Denounce Theo Iversen. Hunt them. Hand them over to Cole's people, who burned shrines and posted warrants for healers, whose fires Serah could smell from the pass two valleys east when the wind turned wrong.

She stood in the yard a long time. The courier shifted his weight. Somewhere behind the inn, the stableboy was cursing at a mule in language that would have gotten him whipped in the capital barracks, and the ordinariness of it made the letter feel even less real, like a stage play performed in a market square.

"Does it require an answer?" she asked.

"No, my lady. Justiciar." He caught himself, went pale. "I mean—I don't—it says—"

"I know what it says." She folded the letter along its original creases, precisely, the way she'd been taught to fold warrants twenty years ago. "Tell them the answer is coming."

He rode off fast, glad to be gone from her.

* * *

She didn't burn it that day. That was the thing she would remember afterward, the detail that shamed her most: she didn't burn it. She carried it in her saddlebag for two days while she tracked Theo's trail through the high passes, reading the same three smuggler-marks on the waystones, and every night at camp she took it out and read it again by firelight, and told herself she was studying it the way she'd study any document—for weaknesses, for lies, for the truth between its lines.

But there was no truth between its lines. It was exactly what it appeared to be: everything she had lost, offered back to her, priced at one bone-carver's life.

On the second night she made camp in the ruins of an old watchtower, one of the pre-Crown structures that dotted the Thornfell like knucklebones scattered by a giant. The walls still held the faint carvings of the old faith, worn nearly smooth—she traced one without thinking, a spiral, a door, a hand opening. Theo would know what it meant. Theo knew what all of it meant, the whole buried grammar of the mountains, and spoke it fluently to the dead and couldn't lie to save their life.

That was the trouble. That was the whole rotten heart of her trouble, and she set the letter on her knee and admitted it to the fire:

She believed them.

Four months ago she had ambushed a stranger on a smuggler's pass with a blade at their throat, and everything she'd expected—cult courier, fanatic, liar—had dissolved under contact with the actual person. Theo Iversen, who apologized to the bones in their pack when they jostled them. Who wept, once, quietly, after carrying a dead child's last words to a mother in a valley village, and then ate dinner as if nothing had happened. Who had shown her, without hesitation, without any apparent awareness of how damning it was, relic after relic proving that the Crown's own Spire was eating its king alive—that the thing signing pardons in Aldric's name was not Aldric, and had not been for some time.

She had spent her career weighing evidence. She had never once had evidence this clean. And she had never once wanted evidence to be dirty this badly.

Because if the letter was honest—if the Crown was salvageable, if the corruption was one chancellor's ambition rather than the state itself—then she could go home. She could have back the only thing she had ever wanted, which was not rank, not title, not the rooms in the Justiciar's Hall with their good light for reading, but the work. The daily, grinding, unglamorous work of making sure power answered for what it did. She had been good at it. She had been *made* for it. Twenty years, and they had carved her into a tool with one edge, and now the hand that held her had offered to take her back if she agreed to cut the one innocent person she'd met since her fall.

She rehearsed it. That was the worst part, the part she'd confess to no one, the part that sat in her chest like swallowed stone. Lying awake in the watchtower with the fire down to coals, she rehearsed what she would say. Not the denunciation—the other speech, the one she'd give at the hearing, the clever one, the one where she accepted restoration and then worked from inside to bring Cole down, to expose the fabricated evidence, to clear Maren Cottle's name properly, to fix it all from within. *I accept, with reservations.* She could see the Chancellor's face. She could feel the weight of the seal coming back to her hand. She could walk those halls again, and this time she'd have standing, she'd have access, she'd have—

She'd have to deliver Theo to Cole to get through the door.

Every version of the speech ended there. She kept trying to build a version that didn't, some architecture of words where she took the pardon and shielded Theo and outmaneuvered everyone, and every version collapsed under examination, because she had spent twenty years learning how oaths worked and she knew a poisoned condition when she read one. The fifth clause wasn't a formality. It was the doorframe. You did not pass through it without leaving what you loved on the threshold.

*Loved.*

The word arrived quietly, the way true things do, and she sat up straight by the dead fire as if someone had spoken it aloud.

Not admired. Not trusted—though she did, against every professional instinct, trust them completely; the bone-carver who could hear lies in the memories of the dead was the least capable liar she had ever met. Not indebted to—she'd stopped keeping that ledger weeks ago. Loved. Present tense. The kind of love that wasn't a feeling but a direction, a thing her whole attention had been turning toward for months without her permission, the way water finds the low ground.

She thought of dawn in the architect's tomb, holding Theo's hands while the madness of a dead man tried to pour itself into them, talking them back across the dark—*your name is Theo Iversen, you carve bone, you hate the taste of goat cheese, come back, come back*—and the moment their eyes had focused on hers and they'd said, hoarse and astonished, *"You stayed."* As if staying were remarkable. As if everyone else in their life had left, and she had simply failed to notice she was supposed to.

She thought of the way Theo listened to the dead the way other people prayed—helplessly, constantly, with their whole self—and understood that she had fallen for a person who was already married to ghosts, and had somehow not minded, because watching them carry the dead gently was the most holy thing she had ever seen a living person do.

And the Crown wanted her to sell them for a badge.

Serah Vane got up, walked to the tower's broken parapet, and stood looking north toward the capital, a smudge of woodsmoke haze on the far horizon. Somewhere under that city was the Spire, and under the Spire was a god, and on the throne above both sat a hollow thing signing letters in a dead man's hand. The institution she had given her life to was not merely corrupted. It was a limb of the thing in the deep place. Every warrant Cole burned a shrine with, every decree the king signed, all of it fed downward into the dark.

You could not reform a body whose soul had been eaten. She had spent months refusing to know that. The letter had finally made her know it.

She went back down, took the letter from her knee, and read it one last time—all five clauses, the Chancellor's flourish, the crowned A in red wax.

"You're not him," she told the signature. "And I'm done pretending you are."

She fed it to the coals corner by corner, holding each fold until it caught, watching the Chancellor's careful language curl and blacken. The wax bubbled last, hissing, the crowned A softening, losing its shape, becoming just red.

It should have felt like freedom. It mostly did. But underneath, honest as a scar: grief. Because the letter had been the last door, and she had just watched it close, and a woman can grieve even a house that was burning her alive.

* * *

Three days later, at a smuggler waystation called the Drowned Bell, she found Theo arguing with a dead man.

Not loudly. Theo never argued loudly. They sat in the corner with a relic cupped in both hands—a rib, from the look of it, old and yellowed—and their lips moved slightly, and their expression was the particular patient frustration of someone explaining something for the third time. Serah ordered tea she wouldn't drink and sat down across from them and waited, because interrupting Theo mid-hearing was like shaking a diver underwater.

Eventually Theo surfaced. Their eyes found her, and did the thing they always did—brightened first, guarded second, the order of it telling her everything.

"You're back early," they said. "The trail went cold?"

"The trail went fine." She set her pack on the table. "I got a letter."

Something in her face must have shown, because Theo went very still, the way they did when listening to something no one else could hear. "Bad news?"

"It was an offer. Restoration. Full pardon." She kept her voice flat, report-flat, the tone that carried no information. "Five conditions. The last one was you."

Theo absorbed that. She watched them absorb it—watched their hands tighten around the empty teacup, watched them do the arithmetic she'd already done, arriving at the sum she'd already arrived at. When they spoke, their voice was carefully light. "And you're telling me this because...?"

"Because you deserve to hear it from me and not from an inquisitor's blade." She leaned forward. "And because I want you to know what I did with it."

"What did you do with it?"

"I burned it."

Theo let out a breath that seemed to have been held for days. Then, because they were who they were: "You should have kept it."

"Don't."

"You should have. Serah—" They glanced around the near-empty room, lowered their voice. "You gave up everything once already. For Maren Cottle, for strangers, for people who never thanked you. I've been waiting months for you to realize you don't owe me anything, and now the Crown offers you a clean way out and you burn it? For me? You barely know me. Four months. You knew the law for twenty years."

"I knew the law," Serah said, "and the law turned out to be a mask nailed to a face that isn't a face anymore. I've met the law's new master, Theo. It signs letters in a dead king's hand." She reached across the table and, deliberately, turned their teacup so the handle faced them, a small stupid courtesy, an anchor. "Twenty years I served something that was already hollow. I'm not going to spend the rest of my life apologizing for choosing what's actually alive."

Theo stared at her. And then, quietly, with the terrible directness they never seemed to know they possessed: "You rehearsed it. Before you burned it. What you'd say to them."

It wasn't a question. It was worse than a question. Serah felt her composure crack down the middle like river ice. "How could you possibly—"

"You're doing it right now. Your lips move when you're composing. You did it at the tomb, before you woke me. You do it when you're drafting reports in your head." Theo's voice gentled. "What did you say? In the rehearsal."

"That I'd take the pardon and fix everything from inside." She heard herself say it and marveled at her own mouth. "That I'd bring Cole down. Clear Maren's name. That there was a version where nobody had to lose."

"And?"

"And every version ended with handing you to them." She said it plainly, because Theo could hear a lie in the living if she let one stand, and she refused—refused—to let one stand between them. "So I burned the speech with the letter. All of it. There's no inside anymore, Theo. There's just the Spire, and what's under it, and us."

Theo was quiet a long moment. Around them the Drowned Bell creaked and muttered, the innkeeper wiped down tables that were already clean, and somewhere upstairs a floorboard announced a guest who was probably not a guest.

Then Theo turned their cup, absently, the handle away from them again, and said: "Queen Isolde."

Serah blinked. "What?"

"One of the dead told me. An old chambermaid, died two winters back, still walks the palace halls in her memories." Theo's eyes had gone distant, focused on something behind the world. "She says the queen knows. About the king. She's known for a year. She's been holding the kingdom together with her own hands while the thing up there signs decrees." Their gaze snapped back to Serah, sharp with purpose. "If we're going to reach the Spire, we'll need help from inside the capital. And there's exactly one person in that city with reason to want the truth out and the power to open a door."

Serah sat back slowly, and felt—beneath the dread, beneath the enormity of what they were proposing—the strangest sensation settle over her, warm and unwelcome and unmistakable.

Relief. The relief of having a duty again. Not the Crown's duty. Hers.

"All right," she said, and was surprised at how steady it came out. "The Queen Who Wasn't. Let's go find out what she knows."

Theo smiled—small, tired, real—and began wrapping the rib back in its cloth.

Outside, far to the south, thunder rolled over the capital, though the sky above the mountains was clear.
