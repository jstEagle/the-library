## 10. Bargains in Bone

The relic sat on the table between them like a thing that knew it was being watched.

It was a knucklebone—no, Theo corrected themselves, turning it in the lamplight. A finger bone, the second joint of a right hand, carved so finely the whorls looked like frost ferns. Along its length ran lines of script no living language used, each character cut shallow and precise as a surgeon's stroke. The architect's work. The man who had built a cage for a god and then, presumably, tried to tell someone how it was done.

"Where did your smugglers get this?" Serah asked. She hadn't touched it. She'd been careful not to touch anything in the safehouse all evening—the chipped mugs, the sagging cot, the salt circle Theo had poured across the doorway. Careful the way soldiers are careful around things that have already killed their friends.

"A tomb outside Vael's Crossing. Officially a plague pit." Theo set the bone down. "Unofficially, the architect's family kept him until he died, and what he left behind was too dangerous to bury loose."

"And you're sure reading it will give you the wards."

"I'm sure it's the only thing that will." Theo flexed their fingers. They ached already, the way they always did before a deep reading, an old sympathy pain like the ghost of a break. "The Spire has nine gates and a binding-stone at the heart. Cassia's map shows the way down but not what holds the doors shut. If we go in blind, we die in the first undercroft, or worse—we open something for her."

Serah pulled a chair around to face the table and sat, elbows on knees, close enough that Theo could see the gray coming into her temples even by bad lamplight. "Tell me exactly what happens when you read something like this."

"You've seen me do it. The smuggler's ring. The shrine-keeper's amulet."

"I've seen you read *memories*. A life, laid out flat." Her voice was level, but her hands weren't; she laced them together to still them. "This is different. You said so yourself. You said the last time you touched a carving this old, you lost three days."

"I lost three days because I was alone in a snowdrift with nobody to call me back." Theo met her eyes. "That's why you're here. That's the whole arrangement. I go under. I carry him—his dying, his mind, whatever's left in the cut of these lines. And when I start forgetting which name is mine, you say my name. Loudly. Repeatedly. Until I come back up."

"What if saying your name isn't enough?"

"Then hit me."

Serah almost smiled. It didn't reach anywhere near her eyes. "I'm not going to enjoy this."

"No," Theo agreed. "Neither am I."

They took off their coat. Rolled up both sleeves. Poured a line of salt around the table, then a second one, then hesitated over the third and made it anyway—a ring within a ring within a ring, the old hearth-geometry the dead respected and the living had forgotten. The lamplight bent strangely near the bone. It had been doing that since sundown.

"Theo." Serah said it quietly, before they could pick the relic up. Not a question. Just the name, set down between them like a coin on a bar.

Theo understood. She wanted to know what it sounded like now, while they were still themselves. So they'd know later, when it mattered, whether she'd gotten through.

"It sounds like home," Theo said. "Ask me again at dawn."

They picked up the bone.

* * *

Falling into a reading was like falling downstairs in the dark—you felt every step and couldn't stop any of them.

First the script lit up under their thumb, warm as a fever blister. Then the room went: the lamp, the salt, Serah's steady breathing, all of it folded away like laundry put in a chest. Then the cold came, the particular cold of stone older than kingdoms, and Theo was standing—

Standing in a corridor that did not exist, in a body that was not theirs, looking down at their own hands. Broad hands. Mason's hands, scarred white across every knuckle. Chisel-calluses worn into the fingers like second knuckles.

*Ilyan,* the memory supplied. *My name was Ilyan Vaunt, and I built the door that should never have been opened.*

He was old in this memory. Old and dying, propped against a wall in a room Theo recognized from maps—the upper archive of the Spire, though in his day it had been bright, hung with lamps, alive with scribes. Now in the memory it was empty except for him and the bone in his hand, and the knife he'd used to carve it, and the knowledge sitting in his chest like swallowed coals.

*Listen,* Ilyan told himself, told the bone, told whoever would hold it a hundred years hence. *Because I will not be sane much longer, and after tonight I'll only be useful if you catch me early.*

And he began to teach.

He taught the wards. Nine gates, nine seals, each keyed to a grief—he'd built them that way deliberately, because grief was the one thing kings never ran out of and never learned to fake. The first gate opened to a mother's mourning. The ninth opened to a king's regret, and no king who fed the god would ever feel enough regret to pass it. Clever. Cruel. Theo walked through each seal in memory, learning the words that turned them, the hand-signs, the order of the turning. It poured into them like water into a dry field, and they drank greedily, because this was why they'd come, this was the prize—

—and then Ilyan stopped teaching, and started screaming.

Not aloud. Inward. The memory turned like a page and Theo was inside the rest of it: the years after the binding, when the god had begun whispering up through the stone, and Ilyan had heard it the way Theo heard the dead—constantly, intimately, patiently. The god didn't threaten. It *offered*. It offered Ilyan his dead daughter's voice, reconstructed from his own memories, perfect down to the gap in her teeth. It offered him sleep. It offered him forgiveness, though he'd never asked for any. Every night for forty years it offered, and every night Ilyan lay awake in the dark above his own masterpiece, listening to a thing he'd caged pretend to love him.

*That's how it gets in,* he gasped, carving faster, carving the warnings into his own finger bone with a hand that shook. *Not through walls. Through want. Whatever you love most, it will wear that love like a mask, and you will open the door yourself, and you will thank it—*

The memory buckled. The archive darkened. Ilyan's mind came apart the way wet rope comes apart, strand by strand, and Theo was holding all of it now, the wards *and* the madness, the geometry *and* the forty years of whispers, and there was no longer any seam between what the architect knew and what Theo knew. His daughter's name was in their mouth. His shame was in their chest. His final night was their final night, and he had spent it alone in a bright room going dark, talking to a bone because no living person would listen—

*No one listened,* Theo thought, drowning. *No one ever listens, that's the whole of it, everyone carves and screams and no one—*

Somewhere very far away, a voice said their name.

* * *

"—theo. Theo. Look at me. *Look at me.*"

Lamplight. Salt. Cold sweat soaking through their shirt. Their hands were on the table, gripping its edge hard enough to splinter it, and their mouth was moving, and the sounds coming out were not words they had chosen.

"He wears her face," Theo heard themselves say. "She had a gap in her teeth, she fell from the orchard wall, she was seven, she was *seven*, and he built the cage and it talks to him in her voice every night, every night, Serah, forty years—"

"I know." Serah's hands were on their shoulders, solid, ungentle in the way that helps. "That's him. That's not you. Say your own name."

"Theo," they said, and it came out wrong, doubled, Ilyan's accent underneath. "Theo Iversen. Thornfell. Bone-carver. Third of four children. Mother wove nets, father—" They stopped. Father's face wouldn't come. Father's face had been replaced by a bright archive room and a dying man's hands.

"Keep going."

"Father mended cartwheels. I left at fifteen. I can hear the dead through what they carve." Each fact set down like a foot on ice, testing weight. "I am afraid of deep water. I am afraid of becoming my mother. I am—" Their breath hitched. "I am carrying a man who died alone, and he is very heavy, Serah, he is so heavy—"

"Then put some of it down on me." She dragged her chair around the table, close, knee to knee. "Talk. Don't make sense. Just talk."

So Theo talked.

They talked about Ilyan's daughter until the girl stopped being Theo's grief and became a story, a sad story, a stranger's story. They talked about the nine gates and recited the seals twice through, clean, correct, and Serah wrote them down in her blunt soldier's hand without asking how Theo could possibly still be coherent—they weren't, not really, they were a lantern guttering, and the recitation was the last oil in them. They talked about the god's voice, how patient it was, how kind it pretended to be, and somewhere past midnight Theo realized they were crying and couldn't remember starting.

"He held out forty years," they whispered. "Forty years, alone, with it wearing his daughter. Nobody checked on him. Nobody *sat with him*. He died in a bright room full of lamps and not one person—"

"I'm sitting with you," Serah said.

"Yes."

"So it's different now."

Theo laughed, wet and cracked. "Is it?"

"It is." She said it with the flat certainty she used for verdicts, back when she'd had the authority to pronounce them. "You're not alone in a bright room. I'm here, and I'm armed, and I will sit here until sunrise telling you extremely boring facts about garrison supply ledgers if that's what it takes. Ask me about grain tariffs. I *dare* you."

Theo laughed again, properly this time, and something in their chest unlocked a notch. "What happened to the man who embezzled the flour at Cael Ford?"

"Hanged for theft, pardoned posthumously when they found out it was his captain skimming. I filed the pardon myself. My superiors called it sentiment." Her jaw tightened. "I called it the job."

The night wore on. Theo surfaced by degrees, the way a diver comes up slow to avoid the bends—Ilyan's madness settling out of them layer by layer, named and spoken and thereby made bearable. At some point Serah put tea in their hands and they drank it without tasting it. At some point she draped her cloak over their shoulders, and Theo was lucid enough to notice and too tired to pretend they hadn't.

Near dawn, quiet, watching the window go gray: "Why did you stay? All night. You could have shaken me and paced the hall."

Serah was quiet long enough that Theo thought she wouldn't answer. "When they stripped my office," she said finally, "the worst part wasn't the crowd. It was that everyone I'd worked beside for eleven years suddenly couldn't look at me. Like disgrace was catching. Like I'd become a room people avoided." She turned her mug slowly on its saucer. "I know what it is to be in a room where nobody will sit with you. I wasn't going to leave you in one."

Theo looked at her—at the exiled justiciar keeping vigil over a bone-witch in a smuggler's safehouse, breaking half a dozen laws before breakfast—and felt something settle in them that had no clean name. Not gratitude. Something with more weight and more future in it.

"Ilyan's last warning," they said. "Before the memory ends. He says the god doesn't break doors. It makes you want to open them. So whatever we feel down there—grief, longing, homesickness for people we've lost—if it arrives too conveniently, we name it out loud. We say *that's not mine.* Promise me."

"I promise." Serah stood, stiff from the long vigil, and gathered the written seals off the table. Then she paused. "Theo. The ninth gate opens to a king's regret. Aldric's soul is still in there, fighting. If any part of him can still feel regret—"

"Then part of our way in is also his way out," Theo finished. "I know. I've been thinking about that all night." They looked down at the architect's finger bone, spent now, its script gone dull and ordinary, just a dead man's last lesson fully learned. "There's one more thing he carved. At the very end. I couldn't reach it while I was under—it was too deep, too far gone. But it's still in me, settling. A location."

Serah went very still. "Where?"

"Not a place in the Spire." Theo pressed a hand flat against their own sternum, as if checking that the heart beneath was still theirs. "A place under it. Below the binding-stone. Ilyan didn't build the prison all the way down, Serah. Someone else built what's underneath. And according to the last sane thought he ever carved, whatever's down there has been waiting a very long time for someone to come down and knock."

* * *

Outside, the mountain wind carried a sound neither of them had heard in weeks: bells. Faint, wrong-pitched, ringing from the direction of the lowlands—from the capital, days away, ringing for no occasion either of them could name.

Serah reached for her sword out of pure habit. Theo reached for the dead.

And for the first time since the Thornfell roads had begun to hum, the dead said nothing at all.

Every voice, everywhere, had gone silent—listening.
