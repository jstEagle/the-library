## 11. Mother Cassia's Truth

The sanctuary woke slowly, the way a body wakes from fever.

Theo had been at the Cloister of the Open Door for six days now, and each morning began the same way: with the sound of forty people breathing in the dark of the common hall, all of them facing east, toward the mountains that hid the capital. They breathed together. They ate together. They spoke in the low, unhurried voices of people who had already decided something and were only waiting for the rest of the world to catch up.

Theo had spent six days telling themselves it was peace.

It was not peace. It was patience.

On the seventh morning, Mother Cassia sent for them.

* * *

Her cell was at the top of the cloister's one tower, a room that should have been austere and was instead full of small luxuries: honey in a clay pot, a rug dyed the deep red of arterial blood, candles that smelled of myrrh and something older underneath, something like a tomb opened after rain. She sat by the window with her hands folded, and she did not look surprised to see Theo climb the last stair.

"You've been counting the doors," she said.

Theo stopped on the threshold. "What?"

"Every night since you arrived. You lie awake and you count the ways out. The postern gate behind the goat shed. The drainage culvert under the east wall. The gap in the palisade where the old oak fell." Cassia smiled without warmth. "I don't mind. I'd think less of you if you didn't. Sit down, bone-carver."

Theo sat, because refusing would have been its own kind of answer, and they were not ready yet to give any answers. Their pack sat against the wall where they always kept it—within reach, straps loose. The dead inside it murmured softly, the way they did when Theo's heart rate climbed, as if they could smell fear through the bone.

"I want to thank you," Cassia said. "For what you're about to hear, I mean. Most people who come here never learn the whole truth. They give themselves to the Door piece by piece, a plank at a time, until one day they look down and there's no floor left. You deserve better than that. You've earned better." She poured honey into a cup of warm water, stirred it, and pushed it across the table. "You heard the scream in the smuggler's relic. *The crown is hollow.* Tell me—when you heard it, did you understand whose voice it was?"

"No."

"It was mine," Cassia said. "Forty years ago. I was younger than you are now, and I stood where you're going to stand, and I screamed because I understood too late." She drank her own cup slowly. "So let me be early this time. Let me give you the truth while you can still walk away from it. That is the only kindness I have left in me, and I'm spending it on you."

Theo said nothing. Outside the window, the cloister bells rang the hour—a soft, cracked sound, like a coin dropped in an empty room.

"The cult you think you joined," Cassia said, "the one that sings to the old gods and lights the shrines and prays for their return—we were that once. A hundred years ago we were that. We lit fires in the high places and called the gods home, and nothing came, because the gods cannot come. They are not *away*, child. They are *held*. One god, the last of them, bound beneath your precious capital in a prison of stone and law, and every king since the founding has fed it spoonfuls of his own soul to keep it dreaming." She set down her cup. "You know this already. Don't look so startled. We knew before you did. We have always known first."

"The architect's relic," Theo said quietly. "You knew what it was when you gave it to me."

"I knew what you'd find in it. Yes." Cassia leaned forward. "And now the king has stopped feeding it. Now it feeds itself. Now the door is almost open—and here is where I must ask you to listen very carefully, because everything depends on the next words."

She folded her hands again, and for a moment she looked every year of her age, and then some—looked like a woman who had carried a single idea so long it had worn grooves into her.

"We do not want the god freed."

The sentence dropped into the room like a stone into a well. Theo waited for the splash and heard none.

"Freed, it would remake the world," Cassia went on. "Freed, it would pour itself into every crack and hollow of creation, and what emerged would not be the world—it would be the god wearing the world as a skin. We learned that from the architect too, in our own long reading. Freedom is annihilation. We are not fools. We do not worship annihilation." Her eyes caught the candlelight and held it. "We want to *merge* with it. To walk into the binding willingly, all of us, soul by soul, and become part of what it becomes. Not servants. Not subjects. *Constituents.* When the Spire opens, we will not be standing outside it. We will be the thing that steps through."

Theo's mouth had gone dry. "That's not freedom either. That's just being eaten with permission."

"Eaten." Cassia laughed, short and sharp. "Yes. That's what the small-minded call it. But consider, carver—you of all people. You carry the dead in a leather pack. You spend your nights listening to their griefs and their loves and their petty unfinished business, and you call it a gift. What is your gift, if not a rehearsal? What are you, if not already merged—a living person threaded through with the dead, carrying them, speaking for them, keeping them from silence?" She spread her hands. "We only want to do what you do. At scale. Forever."

"That's not—" Theo started, and stopped, because the horrible thing was that the argument almost worked. It had a shape. It had a logic. It took the loneliest fact of their life and called it holy.

Cassia saw the flicker. Of course she saw it. She had been waiting for it.

"And here," she said gently, "is why we needed you. Why we have always needed someone like you. The binding-stone at the heart of the Spire does not open to force or faith or prayer. It opens to a voice it recognizes—the voice of a hearer, one who has carried the dead and been trusted by them. The dead are the lock's memory, carver. Every soul ever fed to that god is still in there, pressed into the stone like flowers in a book. Only someone who can hear them can persuade them to move aside. Only a bone-carver can open the Door." She smiled, and it was the smile of a woman offering a gift and a bill in the same hand. "You are not our guest, Theo Iversen. You are our key."

* * *

Theo walked back down the tower stairs on legs that belonged to someone else.

The cloister went about its morning around them: brothers hauling water, sisters sorting grain, two children chasing each other between the herb beds with the shrieking joy of children who did not know they were ingredients. Everyone nodded to Theo as they passed. Everyone smiled. And Theo looked at every smile now and saw the same patient hunger underneath, the same settled certainty, and understood that the warmth they had felt for six days—the sense, so foreign and so sweet, of belonging somewhere—had been the warmth of a trap already sprung, they simply hadn't checked the latch.

*No refuge is neutral,* Serah had said once, on the pass, spitting blood after the fight. *Everybody who helps you wants something. The trick is knowing what before you owe it.*

They owed it now. That was the sick center of it. Six days of food and shelter and kind words, and the bill had come due in a tower room over honeyed water: *you are our key.*

They reached their cell and closed the door and put their forehead against the cool stone and listened to the dead in the pack murmur, and for the first time since childhood the sound of them was not comfort but accusation. Because Cassia was right. That was the worst of it. Theo *was* already a merging—one person braided through with dozens of others, boundaries worn thin from years of listening. The cult hadn't invented the idea. They had just found the one person alive who proved it could work.

A knock at the door. Soft. Two taps.

"Theo?" Brother Anselm's voice, warm as bread. "Mother asks if you'll join the evening rite. She says there's a place for you at the front."

"Tell her I'm resting," Theo called, and made their voice sound tired, and heard him pad away satisfied.

They had until evening. Maybe less. Cassia had told them the truth, which meant she no longer needed to convince them—which meant, in the arithmetic of people like Cassia, that persuasion was over and arrangements could begin. There would be guards on the gates tonight. There might already be eyes on the pack.

Because the pack. The relics. The architect's map.

Six nights ago, reading the dying madness of the man who built the Spire, Theo had traced more than wards. In the last lucid fragments—the architect had been a careful man even while unraveling—he had described a map, drawn on vellum, hidden in the cloister's reliquary, showing every approach to the binding-stone: the drowned stairways, the undercrofts, the gate that Cole would inevitably guard. Cassia had given Theo access to the reliquary on the second day, generous as spring. The map was still there. Theo had seen it, touched it, memorized half of it already.

But half wasn't enough. Half got you lost forever under the capital.

* * *

Evening came down over the cloister like a lid.

Theo watched from the narrow window of their cell as the faithful filed into the hall below, forty shapes moving through lamplight toward the east wall, toward the great painted Door that dominated the rite—a door painted on stone, black on black, with a seam of gold down the middle that caught the light like an eye opening. Cassia would stand before it. Cassia would speak. And somewhere in the liturgy there would come the moment when every face turned east and every mind turned inward, and the cloister would forget, for the length of a prayer, that it had a guest.

Theo slipped out during the third hymn.

The reliquary was a low vault off the north corridor, iron-banded, locked—but locks were for thieves, and Theo was not a thief; Theo was a carver, and the lock was brass, and brass remembered the hands that had made it. They pressed their palm flat against it and listened the way they listened to bone, to the faint residual song of the smith who had cast it thirty years ago, a man named Odo who worried about his daughter's cough and sang while he worked. The tumblers wanted to fall the way Odo's rhythm had fallen. Theo hummed it under their breath, barely a thread of sound, and the lock opened like a throat.

Inside, among the gilded skulls and fingerbones of saints nobody could name, the vellum map lay in a cedar case exactly where pride had left it.

Theo took it. Slid it into the false bottom of the pack, against the spine, where the oldest relics rode. The dead shifted around it like water closing over a stone.

And then, in the doorway of the reliquary, they stopped—because Cassia was standing in the corridor.

Not rushing. Not shouting. Just standing, framed in lamplight, hands folded, head tilted, looking at Theo with an expression that was almost tender.

"I wondered which night it would be," she said. "The fourth or the seventh. It's always the fourth or the seventh. Long enough to feel at home, short enough to still remember why they came." She stepped aside, clearing the corridor. "Go, then."

Theo didn't move. "Why?"

"Because a key that turns unwillingly breaks in the lock," Cassia said. "I told you—I spent my kindness on you. And because you'll be back, carver. When you stand in front of that binding-stone with your hammer or your voice or whatever you're planning, and the dead in the stone start screaming, and you realize you cannot carry them all alone—that you were never meant to carry them alone—you will remember that there are forty people in these mountains who have already agreed to carry them *with* you. Forever. Go. Run to your justiciar. Run to your queen. The Door is patient. So am I."

Theo ran.

Through the north corridor, past the goat shed, down to the postern gate they had counted every night for six nights—the gate that opened, as promised, onto the dark shoulder of the mountain and the road below. Behind them the hymn swelled, forty voices rising together, and for one vertiginous moment the sound followed Theo down the slope like a tide, like hands, like the feeling of being loved by something enormous that intended to digest you.

Then the trees took them, and the hymn faded, and there was only breath and frost and the road winding down out of the Thornfell toward the river gorges, toward the capital, toward the Spire.

Toward Serah—who would want to know why Theo's hands were shaking, and who would deserve the truth, and who might, if the truth landed wrong, decide that the woman who wanted to merge with a god had been right about one thing after all:

No one carries the dead alone.

Theo clutched the stolen map through the leather of the pack and ran faster, and far behind them, faint as a bell in another valley, the singing went on and on.
