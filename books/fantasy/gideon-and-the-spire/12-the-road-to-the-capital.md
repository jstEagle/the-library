## 12. The Road to the Capital

The rain found them an hour after they left the sanctuary, as if Cassia had ordered the sky itself to slow them down.

Theo ran with the architect's map pressed flat against their chest inside an oilcloth wrap, and every step on the wet mountain trail sent a jolt of pain through a shoulder that had hit a doorframe hard enough to crack wood. Behind them, up through the pines, torchlight bloomed and multiplied. Not two lights. Not five. A dozen at least, spreading out along the ridge like embers shaken from a fire.

"They're not chasing," Serah said beside her, breath ragged but voice level. She'd taken the lead without asking, the way she took everything—by right of habit. "They're herding. See how they hold the high ground? They want us on the river road."

"Is that bad?"

"It's where they'll be waiting." Serah cut left off the trail without breaking stride, into brush that clawed at Theo's sleeves. "So we won't be there."

Theo followed. It was strange, they thought, how quickly the body learned to trust someone it had known for mere weeks. Strange and dangerous. Every person who had ever claimed to protect them had wanted something back—the smugglers wanted silence, the cult wanted a key, the Crown wanted a witch's head on a spike. Serah Vane wanted justice, which was its own kind of debt, and Theo kept waiting for the bill.

But she had held the door of the sanctuary while Theo ran. She had not asked what the map cost them. She had simply run too.

The dead in Theo's pack murmured against their spine—a smuggler's knucklebone, a shrine-keeper's finger-bone, three relics carried out of Lowmark before Cole burned it. They didn't speak words anymore. They spoke weather. Pressure behind the eyes, cold along the jaw. *Below*, they said. *Water. Old water.*

"The gorges," Theo gasped. "The dead say the gorges."

Serah glanced back, rain streaming off her chin. "Your ghosts have opinions about our route?"

"They always do. Usually terrible ones."

For the first time since the sanctuary, Serah almost smiled. Then the trees ended, and the world dropped away.

* * *

The gorge swallowed the river whole. Black water thundered sixty feet below a ledge no wider than a coffin, and the spray rose up and turned the last light to grey smoke. Somewhere ahead, the river bent around a spur of rock, and beyond the bend—Theo could feel it even before the dead confirmed it—people waited.

Serah crouched at the lip of the ledge and went very still, the way she did when she was counting enemies by sound alone. Theo had watched her do it twice now and still didn't understand how. She tilted her head like a bird, eyes half-closed, reading the rain.

"Four on the spur," she said quietly. "Crossbows. And more behind us on the trail—we've got maybe ten minutes before they reach the ledge."

"Cassia's people?"

"No. Look at the spacing. Soldiers wait in pairs; these are spread like a picket line." She paused. "Inquisitors. Cole's."

Theo's stomach dropped. "Then behind us is—"

"Cassia's faithful. Yes." Serah drew her sword, and the steel came out with a whisper that somehow cut through the river's roar. "Between the law that wants you burned and the faith that wants you used. You have a talent for making friends, Iversen."

"I was invisible for thirty years," Theo said. "I don't know what happened."

"You answered a prayer." Serah's eyes flicked to them, quick and unreadable. "That's all it ever takes."

She moved before Theo could answer, low along the ledge, hugging the rock face where the spray was thickest. Theo scrambled after, heart hammering, one hand on the pack straps, feeling the dead press close—not frightened, the dead never were, but *attentive*, like dogs scenting a storm.

The first crossbowman died without understanding why. Serah came over the rocks at the spur and put him down with the pommel before he finished turning, and the second man loosed a bolt that went wide because his hands were shaking, and then her sword was through him and he sat down against the stone like a tired child. The third dropped his weapon and ran. The fourth—

The fourth was young. Sixteen, maybe. He stood with his back to the drop and his crossbow levelled at Theo, and his face was the face of every boy the Crown had ever fed to its wars, and Theo heard—through the spray, through the roar—the faint rattle of the bones in their own pack answering him. He was wearing a relic at his throat. A little carved vertebra, a grandmother's love-token, and the grandmother was *screaming* at the boy to run.

"He can hear you," Theo whispered, and didn't know if they said it aloud.

The boy fired wide. Serah's blade stopped a hand's width from his neck.

"Down," she said. "On the ground, hands flat. You were never here."

He went down. He was crying. The grandmother's voice faded toward something almost like gratitude as they stepped over him, and Theo hated this road, hated the crown that hollowed kings and the god that hollowed boys, hated that mercy now had to be issued like a ration.

Then the ledge behind them lit with torches, and the shouting began, and there was no more time for hating anything except the next hundred yards.

* * *

They lost the supplies at the second bend.

It happened fast, the way disasters do. A fusillade of bolts from above—one of Cassia's archers had found the high path—and Serah shoved Theo into a crevice and took a shaft across the pauldron instead of through the throat, and the pack went over the edge with Theo's fingers still hooked in the strap. The dead fell screaming into the water. Theo screamed with them. It was not a sound they made consciously; it tore out of them like something ripped loose at the root, and Serah had to grab their collar and haul them back from the edge while the river below filled with voices going silent one by one.

"That was—" Theo's voice broke apart. "Those were people. Those were *people*, they trusted me to carry them—"

"I know." Serah had her back to the rock, bolt jutting from her shoulder-plate, blood threading down her arm. Her face was grey. "I know. Grieve later. Move now."

"Don't tell me when to grieve."

"Someone has to." She said it without cruelty, which was worse. "You want to stand at that edge until Cassia's people arrive? Because they will arrive, and they will be gentle with you, Iversen. That's what frightens me about them. Cole burns you. Cassia *keeps* you."

Theo looked at the water. The voices were gone now—all gone, even the echo of them, and the silence where the dead had been was a physical thing, a hand pressed over their mouth. Thirty years of company, and they had let it fall into a river because they flinched.

They moved.

* * *

Night caught them in a shepherd's hut halfway down the gorge, a dry-stone shelter older than the kingdom, and Serah finally let herself stop moving. That was the frightening part—watching her sit down against the wall and go pale and precise, cataloguing her own damage like a quartermaster counting stock.

"Bolt's not deep," she said, before Theo could ask. "Grazed the plate, kissed the meat. I've had worse from a training yard."

"Liar."

"Obviously." She almost smiled again. "There's a field kit in my saddlebag. If the horse survived, which—" She listened to the rain. "Which it didn't. So. Knife, thread, your hands. Unless you'd rather pray about it."

Theo knelt and cut the strap away, and worked by candle stub while Serah talked—not to fill the silence, Theo realized, but to keep Theo's hands steady. She told them about the first execution she'd ever attended, at nineteen, and how she'd thrown up behind the scaffold and reported for duty the next morning anyway. She told them about the shrine-keeper, Maren, whose innocence Serah had proven one day too late to matter. She told it flatly, without apology or self-pity, the way soldiers count the fallen.

"Why are you telling me this?" Theo asked, tying off the last stitch.

"Because you carry the dead for strangers." Serah's eyes found theirs in the candlelight, and for once there was nothing armored in them. "Someone should carry yours. Or at least hold the lantern while you do it yourself."

Theo sat back on their heels. Outside, the river went on roaring, indifferent, ancient. Inside, in a hut older than the kingdom, a disgraced justiciar bled calmly and offered the only thing she had left: witness.

"The dead used to be enough," Theo said quietly. "Company, purpose, a trade. I told myself I didn't need the living. Then the shrines started answering prayers, and the dead started screaming about a hollow crown, and I thought—if I just follow it far enough, I can fix it and go back to being nobody." They laughed, and it came out wrong. "I'm not going back, am I."

"No," Serah said. "People like us don't get to go back. We only get to choose what we become on the way forward." She flexed her bandaged shoulder, testing it, and winced. "I spent eleven years believing the law was the shape of justice. Then the law exiled me for holding the wrong shape. So now I'm choosing. You're the first thing I've chosen that wasn't a duty."

Theo didn't trust themselves to answer. They banked the candle and lay down on the cold stone floor an arm's length away, and listened to her breathing slow into sleep, and understood—with a terror and a warmth that arrived together—that they would break the world before they let anything happen to her.

The dead, wherever they were now, were probably laughing at them.

* * *

Dawn brought the ambush.

Not Cassia's people. Not Cole's. Worse: both, arriving at the same ford from opposite banks, because the gods—or whatever remained of them—had a sense of humor, and it was cruel.

Theo saw them first from the ridge: red-and-black inquisitors wading the shallows from the east, white-robed faithful descending the scree from the west, and between them, on the gravel bar in the middle of the river, Serah standing alone with her sword drawn, buying time while Theo circled the high path with the map.

"VANE!" Cole's voice carried across the water, silk over iron. "Last chance, Justiciar. Bring me the bone-witch and your pardon lives. Die protecting it, and you die *recorded*. Is that what your precious justice looks like?"

Serah's answer was to advance.

Theo never saw the whole fight—they were scrambling through scree, map clutched to chest, hearing it in fragments: steel ringing, someone crying out, Cole's voice rising into something less human, more delighted. What they saw, cresting the final rise, was this:

Serah, bleeding from a dozen places, backed to the river's edge. Three faithful dead at her feet. Cole advancing with a brand in one hand and a warrant in the other, inquisitors fanning out behind him. And on the far bank, Mother Cassia herself, white-robed, serene, arms open as if greeting a prodigal child.

"Theo!" Cassia called, and her voice reached them impossibly clear across the roar of the water. "Child, look at what they do to the ones who shield you! Drop the map. Walk to me. No one else needs to bleed."

And the horrible thing—the thing Theo would carry longer than any scar—was that for one full heartbeat, they considered it. One heartbeat of *yes, end it, stop the bleeding, give them what they want.*

Then Serah, kneeling in the shallows, spat blood into the river and started laughing.

"You want a *warrant*?" she called to Cole, rising. "Here's my testimony, Inquisitor, free of charge: I've seen the king's eyes. There's nothing home. Your Crown is a puppet and your god-prison is waking, and you're burning shepherds while the world ends. History will file you under *symptom*."

Cole's face went through several expressions too quickly to name. The brand wavered.

And Theo—Theo did the only thing left. They pulled the architect's map from its oilcloth and held it high, and they opened their gift wider than they ever had, wider than was safe, and they *screamed* into the valley with the voices of every dead thing they had ever carried: the smuggler, the shrine-keeper, the grandmother in the boy's vertebra, the century of bones from the Spire's archives. The scream rolled down the gorge like an avalanche, and horses reared, and men dropped their weapons and covered their ears, and Cassia's serene smile cracked for the first time, because even she hadn't known the hearer could do *that*.

In the chaos, Serah waded out of the river. Theo ran down the scree. Their hands found each other in the spray and mud, and they ran—south, always south, toward the flat horizon where the capital's distant spires caught the morning sun like needles.

Behind them, the gorge slowly emptied of enemies, each faction blaming the other for the failure, each retreating to report, regroup, and follow.

Ahead, the road ran straight through country with no cover at all.

Serah limped beside them, drenched, slashed, grinning like a woman possessed. "That scream," she panted. "Remind me never to make you angry."

"Noted." Theo clutched the map. Beneath their feet, through miles of stone, faint as a heartbeat, the Spire pulsed—and for the first time, it pulsed *back* at them, aware, patient, hungry.

*The door is almost open,* whispered a voice that came from nowhere and everywhere, from the water and the stone and the space behind Theo's teeth.

*Come home, little hearer.*
