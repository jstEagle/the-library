## 15. Descent

The key the Queen had given them was small enough to hide in a fist—a sliver of black iron shaped like a stair-step, worn smooth by a century of hands that should never have held it. Theo kept it in their pocket and touched it every few minutes, the way you'd touch a wound to be sure it was still yours.

They went down through the Palace of Aldric's ancestors, which meant they went down through lies.

The undercrofts began as anything else did: stone, torchlight, the ordinary dust of a building that had stood too long to remember what it was for. Serah walked ahead with her sword drawn and her old justiciar's bearing draped over her like a borrowed cloak. She'd been in these cellars before, she said, years ago, escorting condemned prisoners to the deep courts. She remembered the way.

"The deep courts are real?" Theo asked.

"Real enough. Trials no one hears about. Verdicts no one reads." Serah lifted a hanging tapestry aside—it hid an archway older than the wall around it—and gestured Theo through. "The Crown has always known what's under it. That's the part I can't stop chewing on. We weren't betrayed last year. We were betrayed four hundred years ago, and everyone since has just been keeping the secret."

Theo didn't answer, because the dead had started talking.

Not loudly. Not yet. It came the way weather comes—first a pressure behind the ears, then a smell of rain that isn't there. They were three levels below the palace proper when Theo first heard it clearly: a voice, thin as wire, saying *down, down, we're all down here, down.*

"Stop," Theo said.

Serah stopped instantly. She'd learned that much about them on the road—the dead spoke in their own time, and rushing them got people hurt.

"There's someone below us," Theo said. "A lot of someones."

"How many?"

Theo closed their eyes and let the hearing open, the way they'd done ten thousand times over relics and grave-goods and the bones of strangers. Over the smuggler Marten's finger-bone, over the architect Vessic's mad slate, over every scrap of carved death they'd ever carried.

It was like opening a door onto a fire.

The sound hit them physically—a wall of voices, hundreds, thousands, layered and tangled and *pressed*, packed together like roots in a pot grown too small. Theo staggered. Serah caught their arm without a word, braced them against the cold stone, and waited with the patience she'd only ever shown one other person, and that person had been dead.

"They're not echoes," Theo managed. "Down there. The dead in the capital—I told you they were thin, worn out, repeating themselves. That's what I thought. I was wrong." Their breath came ragged. "These ones are whole. These ones are *aware*. And they're not resting, Serah. They're being kept."

"Kept how?"

"I don't know. Like—" Theo searched for it and hated what they found. "Like water behind a dam. Every soul that ever died above this place, maybe. Drained down here and stored."

Serah's jaw tightened. "The kings fed it memories. You said. Soul-memories."

"And nobody asked where the memories came from. Or who they used to belong to." Theo pushed off the wall and stood straight. The voices were still there, still pressing, but they'd noticed Theo now. He could feel attention turning toward them from below, hundreds of points of focus, like moths finding a lamp. "They know I can hear them. Oh—oh, Serah, they *know*."

"What are they saying?"

Theo didn't want to say it. Saying it would make it true in a way hearing it hadn't.

"They're asking me to carry them out."

* * *

The drowned stairways began at the bottom of the undercrofts, where the builders of the old city had simply built over whatever came before. Water ran down the steps in a thin black sheet, ankle-deep and cold enough to ache, flowing *inward*—toward the Spire's heart, not away from it. Theo tried not to think about what fed the flow.

Their boots found each step by feel. Serah held a shuttered lantern low, its light a coin on the water. Neither of them spoke much. The dead spoke enough for both.

They pressed close now. That was the horror of it—not distance, but closeness. In the world above, the dead reached Theo through objects, through bone and stone that had held their shapes. Here they needed nothing. They came straight into the hearing, unmediated, and every one of them wanted something.

*A boy's voice:* I was a cooper's son. I fell in the river. Why am I here? I fell in the river, why am I—

*An old woman:* My name was Anthe. Tell them my name was Anthe. Someone must remember my name was—

*A soldier, clipped and disciplined even in death:* Hearer. Hearer, if you can hear me, listen—we've been counting. There are more of us every year. The king stopped feeding it and now it takes directly, it takes from the deathbeds, it takes from the—listen to me, hearer, we have counted nine thousand and—

"Stop," Theo whispered, and pressed both palms against their temples, and it did nothing at all.

Serah turned back, lantern light cutting up across her face. "What is it?"

"They're all talking at once. They've been waiting—" Theo's voice cracked and they hated that too. "Serah, they've been waiting for someone who could hear them. Some of these voices have been down here for centuries. And the first thing any of them ever gets is me, walking past, going *deeper*."

"You're going down to end it."

"They don't know that. All they know is I'm the first warm thing that's ever listened." Theo waded another step down. The water pulled at their ankles, patient as everything else down here. "Do you understand what it's like? Up top, hearing the dead was a gift. It cost me something, but it gave things back. Grief had a shape. You could hand it to someone." They shook their head. "This isn't grief. This is nine thousand people drowning, and I'm the only shore any of them has ever seen, and I'm walking away from them."

Serah was quiet a moment. Then she said, in the flat, careful voice she used when the truth mattered more than comfort: "You're not walking away. You're going to break the thing that's holding them under. Those aren't different things, Theo. Don't let it tell you they are."

"*It*?"

"The god. Whatever's down there." Serah's eyes flicked to the dark ahead. "I've watched it work on Cole. I've watched it work on Cassia's faithful. It finds the crack in you and pours itself in. Yours is guilt. So it'll drown you in exactly this." She started down again. "Keep walking. Feel awful if you have to. But keep walking."

Theo kept walking.

It didn't help. The voices came anyway, and Theo carried them, because carrying was the only thing they'd ever known how to do.

* * *

Halfway down the drowned stairs, the dead found a way to touch them.

It happened between one step and the next. A pressure at the small of Theo's back, light as a hand, and then cold flooding through them—not the water's cold, a different cold, an *inside* cold—and suddenly they were hearing from everywhere at once, every voice in the dark converging, and the convergence had weight. It leaned on them. Nine thousand souls' worth of desperate hope, focused on one exhausted carver standing in black water.

*Carry us,* the voices said, together, in harmony, in a chord that shook the water on the steps. *Carry us out. Carry us out. You carry the others. We know what you are. Carry us.*

Theo went down on one knee in the flood.

They didn't decide to. Their legs simply stopped being theirs. The cold spread up through their chest and they felt their own heartbeat getting harder to find, as if the dead were learning the shape of a living body by wearing it from within.

*"Theo."*

Serah's voice, far away. Then her hand, not far away at all—gripping their collar, hauling them upright, turning them around. Her face swam into focus. Behind her the lantern had fallen and lay guttering in the shallows, and beyond the lantern the stairwell stretched up into darkness and down into worse darkness, and the whole of it was full of the dead, and every one of them was looking at Theo.

"Look at me," Serah said. "Not them. Me. What did I tell you on the gorge road?"

"That I get to choose whose voices I carry."

"Say it again."

"I get to choose." Theo's teeth were chattering. "But Serah—they're *right here*. They're not relics, they're not memories, they're people, they're *drowning people*—"

"And you can't carry nine thousand." She said it without cruelty, the way you'd state the depth of a river to someone about to swim it. "You couldn't carry ninety. You nearly died carrying one architect's madness for a single night. This is the same thing, Theo, just bigger. It wants you to try anyway. It *wants* your hands full."

The cold in Theo's chest pulsed. For a moment—one long, terrible moment—they saw it plainly: the trap laid out with beautiful simplicity. Take hold of the dead. Try to lift them. Drown under the weight, here, at the threshold, and become one more voice in the dark, one more soul for the thing below to wear. The god didn't need to fight them. It only needed to offer them exactly what their heart most wanted and let their own love do the killing.

Theo breathed. Once. Twice. The way Serah had taught them at dawn after Vessic's madness, counting breaths against the dark.

"I can't carry you," they said aloud, to all of them, and the words tore coming out. "I'm sorry. I can't carry you out. But I can break what's holding you. I swear it on every bone I've ever carved. Hold on a little longer. Hold on for me."

The pressure eased.

Not gone—never gone—but eased, the way a crowd eases when someone finally says the thing everyone already knows. The cold drained out of Theo's chest and pooled away into the black water, and the voices receded to a murmur, and the murmur, God help them, sounded almost like hope.

Serah fished the lantern out of the shallows. Its flame had survived. Of course it had; flame always did better than people.

"Nine thousand," she said quietly. "You heard a number."

"One of them counted. A soldier." Theo wiped their face, though the water on it was indistinguishable from anything else. "He said it takes from the deathbeds now. Directly. Since Aldric stopped feeding it."

"Then every day we stand here, it's fuller." Serah relit the lantern properly and looked down the remaining stairs, into the dark where the water flowed. "So we stop standing."

* * *

The last stair ended at a door.

It shouldn't have been impressive. After everything—the drowned steps, the pressing dark, the chorus of the kept dead—a door was almost comic. But it was made of the same black iron as the Queen's key, and it was tall as three men, and it bore no lock, no handle, no hinge-gaps. It stood in its frame the way a tooth sits in a jaw.

And it was warm. Theo felt the warmth from six feet away, radiating off the iron like heat off a forge, and beneath the warmth, faint but unmistakable, came a sound they knew from a lifetime of listening.

Singing. Something vast, singing to itself, tuneless and content, the way a child hums while it plays with its food.

"It knows we're here," Theo said.

"Of course it does. It's been watching us since the courtyard." Serah set her back against the wall beside the door, out of the direct line of it—an old habit, Theo realized, from kicking in doors for a living. "Last chance to turn around. Not that either of us will."

"No." Theo took out the key. It was warm too, warmer than their pocket should have allowed, and it tugged gently toward the door like a compass needle finding north. "Serah. Before we go through. If it offers me something—if it gets inside my head the way it got inside Cole's—you bring me back. Whatever you have to say. However many times. You bring me back."

"I did it once at dawn on a mountain," Serah said. "I can do it twice."

She said it lightly, but her hand found Theo's shoulder and stayed there a moment longer than the joke required, and Theo covered it with their own, and neither of them made it mean less than it meant.

Then Theo pressed the key to the door.

The iron drank it. There was no click, no mechanism—simply a spreading softness, the metal going brief and pliable as wax, and then the whole vast slab swung inward on silence, revealing a stairway that went down farther than the lantern's light could argue with. Warm air rose out of it, smelling of honey and old blood.

From the depths, the singing paused.

A voice came up the stairwell—Aldric's voice, or the memory of it, worn smooth as the key had been smooth. It sounded tired. It sounded, horribly, *kind*.

"Oh good," it said. "The hearer's come at last. Come down, little bone-carver. We've been keeping so many voices warm for you."

Behind Theo, in the dark of the drowned stairwell, nine thousand of the dead began, very softly, to scream.
