## 16. Cole's Judgment

Inquisitor Cole had burned forty-one people in his nineteen years of service to the Crown, and he could name every one of them.

That was the discipline of it. That was what separated an inquisitor from a butcher. Any man with a torch and a grudge could burn a witch; it took a servant of the law to know their names, their villages, the particular way each of them wept or sang or went silent at the end. Cole kept a ledger. Leather-bound, iron-clasped, written in his own careful hand: *name, crime, evidence, confession, date.* Forty-one entries. He read it every morning before prayer, because a man who did not remember his work would soon stop believing in it, and a man who stopped believing was no better than the heretics he served the fire to.

He told himself that again as he descended the undercroft stairs beneath the Palace of Aldric's fathers, lantern high, six picked men behind him, and the Spire gate somewhere below like a wound in the world.

He had been following the bone-witch for three months. He had burned the Lowmark shrine and scattered its smuggler roads, and still the trail slipped through his fingers like water through a net—until tonight, when the palace itself had begun to *hum*, and every ward-scribe in the capital had come down with nosebleeds at the same hour, and Cole had understood with cold clarity that whatever the bone-witch wanted, they were already inside.

The stairs went down farther than the palace had foundations. He knew this because he had studied the architect's plans, the ones sealed in the royal archive, the ones that ended in a page torn out two hundred years ago. The missing page bothered him more than the humming did. A man who tore out a page of a plan was a man admitting the plan had gone wrong.

"Inquisitor." Sergeant Brakke, at his shoulder, voice low. "The men are asking what's down here."

"The king's business," Cole said.

"And what is the king's business?"

Cole did not answer, because he did not have an answer he liked. For a year now the king's business had been strange. Decrees signed at odd hours by a king who no longer looked anyone in the eye. Audiences cancelled. The queen's household quietly doubled in guard strength, as if she feared something inside her own marriage bed. And always, underneath everything, the shrines waking up along the mountain roads like old wounds reopening.

Cole was not a superstitious man. Superstition was for peasants and priests. But he had learned long ago that the world held things the law had not yet written statutes for, and the wise inquisitor wrote those statutes himself, in blood if necessary, before someone else wrote them first.

The stairs ended.

* * *

The gate was not what he expected.

He had imagined iron, bars, a lock the size of a coffin. What he found instead was a doorway of black stone set into living rock, tall as three men, its surface carved so densely with figures that the eye slid off it—the carver had made ten thousand small human shapes climbing over one another toward some apex, and the apex was worn smooth, polished by centuries of something touching it. Or wanting to touch it.

The air around it moved wrong. Not wind. More like the air was being slowly inhaled by the stone.

And there, before the gate, lanterns guttering, stood two figures. A tall woman in travel-stained leathers with a justiciar's sword at her hip—a sword he recognized, gods help him, he had signed the order confiscating it two years ago when Serah Vane was stripped of her office. And beside her, slight and gray-cloaked, hands ink-stained and scarred with knife-work: the bone-witch. Theo Iversen. Younger than the warrants suggested. Tired in a way that went past the body.

"Serah Vane," Cole said, and his voice carried down the stone throat of the stairwell without echo, which was wrong too. Sound should echo. "You are under arrest for trespass against the Crown, conspiracy, and heresy."

She turned, and he saw she'd put herself between him and the smaller figure without seeming to think about it. Old habit. Good instincts. Wasted on a traitor.

"You're a long way from your pyres, Cole."

"I go where the fire is needed."

"That's what worries me," she said. "It used to be where the *crime* was. I remember you, Cole. Before. You were the only inquisitor who ever apologized to a family after a burning. What happened?"

"The world happened," Cole said. "Step aside."

Behind Vane, the bone-witch spoke, and their voice was quiet and cracked and utterly certain. "Inquisitor. Don't come closer. It knows you're here."

"It," said Cole. "The god."

They flinched at the word. Good. Fear was leverage; fear was a handle by which a man could be steered. Cole had built a career on handles.

"I've read the old archives," he said, advancing a step, boots loud on stone that swallowed the sound. "I know what this place is. A prison. Built by the founders to cage something older than the kingdom. And I know what the bone-readers found in the relics—that the kings have fed it for a thousand years, soul by soul, memory by memory, and that Aldric stopped feeding it, and now it feeds itself, and now the whole capital hums like a struck bell." Another step. "What I don't know is why two criminals think they can do anything about it except die here, tonight, by my hand or by that thing's."

"Because we're going to end it," Vane said. "For good. Shatter the binding."

Something turned over in Cole's chest. He kept his face still—he had a face like a locked door, subordinates said—but inside, the words *end it, shatter it* rang against a thought he had buried years ago and never let himself dig up:

*If it can be shattered, then everything the Crown has done to contain it—the burnings, the purges, the shrine-keepers hanged and roasted along every road where the old faith flickered—was never protection. It was maintenance. It was feeding.*

He buried the thought again. Deeper this time.

"Sergeant," he said. "Take them."

* * *

His men got four steps.

Then the gate exhaled.

There was no other word for it. The black stone door breathed outward, and what came out was not air but *attention*, vast and slow and ancient, rolling over them like deep water over a drowned village—and Cole felt it find him. Feel him. *Read* him.

The visions began.

Not visions like dreams. Visions like memories played back by something that had been standing behind him his whole life, watching, taking notes.

He was in Marrowfield, twenty-two years old, holding his first torch, and the woman tied to the stake was named Elsbet Corren and she had a wart on her left cheek and she was *innocent*—he knew it even then, some part of him had known it, the confession had been extracted with pliers and fear, and he had lit the fire anyway because the magistrate ordered it and because the crowd was watching and because a young inquisitor who hesitated was a young inquisitor who washed out—

He was in Duncarrie, and the shrine-keeper Tomas was singing a hymn to a god whose name Cole had never learned, and Tomas's daughter was screaming in the front row of the crowd, and Cole gave the signal anyway, and afterward he wrote in his ledger *confession given* knowing full well the confession had been the location of a hidden icon beaten out of a man who worshipped nothing but rain and growing things—

He was in the capital, last spring, and the accused was a boy of sixteen who carved bones, just carved them, sold little charms at market, and there was no evidence, none, and Cole had looked at the boy's terrified face and seen the bone-witch's face, the same trade, the same ink-stained hands, and he had sentenced him anyway because the warrant had come down from above with the king's seal and the king's seal was law and law was all he had—

Forty-one fires. They came faster now, all of them at once, overlapping, a cathedral of flame built entirely out of his own ledger, and in the heart of every fire the condemned turned to look at him—not screaming anymore, not anymore, just *looking*, with the terrible patience of the dead, and their mouths all moved together and said, in a voice like the gate breathing:

*You kept our names. Why did you keep our names?*

"To remember," Cole whispered. His knees had gone. He didn't remember kneeling. "To honor the law—"

*You kept our names because you knew,* said the dead, gentle as snowfall. *You always knew. The ledger wasn't remembrance. It was penance you were too cowardly to perform.*

"No." He was shaking his head. Somewhere far away Sergeant Brakke was screaming, and one of his men was running back up the stairs, and another had simply sat down and begun to pray to gods whose shrines Cole himself had burned. "No, I served the Crown, I served the *law*—"

*The law is a wall,* said the thing in the gate, and its attention pressed closer, curious almost, a child examining a beetle. *And you were the mortar between its stones. Do you want to see what the wall was hiding?*

And it showed him.

It showed him the founding. It showed him the architects carving this very gate, ten thousand figures climbing toward an apex, and the apex was a crown, and the crown was hollow—it showed him a thousand years of kings descending these stairs with lanterns, alone, carrying jars, pouring themselves into the dark a little at a time, buying the kingdom's safety with pieces of their souls, and it showed him Aldric, the current Aldric, standing where Cole knelt now, three years ago, saying in a voice gone thin and papery: *"I have almost nothing left to give it. Find another way."* And then it showed him what answered from the dark, wearing the king's voice, saying: *"There is no other way. There is only me."*

Cole vomited. Bile and terror and nineteen years of certainty, all of it coming up.

When he raised his head, the bone-witch was crouched in front of him. Close enough to touch. Vane stood behind them, sword drawn but lowered, watching him with an expression he had last seen on the faces of the condemned: pity.

"You saw it," the bone-witch said softly. "All of it."

"I saw—" His voice came out in pieces. "I saw that it was never about the shrines. The burnings. None of it. We weren't protecting the kingdom from the old gods. We were—" He laughed, and the laugh broke in the middle. "We were keeping people from praying for help. Because help might actually come. And then the thing in the cellar would wake up hungry."

"Yes," said the bone-witch.

"Forty-one people." He stared at his own hands. They looked like someone else's. Old man's hands. When had they gotten old? "I kept their names in a book. I read it every morning. I thought that made me better than a butcher."

Vane crouched beside him now. "It doesn't unmake what you did," she said. No softness in it, but no cruelty either. Justiciar's honesty. "But what you do next is still yours. It's always been yours, Cole. That's the whole point of a ledger."

He looked up at her. "They'll hang you for this. Both of you. Even if you save them."

"Probably," she said.

"Then why?"

"Because justice doesn't need a badge," she said. "I learned that the hard way. You're learning it now."

* * *

The god's attention was still on him. He could feel it, patient as stone, waiting to see what the beetle would do. And Cole understood, with the last clear thought of his old life, that this was the truest judgment he had ever faced. Not the tribunals, not the trials, not the arch-inquisitor's bench. This. Kneeling in the dark before a door that ate souls, with his ledger of forty-one names burning a hole in his breast pocket like a coal.

He took it out. The leather was warm from his body. Iron clasp, careful handwriting, nineteen years.

*Name, crime, evidence, confession, date.*

Every entry a lie he had signed. Every confession a scream transcribed into tidy script.

"Brakke," he said. His voice steadied as he spoke, which surprised him. "Sergeant. Look at me."

Brakke was white-faced, weapon half-raised, tears cutting tracks through the dust on his cheeks. "Sir?"

"What you just saw. What it showed you. Every man here saw his own fires, yes?" Nods, broken breathing. "Then hear my order, and know it's the last order I will ever give in the Crown's service. Go up. Go home. Tell no one what's below—or tell everyone, I no longer care which. But you will not raise a hand against these two, and you will not stand between them and that door. Am I understood?"

"Sir—they'll call it treason—"

"They called it treason when Serah Vane refused to burn an innocent man," Cole said. "History will sort out the word later. Go."

They went. All six, stumbling up the stairs, Brakke last, looking back once with an expression Cole chose to read as grief rather than contempt.

Then it was the three of them, and the gate, and the humming that was getting louder, and somewhere below—deep below, past the drowned stairways and the cells full of clinging dead—the sound of many voices chanting in unison. Cassia's faithful. Coming down the other way.

Cole got to his feet. His legs held. He took the ledger, opened it to the first page—*Elsbet Corren, Marrowfield*—and held it out to the bone-witch.

"You'll want this," he said. "Names matter to your kind. Let them be remembered properly, at least. Not as evidence. As people."

Theo took it with both hands, the way one takes a wounded bird.

"And you?" Vane asked. "Where does an inquisitor go, after?"

Cole looked at the gate. At the worn-smooth apex of the carving, the hollow crown that ten thousand climbing figures reached for. He thought about the torn-out page in the architect's plans, and about the boy with the carved bones he had sentenced last spring, and about how a man who stops believing in his work becomes worse than the heretics he burns.

"Down," he said. "With you. If you'll have a fourth pair of hands. I know these undercrofts better than any map. And I owe—" his voice caught, steadied— "I owe forty-one debts, and I am done paying them in ash."

Vane studied him for a long moment. Then she stepped aside from the gate.

Below them, faint but rising, the chanting swelled—and the black stone of the door began, very softly, to glow.

"Then keep up," she said, and led the way into the dark.
