## 17. The Thing Wearing the Crown

The stair went down past the point where down should have stopped.

Theo counted the steps because counting was a thing hands and feet could do while the rest of them screamed. Four hundred and eleven. Then the stone changed underfoot—older, smoother, worn into a shallow channel by centuries of feet that had come down carrying something heavy and gone back up empty.

The dead here didn't whisper. That was the first thing Theo understood, and it was worse than screaming. In the Thornfell, the dead murmured like wind through wheat, fragments of memory, griefs half-dissolved. Here they pressed against the inside of Theo's skull all at once, whole and coherent and *aware*, and every one of them was saying the same word in different mouths:

*Stay.*

Not stay as in remain. Stay as in stop. Stay as in *we have been falling for nine hundred years and you are the first hand to reach the rail.*

Serah's torch guttered behind them. "Talk to me," she said. Her voice had gone rough hours ago, somewhere around the drowned stairway where the water had been warm as blood. "I can't hear what you hear. So talk."

"They're awake." Theo's own voice sounded borrowed. "All of them. Every soul the Spire ever took. They're not echoes, Serah. Echoes don't know they're echoes."

"Can we help them?"

"Not yet." Not maybe. Not I hope so. Theo had learned on the descent that hope was a currency the dead could smell, and false hope made them cling harder. "The binding-stone should be at the center. If it's real, if the architect's map wasn't Cassia's lie—"

"It wasn't a lie." Serah said it flat, the way she said everything now, as if conviction were a weight she carried in her sword arm. "She wanted us here too badly for it to be a lie."

That was the part Theo couldn't stop turning over. Cassia had let them run. Had watched them steal her map and walk out of the sanctuary with her faithful howling at the gates—and then the pursuit had been slow, and sloppy, and full of gaps exactly wide enough for two exhausted fugitives to slip through. As if the Open Door needed its key delivered, not captured.

As if everything—the cult, Cole, the shrines waking along the mountain roads—had been one long funnel, and Theo had been walking down its throat since the day a dying smuggler's bone sang with a stranger's scream.

*The crown is hollow.*

Four hundred and sixty-three steps. The stair opened.

* * *

The heart of the Spire was not what Theo expected, which was itself a kind of horror, because Theo had spent their whole life expecting the worst from old places and being right.

They expected a dungeon. A pit, chains, something writhing. What they found was a throne room.

It rose in a perfect column of black stone, wide as a cathedral nave, ribbed with buttresses that arched overhead and met at a point so far above the torchlight lost it. And everywhere—walls, floor, the ribs themselves—bone. Not scattered relics, not carved charms. Bone as architecture. Femurs laid in courses like brick. Skulls set into the vaulting, hundreds of them, thousands, each one facing inward toward the center of the room, each one with its jaw wired shut with silver.

At the room's heart stood a dais, and on the dais stood a man.

He was dressed in what had once been royal finery—crimson and gold, a mantle heavy with embroidery—but the finery hung off him like clothes left out in the rain. He was thinner than any living man had a right to be. His skin had the gray translucence of someone who hadn't eaten in a long time and had stopped minding. His hair had gone white at the temples, though by Serah's accounts of the court Aldric was barely forty.

And he was standing, not sitting, on the dais beneath an empty throne, both hands braced against a slab of black stone taller than he was—the binding-stone, unmistakable, its surface crawling with script that moved when Theo looked directly at it, rearranging itself like insects disturbed under bark.

The man turned his head.

For one heartbeat Theo saw King Aldric: the strong jaw from the coinage, the tired eyes from the portraits. Then the eyes changed. Not the pupils. Something behind them, the way a window changes when someone walks up to it inside a dark house.

"Ah," the thing said, in a voice like a bell struck underwater. "The hearer."

Serah's sword came out with a sound like tearing cloth. She stepped in front of Theo without seeming to decide to, the way she'd been doing since the gorges, and Theo felt the familiar useless flare of guilt about it.

"Stay behind me," she said.

"No," said the king—or the mouth of him. "No, that's wrong. Let them come forward. Please." And the voice broke, mid-word, into something else entirely—a man's voice, cracked and human and terrified. "*Please.*"

Theo knew that voice. Not Aldric's. Theo had never heard Aldric speak. But every dead person Theo had ever heard had that same fracture in it, right at the end, where the person ran out of time.

"Aldric?" Theo said carefully.

The king laughed. It was a terrible sound, wet and relieved. "You can hear me. Nine months. Nine months I've been shouting down my own throat and no one—" He sagged against the binding-stone. Up close Theo could see his hands: the fingers had been broken and reset, broken and reset, over and over, calluses layered on calluses. "I've been holding this. Since it stopped taking my memories and started taking *me*. You push, and it pushes back, and there is nowhere to sit down. There has been nowhere to sit down since spring."

"What is it?" Serah demanded. She hadn't lowered the sword. "What are you?"

"I'm the king," Aldric said, with a ghost of the pride that must have filled that title once. "Or the rind of one. It ate everything else. My father fed it before me, and his mother before him, and none of us were ever told until the night we came down here and the door closed." He looked at Theo, and his eyes were clear for a moment, human, pleading. "Bone-carver. Hearer. Whatever your name is. I need you to do something, and you're going to hate it, and I need you to do it anyway."

"Tell me," Theo said.

"Kill me."

* * *

The silence after that had texture. It pressed.

"I've fought it," Aldric went on, fast now, a man who had rehearsed this speech alone in the dark for months and finally had an audience. "Every hour. While it wears me upstairs and signs things and smiles at my wife—" his face crumpled and reassembled, "—while it does whatever it does with my body, I am down here, pushing. And I can feel myself getting thinner. Not tired. *Thinner.* Like a candle. And some nights I think: one more week of fighting. One more month. And some nights I think about letting go, just to rest, just to sit down, and those nights it gets stronger, because wanting to stop is a crack and it pours itself into cracks—"

"You're rambling," Serah said. Gently, Theo thought. For Serah.

"I know. I know. I haven't talked to anyone in—" He shook his head. "Here's what matters. It's almost done with me. When it finishes, it won't need this body anymore—it'll be able to make its own shape, or wear anyone's. Everything the bindings cost it, it will have bought back with nine hundred years of interest. And the door above—" he pointed upward, at nothing, at the capital, at the world, "—is already open a hand's width. You've seen it. Your singing shrines. Your answered prayers. It's been leaking through the seams for years, buying, tasting, learning what people will trade."

"The mother in Lowmark," Theo whispered. "It took her memory of her daughter's face."

"And gave the child a doll that would never break. Yes. Small trades first. It's patient. It was patient for nine hundred years; it can be patient for nine more." Aldric pushed himself upright against the stone, and Theo saw the effort cost him something visible—a shudder running through him like a current. "But it's done being patient now. Because of you."

Theo's stomach dropped. "Me?"

"The hearing. The gift. Do you know why the old gods bound this one instead of killing it? Killing a god spills it. Binding it keeps the power intact, coiled, *available*. The Spire isn't a prison, carver. It's a vault. And a vault needs a key that can turn." Aldric's eyes—both sets of eyes, the man's and the thing's, flickering between them like a bad lamp—fixed on Theo. "A voice that speaks to souls. A hand that shapes bone. Someone who can stand at the lock and be heard on both sides of it. It has been dreaming about you for longer than you've been alive."

"That's why the shrines woke," Theo said. The whole rotten architecture of the last year assembled itself in their head at once. "Why the prayers got answered. Why the dead started screaming about the crown. It was calling me. All of it was bait on a hook."

"Yes," said Aldric.

And then the other voice said it too, at the same moment, in harmony, and the harmony was the most beautiful sound Theo had ever heard, and that was how they knew they were already in danger.

* * *

The change was seamless. One breath Aldric was a ruined man begging for death; the next, the ruined man's mouth curved into a smile of perfect, ancient kindness, and the torchlight bent toward him like plants toward sun.

"Hear me, little carver," it said, and it used Aldric's voice because that was crueler, and it knew cruelty the way rivers know downhill. "You have carried the dead since you were six years old. Do you imagine I don't know? I have listened to you listening. Every relic you've ever carved, every voice you've tucked into bone to keep it safe—I heard them too. We are colleagues, you and I. Custodians. The only difference between us is scale."

"Don't listen to it," Serah said. But her sword arm had dropped an inch, because the voice did something to the body, loosened the knees, made resistance feel like rudeness.

"Your mother," the god said softly.

The word went through Theo like a blade finding the gap in armor.

"She died badly, didn't she? Fever, in the winter, and you were twelve, and you held her hand and heard her go—heard the exact moment the person became the echo—and you have spent twenty years carving her voice into scraps of antler and ivory just to hear her say your name again. Say it wrong, sometimes, because memory frays even in bone. Wouldn't you like to say it right?"

"Stop," Theo said.

"I could give her back." The god spread Aldric's ruined hands, gracious, magnanimous, a king bestowing boons. "Not the echo. Not the fraying scrap. *Her*. Whole. The binding holds enough of me that I could reach into the great quiet where the finished souls sleep, and find her, and pour her into a morning, and you would wake and smell her bread and hear her scold you for carving at the table. Is that not worth a price? You have paid worse prices for less. You crossed mountains. You buried a smuggler boy who died buying you ten minutes. You carry his voice in your pack right now and flinch every time it speaks."

Theo's hand had drifted, without their permission, to the pack. To Marek's relic. Their eyes were burning.

"And the others," the god continued, relentless, tender. "All of them. Everyone you've ever carried, everyone you've ever failed to save, everyone whose last words you keep like stones in your pockets. I can unmake every death you have ever hated. That is what I am, little carver. I am the door between what happened and what could have. Your kings locked me away because humanity begged them to—begged to be protected from their own longing. And look what it built them. A kingdom of bells no one remembers ringing. A queen keeping her husband's corpse on a leash. A justiciar exiled for the crime of being right." It leaned closer, and Aldric's gray lips smiled. "I am not offering you power. Power is such a small word. I am offering you *amends*."

The terrible thing—the thing Theo would remember with shame for the rest of their life, however long that turned out to be—was that for three full seconds, they wanted it.

Not abstractly. Specifically. They saw the kitchen. The steam on the window. Mother's hands dusted with flour, turning, saying *Theo* in the voice that had frayed down to a whisper in the oldest piece of antler in their pack, the one they didn't let themselves read more than twice a year because each reading wore it thinner—

"There you are," the god murmured, watching their face. "There it is. Don't be ashamed. Longing is the oldest prayer. I answer it better than any god ever did."

"Theo." Serah's voice, low, steady, anchored to nothing but herself. "Look at me. Not it. Me."

Theo looked. Serah Vane, disgraced, warrant on her head, torch burning low, standing between a god and the only person in the world who could open its cage—and her eyes weren't afraid. They were furious. Furious on Theo's behalf, which was somehow the most frightening and most steadying thing in the room.

"It's lying," she said. "Or worse—it's telling the truth. Either way, ask it the price."

"Oh, there's no price," the god said smoothly. "Only a turn of the key. Only your hand on the stone, and your gift in the lock, and a word spoken in the old tongue—which I will teach you, freely, gladly—"

"It eats what it's given," Theo said. Slowly. Watching Aldric's face. "Memories. Souls. It took a mother's memory of her daughter's face for a doll. What does it take for a resurrection?"

The smile stayed. The smile was designed to stay. But behind it, deep in the black stone, the crawling script sped up, and every skull in the vaulting strained against its silver wire, and four thousand dead voices screamed one word into Theo's skull:

*RUN—*

"Everything," Theo answered themself. "It takes everything. That's the trade. That's always been the trade. The shrines, the prayers, the king—all of it, feeding. And if I open the door all the way—"

"You would remake the world," the god agreed, serene. "Grieflessly. Isn't that worth—"

"No," Theo said.

The word came out small. Theo said it again, louder, and reached for the hammer at their belt—the architect's hammer, pried from the madman's tomb, its head wrapped in lead and prayer-iron. "No. Because my mother wouldn't want to come back at that price. She taught me that everything carved costs the carving. And because you don't offer gifts." Their hand closed on the haft. "You offer hooks."

The kindness fell off the god's face like a mask slipping its strings.

What remained was older than kingdoms, and it looked at Theo with Aldric's hollowed eyes, and the temperature of the vast room dropped so fast the torchflame shrank to a blue bead.

"Then I will wear you instead," it said. "The key turns either way."

Behind it, pinned against the binding-stone, the remnant of King Aldric found the last of himself, and screamed—not with his mouth, which no longer belonged to him, but straight down the hearing, into the marrow of Theo's bones:

"*THE STONE—STRIKE THE STONE—NOW—*"

Above them, faint through nine hundred feet of rock, came the sound Theo had been dreading since the sanctuary doors: many feet on stairs, and a woman's voice leading them in hymn.

Cassia had arrived.

And Serah turned to face the stair, drawing steel, and said the words Theo would carry longest of all:

"Whatever you're going to do—do it fast."
