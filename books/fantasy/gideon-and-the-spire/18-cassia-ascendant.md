## 18. Cassia Ascendant

The first thing Serah heard was singing.

It came up through the drowned stairways behind them, thin and sweet and wrong—a hundred voices braided into one, rising through the water-stone like bubbles through a well. She had spent six years listening for trouble in the dark, and every instinct she owned told her this was worse than trouble. This was worship.

"Theo." She caught their sleeve at the landing where the stair split—the long way down to the binding-chamber, the short way back toward the entrance they'd fought so hard to reach. "Do you hear that?"

Theo's face did something terrible. They went still the way the dead went still, their head tilting, listening with more than ears. "Cassia," they said. "She's inside. She's *inside*—"

"How?"

"The faithful. The ones we lost in the gorges—they weren't all chasing us. Some of them were carrying her." Theo's hand pressed flat against the wall, against the old stone that sweated cold. "The Spire has doors the architect never drew. She's been walking toward this since before Lowmark. Since before any of it."

The singing swelled. Somewhere below, deep beneath the capital's foundations, something vast turned over in its sleep—or stopped pretending to sleep—and the walls of the stairwell groaned like a ship's hull.

Serah had held gates before. She'd held a bridge at Cael's Ford with eleven soldiers against forty raiders, and she'd held the door of a burning courthouse while clerks fled past her with armfuls of records no one would ever read again. She knew what holding a position meant: it meant deciding, here and now, who you were willing to be when the cost came due.

"Go down," she said.

Theo stared at her. "You can't hold this alone."

"I'm not alone." She drew her sword—not the ceremonial blade they'd taken from her when they stripped her office, but the plain soldier's steel she'd bought from a smuggler with stolen coin. It felt right in her hand. It had always felt right. "I've got a stairway about four men wide, and I've got the high ground, and I have absolutely nothing left to lose. That's better odds than Cael's Ford."

"Theo—" She said their name again, softer, and made herself say the rest. "Whatever's down there, whatever the god offers you—you remember what Aldric begged. You remember what the dead in these walls are begging right now. You're the only one who can hear them. So go be their voice. Let me be your shield."

For a moment she thought they would argue. She almost wanted them to. But Theo had learned, somewhere on the road out of Thornfell, the same hard lesson she had: that love sometimes looked like leaving someone behind at a doorway, trusting them to still be standing when you came back.

"Come back," Theo said. Not a plea. An order, given in the flat voice of someone who had carried too many dead voices to waste breath on pretty words. "I don't care how. Bleed on everything. Come back."

"I'll bleed on everything," Serah promised. "Go."

They went—down the long stair, into the dark where the god waited, their footsteps fading until the singing swallowed them whole.

* * *

Serah counted the singers by ear as they came.

Forty, maybe fifty voices, but not fifty people—the sound kept doubling on itself, harmonizing with echoes that hadn't been sung yet. The cult came up the drowned stairs two abreast, robed in undyed wool gone grey with river-water, each of them carrying a candle stub that burned without heat. Their faces were calm. That was the worst part. She had faced fanatics before, inquisitors mostly, and fanatics always had fire in their eyes. These people had peace. Cassia had given them peace the way a poisoner gives wine.

She let them get halfway up the flight.

Then she stepped onto the landing with her sword bare and her boots planted and her voice pitched to carry the way a justiciar's voice carries over a courtroom, over a riot, over a battlefield.

"Cassia's faithful!" she called. "Hear me. I am Serah Vane. You may know my name—I held law in this kingdom for nine years, and I broke it once, for an innocent woman, and I'd break it again. I'm telling you now as I told the Crown then: what your mother is doing down there will not free the old gods. It will feed one god a world, and you will not survive the meal."

The singing faltered. A few faces turned toward her voice. Good. Words were weapons too; she'd won half her cases before steel ever cleared a scabbard.

"You were told the unbinding opens a door," she went on, backing one step, drawing them up the stair after her, buying Theo time with every foot of height she surrendered. "Doors open both ways. Ask yourselves why a god needs *you*—why it needs a bone-carver, why it needs a rite, why it needs anything at all, if it's strong enough to remake creation. Things that need help aren't gods yet. They're prisoners bargaining."

A man near the front raised his candle. "Mother Cassia says—"

"Mother Cassia says a great many things." Serah shifted her grip. "I've read her letters. I've heard her speak. Do you know what she never says? What happens to *her* in the merging. Ask yourself that. Every faith that ever asked people to burn asked them to burn for someone else's ascension."

For three heartbeats, the stair was silent except for the drip of water and the distant, swelling song.

Then the front rank charged, and Serah stopped talking.

* * *

Fighting on stairs is arithmetic.

Four men wide meant she could only be engaged by two, maybe three at once if they were brave and stupid. High ground meant her blade worked downhill and theirs worked up, which tired their arms twice as fast. And the drowned stone was slick, which meant the third and fourth ranks pushing from behind did her work for her, shoving their own faithful onto her point.

She gave ground slowly. That was the discipline of it—give ground slowly, make them pay for every step, count bodies not by the fall but by the hesitation of the ones behind. A woman with a boat-hook came at her left side and Serah took the hook on her crossguard, twisted, put the woman down into the arms of the men behind her rather than through her. She wasn't sure why she bothered sparing them. Habit, maybe. Or maybe because she had finally understood, somewhere between a burned letter and a mountain road, what she was actually defending.

Not the law. The law had exiled her. Not the Crown; the Crown was hollow, wearing its king like a mask. Not even the kingdom, not really—kingdoms endured things like this or didn't, and either way they'd still be there tomorrow morning, needing taxes and roads and someone to argue about borders.

She was defending a person. One person, with ink-stained fingers and a pack full of other people's last words, currently descending into the dark to do the bravest and most terrible thing Serah had ever witnessed. Everything else—the stair, the fight, her own life—was just the shape her protection took.

A blade opened her forearm. She barely felt it. Another caught her thigh above the knee, and that one she felt plenty, and she laughed—actually laughed, because pain meant she was still here, still spending herself exactly where she'd chosen to spend herself.

"Is that all?" she shouted at them, blood running into her boot. "Your god is waking and this is all it sends me? I've been threatened by *better*!"

That was when the candles guttered—all of them at once, fifty flames bending sideways toward the depths, toward the source, like iron filings snapping toward a lodestone. The singing changed key. Rose. Became something with structure underneath it, syllables in a language that predated language, and Serah felt it in her teeth, in the scar tissue of her old wounds, in the place behind her eyes where Cole's visions had shown her what power did to the people who served it.

The faithful stopped fighting.

They all stopped, mid-swing, mid-shove, and turned their heads downward in perfect unison, and began to climb faster—not attacking her now, just flowing past her along the walls like water around a stone, pouring down toward the binding-chamber, toward Cassia, toward the rite. One of them, a grey-haired woman, paused beside Serah long enough to touch her bleeding arm with gentle fingers.

"He's almost open," the woman said, with such tenderness that Serah's skin crawled. "Thank you for keeping the way. We couldn't have done it without you."

Then she was gone, down the stairs, and Serah stood alone on a landing littered with the groaning and the dead, holding a sword she suddenly didn't know what to do with.

* * *

She ran.

Down the long stair, taking steps three at a time, her wounded leg screaming, following the tide of grey robes and the light of heatless candles and the song that was no longer a song but a sentence being spoken very slowly by a hundred mouths. The air grew warmer as she descended. Warmer, and thicker, and full of a pressure like the moment before a summer storm breaks, and the walls around her had begun to sweat not water but something faintly luminous, gold-white, dripping upward.

She burst into the antechamber outside the binding-chamber in time to see the last of the faithful kneel in a great ring around the chamber floor, and Mother Cassia standing at its center with her arms raised, her voice climbing through registers no human throat should own—

—and beyond her, across the chamber, at the base of the broken binding-stone, Theo.

Theo, hammer in hand, frozen. Not from fear. From listening. Serah couldn't hear what they heard, but she could see it land on them like weather, wave after wave, their whole body swaying with it, and she understood with sick clarity that Cassia had timed her rite for exactly this moment—when Theo stood closest to the god's voice, drowning in it, easiest to tip.

"*Theo!*" Her voice cracked across the chamber.

Their head turned. Found her. And in their eyes she saw the war happening—the god's offer, the dead they loved, the power to unmake every grief they'd ever carried—and beneath it, faint but stubborn, the sight of her: bleeding, swordless now, dropped somewhere behind her on the stairs, standing in a doorway because that was the only thing she knew how to be.

Cassia's voice reached its peak. The ring of faithful flared with light. The binding-stone groaned, hairline cracks spidering gold across its face—

And Theo raised the hammer.

Serah threw herself forward across the chamber floor, toward them, toward the choice being made, as the first crack of the shattering split the dark like the world's own bone breaking—

—and the light went white.
