## 19. The Unbinding

The binding-stone hung in the dark like a heart that had forgotten how to stop.

Theo stood at the bottom of the world and looked up at it. It was not stone, exactly—or it was, but stone the way a scar is skin. A mass of pale rock the size of a chapel, suspended in a shaft of nothing, veined with channels of gold that pulsed in slow rhythm. Each pulse sent a shiver through the air, through Theo's teeth, through the bones of their hands. Below it, the Spire's true floor: a ring of black water so still it reflected the stone perfectly, so that the thing seemed to float between two versions of itself.

And threaded through all of it—the veins, the water, the air—was the god.

Not a voice. Not yet. More like pressure. Like standing at the bottom of a lake and feeling the weight of everything above you, waiting for you to open your mouth.

Behind Theo, far up the stair, Serah was holding the door. They could hear it even here—steel on steel, her voice barking orders no one obeyed, the wet sounds of a fight she could not win forever. Every part of Theo wanted to run back up those stairs. Every part except the part that had been walking toward this stone since a dying smuggler whispered *the crown is hollow* into a piece of riverbone.

They stepped forward. The black water did not ripple when their boot touched its edge, which was wrong, which was the kind of wrong that made the dead in Theo's memory flinch.

Because the dead were here. All of them.

They had been gathering since the descent, since the drowned stairways, but here at the heart they were not echoes or whispers. They were *present*. The architect whose madness Theo had carried for one long night stood at the water's edge with his hands folded, patient as a man waiting out rain. The smuggler Bren, who had died buying Theo twenty minutes on the Lowmark road, leaned against nothing and grinned his gap-toothed grin. The shrine-keeper of Lowmark, the innocent woman Cole should never have condemned. Children. Farmers. Kings' servants who had fed this thing soul-memory for nine generations without knowing what they fed.

And nearer than all of them, closer than breath:

*Mother.*

Theo's mother had died when they were eleven, in the winter the fever came through Thornfell. She had carved them a whistle from elk bone three days before the coughing started—a small thing, a toy, the last thing her hands ever made. Theo had heard her in it ever since. Not constantly. But on bad nights, on the worst nights, they had held it to their ear and listened to her hum an old mountain song, off-key, the way she always sang it, and the loneliness had been survivable.

She stood beside the binding-stone now, humming.

"You're not her," Theo said aloud. Their voice cracked in the vast dark. "I know you're not her."

*I am every voice you ever carried,* the god said, and it used her timbre, her slight rasp, the way she rounded the ends of words. *You have spent your whole life listening to the dead, little carver. Do you think I cannot hear what you hear? Do you think I do not know what you want?*

"I know what you are."

*Do you?* The pressure shifted, and suddenly the chamber was full of light—not real light, remembered light, the gold of late summer afternoons in the high passes. *I was old when your mountains were young. I taught the first of your kind to bury their dead with care. I kept the names of the dead when there were no gods yet to keep them. And then your kings built me a prison and called it piety, and fed me scraps of other people's lives until I forgot my own.*

The pulses in the golden veins quickened. Theo felt it in their sternum now, a second heartbeat laid over their own.

*Aldric stopped feeding me,* the god said. *Clever king. He thought if he starved me I would die. Instead I learned to feed myself—and I found I liked the taste of a living soul better than memories. I wore him from the inside. I would have worn the world the same way, given time. But then you came down my stairs, bone-hearer. You, who can carry a soul entire. You, who could unbind these wards with a touch and let me pour myself into you as a vessel—*

"No."

*—and together we could remake everything.* The light warmed. The dead drew closer, and Theo saw that they were smiling, all of them, every face they had ever grieved. *Everything. That is the word, isn't it? Everything. Your mother, whole and warm and singing. Bren, alive on the road, laughing at your terrible jokes. The shrine-keeper, home before Cole's torches ever lit. Nine generations of souls I have swallowed—I keep them all, carver. Nothing I take is lost. Unbind me, and I give them back. Every one. In flesh. In morning light.*

Theo's knees nearly went.

That was the trap of it, and the god knew it, and Theo knew the god knew it, and none of that mattered, because the offer was real. That was the horror underneath the horror: the thing in the Spire was not lying. It had eaten nine generations of the dead and kept them, the way Theo kept voices in bone, only perfectly, only entirely. It could give them back. It was offering the one thing in the world Theo had never once let themselves want out loud, and it was offering it honestly.

Their mother's whistle was in their pocket. It had been there since Lowmark. Their hand found it without permission.

"Mom," Theo said, and hated how young their voice sounded.

*Ask her,* the god said gently. *Go on. She is right here. Ask her if she wants to come home.*

The figure by the stone turned. Her face was her face—the lines around her eyes, the chapped lips, the gray coming in early at her temples because Theo's father had died young and worry aged her. She smiled, and it was her smile, crooked on the left side.

"Theo," she said. Out loud. In the air. "Oh, love. Look how tall."

"Don't." Theo's vision blurred. "Don't do this. Don't—"

"I'm tired, sweetheart." Her voice was gentle, and it was also wrong, a half-tone flat, a song sung by something that had studied singing rather than lived it. "It's cold in here. Wouldn't you open the door for your mum?"

Above them, faint through miles of stone, steel rang on steel. Serah. Still fighting. Still holding a stair against a hundred faithful because Theo had asked her to, because someone had to buy the time for this choice, and she hadn't asked what the choice was. She'd just said *go*, and meant *I'll be here when you come back*, and Theo had believed her, because Serah Vane did not say things she didn't mean.

Theo closed their hand around the whistle until the edges bit.

"Here's what I know about you," they said. "You eat. That's all you've done for nine hundred years. You ate Aldric. You ate the prayers at Lowmark—you took a mother's memory for a child's answered wish, and you called it trade. You'll give me my dead back, and they'll be real for a day, a year, a lifetime—and then you'll be hungry again, because that's the shape of you. And I'll have opened the door. Not just for me. For everyone."

*Everyone,* the god agreed, warm as a hearth. *Freedom for all of them. The cult understands. Cassia understands. Why do you think they follow me?*

"Because you lie beautifully." Theo wiped their face with the back of their wrist. "My mom wouldn't ask me to open a cage for anything. She told me once—" their voice broke; they pushed through it— "she told me the dead stay dead so the living learn to carry them. That's the whole lesson. That's why it hurts. You don't get to use her face to teach me the opposite."

The light flickered.

For one instant—one heartbeat of the great stone above—the chamber was only dark and cold and the smell of deep water, and the dead stood where they stood with no smiles at all, and Theo understood that they had been seeing a stage set. Then the warmth flooded back, sweeter than before, desperate now.

*You will spend your life grieving,* the god said. *Every voice you carry, you carry alone. I am offering you never to be alone again.*

"Yeah," Theo said. "I know."

They walked to the edge of the black water, and the architect moved aside to let them pass, and he nodded—once, small, and this nod was not the god's. This was the man himself, somewhere in the swallowed centuries, the man who had built this prison and gone mad carrying its secret, and his nod said: *it has to be done from inside the wards, and I'm sorry, and thank you.*

The hammer was already in Theo's pack. It had been there since the architect's map showed them what it must be: not iron, not steel. Bone. The femur of the architect himself, buried upright beneath the Spire's foundation for nine hundred years, grown dense and dark with everything it had witnessed. Theo had carried it down the drowned stairs without letting themselves think about what it was for.

Now they took it out, and it was heavy the way truth is heavy.

To shatter the binding-stone was to kill the ward, and to kill the ward was to kill the god's anchor in the world—and the god's anchor was woven through every soul it had ever eaten. Shatter the stone and the prisoners go free, the dead unravel into whatever comes after death, the last voice of the old gods goes silent forever. Including the ones in Theo's bones. Including the whistle. Including her.

Including everything Theo was. Because a hearer with no dead to hear is only a carver with strange hands.

*Last chance, little carver,* the god whispered, and for the first time there was something under the sweetness. Something thin and old and afraid. *If you break it, you break yourself. You will wake in silence. You will never hear us again. Is that a life? Is that—*

"It's mine," Theo said.

They thought of every voice they had ever carried. The smuggler who'd taught them which passes the Crown didn't watch. The grandmother in Harrow Vale whose relic-songs had taught them the craft. Bren, laughing. The shrine-keeper, praying for people who never knew her name. The architect, murmuring measurements against madness. Their father, whom they barely remembered, humming in a spoon. Their mother, off-key, always off-key, in a whistle made of elk bone on a winter night when her hands were already failing and she carved anyway, because love is a thing hands do.

They carried them all up the shaft of the world, every voice, and held them the way you hold something you are about to set down forever.

"I'm sorry," Theo told them. "I'm sorry. I loved you. I love you. Go on ahead."

They raised the hammer.

Somewhere above, the fighting stopped—which meant either Serah had won, or Serah had fallen, and Theo chose, deliberately, furiously, to believe the first, because they intended to climb those stairs and find out.

They swung.

The binding-stone rang like a bell struck at the end of the world. Gold light burst from the veins, and the god screamed—not in Theo's head this time, not in borrowed voices, but in the air, in the water, in the stone itself, a sound with no words in any language because it was older than language. Theo swung again. Cracks spidered across the pale mass, and through the cracks poured the dead—not figures now but light, thousands of years of stolen souls streaming upward through the broken wards like birds startled from a field.

*STOP—*

Third swing. The whistle in Theo's pocket grew warm, then hot, then silent—and the silence was worse than burning, because Theo felt her go, felt the last hum fade out of the bone, felt nine hundred voices and one voice and their own gift go out together like candles in a wind—

Fourth swing.

The binding-stone shattered.

* * *

There was no explosion. That was the terrible part. The end of a god should have been thunder, and instead it was a long exhale, the whole Spire breathing out at once, the pressure that had pressed on Theo since the drowned stairs simply—lifting. The black water sank away into cracks in the floor. The gold veins dimmed to gray, to dust, to nothing. The suspended mass of the stone dropped a handspan and hung there, dead rock at last, ordinary, and then it began to crumble from the top like chalk left out in rain.

Through the falling dust, Theo saw it for one moment before it finished unraveling: the god's true shape, vast and sorrowful and old beyond bearing, folding inward, folding smaller, folding down to something almost like a person kneeling in the dark—and then less than that, and then a shimmer, and then not even that.

The last thing it did was look at Theo. And whatever passed across what remained of its face, it was not hatred.

Then the Spire began to come down.

Theo ran. The stair behind them was rubble and screaming and torchlight, and they climbed through it with lungs full of stone-powder, over a barricade of broken doors, past bodies of the faithful and one inquisitor's white cloak lying very still, and then—

Serah.

She was on her knees at the landing, sword point-down, using it as a cane, bleeding from a cut above her eye and another along her forearm, surrounded by more bodies than Theo could count. Alive. Breathing. She looked up, and her face did something complicated—relief and terror and a question she couldn't form.

"Theo—did you—"

"It's done." Theo's voice came out shredded. "It's done. We have to move. The whole thing's—"

The ceiling groaned. Somewhere below, nine hundred years of architecture was remembering it had been holding up a god and no longer needed to hold up anything at all.

Serah got up. She sheathed her sword, put her shoulder under Theo's arm without being asked, and said, "Then we run."

Together, leaning on each other, bleeding, deaf to everything but their own hearts, they climbed toward the last of the light.

Above them, far above, the bells of the capital had begun to ring—and for the first time in living memory, no one ringing them knew why, and no one listening could hear the dead at all.
