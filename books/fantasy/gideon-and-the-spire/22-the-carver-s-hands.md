## 22. The Carver's Hands

The stone was river granite, pulled from the shallows below Lowmark where the water ran cold and clear over grey shoulders of rock. Theo had carried it up the hill themselves, arms burning, and set it on the trestle outside Maren's old workshop with a view of the whole valley: the terraced fields going gold at their edges, the shrine—rebuilt, twice the size now, its keeper a round-faced widow named Odile who prayed loudly and paid her debts in bread—the road winding down toward the lowlands where the world had decided, mostly, to go on.

Theo wet the chisel and began.

They worked without gloves. Their hands had changed in the months since the Spire; the calluses sat differently, the scars from the binding-stone's shattering had healed into pale ridges that caught the light like fish-scales. But the hands still knew their work. That was the mercy no one had warned them about—that the body would remember even when everything else went quiet.

The memorial was for Tam Vess, the smuggler who had died buying them time on the gorge road, shot through the throat by one of Cole's crossbows while he shouted directions to a path only he knew. His mother had come to Theo in early spring with a lump of raw granite and no money and an expression that made payment irrelevant. *He liked the river,* she'd said. *Put the river on it.*

So Theo carved the river.

It was slow work, honest work. The chisel sang against the stone—a real sound, made of air and force and nothing else—and Theo listened to it the way they had once listened to bones. Around them the morning assembled itself: smoke from Odile's chimney, a dog barking two farms over, the creak of the shrine gate as the first pilgrims of the day came up the hill to ask small favors of whatever remained to hear them.

Nothing remained. Theo had checked. Every day since the shattering, some part of them had checked—held a relic to their ear, pressed a palm to the rebuilt shrine's foundation, walked the graveyard at dusk with their head tilted like a bird's—and every day the answer was the same beautiful, terrible nothing. The dead were gone. Not sleeping, not silent, not waiting. Gone, the way a note is gone after it finishes, the way a road is gone behind you when you don't look back.

Some mornings that nothing felt like a wound. Some mornings it felt like a window opened in a stuffy room.

Today it felt like both, which Theo had learned was what peace actually sounded like.

* * *

Maren found them at midday, as she always did, carrying bread and opinions in equal measure.

"You've been out here since dawn." She set the loaf on the trestle beside the half-finished river and squinted at the carving. "That's good. That's better than good. The way you got the water to look like it's moving—" She shook her head. "You could charge triple for this."

"It's for Tam's mother."

"Which is why you won't." Maren sat down on the woodpile, which groaned but held; she was a broad woman built like the doorframes of her workshop, and she had taken Theo in during the worst weeks after the collapse, when the regency's investigators were still crawling through the Thornfell asking questions and Theo couldn't sleep in any room with a closed door. "Any word from the hearings?"

"Serah wrote last week. The Vellin case goes before the full panel next month."

"The shrine-keeper they burned. The innocent one."

"The one Serah refused to burn." Theo set down the chisel and flexed their fingers. "She's testifying. In front of the panel, in front of the families, in front of anyone who wants to come. No title, no office. Just her name and what she saw."

"And the regency allows this?"

"The regency *tolerates* it," Theo corrected. "There's a difference. Tolerance is what you give a thing you're afraid to kill because you're not sure it won't come back stronger."

Maren laughed, then sobered. "And you? When do you testify?"

"I don't." Theo picked the chisel back up. "I'm dead to the record books. Bone-witch, wanted, presumed fled east. Let them keep me that way. It's the safest I've ever been."

"You used to say invisibility was the safest you'd ever been," Maren said quietly. "Before all this."

Theo looked at the stone, at the river taking shape under their hands, water that would never move and never stop moving. "Yeah," they said. "Turns out being seen isn't so bad. If it's the right people doing the seeing."

* * *

The afternoon brought rain, soft and steady, and Theo worked under the awning while the valley steamed. They finished the river. They started the lettering—TAM VESS, SON OF THE THORNFELL, WHO KNEW THE ROADS—and was three letters into the second line when they heard hoofbeats on the switchback below.

One horse. Unhurried. Theo's hands did not stop moving, but something in their chest lifted and turned toward the sound like a plant toward sun, and they hated how easy it was, how transparent, how little the months had done to disguise it.

Serah Vane came around the last bend with rain running off her hood and her horse blowing steam, and she looked—different. Thinner, maybe. Or just rearranged, the way a house looks different once you've moved the furniture to fit a new life. She wore no colors. No badge. A plain oilskin cloak, a plain sword, and the same stubborn jaw that had once stared down an inquisitor's pyre and refused to light it.

She dismounted and stood in the rain looking at the memorial stone for a long moment.

"Tam Vess," she said.

"You remember him."

"He bought us forty seconds on the gorge road. I counted." She pushed back her hood. Her hair was shorter than Theo remembered. There was a new scar at her hairline, thin and white. "His mother wrote to me too. Asking if the stories were true. That someone was carving him a stone up here."

"The stories are true. The Crown's stories are also true, incidentally. Bone-witch, fled east. Very dramatic."

"Mm." Serah came under the awning, dripping, and stood close enough that Theo could smell wet wool and road dust and, underneath it, the particular iron-and-ink smell that was just *her*, unchanged by titles lost or gained. "I passed three of Cole's old warrants nailed to the tollhouse at Fennick's Crossing. Your face is getting better drawn. Someone finally captured the chin."

"They always get the chin wrong. It's my best feature."

"It is a feature." Serah smiled, then let it fade into something more careful. "Theo. I need to tell you why I'm here, and I need you to let me finish before you decide anything."

Theo set down the chisel. "That sounds ominous."

"It might be. It might be the opposite." She took a breath, and Theo watched her do the thing she did—square her shoulders, plant her feet, prepare to hold a position alone against whatever came up the stairs. They had watched her do it against Cassia's faithful. They had watched her do it against the regency's men. They had watched her do it, once, in the dark of the Spire's drowned stairway, holding back the world so that Theo could reach the binding-stone and break it.

"The panel confirmed the Vellin case yesterday," Serah said. "Full exoneration. Posthumous pardon for the shrine-keeper, reparations to the family, and a formal censure of Inquisitor Cole's methods—which is meaningless, because Cole is dead, but it's written down now. It's in the record. What happened at Lowmark is officially a thing that happened, not a thing that was necessary." Her voice stayed level, but her hands, Theo noticed, were fists. "And the regency, having admitted all that, offered me reinstatement. Full rank. Chief justiciar of the Thornfell provinces. They want me to rebuild the office from the inside. Make it what it should have been."

The rain filled the silence. Somewhere down the hill, the shrine bell rang, though nobody remembered anymore why bells rang at all—it was just a thing the kingdom did, like breathing, like carving names into stone.

"And you said no," Theo said.

"I burned the letter." Serah's mouth twisted. "Again. Second one. I'm collecting them."

"Why?"

"Because I rehearsed the answer. Both answers. All night, both nights. And every time I rehearsed saying yes, I heard myself explaining to the panel why the office I'd be rebuilding needed to exist at all—and I couldn't. I kept arriving at the same place. The law didn't fail Vellin because it was badly built. It failed because it was built to serve the crown, and the crown was hollow, and nobody inside the law was allowed to notice." She shook her head slowly. "Justice shouldn't live in a building, Theo. It should live in people who refuse to look away. So I'm going to keep standing witness. Hearings, testimonies, the ugly public work of making the Crown look at what it did. Without a badge. Without their permission, if it comes to that."

"That sounds lonely."

"It was," Serah said. "For about four days. And then I realized I was riding north every chance I got, and inventing reasons, and the reasons were getting worse—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "I realized the loneliness was a choice I was making, and I didn't have to make it."

Theo's heart was doing something unprofessional. "Serah—"

"Let me finish. You said let me finish."

"You haven't asked anything yet."

"No. Because the question is the hard part." She looked at the memorial stone—at TAM VESS, SON OF THE THORNFELL—and then at Theo, and her voice, when it came, had none of the courtroom in it. It was just a woman on a mountain in the rain, asking for something she couldn't command. "Where does the road go next? Yours. Because mine doesn't go anywhere without an answer to that."

* * *

Theo did not answer immediately. They turned back to the stone instead, because their hands needed something to do while the rest of them reorganized itself, and picked up the chisel, and cut the next letter. S. Then O. Then N.

Serah waited. She was good at waiting. It was one of the thousand things Theo had learned about her in the dark months—she could hold a position, physical or otherwise, long past the point where anyone sensible would have broken.

"I spent last winter listening to nothing," Theo said finally. "Did I tell you that? Every night. Bones, stones, the foundations of houses. Nothing. And I grieved it like a limb. Like I'd cut off my own arm to save the world, which—I suppose I did." The chisel moved. "But here's what I figured out around midwinter, carving Maren's doorframe. I never heard the living. Not once, not in thirty years. The gift was only ever half a sense. And the half I've got left—the ordinary half, eyes and ears and hands—I never used it properly. I spent my whole life hiding behind the other one."

"So you're learning to listen to the living."

"I'm terrible at it. I miss things. People say things and I'm waiting for the echo underneath and there isn't one, and I lose the actual words." Theo laughed—not bitterly, or trying not to be. "But I'm better than I was. Ask Maren. I noticed she was grieving her brother in the spring, before she said anything. From her *face*. Like a normal person."

Serah huffed something that was almost a laugh. "A triumph of the mundane."

"Don't mock the mundane. The mundane saved the world. The world-saving part was just a bunch of mundane things done in a row by tired people." Theo set the chisel down and turned to face her fully. Rain drummed the awning. Down in the valley, lights were coming on in windows, one by one, the ordinary constellations of people going about their evenings. "The road goes here for now. There's work. Half the graves on this mountain lost their markers in the flood two years back, and the families are asking, and I can carve. I can't hear them anymore, but it turns out that was never the point. The point was that somebody remembered them. Somebody put their name in stone."

"And after the stones?"

"After the stones?" Theo considered it honestly, the way Serah deserved. "After the stones, I don't know. Maybe the hearings. You'll need a witness who can read relics—there are thousands of them in evidence stores now, everything the cult hoarded, everything Cole seized. Somebody has to go through them and find out whose memories those are and get them home to their families. Not by hearing them. By *paperwork*. Provenance, records, family trees. God, it'll be awful. It'll take years."

Serah was smiling. She tried to hide it and failed. "You're describing a life."

"I'm describing a job."

"You're describing a job that requires two people, minimum, and one of them has to know the law well enough to fight the archivists, and there's exactly one disgraced justiciar in the kingdom qualified to—"

"All right, yes." Theo's ears were hot. Thirty years of hearing the dead and they still couldn't manage a simple sentence in the rain. "It requires two people. If—if the second person wanted it. If she wasn't needed somewhere more important. If she could stand the mountains, and the mud, and Maren's opinions, and—"

"Theo."

"—and a carver who talks to rocks now, just talks, out loud, like a madman, because the habit of narrating to the dead hasn't quite—"

"Theo." Serah crossed the space between them and took their face in both hands, rain-cold palms, steady as a keel. "Yes. Whatever the question is. Yes."

"Oh," Theo said. "Good. Because I hadn't actually gotten to the question yet."

"I know. I got impatient. It's a flaw."

"It's your best feature."

"My chin disagrees." And Serah kissed them, there under the awning with the rain coming down and the memorial stone half-finished between them and the whole valley lighting up below, and it was—Theo would remember this later, would carry it the way they had once carried voices—ordinary. Entirely, gloriously ordinary. Two people, alive, choosing each other with no god watching and no ghost listening and no price attached except the ordinary ones, which they would pay together, gladly, for as long as the road lasted.

* * *

They finished the stone the next morning, both of them, Serah holding the chisel steady for the final strokes while Theo guided her grip—her first lesson in carving, clumsy and laughing, her knuckles white with concentration. TAM VESS, SON OF THE THORNFELL, WHO KNEW THE ROADS. Beneath it, smaller, the line his mother had asked for:

HE LIKED THE RIVER.

They set it above the village cemetery in the afternoon sun, and Tam's mother wept and fed them until neither of them could move, and Odile from the shrine came up to look and pronounced it the finest work on the mountain, which it was.

And that evening, walking back up the hill side by side as the light went amber and then violet, Theo stopped at the crest where the whole Thornfell spread out below them—every road they had bled on, every shrine, every grave—and felt, for the first time in longer than they could measure, completely and simply present. One life. One pair of hands. One person walking beside them whose heartbeat they could not hear at all, and whose meaning they understood perfectly.

"Tomorrow," Serah said, "we start on the archive petitions."

"Tomorrow we start on the archive petitions," Theo agreed. "But tonight—"

"Tonight the road goes nowhere at all."

Below them, the first star came out over the Spire's distant grave, where the earth had settled smooth and green, and nothing stirred beneath it, and nothing ever would again. Theo watched it a moment—not with dread, not with grief, just with the respect owed to a finished story—and then turned for home.

The mountain road went on ahead of them, washed clean by rain, going wherever they decided to take it.
