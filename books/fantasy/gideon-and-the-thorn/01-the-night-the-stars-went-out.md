## 1. The Night the Stars Went Out

The night market of Vell opened at dusk and closed at dawn, and in between it sold everything the daylight city pretended not to want.

Kestrel Nakamura had worked its edges for seven years. He knew which lanterns burned true flame and which were glamoured paper, which vendors watered their wine and which watered their poisons, which alleys swallowed sound and which carried a whisper three streets to the wrong ears. He knew the market the way he had once known coastlines — intimately, obsessively, with the particular love of a man who maps things because he cannot afford to be surprised by them.

He kept his stall small and unremarkable: a folding table near the tanners' row, where the smell of hides covered the smell of what he actually sold. Charms. Small ones. A knotted cord that eased a child's fever. A pressed sprig of something that made a drunkard's hands steady for an hour. A sliver of river-glass that, held to the ear, let you hear your mother's voice one more time — or something shaped like it. Nothing that would rouse the Wardens. Nothing with teeth.

Vell was an anti-magical city. That was not a slogan but a structure, written into its charter and its gutters and the posture of every citizen who walked past his table without meeting his eyes. Magic had been scoured from Vell's stones two centuries ago, and the city wore its emptiness like a woman wears pearls — proudly, constantly, touching them to be sure they're still there. The border wards, great woven veils of nullity strung along the northern rim, kept the Thornlands out. Kept the thorn-witches out. Kept the old war out.

Kept men like Kestrel out, if they'd been honest about what they were.

He wasn't honest. He hadn't been honest since he was nineteen years old, when he'd stood over his mother's grave in a country that no longer appeared on any map and made himself a promise with two edges: never use it, never be found. Seven years in Vell had honed both edges smooth. He drew maps now — beautiful ones, licensed ones, sold through the Cartographers' Guild with wax seals and clean provenance — and he sold charms on the side because a man can bury a fire but the embers still warm him, and because there were people in the tanners' quarter whose fevers didn't care about charters.

"River-glass," said a voice at his elbow. "You still have it?"

Kestrel didn't look up from the charm he was knotting. "Sold out."

"You always say that."

"And I'm always lying." He slid the glass across the table under a fold of leather. The buyer was a dye-worker named Ossa, broad-shouldered, gray at the temples, with the careful hands of someone who handled color for a living. Her mother had died in the spring. She came every third week and paid too much and never asked where the voices came from, and Kestrel loved her a little for that discretion, the way you love a wall that holds.

Ossa pressed the glass to her ear, closed her eyes. Whatever she heard made her mouth tremble and then settle. She paid. She left. And Kestrel turned back to his knots and told himself, as he did every night, that this was nothing — a kindness, a commerce, a candle-end of a gift that would never light anything larger than itself.

Above the market, where the rooftops broke apart into open sky, the stars were out in full. Vell's skies were famously clear; the founders had built the city high and dry, above the fogs of the lowlands, and on autumn nights the constellations came down close enough to read by. Kestrel had charted them often. Old habit. A cartographer charts whatever is left to chart.

There was a constellation the old texts called the Thorn Crown, though no map printed in Vell dared name it so. It hung low over the northern rim, over the wards, a ring of seven white fires that sailors and exiles alike had used to find their way home for longer than either nation had existed. Kestrel checked it nightly without meaning to, the way a man checks a scar. Still there. Still burning.

That night, at the hour the market calls the deep bell — when the wine stalls do their best trade and the pickpockets do theirs — Kestrel felt the world flinch.

There was no sound. That was the terrible part, later, the part he could never make anyone understand. He had expected, all his life, that if the wards ever failed there would be a sound — a crack like the sky splitting, a thunderclap rolling off the mountains, some announcement worthy of the event. Instead there was only a sensation, brief and total, like the moment a bone goes wrong inside you: a wrongness felt before it is understood.

Every hair on his arms rose. The knot he was tying slipped apart in his fingers. And the ember he had banked for seven years — the thing he kept below thought, below name, folded small and dark in the place behind his sternum where his mother had once put her hand and said *this is yours, and it will be the death of you unless you starve it* — that ember *leaned*.

Toward the north. Toward the rim. Like a plant toward light. Like a dog toward a whistle only it can hear.

Kestrel went cold all over. Because magic in Vell was dead stone, drowned seed, ash — and yet his ember had just moved, which meant somewhere north the pressure that crushed it flat had lifted, which meant—

He looked up.

The sky was empty.

Not clouded. Not fogged. *Empty.* From horizon to horizon, every star — gone. As if a hand had passed over the world and wiped it clean. The Thorn Crown first — he would have sworn it went first, the seven white fires snuffed in sequence like candles pinched by a finger — and then everything else, all of it, the whole burning archive of the night, erased between one heartbeat and the next.

The market did not notice at once. Markets rarely do; they are too busy being markets. But then a wine-seller looked up mid-pour and stopped pouring, red running over his own hand and onto the cobbles, staring. A child pointed. Someone screamed — one short, torn note — and then another, and then the sound moved through the tanners' row like fire through dry grass, and the whole night market of Vell looked up together into a sky with nothing in it.

For a moment nobody moved. Three thousand people, motionless, heads back, mouths open, in the oldest collective silence Kestrel had ever witnessed. He understood, in that stretched instant, that he was watching a city lose its innocence — that every soul here had grown up believing the stars were permanent, the way they believed the wards were permanent, the way they believed the war was over — and that belief, tonight, had died of a single look upward.

Then the screaming started in earnest, and the market came apart.

Stalls overturned. The crowd packed itself toward the gates and jammed there, a thousand bodies pressing, someone falling, someone trampled. Bells began to ring from the Warden towers — not the measured alarm of drills but a ragged, overlapping clangor, tower answering tower, the city's iron voice cracking with panic. Somewhere a crier was shouting curfew, curfew, return to your homes, and being swept away by the very crowd he addressed.

Kestrel stood at his little table in the middle of the apocalypse and did the one thing he had trained himself never to do in public: he reached for his magic.

Just a touch. Just enough to listen. He let the ember rise a finger's width behind his breastbone and *listened* with it, the way his mother had taught him before he had words — and what he heard made his knees nearly buckle.

The wards weren't broken. Broken wards rang like struck glass; he'd have felt the shatter in his teeth. These were *unmade*. Unwoven. Thread by thread by thread, with patience, with knowledge, with a hand that knew exactly which knots to pull. Someone had taken Vell's great veil apart the way you'd take apart a loom — carefully, lovingly, from the inside.

And the ember in his chest knew the technique. Recognized it the way you recognize a handwriting. Thorn-work. His mother's craft. His mother's *school*. Which was impossible, because everyone who practiced it was supposed to be dead, scattered, or exiled beyond the rim — and yet the evidence was singing against his ribs like a plucked string.

His past had not merely survived. It had come looking for him.

"*Move,*" said a voice, and a body slammed into him, and Kestrel came back to himself in the middle of a stampede. He grabbed his satchel — the charms, the stock, the evidence, gods, the *evidence*, a table full of contraband sitting in plain view while the Wardens swept the district — and ran.

He knew the ways out. Of course he knew the ways out; mapping escape routes was half his trade and all his vice. Left off the tanners' row, through the gap between the glue-works and the wall, down the stair that smelled of fat and lime, into the drain-cut where the crowd couldn't follow. He ran with the satchel banging his hip and the starless sky glaring down at him like the inside of a closed eye, and behind him the bells went on ringing, and ahead of him, at the mouth of the alley —

Lanterns. White ones. Warden-white.

Three of them, moving against the flow of the fleeing crowd, unhurried, disciplined, sweeping doorways with long poles tipped in null-iron. Checking for exactly the kind of thing Kestrel carried. Exactly the kind of thing Kestrel *was*.

He pressed himself into a doorway behind a stack of empty barrels and made himself small, made himself quiet, made himself — with an effort that cost him more than the running had — ordinary. Through a gap in the barrels he watched the patrol come on. Two men and a woman, armor dulled, faces set in the grim professional calm of people who had trained for riots and gotten a miracle instead. The tall one carried the null-iron pole, and as it swung past each doorway Kestrel saw the metal shiver faintly, tasting the air for anything that shouldn't be.

Anything alive, in a city that was supposed to be empty of alive.

They were ten paces away when the satchel shifted against his hip. One of the river-glass charms — cracked in the crush, poorly made, rushed — began, very softly, to sing. His mother's voice. A lullaby in a language banned for two hundred years.

Kestrel's blood turned to ice water. He got his hand around the charm, cupped it, smothered it against his palm — but the tall Warden had already turned his head. Stopped. Raised a hand, and the other two halted, and the null-iron pole swung around and pointed, trembling, at the stack of barrels.

At him.

Kestrel closed his eyes. Behind his sternum the ember rose unbidden, hot and terrified, begging to be used — a flash of light, a twist of luck, a misdirection woven quick and dirty, the kind of working that would save his skin and burn a signature into the air that every sensitive within a mile would feel. *Starve it,* his mother had said. *Or it will spend you.*

He chose the barrels instead. He threw his shoulder into the stack and shoved, and forty pounds of staves and hoops went over into the alley with a crash that echoed off both walls, and in the ringing chaos of it — the Wardens shouting, the crowd beyond screaming about the sky — Kestrel went over the back wall of the doorway, up a drain-spout he'd mapped years ago and never thanked himself for, onto the roof, and away.

He ran across the tops of Vell until his lungs burned, vaulting the gaps between buildings in a city gone mad beneath him, and the whole time the sky pressed down, black and absolute, without one single star to steer by. Below, fires were starting — panic-fires, small and scattered, and the bells would not stop, and somewhere a herald was already crying the Council's first lie into the dark: *thorn-witches, thorn-witches, the thorn-witches have breached the veil.*

Kestrel finally dropped into the yard of a shuttered chandlery two districts south, and leaned against the wall, and shook.

After a while he opened the satchel and took inventory by feel. Most of the stock had survived. The cracked river-glass had split entirely; he wrapped the pieces and buried them in the yard's muck, heart hammering the whole time. And at the bottom of the bag, wrapped in oilcloth, he found the one thing he'd forgotten he carried — the one thing he had never been able to bring himself to sell or burn.

A compass. Brass-cased, needleless, older than his time in Vell. It didn't point north. It had never pointed north. On the night his mother died she had pressed it into his hands and told him it pointed toward *what remains*, and for seven years in Vell it had hung in his satchel pointing stubbornly, uselessly, toward the northern rim — toward the wards, toward the Thornlands, toward everything he had crossed a border and buried a name to escape.

Tonight the compass was shaking. The invisible needle strained against nothing, quivering, *yearning*, aimed hard at the north like a dog at a door.

And along the brass rim, where for seven years there had been only blank metal, fine characters had surfaced — risen like bruises, like writing under skin — in a hand Kestrel knew better than his own.

His mother's hand. Four words, scratched small and urgent, decades old and fresh as blood:

*It remembers. Come home.*

Kestrel sat down in the dark of the chandlery yard, under a sky with no stars in it, and read the words until he had them by heart, and understood that the life he had built — the licenses, the aliases, the seven careful years — was already ash.

Whatever had unmade the wards knew his name.
