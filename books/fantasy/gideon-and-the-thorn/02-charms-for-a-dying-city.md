## 2. Charms for a Dying City

Kestrel woke to the sound of hammers.

Not the ordinary morning percussion of Vell — the cobbler's mallet, the smith's rhythm, the endless municipal tapping of men repairing what rain and time had loosened. This was different: deliberate, heavy, iron on stone in a pattern he knew too well from his years mapping border towns. They were driving ward-stakes into the streets.

He lay in his narrow bed above the map shop and listened to the city arm itself against a sky that had betrayed it.

The ceiling had a water stain shaped like the Thornlands, an accident of plumbing he'd never corrected because correcting it would have meant looking at it. He looked at it now. The stain's ragged eastern edge seemed darker than yesterday, though that was impossible. Everything was impossible since last night. Every star in the sky had gone out at once, like candles pinched by a single enormous hand, and Kestrel Nakamura — disgraced cartographer, seller of small illegal magics, keeper of a buried name — was the only man in Vell who understood why.

Or thought he did. Or feared he did. The three had become difficult to separate somewhere around the third hour of lying awake with his heart hammering out a message in old Thorn-cant: *they know, they know, they know.*

He got up, dressed in the dark, and went down to the shop.

* * *

The morning light through the shutters was wrong. He'd spent seven years learning every quality of light that came through those slats — the honeyed gold of high summer, the pewter wash of winter, the green flicker when the glassmaker across the lane fired his kilns. This morning's light was gray without being overcast, flat without being dim, the color of ash stirred into milk. Starless nights bled into starless days. The sun still rose; he could see its dull disc through the shutter-gap, a coin of tarnished silver. But the world had lost its shine, as if someone had wiped the varnish off everything.

He opened the front door to fetch the milk bottle and found the street already watching him.

Old Marta from the dyehouse stood at her step with her arms folded. The Ferro twins paused mid-argument on their corner. Down at the pump, a knot of neighbors turned — not all at once, not obviously, but with that slow collective rotation of heads that is somehow worse than any single stare. Tam Wicke, who had bought a sleep-charm from Kestrel not three weeks ago to quiet his wife's night terrors, looked at him for one beat too long before turning back to the pump handle.

"Curfew's called," Marta said. Her voice was carefully neutral, which in Vell was its own kind of accusation. "Sundown to sunup. Wardens posted at every corner. You heard?"

"I heard."

"They say the wards didn't break." She said it the way people say *they say* when they mean *I want you to tell me it isn't so.* "They say the wards were *unmade.* From inside."

Kestrel kept his face doing what faces in Vell were trained to do: nothing. Seven years of practice. "Wards don't unmake themselves," he said, and it was true, and it was also the most dangerous sentence he'd spoken aloud in half a decade, and Marta's eyes narrowed a fraction, and he cursed himself with every curse in two languages.

"No," she agreed softly. "They don't."

She went inside. The street resumed its business around him like water closing over a dropped stone, but the current had changed. He could feel it against his skin. These were people who had bought warmth-charms and luck-knots and little folded paper birds that sang when you pressed their wings — people who had been, if not his friends, then his customers, his neighbors, his camouflage. A man known for selling charms was merely eccentric. A man known for selling charms on the morning after magic failed the entire city was something else entirely. He was a thread sticking up from the weave, and threads that stuck up got pulled.

He brought in the milk, locked the door, and threw the bolt.

* * *

By noon the proclamation had gone up on the notice board outside the chandlery, pasted there by a Warden clerk with the brisk efficiency of a man who expected to be pelted. Kestrel watched from his window rather than brave the street.

BY ORDER OF THE COUNCIL OF VELL:

A CURFEW IS HEREBY IMPOSED UPON ALL PERSONS FROM SUNDOWN UNTIL SUNUP. ALL CITIZENS SHALL REMAIN INDOORS. GATHERINGS OF MORE THAN THREE PERSONS ARE FORBIDDEN. THE SALE, BARTER, OR POSSESSION OF CHARMWORK, TALISMANS, OR ANY ARTICLE OF UNLICENSED MAGIC IS HEREBY A CAPITAL OFFENSE.

ANY PERSON HAVING KNOWLEDGE OF SUCH ARTICLES OR THEIR PURVEYORS SHALL REPORT TO THE NEAREST WARDEN POST. REWARD OFFERED. ANONYMITY GUARANTEED.

*Capital offense.* He read it twice. Last spring, charm-selling had cost a fine and a public shaming. The city's fear had grown teeth overnight, and teeth meant blood, and blood meant panic feeding on itself. Below the proclamation, someone had already chalked a crude drawing: a thorned branch, and beneath it, *THE THORN-WITCHES DID THIS.* Someone else had crossed it out. Someone else had written *BURN THEM ALL* beside it. Three hands, three arguments, all in chalk that rain would erase but fear would not.

Kestrel sat down at his worktable among his maps and his inks and his beautiful, careful, useless precision, and made the coldest calculation of his life.

Every charm in this room was now evidence. Not just contraband — evidence. Each one carried traces of his working, and his working carried traces of a lineage no one in Vell was supposed to possess. If the Wardens swept the street, if Tam Wicke or Marta or anyone traded his name for the Council's reward, the charms would tell them things about him that even he didn't fully understand. He had spent seven years burying what he was under layers of ordinariness, and last night the ground over the grave had shaken.

He built the fire in the back room high and hot.

It took most of the afternoon. The luck-knots went first, forty of them, braided horsehair that curled and blackened and let go of their small held breaths with a sound like sighing. Then the warmth-stones, which cracked in the heat and released their stored summer in brief golden flares that made him flinch each time — little deaths of comfort in a city that would have none. The singing birds burned fastest, paper and spellwork together, and for one terrible moment the fire sang with three voices at once before the magic guttered out. He fed the flames until his eyes streamed and his throat rawed and the last of his stock was ash.

He kept one thing.

He told himself it was practicality. A cartographer alone in a destabilizing city needed a compass; that was simply fact. But his hands knew better than his reasons, and they had set the compass aside before the first knot ever touched flame, the way a man saves one photograph from a house fire without deciding to.

It was a small thing, brass-cased, older than anything else he owned — older than his exile, older than his name-change, older than the war's ending that everyone pretended had ended anything. His mother had carried it. It had come to him in a bundle of effects delivered by a stranger who never gave his name, on the day Kestrel turned fourteen, along with a note in a hand he'd know anywhere: *Keep it pointed at what matters.*

In Vell, compasses pointed north. That was the law of them, enforced by guild charter and Warden inspection; a compass that wavered was recalibrated or destroyed. For seven years Kestrel's compass had pointed north, obediently, dully, because he had warded it himself to do so, smothering whatever it originally remembered under a blanket of civic compliance.

This morning, when he took it from the drawer where it had slept untouched for a year, the needle was trembling.

Not pointing north. Not pointing anywhere near north. It swung slowly, deliberately, with the sureness of a bloodhound catching scent, until it settled toward the southeast — toward the river district, toward the old quarter, toward the sealed gates beneath the city that appeared on no map Kestrel had ever drawn or sold. And it wasn't pointing at a direction at all. He'd learned enough of his mother's craft, in the years before, to feel the difference. A compass points at a place. This was pointing at a *presence.* Something alive. Something that had woken up.

Last night, when the stars went out, the compass had felt it too. He was certain of that now. Whatever slept beneath Vell — whatever his mother had died keeping secret, if Maren's rumors and Brammel's whispers and his own worst nightmares were to be believed — had stirred when the wards fell, and the needle had turned toward it like a flower toward a light that wasn't there anymore.

He should burn it. Every rational instinct said burn it, melt the brass, scatter the ashes in the canal. It was the single most incriminating object in his possession, a piece of Thorn-craft disguised as guild-standard toolwork, and possession was now a capital offense, and he was already the most suspicious man on his street.

He closed his hand around it instead. The brass was warm. Compasses didn't get warm.

"Not yet," he told it, and hated how much his voice shook.

* * *

Evening came early, or seemed to. Without stars the dark had no gradations; it arrived all at once, total, like a lid coming down. At the first bell of curfew Kestrel stood at his window and watched the city transform.

Wardens moved through the streets in pairs, lanterns hooded, halberds shouldered. From the rooftops he could see the new ward-stakes driven at every intersection, each crowned with a dull gray crystal that drank the lamplight and gave nothing back. Null-crystals. Vell hadn't manufactured them since the war. Somewhere in the Council vaults they had waited seventy years for a night like this, and now they bloomed on every corner like iron flowers, and the air around them tasted faintly of copper and dead things.

A patrol passed beneath his window. He stepped back from the gap in the shutters, heart ticking, and heard them stop almost directly below.

"—third house tonight with charm-scraps in the grate," one was saying. Voices carry in starless streets; sound has nothing left to bounce off but itself. "Burned them. Whoever it was got spooked fast."

"Half the street's spooked. Half the street's *guilty,* more like. Wicke says the mapmaker—"

Kestrel stopped breathing.

"—the mapmaker had drawers full of the stuff, everybody knew it. Bought a luck-knot off him myself, year back. Never worked anyway."

Laughter, short and nervous. Boots moving on. The lantern-glow slid across the shutter-gap and away.

Kestrel sat down on the floor with his back against the bedframe and put his head in his hands and laughed too, once, a dry horrible sound with nothing in it. *Never worked anyway.* That was his epitaph, then, if the Wardens wrote it: a purveyor of defective magic, caught between the city's fear and his neighbors' ingratitude. Tam Wicke had sold his name for the price of a reward he probably wouldn't live to collect, and had done it casually, between sentences, the way you'd mention weather.

He had known this day would come. He had known it for seven years, the way a man with a cracked foundation knows the next earthquake will finish the house. He had chosen invisibility — the smallest possible life, the flattest possible name, a trade so meticulous and harmless that no one would ever look twice. And it had worked. It had worked beautifully right up until the night the sky itself reached down and tore his cover story off the face of the world.

Because here was the truth he couldn't burn, couldn't bury, couldn't map his way around: the wards hadn't broken. He had *felt* them unmake, felt the specific unraveling signature of a working undone from within, and only three kinds of hands could have done it — Warden archivists with the founders' keys, the exiled Thorn remnant everyone believed extinct, or a Thorn heir. And there was exactly one Thorn heir left alive, and he was sitting on the floor of a map shop in Vell with a warm brass compass in his fist and his neighbor's betrayal cooling in the air outside.

Which meant that sooner or later — soon, now, very soon — someone was going to come to this door. Wardens with questions, or exiles with kneeling and terrible reverence, or both. The compass needle trembled against its glass, insistent, patient, pointing southeast toward something alive beneath the city, and Kestrel understood with sudden perfect clarity that the choice he'd been deferring for seven years had just walked up to his door and knocked.

The knock, when it actually came, nearly stopped his heart.

Three raps. Measured. Unhurried. The knock of someone who knew he was home and knew he'd heard and was willing to wait.

Kestrel rose in the dark, compass clutched to his chest, needle straining toward the door — toward whoever stood behind it — and through the frosted pane of the transom he could see the silhouette of a figure in a Warden's coat, and the pale gleam of a badge, and the unmistakable outline of a woman who had once, four years ago, followed one of his maps out of a collapsing mine shaft with her whole squad alive behind her.

"Sable Ives," he whispered, and the word *traitor* and the word *gratitude* fought each other in his throat, and lost, both of them, to plain animal fear.

The knock came again. Softer this time. Almost kind.

"Master Nakamura," said the voice of Warden Sable Ives through the door, in the tone of a woman who had already read his file twice. "Open the door. We need to talk about a deed you signed seven years ago."
