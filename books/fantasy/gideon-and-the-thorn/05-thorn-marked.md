## 5. Thorn-Marked

The undercity smelled of rust and old water, and Kestrel had been walking in it for two hours when he realized he was being followed.

He'd told himself the compass was leading him deeper for good reason — that whatever his mother's message meant by *the door remembers*, it would be found below the sealed gate, not above it. But the truth was simpler and worse: he hadn't been able to stop. The words scratched into the iron — *if you are reading this, little star, then I failed to keep you small* — had lodged in him like a splinter working its way toward the heart. He needed to know what she had done down here. What she had been. Who *little star* was supposed to become.

So he followed the compass's faint pull through tunnels where the lamplighters' rounds had never reached, past cisterns black as spilled ink, along a maintenance gallery whose brickwork predated Vell itself. The charm sat warm in his palm, its needle trembling not northward but inward, toward something alive.

That should have warned him. Nothing alive lived down here. Not officially.

The attack came at a place where the tunnel narrowed between two fallen arches, and it came from everywhere at once.

A net of woven wire dropped over him first — no, not wire. He realized with a lurch that it was *vine*, black and thorned, moving with the slow muscular patience of something growing rather than thrown. It cinched his arms to his sides before he could raise them. Then boots, many boots, striking the wet stone in unison, and lantern-light bloomed from four directions, and figures stepped out of the dark wearing gray travel cloaks and hoods pulled low.

Kestrel did the arithmetic instantly and hated the answer: six of them, exits blocked on three sides, the fourth opening behind them filled with more shadows than shadows should hold. His knife was in his boot. His magic was buried so deep beneath seven years of careful nothing that he sometimes convinced himself it wasn't there at all.

"Wait," he said, because it was the only word left. "I'm not— I'm just a mapmaker."

"We know what you are," said the one directly ahead.

The voice was a woman's, roughened by years of smoke or shouting. She pushed back her hood, and Kestrel saw her face first — weathered, sharp-boned, maybe fifty — and then saw the rest, and forgot how to breathe.

Her forearm, bare where the cloak fell back, was tattooed from wrist to elbow in a climbing briar. Except it wasn't a tattoo. Even in the bad light he could see it move — a slow peristalsis beneath the skin, thorns budding and browning and budding again, green flickering at the tips like a pulse. Living ink. Living *thorn*.

He knew the mark. Every child in Vell knew it, though none living had seen it outside of propaganda woodcuts: the sign of the Thornlands, of the exiled orders, of the war that ended when the wards went up and the border closed and an entire country became a story mothers used to frighten children into eating their supper.

*Thorn-witch,* the woodcuts said. *Thorn-curse. Thorn-rot.*

Six of them now stood in a half-circle around him, and every one bore the mark somewhere visible — wrist, throat, the back of a hand — and every mark moved.

"You've got the wrong man," Kestrel said, and heard how thin his own voice came out. "I sell charms. Cheap ones. Half of them don't work."

"The compass works," the woman said.

His hand, still half-curled around the charm, went cold. He hadn't shown it to anyone. Hadn't taken it out since the gate.

"How do you—"

"It called us." She crouched, bringing her face level with his, and up close he could see the exhaustion carved into her, the kind that doesn't come from one bad night but from years of them. "It's been calling since the stars went out. A needle pointing at the last living thing in this city. We felt it like a shout across a quiet room. And then tonight it stopped pointing at the tree—" her eyes flicked to the vine binding him, almost gently, "—and started pointing at *you*. Because you touched it. Because it recognized you."

"I bought it at market. From a peddler. It's a trinket—"

"Kestrel." Her voice changed. Something older came into it, something that made the tunnel feel suddenly smaller. "Open your hand."

"No."

"*Little star.*"

The world tilted.

Not the floor — the floor stayed exactly where it was, wet stone and dead air. It was everything else that turned over: seven years of careful anonymity, the forged guild papers, the accent scrubbed of the borderlands, the name Nakamura worn smooth as river glass until even he half-believed it was only a name. All of it balanced on the assumption that no one alive remembered the other thing he'd been called. That his mother had died alone in a Warden cell and taken the last of it with her.

"Who told you that?" he whispered.

"A dying woman," said a new voice, deep and unhurried, from the mouth of the fourth tunnel. "Twenty-two years ago. In a cellar in Harrow's Ford, while the Wardens burned the district above her."

The man who stepped into the lantern-light was enormous — not tall so much as *built*, like something quarried rather than born — with a gray beard cropped close and a face that might once have been handsome before someone had rearranged it. Across his left cheek ran the thorn-mark, thick as a rope, its barbs shifting slowly like something dreaming.

"Brammel," the woman said, by way of introduction, and the six exiles did something Kestrel would replay in his mind for weeks afterward.

They knelt.

All six, in the filth and standing water of a sewer gallery beneath the proudest anti-magical city in the world, they went down on one knee and bowed their heads, and the thorns on their arms brightened — actually brightened, green light seeping up through skin — and the leader of them said, in a voice pitched low enough that it seemed less like speech than like a ritual remembered:

"Heir of the Thorn line. Blood of Gideon's blood. We were told you'd come, and we didn't believe it, and here you are anyway."

"Get up," Kestrel said. The vine loosened — he felt it slacken against his ribs — and he barely noticed, because his legs had gone strange beneath him. "Get *up*. You've made a mistake. There's been some kind of— I'm a cartographer. I draw maps. That's all I am. That's all I've ever—"

"Sit down before you fall down," Brammel said, not unkindly, and gestured, and one of the kneeling exiles rose and dragged a crate from the shadows for him.

Kestrel sat. It was that or the water.

* * *

They gave him tea, which was absurd, and he drank it, which was more absurd still — some part of his brain noting distantly that he was accepting a hot drink from strangers who had just ambushed him in a sewer, and a larger part of his brain answering that these strangers had called him by a name no one living should know, and that the tea was therefore the least strange thing happening.

Brammel settled onto a broken pillar across from him, elbows on knees, and studied him for a long moment before speaking.

"Do you know why the stars went out?"

"Everyone has a theory," Kestrel said. "The Council says sabotage. The priests say judgment. The taverns say both."

"The Council is lying and the priests are accidentally right." Brammel turned his marked cheek toward the lantern-light, and Kestrel saw the thorns there shift, quickening, as if the mention of it woke them. "You've seen the borderlands. You mapped them, once. Before."

Kestrel's stomach dropped. "I don't know what you're talking about."

"Seven years ago you sold a survey map of the eastern marches to a buyer in the archive district. You took payment in silver and asked no questions, because asking questions is how cartographers in Vell end up in the canal." Brammel's tone carried no accusation; it carried something worse, which was understanding. "We bought a copy. We've studied it for seven years. Your hand is distinctive, little star. You draw rivers like veins."

"That map was routine. Guild survey work. There was nothing in it about—"

"About the heart-tree?" For the first time, Brammel smiled, and it was a terrible thing, full of grief. "No. There wouldn't be. You drew what your guild masters let you draw. But you drew *accurately*, because you can't help yourself — I've read your field notes, boy, you have the disease of precision — and accurate maps show absences. You charted the Thornlands' edge and marked the forest there as *sparse canopy, declining*. Declining. Seven years ago it was already dying, and you wrote it down without knowing what you were writing, and we read it and understood that we had very little time left."

"Dying," Kestrel repeated. The word felt foreign in his mouth, a coin from another country. "What's dying?"

Brammel looked at him for a long time. Around the gallery, the other exiles had risen and drifted closer, forming a loose ring, and their marks glowed faintly in the dark, and Kestrel understood suddenly that this was not intimidation. This was ceremony. This was how they gathered when the news was too large for one voice.

"The Thornlands are not a country," Brammel said. "That was always the lie Vell told — that beyond the wards there was a rival nation, an army, a threat. There was never an army. There was a *tree*. One tree, older than either land, older than the names we call things. Its roots run under the whole of the east — under our forests, under your city's foundations, under the border itself. Everything green in the Thornlands grew from it. Every spring, it flowered. And every night—" he paused, and his voice roughened, "—every night, its leaves caught the light of the day and held it, and lifted it into the sky, and that is what stars are. Or were. Stars are leaves, Kestrel. They always were. Your astronomers spent three hundred years measuring the branches of a tree and calling it heaven."

Kestrel laughed. It came out wrong, cracked in the middle. "That's— no. That's a fable. My mother told me stories like that when I was small, before I understood they were just—"

He stopped.

His mother. Scratched letters inside a sealed iron gate. *If you are reading this, little star.*

The tea went tasteless in his mouth.

"She told you true," Brammel said quietly. "She was of the Thorn line — the oldest of the guardian families, the ones sworn to the tree since before Vell existed. When the founders built the wards, they didn't just wall out the Thornlands. They *tapped* it. The wards that keep magic out of Vell drink power to run, boy. Magic doesn't come from nowhere. It comes from the tree. Three hundred years of gentle siphoning, and then, forty years ago, the founders' descendants got greedy — the wards were failing, the city expanding, and they cut deeper. They poisoned the root-system with a rite of severance. Slow. Deniable. Designed to look like natural decline."

"And the stars—"

"Went out leaf by leaf. The far constellations first. Then the nearer ones. Until the night the last ward-anchor tore loose, and the tree, in its death-throes, dropped everything it had left." Brammel spread his hands. "You watched it happen from your night market. So did we. The whole sky going dark at once isn't a curse or a judgment. It's a tree losing its crown."

The silence afterward was total. Even the water dripping somewhere in the dark seemed to hold itself back.

Kestrel set down the cup. His hands were shaking, and he watched them shake with a strange detachment, as if they belonged to a drawing he'd made of himself long ago and never gotten quite right.

"My mother died in a Warden cell," he said. "When I was nine. They said she was a spy."

"She was a guardian. She came to Vell to plead with the founders — the last of the line to try diplomacy. They arrested her. She expected it. What she didn't expect was that she was already carrying you." Brammel's gaze didn't waver. "Your father was Vellan. A surveyor. She hid what you were, raised you on stories disguised as stories, and when the Wardens finally took her, she had already smuggled you out to friends in the guild. She spent her last year arranging your invisibility. Forged papers. A new name. A life so dull no one would ever look twice at it."

"She never told me."

"She was protecting you. The heir of the Thorn line, living inside Vell? If the founders had known, they'd have killed you before you could walk. If the exiles had known—" he gestured at the ring of marked faces, "—we'd have put you on an altar of expectations you couldn't survive. She chose the cruelest kindness available. She made you nobody."

*Nobody.* Kestrel thought of the burned charms, the scrubbed accent, the forged guild seal, the seven years of being precisely, perfectly unremarkable. He had thought he'd built that life. It had been built *for* him, brick by brick, by a dying woman who loved him enough to erase him.

"Why now?" he asked. His voice came out hoarse. "Why tell me now? Why does any of this matter if the tree's already—"

"Because it isn't dead." The woman who had spoken first — Brammel called her Vess, later — stepped forward and held out her marked arm, and in the lantern-light Kestrel saw that at the crook of her elbow, among the black thorns, one tendril burned steady green. "Every exile carries a cutting of the tree in their blood. As long as one of us lives, it lives. And as long as it lives, it can be saved. But the window is closing. The Council means to re-seal the border — permanently, this time, with a rite that will finish what the founders started. When they do, the tree dies, the Thornlands die, and everyone trapped outside the walls when the wards went up dies with it. Thousands. Farmers, children, people who never held a sword in their lives."

"So we need you," said a younger exile, a wiry man with the mark coiling up his neck, and there was no ceremony in his voice at all, only hunger. "The old gates answer to Thorn-blood. The wards, the seals, the rites — all of it keyed to the guardian line. You're the key, heir. The only one left."

"I'm not," Kestrel said. "I don't have— I've never done anything. I hide charms in my coat and I draw maps and I've spent my whole life making sure nothing in me was *anything*—"

"Show him, Vess," Brammel said.

Vess knelt in front of him and took his hand — he was too stunned to resist — and pressed his palm flat against the green tendril on her arm.

The world *sang*.

There was no other word for it. Light poured up through his fingers, green-gold, and the vine-net that had bound him earlier stirred from the ground and reached toward him like a dog toward its master, and every thorn-mark in the gallery flared bright enough to throw shadows, and underneath it all he felt — vast, patient, agonized — a presence the size of a country turning its attention toward him. Toward *him*. Recognizing him.

He ripped his hand back. The light died. The exiles were staring at him with expressions he couldn't bear to catalog — hope, mostly, which was the worst one.

"I felt it," he whispered. "What was— what did I just feel?"

"The tree," Brammel said. "Feeling *you*. First contact in twenty-two years." He rose, and his shadow fell long across the flooded floor. "You can walk away, little star. Go home, burn the compass, keep being nobody. We won't stop you — your mother bought that right for you with her life. But understand what you'd be choosing. The Council moves within days. After the sealing, there is no tree, no Thornlands, no stars, ever again. And you'll spend the rest of your ordinary life knowing that the one person who could have opened the door chose to keep it shut."

Kestrel looked down at his palm. It still tingled. In the lines of it, faint as watermark, he could almost believe he saw green.

Above them, muffled through thirty feet of stone and brick, a bell began to toll — the curfew bell, third watch, the sound of a city locking itself down against a darkness it didn't understand. Somewhere up there, Wardens were hunting thorn-marked remnant on the Council's orders. Somewhere up there, Sable Ives was probably doing the hunting.

And somewhere below, an iron gate waited with his mother's handwriting on the inside of it.

"When do we leave?" Kestrel heard himself say.

Brammel smiled again — the same terrible, grieving smile — and behind him, six thorned arms rose in salute, green light washing the tunnel walls like dawn through leaves.

"Tonight," he said. "Before the Wardens trace the compass's song. Because they will, little star. They always follow the light."
