## 10. Maren's Ledger

The cell smelled of lye and old iron, and Maren Voss had spent three days learning its geography by touch.

They had taken her spectacles first — of course they had, the Wardens were thorough if nothing else — and so the world beyond arm's length was a watercolor blur, all gray shapes and suggestion. She had catalogued the cell the way she catalogued everything: two walls of dressed granite, one of older, rougher stone that predated the city above it, a drain in the floor that had not been cleaned in a decade. A bench bolted at an angle that suggested the building had settled. Somewhere beyond the door, a guard shifted his weight every quarter hour, favoring his left knee. An old injury. A man who had been a soldier before he was a jailer.

Maren had been an archivist for thirty-one years, and she had learned long ago that everything was a record if you paid attention. The lichen in the corner told her the cell flooded in wet seasons. The scratch marks low on the wall, in a child's alphabet, told her this had not always been a Warden holding cell — that Vell had repurposed something older, the way it repurposed everything, the way it had repurposed a poisoned forest into a border and called the theft *order*.

She was sitting on the bench, hands folded, composing her third mental draft of a confession she had no intention of giving, when the lock turned and the blur in the doorway resolved into a tall woman in a Warden's coat with her hood pushed back and her face set like a woman arriving at a decision she had already made and hated.

"You," Maren said. "The Ives woman."

"Archivist Maren Voss." Sable Ives closed the door behind her. She did not sit. "You've been in Warden custody for three days without charge. Your name appears in no log."

"Because my name appears in no ledger worth finding." Maren smiled without warmth. "That was rather the point of my life's work."

"Your life's work," Sable said, "is a ledger. I've seen it. Or I've seen the parts of it that survived the fire in your archive office, which was very conveniently set the night after you were taken."

Maren's smile faded. She had heard the smoke from her cell — had known, with a grief she had not permitted herself to show, that thirty-one years of careful scholarship was burning. "Copies exist," she said quietly.

"Where?"

"That, Warden Ives, depends entirely on why you're asking."

Sable crossed the cell in three strides and crouched in front of her, and Maren, even half-blind, could see the exhaustion carved into the woman's face — the particular exhaustion of someone who has discovered that the institution she serves is not merely flawed but rotten to the root, and who has decided to keep standing in it anyway.

"I'm asking," Sable said, "because in seven days the Council intends to perform a sealing rite that will kill whatever is left alive beyond the border, and they will do it while telling ten thousand people it's a memorial. And I am asking because I have spent my whole career believing that the Wardens were the wall between Vell and the dark, and I have just learned that we built the dark ourselves and locked it out so we wouldn't have to look at it. And I am asking," her voice dropped, roughened, "because a man I — a man named Kestrel Nakamura is about to do something that will get him killed, and I think your ledger might be the only thing that can stop him."

Maren was silent for a long moment.

Then she said, "Sit down, Warden. This will take some time, and my eyes are bad, and I would like to watch your face while I ruin your life."

* * *

They had recruited her at nineteen, the way they recruited all the archivists of the Inner Vault: not with an offer but with a test. Maren had been a scholarship student at the Cartographic Guild, brilliant and poor and hungry, and a soft-spoken woman named Chancellor's Secretary Eluned had put a single document in front of her — a shipping manifest from the founding year, listing forty barrels of *greybark tincture* delivered to a border station that had no medical staff — and had asked her what she noticed.

Maren had noticed everything. She had always noticed everything. That was the disease of her.

"Greybark tincture was an herbicide," she told Sable now, in the cell, her voice taking on the lecturing cadence she had never managed to shed. "Distilled from a lichen that grows only in the Thornlands. Harmless to people. Lethal to heartwood — to the deep living tissue of the great trees. Forty barrels a season, for eleven years, delivered to a station that officially existed to *study* the forest. And the manifest was signed by three of the seven founders."

"And the Vault's purpose?"

"The Vault's purpose," Maren said, "was to make sure that no one else ever noticed what I noticed. We were not archivists, Warden. We were a memory hole. Everything that contradicted the founding story went into the Vault, and the Vault went into the ground beneath the archive, and every generation we trained two or three more hungry brilliant children to guard the hole and call it scholarship." She laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "I told myself for thirty years that we were preserving the truth *for someday*. That someday the city would be ready, and we would open the Vault, and the truth would come out whole and vindicated. Do you know what I learned, three days before they arrested me?"

"Tell me."

"That someday is a lie we tell ourselves so we can keep drawing our stipends. There were eleven chances. Eleven generations of archivists, Warden. Eleven times one of us stood where I stood and said *now, we do it now*, and eleven times the Chancellor of that era sat them down and explained the war, the dead, the fragile peace, the *necessity* — and eleven times we went back to our desks and shelved another confession." She pressed her palms flat against her thighs. "The rot isn't in the Council. The Council is just the part of the rot that shows."

Sable had not moved. Her face, in the lamplight through the cell's high slit of a window, was very still. "The poison," she said. "The founders poisoned the heart-tree to fuel the wards."

"The wards run on stolen life. Yes. The founders discovered that a dying heart-tree bleeds power — that its death could be *harvested*, slowly, over centuries, and channeled through the border stones. They didn't break the Thornlands, Warden. They planted them like a crop. A crop of dying." Maren's hands had curled into fists without her permission. "Every light in Vell. Every ward, every warming-stone, every blessed lantern in every window — all of it fed on a forest bleeding out in the dark for three hundred years. And when the stars went out last week, do you know what the Council's first instinct was? Not horror. *Accounting*. I heard them, before they took me. They were worried about yield."

* * *

Sable asked the question Maren had been waiting for somewhere past midnight, when the guard's knee had shifted eleven hundred times and the lamp was burning low.

"The Nakamura woman," Sable said. "Ilse Nakamura. Your records — I found a reference. A redaction. Her file is one of the only files in the entire archive that's been *removed*, not sealed. Removed."

Maren closed her eyes.

She had known this moment would come. She had rehearsed it in the dark of the cell, and in the dark of thirty years before that, though she had not known she was rehearsing.

"Sit forward," she said. "And don't interrupt me, because if you interrupt me I will stop, and I have only ever been able to tell this once."

Sable sat forward.

"Ilse Nakamura came to the Vault in the year the old Chancellor died. She was a junior archivist — brilliant, like me, hungry, like me. And she was Thorn-blooded, though only three people in the building knew it, and I was not one of them at the time. She was better than all of us. Faster, sharper. She found things in the founding documents that the rest of us had walked past for decades, because she read them the way an exile reads — looking for the wound." Maren's voice had gone very quiet. "She found the harvest ledgers. The full chain. Not just the tincture shipments — the *rituals*. The names of the founders who performed them. The binding oaths. And she found the thing we had never found, the thing that made her dangerous in a way none of us had ever been."

"What thing?"

"She found out it was *reversible*." Maren opened her eyes and looked at Sable, and was distantly gratified to see the woman flinch. "A willing heir of the Thorn line, pouring their own blood-magic back into the heart-tree — the founders' own binding documents describe it as the one rite that could undo theirs. They wrote it down, Warden. They were proud of it. They thought no heir would ever be fool enough, or brave enough, or *found*. So they left the door unlocked in their own confession, and Ilse Nakamura read it, and she understood what she was."

The lamp guttered. Somewhere above them, the city curfew bell rang the second hour.

"She didn't go to the Council," Sable said. It wasn't a question.

"No. She went to the Thorn exiles. She made contact — we found the letters later, or what was left of them. She intended to bring them proof, and a path, and an heir. She intended to end it." Maren's breath caught, and she let it. "The Chancellor of that era was a man named Aldous Veyre. The current Chancellor's father. He had been watching her for a year — the Vault watched all of us, that was the other thing nobody tells the hungry brilliant children. And when she packed the evidence to carry it out of the city, he didn't send Wardens. He sent the fire."

The cell was very cold. Maren realized her hands were shaking and did not try to stop them.

"She died in the archive stairwell," Maren said. "Third landing. The official record says a lamp accident. I was twenty-four years old and I stood in the corridor outside the burned stairwell and I watched them carry her out under a tarp, and Chancellor Veyre put his hand on my shoulder and said, *Grief makes us careless, Archivist. Let this be a lesson in care.* And I went back to my desk. I went back to my desk, Warden Ives. I catalogued her removal from the rolls myself. I wrote *resigned, destination unknown* in the ledger with my own hand, because he stood behind me while I wrote it." Her voice broke and she hated it and she kept going. "And seven years ago, when a cartographer with her face and her name sold a map to a buyer I recognized as a Vault contact, I reported it. I reported *him*. I put his name in a ledger, and that ledger is the reason the Wardens have been circling him like crows, and I have had three days in this cell to understand that I have spent my entire life being the careful hand that Veyre's father put on my shoulder."

Silence. Sable's face had gone the color of ash.

"The map," she said finally. "The one he sold. What was on it?"

"Every erased landmark between Vell and the heart-tree. Every village the founding story says never existed. Every border stone with its true purpose carved on the reverse. He didn't know what he'd drawn, I don't think — he was a child when his mother died; she'd trained the knowledge into him the way you train a song into a child, so deep he thought it was just *how you drew maps*." Maren laughed again, and this time it was closer to weeping. "The founders' greatest crime, and their greatest fear, and it walked out of this city in the satchel of a disgraced mapmaker who thought he was just selling a pretty commission. And I reported it. I reported it, and the Vault burned it, or tried to, and now the only copies left in the world are in the memory of a man the Council wants dead and the mind of a Warden who is sitting in my cell asking me to help him."

* * *

"Why tell me?" Sable asked, near dawn. Her voice was hoarse. She had not moved from her chair in hours. "You don't know me. I could walk out of here and hand all of this to Veyre and you'd die in this cell knowing you'd failed twice."

Maren studied her — the blur of her, the set of her shoulders, the way she had not once looked at the door.

"Because you falsified your report," Maren said. "Three days ago. The Thorn remnant sighting in the undercity — you logged it as feral dogs. I know because the guard outside has a loose tongue and a gambling debt, and because I have spent thirty-one years learning what people sound like when they are lying for a good reason instead of a bad one. It sounds exactly like that, Warden Ives. It sounds like a woman who has just discovered the wall she's been guarding has a door in it, and she's decided not to tell anyone which way it opens." She leaned forward, and her voice dropped to something fierce and old. "So here is what you will do. The Vault's master ledger — the true one, the one with Ilse Nakamura's removal and the harvest rituals and eleven generations of buried confession — has a copy. One. I hid it the night before they took me, in the one place in this city no Warden would ever think to search, because it is the one place in this city that has never once been *recorded*."

"Where?"

Maren smiled, and for the first time in three days it was not a bitter smile. It was the smile of a woman who had spent her life learning that everything was a record — and who had finally, at the end, made one that mattered.

"Under the heart of the old archive, there is a sealed cistern from before the founding," she said. "The founders used it to hide the first harvest stones. I put the ledger where they put their first crime — because I am an archivist, Warden, and I believe evidence belongs with its context." She held out her hands, thin and trembling, palms up. "Now. You have a choice to make, and I would suggest you make it fast. You can walk out of this cell and forget everything I've said, and in seven days this city will seal its border over a murdered world and call it peace. Or you can take my testimony, and my ledger, and the name of a dead woman who died so that a mapmaker with her eyes could finish what she started — and you can go and find him before he does something noble and fatal, and you can put this ledger in front of ten thousand people and make them *look*."

Sable stood. For a long moment she said nothing, and Maren watched the war move across her face like weather.

Then Sable pulled the cell keys from her belt, and unlocked the door, and said, "Can you walk?"

Maren rose on legs that had forgotten standing, and gripped the Warden's offered arm, and answered with the only honest thing she had left.

"I have been walking toward this for thirty-one years," she said. "Take me to the boy. And then take me to the Council chamber — because I have a ledger, Warden Ives, and I intend to read every line of it aloud."
