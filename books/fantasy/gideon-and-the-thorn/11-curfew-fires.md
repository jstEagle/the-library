## 11. Curfew Fires

The fires started on the fourth starless night, in the tannery quarter, and by morning they had a name.

Kestrel heard it first from the widow Odile across the landing, who leaned over her rail with a candle she was no longer allowed to light and whispered it down the stairwell like a prayer: *thorn-witches.* The word moved through Vell the way water moves through chalk — silently, thoroughly, finding every crack. By noon the criers were shouting it from the corners. By dusk the Council had posted its proclamation on the guildhall doors, and Kestrel stood in the crowd reading it twice because the first time his eyes refused to believe what they were seeing.

*By order of the Chancellor and the Council of Nine: any person found practicing or concealing the thorn-craft shall be taken into custody. Any person harboring such persons shall share their sentence. The border catastrophe is an act of sabotage. The saboteurs are among us.*

Among us. He read the words again and felt them settle into his chest like a splinter working toward the heart. Seven years he had built a life out of smallness — a shop too narrow to notice, a name too plain to remember, a magic buried so deep he sometimes convinced himself it wasn't there at all. And now the city had decided that buried things were the enemy. That the dark under every floorboard might be hiding a witch.

That night he burned his stock.

He did it in the hearth, a handful at a time, feeding the fire charms he had spent a decade making: luck-knots and fever-binds, a charm against nightmares stitched from river-reed, half a dozen compasses like the one hidden in his boot. Each one went up with a soft sound, almost a sigh, the magic leaving them in a smell like rain on hot stone. He worked without expression, the way he did everything that mattered, and when the last of it was ash he sat for a long while watching the coals and thinking about the one charm he had kept — the living compass, the one that pointed not north but toward something breathing — and how keeping it was no longer caution but defiance, though he could not have said whose.

Across the courtyard, someone's shutter closed quickly when he looked up. Old habits of watching. New reasons for it.

* * *

Sable Ives stood in the Warden barracks armory and listened to a man she had once trusted explain how to burn people properly.

"The pitch matters," Sergeant Corvel was saying, tapping the diagram chalked on the slate. "Green wood smokes too much; the crowd wants to see. We stake them at the fountain plaza — good sightlines from three streets, and the aqueduct wall holds back the press if it turns." He said *if* the way other men said *when*, and no one in the room corrected him.

There were eleven Wardens present. Sable counted them without meaning to, an old habit from mapping hostile ground — exits, temperaments, who stood close to whom. She had falsified her report six days ago. She had written *no thorn remnant located; investigation ongoing* in her careful Warden's hand and signed it, and every day since, the lie had grown heavier, like carrying water in cupped palms across a long distance. She had told herself it bought time. She had told herself the exiles were not the danger the Council claimed. She had even, in a weaker hour, told herself that Kestrel Nakamura owed her the truth and would come to her with it.

He had not come. And the Council had stopped waiting for truth altogether.

"Ives." Corvel's voice pulled her back. "You'll take the east approach with Denna. Crowd control only. The executions are Civic Guard business."

*Executions.* Plural. There were four names on tomorrow's list. Sable had read them that afternoon in the holding ledger, and one of them had made her stop breathing for a moment: **Tamsin Reeve, hedge-craft, charms sold at night market.** Tamsin Reeve was seventy-one years old. Tamsin Reeve had once sold Sable's younger sister a charm against bedwetting, for two coppers and a smile, and it hadn't worked, and Tamsin had laughed and returned the coppers anyway. That was the whole of Tamsin's sorcery. Two coppers and a smile, refunded.

"Understood," Sable said, and her voice came out level, and she hated how easily the training held, how the uniform fit her like a second skin even as everything underneath it curdled.

After the briefing, Captain Ashe caught her at the door. He was a gray, deliberate man who had signed off on her falsified report without comment, which meant either that he hadn't read it or that he had, and both possibilities frightened her equally.

"You look tired, Ives."

"I sleep badly lately, sir. Everyone does."

"Mm." He studied her with pale, patient eyes. "The Chancellor's office asked after your investigation. The Thorn remnant. They want progress." A pause, weighted. "Or closure. Either serves."

"They'll have one or the other," Sable said.

"Yes," said Captain Ashe. "They will."

She walked home through the curfewed streets with her lantern hooded, past doors where families had painted the old warding signs back onto their lintels — signs that had been decorative for a generation, folk art, and were suddenly desperate again. In a doorway near Tanner's Row, a child had chalked a sun on the cobbles. Someone had already scrubbed at it, but the ghost of it remained, a pale circle in the dark, pointing at nothing. Pointing up, at the sky where stars used to be.

Sable stood over it longer than a Warden should have.

* * *

The executions began at dawn, because the Council wanted the light.

Kestrel told himself he wouldn't go. He had work — he always had work, maps to copy, commissions to refuse — and there was nothing he could do, and a man who had spent seven years surviving by being invisible did not stand in crowds. He repeated all of this to himself while he put on his coat, while he took the side streets toward the fountain plaza, while he told himself he was only going to see, only to witness, only to know the shape of what his city was becoming. Lies, all of them, and he knew their texture intimately. He was going because silence had a weight, and he could feel it starting to crush him.

The plaza was full. It astonished him, how full — thousands pressed behind the aqueduct wall and packed along the colonnades, faces turned toward the four stakes erected around the dry fountain basin. The mood was not bloodlust, exactly. It was worse than that. It was hunger for explanation. Four years of dimming harvests, seven nights of starless sky, wards fallen, borders sealed, children asking questions their parents couldn't answer — and here, finally, was something the city could do about it. Here were four people who could burn, and maybe the sky would notice, and maybe the sky would care.

Kestrel found a place beneath the colonnade's shadow, hood up, and hated himself for the hood.

They brought the prisoners out in gray shifts, wrists bound, flanked by Civic Guard in black lacquer. An official read the charges in a voice trained for distance. *Trafficking in thorn-craft. Concealment of magical practice. Sabotage of the civic wards.* The words fell on the crowd like coins into a well — everyone leaning in to hear the splash.

Then they brought out Tamsin Reeve.

She was smaller than Kestrel remembered, if he had ever actually seen her — a round, bent woman with white hair escaping its braid, and she was looking at the crowd, really looking, meeting eyes the way you'd greet neighbors at market. When they tied her to the stake she cooperated, lifting her arms to help, murmuring something to the guard — an apology, perhaps, for the trouble. And then, as the executioner approached with the torch, Tamsin Reeve lifted her chin and called out in a cracked, clear voice that carried across the whole hushed plaza:

"It's all right, loves. Whatever they tell you — the stars aren't angry. Stars don't get angry. Somebody *did* this, but it wasn't us, and it wasn't you, and it wasn't—"

The torch touched the pitch.

What happened next happened fast and would stay slow in Kestrel's memory forever. The flame took the pitch with a whump that he felt in his teeth. Tamsin's speech ended in a sound he refused to let himself describe, even inside his own head. The crowd made a noise — not a cheer, thank whatever gods still listened, not a cheer, but a great collective intake of breath, thousands of people inhaling at once, and the sound was obscene in its own way, the sound of a city feeding.

Kestrel did not look away. He had decided that beforehand, standing in his cold room, and he held to it now with the discipline of a man gripping a rope over a drop. He watched the fire take her, watched her go silent far sooner than the fire did, watched the smoke climb straight up into the starless black where it simply stopped, visible against nothing, a gray column with nowhere to go. Around him people wept. Some prayed. A woman beside him whispered *there, see, it's done, it's done* as if soothing a child, as if the burning were medicine and the screaming the worst part of the cure.

And Kestrel understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that he had helped build this.

Not the fire. Not the stakes. But the silence — seven years of it, his silence, the silence of every person in Vell who knew that magic was not a monster, who knew that the wards ran on something older and darker than civic virtue, who had watched the founding families' names carved in marble above the archive doors and thought *not my fight, not my grave, better invisible than dead.* He had sold his map and buried his blood and called it survival, and survival was a fine word, it was the finest word, right up until the morning you stood in a crowd and watched a harmless old woman burn for the crime of being what you were.

Silence was complicity. He had known it abstractly the way you know the sea is deep. Now he knew it the way drowning men do.

The second prisoner was a young man, barely grown, sobbing before the torch even came near. Kestrel turned away from that one — not from cowardice, he told himself, but because he had gotten what he came for, and what he had gotten was a decision. It sat in him now, hard and bright as a coal: *No more.* No more burying. No more smallness. If the city wanted a thorn-witch to blame, then it would have one — the real one, the heir, the whole rotten truth of Vell's founders laid open in the light where Tamsin Reeve had burned.

He slipped out through the colonnade's shadow, and no one marked the hooded man leaving, and for the first time in seven years that felt like failure instead of victory.

* * *

Sable stood at the edge of the plaza until the fires were ash and the crowd had drained away, and then she walked, because walking was the only thing that kept her from breaking something.

She walked past the fountain basin where workers were already chipping char off the stone, efficient, practiced, as though this were a maintenance task like any other. She walked past the holding ledger in her mind, four names, three more to go. She walked past the barracks, past the guildhall, past the archive where a certain cartographer's deed lay in a drawer she had no right to have memorized. Her feet chose the route. She let them.

She ended up, without surprise, on the street where Kestrel Nakamura lived.

His window was dark. Curfew. Of course it was dark. But a thread of chimney smoke rose from his roof, thin and steady, and Sable stood across the lane in her Warden's cloak and looked at that smoke for a long time. Somewhere behind that wall was a man who lied to her with a straight face and drew maps that had saved her life and was, by every law she had sworn to uphold, exactly the kind of person she was supposed to drag to a stake.

Somewhere behind that wall was also the only person in Vell who might know why the stars had gone out.

"You'd better be worth it," she said quietly, to the window, to the smoke, to herself. "Because I'm running out of reports to falsify."

Above her, the sky stayed black and empty, and the city below it slept badly, and three names waited in a ledger for their turn at the fountain. Sable pulled her hood up and turned toward the barracks — and stopped.

A figure stood at the far corner of the lane. Hooded, slight, motionless. Watching her watch the window. Even at that distance, even in the dark, something in the set of the shoulders was familiar — the stillness of a man used to being overlooked, except that he was choosing, deliberately, to be seen.

The figure raised one hand — not a wave, not a greeting. An invitation. Then it turned and walked away down the curfewed street, unhurried, certain that she would follow.

Sable stood in the empty lane with her heart hammering against her ribs like a fist on a locked door, and after a moment that felt like the rest of her life, she followed.
