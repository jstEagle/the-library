## 12. The Long Road Out

They left Vell before dawn, when the curfew fires had burned down to sullen orange coals and the Wardens' boots on the cobbles had slowed to the shuffling rhythm of men who had been awake too long to believe in anything.

Kestrel went last through the drainage tunnel, as he had insisted, because he was the one who had drawn it — a map made seven years ago and sold to a man whose face he still could not summon with any certainty, only the weight of his coin and the smell of lamp oil on his gloves. The tunnel was exactly as he remembered it: knee-deep water gone to ice at the edges, brick sweating cold, the air tasting of iron and old rain. What he had not remembered — what no map could hold — was the sound of six people breathing behind him in the dark, trusting him with their lives because a piece of paper said he knew the way.

Brammel walked second, thorn-marks coiled down both forearms like sleeping serpents. Maren came third, wrists still ringed with the bruises of her captivity, saying nothing, watching everything. Sable walked beside Kestrel without touching him, her Warden's coat traded for a smuggler's gray wool that made her look younger and more dangerous at once. Two of Brammel's exiles — the silent woman called Ashka and a boy of perhaps seventeen named Pell who could not stop looking over his shoulder — took the rear.

The tunnel ended at a rusted grate, and beyond the grate, the world simply stopped being Vell.

Kestrel had expected the broken wards to feel like something. A scar in the air, a hum, a wrongness. Instead there was nothing at all — a gap where a wall had been, so complete that his body passed through it without protest, and it was that completeness that frightened him. Walls that are broken resist you. This was as if someone had reached into the fabric of the border and unpicked it stitch by stitch, leaving not a ruin but an absence.

He stopped on the far side and turned around. Behind him, Vell's walls rose black against a starless sky, torchlit, indifferent. Ahead of him lay the Thornlands.

"Oh," said Pell, very quietly, and Kestrel understood exactly what the boy meant.

* * *

The forest began a half-mile from the border, and it was gray.

Not dead-gray, not ash-gray precisely, though there was ash in it — but gray the way a photograph is gray, all the color drained out and yet every shape preserved. Trees stood in their thousands across the rolling country, oaks and rowans and thorn-apples by their silhouettes, and every one of them was frozen mid-death. That was the phrase that kept arriving in Kestrel's mind and refusing to leave: *mid-death*. Not fallen, not rotted, not burned. Caught. Leaves hung on branches they should have shed years ago, curled and brittle and gray as old paper. Bark split along lines that looked almost deliberate, as if the trees were trying to open and could not finish. One great oak near the road had begun to bud — Kestrel could see the tight gray fists of new leaves along its lower boughs — and the budding had simply stopped, arrested halfway between winter and spring, and stayed that way for seven years.

Ash fell from a sky that held no clouds worth the name. It drifted down fine and slow, settling on their shoulders and hair, filling the wheel ruts of a road that had once been cobbled and was now a suggestion. Sable caught a flake on her gloved palm and studied it.

"It's warm," she said.

Kestrel touched it himself. She was right. The ash was faintly, terribly warm, like the last heat of a fire that has been out for days. Like something still burning somewhere far beneath them, very slowly, with nothing left to burn.

"The tree," Brammel said. He did not look at either of them. "Everything here is the tree. The soil is its roots, the rain its sap, the light—" He stopped. Started again. "The stars were its leaves, cartographer. You know this now. When you stand under a clear sky in the Thornlands and look up at ten thousand stars, you are looking at the canopy of a single living thing. And we cut it down." His jaw worked. "*Vell* cut it down."

"Your founders," Sable said. "Say it plainly, exile. I'm done letting Vell hide inside the word 'we.'"

Brammel glanced at her then, and something passed between them that Kestrel could not read — the old hostility reshaping itself into something more complicated, two people who had spent their lives on opposite sides of a wall discovering the wall had always been paper.

"My founders," Brammel agreed. "Yours too, Warden. Everyone who ever paid a tax in that city bought a branch of the poison."

They walked on. The road descended through the gray forest toward a valley where a river should have been; Kestrel knew it from his mother's maps, the Vessa, wide and quick and silvered with fish, the spine of everything the Thornlands had been. They found instead a channel of gray glass — the water frozen not in white but in that same drained color, motionless, holding within it the shapes of reeds and a drowned boat and, once, something long and sinuous that Kestrel chose not to examine closely. Fish, he told himself. It had been a fish.

His magic woke at the border and had not stopped hurting since.

That was the thing he could not say aloud, walking among people for whom this land was grief itself: that the blighted Thornlands *sang* to him. Every step deeper pulled at something behind his sternum, a hook set in meat, gentle at first and then less gentle, until by the time they reached the frozen river he was clenching his teeth against it and Sable kept glancing at him because she could hear the change in his breathing. It was not pain exactly. It was recognition. It was the feeling of walking into a house you had been torn from as a child and finding your own handprints still on the doorframe, small and gray with dust, waiting.

The blood in him knew this place. The blood in him was *from* this place. And the blood in him was rising toward whatever remained alive at the center of all this ruin like a dog straining toward a whistle only it could hear.

"You feel it," Brammel said. It was not a question. He had fallen back to walk beside Kestrel, and his dark eyes held something Kestrel had seen before only in desperate men at card tables: hope, which is worse than greed. "Since the border. Don't lie to me, heir. I've watched you since the gate. You walk like a man being pulled."

"I walk like a man with sore feet," Kestrel said.

Brammel smiled without humor. "My grandmother could feel the heart-tree from forty miles. Said it was like a second heartbeat under your own, slower, patient. When it began to die, she said, the heartbeat went irregular. Stuttering." He put his hand flat against a gray trunk as they passed, an unconscious gesture, the way a man touches a sleeping child. "You're stuttering, Nakamura. I can see it in your hands."

Kestrel looked down. His hands were trembling. He closed them into fists and put them in his pockets and hated Brammel with a clean, simple hatred that lasted four steps and collapsed into exhaustion, because the exile was right, and they both knew it, and lying about it cost more than the truth.

"Yes," he said. "I feel it. Are you satisfied?"

"No," Brammel said softly. "But thank you."

* * *

They camped that night in the shell of a waystation — one of the old border houses that had served travelers between the lands in the years of uneasy peace, before the wards, before the war, before all of it. Its roof was mostly intact. Its hearth was cold, and Ashka built a fire in it from gray wood that burned with a flame the color of weak tea, giving off light but almost no warmth, and a smoke that smelled of nothing at all.

No stars. Kestrel stood outside the doorway after the others slept and looked up at the hole in the sky where the Thornlands' heavens used to be, and felt the absence like a tooth-socket, the tongue going again and again to the gap. In Vell, the starless night had been horror — panic in the streets, the Council's voice booming from the bells, neighbors turning on neighbors. Out here, under the actual sky where the stars had actually lived, the horror was quieter and much larger. This was not a city frightened of the dark. This was a wound the size of a nation.

Sable came to stand beside him. She did this often now, he was realizing. Without announcement, without pretext. She simply arrived at his shoulder the way weather arrives, and stayed.

"Pell asked me today if Vell knew," she said. "What was happening out here. While the tree died." Her breath fogged in the cold. "Seven years, Kestrel. Seven years of gray forests and no stars and children born under an empty sky, and my city lit its lamps and argued about grain tariffs."

"They didn't know."

"Some knew." She turned to look at him, and the tea-colored firelight through the doorway behind them caught the edge of her face. "Veyre knows now. The archivists knew. My superiors gave me an execution order written in language so clean you could frame it." She shook her head slowly. "Don't defend them to make me feel better. I'd rather be angry."

"I wasn't defending them." Kestrel looked back at the sky. "I was defending myself. I lived in Vell for seven years too, Sable. I sold charms to people who couldn't afford bread and told myself it balanced out. I drew maps of a city built on a poisoned grave and never once asked what the borders were hiding. If Vell is guilty, I'm the most guilty kind — the kind that could have looked and chose not to."

"And now?"

"Now I'm looking." He laughed, and it came out wrong, ragged. "Gods help me, Sable, now I can't stop looking. Do you know what this place does? Every mile we walk, it shows me another thing. The river. The waystation. There will be a village tomorrow — Brammel says there's a village — and it will have names, and those names will have belonged to people, and some of those people will have been my mother's friends, and—"

He stopped, because his voice had cracked, and because Sable had taken his hand.

She did it the way she did everything — decisively, without apology, her grip strong and cold from the night air. She did not say it would be all right. She was a Warden; she had seen enough to know better than to promise that. She just held his hand in the starless dark while the warm ash came down around them like snow that remembered being fire, and after a while Kestrel found that he was leaning into her, forehead against her temple, breathing.

"The compass pointed here," she said at last, quietly. "Your charm. Before we ever left. It was pointing at the heart-tree the whole time, wasn't it. At whatever's still alive."

"Something's alive," Kestrel said. "Something at the middle of all this is still alive, and it's dying, and it's calling, and I think—" He swallowed. "I think it's calling *me*, Sable. Not the exiles. Not Brammel with his grand plans. Me. By name, maybe. By blood."

She was quiet for a long moment. Then: "And what do you want to do about that?"

Nobody had asked him that. Brammel had assumed. The exiles had knelt. Maren watched him like a ledger awaiting entries. Even Sable, until now, had treated him as a piece moving on a board she was trying to protect. But standing in the ruins of a nation with her hand in his, she asked him the question as if his answer were the only one that mattered, and Kestrel discovered he did not have one.

"I don't know," he said. "I honestly don't know. Part of me wants to run. Part of me wants to reach the tree tonight. Part of me wants to sit down in this ash and never get up." He turned his head and found her eyes. "What would you do? If it were your city dying. Your blood in its roots."

"My city *is* dying," Sable said. "It just doesn't know it yet. And I'm doing the only thing I can — walking toward the truth of it, one step at a time, with people I trust." She squeezed his hand. "So keep walking, cartographer. We'll figure out the rest at the tree."

* * *

The village came on the second day, and it was worse than Kestrel had braced himself for, because it was intact.

Houses still stood, thatched roofs sagging but whole. A well waited in the village square with its bucket still tied on. Laundry lines stretched between doorposts, hung with gray cloth frozen stiff as slate, shirts and dresses holding the shapes of the bodies that had worn them. A child's wooden horse lay in the road where it had been dropped. Everything preserved, everything empty, everything the exact color of forgetting.

"They evacuated," Brammel said, but his voice had gone strange. He walked the street slowly, touching doorframes, reading the lintels where families had carved protective marks — thorn-signs, old ones, older than the war. "Most of them. When the stars first dimmed, before the collapse. The Order moved everyone south to the deep settlements." He stopped before a house with a carved door, and his hand rose toward the mark on it and did not quite land. "This was the Ellery household. Mira Ellery taught me to read. She'd be—" He calculated, and his face did something complicated. "She'd be dead of age by now regardless. That shouldn't comfort me and it doesn't."

Maren stood apart from all of them in the square, notebook out despite the cold, sketching the village with an archivist's terrible precision. "Dates," she said, half to herself. "If I can date the abandonment against the ward construction, we'll have the sequence. Proof of what Vell did *before* the exiles were blamed for it. This village is evidence, Nakamura. It's a witness that can't be silenced."

"It's a home," Kestrel said.

Maren looked up, pen paused, and for a moment the archivist's armor slipped and he saw the woman underneath, young and appalled. "Yes," she said. "Both things. That's rather the point of everything, isn't it."

They watered the horses — Brammel had brought two lean pack-mules — at the well, and ate cold rations in the shadow of the meeting hall, and it was Pell who found the thing that none of them would speak of for the rest of the day.

He came running back from the far lane, seventeen years old and suddenly younger, his thorn-marks writhing visibly on his neck the way they did when he was frightened. "There's a shrine," he said. "Behind the last house. Someone stayed. Someone *stayed* and—"

The shrine was a thorn-tree, a true one, small and gnarled, planted in a stone circle behind the village's last cottage. It was as gray as everything else. But wound through its dead branches, woven with hands that must have shaken, were hundreds of ribbons. Prayers, Kestrel realized. The old custom — his mother had taught it to him without naming it, tying a strip of cloth to any living thorn and whispering a wish into the knot. These ribbons were gray too, colorless, but they had not rotted. Nothing here rotted. Everything here only *stopped*.

And scratched into the stone circle's rim, in letters deep and desperate, in a hand Kestrel knew better than his own —

He was on his knees before he decided to kneel.

*Kest. Be brave. Be kind. Remember the green.*

His mother's handwriting. His childhood nickname, known to three people in all the world, two of them dead. Here, at the edge of a village sixty miles past the border, in a place he had never been, carved into stone sometime in the years before the wards fell — or after; he could not tell; the stone kept no dates — by a woman who had died protecting the evidence that Vell's founders had murdered a world.

"She came through here," Brammel said behind him, very quietly. "In the last year, before the end. We lost track of her near the Vessa. We thought—" He didn't finish. He didn't need to.

Kestrel pressed his palm flat against the stone, over the letters, and the thing behind his sternum — the hook, the pull, the stuttering second heartbeat — wrenched so hard that he gasped aloud. Warmth flooded up his arm, unasked-for, terrifying: his buried magic waking fully for the first time in his life, answering the land the way a child answers its mother's voice. Gray light moved under his skin. For one heartbeat the ribbons on the dead tree stirred in a wind that was not blowing.

Then it passed, and he was only a man kneeling in ash, and Sable's hand was on his shoulder, and the sky above the Thornlands was still a black and starless wound.

"She's leading us," Kestrel heard himself say. His voice sounded far away. "Even dead, she's leading us. Come on. We're close. I can feel how close."

He stood, and took the first step, and the whole gray forest seemed to lean after him like a congregation.
