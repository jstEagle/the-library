## 13. What Brammel Buried

Sable found the truth the way she found most truths: not by looking for it, but by refusing to look away from something small.

They had made camp in the ruin of a waystation two days past the border — a squat stone building that had once housed wardens of the old Thorn garrison, its roof half-collapsed, its walls furred with gray moss that was neither alive nor quite dead. The exiles had spread their bedrolls among the rubble with the ease of people who had slept in worse. Kestrel sat apart, as he always did now, sketching by candlelight: the road behind them, the blighted treeline ahead, the shape of a country remembering how to be a country. Maren dozed against her freed hands, still rubbing the rope-burns Brammel's people had left on her wrists.

And Brammel stood at the eastern window, watching the ash fall.

Sable had been watching him for three days. Not because she suspected anything — at first she hadn't. She watched him because that was what she did, because seven years as a Warden investigator had taught her that people told you everything they were if you only paid attention to where they put their weight. Brammel put his weight like a man standing on a bridge he expected to collapse. He checked sightlines. He counted heads twice. He spoke to Kestrel with the careful reverence of a man handling something borrowed and breakable.

But it was the ledger that undid him.

Maren's ledger — the one the archivists had kept for generations, the one that named the founders' poison and the price of Vell's wards — had been passed around the campfire the night before, each exile reading it in silence and handing it back like a relic. Sable had read it last, after Kestrel, and when she'd looked up from the final page she'd caught Brammel watching her read it. Not watching the ledger. Watching *her*. Measuring what she would do with the knowledge, the way a man measures a load-bearing wall before he knocks out another one.

She should have let it go. They were two days into the Thornlands, committed, past the point where suspicion served anyone. But Sable Ives had built her whole life on the principle that the moment you stop asking questions is the moment you become the thing you were asking questions about.

So while the camp slept, she went through Brammel's pack.

She hated herself for it even as she knelt beside it. It was the kind of thing Wardens did — the kind of thing she'd spent the last weeks learning to despise about her own order. But her hands moved anyway, steady and apologetic, lifting out oilcloth bundles and dried meat and a whetstone worn concave from decades of use. And there, wrapped in a scrap of green cloth so faded it might once have been a banner: papers.

Maps. Old ones, copied in a cramped hand onto whatever paper the undercity could spare — scraps of billposting, the backs of proclamations, one page torn from a child's primer. Maps of Vell's border wards. Not the public maps, the ones sold to merchants showing the safe corridors. These were the working maps, the warden's maps, annotated in Brammel's hand with notes that made Sable's blood slow and cold in her veins.

*Anchor stone four: hairline fracture, self-mending, will hold unless stressed.*

*Anchor stone nine: weakest point. Stress here and the cascade runs east to west.*

*The keystone answers to blood. Any blood. The wards do not care whose.*

And beneath that, dated seven years ago, a single line in handwriting gone careful and deliberate, the hand of a man writing down something he intended to live with:

*It can be done. It must be done. Let them look at what they built.*

Sable sat back on her heels in the dark and felt the floor tilt under her.

Seven years ago. The wards hadn't failed. They hadn't been broken by saboteurs or eroded by neglect or cracked by some catastrophe beyond the border. Brammel had found the weak anchor stone and he had *stressed* it, deliberately, precisely, with the patience of a man who understood that a dam doesn't burst all at once but gives you time to watch it happen. He had unmade the wards himself. He had pulled down the wall between Vell and the Thornlands — the wall that kept the blight out, yes, but also the wall that trapped thousands of people outside it. Farmers. Villagers. Children born in the gray country who had never seen a star.

He had gambled them. All of them. To force a reckoning.

*Let them look at what they built.*

She heard footsteps behind her and didn't turn. There was no point. Brammel moved quietly for an old man, but not that quietly, and besides — she thought he had probably been waiting for her to find this since the moment he'd watched her read Maren's ledger. Perhaps longer. Perhaps since the day he'd knelt to Kestrel Nakamura in the sewers and called him heir.

"You went through my pack," Brammel said. His voice was level. Almost gentle.

"You wanted me to."

"I hoped you would." He lowered himself onto a fallen beam across from her, joints cracking, and set his hands on his knees. In the faint light of the banked fire he looked older than he had in the undercity — or perhaps just more honestly carved, every line of his face finally admitting how many years he had spent carrying this. "I'm too old to lie well anymore. And too tired to want to."

"Then don't." She held up the page. *The keystone answers to blood.* "Tell me I'm reading this wrong."

Brammel looked at the paper for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice didn't waver.

"You're reading it right. I brought the wards down. Seven years ago, the night the stars went out. Anchor stone nine, on the western run, where the foundations had already begun to rot because no Warden had inspected them in forty years." He said it plainly, the way a man confesses to a debt. "I used my own blood. It took three attempts. The first two healed over. The third didn't."

Sable's hands had gone very still in her lap. Somewhere across the camp, one of the exiles turned in their sleep and murmured something in a language she almost recognized — old Thorn-tongue, the language of the trees.

"Do you know what the western reaches were like?" Brammel asked. "Before? No — of course you don't. You were raised inside the wall, inside the light, taught that the Thornlands were empty. They weren't empty. There were eleven villages between the border and the heart-tree when Vell sealed it. Eleven. I was born in one of them — Harrowmere, if the name means anything to you. It won't. Vell struck it from every map your guild ever printed. My mother died there when I was six, waiting for aid that never came, because the city on the other side of the wall had decided we were the cost of safety."

"The wards kept the blight out," Sable said. Her voice sounded far away to her own ears. "Whatever else they did—"

"They kept the *blame* out." For the first time, something moved behind his eyes — not anger exactly, but the heat underneath it, banked for decades. "Your founders poisoned the heart-tree, Warden Ives. They fed it death for two hundred years and called the resulting darkness a curse, a wilderness, an enemy. Then they built a wall around their crime and posted guards on it and taught their children that the people dying on the wrong side of that wall were casualties of war rather than witnesses. I spent thirty years trying to make Vell look. Petitions. Smuggled testimony. I got three archivists killed and one exiled — Maren's predecessor, a good woman, who died in the gray country coughing up her lungs and cursing the day she ever believed a petition could work." He shook his head slowly. "Vell does not look, Warden. Vell looks away. So I made the sky go dark over their heads and forced them to raise their eyes."

"And the eleven villages became twelve," Sable said quietly. "And thirteen. Everyone trapped outside when the wards fell lost whatever protection the border stones still gave. The blight came through the gap you cut. People died, Brammel. People who had done nothing but be born on the wrong side of your righteous arithmetic."

"Yes." He didn't flinch. That was the worst part — she had confronted murderers before, and they always flinched, always reached for justification or denial or blame. Brammel simply sat with it, the way a man sits with a wound he has carried so long it has become part of his skeleton. "People died. I have their names. I keep them written in the back of my map book, and every morning I say them, and every morning I decide again whether the trade was worth it." He met her eyes. "I have never once decided that it wasn't. Do you understand what I'm telling you? I have looked at the ledger every single day for seven years, and I would make the same trade tomorrow. Because the alternative was another two hundred years of the same, and then nothing at all — a dead tree, a dead land, and a city that never once had to say sorry."

Sable looked down at the papers in her hands. The fire had burned low; the shadows in the ruined waystation pooled like water.

She thought of the riots. Of the hedge-witch they'd burned in Copperlane Square while the Council blamed thorn-witches for a darkness Vell itself had planted. Of the curfew fires and the frightened neighbors and Kestrel burning his charms in a grate because invisibility was the only safety a magical man had ever been allowed. Seven years of that. Seven years of a city eating itself in the dark, and every hour of it traced back to this quiet old man's deliberate hand.

And she thought of Maren's ledger. Of the proof that the Council had known — that generations of archivists had known — and buried it. Of Chancellor Veyre signing orders with full knowledge of what the sealing rite would finish.

The ugly arithmetic. That was what her father had called it, back when she was a girl, when he'd come home from patrol duty smelling of woodsmake and silence. *Every choice costs someone, little bird. The question is whether you're honest enough to count.* She had built her career on being honest enough to count. She had falsified her first report three weeks ago and hated herself for it, and here was a man who had falsified seven years of a nation's history and slept soundly enough to check his sightlines twice a night.

"I should arrest you," she said. "If Vell still had law that meant anything, I'd have arrested you the moment I opened the oilcloth."

"If Vell still had law that meant anything," Brammel agreed, "we wouldn't be sitting in this ruin."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only answer there is." He leaned forward, elbows on knees, and for a moment the firelight caught the thorn-mark on the back of his hand — the living tattoo that all the exiles bore, though his had gone gray and stiff with age, a bramble grown woody. "You want me to say I regret it. I know you do. It would make this easier for you — you could hate me cleanly and move on. But I won't lie to you, Warden, because you're the first person from inside that wall who has ever bothered to walk out here and see the gray country with her own eyes, and you deserve better than a comfortable lie." His voice dropped. "I regret the names. Every one. I will carry them into whatever grave is waiting for me. But I do not regret the act. A dying tree needed a surgeon, and no one in Vell would even admit it was sick."

Silence settled between them, thick as ashfall.

Sable turned the pages over in her hands. The cramped annotations. The careful dates. Thirty years of patient, murderous hope. She thought about what justice would even look like here — Brammel in irons, dragged back to a city that was currently burning innocent women in its squares, tried by a Council that had poisoned a god-tree and gotten away with it for two centuries. Justice administered by the guilty. What a bitter joke that would be.

"What happens to me now?" she asked. "You knew I'd find this. You let me find it. Why?"

"Because Kestrel needs to reach the heart-tree," Brammel said simply, "and you are the reason he'll survive getting there. I couldn't have you finding this out after — on the far side of it, when it was done and couldn't be changed. You'd have spent the rest of your life wondering what else I'd hidden. Wondering whether you'd been a tool." He smiled, and it was a terrible, tired smile. "Better you know now. Better you choose with open eyes. Whatever you choose."

"And if I choose to turn back? To report you?"

"To whom?" He spread his hands, gentle as a teacher. "To Veyre? To the Council that lit the pyres in Copperlane Square? Walk back alone through the blight if you like, Warden. No one here will stop you. But ask yourself what you'd be walking back *to*, and ask yourself whether the woman who falsified her first report to protect a cartographer is really going to hand the last chance this land has over to the men who killed it."

Sable stood. Her legs were stiff; she'd been kneeling by the pack longer than she'd realized. Across the camp, Kestrel had blown out his candle at some point — or maybe he'd never lit it, maybe he'd been sitting in the dark the whole time, listening. She couldn't tell. The thought sat in her chest like a swallowed stone.

She looked down at Brammel, at the gray thorn-mark, at the map book where the dead of his arithmetic lived in his own handwriting.

"I'm not forgiving you," she said. "Understand that. Whatever happens at the heart-tree, whatever comes after — I am not forgiving you. Those names deserve someone who remembers, and I'll remember."

"I wouldn't respect you if you did forgive me," Brammel said. "Remembering is worth more."

She walked back toward the fire, toward the shape of Kestrel sitting motionless in the dark, and it wasn't until she'd nearly reached him that she saw what he held in his lap, faintly luminous in the starless night: the living compass, its needle straining toward the heart-tree — and trembling now, quivering like a plumb line in an earthquake, as though whatever it pointed toward was running out of time faster than any of them had calculated.

Kestrel looked up at her, and the fear on his face told her he'd heard everything.

"Sable," he said quietly. "The needle just started doing this an hour ago. It's never done it before." He swallowed. "Something's wrong at the tree. Something's happening *now*."

Far to the east, beyond the frozen forests, the darkness seemed — for a heartbeat, for less than a heartbeat — to deepen, as if the last green vein of the world had flinched.
