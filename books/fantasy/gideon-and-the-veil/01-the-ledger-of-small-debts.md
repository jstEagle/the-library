## 1. The Ledger of Small Debts

The guildhall smelled of tallow, ink, and old paper — the three scents Marguerite Ashgrove had come to think of as home, though she would never have said so aloud. Home was a word with teeth. It bit the hand that reached for it.

She lit her lamp before dawn, as she always did, and set it on the corner of the great audit desk where the winter accounts lay stacked like fallen soldiers. Forty-one ledgers this season. She ran her thumb along their spines, feeling the faint warmth that clung to any book in which an impossible thing had been written down. Paper remembered. That was the first lesson every clerk of the Guild of Impossible Things learned, and the last one most of them ever mastered: the ledger does not record the trade. The ledger *is* the trade.

Outside, Copper Street was still dark, but she could hear the city waking in its small ways — the milk cart's iron-rimmed wheels, a shutter banging somewhere on Tanner's Row, the cough of the chimney-sweep's boy who climbed before his lungs were ready for it. Winter was coming early this year. Everyone said so. The geese had gone south in September without quarreling about it, and the frost had come down from the hills like a creditor who no longer bothered to knock.

Marguerite dipped her pen and opened the first ledger.

*Account of Maren Doss, widow, of Copper Street.* Below it, in her own careful hand from the spring: *One (1) bottled lullaby, received in trade against debt of luck incurred at cards, value assessed at three years' good fortune.*

The lullaby sat in its stoppered glass vial in the strongroom below, glowing faintly amber when the light hit it right, humming so softly that only someone who listened for a living could hear it. Marguerite could have hummed it herself once. She'd appraised it personally, holding the vial to her ear the way a physician holds a watch, and inside had been a woman's voice singing a child down into sleep — some copper-street mother dead now, probably, or gone, but her voice still warm and patient in the glass, still singing to no one.

Three years' good fortune. Maren Doss had lost those years at the Silver Eel, losing them to a luck-thief named Ferren Vale, which was how such things always went: the poor gambling away what they couldn't spare, the rich never having to gamble at all because they simply took.

Marguerite wrote the entry for the sale. *One (1) bottled lullaby, sold to Lady Vess Corvane-Ilane, of the Hill Houses, against her account, price: eleven years' good fortune, payable in installments.* The guild bought grief cheap and sold it dear. That was not cruelty; that was arithmetic. But her pen hesitated over the line all the same, hovering above the paper like a bird deciding whether to land.

*The ledger does not care what you feel,* she reminded herself, and brought the nib down.

* * *

By midmorning the hall was awake around her. Old Sef, the doorkeeper, had gotten the fires going, and the smell of woodsmoke threaded through the ink-smell. A client waited on the bench by the door — a young man, threadbare coat, hat wrung in his hands like laundry. Marguerite knew the type. First-time sellers always wrung their hats.

"Name?" she asked, though she already knew it. Everyone on Copper Street knew the clerk with the gray eyes who never smiled and never cheated you, either, which in that neighborhood amounted to sainthood.

"Tobin Marsh." His voice cracked on it. "I've come about — I want to borrow. Not sell. Borrow."

"Borrowing is more expensive than selling," Marguerite said, not unkindly. "Selling is clean. Borrowing means interest."

"I know." He swallowed. "It's my sister. She's dying, and the physicians say she won't see the thaw. I want to borrow five years — just five — long enough for the new medicines from the coast to reach us. I'll pay back whatever—"

"Five years is a great deal to pay back," Marguerite said. "Where will you get them?"

"From myself. When they come due. When she's well, I'll—" He stopped, because he understood, even if he couldn't say it. Paying back borrowed years meant giving up years you hadn't lived yet. It meant arriving at your own future shortchanged, gray early, tired early, done early.

Marguerite looked at him for a long moment. Then she pulled a fresh ledger toward her and uncapped the ink.

"The guild will lend four," she said. "Not five. Four is survivable. Five is a grave with extra steps." She wrote as she spoke, each character precise. "Terms: repayment begins the spring after your sister recovers, at one year per annum, drawn from your natural span. If she dies anyway, the debt passes to her estate, and I will personally make sure the guild takes nothing from a dead woman's children. Initial here."

Tobin Marsh stared at the page as if it were written in a language he'd been promised he'd never have to learn. Then he signed, and his hand shook so badly the mark looked like a small bird lying on its side.

When he'd gone, Sef shuffled over with tea and set it down beside her elbow without being asked. He'd been doing that for six years, since the day she'd arrived with a sealed letter of introduction and a sword wrapped in oilcloth that nobody was permitted to ask about.

"You work too hard, missus clerk," he said.

"The accounts don't audit themselves."

"The accounts don't notice whether you eat, neither." He shuffled off again, and Marguerite looked at the tea, and drank it while it was hot, because it seemed rude not to, and because hot tea was one of the few pleasures that cost nothing and owed nothing.

* * *

The afternoon brought the shadow trade.

Borrowed shadows were delicate business — the guild's bread and butter in the autumn months, when the light came in low and long and made everyone hungry for a self they didn't have. The seller was a Hill House dandy named Pellam Ostrin, who wanted to be rid of his shadow until spring because, he claimed, it had begun to remind him of his father.

"It's a fine shadow," Marguerite said, examining it where it lay pooled under his chair, thin and autumn-colored. "Good lineage, I'd guess. Shadows inherit, you know. Yours has been passed down through at least four generations of Ostrins. There's a certain weight to it."

"That's exactly the problem." He shuddered elegantly. "What will you give me?"

"For a four-generation shadow in good condition? Storage through winter, plus a modest fee." She named the figure. He winced, haggled, settled. She wrote the contract, and when she peeled his shadow off the floor — a process involving silver tweezers, a sheet of treated vellum, and a patience that would have astonished anyone who thought clerical work was soft — it came up shivering and folded itself onto the vellum like a bat settling to roost.

Pellam Ostrin left lighter and visibly relieved, walking out into the low afternoon sun with nothing trailing him at all, and Marguerite filed the shadow in the flat cabinet with the others, where they rustled faintly when the draft touched them, like a drawer full of sleeping birds.

Alone again. The hall emptied the way it always did after four bells — clients gone, Sef gone, the other clerks gone up to their rooms — and Marguerite stayed at the great desk with the lamps and the ledgers, because the ledgers were better company than people. They never pretended. Every debt in them was exactly what it said it was, no more, no less. You could trust a ledger. Trust was the one thing the guild traded in that it never listed on its books.

She worked until the candles burned low. And then, when the hall was dark and safe, she did the thing she did every night, the ritual no one knew about, the one that would cost her everything if it were ever witnessed.

She unlocked the bottom drawer of the great desk and lifted out the oilcloth bundle.

The sword inside was old, older than the guild, older than the crown. Its name was Verity, and it had not been drawn in nineteen years. She unwrapped just enough of the cloth to bare the hilt, and laid her palm flat against the cold guard, and felt the faint answering pulse there, patient as a heartbeat in sleep.

*I am still yours,* the blade said, the way it always did. Not in words. Swordspeakers' blades didn't speak in words. It was more like remembering something you'd never actually known — a certainty arriving whole.

"And I am still yours," she whispered. "And I am still bound."

*Bound to truth,* the oath said in her blood. *Bound to speak truth with steel and to withhold it never. Bound never to strike the crowned. Bound until the order ends or the world does.*

The Order of the Veiled Blade had ended twenty-two years ago, slaughtered in a single night by men whose faces she had been too young to remember clearly — and there was the wound inside the wound, the hole in her memory where that night should have been, smooth and blank as river ice. She knew the order had died. She knew she had survived. She did not know how, and no amount of searching found anything where the knowing should have been, only that pale, featureless absence, like a page torn cleanly out of a book.

So she kept the oath anyway, alone, in a guildhall basement, with clerk's ink on her fingers and no one in the world who knew what she was. The last swordspeaker. An order of one. She hid Verity beneath the ledger of small debts, and she hid herself beneath the name Ashgrove, and both concealments held, year after year, because no one ever looked closely at clerks.

She rewrapped the blade and locked the drawer and sat for a moment in the dark, listening to the building settle around her. Somewhere above, wind worried at the eaves. The frost the milk-wives had been muttering about had finally come down off the hills; she could feel it pressing at the windows like a face.

Loneliness, at this hour, had a specific texture. It wasn't sadness exactly. It was more like standing in a room full of valuable things that belonged to other people, knowing every item's provenance and price, and owning nothing. She belonged to the ledger. The ledger did not belong to her. No one had ever signed for her.

She blew out the lamps.

* * *

She was halfway up the stairs to her room when Sef's lantern appeared at the bottom, and with it his voice, pitched strange — careful, the way he spoke only when Guildmaster Corvane sent word.

"Clerk. Missus Ashgrove. You're wanted. Master's study, tonight, if you please." He paused, and his old face in the lamplight wore something she had never seen on it before. Worry. "He says — he says it's about the oldest contract. The one sealed before the grandfather-king's reign. Says the Crown itself has asked to see it."

Marguerite stood very still on the stairs, one hand on the rail, and felt the frost press harder against the windows, and felt — faintly, absurdly, unmistakably — the sword in the locked drawer below go cold as a held breath.

"The Crown," she repeated.

"The Crown," Sef said. "And missus clerk — the seal on the request. I saw it, carrying the message up from the palace. I'm an old man and my eyes are bad, but I'd swear on my mother's grave I've seen that mark before. Drawn in frost, on the inside of a window. Last week. On Copper Street."

Marguerite descended the stairs past him without a word, her footsteps quiet on the stone, toward the study where the guildmaster waited with the oldest contract in the guild's keeping — and a seal she suddenly needed very much to see.
