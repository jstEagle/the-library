## 3. What the Guild Owes the Crown

The summons came at the hour when the guildhall's lamps were trimmed low and the clerks had gone home to their suppers, leaving Marguerite alone with the winter accounts and the particular silence of a building full of other people's secrets.

It arrived not as a letter — Corvane did not write to his own people; writing could be stolen, read, held against you — but as a boy of perhaps ten years, breathless from the stairs, holding out a plain card of grey stock with nothing on it but her name in the Guildmaster's narrow hand.

*Marguerite Ashgrove. The deep archive. Now.*

She looked at the card for a long moment before she looked at the boy. "Did he say why?"

"No, miss." The boy shifted his weight. He was one of the foundlings who swept the counting rooms, and his eyes went, as all the foundlings' eyes did, to the ledger open on her desk — drawn to it the way moths were drawn to flame, or debtors to the promise of forgiveness. "He only said *now*, miss."

"Then we won't keep him waiting."

She closed the ledger of small debts — three hundred and eleven entries since the first frost, each one a little impossible thing bought or sold: a bottled lullaby traded to a sleepless merchant's wife, a borrowed shadow returned to its owner with interest, a jar of someone else's courage that had gone sour in storage and would need decanting before spring. She locked it in the iron cabinet, turned the key twice, and felt the small familiar satisfaction of order maintained. Whatever else the world was doing — whatever strange frost was blooming in widow's parlors on Copper Street, whatever price was being collected for answered prayers — the ledger balanced. That much, she could still make true.

"Lead on," she said to the boy, and followed him down.

* * *

The deep archive lived beneath the guildhall like a heart beneath ribs, and reaching it meant descending through the building's whole history. Past the counting rooms with their green-shaded lamps and their clerks scratching entries into books that would outlive them. Past the vaults where the more valuable holdings slept — years in stoppered glass, names folded into paper like pressed flowers, a grief so large it occupied an entire crate and hummed faintly when you walked by. Past the door marked *REDEMPTIONS* where desperate folk came to buy back what they'd sold, and where most of them learned that some things, once sold, appreciated beyond any buyer's reach.

And then down again, past all of that, to the oldest part: the archive proper, where the air tasted of cold stone and old ink, and where the guild kept the contracts it did not speak of above ground.

Corvane was already there.

Guildmaster Edran Corvane was not a large man, but he filled rooms the way water filled a hull — quietly, inevitably, from the bottom up. He stood tonight among the oldest shelves with a single lamp beside him, and the light made hollows of his eye sockets and turned his silver hair the color of frost. In sixty years he had built the guild from a smugglers' ring into an institution with a charter, a seal, and a seat at the king's council table, and he had done it, Marguerite had always believed, through the simple principle that everything could be entered in a ledger, and everything entered in a ledger could be governed.

"You came quickly," he said, without turning around.

"You said now."

"I did." He lifted the lamp and began walking down the aisle between the shelves, and she fell into step beside him because there was no other place to be. "Tell me what you know about our founding, Ashgrove. Not the story we tell the crown. The true one."

Marguerite kept her face still. This was a test, or a trap, or both, and the difference mattered less than her footing. "The guild was chartered ninety-one years ago under King Aldric's grandfather, Osric the Second. Before that, we were unlicensed brokers operating out of the Ropewalk district. The charter legitimized us in exchange for regulation, taxation, and first refusal on any trade involving the royal household."

"And the charter itself? Where is it kept?"

"Here," she said. "Third shelf, iron case, sealed with the guild mark and the royal signet."

Corvane stopped walking. He set the lamp down on a reading table between two shelves, and in its light Marguerite saw what he had come down here to show her — or perhaps what he had come down here to look at himself, alone, before she arrived, and had failed to finish looking at.

A contract lay open on the table. It was not the charter. It was older than the charter — the vellum had gone the color of weak tea, and the ink had faded to the brown of dried blood, and along the left margin someone had sewn a reinforcement strip of leather so ancient it had cracked into scales. But it was the seal at the foot of the page that stopped Marguerite's breath.

Not wax. Wax melted; this had been chosen to last. The seal was pressed metal, a disc of grey-silver alloy worked into a design she knew — knew intimately, terribly, from four nights ago, from a widow's parlor on Copper Street where frost had bloomed indoors in autumn in patterns too regular to be weather.

Frost sigils. Interlocking lines like feathers of ice, radiating from a center, enclosing — she leaned closer, and her stomach turned over slowly, politely, like something trying not to be sick — enclosing a shape she had not been able to see in the widow's window, because there the pattern had been incomplete. Here it was whole. At the heart of the frozen feathering sat a crowned figure, small as a thumbnail, arms raised.

"Where did this come from?" she asked. Her voice came out level. She was proud of that, later.

"The deepest shelf," Corvane said. "The one we don't catalog. It has been here since before my predecessor's predecessor, and no one living knows how it arrived. It predates the charter, Ashgrove. It predates the *city's* charter. And tonight the palace sent for it."

"The king requested it?"

"The king requested it." Corvane's mouth did something that was not quite a smile. "Or rather — a request arrived bearing the king's signet, in the king's hand, asking for the guild's oldest contract by name. A contract, I will remind you, that appears in none of our catalogs. That I have never shown to another soul. That I myself learned of only when I inherited this office, from a list of uncatalogued holdings passed from Guildmaster to Guildmaster for ninety years, each entry in a different hand, each entry saying only the same thing: *Do not trade this. Do not display this. Do not speak of this above ground.*"

"And yet the king knows of it."

"And yet," Corvane agreed softly, "the king knows of it."

* * *

Marguerite moved around the table slowly, the way one moved around a sleeping animal, and read what she could of the ancient contract without touching it.

The language was archaic — older than the common script, shot through with formal constructions the courts had abandoned centuries ago. But she had clerk's training and a clerk's patience, and she pieced together the bones of it while Corvane watched her in silence.

It was a contract of payment. That much was clear from the opening clauses, which enumerated — in the manner of all guild contracts, in the manner of every ledger she had ever kept — what was owed and to whom. But the currency was wrong. Not coin. Not luck, not years, not names, though those appeared further down, in lists that read like inventories: *forty years, remitted; seven names, surrendered; the grief of a city, harvested.* The primary payment, the consideration for which the whole instrument existed, was named in the first line, and the word used was one she had seen only once before, in a text she should never have been able to read, in a life she was supposed to have put behind her.

*Remembrance.*

"They are paying in memories," she said. "Someone is paying the crown in memories. Or—" She stopped. Read the line again. The grammar of the old tongue doubled back on itself, and the second reading was worse than the first. "No. Someone is paying *for* memories. The crown is the seller. These clauses here—" her finger hovered over the inventory lists without touching the vellum, an old habit; you did not touch another party's obligations — "these aren't payments received. They're deliveries promised. The crown owes these. Forty years. Seven names. The grief of a city. It's a supply contract, Guildmaster. The crown has contracted to *provide* memory to — to whoever holds the other seal."

"To whoever holds the other seal," Corvane repeated. "Yes. I reached the same reading. Now tell me what you noticed about the sigil that made you go pale as milk, because you have not gone pale as milk in the six years you have served me, including the night the Vesper Street vault flooded and we lost nine hundred years of stored luck."

Marguerite straightened. She considered lying to him. She considered it seriously, and for longer than she liked — because the truth involved Copper Street, and a widow named Sera Ilven who no longer remembered her son's face, and four nights of Marguerite's own investigation that she had conducted quietly, off the books, without reporting to the man who owned her wages and, she had begun to suspect, considerably more than her wages.

But Corvane had just shown her an uncatalogued contract that predated the guild's founding, and had admitted he did not know its origin. Men like Edran Corvane did not admit ignorance. They admitted it only when ignorance had become more dangerous than confession.

So she told him. About the widow Ilven, whose prayer for rain had been answered with a storm that fell on one street and one street only, in autumn, in a city that hadn't seen rain in two months. About the woman standing in her doorway afterward, weeping, unable to say why — until Marguerite had asked after her family and the widow had said, *I had a son,* with the flat bewilderment of someone reciting a fact stripped of all flesh. *I know I had a son. I can't remember his face. I can't remember anything about him except that I loved him, and even the love feels like a coat someone else left in my house.*

About the frost on the inside of the windows. The sigils in it. The way they matched — feather for feather, line for line — the seal on this table.

"And the prayer," Marguerite finished. "I asked her what she'd prayed for, and to whom. She said she prayed the way everyone prays now, to the old gods, the ones nobody believes in anymore. She said it half-laughing. Like it was a habit. Like lighting a candle for a friend who'd moved away." Marguerite looked down at the crowned figure at the heart of the frost. "Her prayer was answered, Guildmaster. Impossibly, specifically, within hours. And the price was collected immediately, and the price was memory. Which means either the old gods have woken and started charging market rates — or something is wearing the shape of an answered prayer, and feeding."

The lamp flame bent in a draft from the stairwell, though no door had opened. Corvane did not seem to notice, or chose not to.

"You believe the crown is selling," he said. "That King Aldric — or someone acting with his signet — is supplying memories to satisfy this contract."

"I believe the law and the guild are entangled, and have been entangled far longer than either of us has been alive, and that this document is the knot." She tapped the air above the delivery clauses. "Look at the dates, if they are dates. The intervals between deliveries shorten toward the end of the page. Whoever bound this contract built acceleration into it. Either the buyer's hunger grows, or the seller's ability to pay diminishes, and the schedule tightens to compensate. And the final clause—" she had saved it, had been circling toward it, dreading it— "the final clause isn't a date at all. It's a condition. *Until the vessel is empty.*"

Silence filled the archive the way cold filled it: completely, and without effort.

"Vessel," Corvane said at last.

"Aldric's grandfather signed the charter. Aldric's father renewed it. The contract predates them all — which means the crown party to it cannot be a specific king. It has to be the crown itself. The office. Something attached to the throne that passes from head to head regardless of who wears it." Marguerite heard her own voice going quiet, going careful, the way it went when she was approaching a number in a ledger that she already knew would not balance. "Something is wearing the crown, Guildmaster. And it has been drawing payments against the kings of this city for longer than the guild has existed. And if the vessel is the king — if the memories being delivered are coming out of *him*, out of the man and not the office — then the request tonight isn't a request. It's a withdrawal. The account has come due, and the thing holding the note wants to inspect its collateral."

Corvane was silent for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice had changed — lost the dry authority, gained something older and wearier, the voice of a man who had spent sixty years believing every problem could be entered in a ledger discovering the limits of his faith.

"There is one more thing you should see," he said. "Before you decide what you've concluded. Because I have watched you work, Ashgrove, for six years, and you are the best auditor I have ever employed, and the most dangerous kind of honest, and I am about to ask you to do something that may require all of both." He reached into his coat and drew out a slim folio, tied with black cord, and laid it on the table beside the ancient contract. "The palace request specified a courier. Not a guard, not a minister. An auditor. Someone who could verify the contents against the original terms and certify the delivery. They named you."

"They couldn't have. No one at court knows my name."

"They didn't give your name." Corvane untied the cord with fingers that were, Marguerite noticed, not entirely steady. "They gave a description. Age, build, coloring. And a phrase — a passphrase, of the sort the guild uses for high-value transfers. I assumed it referred to some broker you'd dealt with. Until I checked the archives." He opened the folio. Inside lay a single sheet of old paper, and on it, in a hand Marguerite recognized with a lurch she could not suppress — a hand she had last seen decades ago, in a hall that no longer existed, spoken words now written down in ink gone brown —

Corvane watched her read it. "You know this," he said. "Don't you. You know this hand, and you know these words, and I want you to tell me right now, Ashgrove, before that courier request arrives at dawn and you walk through the palace gates carrying our oldest sin in your hands — I want you to tell me what the guild has been keeping in its deepest shelf all these years, and what it is that knows your *other* name."

The lamp flame stood perfectly still. The archive breathed its cold around them. And Marguerite looked down at the passphrase written in a dead swordspeaker's hand — *the blade speaks truth, and truth strikes the crowned* — and understood, with the terrible clarity of a ledger finally balancing, that she had not been summoned tonight to carry a contract to the palace.

She had been summoned because the contract had been waiting for her all along.
