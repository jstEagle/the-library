## 4. The Thing Wearing His Crown

The summons came at dawn, written on paper the color of old teeth.

*Marguerite Ashgrove, Keeper of Accounts, is commanded to attend His Majesty King Aldric at the Winter Palace, third hour after sunrise. By order of the Crown.*

She read it three times by candlelight, and each time her eyes snagged on the same detail: the wax seal was not the seal of Aldric's house. It was older. A circle broken by a vertical line, like an eye that had been stitched shut — and around its edges, pressed into the cooling wax by some hand long dead or long hungry, a tracery of frost-ferns. The same sigils she had found blooming across Widow Hessa's window glass on Copper Street. The same frost that had grown *indoors*, in autumn, in a warm room where a woman had prayed for rain and woken unable to remember her son's face.

Marguerite set the letter down on her desk, next to the ledger of small debts, and looked out the window at the guildhall courtyard, where the first real cold of the season was silvering the pump handle. Somewhere in this city, prayers were being answered. Someone — something — was collecting the change.

And now it had requested her by name.

*Commanded,* she thought. Not invited. Commanded, as one commands a clerk to bring the books. Which meant the palace knew what she was to the guild, which meant Corvane had talked, which meant —

Which meant she would go. Of course she would go. She was oath-bound, and beneath the oath she was curious, and beneath the curiosity she was lonely in the particular way of people who belong to ledgers rather than to anyone. She dressed in her good grey coat, the one with ink stains at the cuffs she could not fully remove, strapped her sword beneath it where no courtier would think to look, and walked out into a morning that smelled of woodsmoke and coming snow.

The Winter Palace rose at the city's heart like a held breath. Marguerite had passed it a thousand times without entering; guild clerks did not walk through those gates unless they carried debts, and even then they entered by the servants' door and left by the same. Today a footman in livery the blue of deep ice met her at the gate itself and said nothing at all, only turned and walked, and she followed him up avenues of bare lindens whose branches clicked together overhead like counting beads.

She noticed the strangeness before she reached the doors: the fountains were running. In late autumn, with frost on the ground, every fountain in the palace grounds flowed freely, and the water in them did not ice over. It steamed faintly, instead, as though the cold had been drawn elsewhere. Drawn inward. She thought of frost ferns on the inside of widow Hessa's windows and felt the hair rise along her arms.

*The impossible has a price,* she reminded herself. *Someone is collecting.*

The footman left her in an antechamber hung with tapestries so old their dye had faded to ghosts — hunts, coronations, a harvest scene where every face had worn away to blank linen. She waited there alone for the better part of an hour, and in that hour she counted things, because counting was how she kept the world from sliding. Forty-one steps in the marble floor pattern. Nine candles, six lit. One draft, persistent, coming not from the windows but from *below*, rising through the floor seams like breath through a keyhole, carrying a smell she could not place until suddenly she could: it was the smell of the inside of the guild's memory-vaults. Cold stone and old paper and something sweet gone slightly wrong, like honey left too many winters.

When the chamberlain finally came for her, he did not announce her. He simply opened the far doors and stood aside, and Marguerite understood that whatever waited beyond them already knew her name.

The king's study was small — smaller than any room she'd expected in a palace, low-ceilinged and crowded with books, warm where the antechamber had been cold, lit by a fire that crackled with the honest sound of ordinary oak. And standing at the window with his back to her was a man she recognized from coins and proclamations and one distant coronation she had watched from a crowd at fourteen: King Aldric, third of his name, broad-shouldered, grey at the temples, dressed not in state robes but in a plain wool coat with the buttons done wrong.

"Master Ashgrove." He did not turn. "Or is it Mistress? Forgive me. I find titles slip away from me lately, like fish through cold hands."

"Keeper," she said. "I keep accounts for the Guild of Impossible Things."

"The Guild of Impossible Things." He said it slowly, tasting each word, and turned at last.

Marguerite had prepared herself for many things. A tyrant. A puppet. A man already hollowed, eyes like the inside of a dropped egg. What she had not prepared for was warmth. Aldric's face was tired but kind, creased around the mouth with the habit of smiling, and when his gaze settled on her it settled *gently*, the way a hand settles on a frightened animal.

"You keep accounts," he said. "Then you'll understand me when I say I have been trying to balance my own, and the sums will not come right."

"Your Majesty?"

"There are hours missing." He laughed — a short, embarrassed sound, utterly unkingly. "That sounds mad. Strike it. I summoned you because your guild holds a contract older than my grandfather's reign, and I am told it concerns the crown itself, and I wanted—" he paused, and something moved behind his eyes, "—I wanted to look at the person who keeps such books. To see if she seemed honest."

"And do I seem honest, Your Majesty?"

His eyes flickered.

It was small — so small that anyone else would have missed it, would have called it lamplight or tiredness. But Marguerite had spent her life reading faces the way she read columns of figures, hunting the discrepancy, the digit that didn't carry. Aldric's warmth went out like a snuffed candle. For half a heartbeat his eyes were not kind at all. They were pale, and patient, and *old* — old the way winter is old, old the way the space between stars is old — and they looked at her with an attention that had nothing human in it, an attention like a ledger being opened.

Then it was gone. He blinked, and the kindness returned, and he frowned faintly, as if he himself had felt the absence.

"Forgive me," he said again. "What were we—"

"Accounts," Marguerite said carefully. "You were asking whether I seemed honest."

"Honest." He nodded slowly. "Yes. You do. That's worse, you know. Honest clerks ask questions." He smiled, and the smile was entirely his own, and entirely exhausted. "Thank you for coming, Keeper. Give my regards to your Guildmaster. Tell him the crown remembers what it owes."

*The crown remembers.* She carried the phrase out of the palace like a stone in her shoe, turning it over and over, and it was not until she was through the gates that she let herself name what she had seen in that flicker. Not madness. Not illness. Something else had looked out through the King of Vellamere's eyes, and it had known her, and it had been *checking its figures*.

* * *

Physician Maren Ostrow had served the crown for twenty-two years, and in all that time she had never once lied to a royal personage, withheld a diagnosis, or doubted her own craft. She told herself these things now, standing in her cluttered surgery above the palace infirmary, while the guild-clerk with ink-stained cuffs sat across from her and waited with terrible patience.

She had begged the woman to come up privately. That alone was a confession.

"Hours," Maren said at last. "He loses hours. Whole hours, sometimes most of a day. It began in spring — little gaps, minutes, nothing a busy man would notice. By midsummer it was mornings. Now—" She stopped, pressed her lips together, started again. "Now there are days I cannot account for. He wakes with frost on the inside of the windows. He writes letters in a hand that is not his own. Last month I found him in the snow garden at midnight, barefoot, talking to the hedges as if they were old friends, and when I brought him in he wept and asked me who had won the war."

"What war?" Marguerite asked quietly.

"That's the thing." Maren's voice dropped. "There hasn't been a war in his lifetime. But he grieved for it. He grieved the way men grieve for sons. And the names he said over and over — I wrote them down." She pulled a scrap of paper from her sleeve with a physician's precise hand. "I don't know them. No battle in our histories carries them."

Marguerite took the paper. The names were old. Old the way the contract-seal was old, old the way the frost sigils were old, and two of them she had seen before — carved into the lintel of the guild's oldest vault, worn nearly smooth, above words in a dead tongue that the guild's founding charter translated only once and then forbade translation again.

"These are swordspeaker names," she said, before she could stop herself.

Maren stared. "The swordspeakers are a story. An order that died out centuries ago."

"Yes," Marguerite said. "We are." She heard her own voice go flat and careful, the voice she used for closing accounts, and hated it. "Tell me about the frost."

"It comes indoors. It blooms on glass, on mirrors, on the still water in basins — always in patterns, always the same patterns. And here is what I cannot put in any journal, Keeper, what I will deny saying if you repeat it." Maren leaned forward, and Marguerite saw that the physician's hands were shaking. "The frost follows grief. When the king weeps — and he weeps more and more, though he cannot say for what — the frost spreads. When the household mourns — we lost a scullery maid to fever in the autumn, and the kitchen girls cried over her — the frost came down the whole east corridor overnight, ferns of it, beautiful, covering the walls where the girls had sat and sobbed. And the next morning those girls had forgotten her. Both of them. They set her tray out of habit and could not tell you whose it was. They laughed about it. They *laughed*, and I have not slept properly since."

The fire popped. Somewhere below, the palace went on with its enormous indifferent day.

"He is being eaten," Maren said. "Not his body. His body is sound. Whatever it is, it eats what he *remembers*, and it does not stop at him. It takes the grief around him, the sorrow of everyone under this roof, and it leaves them light and empty and strangely content, and I am the only one in this palace who seems to find that horrifying, and I am an old woman with a bad heart, and I cannot fight it, and neither can you—" she stopped, and looked at Marguerite's coat, at the ink-stained cuffs, and her eyes narrowed with a clinician's sudden precision. "But you didn't flinch. I've described an impossible thing eating a king alive, and you didn't flinch. You checked the names against something. Who *are* you?"

"I'm a clerk," Marguerite said.

"You're a clerk who knows dead swordspeakers' names on sight."

"I'm a clerk," Marguerite repeated, "who keeps accounts of impossible things. And what you've just described, Physician, has a price attached. Prayers answered. Grief collected. Someone is spending this city's sorrows like coin, and the mint is under this roof." She stood, tucking the scrap of names into her sleeve beside the summons. "How much longer does he have? Honestly. Physician to clerk."

Maren Ostrow closed her eyes. When she opened them, all the professional composure had burned away, and what remained was simply a woman who had loved her patient for twenty-two years the way physicians love — fiercely, uselessly, at arm's length.

"By my reckoning," she said, "he has until the first true snowfall. After that, I don't believe there will be enough of him left to miss."

Marguerite walked back through the palace corridors, past the fountains that steamed in the cold, past servants who smiled too easily and laughed at nothing, and the horror grew in her with every step, quiet and total, the way frost grows on glass — beautiful, patterned, and eating inward. She had come expecting a corrupt court, a greedy guildmaster's entanglement, a debt to be audited. Debts she knew how to handle. Debts had columns, and columns had sums, and sums could be balanced.

But this was not a debt. This was a *hunger*, wearing a crown, keeping its own ledger — and in its ledger, she was beginning to suspect, her own name had been entered long ago.

At the gates she paused and looked back. High in the king's study, the window had frosted over from the inside, ferns of white spreading across the glass in patterns she now recognized perfectly, and behind the frost a single lamp burned steady, like a heart refusing to stop.

Winter was coming early this year. And it had already learned the king's face by heart.
