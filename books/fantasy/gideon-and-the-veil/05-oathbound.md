## 5. Oathbound

The mountain did not have a name anymore, but it had once, and the swordspeakers had agreed that forgetting its name was part of the price of coming there.

Marguerite Ashgrove was seventeen years old and had been climbing for three days when she finally saw the hall: a low stone shoulder jutting from the snowfield like the back of a sleeping animal, its roof furred with ice, its single door facing east so that the first light of every morning fell across the threshold whether anyone rose to meet it or not. Smoke curled from a vent-stone near the peak — thin, gray, almost apologetic smoke, as if even the fire here had taken a vow of restraint.

Her guide, an old woman named Serah who had spoken perhaps forty words in three days, stopped at the edge of the snowfield and would go no farther.

"They'll ask you what you're willing to lose," Serah said. Her voice sounded rusted from disuse. "Don't answer quickly. The quick answers are the ones they take."

"What did you lose?" Marguerite asked.

Serah smiled, and it was the first time Marguerite had seen her do it, and it made her face briefly, terribly young. "I don't remember," she said. "That's rather the point." She turned and started back down the mountain, and Marguerite watched her shrink to a dark stitch in the white cloth of the world before she turned to the door herself.

The door was oak older than any kingdom, iron-banded, unmarked by sigil or crest. It opened at her touch without a sound, which frightened her more than any lock could have.

* * *

Inside, the hall smelled of cold stone and lamp oil and something underneath both — a green smell, like sap, like a living thing at the heart of the building. Torches burned in brackets along the walls, but they gave less light than warmth, and the shadows between them were deep enough to drown in. At the far end of the hall stood the oath-stone.

She had heard about it her whole childhood, in fragments, in the way children hear about sacred things: secondhand, half-whispered, always slightly wrong. It was supposed to be a boulder. It was supposed to be a slab. It was neither. It was a pillar of black glass taller than two men, rising out of the floor as though the floor had grown around it, and its surface was not smooth — it moved, very slowly, the way ice moves on a river, and if she looked too long at it her eyes watered and her teeth ached and she had the distinct sensation of being read.

Around the stone, in a wide crescent, knelt eleven figures in gray. Not robes exactly — traveling clothes, sword-worn boots, cloaks patched at the shoulders where scabbards rubbed. Swordspeakers did not dress like priests because they were not priests. They were something older and more practical and far more frightening.

One of them rose. He was tall and narrow as a winter tree, with white hair braided back and a face so lined it looked like a map of somewhere Marguerite wanted desperately to go. His sword hung at his hip in a plain leather scabbard, and she noticed — with the strange clarity that would stay with her for the rest of her life — that his hand never left the hilt, not even to kneel, not even to greet her. As if the sword were a limb he could not bear to be parted from. As if letting go might mean something.

"Marguerite Ashgrove," he said. "Of Copper Street, of the tallow-chandler's family, of the city that has forgotten we exist."

"Yes."

"Do you know why we are called swordspeakers?"

"Because your blades speak truth," she said. "Because a lie cannot pass the edge."

"Close enough for a child. Wrong enough that you must learn better." He gestured, and she came forward across the stones, her boots loud in all that silence. "Our blades do not speak truth. Our blades *remember* truth. There is a difference, and the difference is everything. Any fool can speak a true thing. To remember it — to carry it, year after year, when carrying it costs you — that is the work. That is the vow." He studied her the way she had seen merchants study coin suspected of being clipped. "You come recommended. Your grandmother's grandmother held a blade here. Do you know what that means?"

"That I have good blood?"

"It means you have debts," he said, and something in his tone made her stomach drop through the floor. "Everything in this world is owed to something else, child. The mountain to the sky. The river to the rain. You to the women who carried this oath before you could walk. We do not ask you here to honor us. We ask you here because the debt has come due, and you are the heir."

Marguerite looked at the eleven kneeling figures, at their patient faces, at the black glass breathing slow as a sleeper behind them. She was seventeen. She had walked away from a chandler's shop and a mother who cried easily and a city that smelled of tallow and river mud, and she had told herself she was walking toward glory.

"I'm listening," she said, which was the bravest sentence she owned.

* * *

They fed her first. This surprised her; she had expected fasting, vigil, some theatrical deprivation. Instead they sat her at a long table in a side room and gave her barley soup with marrow and dark bread and a cup of small beer, and an old woman with a sword scarred down one cheek sat across from her and ate with the hearty indifference of a farmer.

"Eat well," the woman said. "It's the last meal you'll have as only yourself."

"What do you mean?"

"The oath takes a tithe. Everyone pays differently. Some lose a color. Some lose a season — one man lost every spring he'd ever lived through, woke up the next morning unable to remember what thaw smelled like, poor bastard wept into his porridge for a week." She tore her bread and shrugged. "I lost my sister's laugh. I know I had a sister. I know I loved her. I can tell you the color of her dress at her wedding. But the laugh is gone, and when I try to reach for it there's just — a shape where it was. A hole with edges." She pointed her spoon at Marguerite. "So eat. And think hard about what you'd least mind losing, because the stone doesn't negotiate, but it does listen. It takes what you hold loosest. Hold tight to what matters."

"And if I hold tight to everything?"

The woman smiled, and the scar pulled white across her cheek. "Then you're not honest yet. Nobody holds tight to everything. There's always something we're quietly glad to be rid of. The stone finds it. The stone always finds it." She leaned closer, and her voice dropped. "My advice? Decide what you're willing to give before you kneel. Choose your wound. A chosen wound heals clean. An unchosen one festers."

* * *

They came for her at moonrise.

The hall was colder now, or she was simply aware of the cold for the first time — the way you become aware of your own heartbeat only when it stumbles. The eleven stood in their crescent around the black glass, and the master — his name was Aldous Vane, she had learned, though everyone called him First Blade — stood closest, his hand still wrapped in the leather of his hilt.

"Bare your sword," he said.

She drew it. It was her father's — no, her grandfather's, a blade older than she was, kept oiled in a linen wrap beneath her childhood bed, the one object in the chandler's house that had never been for sale. The steel caught the torchlight and threw it back wrong, somehow, softer than steel should be, like light through water.

"Lay the flat against the stone."

The black glass was warm. That was the detail that undid her composure, then and forever after — she had braced herself for cold, for the bite of winter, and instead the stone met her blade like a hand meeting a hand, warm as a living palm, and she felt the sword *answer*, felt a tremor run up through the grip into her wrist, her elbow, her sternum, a hum like a struck bell that had been waiting decades for the mallet.

"Now," Aldous Vane said, "the words. Speak them knowing they are not decoration. Speak them knowing each clause is a chain, and that you will wear these chains longer than you can currently imagine, because you are seventeen and seventeen cannot imagine thirty, let alone sixty. Speak them anyway."

She spoke them.

*"I swear my blade to truth above mercy, above law, above love. What my edge witnesses, my mouth shall not deny, though the denying save my life. What my edge must do, my heart shall not refuse, though the refusing spare my grief.*

*I swear my blade to the memory of the bound thing. While one swordspeaker draws breath, the seal shall not want for a keeper.*

*I swear my blade shall never fall upon the crowned. King or tyrant, just or rotten, the crown is outside my reach, now and until my death unstrings my arm.*

*This I pay: whatever the stone requires. This I keep: the truth entire. So sworn, so witnessed, so owed."*

The last word left her mouth and the stone drank it.

There was no thunder. No light. The torches did not gutter. What happened was smaller and vastly worse: the hum in the blade deepened until it passed below hearing and became a feeling, a settling, like a house shifting onto new foundations, and Marguerite felt something leave her.

She had decided, in the hours since the scarred woman's advice, what she would hold loosest. She had decided to offer her fear of her mother's disappointment — a small, shabby fear, a splinter she'd carried since she was nine, and she had imagined the stone taking it and her walking lighter ever after.

The stone took something else.

It took the harbor at home. All of it — the gulls and the tar-smell and the way the morning fog came off the water in slow gray ropes, the specific creak of the third pier, the fishwives' cries, the lanterns doubled and trembling on the tide. She knew, instantly and horribly, that she had loved it, because love was the shape of the hole it left. She could name every fact about it. She could draw it, probably, from descriptions she'd heard. But the *living* of it was gone, scooped out clean as flesh from a shell, and in its place was a smooth blank wall with her own name written on the other side of it.

She did not cry. She would later understand that this was the first lesson of the order, taught before any other: the stone does not stop for tears, and neither may you.

"It took the harbor," she said. Her voice came out flat and strange.

"The stone chooses," Aldous Vane said, and there was no apology in it, and no cruelty either — only the terrible gentleness of a man who had watched this a hundred times and learned that apology made it harder. "It takes what anchors you to the world you're leaving. You cannot serve truth and belong anywhere else. That is the cost of belonging to us." He paused. "Do you wish to withdraw?"

And here was the trap, laid bare, and she saw it even at seventeen: the question came *after*. The tithe was already paid. Withdrawal would not return the harbor. There was no path now that led back to the girl who had climbed the mountain, and the masters knew it, and asked anyway, because the asking was itself part of the vow — the moment where you chose, freely, to remain in the country you'd already been exiled into.

She looked at the eleven faces. Patient. Weathered. Each one carrying holes of their own, smooth-walled, name-written. Each one still standing watch over a seal that the world below had stopped believing in, guarding a hunger most people thought was a story mothers told to make children come inside before dark.

"No," Marguerite said. "I'm staying."

Aldous Vane nodded once, and something in his shoulders loosened — relief, she realized, old and worn thin, the relief of a man who had feared, again, that he would watch another heir turn back down the mountain with a hole in her chest and nothing gained to show for it.

"Then rise, Swordswoman Ashgrove," he said. "Your blade remembers truth now. Feed it honestly, and it will serve you past your death."

* * *

For six years she served.

She learned the ledger-keeping that underpinned the vows — the order had always kept accounts, debts of witness and debts of silence, columns of names going back further than any kingdom on the map. She learned that the Veil was real, that it slept beneath the palace hill in the capital, that the first swordspeakers had bound it with memories the way you bind a door with nails, and that the binding thinned a little every generation, the way all bindings do. She learned why the oath forbade striking the crowned: not reverence, but *proximity* — the crowned lived nearest the root, and a swordspeaker's blade driven into a king would crack the seal from above like an axe through a frozen pond.

She learned the others' losses, eventually, traded across late fires like currency. One woman had lost her wedding day. One man had lost the taste of honey. Aldous Vane, when he was very drunk once a year on the winter solstice, admitted he had lost his own father's face, and that he had spent fifty years remembering a face he'd invented, and that he no longer knew which parts were real, and that this was, he supposed, fitting, for a keeper of truths.

"We are the order that no longer exists," he said that night, staring into the fire. "The kingdoms forgot us. The guilds forgot us. Good. Forgetting is armor. But the stone doesn't forget, girl, and neither does what sleeps beneath it. Remember that, whatever else you lose. *We are remembered by the thing we guard.* It knows our names. It counts our numbers. And it is patient the way winter is patient."

She laughed at him, gently, the way the young laugh at the old. Six years was long enough to learn the work and short enough to still believe the seal would hold forever, because seventeen-year-olds become twenty-three-year-olds and twenty-three-year-olds believe in forever too, just more quietly.

On the last night of the sixth year, the mountain sent no dream, the stone gave no warning, and Marguerite descended to the valley village for supplies and returned to find the hall door standing open and the hearth cold and eleven swordspeakers gone as completely as if the snowfield had folded over them.

No bodies. No blood. No note. Only the oath-stone, humming faintly, warm as a living palm — and, laid neatly on the altar step where Aldous Vane had always stood, a single sheet of paper bearing a seal she did not recognize: a circle of frost, eight-pointed, delicate as breath on glass.

She carried it down the mountain in her breast pocket, over her heart, the way you carry a debt you don't yet understand.

It would take her ten years and a widow's empty eyes on Copper Street to learn what it meant — and by then, the frost would already be blooming indoors, and the counting would have begun.
