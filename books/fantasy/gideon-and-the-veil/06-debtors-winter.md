## 6. Debtors' Winter

### I. The Luck-Thief

There were three rules to stealing luck, and Wren had broken all of them before his seventeenth winter.

The first rule was: never take from the desperate. The second was: never take more than you can carry. The third — the one that mattered, the one that kept a luck-thief's pockets from turning to holes — was never, ever steal from someone who could name what they'd lost.

Wren sat in the back booth of the Drowned Bell with his hands wrapped around a cup of cider gone cold an hour ago, and he thought about rule three because he'd broken it again last night. A merchant on Copper Street, fat and smug, luck pooling around him like spilled honey. Wren had skimmed it the way he always did — two fingers, a whispered word, a little tug at the world's hem — and the merchant hadn't even stumbled. But when Wren got home and counted his take, there was something wrong with it. It tasted of frost. It tasted of *prayer*.

Luck wasn't supposed to taste like anything. That was the whole point of it. Good luck was like clean water; bad luck was like standing in a draft. But this — this had a flavor, sharp and sweet and grieving, like snow falling on a grave, and Wren had spent the whole morning trying to spit it out and failing.

Which was why, when the woman in clerk's ink slid into the opposite side of his booth without asking, he didn't even flinch. He'd been expecting her for three days.

"Mistress Ashgrove," he said. "I haven't defaulted on anything."

"No." Marguerite Ashgrove set a ledger on the table between them — not her working ledger, not the guild's great book with its brass clasps, but a smaller one, bound in grey leather, its pages soft as moth wings. "You've done something worse. You've been *stealing well*."

"I don't know what that means."

"It means your last three takes are heavier than they should be. It means you're carrying luck that isn't yours and doesn't want to be yours, and it's starting to whisper to you. Am I right?"

Wren looked down at his cider. The surface had frozen over while they talked. Not iced — *frozen*, a skin of clear glass across the top, and in the glass, if he tilted his head, something like a pattern. A sigil. Frost-fern and crown.

He pushed the cup away so fast it rang against the wall.

"What is that," he said. His voice came out smaller than he wanted. "What did I take?"

"That," said Marguerite, "is the question I'm paying you to help me answer."

She opened the grey ledger and turned it toward him. On the page, in her neat clerk's hand, was written: *Wren of the Bell Lane — owes the guild nine years' luck, drawn against future fortune, interest accruing.* And beneath it, in fresh ink still shining wet: *proposed adjustment — debt suspended, winter's work, terms negotiable.*

"You're buying my loyalty," Wren said.

"I'm trading favors," said Marguerite. "It's what the guild does. It's what I do. The difference is that I intend to tell you the truth about what you're carrying, and Corvane wouldn't."

"And the truth is?"

Marguerite glanced around the Drowned Bell — the low beams, the sailors' knots in the wood, the fire that burned too quietly, as if it were listening — and lowered her voice.

"The prayers people have been praying since autumn started are being answered, Wren. All of them. Every widow who asks for rain gets rain. Every mother who begs for her fevered child gets morning and health. And every answer costs a memory — not theirs. Someone else's. Someone's past is being spent to keep this city warm and lucky and believing, and the luck you stole off that merchant is part of the spillage. You're carrying crumbs off a god's table."

Wren sat very still. Outside, the first flakes of the season's early snow drifted past the window, fat and unhurried, though it was barely past harvest.

"Whose memories?" he asked, though some cold instinct already knew.

"We don't know yet. That's why I need a thief." She tapped the page. "Debt suspended. In exchange: you find me where the luck is coming from. Follow the flavor."

Wren looked at the frozen cider, at the sigil melting now as the fire's attention returned to the room, at the woman across from him who smelled of ink and steel and something older underneath, like a sword kept oiled in a drawer for years.

"If I say no?"

"Then you keep carrying it. And by midwinter, luck-thief, you won't be able to put it down. It'll start putting things into *you*."

He thought about it for exactly as long as it took to seem prudent.

"Terms negotiable," he said. "I want dinner. Real dinner. And I want my name in that ledger under something other than *owes*."

Marguerite almost smiled. It was a small thing, that almost — but Wren made his living reading the fine print of faces, and he saw that the almost cost her something, and that she paid it anyway.

"Done," she said, and wrote it down.

### II. The Woman Who Sold Her Grief

Sera Vane had sold her grief on a Tuesday, eleven years ago, and had regretted it every day since — which was, she understood, the cruelest joke of the whole arrangement.

That was the thing nobody warned you about, selling a feeling to the guild. They took the grief itself, root and branch, the way a surgeon takes a limb. Her husband's death, the plague-wagon, the small grave behind the mason's row — all of it remained, factually, in her head. She remembered the funeral. She remembered the color of his coat. What she did not remember was *hurting*. And a hurt you cannot feel is a door you cannot close. It just stood open in her, all these years, letting the wind through.

She ran a dyeworks now. Good business. Steady hands. The other women said Sera Vane was the calmest soul in the Tannery Quarter, nothing rattled her, and Sera would smile her flat smile and think: *nothing rattles me because the bell inside was melted down for scrap.*

So when the knock came at her door on the evening of the first snow — three knocks, deliberate, the rhythm guild clerks used — she already knew why the clerk had come. Everyone in the debtor rolls knew why. The whispers had been moving through the margins of the city for a fortnight like water finding cracks: *Ashgrove is asking after us. Ashgrove is calling in favors nobody knew we held.*

She opened the door to sleet and lamplight and Marguerite Ashgrove's tired, level eyes.

"Mistress Vane. May I come in out of the weather? I promise this isn't collection."

"Nothing's ever not collection, with your lot."

"This isn't," Marguerite said. "This is the opposite of collection. Which is why I'd rather not conduct it on your doorstep."

Sera stepped aside. In her parlor, over tea that neither of them drank, Marguerite laid it out plainly, and Sera listened the way she listened to everything now — attentively, completely, and from a great distance.

"You sold your grief to the guild eleven years ago," Marguerite said. "It's still in our vaults. Grief keeps, if it's stored properly. Packed in salt and song, in the deep cellar under the guildhall. Most sellers never ask about it again. But I'm asking you to consider asking."

"Why?" Sera set down her cup. It didn't rattle. Nothing rattled. "So I can buy back my own misery at interest? So I can spend my remaining years hurting properly, like a respectable woman?"

"So you can *feel* again," Marguerite said. "All of it, not just the pleasant residue. Mistress Vane — something is eating this city's memories. Something old, wearing the crown, answering prayers with one hand and emptying people with the other. And I've begun to understand that the only weapons that work against it are the ones it has already taken and failed to digest. Stored grief. Bottled lullabies. Borrowed years. Everything the guild holds in trust." She leaned forward. "The guild's vault is full of the exact currency this thing feeds on. If it comes for the guildhall — and it will, eventually, because we are the largest hoard of memory in the city outside the palace — then every person in these rolls is a meal waiting on a shelf. Unless they're something else first. Unless they're *allies*."

Sera was quiet for a long moment. Snow ticked against the window. Somewhere in the dyeworks yard, a vat cover banged in the wind, once, twice, and went still.

"You want me to take back my husband's death," she said slowly, "so that I'll fight harder when the fighting comes."

"I want you to have the choice," said Marguerite. "I've watched this guild trade in people's insides for sixty years, and I've signed my name to it, and I am done pretending that's neutral. But I won't open your account without your hand on the page beside mine. That's the difference. That's the whole difference, and it's thin, and I know it's thin."

Sera looked at her own hands. Steady hands. Capable hands. Hands that had not shaken in eleven years, not once, not even at funerals, not even when the other widows wept and she stood among them dry-eyed and polite as a fence post.

"Will it hurt?" she asked. "Taking it back."

"Yes," said Marguerite. "That's rather the point of it."

Sera Vane found, to her enormous surprise, that she was crying. Just a little. Just enough to prove the door still stood open somewhere, even if the bell was gone.

"When?" she said.

"Winter's work," said Marguerite. "Soon. There are others I have to gather first. When we meet, it will be in the guildhall, after hours, and what happens there happens outside the law — do you understand me? No contracts. No interest. No ledger entries except the ones we write ourselves."

"Outside the law," Sera repeated. She wiped her face with a steady hand, and felt the strangest flicker under her breastbone, like a pilot light catching. "Mistress Ashgrove, I sold my grief because the law said feelings were property and property could be traded. Whatever you're building out there in the margins — build it fast."

### III. The Boy Made of Borrowed Years

Tobin counted his age in other people's birthdays.

That was how borrowed years worked. Somewhere in the guild's cellar sat forty-one small vials with his name on the wax seals, and each vial held a year that belonged — had once belonged — to somebody else. A farmer's year of good harvests. A sailor's year between voyages. A dying man's final year, sold rather than wasted. Tobin had drunk them all before he was old enough to know what drinking meant, back when the orphanage sold him by the pound to the guild's runners, and now he was fourteen years old and forty-one years deep, and the arithmetic of him didn't add up and never would.

You could see it, if you knew to look. His eyes were old. Not wise-old — *ledger*-old, tallied-old, the look of a sum that had been carried forward too many times. And sometimes, in mirrors or dark windows, he caught glimpses of the others in his face: a gray hair that wasn't his arriving early, a laugh line from a mouth that had laughed in another life, a way of squinting against sea-light he'd never seen.

He lived above the chandler's shop on Rope Walk and did odd jobs for the guild, running accounts, fetching ledgers, being small and quick and unremarkable. Nobody collected on him. That was the strange mercy of it — borrowed years couldn't be repossessed once drunk; they could only run out. Someday, the clerks said, all forty-one would come due at once, and Tobin would age forty-one years in a single afternoon, and that would be that. Until then, he was the richest poor boy in the city and the poorest rich one, and he tried not to think about the afternoon.

He was thinking about it anyway, the night Marguerite Ashgrove climbed his stairs.

She didn't knock. She stood in his doorway with snow in her dark hair and a lantern in her hand, and she said, "Hello, Tobin," and he said, "You're the swordspeaker," and she went very still.

"Where did you hear that?"

"Everyone hears things," Tobin said. "I hear more than most. Forty-one years of hearing adds up." He shifted on his cot. "They say the order died out. They say the last one hid herself in the guild as a clerk. They say her oath is still live, wound around a blade somewhere, and that's why she never sits with her back to a door."

Marguerite set the lantern on his table. In its light her face was tired and young and terribly honest, which was not what Tobin had expected from a guild officer.

"They say a great deal," she said. "Most of it's true. Does it frighten you?"

"Everything frightens me. I'm fourteen and fifty-five." He hugged his knees. "What do you want, mistress? If it's the years back, I don't have them. They're drunk. They're *in* me. You'd have to pour me out."

"I don't want your years." She crouched down to his level, which no adult had bothered to do in years, and looked at him straight. "I want to know what you see."

"See?"

"Borrowed years are strange stock, Tobin. They carry echoes. The farmer's year remembers rain. The sailor's remembers stars. And lately—" she hesitated, and Tobin heard the hesitation, and understood that this woman who traded in truths was afraid of one, "—lately, when you dream, what do you dream of?"

Tobin swallowed. Because he had been dreaming, God help him, everyone on Rope Walk had been dreaming the same dream this fortnight past: a hall of ice beneath the ground, and a throne grown out of it like a tooth out of a jaw, and on the throne a shape wearing a crown and a king's body and *nothing else*, a hunger dressed in a man, singing without sound. And in the dream the throne was built around a stone, an old stone carved with words, and the words were being eaten letter by letter, and every letter it ate, someone in the waking city forgot their mother's face.

"There's a room under the palace," he said. "Made of ice. There's a stone in it, older than the ice, and the ice grew up around it on purpose, like a trap or a coffin. And something's sitting on it. Something that used to be a king, or is using a king, I can't tell which. And it's *hungry*, mistress. It's so hungry it's turned the whole winter into a mouth."

Marguerite had gone white as the snowmelt dripping off her boots.

"The stone," she said. "In your dream. Was there writing on it?"

"One word. Over and over. I couldn't read it awake, but in the dream I could." Tobin's voice dropped. "*Speak.*"

For a long moment the only sound was the lantern's flame and the snow against the shutters. Then Marguerite Ashgrove reached into her coat and drew out the grey ledger — the new one, the one with no guild seal, the one whose pages she was filling with names in her own hand — and she wrote, by lantern light, on a boy's floor:

*Tobin of Rope Walk. Owes nothing. Owed everything.*

"Tobin," she said, "how would you like to stop being a debt?"

### IV. The Ledger of the Gathered

They came to the guildhall on the night of the third snow, by ones and twos, through doors that Marguerite unlocked herself with keys she was, strictly speaking, no longer entitled to use.

Wren arrived first, smelling of frost and stolen fortune, complaining the whole way up the back stairs that the luck in his pockets had started *humming*. Sera came second, walking slowly, one hand pressed flat below her ribs where — three days ago, in the deep cellar, by candlelight, with her hand and Marguerite's together on the page — she had taken her grief back out of storage. She wept the entire evening, quietly, without stopping, and described it afterward as the finest night of her life. Tobin came last, slipping in through the kitchen with bread stolen from his own supper, his old eyes wide at the size of the guildhall's dark.

And there were others. A man who had pawned his courage and bought it back at ruinous interest. Twin sisters who shared a single sold childhood between them. An old ferryman who had traded his fear of drowning and now crossed the winter river grinning like a fool. Eleven souls in all, gathered in the guildhall's reading room around a fire Marguerite had lit herself, and on the great table in the center of them lay the grey ledger, open, blank pages waiting.

"What is this place tonight?" Sera asked, wiping her eyes. "Whose law are we under?"

"Nobody's," said Marguerite. "That's rather the point. Tomorrow morning this room belongs to Corvane and the contracts and the crown's men. Tonight it belongs to whoever's in it. Tonight we are eleven debtors and one apostate clerk, and we are going to count what we have."

She turned the ledger toward them. At the top of the first page she wrote, in her precise clerk's hand:

*Inventory — taken freely, held in common.*

And she began to list it. Wren's stolen luck, humming with the flavor of answered prayer. Sera's grief, restored, raw, and burning like a lamp. Tobin's forty-one borrowed years, each one an echo, each echo a thread leading down — down beneath the palace, toward the ice and the stone and the hungry crowned thing that Tobin dreamed of every night. The twins' half-childhood. The ferryman's fearlessness. The pawned courage, warm in its owner's chest again.

"Memory," Marguerite said softly, writing. "Grief. Years. Fear. Courage. Luck. Do you see it? Everything this thing below the palace wants to eat — we're holding it. Every one of you is carrying a piece of the only treasure it cannot make for itself. It takes and takes, but it can't *own* the way we own. It can't choose. And that's the crack in it, friends. That's where the lever goes."

Wren raised his hand like a schoolboy, then felt foolish and lowered it halfway. "And when it notices us? When the thing wearing the king looks down at eleven debtors hoarding its dinner and comes to collect?"

"Then," said Marguerite, and here she did something none of them had seen before: she laid her hand flat on the table, and beneath the clerk's ink on her fingers they all glimpsed, for half a heartbeat, the ghost of an old callus — the sword-grip callus of a woman who had sworn an oath that forbade her, on pain of unmaking, from ever striking the crowned, "—then we will know exactly what we are, and exactly what it is, and we will settle accounts. Outside any law. The way debts should have been settled all along."

The fire cracked. Snow sighed against the high windows. And in the silence that followed, Sera Vane began, very quietly, to cry again — but this time the eleven of them sat with her, and did not look away, and let her grieve out loud in a room full of strangers who were, by the time the candles burned down, not strangers at all.

It was Tobin who noticed it last, near midnight, when the fire was embers and the grey ledger lay full of names.

Every name in it had been written twice. Once in Marguerite's hand — *owes nothing, owed everything* — and once in a second hand, faint, silverish, appearing on the page like breath on cold glass, in letters of frost.

Frost-fern and crown.

The Veil, it seemed, had found their ledger too.

And beneath the last entry, in that terrible patient script, a single line had been added overnight, addressed to no one and everyone:

*COME AND BE COUNTED.*
