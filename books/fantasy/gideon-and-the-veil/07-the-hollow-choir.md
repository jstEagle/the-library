## 7. The Hollow Choir

The bells of the Cathedral of the Ninefold Dawn rang at midnight, and by morning the children of the congregation had forgotten how to whistle.

Marguerite heard it first as rumor, carried up Copper Street on the backs of the usual gossips — the fishwife who traded in secondhand scandal, the lamplighter who knew everything that happened after dark and sold it cheap before dawn. *Something happened at the cathedral,* they said. *Something holy. Something wrong.* The words arrived tangled together, the way they always did when the city could not decide which of the two it was witnessing.

She went at first light, her ledger under her arm, her oath hidden beneath her clerk's ink as it always was, and she climbed the long white steps of the cathedral into air that tasted of snow that had not yet fallen.

The doors stood open. They should not have stood open. Nine hundred years of guild records taught Marguerite that doors were the first thing people closed when something went wrong — doors and ledgers and mouths. But the cathedral's great bronze doors, worked over with the faces of nine dawn-spirits whose names no one living remembered, had been flung wide as if the building itself were gasping.

Inside, the congregation had not gone home.

They sat in the pews in the gray half-dark, hundreds of them, still in the clothes they had worn to the midnight vigil. No one wept. That was the first thing Marguerite noticed, and the thing that made the back of her neck prickle like frost forming. A cathedral full of people who had lost something should have been weeping. Instead they sat very straight and very quiet, the way debtors sit when the collector comes, and looked at their own hands as though the hands belonged to strangers who had been kind enough to lend them.

A woman in the front pew held a small woolen sock, half-finished, the needles still in it. She turned it over and over.

"I don't know who I was knitting this for," she said, when Marguerite sat down beside her. She said it politely. That was the horror of it — the politeness. "I know it's small. I know it's for a child. But I've been sitting here since the bells, and I cannot remember a child."

"Your child?"

"I don't know." The woman smiled, and the smile was the worst thing Marguerite had seen in eleven years of keeping accounts of impossible things. It was a smile with nothing behind it, like a lamp lit in an empty room. "Isn't that strange? I know I *had* one. The shape of it is there, like a coat hook with no coat. I remember the hook. I keep reaching for the coat."

Marguerite opened her ledger. Her pen hovered. What column did this belong in? What page? She had recorded bottled lullabies and borrowed shadows, debts of luck and years and names, and she had thought she understood the grammar of loss the way a clerk understands letters — completely, and at a distance.

She wrote: *Cathedral of the Ninefold Dawn. Vigil, midnight. Congregation of approximately four hundred. Losses: uncounted. Nature of losses: childhoods.*

The ink seemed to sink into the page too quickly, as if the ledger were hungry too.

"Tell me about the prayer," Marguerite said.

The woman's face brightened — actually brightened, and Marguerite felt her stomach drop. "It was answered," she said. "Father Ossen said our prayers would be heard this winter, truly heard, and they were. I prayed for my sister. She's been ill — she *was* ill — and I prayed, all of us prayed, and the cold came into the cathedral like a blessing, like a hand laid on the whole congregation, and I felt so *light* afterward. So light. And my sister —" the woman frowned, reaching for the coat that was not on the hook — "my sister is better. I think she's better. I remember that she's better. I don't remember her name."

*Every answer strengthens the crown,* Marguerite did not yet know to think. What she thought, standing in that hollow choir, was simpler and colder: *Someone is paying for these prayers, and it is not the people praying.*

She walked the length of the cathedral. Four hundred souls, or what remained of them. An old man who had forgotten the war he'd fought in, though he still flinched at doors slamming. A boy of perhaps ten who had forgotten his own grandmother's face but remembered, precisely and uselessly, the recipe for glue his grandfather had taught him — the grief taken, the trivia left behind, like a house stripped of silver but not of nails. A choir of women who could no longer remember their mothers' voices, and so could no longer remember the songs their mothers had taught them, and so stood in the choir loft humming to fill the silence, because the silence was worse.

The frost had bloomed indoors.

Marguerite saw it on the pillars — not the ordinary frost of a cold morning but fern-patterns, sigil-patterns, spreading up the pale stone in spirals that hurt to look at directly, the way a word on the tip of the tongue hurts. The same patterns she had seen on Copper Street, in the widow's kitchen where rain had fallen from a cloudless ceiling and a son's face had been paid out of a mother's memory to buy it. The same patterns she had seen — and told no one, because there was no one to tell — on the seal of the oldest contract in the guild's vault, the one King Aldric had requested, the one sealed before his grandfather's reign.

The frost climbed toward the altar. At the altar, Father Ossen stood with his arms raised, and his face was radiant, and his radiance was the radiance of a man who has been paid in a currency he has not examined.

"Brothers, sisters," he was saying, to the pews of the emptied, "we are *seen*. After nine hundred years of silence, the old gods have heard us. Whatever we have lost, we have lost to glory—"

Marguerite closed her ledger.

She had been a clerk long enough to know what a cooked account looked like. She knew the smell of it — the particular sweetness of numbers that have been made to say what someone wanted them to say. Father Ossen's sermon was a cooked account. *Whatever we have lost, we have lost to glory.* Somewhere, in some ledger she could not yet read, an entry was being balanced, and the congregation's childhoods were the debit, and something — someone — was taking the credit.

She left before the blessing. Outside, the morning was bright and cold and utterly ordinary, and the ordinary felt obscene.

* * *

It took her three days to trace it, and she could not have traced it alone.

She went first to the guild's map room, a low stone chamber beneath the guildhall where the walls were papered with surveys of the city going back four centuries, and she pulled down every map that marked the old things — the standing stones, the buried wells, the sunken shrines that predated the cathedral, the palace, the guild, and possibly the city itself. The guild traded in impossible things, and impossible things required geography. Luck pooled in certain cellars. Years ran thin along certain streets. Names, once surrendered, did not vanish; they went *somewhere*, and the oldest maps, drawn in faded sepia by clerks who had believed the world owed them an explanation, marked those somewheres with a symbol like a root.

She copied every marked point onto a fresh sheet. Then she did what she did best, the thing that had made her valuable to Corvane and invisible to everyone else: she sat down and did the arithmetic.

The points made no pattern. That was her first conclusion, and it took her a full evening of frustration to distrust it. The points made no pattern *in ink*. But she had stood in the widow's kitchen on Copper Street and seen frost sigils blooming from the floorboards, and she had stood in the cathedral and seen them climbing the pillars, and she had seen the same sigil — she was certain of it now, certain with a clerk's certainty — on the guild's oldest contract. Three locations. Three points on her map.

She marked them in a different color. Then she went back through the winter's accounts — her own meticulous, unloved, faithful accounts — and pulled every entry that smelled of answered prayer. The miller on Tallow Lane whose millstone had begun turning by itself. The drunkard who had sworn off drink in a single night and could no longer remember why he had ever drunk, or what he had been drowning. The midwife whose hands had become uncannily lucky and who had, in the same week, forgotten the lullaby her own mother had sung her. Entry by entry, street by street, she marked them.

The colored points fell along curves. Not lines — curves, gentle and vast, like the sweep of something enormous turning over in its sleep beneath the city.

Ley-lines. The old maps' word for them was *roots*, and the old clerks had drawn them as roots because that was what they looked like when you plotted them: a root-system, deep and branching, drinking from somewhere and feeding somewhere, and every branch, every curve, every fine hair-fine filament of it converged — she checked it four times, and a fifth time with shaking hands — converged beneath the palace.

Not near the palace. Beneath it. Beneath the throne, if the maps were true. Beneath the king.

Marguerite sat in the map room until the lamp burned low, looking at the shape she had drawn, and she thought about King Aldric, whom she had never met, and about the guild's oldest contract, sealed before his grandfather's reign, and about frost blooming indoors in autumn, and she felt the specific cold that comes not from the air but from understanding.

The prayers were not being answered by gods.

They were being answered the way the guild answered debts — by spending something. Every impossible rain, every miraculous recovery, every millstone turning in the night had been purchased, and the currency was memory. The city's remembered griefs. The widow's son. The choir's mothers. The drunkard's reasons. And the payments were flowing, along the old roots of the world, to a single account.

Someone — something — beneath the palace was keeping the books now.

And it was eating politely. That was the phrase her mind kept returning to, the phrase she wrote at the bottom of her map in small, precise letters: *It is eating politely.* No fire, no plague, no screaming in the streets. The city was being consumed the way a fine meal is consumed, course by course, with grace and gratitude and the blessings of the church, and the diners were thanking their host with empty mouths.

* * *

She went back to the cathedral on the fourth night, because there was one thing she had not checked, and clerks check everything.

The vigil was held again. Word had spread — word always spread — and the congregation had doubled. People came with their griefs folded in their hands like letters they could not afford to post, and Father Ossen raised his arms, and the cold came down like a blessing, and Marguerite stood at the back in her clerk's gray and watched the frost sigils bloom across the pillars and thought: *roots. They are roots. I am standing in a field above a root-system, and the field is being grazed.*

She watched a young man pray — she saw his lips move, saw the tears freeze briefly on his cheeks before the warmth of the crowd melted them — and she watched the cold pass over him, and she watched his face go light and empty and grateful, and she wanted to seize him by the shoulders and shout: *Do you not understand what it costs? Nothing is heard for free. Nothing has ever been heard for free. I keep the books. I know.*

But she was a clerk, and clerks do not shout in cathedrals. She wrote instead, by lampllight afterward, in the ledger she had begun keeping for herself, separate from the guild's, separate from anyone's: the miller, the drunkard, the midwife, the widow, the choir. A page of names and losses, debit and debit and debit, with no credit column at all — because she could not find one, because the credit was flowing somewhere she could not follow, somewhere beneath the palace, into an account kept by a thing that wore a crown.

At the bottom of the page she drew the sigil from the frost, from the pillars, from the oldest contract in the vault. She drew it carefully, line by line, and when she finished she sat looking at it for a long time.

It was not, she realized, a sigil at all. It was a signature.

And she had seen it before — not only on the contract. She had seen it in the guildhall, in Corvane's private ledger, in the margin of a page dated sixty years ago, initialed in the guildmaster's own hand beside a payment received from the palace. *Received, per the old arrangement.* She had assumed it was a tax. She had assumed it was land, or gold, or privilege.

She had assumed wrong.

Marguerite closed her ledger and held it against her chest and felt, beneath the leather and the paper and the eleven years of careful, invisible, faithful work, the first true shape of the thing she was caught in. The guild was not innocent. The law was not innocent. The roots ran beneath the palace, and the roots ran through the guildhall, and somewhere above it all a king's eyes flickered between warmth and winter, and no one — no one in the city, no one in the church, no one in the guild — was going to say it out loud.

Someone had to keep the books.

She blew out her lamp and, in the dark, made the kind of entry that does not go in any ledger: *Tomorrow I ask Corvane about the old arrangement. And if he lies to me, I will know it, because I have read his handwriting for eleven years, and I know what his truth looks like, and I know what his fear looks like.*

Outside her window, the first snow of the season was falling — weeks too early, fine and silent and patient, settling on Copper Street, on the cathedral, on the palace, on the whole politely eaten city, like a page being prepared for writing.
