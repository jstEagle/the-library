## 8. Corvane's Bargain

The guildhall smelled of tallow and old paper the way it always did on the last day of autumn, when the clerks sealed the year's accounts with wax and prayer — though only one of those was permitted now, and Marguerite had written the memorandum saying so herself.

She climbed the back stair to Corvane's study with a lantern in one hand and the winter ledger under her arm, her boots quiet on steps worn hollow by forty years of clerks before her. The stairwell windows were black with early dark. Frost had climbed them from the inside again, feathering up the glass in patterns she had stopped pretending not to recognize: spirals, sigils, the slow geometry of something feeding.

Corvane's door stood open. That was wrong. In eleven years of service, Guildmaster Osric Corvane had never once left his door open. His door was oak, iron-banded, older than the guild's charter, and it shut behind every conversation like a mouth.

"Come in, Ashgrove," he said from within, before she could knock. "And bring the winter ledger. You'll want to set it down."

*You'll want to set it down.* Not *I'll want to see it.* Marguerite paused on the threshold, and the frost on the study window caught her eye first — a full pane of it, blooming inward, though the fire was lit and the room was warm as a held breath. Then she saw what sat on his desk.

The oldest contract.

She had heard of it the way clerks hear of everything: in fragments, in warnings. A folio bound in grey leather gone green at the edges, its seal a spiral of interlocking frost-lines pressed into wax the color of dried blood. It matched the sigils on Copper Street. It matched the frost on the palace windows. It matched, if she was honest with herself in the way the oath demanded honesty, the small cold ache behind her own eyes that had been growing all season, like a splinter of winter working deeper.

"You asked for me about the king's request," she said. "The oldest contract."

"I did." Corvane did not look up. He was an old man built like a folded letter, all creases and corners, and his hands — clerk's hands, ink-stained, precise — were smoothing the grey leather flat as if it might otherwise fly. "Sit down, Marguerite."

He almost never used her given name. Her stomach tightened around nothing.

She sat. She set the winter ledger on her knees instead of the desk, because some part of her wanted something between them, even paper.

"Do you know how old the guild is?" Corvane asked.

"Chartered two hundred and six years ago," she said. "Under King Aldous the Second."

"The charter is two hundred and six years old." He looked up then, and his eyes were very tired in a way that had nothing to do with age. "The guild is older. Before we were chartered we were something else. Before we kept ledgers of luck and years and names, we were the ones who *collected*. Do you understand? Every trade needs a founding stock. Ours was memory. The first guildsmen didn't broker impossible things, girl. They *were* the impossible thing. They found that grief could be lifted out of a person like a stone from a wound, and that other people would pay anything to be rid of theirs, and still others would pay more to hold someone else's. We have been selling sorrow since before this city had walls."

Marguerite's hand had gone, without her permission, to the collar where her oath-scar lay beneath the clerk's ink. "That's history," she said carefully. "It's not why you summoned me."

"No." He drew a breath, and she watched him do it — watched the old man gather himself the way one gathers papers before a confession. "The king has requested the oldest contract because the king — or whatever answers when the king speaks — has remembered that it exists. And I am going to give it to him. And you are going to help me carry it to the palace tomorrow, because your name is on it."

The fire popped. Somewhere below, a clerk laughed at something, ordinary and human, and the sound made the study feel colder.

"My name," Marguerite said, "is not on any contract. I've audited every folio in the vault twice. I would know."

"Would you?" Corvane opened the grey cover. "Then look."

* * *

The pages inside were not parchment. She realized that only when she touched one, and felt the strange dry softness of it, the faint resistance like skin that had forgotten it was skin. Vellum, she told herself. Old vellum. But her fingers knew better, and the oath beneath her ink knew better, and a cold line walked down her spine.

Each page bore entries in a hand she recognized.

Her own.

Not her current hand — her younger one, rounder, unbroken by years of ledger discipline. Dates going back further than her service. Further than her apprenticeship. Entries in a child's careful script: *Learned to whistle through grass stems. Mother's birthday, made honey-cakes, burned the third.* Entries from a girlhood she had assumed lived only in her own head, transcribed here in ink that had faded to the brown of old blood.

"What is this," she said. It was not a question. Questions rise; this fell out of her like something dropped from a height.

"The original terms," Corvane said quietly. "Every contract the guild holds is a copy or a descendant of this one. This is the master account. And thirty-one years ago, I began making withdrawals against it." He turned pages with a tenderness that made her ill. "Small ones, at first. A memory of a summer storm, sold to the palace to ease the old queen's melancholy. A face, sold to a courtier who had lost his daughter's. The payments kept the charter alive through three reigns that wanted us dissolved. Kings forget debts, Marguerite. But they remember favors. And there is no favor in the world like handing a grieving man back his dead."

"You sold memories." Her voice came out level, which surprised her. Underneath the levelness something was moving, vast and slow, like ice shifting on a river. "To the palace. For decades."

"To keep two hundred clerks fed. To keep this roof standing. To keep the *work* alive — the work that lifts grief out of widows and puts bread in orphans' mouths. Yes. I sold memories. That is the least of it." He turned the folio around to face her fully, and tapped one page near the middle, where the entries changed hands — where the round childish script gave way to a harder, adult line. "Look at whose account it is."

She looked. She had been avoiding looking, the way one avoids a wound, and now she made herself read the header of the page.

**Ashgrove, M. — Account opened at birth. Custodian: G. Ashgrove (mother). Transferred to Guild custody upon custodian's death. Standing arrangement: annual withdrawal, palace requisition, per Contract of the Veil.**

*Contract of the Veil.*

The words went into her like a blade finding the gap in armor. The frost sigils. Copper Street. The widow who could not remember her son's face. The answered prayers. All of it had a name, and the name was written in her guildmaster's oldest book, in her mother's custody line, in her own childhood handwriting.

"Thirty-one years," she said. "I'm twenty-nine."

"Your mother opened the account before you were born. It's how she paid for the midwife. It's how half this city pays for things, if they only knew." Corvane's voice was steady, but his hands were not; she saw the tremor in them as he smoothed a page that did not need smoothing. "After she died, the account came to the guild, and the guild honored the standing arrangement. Annual withdrawal. One memory, each year, taken from the deepest shelf and sold upward. The palace pays in protection. Protection pays in survival. And the account holder—" he met her eyes, and she hated him for the pity in his, "—the account holder feels it as a thinness. A sense of having been somewhere she can't name. You've felt it. Don't lie to me; I've watched you feel it for eleven years. Every time you stand at a window too long. Every time you say *I don't remember* and mean it."

Marguerite stood up. The winter ledger slid off her knees and hit the floor with a slap that sounded, in the silence, obscene.

"How many," she said.

"Ashgrove—"

"*How many.*"

He closed his eyes. "Twenty-six withdrawals. One for every year of your life until this one. This year's was refused."

"Refused by whom?"

"By the palace. By the crown." He opened his eyes, and now there was fear in them, raw and new, and somehow that was worse than the betrayal. "The requisition came back marked *paid in full* — but not in coin. There was a note. One line, in a hand I have never seen, though the seal was the king's own." He slid a slip of paper across the desk. Frost had grown through it from the inside, silvering the words, but she could still read them:

***We no longer buy. We collect.***

* * *

For a long time neither of them spoke. The fire burned. The frost on the window spread another finger's width across the glass, patient as arithmetic.

Marguerite became aware, distantly, that she was holding the edge of the desk, and that the wood had begun to frost beneath her fingers — that her own grief was coming out of her the way heat comes out of a hand, except it was coming out as winter, and she did not know what that meant, and she did not care.

"Say it plainly," she said at last. "All of it. My oath requires truth and yours apparently doesn't, so grant me this one courtesy. Say what I am to you."

Corvane rose. He came around the desk slowly, an old man crossing a great distance, and stopped an arm's length away.

"You are the finest clerk this guild has ever produced," he said. "You are honest past the point of comfort, thorough past the point of sanity, and loyal in a way I have never deserved. And you are also an asset. You have been an asset since the hour you walked through my door with your mother's death-notice in your fist and nowhere else in the world to go. I chose you for the vault because your account was already open — because a person whose memories are already being spent asks fewer questions about the spending. I kept you close because keeping you close was safer than letting you wander. And I let you believe you belonged here because belonging was cheaper than wages, and kinder than the truth, and because—" his voice cracked, just once, on the old man's honest fracture, "—because I needed it to be true. God forgive me, I needed it to be true."

The ice under her fingers had spread across half the desk. She took her hand away and looked at the frost-shape it left behind: a spiral. The sigil. Even her grief wore the enemy's mark now.

"Twenty-six memories," she said. "What did they take?"

"I don't know. The withdrawals are recorded by weight, not content. The palace's collectors never itemized."

"But you know *where* they're recorded." She wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, furious at the wetness, furious at the steadiness underneath it. "The palace keeps its own books. Everything sold to the crown goes into the royal archive — the deep one, under the old chapel. I've seen the requisition routing. I wrote the audit that flagged it, three years ago, and you told me it was a clerical error."

"It was not a clerical error."

"No," she said. "It was mine. Twenty-six years of me, filed under a king's seal." She straightened. Something had happened in her chest — a settling, the way a building settles after a shock, finding which walls still bear weight. "Here is what happens now, Guildmaster. Tomorrow you will carry the oldest contract to the palace, because if you don't, they will come for it, and they will come for the vault, and they will come for every debtor in this building whose accounts can be leveraged. I understand the arithmetic; I taught half of it to you. But I will carry it in one hand, and in the other I will carry a list, and the list is every withdrawal ever made against my account — dates, weights, seals. Because when I stand in front of whatever is wearing Aldric's crown, I intend to present an invoice."

Corvane stared at her. "They will not honor it."

"They won't have a choice." She picked the winter ledger up off the floor and held it against her chest like a shield, like a child, like a weapon — she honestly could not have said which. "Debt is the one law even gods have to keep. Your own charter says so. *All accounts come due.* Well. Mine has come due, and I am done being the currency."

She reached the door before he spoke again.

"Ashgrove." His voice was rough. "The woman who sold her grief — Sera Voss. And the luck-thief. And the boy. You've been meeting them. Don't think I haven't seen the ledger cross-references; I taught you the cipher, remember." He did not sound angry. He sounded afraid, and tired, and — worst of all — hopeful. "Whatever you're building in the margins of my guildhall. Build it fast."

Marguerite stood in the doorway with the frost breathing down the stairwell behind her and the oldest contract lying open on the desk like a wound that had finally been uncovered.

"I already have," she said. "And Guildmaster — the boy made of borrowed years. Check his account tonight. Check who opened it, and check the seal at the bottom of the opening page."

"Why?"

"Because it isn't the guild's seal," she said. "It's the same one that's on your oldest contract. And I want to know, before tomorrow, whether the thing in the palace has been buying from us all along — or whether we've been selling to it since before either of us was born."

She went down the stairs without a lantern. She found she no longer needed one; the cold coming off her own skin lit the frost along the walls, sigil after sigil after sigil, marking her passage like a road being drawn toward the palace — or like a trap, closing.

In the vault below, the debtors' names waited in her ledger, and beside them, in fresh ink, she wrote a single line before she slept, if she slept at all:

*Day one of the reckoning. The creditor has been identified. Prepare the collection.*
