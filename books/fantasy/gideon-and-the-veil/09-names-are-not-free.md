## 9. Names Are Not Free

The luck-thief's lodgings were above a chandler's shop on Tallow Row, and they smelled of tallow and old rain and the particular sourness of a man who had spent thirty years taking what was never offered to him. Fenwick Loach — Fen to everyone who still spoke to him, which after the guild's winter audits was a shorter list than he liked to admit — kept his shutters closed even at noon, because luck, in his experience, was like light: it came in whether you wanted it or not, and it always left through the same door it entered.

He heard her on the stairs before she knocked. He always did. Marguerite Ashgrove climbed stairs like a woman settling an account — evenly, without hurry, each step exactly as heavy as the one before. Luck told him things about people. It had been telling him about her since the day she'd walked into the guildhall with clerk's ink on her fingers and a swordspeaker's silence under her tongue, and everything it told him made him nervous, because Marguerite Ashgrove was the only person in Vhalsmark whose luck read as *owed*. Everyone else's luck was theirs, however badly kept. Hers belonged to something else. Something patient.

She knocked twice. He opened the door before her knuckles fell a third time.

"Clerk," he said.

"Thief."

"You could've sent a note. I'd have come down. I always come down for you, that's the trouble with me." He stepped back to let her in, and the room's luck shifted around her like water around a keel. He felt it in his teeth. "Sit. Don't touch the blue bottle. The blue bottle is Tuesday, and I haven't decided what to do with Tuesday yet."

Marguerite sat in the one chair that wasn't stacked with jars, and she looked at him the way she looked at ledgers — like she already knew there was an error somewhere and was giving him the courtesy of finding it himself.

"The widow on Copper Street," she said. "Maren Ostrey. Her prayer for rain was answered. Rain out of a clear sky, warm as bathwater, over one roof and no others. And when it stopped, she couldn't remember her son's face. She remembers that she has a son. She remembers his name. But his face is gone from her like a page cut out of a book, and the edges are clean."

"I know," Fen said.

"You *know*?"

"I was there." He turned away from her, busied himself with the kettle, mostly so he wouldn't have to see whatever crossed her face. "Three streets over, under the eaves of the dye-house. I'd gone out to skim a little weather off the top of the day — you can do that, did you know? Take the good of a grey morning and carry it in your pocket, spend it later. Small thefts. Nobody misses them." He set the kettle on the brazier. His hands were steady, which surprised him. "And the rain started, and I felt it *pull*, Clerk. Like a hook through the whole street. Every memory in every house leaned toward Copper Street like iron filings toward a lodestone. Mine too. I lost — " he counted, because counting was the only prayer he trusted — "I lost the smell of my mother's kitchen. Just the smell. Everything else about her, intact. But the smell is gone, and where it was there's a shape, the way you know a tooth used to be there."

Marguerite said nothing for a long moment. The kettle began its low complaint.

"And you didn't report it," she said finally. "To the guild. To me."

"To the guild?" Fen laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "Clerk. Sweet, terrifying clerk. Who do you think the guild would sell it to?"

That landed. He watched it land. She didn't flinch — she was too well-made for flinching — but something behind her eyes reorganized itself, the way a ledger reorganizes when a column refuses to sum.

"That's why you're here," she said quietly. "You think Corvane knows."

"I think Corvane has known since before either of us could write our names. I think the guild's oldest contract isn't a contract at all. I think it's a *feeding schedule*." He crouched by the brazier and fed it a twist of paper, and the fire took it greedily, and he hated how much the fire reminded him of the thing on Copper Street. "But that's not what you came for. You came because you want to understand what's eating us. So listen. This is the one lecture I'm qualified to give."

He sat down across from her, on an upturned crate, close enough that their knees nearly touched, and he did the thing he almost never did. He reached into his coat and brought out his luck and showed her.

It didn't look like much. A dull brass coin, worn featureless, warm from riding against his heart. Any pawnbroker in the city would give him two coppers for it and feel generous. But Marguerite went very still when she saw it, the way animals go still, and he knew she could feel it — the weight of it, the way the room bent slightly toward it, the way the candleflame stood up straighter as if being observed.

"This is my first theft," he said. "I was nine years old. There was a man in the Saltmarket who sold charms, and most of them were buttons and lies, but this one was real, and I stole it because I was hungry and lucky children don't stay hungry. That's all luck is, Clerk. That's the whole secret, the thing the guild charges fortunes for and I'll tell you for free. Luck is just *memory that hasn't happened yet*."

Marguerite frowned. "Say that again."

"Memory that hasn't happened yet." He turned the coin over his knuckles. "Every moment that comes to you arrives carrying its own past with it — the reason it rains, the reason the bread rises, the reason you ducked before the arrow. The world remembers forward. That remembering is luck. And because it's memory, it can be stolen, borrowed, traded, eaten. Same rules as the other kind. Same *appetites* as the other kind."

"And the Veil eats the other kind."

"The Veil eats the other kind," Fen agreed, "and here's the beautiful, horrible part, the part that kept me awake for six nights running until I worked it out. The two kinds are the same coin. You spend one, the other pays. Watch."

He held the coin flat on his palm. Then he closed his eyes, and did the oldest trick he knew, the trick that had kept him alive through three famines and one marriage: he took a small piece of tomorrow and spent it today. He took the certainty that the kettle wouldn't boil over.

The kettle boiled over. Water hissed across the brazier, steam shrieked up, and in the same instant Fen felt the coin grow fractionally heavier in his palm — and felt, faint and far away, like a bell rung in another room, something *answer*.

Marguerite had come halfway out of the chair. "What was that?"

"That," Fen said, mopping at the brazier with a rag that had seen worse, "was the sound of a prayer getting answered somewhere. Because that's what an answered prayer *is*, Clerk. It's not a god being kind. It's a debt being paid. Someone wishes hard enough, and something spends memory to make the wish true — and the wish doesn't care whose memory. The rain on Copper Street cost Maren Ostrey her son's face. My spilled kettle cost someone, somewhere, the smell of their mother's kitchen, or the tune their father whistled, or the way their first love said goodbye. The world keeps double-entry books. It always has. Somebody taught the books to feed themselves."

The steam thinned. The candle burned straight. Marguerite sat back down slowly, and Fen watched her do the arithmetic — he could see it moving behind her face like fish under ice, column after column, and he pitied whoever she finished the sum against.

"Every answered prayer strengthens the crown," she said. Not a question. She'd been to the palace; everyone knew she'd been summoned; nobody knew what she'd seen there except that she'd come back quieter than she'd left.

"Because the crown is the collector." Fen put the coin away, back against his heart, and felt braver with it there, which was the coin working, and he let it work. "Think about the shape of it. Prayers go up. Answers come down. In the old days — my grandmother swore this, and she lied about everything except the gods — answers came down from everywhere. From wells and crossroads and the black space between stars. Now they come down from one place. One direction. One *address*. All the city's grief flows up to the palace, gets spent, and the change comes back down wearing frost sigils. And every time it happens, the thing wearing King Aldric gets a little more real and the king gets a little less. It's not stealing the memories, Clerk. Stealing's my trade; I'd respect it. It's *laundering* them. Grief goes in. Miracles come out. And the difference between the two — the vig, the house's cut — that's what's building the winter."

Marguerite was silent. Outside, a cart rattled past on Tallow Row, and someone was singing, badly, a song about spring, and the song sounded obscene in the cold.

"The Hollow Choir," she said at last. "Last week. Two hundred people prayed in the cathedral and woke up with their childhoods gone. Whole childhoods. What could two hundred prayers possibly have needed to be answered *that* badly?"

"Nothing." Fen's voice dropped, and he hated the fear in it, because fear was a currency too and he'd sworn never to spend it cheaply. "That's what I'm trying to tell you. It wasn't payment. It was a feast. The Veil is sated now — haven't you noticed? Prayers are going unanswered all over the city. Little ones, desperate ones. The widow who prays for her cough to ease gets nothing. The drowning man gets nothing. Because the feeding season is over. It ate its fill all autumn, politely, one widow at a time, and now it's not hungry anymore. Now it's *growing*. And there's only one meal in this city big enough for what it's becoming."

"The king," Marguerite said. "The rest of him."

"The king," Fen agreed softly. "The whole self. The last course."

The brazier ticked as it cooled. Marguerite stood, and standing, she seemed to take the room's remaining warmth with her by accident, the way she seemed to take everything by accident — competence so total it looked like theft.

"You said you worked it out over six nights," she said. "The mechanics. Is there a counter-mechanic? Because I didn't climb three flights of your filthy stairs for a eulogy, Fenwick."

He grinned despite himself. She was the only person alive who could make his own name sound like a summons.

"There might be," he said. "Here's the flaw in any laundering scheme, Clerk, and it's the same flaw in every scheme ever run out of the guildhall: the books have to balance. The Veil spends memory to answer prayers. Which means every miracle it's performed is a *transaction*, and every transaction leaves a mark. A signature. The rain on Copper Street is written somewhere in the world's ledger, in Maren Ostrey's hand, in mine, in two hundred hollowed childhoods from the cathedral. Debts don't stop being debts because the debtor is a god. They just wait."

"You want to call them in."

"I want," Fen said, and the coin against his heart felt suddenly hot, suddenly loud, suddenly like the only honest thing in Vhalsmark, "to do what I have done my entire miserable life, but *properly*, at scale, with witnesses. I want to audit a god."

For the first time since she'd walked in, Marguerite Ashgrove smiled, and it was not a comforting smile, and Fenwick Loach, who had spent his life reading luck, read hers then and saw what he'd always half-known: that beneath the clerk's ink and the quiet loneliness, there was a blade that had been waiting thirty years for a reason.

"Then teach me the rest of it," she said. "Everything. How memories are weighed. How debts are traced. How to find the account book of a thing that thinks it owns the winter."

"It'll cost you," Fen warned. "Knowledge always does. That's the first lesson."

"Good," said Marguerite. "I'm tired of things in this city being free."

* * *

They worked through the afternoon and into the blue edge of evening, and Marguerite filled eleven pages of a pocket ledger in her small, ruthless hand.

Fen taught her the weights first. Memory was not uniform; it had denominations. A face was silver. A name was gold. A first grief — the first time the world showed you it did not care — that was platinum, rare and terrible, and the Veil had been skimming those off the city all autumn like cream. He taught her how to feel a debt's residue: hold a loss in mind, breathe slow, and watch which way the cold leaned. Cold leaned toward its own meals. On Copper Street, standing in Maren Ostrey's parlor while the poor woman slept in her chair, Marguerite breathed in and felt the frost-sigils on the window glass lean toward her like flowers toward sun — and inside the sigils, when she looked long enough, she saw script. Tiny, looping, patient. Entries.

She copied one down. Later, comparing it to the seal on the guild's oldest contract, she found the hands matched.

Fen taught her the tracing. Every answered prayer left a thread — not visible, not quite, but followable by anyone who knew that luck and memory were one substance. He gave her a spool of ordinary red thread from the chandler's stock below and showed her how to knot it seven times around a loss and cast the end into the wind, and the thread would drift, always drifting the same direction, like a compass needle made of grief. From Copper Street it pointed to the palace. From the cathedral it pointed to the palace. From the orphanage on Bell Lane, where forty children had woken last month unable to remember their mothers' lullabies, it pointed to the palace, and Marguerite stood in the orphanage yard holding the trembling thread and felt something in her chest set itself, like a bone.

By dusk she had the shape of the whole machine drawn on a single page: prayers rising, memories flowing, miracles falling, the crown fattening on the difference. At the bottom of the page she wrote one line, boxed, the way she boxed figures that would decide everything:

*All debts leave entries. All entries can be called.*

"Where did you learn all this?" she asked Fen, as she gathered her pages. "Truly. A boy steals a charm and thirty years later he understands the metaphysics of prayer?"

Fen looked at the fire for a while.

"My grandmother," he said finally. "The one who lied about everything except the gods. She wasn't a thief, Clerk. She was a priestess. Of the small gods, the ones that lived in wells and doorways before the crown collected them too. When the order was broken, she kept one duty, and she passed it to me with the coin: *if ever the answering starts again from one place only, run to the swordspeakers' heirs, because the books will need balancing.*" He shrugged, embarrassed. "I thought she was mad. I spent her coin instead of keeping it. Turns out both things were true."

Marguerite stood in his doorway with the evening behind her and the ledger against her chest, and for a moment she looked less like a clerk than like a lit fuse.

"Then we start calling them in," she said. "Every debt. Every entry. Starting with the guild's oldest contract — because I've seen the seal now, Fen, and it doesn't just match the frost. It matches the oath-stone sigils from the old swordspeaker halls. Whatever's wearing the crown has been in our books longer than it's been in the king."

She went down the stairs, even and unhurried, each step weighing exactly what the last one weighed, and Fenwick Loach stood in his doorway holding his grandmother's coin and understood, with the particular clarity of a man watching his own luck turn, that the easy part was over.

Because Marguerite Ashgrove would go to the guildhall next. She would go to Corvane. And Corvane had been selling pieces of her past for decades, and the ledger she carried against her chest was about to show her exactly which pages were missing — and whose handwriting had signed them away.

Fen touched the coin, once, the way you touch a wound to check it's real.

"Balance the books, Clerk," he murmured to the empty stairwell. "And when you find out what you've already paid — try to remember it wasn't your fault."
