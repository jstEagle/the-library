## 10. The King in the Snow Garden

The physician's note came tucked inside a ledger of ordinary debts, folded so small it might have been a moth. *The east garden, after compline. Come alone, or do not come at all.* No signature, but the hand was precise and tired, the hand of a man who wrote too many letters he could not send.

Marguerite went.

She told herself it was for the guild — that Corvane would want eyes on the king before the old contract was unsealed, that an audit of the crown's condition was simply prudent bookkeeping. She told herself a great many things as she crossed the palace grounds in the blue hour after evening bells, her breath ghosting silver, her boots silent on gravel that crunched like sugar. The lies were well-kept, like everything else in her life. Neat columns. Balanced sums. And underneath them all, in ink she did not let herself read: *I need to see him again.*

Because she had not stopped thinking about his eyes. The way they had flickered in the throne room, warm brown to winter-pale and back again, like a candle guttering in a draft. Like two people sharing one face and taking turns.

The east garden had been famous once. Marguerite remembered — or thought she remembered; her memory had begun to feel like borrowed goods, items she could no longer verify against any inventory — rose arbors and a fountain shaped like a sleeping swan, hedges cut into the likenesses of stags. Now frost owned it outright. Every hedge stood white and rigid, the stag-shapes grown gaunt, their legs thickened with ice until they looked less like topiary than like bones. The fountain was frozen mid-pour, water caught in a silver ribbon above the swan's stone beak. And snow was falling — falling only here, in this one garden, out of a clear autumn sky, drifting down soft and endless through air that should have held nothing but leaves.

It fell without wind. It fell upward, sometimes, near the far wall.

Marguerite stopped at the gate and made herself look at it properly, the way she looked at a ledger that would not balance. Frost sigils bloomed across the flagstones in spirals, the same pattern she had seen on Copper Street, the same pattern pressed into wax on the guild's oldest contract. They pulsed faintly, very slowly, like something breathing under the stones.

*You are walking into the mouth of it,* she told herself. *Good. Mouths can be audited.*

He was there, of course. At the garden's center, where the paths met, King Aldric sat alone on a stone bench with no cloak and no crown, wearing a plain grey wool coat buttoned wrong by one. His hands were folded in his lap like a man waiting for a appointment he dreaded. Snow gathered on his shoulders and in his dark hair, and he did not brush it away, and it did not melt.

For a moment — just a moment — he looked so young. Younger than his thirty years. Younger than the coins stamped with his profile, which gave him a jaw of iron and eyes of statecraft. Sitting there in the falling snow with his coat buttoned wrong, he looked like a boy who had been left somewhere and was still waiting, patiently, terribly, for someone to come back for him.

"Master Ashgrove." He didn't turn his head. "You walk quietly. I used to be able to hear my guards from forty paces. Now I don't hear anything until it's already standing next to me."

"Your Majesty." She bowed, shallow and correct.

"You may call me Aldric. There's no one here but the snow, and the snow already knows worse about me than you ever will." He turned then, and she braced herself for the flicker — the other thing looking out — but his eyes were only his own, brown and exhausted and human, ringed with the bruised shadows of a man who has stopped trusting sleep. "One hour. That's what I get. Sometimes less. I've learned to spend it fast." A thin smile. "So. Sit down, Master Ashgrove, and tell me what I've done."

* * *

She sat. Not beside him — across the path, on the bench facing his, because some distances were professional courtesies and some were survival. Between them the frost sigils glowed their slow pulse, and the snow came down without sound, without weight, landing on her sleeves and staying whole instead of melting, which was wrong, which was deeply, quietly wrong, like finding a coin dated tomorrow.

"The court physician sent me," she said.

"I know. He thinks I don't know. He writes to everyone — the guild, the border lords, a woman in Vell who sells charms. He's a good man. He's been trying to save me for a year the way you'd try to bail out a sinking ship with a teaspoon, and I keep telling him the hole is in the hull of the world." Aldric looked up into the falling snow. "What has he told you? Let me guess. I lose hours. I say things in a voice that isn't mine. Last month I signed a pardon for a man I'd never heard of and wept while doing it, and when I woke I was holding the pen and my hand ached like I'd been writing for days."

"He mentioned the hours," Marguerite said carefully. "Not the rest."

"There's a great deal of *the rest*." He flexed his fingers. "Would you like to know what it's like? Everyone asks around it, politely, the way you ask a widow how she's bearing up. Nobody asks directly. So I'll tell you directly, since you're a ledger-keeper and ledgers are honest even when their owners aren't."

That startled a breath of almost-laughter out of her. "You've audited the guild."

"I've read your guild's charter. It's the most honest document in my kingdom. It says plainly: *we trade in what cannot be repaid, therefore all debts are real.* My court's charters say nothing of the kind, and yet every debt in them is also real." He said it lightly, but his hands had tightened on his knee. "Here is what it is like. I am a room. And something else is the tenant. Most hours I'm locked in the cellar of my own skull, and I can hear it upstairs, moving furniture, greeting visitors in my voice. When it lets me up — an hour, sometimes two, always at night, always in the cold — I come to this garden, because it's the only place left where the walls are still mine."

"And the snow?"

"I make it," he said simply. "Or it makes itself out of me. I honestly no longer know which. When I'm frightened, or angry, or — " he hesitated — "when I remember something too hard, it snows. The physicians think it's a sickness. The priests think it's a blessing gone strange. But you're a woman of accounts, so I'll give you the account: three winters ago I prayed. Do you pray, Master Ashgrove?"

"No," she said. It was true, and saying it felt like setting down a weight.

"Neither did I, particularly. But my father had just died, and the war in the north was eating boys faster than the fields could grow men, and my council wanted things from me that I did not want to give, and I went to the old shrine beneath the chapel — the one my grandfather sealed — and I prayed to whatever was down there. I prayed for strength enough to carry the crown." His voice stayed level, but his knuckles had gone white. "Something answered. Something answered *immediately*, which no god does, and it asked so little. Just memories, it said. Griefs, mostly. Sorrows are heavy, and I carried so many. It would lighten me, and I would be strong."

Marguerite's blood went cold in a way that had nothing to do with the garden. *Griefs. The city's remembered griefs.* The Hollow Choir. Copper Street. The widow who could no longer see her son's face.

"How much have you given it?" she asked.

"That's the thing." He laughed, and it was the worst sound she had heard in years, dry and cracking. "I don't know. That's the horror of it, Master Ashgrove. I can't audit myself. I remember my coronation. I remember my mother's funeral — I think I remember it. I remember being happy once, at some festival, with wine and lanterns, but I can't find my own face in the memory. It's like reaching into a drawer and finding the space where the thing should be, and the shape of the space is all that tells you something was there. I have holes in me. And the holes are getting larger, and lately — " he leaned forward, and now the flicker came, one heartbeat of pale winter light behind his eyes, there and gone — "lately I don't think I'm giving anymore. I think it's taking. Directly. From the city, through me, like I'm not a king but a *tap*, a spigot it opened into the world, and every prayer anyone prays in this city comes up through my bones and gets paid for with someone's childhood."

The snow fell harder. The frozen fountain creaked.

"So," Aldric said, and his voice gentled into something terrible, something almost tender. "Now you know what I've done. And here is what I'm asking."

"Don't," Marguerite said.

"You haven't heard it yet."

"I know what it will be. I watched your eyes change in the throne room. I've read the frost sigils on a contract older than your grandfather. Your Majesty — Aldric — whatever you're about to ask, the answer is no, and then I'm going to leave, and then I'm going to fix this some other way."

"There is no other way." He stood, and the snow swirled around him as if delighted. "Listen to me. I have spent a year being polite about this. I have let physicians bleed me and priests chant over me and my chancellor hide documents from me, and every hour it grows stronger and every hour I am less. Yesterday I lost the entire afternoon. I woke at dusk standing in the treasury with a ledger open in my hands — *a ledger*, Master Ashgrove, it was reading your guild's accounts through my eyes — and I had been smiling. My face ached from smiling. Whatever wears me is coming for the guild next, and then the city, and then everything, and it does it all *politely*, it does it all through prayers answered and debts paid, and no law in my kingdom touches it because it *is* the law now, it sits in my chair and stamps my seal." He crossed the path toward her, and the frost sigils flared under his bare feet, and he did not seem to feel them. "There is exactly one person in this kingdom who can end it, and she is sitting in front of me trying not to cry."

"I'm not going to cry."

"Your eyes disagree."

"My eyes are none of your Majesty's business." She stood too, because sitting felt like surrender. "Say it, then. Say what you're asking, since you insist."

"A sword," he said. "Through the heart. Tonight, while I'm still mostly me. While I can still mean it."

* * *

The words landed in the silence of the snowing garden and lay there, and neither of them moved.

Marguerite had imagined, in the long sleepless arithmetic of the past weeks, a hundred shapes the crisis might take. Contracts. Confrontations. Corvane's face when she finally accused him. She had never once imagined this: a young king asking her for death the way a drowning man asks for a rope, and meaning it, and being right.

"I can't," she said.

"You can. You're a swordspeaker. Don't look shocked — the order is dead, but dead orders leave records, and I found yours in the sealed archives when I was still allowed to read my own history. *The blade bound to truth. The vow that cannot strike the crowned.*" He recited it flatly, bitterly. "Yes. I know your oath, Master Ashgrove. I know it forbids you. I'm asking you to break it."

"Then you're asking me to become nothing." Her voice cracked on it, and she hated that. "You think the oath is a chain? It's the last thing left of the order that raised me. Every sword that ever spoke truth spoke through that vow. If I strike a crowned head, I don't free you — I just add one more broken thing to a world full of them, and the Veil wins anyway, because it will have taught me that vows are weather, that oaths melt. That's what it *wants*. It eats what people hold sacred. You'd be handing it my last — "

"It eats *me*," Aldric said, "every day, and no one holds me sacred enough to stop it." He was close now, close enough that she could see the snowflakes land in his lashes and stay unmelted. "Marguerite Ashgrove. I have maybe a hundred of these hours left. Maybe fifty. Each one I stand here begging is an hour it spends elsewhere, learning my kingdom, wearing my hands. Kill me and the tap closes. Kill me and the city keeps its children's faces. Kill me and — " his voice finally broke, just once, a hairline fracture — "kill me and I die as myself. Do you understand? That's all I want. Not to win. To *end as myself*. Everyone else in this story gets to be saved or damned. I only want to be permitted to stop."

And God help her, she reached for the hilt.

Her hand moved before her mind authorized it — the old training, the body remembering what the soul had sworn away — and her fingers closed on the worn leather grip of the short sword beneath her clerk's coat, and the blade spoke.

Not aloud. Swordspeakers' blades never spoke aloud; that was the first lesson and the last mystery. It spoke in the marrow, in the pulse behind the teeth, a voice like a struck bell heard underwater, and it said, with perfect, pitiless clarity:

*No.*

The oath. The whole oath, rising in her like a tide: *truth before mercy, mercy before victory, and the crowned head untouchable, forever, though the world burn.* She had spoken those words at seventeen with a straight spine and a full heart, kneeling on cold stone before masters who were already ghosts, and the vow had gone into her the way roots go into soil, and now it held her upright and it held her fast and it would not, could not, let go.

Her hand opened. Her fingers uncurled from the hilt one by one, each one a small defeat.

"I'm sorry," she whispered.

Aldric watched her hand fall away from the sword, and he nodded slowly, and to her everlasting grief he did not look surprised. He looked like a man who had known the answer before he asked, and had asked anyway, because hope is a debt everyone pays whether or not it's ever honored.

"Yes," he said softly. "I rather thought the oath would win. It's a good oath. That's the trap of good things — they hold us when we most need to be free." He stepped back, and the fury drained out of the garden, and the snow gentled, drifting lazy and fine. "Forgive me for asking. It was cruel of me to put it on you. But I needed someone to try, and the physician can't lift a sword, and my guards would sooner cut their own throats, and — " he smiled, small and wrecked — "and you looked like someone who keeps accounts. Someone who would understand that a debt this size has to be paid by someone, and I would rather it were me."

"It won't be you." Marguerite heard her own voice go low and hard, and realized she meant it more than she had ever meant anything. "Listen to me, Aldric. I couldn't strike you tonight. But I'm going to find another way, because I refuse — do you hear me — I *refuse* to accept that the only choices are your death or the city's. Ledgers balance. That's the whole point of them. Somewhere there's a figure missing, a payment unrecorded, a door this thing forgot to lock, and I am going to find it if I have to read every contract the guild has ever signed back to the founding."

"The founding," Aldric repeated. Something flickered across his face — not the Veil this time, but memory, his own, fragile. "The oldest contract. My grandfather sealed the shrine, you know. Sealed it and then — " he frowned, groping — "then there's nothing. There's a hole right there, in the middle of my family's history. Three years of my grandfather's reign, gone, like pages cut out of a book. I used to think it was scandal. An affair, a murder, something the historians were paid to forget." He looked at her, and his eyes were wide and young and afraid. "Master Ashgrove. What if it wasn't forgotten? What if it was *paid*?"

The frost sigils pulsed beneath their feet — slow, slow, like a heart counting down.

Somewhere beyond the garden wall, the compline bell rang the hour, and Aldric's shoulders dropped half an inch, and she watched the winter come up behind his eyes like a tide reclaiming a beach.

"My hour," he said quietly. Already his voice was smoothing, formalizing, becoming the voice from the throne room. "Go, quickly. And Marguerite — " the last of him, fighting for the doorway of himself — "the contract. Read the contract. Whatever my grandfather paid, it's written there, and I don't think it was ever — "

His spine straightened. His chin lifted. The warmth went out of his face the way heat goes out of a room when a door opens onto snow, and when he spoke again it was in the king's measured tones, courteous and distant and utterly empty:

"You may go, clerk. The audience is concluded."

Marguerite bowed — to the shell of him, to the tenant wearing him — and walked out through the falling snow with her hand pressed flat against her sternum, as if she could hold shut a door that was already opening inside her.

Behind her, in the garden, the snow kept falling on the frozen king, patient as an accounting, and the frost sigils beneath the flagstones pulsed on, and on, and on.

She did not go home. She went to the guildhall, past midnight, past the sleeping clerks, down into the vault where the oldest contracts slept in their lead-lined drawers, and she put her palm against the cold iron of the first drawer, the one sealed before Aldric's grandfather's reign, and she thought of a boy-king begging in the snow, and a grandfather's reign with three years cut out of it like a wound.

*What if it wasn't forgotten? What if it was paid?*

She broke the seal.

The parchment inside was older than any she had ever handled, and the first line was written in ink that still gleamed wet, and it began: *Let it be recorded that the Order of the Speaking Sword does hereby sell —*

And the name on the seller's line was her own.
