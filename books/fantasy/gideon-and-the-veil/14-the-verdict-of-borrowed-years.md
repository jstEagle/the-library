## 14. The Verdict of Borrowed Years

Tam had never been inside the guildhall's great chamber before. He had been in its corners, certainly — the alcoves where debtors waited to be weighed, the stairwells where clerks counted what could not be seen, the cellar where the luck-thief kept her stolen fortunes in jars like fireflies. But the great chamber, with its vaulted ceiling painted with constellations no astronomer would claim and its long table scarred by a century of ledgers being slammed upon it — that room had always belonged to other people. People who owned their own years.

Tonight it belonged to them.

He sat at the table between Sera Vane, who had sold her grief and now wore the absence of it like a borrowed coat that didn't quite fit at the shoulders, and Old Maren, whose hands shook not from age but from the forty years of bad luck she had carried for other people as a profession. The candles were guttering. Nobody had thought to bring more. It seemed wrong, somehow, to spend anything on a night like this, even candlewax.

Marguerite stood at the head of the table with the ledger open before her, and Tam watched her the way he watched everything now — hungrily, counting.

That was the trouble with borrowed years. You learned to count. Every breath was a withdrawal. Every laugh cost a little more than it used to. He had been eleven years old when his mother had gone to the guild and sold twenty of her own years to buy his fever down, and the guild, in its terrible bookkeeping mercy, had not simply taken her time. It had lent him some of hers instead — stitched years into him that were not his, years that ticked in his chest like a watch wound by someone else's hand. He was seventeen now, or he was thirty-one, or he was somewhere in between, depending on how you read the ledger. Depending on how much longer the loan ran.

He knew exactly how much longer. He checked every morning. That was the first thing he did when he woke, before he opened his eyes: he counted.

And tonight, listening to Marguerite lay out the case against the Veil — the hollowed choir, the frost sigils, the king wearing winter like a crown — Tam did the arithmetic he had been avoiding since they'd convened this strange tribunal in the belly of the lawless guildhall.

The Veil could only be unbound by a payment greater than its hunger. That was what the luck-thief had taught them, what the old contracts implied, what the oath-stone's history whispered through Marguerite's grim recitations. A willing payment. Greater than its hunger. And the Veil's hunger was a city's worth of childhoods, a cathedral's worth of first kisses and mothers' faces, a whole winter of remembered griefs.

What could possibly be greater than that?

*Time,* said the arithmetic in his chest. *A debt of time is the only currency the hunger respects. And I am made of nothing else.*

He raised his hand. It was absurd, raising his hand — there were only nine of them, and no one had established rules of order, and Sera Vane looked at him like he'd grown a second head — but he raised it anyway, because his mother had taught him manners in one of the years she'd kept for herself, and those years were precious to him.

"Marguerite," he said. "I want to say something."

The candles guttered. Somewhere above them, snow pressed against the high windows with the patience of a creditor.

"I've been doing the sums," Tam said. His voice came out steadier than he expected. "All night, actually. While everyone was arguing about jurisdiction and whether a god can be tried and whether Corvane should even be allowed in the building—" Old Maren snorted; Corvane had indeed been permitted to attend, chained to his chair by a ribbon of the luck-thief's worst fortune, which was a punishment Tam still didn't fully understand but enjoyed immensely. "—I was doing the sums. And I know what the payment has to be."

"Tobin." Marguerite said his full name — his ledger-name, the one the guild had given him when his mother's own name went into the books beside his debt — and her voice had a warning in it, low and careful, like ice creaking on a pond. "Don't."

"The Veil eats memory because memory *is* time. That's what Nix said. Every year we live becomes a memory, and every memory is a year the Veil can drink." He stood up. His knees felt strange, the way they always did when too many ages tried to occupy them at once. "But memories are secondhand time, aren't they? They're time already spent. What the Veil really wants — what it's been crawling toward all winter, why it stopped answering prayers once it got sated — is time that hasn't been spent yet. Time still in the purse."

"Tobin, sit down."

"And I'm the only person in this room," he went on, louder, because if he stopped he would hear his own heart counting and lose his nerve, "who is *made* of unspent time. Twenty years of my mother's life, lent to me at interest. The guild's oldest outstanding debt. I've read my own page, Marguerite. I know the terms. If the account is called due — willingly surrendered —"

"*Sit down.*"

It was not the volume that stopped him. It was the sound underneath the volume — a faint ringing, metallic and cold, like a blade being drawn an inch from its sheath. Everyone in the room heard it. Sera Vane's hand went to her mouth. Even Corvane, chained and disgraced, leaned forward with the expression of a man watching a contract he'd drafted decades ago finally come due.

Tam sat down.

"Thank you," Marguerite said quietly. The ringing faded. She closed the ledger with a soft, terrible finality. "The tribunal will recess while I consider the terms of verdict."

"That's not how tribunals work," said Nix the luck-thief, from the corner where she lounged with her boots on a chair, juggling a jar of someone else's good fortune. "We're making this up as we go. That's rather the point."

"Nix."

"Fine. Recessing." She tipped the jar toward Tam in something that might have been a salute or might have been pity. He hated both possibilities equally.

They filed out around him — Sera Vane squeezing his shoulder as she passed, Old Maren muttering something about young fools and old debts, the others drifting into the corridor where the snow-light turned everyone gray. Corvane was unchained last and left without looking at anyone, which was, Tam thought, the most honest thing the Guildmaster had done in forty years.

Soon only Marguerite remained, standing at the head of the table with her hand flat on the closed ledger, not looking at him.

"You checked your page," she said finally.

"This morning. Like every morning."

"How many?"

"Twelve years remain on the loan. Eleven if the interest compounds at midwinter, which it does tomorrow." He tried to smile. "Fitting, isn't it? The Veil falls on my due date."

"It isn't fitting. It isn't funny. And it isn't happening." Now she looked at him, and Tam saw something in her face he had never seen there in all the months she'd been trading favors for his loyalty, feeding him supper, teaching him to read the older scripts, asking after his dreams as though dreams were things worth asking after. He saw fear. Not for herself — he suspected Marguerite Ashgrove had made her peace with being spent long ago, back when she swore whatever oath she wouldn't speak of. For him.

"You don't get to decide that," he said. "It's my debt. My years. My—"

"They're not yours." She came around the table, and her voice dropped to something almost gentle, which was worse than shouting. "That's the entire cruelty of it, Tobin. They're your mother's. Lent, not given. Which means the choice was never yours to make, and it isn't mine either — it's hers. And she sold them to keep you alive, not to feed a god. Any reading of the contract that ends with you on an ice throne is a misreading, and I will burn the page before I honor it."

"You burned your own pages," he said. "Chapter and verse. We all watched you do it. You said debts shouldn't own people."

"My pages held *memories*. Yours holds a living boy." Her hand found his shoulder, light as frost. "There is a difference, and I will die defending it, and so will every person who walked out that door just now. You think you volunteered tonight. You didn't. You offered yourself up because you've spent seventeen years believing you're a loan walking around in a boy's shape. You're not. You're a boy. The years are just furniture."

Tam wanted to argue. He opened his mouth and found that the argument had dissolved somewhere between her hand on his shoulder and the word *boy*, which no one had called him — plainly, without ledger-qualifications — since his mother died.

"But the verdict," he managed. "If the tribunal rules the payment must be greater than its hunger, and I'm the only—"

"Then the tribunal rules wrongly, and we appeal to a higher court."

"There is no higher court. That's the whole point. That's why we're doing this."

"Yes," Marguerite said, and the cold ringing was in her voice again, faint and bright, the sound of a truth her oath would not let her soften. "There isn't. So we'll have to find one anyway. Sit tight, Tobin. Keep counting if you must — but count forward. Count spring."

She picked up the ledger, tucked it under her arm like a soldier's shield, and walked out into the snow-light, leaving Tam alone in the great chamber with nine empty chairs, one guttering candle, and the sound of his own borrowed heart, ticking.

He sat there a long while. Then, because he was who he was, he took out the scrap of paper where he kept his sums, crossed out the column he'd written overnight, and began a new one.

*Payment greater than its hunger,* he wrote. *Not necessarily a payment of time.*

He stared at the words. Somewhere beneath the palace, beneath the ley-lines and the ice and the throne where a king was drowning in his own crown, something vast and patient stirred — and for the first time all winter, Tam had the distinct feeling that it had heard him thinking.

* * *

Marguerite climbed the guildhall stairs two at a time, which was undignified for a woman of her years and entirely necessary for a woman of her purpose.

Nix fell into step with her on the landing, moving with that liquid grace that made pickpocketing look like dancing. "You handled that badly," the luck-thief said cheerfully.

"I handled it *exactly*. He lives."

"For now. But you heard the same testimony I did, swordspeaker. Willing payment, greater than its hunger. The old contracts are clear, and the old contracts were written by people who bound the thing in the first place. They weren't sentimental." Nix's grin faded into something rarer and more honest. "So what's the plan? Because I've got three jars of stolen fortune and a very bad feeling, and neither of those is a strategy."

Marguerite pushed open the door to her cramped office, where the frost sigils had begun blooming again along the window glass despite the wards, delicate as lacework and hungry as mouths. She set the ledger on the desk and opened it to the page she had been circling for two days — the oldest contract, the one sealed before Aldric's grandfather's reign, the one whose sigil matched the frost.

"The payment has to be greater than its hunger," she said. "Fine. Then we stop trying to find something bigger than a city's grief."

"We do?"

"We make the payment *worth more*." She tapped the contract, and the frost sigil caught the candlelight and threw it back in tiny, glittering shards. "Debts aren't measured in size, Nix. They're measured in meaning. A king's ransom is worth less than a beggar's promise, if the promise is kept. The Veil has been eating memories wholesale — cheap coin, spent grief, secondhand time. It's gorged and still starving, because none of it was *given freely*. Everything it took was taken."

Understanding dawned slowly across the luck-thief's face, and for once she didn't look pleased about it. "You're going to pay it something it can't discount. Something given, not stolen."

"I'm going to pay it myself. All of me. But not tonight, and not until I've settled two accounts." Marguerite pulled the ledger close and uncapped her pen, and her hand — steady through trials and tribunals and the slow unraveling of her own past — hesitated over the page. "First, I need to see the king. Alone. There's an hour, some evenings, when the winter lets go of him, and I have questions only Aldric can answer."

"And second?"

Marguerite looked at the frost on the window, and beyond it at the buried city, and at the small crooked house on Copper Street where a widow lived who no longer remembered her son's face — and whose son, Marguerite had discovered only yesterday, was still alive, still in the city, still praying every night to gods who had stopped listening.

"Second," she said, "I need to return a widow's memory before the Veil closes its books. Because if we're going to demand justice outside any law, then by every god old and new, we start by paying back what was stolen."

She wrote the date at the top of a fresh page. Outside, the snow fell on, patient as a creditor, and beneath the palace something vast turned over in its sleep — and listened.
