## 20. Spring Thaw

The first thing King Aldric remembered was the taste of snowmelt.

He woke in his own bed — a small mercy, that; he had feared waking nowhere at all — and the first sensation to reach him through the long gray fog of his captivity was water. Water running somewhere below the palace, in the gutters and the cisterns and the old stone throats of the drains, a sound he had not heard since autumn. Winter had held the city so long in its white fist that he had forgotten rivers made noise.

He lay still for a while and let the memories come back.

They came the way floodwater comes down a dry ravine — not gently, but all at once, and with debris. His mother's hands kneading bread in the palace kitchens because she had never trusted servants with it. The smell of the training yard in summer, leather and horse sweat and cut grass. The name of the gardener who had taught him to graft apple boughs when he was nine — Tammas, Tammas with the crooked teeth who whistled through the gap. His coronation, and how terrified he had been, and how the old priest had squeezed his shoulder before the crown came down and whispered, *it is only heavy until you stop fighting it*, which had turned out to be the worst advice anyone ever gave him.

Because he had stopped fighting it. That was the shame at the bottom of everything, the silt beneath the flood: there had been a moment — he could find it now, could put his finger on it as precisely as a clerk finding a line in a ledger — a moment in his third winter on the throne, grieving his father and drowning in petitions, when something cold had slid into the space behind his eyes and he had *let* it. He had been tired. It had offered rest. He had said yes without knowing what yes meant.

And after that — years. Years seen from underwater. The Veil had worn him like a glove wears a hand, and he had watched through the wool of himself as prayers were answered across his city and children's childhoods were eaten politely, one memory at a time, to keep the crown warm.

Aldric sat up. His body ached everywhere, the deep ache of a house long shut up. Through the window, the Snow Garden lay in ruins of its own making: the ice sculptures sagging, weeping, the hedges emerging black and dripping from their shrouds, and along the southern wall a single crocus had punched up through the thaw-mud with the blind audacity of all first flowers.

He remembered Marguerite Ashgrove. He remembered her standing in the undercroft with her sword bare and her voice steady, naming memories back at the hunger that had stolen them — *Tammas, Tammas with the crooked teeth* — until the thing wearing his body had screamed in a voice like a glacier calving. He remembered the last of it: her face going strange and smooth as she paid out her name like rope into a well, and the dark folding closed, and silence.

He did not remember what she looked like anymore. He realized this with a lurch. He had known her — argued with her, begged her, trusted her with his life and his city — and now her face was gone from him, spent in the closing of the Veil, part of the price. Somewhere in the ledger of impossible things there must be an entry for that too: *one face, surrendered, value beyond reckoning.*

Aldric swung his legs out of bed. His feet found the cold floor, and the cold felt honest, and he began — slowly, wincing, like a man learning to walk again after a long illness, which was exactly what he was — to dress himself without help.

There was work to do. There were pardons to sign and laws to break and a guildmaster to answer for them all, and no one else alive had stood where Aldric had stood or owed what he owed.

* * *

He held court in the great hall on the fourth day of the thaw, and he did it standing.

The hall had not been full in years — the Veil-King had preferred empty rooms, rooms where echoes could pool like meltwater — but word ran ahead of him through the city the way the thaw-water ran through the streets, and by midmorning the benches were crowded with lords in travel-stained finery, clerks, guild factors, and ordinary folk who had simply walked in off Copper Street because someone had said the king was awake and *himself*, and they wanted to see it with their own eyes before they believed it.

Aldric stood at the foot of the dais rather than upon it, which caused a rustle of alarm among the heralds, and spoke without a written speech.

"You have wintered badly," he said. "I know some of why. I will not pretend to know all of it, because much of what was done in my name was done by something that wore my face, and I will not insult your griefs by claiming I suffered equally. But I remember." His voice caught, and he let it. "I remember the cathedral congregation that prayed in good faith and woke with their childhoods hollowed out. I remember the widow on Copper Street. I remember every answered prayer and what each one cost, because the thing that took them kept the receipts inside my skull, and I have read them all."

Silence, except for the drip of thaw off the eaves outside, coming in through the high windows like a slow applause.

"So hear the first judgments of the spring," Aldric said. "First: the Guild of Impossible Things is pardoned. All debts owed to the Crown by that guild are struck from the record — not forgiven, *struck*, as though never written — because I have learned in the cruelest school there is that a debt can be a chain, and chains are how gods get built. Second:" and here his voice hardened, though he kept it level, "the trade in memory is outlawed. From this day, no contract may be sealed in this kingdom that buys or sells or borrows a person's past. Not luck, not years, not names. What a person remembers belongs to them as their blood does, and to take it without consent is theft of the highest order, and to take it *with* consent from the desperate is worse, and I will treat it as such."

Murmurs — approval from the back benches, something colder from the guild factors in their gray coats. Aldric met their eyes without apology.

"Third," he said, "Guildmaster Corvane will stand trial."

That brought the murmuring to a stop.

"Not before my courts," Aldric went on. "My courts tried nothing while the city starved of itself, and a court that sleeps through a crime has no standing to wake for the sentencing. No. Corvane will be judged by the tribunal that convened in your own guildhall — the debtors' tribunal, the one that dared to put a god in the dock when no law permitted it. If a ragged company of luck-thieves and sold-griefs and borrowed boys can convene justice when the law fails, then they can render it now, and I will enforce whatever verdict they hand down with the full weight of the crown." He paused, and something almost like a smile touched his mouth. "It seems fitting. He sold decades of the city's memories to keep his guild alive. Let him learn what the city remembers about *him*."

* * *

They came for Corvane on a bright morning, when the guildhall roof was shedding its snow in long silver sheets that crashed down into the yard like cymbals.

The debtors came — that was the remarkable thing, the thing the whole street turned out to watch. Not soldiers. Not the king's men. A luck-thief named Sable Vane with her coat pockets full of other people's fortunes; a broad quiet woman called Hessa who had sold her grief years ago and had spent the whole winter learning, painfully, to feel things again; a boy named Wren who was older than he looked and younger than he should have been, being knitted together out of borrowed years; and a dozen more besides, the margins of the law given faces and boots and a warrant.

Corvane met them in his office, behind his desk, in his gray coat with the guild seal polished bright. He had dressed for the occasion. That was the most damning thing about him, Sable thought later — he had *dressed*.

"Guildmaster Corvane," Sable said, and produced a folded paper, "you are summoned to answer before the tribunal of the guildhall, sitting in judgment by right of necessity, under sanction of the Crown."

"By right of *necessity*," Corvane repeated. His voice was dry as ledger-dust. "That is not a right. That is a mob with minutes."

"It's the only court that showed up," said Wren, from the doorway, "when everyone else was being eaten."

For a moment — just a moment — something moved behind Corvane's eyes that was not contempt. Something tired. Then he straightened his cuffs and reached for his coat.

"I will walk," he said, "since you have evidently decided I am to walk. But I will say this once, and you may enter it in your records." He looked around at them, at the luck-thief and the griefless woman and the boy made of other people's time, and his voice dropped its polish for the length of a single sentence. "Everything I sold, I sold to keep this roof standing. The alternative was the guild dying and the trade going underground to men with no ledgers and no rules. You think I don't know what I am? I have audited myself every night for forty years. Go on, then. Let the city audit me."

Hessa, who had sold her grief to this man's guild and spent a year unable to cry at her mother's funeral, stepped aside to let him pass. She did not speak. She did not need to. Her silence filled the doorway better than any curse.

* * *

The tribunal convened that evening in the guildhall's great room, the same room where a god had been placed in the dock by candlelight, and the city crowded in to watch.

It was not a pretty proceeding. Justice rarely is, when it is done honestly. There were no wigged advocates, only witnesses: the widow from Copper Street, who stood with her son's face still missing from her memory and described, in a voice that never rose and never needed to, what it was to look at a grown man and know he was hers and not know how. An old factor who testified that Corvane had bought pages of a clerk's childhood — a clerk named Ashgrove — and sold them upriver to the palace, batch by batch, for eleven years. Corvane himself, who did not deny a single entry, and whose defense consisted of exactly one sentence, repeated like a man counting beads: *the alternative was worse.*

The debtors deliberated for three hours. When they returned, it was Wren — the boy of borrowed years, the one who had volunteered to be spent and been refused — who read the verdict, because they had voted that the youngest voice should carry it.

"The tribunal finds Guildmaster Corvane guilty of the trade in memory," Wren said, "in eleven thousand four hundred and six counted instances, and uncounted more. For sentence:" he glanced down at the paper, and his borrowed years seemed suddenly very visible in his young-old face, "he is not to be executed. He is to *remember*. Every memory his guild ever bought and sold is to be read aloud to him, in full, in the presence of whoever it was taken from, until he has heard all of them. And when it is done, he will serve the rest of his life in the guild's counting-house, restoring what can be restored, keeping accounts of what cannot — so that no one in this city ever again mistakes a ledger for a conscience."

A sound went through the crowd — not a cheer, exactly. Something quieter and heavier, the sound of a city exhaling a breath it had held since autumn.

Corvane listened to all of it standing, and when it was done he bowed his head — whether in acceptance or merely exhaustion, no one present could say — and said only: "Eleven thousand four hundred and six. Your count is low. But begin."

* * *

Later that night, when the guildhall had emptied and the candles had burned down to their pools, the debtors remained at the long table, too tired to move and unwilling to separate.

Sable Vane turned a copper coin across her knuckles — her own luck, these days; she had given most of the rest back. Hessa sat with her hand flat on the table, practicing the small astonishing miracle of feeling the wood grain. Around them the building ticked and dripped, shedding its winter.

"She should be here," Wren said at last, into the quiet. "She started all this. She burned her own pages. She paid—" He stopped. Nobody liked saying it yet. *She paid her name.* The chair at the head of the table, where Marguerite Ashgrove had sat through the tribunal, stood empty, and none of them had moved it.

"She's upstairs," Hessa said.

They all looked at her.

"In the east room. Where she's been sleeping since the undercroft." Hessa's voice was careful, the way you speak near a wound. "She wakes. She eats. She works — she's already re-inked half the winter ledgers, correcting them. But she doesn't..." Hessa searched for the words. "She knows what she did. She remembers the doing of it, all of it, clear as glass. What she doesn't have is the *name*. You ask her who she is and she looks at you like a woman asked to read a page that isn't there. And the guild rolls — the old rolls, the ones Corvane kept — every entry under 'Ashgrove' went into the Veil with her. There's nothing left to give back."

Sable stopped spinning the coin. It lay still on the table between them, catching the candlelight, heads and tails and neither.

"A name isn't a memory," she said slowly. "That's the thing. Memories are the past. A name is... a door. You can't restore a door that's gone. But you can hang a new one."

Wren sat up. "You're not saying—"

"The guild gives names," Sable said. "It always has. That's half of what a guild *is* — it takes people the world won't call anything and calls them something. We've done it for thieves and runaways and sold-women for two hundred years. Why not for her?" She looked around the table, at each of them in turn, and her thief's grin surfaced briefly, soft at the edges. "Not as debt. Not as payment. As a gift. Freely given, freely taken. Written in the ledger in the gift column, where nothing is ever owed back."

The fire cracked. Somewhere above them, faint through the floorboards, they could hear the scratch of a pen — Marguerite, awake again, correcting the accounts of a winter that had finally loosened its grip.

"She'll refuse," Wren said. "You know she'll refuse. She'll say she hasn't earned—"

"Then we'll vote," Hessa said, "and she'll learn what the rest of us learned this winter. Some things aren't earned. They're *given*, and the giving is the point."

Sable drew the great ledger toward her across the table, opened it to a fresh page, and dipped her pen.

Above them, the pen scratched on. Spring water sang in the gutters of Copper Street. And in the guildhall's east room, a woman with no name sat correcting the record by candlelight, unaware that downstairs, in the column reserved for gifts, her friends were already choosing what to call her.
