## 21. A New Name, Freely Given

Spring came to Copper Street the way forgiveness comes — slowly, and then all at once.

Marguerite stood at the guildhall window on the first morning of true thaw and watched the snow surrender. It slid from the rooftops in long silver sheets, pooled in the gutters, and ran down the cobbles in bright threads that caught the light like spilled coin. The eaves dripped a rhythm she had not heard since autumn: *drip, drip, drip*, patient as a clerk's pen. Somewhere below, children were shouting about puddles. She had no memory of ever having been that child, but she found she could imagine it without pain now, the way one imagines a country never visited — with longing, but without grief.

She had been nameless for nineteen days.

The ledger knew it before anyone said it aloud. In the great book of accounts, where her name had once been written in Corvane's careful hand — *MARGUERITE ASHGROVE, sworn clerk, debts current* — there was now only a pale rectangle where the ink had been lifted out, as if the page itself had exhaled. The guild's ledgers did not forget. They simply recorded what was true, and what was true was that Marguerite Ashgrove had paid herself into the closing of the Veil, every memory braided to her name spent like the last coin in a desperate purse.

What remained was strange and spare. She remembered the guildhall's smell — tallow, old paper, the iron tang of the strongbox. She remembered how to hold a pen, how to audit a column, how to stand when speaking truth to power. She remembered the debtors' faces, though not always their histories; they had become hers too recently for the Veil to have wanted them. She remembered Aldric weeping in the thawing throne room, his crown set aside on the flagstones like something he intended never to wear again. And she remembered the moment under the palace, in the dark beneath the ice, when she had spoken her name into the hunger and felt it go out of her like warmth from an open hand.

Everything else was weather. Everything else was gone.

And yet she woke each morning, and the mornings were hers.

"You're doing it again," said Wren from the doorway — the luck-thief, arms crossed, one shoulder against the frame as if doors were things he refused to fully enter. "Standing at windows like a woman waiting to be missed."

"I'm watching the thaw."

"You're counting your losses." He came to stand beside her, and his reflection in the glass looked, as always, slightly luckier than he did. "There's a difference. One is honest work. The other is just accounting with extra steps."

"I'm a clerk," Marguerite said. "Accounting is my native tongue."

"Was," Wren corrected gently. "You're between tongues now. That's what today is for."

Today. She had almost let herself forget, which would have been its own small mercy, but the debtors had been unmistakable all week — whispering in corners, going quiet when she entered rooms, hiding things behind their backs like children with stolen sweets. Sable had confiscated the good candles from the storeroom. The boy, Tam, who was made of borrowed years and wore them lightly, had asked her three times what her favorite color was, each time pretending it was idle conversation, each time writing down the answer on his sleeve.

"They're going to make a ceremony of it," Marguerite said. "I told them I didn't want one."

"Of course you didn't. You're a woman who paid a god and walked home." Wren smiled, and for once the smile reached all the way up. "That's exactly why they're going to make a ceremony of it."

* * *

The guildhall had never held so many people, or so few of them debtors by trade.

They came anyway — the ones whose prayers had been answered and whose memories had been spent to answer them; the widow from Copper Street whose son's face had been returned to her in the unbinding, whole and laughing and seven years old forever; the cathedral congregation who had woken hollow and were filling again, slowly, like wells after drought. King Aldric came too, without guard and without crown, wearing a plain gray coat that made him look like what he was becoming: a man learning to be a citizen of the city he had nearly eaten. Corvane did not come. He sat in the tribunal's custody in the old courthouse, awaiting the new law's judgment, and Marguerite found she could think of him now without the taste of ash — only a tired old man who had sold pieces of other people to keep a roof standing, which was, she supposed, what guildmasters had always done, only more honestly than most.

They gathered in the hall of ledgers, beneath shelves that rose into shadow like cliff-faces of accounted lives. Sable had indeed taken the good candles, dozens of them, and set them burning along the great reading table until the whole room smelled of beeswax and warm paper. Someone had put spring flowers in an inkwell — crocuses, purple and defiant, the first thing the thaw had raised.

Tam stood on a chair so he could see over the table. He was fourteen years old in any given year, and had been fourteen for longer than arithmetic could politely explain, and he held the guild's oldest ledger open in both hands like something breakable and holy.

"We did it proper," he announced, in the voice of a boy who had rehearsed. "Sable checked the forms. Wren checked the seals. The widow checked the spelling, because Wren can't spell."

"Once," Wren said, "I misspelled one word."

"Once," Tam agreed solemnly, "*Ashgrove.*"

Marguerite's breath caught. She had not known that. There was so much she did not know about the name she had carried — that it had been misspelled once, that it had belonged to a girl who swore an oath on a winter night, that it had been written here in this book in a clerk's careful hand while the girl herself hid her sword beneath her skirts and pretended to be only ink. The name was gone from her, but its traces remained everywhere, the way a river remains in the shape of its valley.

"The old way," Tam went on, "is debt. You write a name in the ledger, and the name owes the guild, and the guild owns the name, and that's how it's been since before anybody remembers, because nobody remembers, because—" he faltered, hearing himself, and Sable put a steadying hand on his shoulder. He started again, quieter. "Because that's how it's always been. But the tribunal said names aren't free. So we decided they should be."

He turned the ledger around.

On the ancient page, beneath the pale rectangle where MARGUERITE ASHGROVE had been lifted away, a new line had been written in fresh ink — but not in the column of debts. It stood in the margin, in the wide empty space where the guild had never written anything, because margins were where nothing was owed. The letters were uneven, four different hands contributing, and beside them, instead of a sum, someone had drawn a small crocus.

**Given freely: a name, to keep.**

Below it, in Sable's precise script: *No terms. No interest. No repayment expected, requested, or permitted.*

"No repayment permitted," Marguerite read aloud, and her voice did something embarrassing at the end of it.

"That's the important part," Sable said. She was a broad, gray woman who had sold her grief decades ago and had spent the winter learning to want it back, and now had it, and wore it like a coat that finally fit. "We argued about it. Wren wanted terms."

"I wanted *one* term," Wren protested. "A single token payment. A copper. Because a gift with no price makes the giver a creditor whether they mean to or not — that's the first law of luck, everything balances — and I will not have this woman owing us anything, ever, not even symbolically—"

"So we paid it for her," said the widow from Copper Street, stepping forward. In her hands she held a single copper coin, worn smooth, and Marguerite recognized it with a jolt: the coin from the fountain on Copper Street, the one the widow had thrown with her prayer for rain, the prayer that had cost her her son's face. "One copper, already spent, already answered. Paid in full before the gift was given. So the account closed clean." She pressed the coin into Marguerite's palm and folded her fingers over it. Her son's face, restored, beamed from the crowd behind her. "You gave me back his face. Let me give you what it cost. We're even, and even means free."

Marguerite looked down at the coin. It was warm from the widow's hands, and ordinary, and worth less than nothing against what it settled, and it was the most valuable object she had ever held.

"And the name itself," Aldric said quietly, from the edge of the candlelight. All conversation stilled; a king's voice still carried the habit of command even when the king was trying to shed it. He stepped forward, self-conscious, and addressed the room rather than the throne that wasn't there. "I owe this hall a debt no ledger can hold. I was the crown it fed through. Every prayer answered from my mouth was paid from your memories, and I remember all of it now — every face, every hollow, every grief I ate without knowing I was hungry." His jaw tightened. "Justice has begun its work on Corvane, and it will reach the thing I was, too, in whatever court tries such things. But tonight is not justice. Tonight I only ask to witness. Because I took a name from this world, and I would like to see one given back."

Tam cleared his throat, produced a scrap of paper from his sleeve — covered, Marguerite now understood, in tally-marks of favorite colors — and read:

"The debtors of the Guild of Impossible Things, being all present and accounted for, do hereby give to the woman who was Marguerite Ashgrove, who paid her name to close the Veil and asked for nothing, the following name, chosen by vote, argued over for six days, and written in the margin where nothing is owed:"

He paused. Six days of argument. Marguerite realized she was holding her breath.

"*Wren wanted to call you Second Chance,*" Tam said, "but we voted that down as sentimental. Sable wanted something plain, because you'd hate anything fancy, and she was right, but plain names kept sounding like debts — Penny, Grace, Faith, all of those are coins somebody minted. The widow suggested Spring, but that's a season, not a person. And then—" he grinned, suddenly, all borrowed years shining in his face, "—and then I remembered what you said. Under the palace. When the ice was talking to you and telling you that you'd always been meant to be spent. You said—"

"*I am not a payment,*" Marguerite whispered. She remembered it. Of everything the Veil had taken, it had left her that sentence, planted like a seed in frozen ground. "*I am not a payment. I am the one who refuses the account.*"

"—so we voted," Tam finished, "unanimous, first ballot, fastest decision in guild history — and your name is **Vesper Ash**."

Silence, except for the candles.

"Vesper," Sable explained, "for evening. For the hour after the day's accounts close. The hour that belongs to no one's labor — the hour people give away."

"Ash," Wren added softly, "for what survives the fire. Not the flame, not the wood. What's left after, that the spring grows through."

"Also," Tam admitted, "it sounds extremely good written down. We tested it."

Marguerite stood very still among the candles, holding a copper coin worth everything, and let the name settle over her like snow in reverse — like warmth falling upward, gathering on her shoulders instead of burying her. *Vesper.* An hour of her own. *Ash.* Proof of fire survived. It was not the name she had been born to, the one sworn on a winter night by a girl with a hidden sword. That name had been an oath, and oaths were cages with pretty bars. This name was a door, left open on purpose.

"It doesn't bind me to anything?" she asked, and heard in her own voice the old clerk's caution, the reflex of a lifetime of fine print.

"Nothing," Sable said. "Read the entry. No terms."

"It doesn't make me belong?"

Aldric answered that one, and he answered it smiling, and for a moment the flicker of winter that had lived behind his eyes all through the long cold was nowhere to be found. "Belonging that binds is just debt wearing better clothes. Belonging that's chosen — that's the only kind that holds. Ask any king." He glanced at the crocus in the inkwell. "Or any flower."

Marguerite — Vesper — picked up the pen.

It was the guild's own pen, the one that had written ten thousand debts, and her hand knew its weight the way her hand still knew the weight of a sword she no longer carried. She leaned over the ancient ledger, over the pale rectangle of her spent name, over the margin where nothing was owed, and beneath the entry the debtors had written, she added one line of her own, in a steady hand, in ink the color of a winter sky deciding to be blue:

*Accepted, freely and in full. Vesper Ash, resident, by choice.*

Then, because some habits are worth keeping, she initialed it.

* * *

Afterward, when the candles had burned low and the crowd had gone out into the dripping, singing evening, Vesper climbed the stairs to the room the guild had given her months ago, back when she was a clerk with a secret and a cage for a heart. Nothing in it had changed — the narrow bed, the window over Copper Street, the shelf where her ledgers lived. But she had changed the room by surviving it, the way thaw changes a frozen river without moving a stone.

She set the widow's copper on the windowsill, where the last light could find it.

Below, the street was loud with meltwater and children, and somewhere Wren was teaching Tam to cheat at dice properly, ethically, with consent, and Sable was grieving on purpose, choosing it, because grief freely felt was the opposite of grief sold. The city was putting itself back together the slow way — not by miracle, not by prayer, but by neighbors, which was the older magic and the stronger one.

Vesper opened the newest ledger — the spring ledger, blank and waiting — and wrote the date at the top of the first page. Then she sat for a while with the pen poised, listening to the drip of the eaves, the sound of a world paying itself back.

There was a knock. Small. Familiar.

In the doorway stood a child she didn't know yet — a girl of perhaps eight, mud to the knees, clutching something in both fists. Behind her, hesitating at the foot of the stairs, waited a cluster of neighborhood children, drawn by rumor: that the guildhall was safe now, that the woman inside wrote answers, that gods might still listen if you asked in the right book.

"Are you the one who decides what things are worth?" the girl asked.

Vesper Ash looked at the child, and at the spring coming up the street behind her, and at the blank page waiting under her hand.

"Come in," she said. "Let's find out together."
