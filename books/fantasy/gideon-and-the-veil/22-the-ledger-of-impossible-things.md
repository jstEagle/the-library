## 22. The Ledger of Impossible Things

Spring came to Copper Street the way forgiveness comes to a hard heart — slowly, and then all at once.

Marguerite stood at the open shutters of her office above the guildhall and watched the snowmelt run silver down the gutters, carrying winter out of the city one bright thread at a time. The eaves dripped in a rhythm like a patient clock. Somewhere below, a cart horse stamped and blew steam into air that no longer bit. The plane trees along the canal had gone from bone to smoke to a haze of green so sudden it seemed the city had agreed upon it overnight, in secret, while everyone slept.

She had lived through three winters in this city before the long one. She remembered none of them now — not truly, not the way memory used to work, warm and whole and hers. What remained was stranger: a collection of facts that behaved like memories but had no weight behind them. *It snows here by the feast of Saint Ovel. The canals freeze hard enough to skate.* She knew these things the way she knew the guild's tariff schedules, because someone had written them down for her, and because she had chosen to believe the handwriting.

Her name was Wren now.

The ledger lay open on her desk, its vellum soft as cloth at the corners after years of turning. She had written in it every day since the thaw, because the guild had asked her to, and because — though she would never have said it aloud in those first raw weeks — the writing kept her anchored. A nameless woman could drift. A clerk with ink on her fingers and accounts to balance could not.

The spring trade had resumed the way water finds its old channels. Debts of luck moved again through the city's quiet veins: a fisherman borrowing a fair wind against next summer's catch, a young mother trading an hour of sleep for her fevered daughter's ease, a merchant purchasing the courage he'd failed to find on his own. Impossible things, all of them, entered in Marguerite's careful hand — *one bottled lullaby, vintage uncertain; one borrowed shadow, returned slightly stretched* — and each entry felt less like bookkeeping than like prayer.

That was the change, she thought. That was what the winter had bought with all its terrible interest. Before, the guild had traded in impossibilities the way smugglers trade in silk — furtive, ashamed, profitable. Now the doors stood open. Aldric's pardon hung framed beside the guildhall entrance, and beneath it his edict outlawing memory-debt, and beneath *that*, in smaller letters that Corvane had fought and lost, the words: *No debt shall be collected from any person's past.*

Corvane himself was gone now, remanded to the tribunal he had never imagined would have jurisdiction over him — a court of debtors and luck-thieves and a boy made of borrowed years, sitting in judgment in the very hall where he'd once signed away other people's childhoods by the page. They had been gentle with him, in the end. Gentler than he deserved, some said. But the debtors had learned something in the undercroft, standing shoulder to shoulder against the Veil's counterattack while the ley-lines screamed: that punishment taken in anger is just another kind of hunger, and hunger was the enemy. Corvane would spend his remaining years working off his own account, entry by entry, in the ledgers he once ruled. There were worse sentences. There were also, Marguerite noted dryly, far better clerks.

A knock at the door — small, hesitant, the knock of someone who had knocked at many doors and been turned away from most of them.

"Come in."

The girl who entered couldn't have seen more than eight winters. She wore a coat too big for her, sleeves rolled in thick cuffs, and she clutched something against her chest with both hands as though it might fly away. Her hair was the color of wet sand and stood up at the crown where she'd clearly been pulling at it. She stopped three steps inside the door and looked around the office with enormous eyes — at the shelves of bottled sounds, at the jar of borrowed courage, at the tall windows full of green light.

"You're the clerk," she said. Not a question. Children in this city had learned, over the winter, exactly who ran the guild now.

"I'm the clerk," Marguerite agreed. "And you're brave, coming up all these stairs alone. Most adults send letters."

"Mama said you don't bite." The girl considered. "Anymore."

Marguerite kept her face grave. "Your mother is well informed."

The girl approached the desk in stages, emboldened by the lack of biting, until she stood close enough that Marguerite could see the shadows under her eyes and the fine tremor in her hands. A child who had prayed during the long winter. A child who had learned what answers cost.

"I brought something," she said. "For trade."

She opened her cupped hands. In them lay a button — brass, worn smooth, threaded with a scrap of red ribbon faded almost to pink. It gleamed dully in the light like something excavated rather than owned.

"It was my grandda's," the girl said. "From his soldier coat. Mama says it's the only thing we got back when he died. She says it's worth more than money." Her chin lifted, defiant, daring Marguerite to disagree. "So it's worth enough. For what I want."

Marguerite looked at the button for a long moment. Around it, faintly, she could see what her trained eye had always seen and what the winter had taught her to fear: the ghost-shimmer of attachment, the bright thread of story running from the object into the girl's chest. A thing loved for years held more weight than gold. The old guild would have weighed it, appraised it, taken it gladly and given the child sixpence and a receipt.

"What is it you want?" Marguerite asked gently. "Before we talk price. Tell me what you want first."

The girl's defiance crumbled all at once, the way children's does, and what was underneath was so bare and simple it hurt to look at.

"I want to know if the gods still listen."

* * *

Marguerite rose and crossed to the window, mostly so the girl wouldn't see her face do whatever it was doing.

Below, Copper Street went about its business. A baker's boy wove between the carts with a basket of the first true loaves of spring. Two old men argued cheerfully outside the chandlery about nothing. On the corner where the widow Maren had once knelt in frost and forgotten her son's face, someone had planted a cherry tree — the debtors' doing, the previous autumn, a living apology the city hadn't known how to make. It was blooming now, absurdly, extravagantly white, dropping petals into the meltwater like a slow snowfall going the wrong direction.

The gods still listened. That was the truth of it, the strange and terrible and wonderful truth that Marguerite carried in the hollow where her name used to be. The Veil was closed — sealed under a mountain of surrendered memory, under a king's grief finally set down, under one woman's name freely given — but closing the Veil had not silenced what lay beyond it. If anything, the listening had grown clearer. Prayers went up from the city now like birdsong, and sometimes — not always, not predictably, never for sale — something answered. A rain that fell only on a dying garden. A fever that broke at dawn for no reason medicine could name. Small mercies, unpriced, unbought. The guild had a standing policy about them, drafted in Marguerite's own hand: *Answers that arrive without payment shall be recorded, witnessed, and never invoiced.*

But the girl wasn't asking about weather. Marguerite understood that. The girl was asking the question every survivor of that winter carried: *If the gods listen, why did they let it happen? And if they answer, what will it take from me?*

She turned back. The girl hadn't moved, except to close her fingers a little tighter around the button.

"Sit with me," Marguerite said, and pulled the second chair — the good one, the one clients weren't usually offered — around to the near side of the desk.

The girl sat. Up close, her eyes were gray-green, the color of the canal in April.

"My name is Wren," Marguerite said. It still felt like wearing borrowed clothes, saying it. It still felt, more and more lately, like clothes that fit. "What's yours?"

"Tansy."

"Well, Tansy. I'm going to tell you something true, and then I'm going to write it down, because in this building we believe true things should be written down. All right?"

Tansy nodded, solemn as a coronation.

"The gods listen," Wren said. "They always listened. Even in the worst of the winter — especially then. But there was a thing between us and them, a great hungry thing, and everything people prayed for, it caught in its teeth on the way through. Every answer came with a bill attached, and the bill was paid out of the pray-er." She watched the girl absorb this with the terrible directness of children who had lived through what Tansy had lived through. "You remember the winter."

Tansy's jaw tightened. "Mama doesn't talk about it."

"No. Many people don't. But you remember."

"Nana forgot her sister." The words came out flat, rehearsed, a grief the girl had carried alone because the adults wouldn't carry it with her. "One morning Nana didn't know who Aunt Liv was anymore. And everyone said it was her age. But I heard her praying the night before. She prayed for Papa to find work." Tansy looked down at the button. "He found work the next day. And Nana lost Aunt Liv."

The office was very quiet. The eaves dripped their patient clock-time outside.

"And now," Tansy said, and her voice cracked down the middle, eight years old and ancient, "now I have something to ask for. My little brother is sick. Not — not dying sick, Mama says, but sick, and I thought — if the gods are listening again, really listening, like people say —" She thrust the button forward across the desk, arm shaking. "Take it. Take all of it. Just tell me what it costs first. Nobody told Nana what it cost."

* * *

Later — much later, after the girl had gone home with her brother's fever already breaking (some mercies arrived early, unasked, and Wren had learned to accept them with gratitude instead of suspicion) — Wren sat alone with the ledger and the button and the long green light, and she wrote.

This was the entry, in her careful clerk's hand:

*Received in trade, from Tansy of Copper Street, aged eight: one brass button, soldier's coat, with ribbon. Appraised value: incalculable. Payment rendered: one answer, given freely, per guild policy.*

*The answer, as given: Yes. The gods still listen. They listened through the hunger, and they listen now that the hunger is bound. But hear this also, Tansy, and keep it: the listening was never the problem. The toll was. And the toll-taker is dead, and no god worth the name will ever again charge a child for asking.*

*Note appended, clerk's discretion: the button is not payment. The button is a gift, and gifts are entered elsewhere. See page one.*

She turned back to page one.

Page one of the Ledger of Impossible Things had been blank for as long as anyone could remember — a superstition, a tradition, a space left empty for reasons the oldest members claimed not to know. After the thaw, Wren had filled it. At the top, in letters larger than her usual hand:

*Given, not owed:*

And beneath it, a list that grew a little every week. *To the city of Gildenholt: its winter back, and its dead accounted. To King Aldric: his hours, restored. To the widow Maren: her son's face, drawn from the king's own remembering and delivered by royal letter, framed above her mantel. To Nana of Copper Street: her sister's name, spoken aloud at the spring festival, so that forgetting it became a smaller wound.* And at the bottom, in fresh ink, the newest line, added the night the debtors had gathered around her with a candle and a pen and refused to leave until she wrote it herself:

*To the clerk called Wren: a name, freely given, no repayment expected or accepted. Entered by unanimous consent of the debtors, who were owed nothing and chose to give anyway.*

She read it twice, as she did every evening. Some nights it steadied her. Some nights it undid her completely, and she sat in the dark office with ink-stained fingers pressed to her mouth, grieving a self she could not picture and marveling at a belonging she had never earned and could never lose.

Tonight it did neither. Tonight it simply warmed her, the way the sun through the window warmed the desk, quietly and without condition.

Outside, the cherry tree on the corner shed another handful of petals. Somewhere a child laughed. Somewhere a prayer went up, small and hopeful, threading toward heaven through air that was only air.

Wren dipped her pen, found a clean line, and began the day's accounts.

*Spring trade, first week of the thaw's end:* she wrote. *One fair wind, borrowed and returned on time, with thanks. One hour of sleep, traded fairly, mother and daughter both resting easier. One courage, purchased at honest rate, spent well by a man who needed it. And one question, brought up the stairs by the bravest client of the season, answered without charge:*

*Do the gods still listen?*

*Yes,* she wrote, and smiled, and meant it all the way down into the hollow where her name had been. *Yes. And this time, so do we.*
