## 1. The Ledger of Impossible Things

Rain is the Vellum Guild's oldest employee. It has never missed a shift, never asked for wages, and never once been entered into the ledgers — which, given what we do record, has always struck me as a small theological scandal.

I was thinking about this because the rain was loud that night. Loud enough to make the gutters sing, loud enough to drown out the sound of my own pen, which is a mercy I have learned to appreciate. When you spend your evenings writing down the price of other people's souls, you begin to prefer not hearing yourself.

The counting room of the Guildhouse on Candlewick Lane is a long, narrow chamber with a ceiling low enough to remind you that the guild does not believe in looking up. Forty-one candles burned in their iron sconces — forty-one, always forty-one, because our masters are particular about numbers the way other men are particular about knives. The shelves ran the length of both walls, and every shelf groaned under ledgers: calfskin, vellum, and in the older cases, something I have never been able to identify and have stopped trying to. Some of those old books are warm to the touch. I try not to think about why.

I am Gideon Winterbourne, and for seven years I have kept the ledgers of the Vellum Guild.

Before that, I was a mercenary, which means I was paid to hurt people who deserved it slightly less than my employers said they did. Before *that* — well. There is no before that. There is a hole where a childhood should be, smooth-walled and clean as a well, and at the bottom of it sits a god who swore an oath with me and then went quiet.

But we will come to the god. We always come to the god eventually, the way water comes to a drain.

That night I was auditing the autumn quarter, which meant I was working through the ledger titled, in the Guild's flat administrative hand, *Borrowed Years — Recoveries & Forfeitures*. If you have never held such a book, let me describe it. Imagine a merchant's inventory list. Now imagine the merchandise is time pulled out of living bodies like thread from a hem. Entry by entry:

*From Aldous Merrin, cooper, three years recovered against default of payment. Condition on return: unused. Disposition: resale.*

*From the widow Anthe of Harrowgate, eleven years surrendered voluntarily against debt. Note: seller wept throughout transaction. Note appended: weeping does not affect valuation.*

That last note was written in Sarelle's hand. Guildmistress Sarelle Voss writes her notes the way she does everything else — precisely, without cruelty, and without a single drop of mercy either, which is somehow worse. Cruelty at least admits it's interested in you.

I turned pages. The candle flames leaned when the wind found the shutters, straightened again. Somewhere above me, the senior clerks were finishing their own work; I could hear the muffled shuffle of feet and once, briefly, laughter, cut off quickly, as though laughter were also an entry that had to be balanced.

Here is what I want you to understand about the Vellum Guild, because understanding it took me years and cost me more than I can itemize. We do not deal in evil. Evil would be simple. Evil announces itself, kicks in doors, takes what it wants and leaves you your hatred intact. No — the Guild deals in *impossible things*, and impossible things require paperwork. Bottled names, each stoppered flask humming faintly when you walk past, like bees dreaming of their former owners. Second chances, decanted into grey glass and sold at a markup that would embarrass a moneylender. Borrowed years. First kisses returned to the recently widowed, at a rate per memory. Luck, weighed on brass scales. Forgiveness — actual forgiveness, the divine article — available in limited quantities to qualified buyers.

And every single item, every flask and phial and folded year, has its line in a ledger. Date acquired. Source. Price. Condition. Disposition.

Everything except the rain. Everything except me.

I paused over that thought the way you pause over a stair in the dark that turns out to have one step fewer than you remembered. It passed. Such thoughts pass, if you're disciplined. I am very disciplined. It is one of the few things I know for certain about myself, and I hold onto it the way a drowning man holds onto a plank he suspects might be a coffin lid.

Because here is the thing about my employment that I have never said aloud, not to Sarelle, not to the clerks, not to the god who isn't listening: I do not remember being hired.

Seven years ago I woke on the steps of the Guildhouse with rain in my face, a sword callus on my right palm, an oath scar over my heart shaped like a closed eye, and a name — *Gideon Winterbourne* — sitting in my mouth like a coin someone had placed there while I slept. Sarelle met me at the door as if she'd been expecting me. She said, "You're late," and put a pen in my hand, and I have been writing in these ledgers ever since, and I have never once wondered — not properly, not with the full weight of a man behind the wondering — whether a man can be *late* to his own beginning.

You see how disciplined I am. I can even write that paragraph and then turn the page and keep auditing.

* * *

The third ledger of the night was the worst one. They keep it chained to the shelf, which is theater — the chain wouldn't stop a determined child — but the theater matters. It tells the clerks: *this book is different.* It is bound in white leather gone yellow at the edges, and it records the Guild's holdings in souls.

Not soul-stuff. Not essence, not the bottled residue that traders sell in the southern markets. Souls entire. Whole ones. The Guild owns perhaps two dozen, acquired over centuries through contracts so airtight that the original owners signed away not just their afterlives but their *selves*, their continuity, the thread that makes a person the same person from one dawn to the next. These souls sit in the ledger like property in a warehouse, and the property appreciates.

I read the entries without letting myself read them, the way you read a funeral notice for someone you almost knew. And then I reached the newest acquisition, ink barely a season old, and stopped.

*Item: one (1) soul, juvenile, provenance royal — see sealed annex. Acquired via intermediate brokers, chain of custody incomplete. Consideration paid: see annex. Status: HELD PENDING COLLECTION. Collection window: thirteen moons from date of contract.*

Juvenile. Royal. Held pending collection.

I sat there for a long moment with my pen hovering, leaving a small dark bloom of ink on the margin, which tomorrow I would have to explain to Sarelle, and which tonight felt like the only honest mark I'd made all evening.

A child's soul. In my employer's inventory. Between a luck-charm lot and a crate of second chances, filed like a bolt of cloth.

And beneath the unease — beneath it, down where the well-wall is smoothest — something stirred that had no words. A recognition, maybe. Or a memory of recognition, the ghost of a feeling whose cause had been scooped out of me. It was like hearing a song you know you used to dance to, except you cannot remember dancing, or the partner, or whether the partner is alive.

I wrote in the audit column: *Documentation incomplete. Flag for review.* Which is true, and useless, and the most I could do, and I hated myself a little for how easily the most I could do fit into a ledger line.

The candles guttered. The rain kept up its endless unpaid labor. And I told myself the feeling would pass, because feelings always pass, if you're disciplined.

It did not pass.

* * *

Near midnight I did what I do every midnight, which is the only part of my life I would fight to keep.

There is a room at the back of the Guildhouse, past the vaults and the flask-cellar, a room no ledger lists. The clerks call it the chapel, without irony and without faith, the way sailors call the toilet the head. Inside there is no altar, no icon, nothing you'd recognize as holy. Just a stone floor worn smooth in one spot — knee-shaped, my shape, seven years deep — and a single candle that I light myself, because the god does not light it for me.

My god has no name I know. Seven years ago, when I woke on those steps with the oath scar burning over my heart, the oath came with me whole: *Keep the accounts true. Guard what is owed. Wait for the word.* Whose word? What is owed? Wait how long? The oath didn't say. Gods, in my limited experience, draft their contracts the way the Guild wishes it could: no loopholes, no clarifications, no customer service.

So I kneel. Every night. On the smooth stone, in the cold, before a silence so complete it has texture, like pressing your ear to a door and hearing nothing and knowing — *knowing* — someone stands on the other side.

"Still here," I said, which is how I open most prayers. It's not devotion exactly. It's more like reporting for a shift where the foreman never arrives.

Silence. The candle flame stood perfectly still, which it shouldn't have, because the draft from under the door moves everything in that room. Everything except the flame. I've decided not to find that encouraging or ominous. I've decided it simply *is*, the way the rain simply is.

"I audited the soul holdings tonight," I told the silence. "There's a child in the book."

Nothing. Of course nothing. A thousand nights of nothing, and I have made a profession of the nothing, knelt into the nothing until my knees wore their confession into the stone.

But — and I want to be careful here, because I am a man who checks his figures twice — tonight the silence was different. Tonight it had a quality I can only call *attentive*. You know how a room feels when it's empty versus when someone is in it, not moving, breathing quietly, watching? That. The silence wasn't absent. The silence was *waiting*, and it had been waiting so long and so patiently that my thousand nights of prayer suddenly looked like a man knocking on a door that was never locked, only closed, with someone standing behind it saying, *not yet, not yet, not yet.*

"Why now?" I asked. "What changed?"

The candle flame bent — toward me, I would swear it bent toward me — and then stood straight again, and the moment passed, and I was a kneeling man in a cold room talking to air, and I got up because a man has to sleep, and discipline is discipline.

But I took the candle with me instead of leaving it, which I have never done. I don't know why. I tell myself the wax was nearly spent anyway.

* * *

I was halfway down the corridor to my cell with the candle cupped in one hand when the knock came.

Three knocks. Soft. Unhurried. The kind of knock that has no fear in it, which at that hour, in that weather, at that door, is itself a kind of wrongness. Beggars knock desperately. Drunkards knock loudly. Guild creditors knock in patterns. This knock was simply *there*, the way the moon is simply there.

The Guildhouse door is oak, banded in iron, warded with sigils that cost us four borrowed years apiece from a hedge-mage who has since sold the rest of himself in installments. Those wards are tuned to sense threat, and fraud, and undeath, and a number of things the clerks won't name in daylight. When anything untoward touches that door, the sigils flare blue along the lintel, and half the house wakes up reaching for weapons.

I stood in the corridor and waited for the flare.

It didn't come.

Instead — and this is the part I have replayed perhaps two hundred times since, and it does not soften with rehearsal — the sigils flared *gold*. All of them, down the whole lintel, a bright seam of gold light spilling under the door and up the walls, and the ward-sigils are not supposed to be able to do that, and every hair on my arms stood up, and somewhere above me a clerk's chair scraped as he shot upright too.

Three knocks again. Patient. Almost gentle.

I set the candle on a shelf. I unhooked the lamp from its bracket, because some instincts survive even a missing childhood, and walked to the door, and — noting, distantly, that I should be calling for Sarelle, that protocol demanded I wake the house, that I was about to violate about six rules and possibly one oath — I lifted the bar.

The rain came in first, silver in the lamplight, and then, standing in it, a child.

She was eight years old, maybe, small enough that the storm seemed to be happening around her rather than to her. Mud to her knees, hair plastered black to her skull, a dress that had once belonged to someone richer and now belonged entirely to the road. Barefoot. Barefoot, in that rain, in that cold, on the mountain road that leads down from passes no cart uses anymore.

She was not shivering.

She looked up at me, and her eyes were the grey of the old ledgers' unidentified binding-leather, and she was completely, absolutely unafraid, and I felt the strangest sensation, as though someone had reached into my chest and turned a key I didn't know I had.

Then she spoke.

Not common tongue. Not any tongue. The sounds came out of her small mouth like water over old stones — syllables that seemed to arrive from further away than her lips, that made the lamplight shiver, that made the gold light along the doorway pulse once, twice, like a heartbeat remembering itself. Behind me, up the corridor, I heard a clerk cry out. I heard another fall to his knees, though I couldn't see him and couldn't tell you how I knew. The language went through the house like cold through a cracked wall.

And I understood her.

That is the part I keep circling. I don't speak the language. I have never heard the language. But the meaning arrived in me whole, the way the name *Gideon Winterbourne* arrived seven years ago, placed there by someone else's hand, and the meaning was this:

*"You kept the candle lit."*

The child stepped closer, out of the rain, and pressed something into my hand — small, warm despite the storm, a copper coin stamped with a sigil: a closed eye.

My oath scar, over my heart, answered her like a struck bell.

I stood in the doorway holding a coin I recognized and could not place, listening to a language I knew and could not speak, while behind me the ledgers of the Vellum Guild sat on their shelves in the dark, patient as debts, and somewhere in their numbered thousands of entries there was — I understood this too, suddenly, the way you understand a trapdoor is under you one heartbeat before it opens —

there was an entry that had my name in it.

Not *Gideon Winterbourne*.

The other one.
