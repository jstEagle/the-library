## 2. A Child at the Door

The knock came at the worst possible hour, which is to say any hour at all.

I had been reconciling the third ledger of the night — *Borrowed Years, Small Denominations* — and the numbers were misbehaving in the way they always do when someone has tried to pay a debt with time they hadn't yet lived. Somewhere in the eastern provinces a farmer had sold four years of his future for seed grain, and then, in a fit of optimism I could only admire, had attempted to buy back two of them with a harvest that hadn't happened yet. The ledger did not care for this. The ledger, like most of my colleagues, preferred its impossibilities pre-approved.

I set down my pen. The knock came again — three raps, spaced evenly, patient as rain.

Now, you should understand something about the Vellum Guildhouse on Cordwainer's Row: it does not get knocked for. People are *summoned*. They arrive by appointment, by summons, by the slow accumulation of debt, or occasionally — and these are my favorite cases to file — by dying somewhere inconvenient and being invoiced for it. The front door of the guildhouse has not been knocked upon in living memory, mostly because it cannot be seen unless the guild wishes to be seen, and the guild wishes to be seen approximately never.

So when the knocking came a third time, small and stubborn and utterly without protocol, every hair on my arms stood up and took notice.

I looked at the wards first. That's habit. The wards run along the doorframe like veins of frozen lightning, old work, older than the building, older possibly than the street, and they tell you things about visitors before you ever see them. A debtor glows faint amber. A courier shivers green. Something that means the guild harm turns the whole frame the color of an angry bruise, and the senior clerks come running with their ink-stained fingers already sketching countermeasures.

The wards were doing none of these things.

The wards were *leaning*. There is no better word for it. The pale light in the doorframe bent inward, toward the door, the way grass bends toward something that hasn't arrived yet but will.

"Well," I said to the empty room, because talking to empty rooms is one of the few perks of working the night audit alone, "that's new."

I took the lamp off the shelf — the good lamp, the one that burns whatever it's pointed at if I speak the right word, which I have never had occasion to speak and privately hope never to need — and went down through the reading room, past the shelves where bottled names murmur to each other in the dark like old men complaining about weather, past the cabinet of second chances (locked; the key lives in Guildmistress Sarelle's left boot, and no, I don't know how she gets it in and out of the boot, and I have decided as a matter of professional survival never to find out), down the corridor to the front hall.

The knocking came a fourth time just as I reached the door. Softer now. Almost apologetic.

I unbarred it the way you're supposed to — sideways, lamp raised, weight on the back foot, the stance they drilled into me in another life I only half remember — and pulled the door open onto the storm.

A child stood on the step.

She was perhaps eight years old, though with children it's always a guess; they're like ledgers that way, you can't know what's written inside until someone opens them. She was caked head to foot in mud of a color I didn't love — river mud, gray-brown, the kind that means she'd come up from the floodbanks, which meant she'd crossed half the city in weather that was currently trying to drown the cobblestones. Her hair was black and plastered flat. Her eyes were the color of the sky just before snow, and they looked at me without a single flicker of the fear that an eight-year-old standing barefoot on a strange doorstep at midnight ought, by all natural law, to be feeling.

"Hello," I said. Because I am, beneath the mercenary habits and the ledger-keeping and the general air of a man who has made his peace with disappointment, polite.

She said something back.

It was not common tongue. It was not anything I'd heard before, and I want to be clear about my credentials here: I have heard a great many languages, most of them in the context of people telling me they intended to kill me, some of them in the context of people apologizing for it afterward. I've traded in three dialects of the coast-people, enough mountain speech to order ale and insult a man's mother, and the hand-sign language the guild uses when it doesn't want the ledgers overhearing. This was none of those. The words came out round and ringing, old-sounding, the way a bell sounds old even when you can't say why, and each syllable landed on the air like a stone dropped into still water.

And the wards *screamed*.

Not the alarm-shriek they make for burglars — I've heard that, it's undignified, like a cat in a sack. This was different. Every vein of frozen lightning in the doorframe flared white-hot, then blue, then a color I genuinely do not have a name for, a color like the moment before a memory comes back to you, and the whole doorway hummed so hard my teeth ached. Behind me, somewhere deep in the house, something answered — a low resonant groan, the sound of very old magic being asked a question it didn't know how to answer.

The girl stopped speaking. She watched the wards flare with an expression of mild scholarly interest, the way I watch other people's disasters.

"Right," I said. "One moment."

I did what any sensible employee of an institution that trades in impossible things would do. I shut the door in her face.

I stood with my back against it for a count of ten, listening to the rain hammer the eaves and my own heart conduct its business entirely too loudly. Then I opened it again, because — and this is the part I keep circling back to, the part I'll be turning over for nights afterward — leaving her out there felt less like prudence and more like abandonment, and I don't use that word lightly. Abandonment has a taste. I know it the way you know a smell from childhood: not the memory of it, just the recognition.

She hadn't moved. Rain ran off her chin. She looked up at me with those pre-snow eyes and waited, patient as a creditor.

"You can't stand out there," I said, which was true even if nothing else about the situation was. "Come in before the wards decide you're a problem instead of a puzzle."

She stepped over the threshold without hesitation, and the wards parted around her like water around a keel. I noticed that. I noticed it the way you notice a knife missing from a rack — not consciously, at first, just a wrongness filed away for later.

* * *

Getting her into the reading room was a matter of wrapping her in the good wool blanket from my chair and sitting her near the fire, which she approached the way other children approach a sleeping dog — with respect, but no particular awe. I fetched bread and the last of the honey from my own stores, because the guild's kitchen is locked after midnight and because, again, politeness, which I maintain is the last luxury available to a man in my profession.

She ate like someone who had learned hunger early and thoroughly: carefully, completely, wasting nothing. While she ate, I studied her, and while I studied her, the wrongnesses accumulated.

Her feet were cut but not frostbitten — she'd been walking long enough to bleed, not long enough to freeze, in this weather? Her mud was river mud, but under it her skin was clean, scrubbed-clean, recently clean. And her hands — I'm a ledger man, hands are my trade, I read hands the way priests read scripture — her hands were wrong for a beggar child. No calluses on the fingertips. Ink stains, faint, in the creases. Someone had taught those hands to hold a pen.

"So," I said, crouching to her level, keeping my voice at the register you'd use for a spooked horse, except she wasn't spooked, she was watching me eat-less-and-stare-more with what I swear was amusement. "You want to tell me who sent you?"

She shook her head.

"Do you have a name?"

Another shake. Then she paused, tilted her head, and said a word in that ringing old language. It sounded like *Wren*, or close enough that I chose to hear it that way, and she tapped her own chest twice when she said it, which even across the gulf of tongues is fairly universal grammar.

"Wren," I said. "Good. One of us has a name."

She smiled. It transformed her entire face, and something in my chest did a thing I did not authorize it to do.

That was when the senior clerks arrived.

They came in a body, five of them, drawn by the wards' tantrum — Master Ellery-Voss first, as always, moving with the ponderous dignity of a man who believes his importance is visible from space. He took one look at the girl, one look at the blanket, one look at me, and his face performed a remarkable maneuver: it went pale from the bottom up, like ink flooding backward through water.

"What," he said, "is *that*."

"A child," I said. "In a storm. At our door. I understand this is irregular—"

"She *spoke*." His voice had gone thin. "We heard it through three floors of warded stone. Do you know what we heard, Winterbourne? Do you have any idea—"

"No," I said honestly. "But I'm guessing you do, and I'm guessing you wish you didn't."

Ellery-Voss's mouth opened and closed twice. Behind him, old Mistress Hark, who has worked the guild's deep archives since before I was hired and who once told me she'd forgotten more than I'd ever learn, and whom I believe, pressed her knuckles to her lips. Another clerk, young Pell, actually sat down on the floor, which no one acknowledged, because acknowledging it would have made it real.

"It's First Tongue," Hark said quietly. "Or its ghost. Or its *grandchild*. Gods' blood, boy, where did she learn it? Nobody speaks it. Nobody has spoken it since—"

"Since when?" I asked.

Hark looked at me. And here is the thing I will remember until whatever passes for my death: she looked at me the way you look at a locked door you suddenly realize you've been leaning against your whole life.

"Since before there were kingdoms," she said. "Give or take."

The fire popped. Wren, wrapped in my blanket, watched the senior clerks of the Vellum Guild lose their composure with the serene detachment of a cat observing furniture being rearranged. Then she set down her bread, wiped her hands on the blanket — my blanket — and stood.

She walked to me. Not to Ellery-Voss, who outranked me by every measure the guild kept; not to Hark, who frightened even the wardens. To me. She reached into the folds of her ruined dress and brought out something she had been holding against her skin, and I understood later that everything — the mud, the walk, the bleeding feet, the knocking on a door that cannot be found — had been undertaken for the sake of what lay in her small palm.

She took my hand. Turned it over. Pressed the object into it and closed my fingers around it, both of her muddy hands wrapped around my fist, and looked up at me with those pre-snow eyes, and spoke one more sentence in the old tongue.

I didn't understand the words. I want that on the record. I did not understand them, and I have interrogated myself about this in the nights since, and the honest answer stands: the meaning arrived some other way, the way heat arrives from a fire you haven't touched.

What I understood was: *This is yours. It was always yours. You forgot.*

Then she let go and stepped back, and the clerks were all staring at us, and I opened my fist.

A copper coin. Old copper, worn nearly smooth, the color of dried blood. Stamped on its face was a sigil: a circle broken by a vertical line, with seven small marks descending along the line like steps going down.

I knew it.

That is the only way I can phrase it, and I have tried phrasing it a dozen ways and they all sound mad, so: I knew it. Not recognized — recognition implies learning, implies a first time. There was no first time. The sigil settled into my mind the way your own name settles there, the way the layout of your own hands settles there, knowledge with no origin and no edges. I knew what the seven marks meant — *depths*, they meant depths, levels going down — and I knew the broken circle meant a door, and I knew, with a certainty that made my mouth go dry, that this coin had been minted to buy something that could not otherwise be bought, and that it had passed through exactly two hands before mine, and that the first of those hands had signed something in—

In.

The thought hit a wall. My own mind, my own memory, hit a wall like a man walking full-tilt into a door he's opened ten thousand times, and behind the wall was a tower, and behind the tower was a fire, and behind the fire was a name —

"Gideon." Ellery-Voss's voice, sharp. "Gideon Winterbourne. What is that coin?"

"I don't know," I said.

It was the first lie I had ever told the Vellum Guild, and it came out smooth as a ledger line, and I closed my hand around the coin before anyone could see it tremble.

Wren watched me lie for her. She didn't smile this time. She nodded once, slowly, gravely, the way one soldier acknowledges another, and outside the storm redoubled against the windows as if something vast had turned over in its sleep, and far above the rainclouds — though I would not learn this until much later — the twelfth moon began, imperceptibly, to die.
