## 3. What the Guild Owes

The Vellum Guild keeps its ledgers in a cellar that smells of tallow and old promises, and I keep mine in my head, which smells of nothing at all.

That was the joke I made to myself as I climbed the stairs toward the counting room, the morning after the child arrived. It was not a good joke. Most of my jokes aren't. When you've spent seven years auditing impossible things — borrowed years, bottled names, second chances sold at a markup — you learn to make your own light, and it's usually the cheap kind.

The girl had been given a cot in the receiving parlor, which was itself an oddity. The guild does not give cots. The guild gives invoices. But Sarelle had looked at the child for a long moment when the wards finally stopped screaming, and then she'd said, "Put her somewhere warm," in a voice that suggested warmth was a line item she'd have to justify to somebody.

So here I was, climbing toward the counting room with a ledger under my arm and a copper coin in my boot, which is where I'd put it at dawn when the night clerk came to change the watch. The coin was warm against my ankle. Coins shouldn't be warm. This one had been warm since the girl pressed it into my hand, through rain and mud and my own clammy palm, and no amount of rational explanation — body heat, hearth proximity, the general thermal incompetence of old buildings — had cooled it.

I recognized the sigil on its face. That was the part I couldn't file anywhere. A mountain with a door in it, and above the door a crown of seven points. I knew it the way you know your own handwriting: instantly, uselessly, without any memory of learning it.

I did not mention this to anyone. I am, whatever else can be said of me, a professional.

* * *

Sarelle Vosk received people in the counting room the way a spider receives weather — patiently, from the center of something sticky. She sat behind a desk of black oak older than the guild's charter, and the desk's surface held only three things: an inkwell, a scale, and a small brass bell that she never rang but liked people to know about.

I took my usual place by the west shelves, where the audit copies lived, and pretended to check folio numbers while two clerks brought the girl in.

She'd been fed, at least. Someone had found her a dress that fit badly and combed most of the mud out of her hair, though a stubborn gray-brown streak remained at one temple, like a mark the storm had signed there personally. She walked between the clerks without hurrying and without being hurried, which struck me as strange. Children dragged into rooms by strangers usually do one of two things: cry or bolt. This one did neither. She looked around the counting room with the mild professional interest of someone appraising the furniture, and then her eyes found me by the shelves, and she tilted her head.

I looked back down at my folio very fast. Folio 114, *One (1) borrowed decade, condition: worn*. Fascinating stuff.

"Sit," Sarelle said.

The girl sat. One of the clerks — Pellam, who had the nerve of a debt collector and the imagination of a receipt — set a slate and chalk before her.

"Now," Sarelle said, folding her hands. "You came to my door in a storm, past wards that would stop a bishop, and you asked for a specific man by name. I'd like to know how."

The girl picked up the chalk. She drew something on the slate — quick strokes, sure ones — and turned it around.

It was the coin. The mountain, the door, the seven-pointed crown. Drawn from memory, or from somewhere better than memory.

Sarelle went very still. I watched her stillness the way you watch ice on a river: noting where it thins.

"Where did you see that mark?"

The girl spoke. It was that language again, the one that had lit up the wards like a festival gone wrong — low, liquid syllables that seemed to arrive slightly before her mouth finished making them. The lamps guttered. Somewhere upstairs, a clerk dropped something.

Pellam shifted his weight. "Guildmistress, should I fetch—"

"No." Sarelle hadn't taken her eyes off the child. "Try common tongue, little one. You knew my auditor's name. You must know some words."

The girl frowned — genuinely frowned, working at it, a child doing sums — and produced, carefully, like coins counted out one at a time:

"Thir... teen." A pause. Her face screwed up with effort. "Moons."

The word landed in the room and nobody moved. Thirteen moons meant nothing and everything; every soul in the guildhouse could count, and every soul in the guildhouse knew what the almanac said about the coming winter, when the twelfth moon would fail and the thirteenth — the dark one, the one that appeared only in the oldest calendars, inked in red by hands long dusted — would rise in its place.

"And?" Sarelle said softly. "Thirteen moons until what?"

The girl thought. Then she pointed down. Not at the floor exactly — through it, down and east and deep, her finger steady as a surveyor's.

"Beneath," she said, and the word came easier than the others, as if it were one she'd said many times, "the moun... tain."

Then, because children are children even when they are impossible, she put down the chalk, folded her hands in imitation of Sarelle's fold, and waited politely for the next question.

* * *

They took her out after an hour. I know because I stayed the whole hour, pretending to audit, and what I actually audited was Sarelle.

She questioned the child the way she questioned everything — sideways, patiently, circling back to the same wells from different angles. *Who sent you? Who taught you words? What is your name?* To that last one the girl answered in her own language, a short bright phrase, and Sarelle wrote it down phonetically and stared at it for a long time, and I saw her thumb move once across the page, the way a person touches a scar.

At some point Pellam raised the obvious question. "Guildmistress, the wards. Nothing crosses those wards without paying toll. What did *she* pay?"

"She didn't cross them," Sarelle said. "They opened."

"That's not possible."

"No," Sarelle agreed mildly. "It isn't. Fetch Brother Anselm anyway. And send word to the Masked Table. Full sitting. They'll want to hear this before the moon turns."

"The moon doesn't turn for—" Pellam began.

"*Before the moon turns,*" Sarelle repeated, and Pellam went, because Pellam had survived eleven years at the guild chiefly by knowing which sentences were doors and which were walls.

When they'd gone, Sarelle sat alone with the slate for a while. Then she said, without turning around, "You're breathing loudly, Winterbourne."

"I'm auditing."

"You're eavesdropping with a folio as a prop. It's one of your better disguises, which is a sad commentary on your others." She turned. Her eyes are pale gray, the color of paper left too long in sun, and they have a way of making you feel like a document she's already read and annotated. "You gave her your name last night. At the door. Why?"

"She knocked on mine."

"Clerks don't answer doors."

"I was closest."

"At midnight. During a ward-storm." She leaned back. "You know what we do with assets, Gideon."

Here is a thing about me: I am a very good liar, and I hate it. The god I swore to — the one whose name I carry on my wrist in ink that hasn't faded in seven years, the one who has not spoken to me in all that time — has opinions about lying. I assume. He's never shared them. But I carry the oath like a stone in the chest, and every lie rattles it.

"She's a child," I said. Which was true, and not an answer, and we both knew it.

"She's a *door* that walks and eats porridge," Sarelle said. "Do you understand what came through my wards last night? Nothing crosses them. Merchants pay. Kings pay double. That child paid nothing, because the wards didn't see her as a visitor. They saw her as—" she stopped herself, jaw tightening, and I realized with a small cold shock that Sarelle Vosk, who has priced griefs I can't imagine, was afraid. "She stays. Locked floor, third landing. Fed, warmed, unharmed. And catalogued. I want a full intake entry by dusk — provenance unknown, acquisition involuntary, classification pending. Can you write that?"

"I can write anything," I said. "That's rather the job."

"Then write it," she said, "and Gideon — the coin she gave you. Intake requires listing all items transferred."

And there it was. The stone in my chest rattled.

I looked at her, and she looked at me, and I understood that Sarelle had seen the exchange — of course she had, she sees everything, she probably has a ledger entry for the exact angle of my shoulders — and that she was giving me one chance to hand it over voluntarily, because some small unpriced thing in her wanted to be able to trust me.

"She didn't give me anything," I said.

The silence lasted four seconds. I counted them the way I count everything; it's a curse of the trade.

"All right," Sarelle said at last, and there was something in her voice I couldn't price at all. "Folio 114 by dusk."

* * *

I hid the coin properly that afternoon, which is to say I did the opposite of what a careful man would do.

A careful man puts contraband in the obvious places precisely because they're overlooked — under the mattress, behind the loose brick, inside the hollow Psalter everyone knows about and therefore checks last. Instead I sewed it into the binding of Ledger Seven, the guild's founding register, the one book in the house that no living hand may open without the Masked Table present and the seals broken. Nobody audits the founding register. It's the one text in the Vellum Guild that outranks the auditors.

There was a logic to it, if you squinted: the safest place for a thing is inside the institution's own idea of itself. But I'll be honest, because the whole point of this account is honesty, however late: I didn't choose Ledger Seven for its security. I chose it because when I stood in the archive holding the coin, asking myself where, some voice below thinking said *with the rest of you*, and my feet carried me to the shelf where our origins sit chained, and my hands did the sewing before my mind caught up.

The sigil on the coin matched a watermark in the founding vellum. Faintly. Almost not at all. A mountain with a door, and a crown of seven points, pressed into the skin of the first page like a bruise under a bandage.

I sat on the archive floor for a long time after that, listening to the building settle, feeling the coin's warmth through leather and thread and seventy years of institutional dignity.

*Seven years.* That's how long I've been Gideon Winterbourne. Before that — nothing. No village, no mother, no boyhood scars with stories attached. I woke on the steps of the Ashen shrine with an oath already inked on my wrist and a mercenary's calluses already on my hands, and when I asked the god who I'd been, he said nothing, and has said nothing every night since, and I built a life in the silence the way you build a house on a cliff: carefully, and without looking down.

Everyone has a hole where their history should be. Mine just happens to be load-bearing.

* * *

Dusk came, and I wrote the intake entry.

*Provenance unknown. Acquisition involuntary. Classification pending.* All true. Ledgers run on true things arranged dishonestly; that's the whole craft. I described the child in the flat grammar of inventory — female, approx. eight years, dark hair, speaks an unidentified language, displays no fear response — and I did not describe the way she'd looked at me across the counting room, head tilted, like she was checking whether I was still the person she'd walked through a storm to find.

I did not list the coin.

At the changing of the watch I carried supper up to the third landing myself, which raised an eyebrow from the duty clerk and no objection, because I'm the man who counts things and counting things apparently makes you trustworthy. The locked floor is five cells and a corridor, used for holdings that are alive, which at the guild means debts that breathe. Wren — she hadn't told me her name yet; that came later, on worse nights — Wren was sitting cross-legged on the cot, watching the barred window where the twelfth moon was climbing, thin and white as a fingernail paring.

She looked at the tray. She looked at me. Then she said something in her language, soft, and patted the floor beside the cot.

I should have reported the conversation. I should have noted that the asset attempted communication, subject matter unknown. Instead I sat down on cold flagstones next to a child I'd known one day, and ate half the bread so she'd believe it wasn't drugged, which it wasn't, but belief matters more than fact in this building.

She talked for a while. I understood none of it, and all of it — the way you understand music, or rain, or the tone of a friend saying your name across a crowded room. Once she touched my wrist, where the oath-ink sits, and made a sound low in her throat that might have been pity, and might have been recognition, and I flinched hard enough that she withdrew her hand and apologized — *sorry*, she said, in careful common tongue, one of perhaps twenty words she owned.

"Don't be," I said. "It's not your fault it's there."

She studied me. Then she pointed at the window, at the failing moon, and held up fingers. Ten. Eleven. Twelve. And then, slowly, she folded her thumb down and showed me the fist.

Thirteen.

"Yeah," I said. "I got that part."

She shook her head, frustrated, and tried again, and this time she pointed at me first — *you* — then at the closed fist, and her eyes were enormous and certain and eight years old, and the stone in my chest went off like a bell.

I stood up too fast. Made some excuse about ledgers. Got as far as the cell door before she said, clearly, in accented but unmistakable common:

"Gideon. *Beneath the mountain.*"

Not a question. A summons.

I stood in the doorway with my hand on the lock and understood, with the particular clarity available only to men who count other people's debts for a living, that the guild had just acquired an asset it intended to collect on — and that somewhere in the columns and folios of this house, in some entry I had maybe filed myself, there was a record of what we owed, and to whom, and that the sum had my name in it somewhere, spelled correctly, in handwriting I almost recognized.

Behind me, the twelfth moon cleared the rooftops, and I swear it flickered. Like a lamp running out of oil.

Like something beginning to count down.
