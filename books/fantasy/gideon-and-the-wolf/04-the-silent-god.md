## 4. The Silent God

The Vellum Guild keeps a chapel the way a miser keeps a coin he never intends to spend: locked, dusted, and shown to no one. It sits at the top of the guildhouse, above the vaults and the counting rooms and the long gallery where the impossible things are shelved — jars of borrowed years glowing faintly amber, lead boxes that hum when you hold them because of the names inside, a single second chance in a glass case that no one has ever worked up the courage to open. The chapel is smaller than any of those rooms. It has one window, one altar, and no god anyone can see.

That suits me fine. I've never been much for looking at gods. I prefer to be looked at by them, which is what an oath is, when you come down to it: standing still in the dark and letting something enormous decide whether you're worth the trouble.

I climbed to the chapel at the eleventh hour, as I do every night, past the sleeping clerks and the wards that taste your blood through your boots and let you pass if you're on the ledger. The girl was three floors down, locked in the strongroom with two guards who kept finding reasons not to look at her. I'd checked on my way up. She'd been sitting cross-legged on the floor, drawing shapes in the dust with one finger — the same shapes over and over, patient as water wearing down stone. When she saw me at the grate she'd held up her copper coin, the twin of the one hidden in my boot, and smiled.

I did not smile back. Smiling felt like agreeing to something.

* * *

The chapel smelled of tallow and old vellum, which is to say it smelled like every other room in the guildhouse, except here the smell had been allowed to mean something. I knelt on the worn stone before the altar, where the only ornament was a blank sheet of parchment under glass. Unwritten. That was our god's whole iconography: nothing. A page that had never been written on, waiting for whatever the god might someday choose to say.

Seven years I'd sworn to that silence. Seven years of kneeling here after the ledgers were balanced, saying the words of the Vigil of the Open Page, asking for guidance I had stopped expecting around year two but never stopped requesting, because an oath isn't a transaction, whatever the guild thinks. An oath is a door you leave unlocked even when nobody comes.

"Vigilant One," I said, "who reads all and writes sparingly. I have kept the accounts. I have taken no entry that was not owed me. I have lied today."

That last part was new. I'd never had cause to say it before. It sat strangely in my mouth, half confession and half boast.

"I lied to Guildmistress Sarelle about a coin. I told her the child arrived with nothing. She carries another, stamped with a sigil I have no right to know, and I know it anyway, the way you know a word on the tip of your tongue — except there's no tongue in me that ever learned it. Unless there was. Unless someone took it out."

The silence received this the way silence receives everything: completely, and without comment.

I should explain about the silence, since you'll be hearing a great deal of it. Most sworn servants of the Vigilant One go their whole careers without a word, and call themselves blessed for it, because the doctrine holds that a silent god is a trusting god — a god who has read your ledger so thoroughly that commentary would be redundant. The loud gods, the ones who thunder and send doves and knock people off horses, are the ones who need to check up on you. Ours trusts us the way a lender trusts collateral: absolutely, until the moment it doesn't.

For seven years I had found this comforting. Tonight, kneeling on cold stone with a liar's tongue and a stolen coin against my ankle, I found it something else. I found it watchful.

You'll laugh at that. Silence is silence; how can it watch? But anyone who's stood alone in a house at night knows the difference between an empty room and a room where something has decided not to move yet. The dark above the altar had that quality. The unwritten page seemed less like emptiness than like restraint. As if somewhere behind the nothing, something enormous had turned its attention toward me — had perhaps been holding that attention for years, patient as the girl drawing her shapes in the dust — and tonight, for reasons of its own, had leaned closer.

I finished the vigil. My voice didn't shake, which I counted as a professional accomplishment. Then I stayed kneeling longer than the rite required, the way you linger in a doorway when you suspect the conversation isn't actually over.

"Whoever you are," I said finally, "you've been quiet for seven years. If you're quiet for seven more, I'll assume we're through. But if you're listening now — if you've been listening all along — then hear this: I don't know what I am. And I'm starting to think that's not an accident. Somebody wrote me. Somewhere there's a ledger with my name in it, and the hand that made the entry knew things about me that I don't." I pressed my palm flat against the cold stone. "If that hand is yours, then write something. Anything. A word. A correction. Even an error would be a kindness at this point. Errors prove somebody's home."

Nothing. Of course nothing. The thousandth silence, give or take — I'd stopped keeping exact count around year four, the way you stop counting a debt once it's clear you'll die inside it.

I rose, blew out the candle, and went down to my cell to sleep, telling myself the watching feeling was exhaustion and a guilty conscience arguing over who got to ruin my night. I almost believed it. That's the trick of a good lie: you don't have to believe it forever. You just have to believe it long enough to fall asleep.

* * *

I dreamed of a mountain.

Not the mountains east of the city, whose names I knew from maps in the guild archive — grey, ordinary peaks that shepherds complained about. This mountain stood alone on a plain of black glass, and it was wrong the way the guild's wares are wrong: too tall, its summit lost in a sky that had no business being there, its slopes carved into terraces that ascended in perfect spirals, tier upon tier, like the rings of a ledger seen edge-on. Every terrace glittered. At first I thought it was snow. Then I understood it was light — the light of thirteen moons, all of them full at once, hanging in ranks around the peak like coins laid out for counting.

And the mountain was hungry. I don't know how I knew that. You look at certain things and knowledge arrives without paperwork, whole and undeniable, the way the sigil on the coin had arrived in my hand. The mountain was hungry, and it was patient, and it had been waiting a very long time for a delivery.

I stood at its base, in the dream, on ground so smooth I could see my own reflection lying under my feet like a corpse in glass. Except the reflection wasn't quite mine. It wore my face, but it wore it uncertainly, the way a clerk wears a borrowed coat — and around its neck hung a chain, and on the chain hung a tag, and on the tag was written a name in letters that kept rearranging themselves whenever I tried to read them.

The voice came from everywhere, which is where voices live in dreams. It was neither male nor female, young nor old. It sounded, if I'm honest, like a ledger being read aloud by someone who loved the ledger.

"Gideon Winterbourne," it said.

"No," I heard myself answer, and my dream-voice was steady in a way my waking voice never manages. "Try again."

A pause. The thirteen moons revolved slowly, ticking past the summit like coins dropping into a tray.

"Aurelian," said the voice.

The name went through me like a blade going through water — no resistance, no sound, just sudden cold spreading outward from a point I couldn't find. *Aurelian.* I had never heard the word. I had always known the word. Both of these things were true at once, and the space between them was where I lived now, a man split along a seam he hadn't known was there.

"That's not—" I began.

"You were entered," the voice said, gentle as an auditor closing a book, "on the ninth day of the dying moon. Collateral against a debt of the blood royal. Term of holding: until called. Condition of return: unspent." A pause, delicate as a knife being set down. "The account is nearly due, Aurelian. Thirteen moons. Beneath the mountain. You are the payment walking toward us on your own legs, and you have been walking for seven years, and you never once asked where the road went."

"I asked," I said, and my voice cracked like river ice. "I asked every night. For seven years I knelt and asked, and got nothing—"

"And what did you swear to?" the voice asked, and now it was amused, and the amusement was the cruelest thing yet. "What did you give your oath to, little entry? A god? Or a page left deliberately blank, so that no one would ever check it against the original?"

The moons ticked. The terraces glittered. Somewhere far above, at the summit I couldn't see, something opened — I felt it rather than heard it, a vast patience unsheathing itself — and the reflection beneath my feet lifted its head and looked at me with my own eyes and mouthed, silently, in a language older than any kingdom, a single word.

I woke.

* * *

I woke the way soldiers wake: all at once, hand already moving to the knife under the pillow, heart hammering out a rhythm I recognized from ambushes in another life — or what I'd assumed was another life, and was now no longer sure of anything about. Grey pre-dawn light seeped under the door. Rain again. It is always raining in this city; I used to find it atmospheric. Now it sounded like pages turning.

*Aurelian.*

I lay still and tested the name the way you test a bruise, pressing on it to learn how deep it goes. It hurt. That was the remarkable thing — it hurt in a way that *Gideon Winterbourne* never had. Gideon was a coat that fit well enough, a name I'd worn for seven years without chafing, because I'd had nothing to compare it to. But *Aurelian* fit like skin. Like something grown into, not put on. And if a false name fits like cloth and a true one fits like skin, then the question that follows is ugly and unavoidable:

Who dressed me?

I got up, splashed water on my face from the basin, and caught my own eye in the small mirror above it. A mercenary's face. Scar through the left eyebrow, jaw like a closed fist, eyes that had learned early to give nothing away. Seven years of memories attached to it: the oath, the guild, the ledgers, the long apprenticeship in impossible arithmetic. All of it beginning, conveniently, seven years ago, on a road outside a town whose name I'd never thought to question, with a headache and empty pockets and a god's silence already settled over me like weather.

Seven years. The girl was eight. Do the arithmetic. I did, standing there with wet hands, and the sum came out wrong in a way that made my stomach drop.

She remembered being someone else. What if I did too — what if the problem was simply that whoever had done the remembering-to had been thorough?

Down the corridor, the guildhouse was waking. I could hear the clerks' footsteps, the clink of the counting rooms, Sarelle's voice giving some instruction in a tone that brooked no footnotes. In a few hours I would descend among them and balance books and pretend that last night's vigil had been ordinary, that the coin in my boot was a trinket, that the child in the strongroom was inventory.

But first — before duty, before the lie I'd have to live inside — I went back up to the chapel. I don't fully know why. Habit, maybe. Or the stubborn conviction that a door left unlocked for seven years deserves one more night of standing open.

The candle caught. The blank page waited under its glass, unwritten as ever.

"All right," I said quietly. "Aurelian. If that's real — if any of this is real — then someone owes me an explanation, and I intend to collect. I've spent seven years keeping other people's accounts. Time somebody audited mine."

The silence held. But as I turned to go, I could have sworn — and I am a man who deals in what can be proven, so mark how strange it is that I say this — the air above the altar moved. Not wind. Not draft. Something like a breath drawn in and held, the way a scribe holds his breath before making a mark he cannot undo.

Or the way a debtor holds his breath when the creditor finally comes down the corridor.

I blew out the candle and went down to work, and behind me the unwritten page gleamed in the dark like an open mouth, and I found myself thinking of the girl in the strongroom, drawing her patient shapes in the dust, and wondering — for the first time with real fear, and real hope, tangled together past any hope of separating them —

what else, in seven years of silence, had I been taught not to hear?
