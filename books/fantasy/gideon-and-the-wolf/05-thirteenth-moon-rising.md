## 5. Thirteenth Moon Rising

The room they put me in is small and has one window and the window is painted over so no moon can look in at me. I think this is funny, a little. They are afraid of the moon looking at me. But it is not the moon that should be afraid.

My name is not Wren. That is the name the tall man with the tired eyes gave me when he asked and I could not say my true name, because my true name is not in my mouth anymore. It is somewhere else. It is under a mountain, sitting on a shelf, being kept like jam. So I let him call me Wren, because a wren is small and quick and nobody eats a wren, or if they do, it is a lot of trouble for very little meat. This is a good name for now. I am practicing being small.

I will tell you what I remember. I will tell it crooked, because my common tongue is crooked, like a fence after wind. But a crooked fence still keeps the sheep in. Mostly.

* * *

Before, there was a palace. I know this word — *palace* — because the tall man said it once, quiet, when he thought I was sleeping, and the word fit inside me like a key into a lock that had been rusted shut. Yes. Palace. White stone with gold in the cracks of it, and stairs that went up and up, and my feet were always bare on those stairs because shoes were for outside and I was never allowed outside. Remember this part: I was never allowed outside. Hold it. It matters later.

I was someone else in the palace. Not a girl. I know how this sounds. The clerks here, the gray men with their gray hands, they looked at me like I was a cat that had started reciting poetry. But I was someone else. I was tall, in the way of remembering. I had a sword that was more for showing than for using, and I had a mother who smelled of cedar oil, and I had a name that people said twice when they greeted me, once for the man and once for the crown he would wear.

A prince. I was a prince. There. I have said it. Now the room can stop pretending it is only a room.

* * *

Here is the thing about being sold. Everyone thinks the selling is the worst part. It is not. The worst part is the counting before.

I remember the counting. Grown-ups in a room with no windows — grown-ups love rooms with no windows, have you noticed? The tall man's guild is full of them — and they counted things while I sat on a stool too big for me. They counted the harvest. They counted the soldiers. They counted the years of my father's father's father, all the way back, like coins poured out on a table. And then my uncle — I think he was my uncle; he had our family's nose, which is a nose like a hawk that lost an argument with a door — my uncle said words I did not understand then and understand now, every one of them, in the dark, at night, when the painted window lets nothing in.

He said: *the mountain takes payment in names.*

And my father said: *there must be another way.*

And my uncle said: *there is always another way. It costs more.*

That is the truest thing any grown-up ever said in front of me. Write it down somewhere. There is always another way. It costs more.

They argued for three days. I know it was three days because I hid in the curtain seat and counted the meals brought in, and there were nine meals, and princes' households eat three times a day even during the end of the world. Especially during the end of the world. On the third day my father stopped arguing, and the silence he made was bigger than all his shouting, and I knew, the way children know things, the way animals know a storm is coming before the sky does — I knew that whatever they decided, it would be decided about me and not with me.

Children are never at the table. We are only ever on it. Or in the ledger. I did not know the word *ledger* then. I know it now. The gray men say it constantly, like a prayer, like a hiccup.

* * *

The night they took my name, there was a thirteenth moon. I should explain the moons, because the tall man will ask, eventually, and I want to have the answer ready, arranged nicely, like flowers in a hand.

There are twelve moons that everyone can see. Everyone knows this. Twelve moons, each with its own month, each with its own little personality — the Harvest Moon fat and orange, the Frost Moon thin as a fingernail, and so on. What nobody says out loud, what everyone knows and nobody says, is that sometimes there is a thirteenth. A moon between moons. It comes when it likes. Old grandmothers bar their doors. Kings postpone coronations. Lovers do not marry under it, except the ones who are sorry later.

The thirteenth moon is the mountain's moon. It is the light the mountain uses to read by.

On that night, they dressed me in white — white is what you dress a soul in when you mean to take it out and use it, same as you dress a lamb — and they walked me down into the deep parts of the palace, down past the wine cellars and the old tombs, down where the stones stopped being cut by men and started being cut by something with longer fingers. There was a door. The door was not locked. Locked doors are for keeping things out. This door was for letting something in, and it opened when my uncle spoke a word I felt in my teeth rather than heard.

Inside, there was a table. On the table, there was a book. I will not describe the book. Some things you keep whole in yourself, like a bone set right, and if you describe them they break again.

A voice came from everywhere, the way cold comes from everywhere. It said my name. My true name, the double-said one, the prince-name. And when it said it, I felt it lift out of me. You cannot imagine this, so I will tell it plainly: it did not hurt. That is the horror of it. It was like setting down a heavy pack after a long road. It was kind. The cruelest things are always kind while they do the lifting.

And then I was small. Small in a way I had not been since I was born, because a name is most of what makes a person big. The rest of me — the girl-body, the dark eyes, the stubbornness (that stayed; stubbornness lives deeper than names) — was folded up small and carried out of the mountain by a man whose face I do not remember, because faces are also a kind of name, and the mountain keeps what it keeps.

I was sold once to the mountain. I know this. What I did not know, until the gray men talked over my head these last days, thinking a child's ears are decoration — what I did not know is that I was sold *again*. Sold onward. Passed along like a coin that nobody wants to spend but everybody wants to hold. The Vellum Guild holds me now. Holds the paper of me. And the paper says they may collect.

Collect. Such a clean word for such a dirty thing.

* * *

Here is what I have learned in three days of pretending to be simple. Pretending to be simple is easy when your common tongue really is broken; you just break it a little more on purpose, and the grown-ups relax, and talk.

The gray men are afraid of me. Not of me-me — of what sits under me, the way a cellar sits under a house. They keep saying *asset* when they pass my door. Asset asset asset. In the old tongue — my first tongue, the one my mouth still knows even though my name does not — we had a word for a thing that is owned but not used yet. The word translates roughly as *meat-that-remembers*. I have not taught anyone that word. Some gifts you keep.

But here is what I need the tall man to understand, and I do not know how to make him understand, because his language has no room in it for the shape of this:

The thirteenth moon is coming back. It comes around like everything comes around. Thirteen moons from the night of my selling — and I have been counting, I am good at counting now, counting is what they left me, counting and stubbornness — thirteen moons from that night, the debt comes due. Not mine. *His.* My father's. The bargain was his signature, his blood, his kingdom's weight behind it. But a dead man's debts fall to his house, and I am the last brick of that house still standing, and the mountain is patient the way stone is patient.

If the debt is not paid before the thirteenth moon turns full, the mountain does not send a bill. The mountain sends collectors. And the collectors do not take coin. Coin is for the living. Collectors take what the coin stands for.

Beneath the mountain, they are already setting the table. I can feel it sometimes, at night, through the floor, through the soles of my bare feet: a faint music, like a feast being prepared very far away, very slowly, for a guest who has not arrived. Me. The feast is for me. Or for the shape of me, the princely shape, the name-shape, which they intend to pour back into its old mold and march around wearing a crown, doing the mountain's will in the world above. A puppet prince. A king of meat-that-remembers.

Unless somebody pays. Unless somebody bargains. And the bargaining — this is the part that makes the gray men go pale and the painted window seem wise — the bargaining must be done in person, beneath the mountain, before the moon turns. No letters. No proxies. The mountain does not trust paper. Which is funny, because paper trusts the mountain plenty. Paper is how I got passed along twice.

* * *

Today the tall man came to my door. He did not come in. He stood there a while, and I pretended to be asleep, which is a skill, and through my eyelashes I watched him watch the painted window, and his face did a thing I recognized from the palace, from my father's face on the third day of arguing. The face of a man who has just understood that the price will be paid by someone, and is beginning to suspect who.

I like him. I will write that plainly too, since I have gotten brave tonight. He has tired eyes but they are honest-tired, not guilty-tired. There is a difference. Guilty-tired eyes look away from children. Honest-tired ones look straight at you, like they are asking permission for something they have not done yet.

When he goes, I press my palm flat against the floor, and I listen with my feet, the way I listened in the palace. Far below — far below even the cellars of this guildhouse, far below the ledgers and the gray men and the wards that flare when I speak my first tongue — far below, the music goes on. Setting the table. Counting the spoons.

Thirteen moons. I count backward now instead of forward. It changes the flavor of the counting. Backward-counting tastes like running.

Tomorrow, maybe, I will tell the tall man some of this. The words for it in common are clumsy — *beneath*, *mountain*, *thirteen* — three stones instead of a bridge. But he seems like a man who can build with three stones. He seems like a man who has built himself out of fewer.

And tonight, one more thing, the smallest thing, the thing I will say to the painted window like a secret, because the paint is thick but paint is not stone:

I remember one other thing from the mountain. When the voice lifted my name out of me, for half a heartbeat — half a heartbeat, no more — something else slipped in. Something the mountain did not mean to give me, the way a door swung wide lets in a draft it did not invite. A word. Not my name. Another word entirely, in my old tongue, warm as bread, and it settled down deep where my name used to live, and it has kept me company every night since.

It means *soon*.

I do not know if it is a promise or a warning. With mountains, it is usually both.

But tomorrow the tall man will come to my door again, because he is the kind of man who comes to doors, and I have decided that when he does, I will stand up, and I will look at him with my honest eyes, and I will give him the three stones — *beneath*, *mountain*, *thirteen* — and see what he builds.

Because the moon is rising somewhere past the paint. The thirteenth one. And it is reading.
