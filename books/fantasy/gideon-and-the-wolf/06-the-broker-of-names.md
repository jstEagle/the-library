## 6. The Broker of Names

There is a particular kind of man who, having been expelled from the Vellum Guild, does not drown his sorrows but files them. Quill was that kind of man. I knew this because I had processed his expulsion myself, seven years ago, entering it into the ledger in my own careful hand: *Asset Quill, broker third class, struck from rolls for unauthorized empathy.* The charge had always struck me as odd. Empathy was not listed anywhere in the guild statutes as a violation. But Sarelle had signed the order, and Sarelle's signature was the closest thing the guild had to a law of nature.

Unauthorized empathy. I had laughed at it once, dryly, alone, the way you laugh at a joke told by a corpse.

Now I walked through the Drowned Quarter with a copper coin in my pocket that made my teeth ache when I touched it, looking for a man I had personally erased from existence — or as close to erased as paper can manage.

The rain had stopped sometime before dawn, which in Grayharbor counts as a miracle and is treated with corresponding suspicion. The Drowned Quarter did not so much flood as *surrender*, streets giving way to brown water that smelled of fish and old decisions. Houses leaned against each other like drunkards keeping each other upright. Laundry hung between them like flags of small defeated nations.

Quill lived, according to the guild's own records — records I had written — above a chandler's shop on Candlewick Stair, in rooms that had been condemned twice and would be condemned again, because Quill paid his rent in bottled years and the landlord was eighty-one going on thirty.

I climbed the stair. It complained the whole way, as stairs do when they have been asked to bear more than wood should bear.

The door was unlocked. Of course it was. A man who deals in impossible things learns that locks are a formality, a courtesy extended to burglars so they don't feel unwelcome.

"Go away," said a voice from within. "Unless you're the landlord, in which case come in, I've bottled something for you."

"It's not the landlord."

Silence. Then: "Winterbourne." The name came out flat, the way you'd say the name of weather. "The guild sends its auditor to my door after seven years. Either I'm being audited posthumously or someone upstairs has finally noticed I still exist."

"No one sent me." I stepped inside.

The room was exactly what I expected and nothing like it. I expected squalor; I got *order*. Every surface held something, but every something had its place. Bottles in ranks on shelves built into the walls, each labeled in a hand so fine it looked printed. Jars of what I chose not to identify. And everywhere, everywhere, paper — but organized paper, filed paper, paper that had been forgiven and given a home.

Quill himself sat at a desk beneath the one clean window, a small man gone soft at the edges, with ink-stained fingers and eyes that had done too much reading by candlelight. He wore a dressing gown that might once have been brocade and now was mostly memory.

He looked at me the way an accountant looks at an error he suspects is his own.

"You haven't changed," he said. "That's the first strange thing. Men change, Winterbourne. They thicken, they gray, they grow bitter in new and interesting shapes. You look exactly as you did the day you struck my name from the rolls. Exactly." He tilted his head. "How long has it been?"

"Seven years."

"Mm," he said, in the tone of a man filing something away. "And what brings the guild's most faithful hound off its leash? Because I'll tell you now, I have nothing worth taking. I checked."

"I'm not here for the guild."

That interested him. He set down his pen — a quill, naturally; the man had committed to the bit decades ago — and folded his hands. "Then for whom?"

"For myself." Saying it aloud felt like stepping off a dock. "Maybe. I don't know yet. I need someone who knows about things the guild doesn't trade anymore."

"The guild trades everything."

"The guild trades everything *it understands*," I said. "I have something it doesn't understand. Something the wards reacted to. Something that made three senior clerks go pale and one of them start praying to a god none of us can hear."

Something moved behind Quill's eyes. Not fear. Recognition's nervous cousin.

"Show me," he said.

I took out the coin.

* * *

Here is what I had learned about the coin in the two days since Wren pressed it into my hand: it was warm. Not sun-warm, not pocket-warm. Warm like a held hand. It never cooled. I had left it on my desk overnight and found it in the morning with a faint ring of condensation around it, as though the desk had been sweating.

And when I slept near it, I dreamed of mountains.

I set it on Quill's desk, and the room changed. I cannot describe it better than that. The candle flames all leaned toward it, very slightly, like courtiers bowing. The bottles on the shelves hummed — actually hummed, a sound below hearing, felt in the fillings of teeth and the wet of the eyes.

Quill did not touch it. He leaned over it instead, close enough that I saw his face go through several expressions in sequence: professional curiosity, professional curiosity dying, and then something older and worse.

"Where," he said quietly, "did you get this?"

"A child gave it to me. Eight years old. Mud to the knees. She spoke a language that made the wards flare."

"Describe the language."

"I can't describe it. That's rather the point of languages."

"Try."

I closed my eyes. "It sounded like... water deciding to be words. Like something that was old before it learned to make sounds, and never quite got comfortable with them." I opened my eyes. "One of the clerks translated a fragment. *Thirteen moons. Beneath the mountain.*"

Quill sat back. His hands had begun, very slightly, to shake.

"That's not a language," he said. "That's *the* language. Court-tongue. Under-mountain speech. There are perhaps four people alive outside the mountain who can parse it, and I am not one of them, and neither are your pale clerks, whatever fragments they managed." He wiped his mouth with the back of his hand. "Now the coin. Look at it properly. Not the sigil — everyone looks at the sigil first, and the sigil is a distraction, a coat of arms, decoration. Look at the *rim*."

I looked. Around the edge of the copper ran a line of tiny characters, worn nearly smooth, the same script I had seen flare across the guildhouse wards.

"Minted currency," Quill said. "Not guild scrip, not kingdom coin. Court currency. From beneath the mountain." He finally touched it, one fingertip, and snatched the finger back as if scalded — though I noticed he did not snatch it far. "Do you know what the under-court mints coins *for*, Winterbourne? Kingdoms mint coins for bread and boots and bribes. The under-court mints for transactions of weight. This denomination—" he turned it over with the tip of his pen, not touching it again, "—this denomination is minted for one category of purchase only."

"Don't," I said.

"Souls," said Quill.

The word sat in the room. The candles bowed lower.

"That's a child's coin," I heard myself say. My voice came out wrong, flattened, like a voice heard through a wall. "She's eight. She pressed it into my hand like — like payment. Like she was *paying* me."

"Children learn early under the mountain what things cost." Quill had gone white. Not pale — white, the white of paper, of vellum, of the guild's finest stock. "Or she learned somewhere else. Or someone taught her. Either way, Winterbourne, ask yourself the question you're avoiding, because I can see you circling it like a dog around a wound."

"What question?"

"Why did she give it to *you*?"

I had no answer. I had been carefully not having an answer for two days. The girl had looked at me — I remembered it now with terrible clarity — she had looked at me the way you look at a thing you recognize. Not a stranger. Not a rescuer. A *line item*. Something entered in a ledger she could read and I could not.

"She knew me," I said slowly. "Or she knew *of* me. She walked through a storm to a guildhouse full of brokers and warders and enforcers, and she walked past all of them to put a soul-coin in the hand of the man who keeps the ledgers."

"Yes," said Quill softly. "And there is only one reason a child carrying under-court currency seeks out a ledger-keeper. She wanted her transaction *recorded*. Witnessed. Made real in the only way her world makes things real." He met my eyes. "Someone sold something, Gideon Winterbourne. And I suspect — I *suspect*, mind, I'm a disgraced broker, suspicion is all I have left — I suspect the something sold was her."

* * *

We sat for a while after that. Quill poured something amber from a bottle without a label and we drank it, and it tasted the way old promises taste, and neither of us remarked on the fact that a man who traded in bottled years was drinking something that was almost certainly somebody's summer.

"The guild doesn't deal in souls," I said at last. "It's the one prohibition. First statute. Written before the statutes. *No flesh, no souls, no names freely given.*"

"Freely given," Quill repeated. He smiled, and it was a terrible smile, the smile of a man watching an old wound reopen on schedule. "You've read the statutes your whole life, haven't you? Recited them. Audited against them. Tell me, faithful hound — did you ever wonder why the first statute needs a qualifier? Why *freely*? What other kind of giving is there, that the founders needed to exclude it?"

I had wondered. Once. Early. Before wondering became a habit I'd trained out of myself, the way you train a dog out of barking.

"Sarelle went white," I said. "When the girl spoke. Sarelle, who I have watched appraise a dead man's last breath without blinking. She ordered the child locked in the east vault. Called her an *asset*." I turned the cup in my hands. "Assets get collected. Assets get *sold*."

"Now you're thinking like a broker," Quill said. "Congratulations. Condolences. They arrive together."

"If the guild holds a claim on that child—"

"Then the guild will collect it. The guild always collects. It's not cruelty, Winterbourne, that's the horror of it. It's *bookkeeping*. Somewhere in your ledgers — maybe in a folio you yourself have balanced — there is an entry that says a certain asset arrived on such a night, in such a storm, and was secured pending verification of provenance." He drained his cup. "You wrote the entry yourself, didn't you?"

I had. In my own careful hand. *One (1) child, mute, provenance unknown, secured east vault, value pending.*

Value pending. I had written those two words and slept soundly after, and prayed to a silent god, and felt virtuous in the silence.

"I need to see the original contracts," I said. "Whatever the guild holds on her. Provenance, chain of title, all of it."

"Those are sealed. Deep-seal. Regent-tier."

"I keep the ledgers, Quill. I know where the seals sleep."

He studied me for a long moment. Outside, the Drowned Quarter went about its morning — gulls arguing, water lapping, some neighbor hammering at something that would need re-hammering by evening. Ordinary sounds. Sounds from a world where coins bought bread.

"You understand what you're asking," he said. "You're asking me — a struck broker, a man whose name exists nowhere but in your own expulsion entry — to help an active officer of the guild steal from the guild's deepest archive. If we're caught, they won't strike us from the rolls. They'll enter us. There's a difference, and the difference is that struck men can still be found."

"I know."

"Do you?" He leaned forward. "Because here is what I know, Gideon Winterbourne, and it has kept me alive and exiled and mostly sober for seven years: the guild does not lose. It *absorbs*. Every enemy becomes an entry. Every rebellion becomes a fee. I once watched them foreclose on a war. A whole war, both sides, collateralized." He tapped the table. "And you want to step off the page."

"Yes."

"Why?"

It was a fair question and I hated it. I turned the answer over, looking for the honest face of it, and found it wasn't complicated at all. It was a mud-caked girl standing in a doorway with rain running off her chin, unafraid, pressing a warm coin into my hand as if I were the one owed something.

"Because she paid me," I said. "And no one has ever paid me for anything. I'm the one who collects. And because when the wards flared and the clerks went pale, I wasn't afraid *for myself*. Seven years I've served a god who says nothing, and I've told myself the silence is faith, and last night I stood vigil and the silence felt like it was *watching* me, and I realized—" I stopped. Started again, quieter. "I realized I don't know what I'm faithful *to*. Not the god. Maybe not even the guild. Maybe not even—" I gestured at myself, at the room, at the whole gray city. "*This*. Whatever this is. Whatever I am."

Quill was quiet for a moment. When he spoke, his voice had lost its broker's polish.

"You know what my crime was? The real one, under the funny word they used?" He didn't wait. "A farmer sold his daughter's laughter. Perfectly legal. Beautifully documented. Three signatures, a witness, fair market price. And I sat across from him while he signed, and I saw his hands shaking, and I said — out loud, in front of witnesses — *sir, do you understand what you're selling?* One question. One moment of unauthorized empathy." He laughed, short and bitter. "They struck me for it. Not for breaking a rule. For *pausing*."

"And now you're pausing again."

"I'm considering it," he corrected. "Pausing implies I'll resume." He looked at the coin on his desk, still warm, still humming its note below hearing. "If I do this — if I help you open the deep-seal folios — I want to see the original contracts too. All of them. Hers and anyone else's. Because I have spent seven years telling myself the guild is merely a machine, and machines can be survived alongside. If I'm going to die for it, I want to know whether it's a machine or something worse."

"Something worse than a machine that sells children's laughter?"

"Oh yes," Quill said. "Machines don't leave themselves clues. Machines don't let a disgraced broker live in comfort for seven years within walking distance of their archive. Machines don't send their most faithful auditor to an exile's door the very week a child arrives speaking the court-tongue." He picked up the coin at last, holding it in his palm, and his face did something complicated. "This isn't a trail, Winterbourne. This is an *invitation*. Someone wants you off the page. The only question — the one that should keep you awake tonight — is whether they want you off the page to be *free*..."

He turned the coin over. On its face, in the worn court-script, the sigil caught the candlelight: a tower, burning.

"...or to be *collected*."

The candles guttered, all together, though there was no wind. And in the silence that followed, Quill looked up at me with the expression of a man reading a ledger entry he wished he hadn't opened, and said:

"Tell me again, exactly, what the child said when she gave you this. Word for word. Every syllable you remember."

I told him. And when I reached the part I had not understood — the part I had assumed was a child's nonsense, a babble, noise — Quill's face went the color of his own best vellum, and he whispered a single word in a language I had never been taught and somehow, horribly, almost recognized.

Outside, the twelfth moon rose thin and wrong-colored over the Drowned Quarter, and somewhere beneath the mountain, I understood, a clock I had never seen had already begun to run.
