## 7. Sold Twice

There are three kinds of visitors who come to a disgraced broker's door in the dead hours of the morning. The first kind are debt collectors, and they knock. The second kind are former colleagues come to gloat, and they don't bother knocking at all — they simply appear, because gloating is a discipline and true professionals arrive unannounced.

The third kind is Gideon Winterbourne, standing in my doorway with rain running off his jaw and a look on his face like a man who has found an error in his own arithmetic and cannot decide whether to correct it or burn the book.

I had known him for four years, back when I was still Quill of the Vellum Guild, Keeper of the Second Ledger, and he was still the guild's quiet mercenary with the quiet god and the quiet way of watching everything. We were never friends. Friendship requires trust, and trust requires honesty, and neither of us could afford either. But we had shared a hundred suppers in the guildhouse refectory, passing the salt in comfortable silence, two men who understood that silence was sometimes the only honest currency left.

Then they took my ledger keys, my seal, and my name from the rolls, and I came here, to this crooked house above the tannery where the air smells permanently of regret and cured hides, and I did not expect to see him again.

"Quill," he said.

"Winterbourne." I stepped aside, because it was raining and because some habits outlive exile. "You're wet, armed, and carrying a child's copper coin in your left pocket. Come in before you drip on something I care about."

He blinked. "How did you—"

"You're holding your hand away from your hip like it's embarrassed. Old broker's trick. We learn to read pockets before we learn to read contracts." I shut the door behind him. "Tea?"

"I need answers."

"Everyone who comes through that door needs answers. Tea is what separates the ones worth answering from the ones worth throwing out." I put the kettle on the little iron stove, mostly so my hands would have something honest to do while my stomach finished tying itself into knots. Because here was the truth I wasn't saying: Gideon Winterbourne coming to my door meant trouble with a capital letter, and trouble with a capital letter meant the guild, and anything involving the guild these days made me want to check the exits.

* * *

He laid the coin on my table between us.

It was a small thing, copper gone green at the edges, stamped with a sigil I recognized the way you recognize a scar in a mirror — instantly, painfully, and with the full knowledge of how you got it. A mountain, split by a door. A crown floating above the split, not resting on any head.

"Where," I said, very carefully, "did you get this."

"A child brought it to the guildhouse last night. Eight years old, maybe. Mud to the knees. She spoke a language that made the wards scream and old Master Corven sit down on the stairs like his legs had resigned." He watched my face. I could feel him watching it, the way he always watched things — patiently, like a man waiting for a lock to tell him its secret. "Sarelle has her locked in the east room. Called her an asset."

"An asset." I laughed, and it came out wrong, thin and bitter as vinegar. "Yes. That's one word for it."

"What word would you use?"

I looked at the coin. The coin looked back, or seemed to, the way under-court currency always does. There's a reason brokers in the capital won't touch mountain-mint after sundown. It isn't superstition. Well. It isn't *only* superstition.

"It's court coin," I said. "From beneath the mountain. And not common trade scrip — see the crown? Uncrowned, floating? That mark is reserved for transactions of a particular class. Transactions involving persons." I turned it over with one finger, not quite touching it. "Not slaves, before you ask. Slaves are legal. Slaves have whole markets and tax stamps and boring, honest paperwork. This mark is for the other kind. Souls."

The kettle began to whistle. Neither of us moved to take it off. It whistled itself hoarse and died, and the silence afterward was worse.

"The girl," Gideon said slowly, "is a transaction."

"The girl is a *contract*, Winterbourne, which is worse. A transaction ends. A contract lives. A contract accrues interest." I finally got up and poured the tea, because if I didn't give my hands something to do I was going to start pacing, and pacing in front of a mercenary is admitting fear. "Tell me exactly what she said. Every word. In whatever tongue you heard it."

"She said words Sarelle's translator gave as 'thirteen moons' and 'beneath the mountain.' She said—" he hesitated, and I saw something move behind his eyes that I'd never seen there before. Fear, maybe. Or its cousin, grief. "She said she remembers being someone else. A palace. A bargain. She said adults sold her name."

I sat down. My tea went cold in front of me untouched, which for a man of my habits tells you everything.

"There was a case," I said. "Seven years ago. Before your time at the guild, or near enough. A princeling of a house that no longer appears on maps — the kind of house that gets erased rather than conquered, because conquest leaves ruins and erasure leaves nothing to mourn. The house fell. The court beneath the mountain took payment. And somewhere in the settling of accounts, a child's soul changed hands."

"You processed it."

"I *filed* it." The correction mattered to me more than it should have. "A junior broker brings me a contract for entry into the Second Ledger. Standard form, unusual collateral. I read it. I remember reading it, because the ink was wrong — under-court ink dries black and then ages red, like a wound healing backwards, and it made my eyes water to look at. And I remember thinking: this is a soul, and souls aren't supposed to be collateral, they're supposed to be the *signatory*. And then I stamped it anyway, because I was twenty-six years old and ambitious and the fee was excellent, and I have spent every night since telling myself that stamping a document is not the same as signing one." I met his eyes. "It is the same, Winterbourne. That's the joke. That's always been the joke."

"Sold to whom?" His voice was flat. Careful. The voice of a man walking on ice and listening for the crack.

"That's the part you're not going to like."

"I haven't liked any of it."

"No," I agreed. "But this part has teeth." I got up, went to the loose stone behind the hearth — yes, I keep my secrets behind the hearthstone, I'm a disgraced broker, not an original thinker — and drew out the one thing I'd kept from my old life. Not gold. Not seals. Paper. A copy. Forbidden, felonious, and the single wisest theft of my career.

I laid it beside the coin. The two documents matched like a hand fits a glove.

Gideon read. I watched him read. I've watched a thousand people read contracts, and most of them read the way children eat sweets — fast, greedy, missing the pit. Gideon read the other way. Slow. Twice over certain lines. Three times over one clause near the bottom.

"Resold," he said at last. "As collateral. To secure a debt owed by..." He stopped. I watched him arrive at it, watched the crack travel across the ice of his face. "To the Vellum Guild."

"To *us*," I said gently, because someone had to be gentle about it and he clearly wasn't going to manage it himself. "The under-court held the girl's soul as payment for her house's debts. Then, six years ago, the guild extended a line of credit to a party whose name is sealed on this copy — sealed even from me, and I built half the sealing protocols we used. Against that credit, the under-court pledged collateral. Guess what they pledged."

"They sold her twice." His voice didn't rise. That was the frightening thing about Gideon Winterbourne; the angrier he got, the quieter he became, like a fire going down to coals. "They sold a child's soul twice, and my employers — our employers — bought the second lien."

"And here is what a lien means, in plain language, since you're a soldier and not a clerk." I tapped the clause he'd read three times. "The guild doesn't merely hold paper. The guild intends to *collect*. Collection on a soul-debt means conveyance. Physical conveyance, of the asset, to the creditor, by the thirteenth moon — when the contract window closes and the debt converts. Do you understand what happens at conversion? The soul reverts to the under-court permanently, and the vessel—" I almost said *the girl*. I made myself say it. "*Wren* becomes an empty house. Walking, breathing, eating, and no one home. Forever. And the guild will deliver her to the mountain themselves, with proper documentation, because the Vellum Guild has never once failed to honor a contract. It's the only thing we've ever been proud of."

* * *

He was quiet for a long time. Rain ticked against the shutters. Downstairs, the tannery vats breathed their sour breath up through the floorboards.

"You knew," he said finally. Not accusing. Worse than accusing — *ascertaining*. "When you saw the coin. You knew what she was."

"I suspected. Now I know. There's a difference, and the difference is this document, which I stole illegally and can hang me, and which I am showing you anyway." I leaned forward. "Listen to me, Winterbourne, because I'm only going to say this once and then I'm going to pretend I never said it. You came here tonight as a guild man asking a guild question. I am answering as something else. What I'm about to tell you makes us both criminals, and I need you to understand that I know that, and I'm doing it regardless, and I'd thank you not to make me examine why."

"Why?" he asked. Of course he asked. He would.

"Because when I stamped that contract seven years ago, the ink made my eyes water, and I told myself it was the fumes." I stood, went to the window, looked out at the rain rather than at him. "Because I have spent seven years telling myself versions of that sentence. Because the Vellum Guild trades in impossible things, that's what we call ourselves, that's what we *brag* — borrowed years, bottled names, second chances. And do you know what I learned, in fifteen years inside those ledgers? Nothing in this world is impossible to buy. Everything is impossible to sell honestly. Every entry in every ledger I ever kept was somebody's grief with a price column next to it. And I told myself we were neutral. Brokers. Scales, not swords." I laughed again, and this time I didn't bother making it sound like anything but what it was. "We're not scales. We're the wolf that counts the sheep and charges the shepherd for the service."

"Gideon." I turned around. He was still looking at the contract, at the clause, at the conversion date written in aging-red ink. "The sealed party. The one who owes the guild, whose debt the girl's soul secures. You've worked for the masters seven years. You wear their oath-mark. If they called in that debt tomorrow — if they needed a body delivered, quietly, no questions — who would they send?"

The silence stretched.

"Don't," he said.

"I'm not accusing you. I'm telling you what they'll do, because I know them. They will not send Corvane, not at first. Corvane is loud, deliberately loud, a hammer they show you so you never notice the knife. They'll send someone trusted. Someone quiet. Someone with a mercenary's skills and a servant's obedience and a god so silent he's started to mistake the silence for faith." I let that sit. "They will send *you*, Winterbourne. Maybe not today. Maybe not this month. But the thirteenth moon is turning, and when it does, someone has to carry that child to the mountain. And I promise you, on whatever's left of my honor, that the name on the delivery order will be yours."

"You can't know that."

"I can read a contract better than anyone alive, including the people who wrote this one. And this one has your fingerprints all over it even though your name appears nowhere in it. That's how they build things, the masters. That's how they've *always* built things. Contracts within contracts, parties within parties, until the whole edifice is one great machine that grinds grief into profit, and everyone working the machine believes they're just doing their job." I picked up the cold tea and drank it anyway, because my throat had gone dry. "You asked me what word I'd use for the girl instead of asset. Here's my word: *evidence*. She is the living evidence of what the guild actually is. And evidence, in my experience, gets destroyed."

* * *

He stood to leave. At the door he paused, hand on the latch, rain-grey light cutting across his face.

"The sealed party," he said. "The debtor. Can it be unsealed?"

I should have lied. Any sensible man would have lied. Instead I heard myself say, "Everything can be unsealed. Seals are just promises made of wax. But whoever breaks this one will find a name at the bottom of it, and Winterbourne — I've read enough contracts to know that names at the bottom of contracts like this one are never strangers."

"What does that mean?"

"It means," I said, "that the under-court doesn't lend to just anyone. It lends to people it already owns a piece of. It means that whoever owes the guild — whoever's debt that child's soul is nailed to — has been theirs a long, long time. And it means that when you go back to that guildhouse tonight, you should look at your own oath-mark a little differently. Count the moons in it. Tell me how many you see."

He looked at his wrist. I watched him look. I watched the color leave his face one shade at a time, patient as winter.

"Thirteen," he whispered.

"Get her out," I said. "Before the moon turns. And Winterbourne — whatever you do, don't sign anything."
