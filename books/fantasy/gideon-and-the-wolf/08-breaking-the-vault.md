## 8. Breaking the Vault

There is a particular kind of silence that lives in a guildhouse the night before a ceremony. Not peace — I want to be clear about that. Peace is what you find in a chapel, or a well-run kitchen. This was the silence of held breath, of a hundred clerks pretending not to count the hours until the twelfth moon finished its turn and the thirteenth began its climb. The Vellum Guild did not celebrate moon-turnings. It *settled accounts* on them.

I had spent eleven years learning to read that silence the way other men read weather.

I sat at my desk in the counting room with a candle burned down to a stub and the great ledger open before me — the one they let me touch only because my oath-bound hands were steadier than any clerk's, and because a man sworn to a silent god is presumed to have no ambitions worth stealing for. The entry I was reading was short. Guild entries about children are always short. Nobody wants to spend ink describing what they're doing.

*Asset 114: female child, approx. eight years. Acquisition: foundling, storm-borne. Status: secured, east vault. Disposition: pending collection, thirteenth turn.*

Pending collection. Four words, and every one of them a knife laid flat.

I had copied that entry three times in my head since Sarelle's interrogation, and each time it looked less like bookkeeping and more like a butcher's order. *Asset.* They hadn't even troubled to learn her name. Wren. She'd told me her name in that broken common of hers, pointing at her own chest with the solemn gravity of a queen conferring a title, and I had written it in the margin of my private notebook like a fool writing down a pretty word — and then, later, scratched it out again, because margins get audited.

But ink remembers even when you scratch it. That's the first thing they teach you here. Ink is the most honest substance in the world, which is why everyone who works with it lies constantly.

I closed the ledger. I blew out the candle. And I admitted to myself, in the dark, what I had been refusing to admit for two days: that I was going to do this. That some part of me had decided the moment she pressed that copper coin into my palm and looked at me with eyes that recognized something in my face that I myself could not find there.

The trouble with oaths is that they don't break all at once. They fray. And you tell yourself, right up until the last thread parts, that you are still holding on.

* * *

The east vault was reached through the reliquary corridor, which was fitting, because everything the guild kept was a relic of someone. Bottled names murmured on their shelves as I passed — I felt them the way you feel a draft under a door, small voices pressing against glass. Borrowed years ticked softly in their lacquered boxes, like insects dreaming. I had walked this corridor a thousand times and never once listened. Tonight I listened, and it was like walking through a crowd of people quietly asking to be let out.

Two wardens stood at the vault door. Hask and Merrin. I knew them both. Hask owed me money from a dice game in autumn; Merrin had once asked me to write a letter to his mother in Corvalese because his own letters came out backwards, and I had done it, and had never mentioned it since. These are the things you know about men when you keep their ledgers. It is remarkable how much of a person ends up written down somewhere, if you're the one holding the pen.

"Evening, Winterbourne," Hask said. "Audit running late?"

"Reconciliation," I said. "Sarelle wants the vault inventory verified before the ceremony. She wants your keys counted too." I produced a slip of paper I had written an hour earlier, bearing the guild seal I had been forging — no, *practicing*, let's be honest — for six years, on the theory that a skill unused is a sin against craft. "Sign here."

Merrin signed. Hask hesitated, and I watched him weigh his suspicion against his fear of Sarelle, and I watched Sarelle win, because she always won, that was the whole architecture of the place. He signed. The lock turned. And I walked into the east vault carrying the two most dangerous things a man can carry: a forged document, and a decision.

Wren was sitting cross-legged on the floor in the corner farthest from the lamp, and she was awake — of course she was awake; children always know the night before something happens, the way animals know weather. She didn't cry out or shrink back. She looked at me, and then she looked at the door behind me, and then she looked at me again, and she nodded once, slowly, the way you nod at a plan you've already made yourself and are relieved to see someone else finally catch up to.

"I'm getting you out," I said. Then, because common clearly wasn't going to carry the weight alone, I tried the phrase she'd taught me on the stairs two nights past, the one that meant roughly *I see you, I am coming*. My accent was apparently criminal. Her mouth twitched — the ghost of a smile, the first one I'd seen on her — and she corrected my tongue placement with two fingers held up like a schoolmistress, and something in my chest went loose and strange.

She took my hand. Small fingers, mud still faintly in the creases of her knuckles from the night she arrived, though they'd bathed her twice since. She trusted me. That was the thing that made my hands shake as I led her out — not fear of Sarelle, not fear of Corvane, whom I could hear giving orders somewhere above, but the weight of being trusted by someone who had been sold twice already and had decided, against all evidence, to trust a third time.

We got as far as the reliquary before the wards woke.

I should have expected it. Moon-turning night, the guild's protections drawn tight as drumskin, and a soul passing through them that the wards had already tasted once and flagged. The lamps along the corridor flared violet, one after another, like a scream traveling down a throat. Somewhere above us, a bell began to ring — not the alarm bell, worse, the *inventory* bell, the one that meant an asset was moving unrecorded.

Wren's grip tightened on my hand. I did the arithmetic every mercenary does — exits, distances, odds — and the arithmetic was bad. So I stopped doing it. There is a moment in every fight where calculation becomes its own kind of cowardice, and the only move left is the one you make with your body instead of your head. I pulled her into the scriptorium, through the rows of copying desks, and out the window the novices used for sneaking smoke, and dropped us both into the courtyard below, my knees taking the landing like they'd been waiting eleven years for permission to do something honest.

Then I ran. With her on my back, because eight-year-old legs don't hold a pace, and she weighed almost nothing, and weighed everything.

* * *

They caught up with me at the gate. Of course they did. I had planned for dogs, for wardens, for the wards themselves — I had not planned for Sarelle, because some part of me had understood that you cannot plan for Sarelle. You can only arrive where she already is.

She stood beneath the gate arch with a lantern held at hip height, and she was not out of breath, and she was not angry, and those two facts frightened me more than a dozen wardens would have. Behind her, the guildhouse blazed with light and noise, and none of it moved toward us. She had, I realized, called them off. Or told them to wait. With Sarelle those amounted to the same thing.

"Gideon," she said.

"Sarelle."

"You're holding a stolen asset."

"I'm holding a child."

"The distinction is in the ledger," she said, "and the ledger says asset." She lifted the lantern slightly, and the light climbed her face, and I saw — this is the part I still turn over at night — I saw that she was tired. Not weary the way I was weary. Tired the way a person gets from carrying something heavy for a very long time and being the only one who can see it.

"Step aside," I said. My voice came out steadier than I had any right to expect. Wren's arms were around my neck, and I could feel her heartbeat against my spine, quick as a bird's.

"No," Sarelle said. And then, while my stomach dropped through the flagstones: "But I'm not stopping you either."

I didn't understand. I said so, or started to, and she cut me off with a raised hand.

"Eleven years, Gideon. You've balanced our books for eleven years without a single discrepancy, and in all that time you've never once asked what we do with the entries you reconcile. Never asked where the borrowed years come from. Never asked why the bottled names whisper." She set the lantern down on the cobblestones, deliberately, the way one lays down a card. "I told myself you were devoted. A man sworn to a silent god, content with silence. But I've read the old contracts lately. All of them. And I find I can no longer pretend the guild is what I told myself it was when I was young enough to believe in ledgers."

"So you're letting us go."

"I'm letting *you* go." Her eyes flicked up to Wren, then back. "Understand what that costs. The guild will dispatch retrievers by dawn. Corvane will lead them, and Corvane does not negotiate, he collects. And the debt doesn't stay behind when you run, Gideon. Debts follow. That's the entire business we're in. That's the entire business *everything* is in, underneath. Every road you take tonight, you'll be spending something to walk it. I need you to hear that from me now, while it's cheap, rather than learn it later, when the price has gone up."

"I hear you."

"Do you?" She stepped aside, out of the gate's arch, and the road beyond ran black and silver under the failing light of the twelfth moon. "You have a mercenary's hands and a clerk's conscience and a hole where your history should be, and you've just made yourself an enemy of the only institution that ever claimed to know what you are. Whatever you find under that mountain — and you'll find it, because you're the type — remember that I warned you with the gate open. Remember that not everyone who stands in your way is your enemy, and not everyone who opens a door loves you."

Wren stirred against my back. And then, in her cracked, careful common, aimed at Sarelle with the unnerving directness of children and prophets, she said: "You come too. Later. When you are ready."

Sarelle went very still. I watched something move behind her face — the look of a woman hearing her own secret spoken back to her in a language she thought only she knew. She didn't answer. She bent, picked up her lantern, and turned back toward the burning-bright guildhouse, and over her shoulder she gave me the last words I would hear from her for a long time:

"The thirteenth moon turns in nine days, Gideon. After that, no bargain holds. Run like the books are on fire."

And I ran. Out the gate, onto the mountain road, into the dark, with a stolen child on my back and a forged signature drying behind me and the whole machinery of the Vellum Guild waking to my name. Fear rode with me the whole first mile — cold, ordinary, animal fear, the kind that makes your breath come short and your legs feel borrowed.

Somewhere in the second mile, Wren leaned forward against my ear and spoke a soft string of words in her old language, and though I didn't know them yet, I understood their shape. Comfort. Encouragement. Perhaps a joke at my expense; her tone had a certain conspiratorial lilt.

And the fear changed. Not left — changed. It went down into my boots and became stride, became purpose, became the oldest thing I knew how to be: a man moving toward a fight he intended to win. I had spent eleven years keeping other people's accounts. Tonight, for the first time, I was spending my own.

Behind us the guildhouse bells rang on. Ahead, under the dying light of the twelfth moon, the mountain waited — patient, patient, patient — like a creditor who knows exactly where you live.
