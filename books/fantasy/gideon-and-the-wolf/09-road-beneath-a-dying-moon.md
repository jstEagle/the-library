## 9. Road Beneath a Dying Moon

**GIDEON**

The twelfth moon was dying the way all the guild's assets eventually died — slowly, and with an air of having been cheated.

I noticed it first on the second night out from Grayhollow, when I stopped to check our backtrail and caught the light coming off it thin as watered wine. A month ago that moon had thrown shadows sharp enough to shave by. Now it hung in the sky like a coin rubbed nearly blank, and every night it gave less, and every night the dark came up to meet us a little earlier, like something patient walking beside us on the road.

Wren didn't seem to mind. She walked the way children walk when they've finally been let out of a room — which, I supposed, she had been, in a manner of speaking. The guildhouse had been her room for however long Sarelle had kept her locked in the east archive, and before that, if Quill was right, there had been other rooms, worse ones, beneath a mountain. She was eight years old and had been sold twice, and here she was skipping over the puddles left by the day's rain as though the world were a place worth skipping through.

I envied her that. I envied it the way you envy a man his sleep when you haven't slept yourself in years.

"Keep to my left," I told her, for the fourth time since dawn. "If anyone comes up the road, they'll come from behind. You get into the trees and you don't come out until I whistle twice."

She looked at me with that expression she had, the one that made me feel like a ledger entry being audited. "You say this *every* time."

"Because every time is a time someone could come."

"You say it like prayer." She said it without cruelty, the way children say devastating things. "Prayers you say again because nobody answers."

I had no answer to that either, so I adjusted my pack strap and walked on.

We made poor time, honestly. A mercenary travels fast alone; a mercenary traveling with an eight-year-old travels at the speed of an eight-year-old's legs and bladder combined. But speed wasn't the whole of it. The thirteenth moon would turn when it turned, and no amount of hurrying would hurry it. What mattered was arriving at the mountain with our souls still our own, and the only way I knew to manage that was caution, and food, and not dying on the road like idiots.

So we walked, and the moon thinned above us, and somewhere behind us — though I hadn't seen them yet — I knew the guild's retrievers would be coming. Debts follow. Sarelle's parting words at the gate, delivered with that terrible gentleness of hers, the way you'd warn a child about a hot stove: *Go, then, Gideon. But understand what you're carrying. The guild does not forgive; it collects.*

Ahead of us, north and east, the mountains stood up against the horizon like a wall of broken teeth, and the tallest of them wore snow even in this season, and beneath that snow was a court that bought and sold what should never have been for sale.

And I was taking a child back to it.

Some nights I told myself this was rescue. Other nights, in the honest dark, I wondered if I was simply delivering her home to her owner, and whether the difference between those two things was anything more than the story I preferred to tell myself.

* * *

**WREN**

The man Gideon did not know how to be quiet.

Not with his feet — his feet were very good, soft on the road, always listening behind us. Quiet with his *face*. His face was loud all the time. It said things his mouth did not say. When he thought I slept, his face said *afraid*. When he counted our food, his face said *not enough*. And when I spoke the old words, his face did a thing I had no word for yet, in any tongue.

So I decided to teach him some.

"Say after me," I told him, on the third morning, when we sat eating hard bread beside a stream that talked to itself the way streams do. "*Sel-eth.*"

"Sell-eth," he said, wrong.

"*Sel-eth.*"

"*Sel*-eth." Closer. He frowned at the bread like the bread had tricked him. "What does it mean?"

"It means *the light that stays*." I pointed at the sky, where the twelfth moon hung pale even in daytime, like a scar that hadn't finished healing. "The old people called the moons that. Each one is a light that stays when everything else goes dark. There were thirteen once."

"And now?"

"Now twelve." I ate my bread. Some things you say plain and some things you don't, and that one I said plain so he would stop asking.

He was quiet a while. Then: "Teach me another."

That surprised me. Gideon did not like not-knowing things. I had watched him not-know things on the road and get angry about it, quiet-angry, the way he got about most things. But he asked again. "Another word. Please."

So I taught him *vessa*, which means *to carry someone because you love them and not because you must*. He repeated it three times and then went silent, looking at the stream, and his face was doing the thing again, the thing with no name.

"Why do you want my words?" I asked him. It had been bothering me. Fugitives do not take lessons.

He took his time answering, which was also like him. "Because when you speak it," he said finally, "the wards in the guildhouse flared like a bonfire. Because Sarelle went white as paper. Because whatever your language is, it's older than their ledgers, and older things scare younger things." He turned the bread crust in his fingers. "And because last night I dreamed in it. Words I don't know, saying themselves in my head in a voice that isn't mine. If your language is living in my dreams, girl, I want to at least learn its name."

I looked at him for a long moment. Then I said the word I had been saving, the way you save the sweetest plum in the bowl.

"*Aethren.*"

Something happened to him.

I saw it happen. His whole body went still, stiller than still, the way a deer goes when it hears the bowstring. His eyes fixed on the middle distance where nothing was. And he whispered — not repeating it, not learning it, but *answering* it, the way you answer your own name:

"I know that word."

"Do you?" I said, though I already knew he did. That was why I'd saved it.

"There's a tower." His voice had gone strange, flat, like a man reading from a page he couldn't see. "Stone. High windows. There's a fire — no. Not a fire. Light. Light coming *up* through the floor, gold, wrong-colored, and someone is shouting, and there's a table, and a man is writing — signing — his hand is bleeding, he's signing in blood because ink won't hold it, ink won't *hold* it—"

"Gideon."

"—and the word for what he's signing is *aethren*, it's a contract, it's the oldest kind, and the man looks up and he has my—" Gideon stopped. Blinked. Came back to himself the way a man surfaces from deep water, gasping, and found me watching him with my bread halfway to my mouth.

His hands were shaking. He put them flat on his knees to make them stop, which did not work.

"What did you see just now?" I asked him gently, though I knew this too. I had seen the tower. I had seen the fire that was not a fire. I had been small in a corner of that room, or the soul that became me had been, and I had watched a man sign away something in blood because ink wouldn't hold it.

"A tower," he said slowly. "A fire. A man signing a contract in his own blood." He laughed, one short ugly note with no humor in it anywhere. "I've never seen any of those things. I'm a guild clerk. I audit ledgers in Grayhollow. My life began seven years ago with an oath and a blank page, and before that there's nothing, there's just—" he tapped his own chest, twice, like knocking on a door, "—nothing in there. No tower. No fire. So explain to me, Wren, why I know the word for a blood-contract in a language dead before any kingdom I can name."

I could have explained. I knew pieces of it, more than he did, maybe. But I was eight, and tired, and some truths are too heavy to hand a man while his hands are still shaking. So instead I reached over and took his shaking hand, the way you steady a bird, and I said the only true thing I had:

"Whoever you were before, you are the one who carried me out. *Vessa.* That word is yours now too."

He looked at our hands. Then he looked north, at the mountain with its white crown, and his face went quiet-loud again, and I read it easily this time.

It said: *I am going to find out what I signed, and who I was before I signed it, and I am afraid of both answers.*

Mine said: *Good. Because I cannot carry us both.*

* * *

**GIDEON**

That night we sheltered in the shell of a shepherd's cot, walls of dry stone missing half their height, roof long gone, and the dying moon gave us barely enough light to see each other eat. I built a small fire anyway, shielded in the corner, because Wren was shivering and pride is a luxury for men with warm children.

She fell asleep mid-sentence, the way the young do, dropping off between one question and the next. Her last question had been whether gods can die. I'd told her I'd let her know the moment I got an answer from mine. She'd giggled. Then she was gone, breath slow, one hand curled around the copper coin she wouldn't let me carry anymore — *it's yours but you hold it wrong*, she'd said, which meant nothing and felt like everything.

I sat with my back to the wall and watched the moon.

Twelve lights that stay, she'd said, and one gone. Thirteen moons once. I turned the arithmetic over in my head the way I'd turn a suspicious ledger entry, looking for the false column. A language older than kingdoms. A contract signed in blood because ink wouldn't hold it. A tower I had never entered and a fire I had never fled, surfacing in my skull like corpses rising in a pond, called up by four syllables in a child's mouth.

*Aethren.*

I said it silently, carefully, the way you test ice. Nothing came. Whatever door that word had opened that morning had closed again, or perhaps it had only ever opened onto a room I wasn't ready to stand in. But I remembered the feeling of it — the vertigo, the sense of standing at the edge of a life that had been taken from me rather than merely forgotten. Forgotten things sit quietly. Taken things *ache*.

Seven years ago I had woken, in the guild's telling, with an oath on my lips and a god's mark on my forearm and no history worth mentioning. Everyone had accepted it. I had accepted it. Men drift into the Vellum Guild the way debris drifts into harbors; nobody asks the flotsam where it's been.

But flotsam doesn't dream in dead languages. Flotsam doesn't know the word for a blood-contract.

And my god — my silent, thousand-nights-silent god — had marked me anyway. Had sworn me. Had taken my devotion and given nothing back for seven years, and I had called that silence faithfulness, and called my emptiness humility, and never once, not once, asked the question that a competent auditor asks of any account that refuses inspection:

*What is being hidden, and whose benefit is the hiding for?*

Wren stirred in her sleep and murmured something in the old tongue. Just a fragment, half-dreamed. But my skin prickled, because I almost understood it. Not translated it — understood it, the way you understand a voice calling from another room, meaning arriving ahead of the words.

The phrase, as near as I could hold it: *the door knows what it is owed.*

Lovely. I filed it under omens I didn't ask for, next to the dream of the mountain and the voice that called me by a name that was not mine.

I banked the fire, checked the road south one last time — empty, black, the moon too weak to give me even a lie of safety — and settled in to keep the watch.

Somewhere out in that darkness, I was certain, Corvane or his kin were moving toward us with a contract in hand. Somewhere behind us, Sarelle was choosing, or failing to choose. Somewhere ahead, under all that snow, a Pale Regent sat at a frozen feast wearing a face he'd bought from somebody's corpse-or-worse, waiting for the thirteenth turn.

And here in the ruin of a shepherd's cot, a clerk with a mercenary's hands sat guard over a sleeping child who was, by every law the guild recognized, not a child at all but an asset in flight.

I touched the mark on my forearm, the god's sigil, cold as ever.

"Hold on," I told it quietly. "Whichever of us is real."

The moon gave no answer. It was nearly out of light to answer with.

But far to the north, past the dark miles and the dark hills, I saw — or thought I saw, and by then I had learned to trust that distinction less and less — a single point of light on the mountain's shoulder. Not snow-glow. Not starlight. A lamp, perhaps. Or a window.

Or a door, opening early, because it already knew what it was owed.

In the morning, we would reach the river, and the river had only one crossing for fifty miles, and I had heard what the keeper of that bridge charged for passage.

I lay back against the cold stone and did not sleep, and the dying moon watched me with what remained of its eye, and together we waited for the light to fail completely.
