## 10. The Toll Bridge

The river had no name on any map I'd ever owned, which by now I took as a bad sign. Named things can be bargained with. Unnamed things do the bargaining.

We'd walked two days out of the village of borrowed years, following a road that grew less like a road and more like a rumor the farther north we went. The twelfth moon hung low and bruised-colored over the hills, its light thin as watered wine, and Wren kept glancing up at it the way a debtor glances at a calendar. Thirteen moons, she'd said. Beneath the mountain. We had one turn left, maybe less, and every mile of it was uphill.

The bridge appeared around a bend in the gorge without warning — which was strange, because there had been no bend in the gorge before it. One moment we were walking along a shelf of wet stone with the river roaring below, and the next there it stood: a span of black timber, old enough that the planks had gone soft and silvered, wide enough for two carts or one regret. At its near end sat a small house of the same black wood, and in the doorway of the house stood a figure wrapped in a gray cloak, hands folded, patient as furniture.

"Finally," I said. "Something with a door."

Wren tugged my sleeve. She said something in her old language, quick and low, and though I still understood only fragments of it, I caught the shape of the words. *Old one. Careful. It counts.*

"It counts what?"

She looked at me with those grave eyes of hers, mud-brown in a face that had once, apparently, belonged to a prince, and pressed her fingers to her own temple. Then she pointed at me.

"Wonderful," I said. "A toll collector for the head."

* * *

The bridge-keeper did not move from the doorway as we approached. Up close I could see it was difficult to say whether the figure was old or young, man or woman; the cloak's hood held shadow the way a cup holds water, and the face within it shifted like something seen through river ice. Its voice, when it came, was dry and courteous and utterly without warmth.

"Travelers. The crossing is maintained. The crossing is safe. The crossing is not free."

"I'd assumed." I patted my purse. "Coin? The guild pays in—"

"The guild," said the bridge-keeper, "pays in paper. Paper is a promise made by someone else. This bridge does not accept promises made by someone else."

Wren stepped forward and held out her hand, palm up, offering the copper coin she carried — the coin stamped with the sigil I'd recognized without knowing why, court currency from beneath the mountain, minted for transactions involving souls. Quill had told us as much, going white around the mouth. The bridge-keeper looked at the coin for a long moment.

"No," it said. "That is already owed elsewhere. One cannot pay a debt with a debt."

Wren withdrew the coin slowly, and I noticed her hand was shaking. Not fear, I thought. Anger. She was learning, this child, how many things in the world were designed to take from her.

"What do you accept?" I asked.

"Memory," said the bridge-keeper. "A true memory, freely given, wholly surrendered. You will not know you lost it. You will not miss it. You will simply be a man who never had it." The hood tilted. "Small memories cross cheaply. Large ones are dearer. But the large ones hold better against the current."

I should have turned back then. I want that recorded somewhere, even if the only ledger left to record it is my own skull: I knew what this thing was, and I considered turning back, and I didn't, because behind us lay the guild, and Corvane, and a contract with my signature on it that Sarelle feared, and ahead lay the only chance a child had of getting her soul back before the thirteenth moon turned. When the choice is between losing a piece of yourself and losing all of someone else, the arithmetic is ugly but it isn't hard.

"How much for two crossings?" I asked.

The bridge-keeper was quiet for a moment. When it spoke again, there was something almost like interest in the dry voice. "Two crossings. Two memories. But they need not come from the same traveler." The hood turned toward Wren. "The small one has little to give that has not already been taken."

"She gives nothing," I said, faster than thought. "Whatever the price is, I pay it. Both shares."

"That is not how tolls work," said the bridge-keeper, "but it is how fathers work, and bridges have seen both." It raised one gray hand. "Very well. Both memories from you. Choose them yourself, or I will choose for you, and I promise you will not enjoy my taste."

* * *

Here is the trouble with choosing a memory to surrender: you have to look at it first.

I stood at the edge of that black bridge with the river roaring underneath and went through myself like an auditor through a suspect ledger, line by line, looking for entries I could bear to strike. There weren't many. Seven years of life, and most of it was guild-work and god-silence and the particular loneliness of a man who suspects his own past is a forgery but can't prove it. My memories were mostly small change. A tavern fight in Marrowfield. The smell of ink. The first time I'd held a ledger bound in vellum and felt, absurdly, at home.

And then, underneath everything, the way a foundation sits under a house: my mother.

I don't mean I found the memory. I mean it found me, the way it always did, unbidden — her hands kneading bread on a scarred table, flour to the wrists, humming something tuneless. Except when I reached for her face, the way you reach for a candle in a dark room, my hand closed on smoke. That was the strange mercy of it, the strange horror. I could remember everything about her except the one thing children are supposed to carry longest. Her face was a smudge where a face should be, worn away by seven years of not-looking-at-it, because I had no portrait, because she was gone before Gideon Winterbourne existed, because whoever built me had built me without mirrors into the past.

So here was the joke the universe had written into my contract: the memory I valued most was already broken. I would be paying with a cracked coin. And yet — and this is the part I keep circling, even now, even without — some stubborn clerk-part of me whispered that the face itself wasn't the whole of the memory. The humming was there. The flour. The warmth of a kitchen I couldn't picture. If I gave it up, I wouldn't miss it, the thing had promised. I would simply be a man who had never been anyone's son.

Which, if Quill was right about what I was, might be truer than I wanted to admit.

"This one," I heard myself say. "My mother. What remains of her."

The hood regarded me. "A damaged memory. I will discount it accordingly — but understand what you purchase with the difference. The crossing will hold for you, traveler, but the river remembers what the mind forgets. Somewhere below, in the water, your mother's humming will go on without you. Do you consent?"

Somewhere below, in the water. I looked down through the gaps between the planks at the black water churning, and I thought: somewhere down there, a kitchen will hum forever to no one. It seemed, strangely, a kinder fate than forgetting entirely. At least the song would survive me.

"I consent."

"Kneel," said the bridge-keeper, "and open your mouth."

* * *

I knelt on cold stone. Wren grabbed my arm, saying her sharp syllables — *no, no, wrong* — and I put my hand over hers.

"It's all right, little wren. It's just a payment. I've paid worse things to worse creatures for less reason."

She didn't believe me. Children never believe that, which is either their great flaw or their last remaining virtue.

The bridge-keeper bent over me, and beneath the hood there was no face at all, only a depth, a well-shadow, and from that depth came a breath that smelled of river-stone and old paper. Something long and thin — I will not call it a tongue — touched my forehead, light as a clerk's pen lifting a word off a page.

And it lifted.

I felt it go. That's the part no one tells you about selling a memory: you feel it leave, one clean stroke, like a line ruled through an entry, and for half a heartbeat you see the whole of what you're losing — flour-dusted hands, the tuneless humming, a voice saying a name that wasn't Gideon, a name I couldn't hold onto even as it slid past, warm as bathwater, gone — and then the half-heartbeat closes and the entry is struck and there is nothing where it was. No ache. No gap. Just a smooth place in the ledger of me, and no way to know a line had ever been written there.

I stayed kneeling a moment longer than I needed to. Then I stood, and brushed off my knees, and found Wren staring up at me with tears running freely down her face.

"What?" I said. "Did it take something of yours too?"

She shook her head, hard, and wiped her cheeks with the heel of her hand, and pointed at me, and then at her own eyes, and shook her head again, and the crying got worse. She said something in the old language, low and fierce, and pressed her fist against my chest, right over the heart, and left it there.

I didn't understand her. I want to be honest about that, since honesty is about all I'll have left by the time this road ends. I didn't understand the words. But I understood the fist. Somewhere in me a smooth place sat where a mother had been, and I felt nothing about it, and this child — who had been sold twice before she was nine, who had more right to grief than anyone I'd ever met — was grieving on my behalf, for a woman whose name I no longer knew I'd ever known.

"There now," I said, uselessly, the way people do. "It's fine. Whatever it was, it's fine."

It was not fine. Nothing about a man who cannot mourn his own losses is fine. But the bridge-keeper was already stepping aside, gesturing us across with terrible courtesy, and the moon was dying overhead, and there was a mountain waiting to eat the rest of what I was.

* * *

The planks of the bridge gave slightly underfoot, alive-seeming, and the river spoke underneath in its endless gravel voice. Halfway across, Wren stopped and made me stop too. She pointed down through a gap between two boards, at the black water.

At first I saw nothing. Then — movement. Shapes in the current, pale and slow, like fish made of fog. And threaded through the roar of the river, faint as a thread pulled from cloth, I heard humming. Tuneless. Patient. A song with no end and no listener, going on and on in the dark under the world.

Wren's hand found mine and gripped it hard. She was crying again, silently now, watching the water as if she could see the memory swimming down there — my mother's kitchen, dissolved into the current, humming to the stones.

"Don't," I said gently. "It's nothing. Just the river."

But she looked up at me with red-rimmed eyes and said, in her broken common, the words she'd been hoarding since the bridge-house: "You had. Someone. Who loved you first." And then, in the old language, a phrase I didn't know yet, though I would learn it later and it would undo me: *you spent her so I could cross.*

"I'm sure I don't know what you mean," I said, which was a lie, and she knew it was a lie, and she gripped my hand tighter anyway, and we crossed.

* * *

On the far bank the road climbed immediately, switchbacking up through pine and shadow toward a shoulder of rock that might, on a clear day, have shown us the mountain. It was not a clear day. The twelfth moon was failing visibly now, its edges fraying like burnt cloth, and Wren checked it after every switchback with the grim diligence of a merchant counting down stock.

We made camp in the lee of a boulder, a small fire, no supper worth mentioning. Wren sat close against my side, the way she'd taken to doing since the village, since she'd learned that my body heat was a thing freely given and not another bargain. After a while she looked up at me.

"Gideon," she said. My name in her mouth still sounded like a foreign coin — correct weight, wrong mint. Then she tapped her temple, the same gesture she'd used at the bridge-house, and pointed at me, and made a slow, deliberate motion of taking something out and giving it away.

"Yes," I said. "I know. I paid. Don't fret over it."

She frowned, unsatisfied, and tried again. She pointed at me. Tapped her temple. Then she spread both hands wide — *everything* — and pointed at the dark shape of the mountain, far off now but unmistakable, a jagged tooth against the failing moonlight. And then she looked at me with an expression I'd seen once before, on the night she'd arrived at the guildhouse door: recognition without memory. Dread blooming into protectiveness, except pointed the other way. She was afraid for me. Of the price the mountain would ask.

"You think I'll have to pay again," I said. "Up there."

She nodded once, slow.

"Well," I said, poking the fire, "then let's hope I've got more in the account than I think." I meant it as a joke. It came out sounding like a prayer, which, given that my god had been silent for seven years, meant it probably wasn't going to be answered either.

Wren didn't laugh. She leaned her head against my arm, and just before sleep took her, she murmured something in the old language — soft, half-dreaming, the tone unmistakable even when the words weren't. Comfort. The kind of thing said to a child after a funeral.

I sat awake a while after she slept, listening to the wind come down off the heights, carrying with it, very faintly, a sound I told myself was imagination: humming. Tuneless. Patient. Rising from the river far below, or rising from somewhere deeper than rivers go.

Then, nearer — much nearer — a branch snapped on the slope above our camp.

I was on my feet with a knife in my hand before the echo died, and Wren was awake and pressed against my back, and out of the dark between the pines came a smell I knew from seven years of guild-work: lamp oil, wet wool, and the particular oiled steel that retrievers carry.

Corvane had found us.
