## 11. Hunters of the Guild

There is a particular kind of silence that settles over a guildhouse after a theft. It is not the silence of peace. It is the silence of an abacus with one bead missing — everything else in its place, and one small absence rattling around in the dark where it shouldn't be.

I stood in Gideon Winterbourne's empty office at the hour before dawn and listened to that absence.

The ledger lay open on his desk. That was the first wrong thing. Gideon was many things — devout to the point of tedium, punctual to the point of insult, and possessed of a face like a locked drawer — but he was never careless with a ledger. He closed them. He squared their corners. He treated each volume with the tenderness most men reserve for lovers or mothers. An open ledger was a sentence left unfinished.

The second wrong thing was the ink. Fresh, still faintly gleaming in the lamplight. A single line entered in his neat, upright hand:

*One (1) soul, juvenile, unregistered — removed from vault pending audit.*

Pending audit. I almost laughed. Seven years I had employed this man, and in seven years he had never once lied to me on paper. He had lied with his face, certainly; everyone did, faces being the cheapest currency in the building. But the ledgers were sacred to him in the way that only a man with no other religion can make a thing sacred.

And now he had written himself a lie so thin you could read the truth straight through it.

"He took the girl," said Corvane behind me.

I hadn't heard him come in. I never do. Corvane moves through the world the way water moves through cloth — everywhere at once, and leaving everything damp and diminished. He is my enforcer, which is a polite word for what he actually is, and he had been standing in the doorway long enough to hear me not laugh.

"Obviously," I said. "He also wrote it down. Which is either sentiment or strategy, and Gideon has never once in his life been strategic."

"You sound fond of him."

"I sound like a woman doing arithmetic." I turned to face him. Corvane is not an easy man to look at, though not for the reasons people assume. The scars are nothing; scars are just biography worn on the outside. What unsettles people is that he looks *attentive*. Most men's eyes wander. Corvane's eyes file things away, and you can feel yourself being filed.

"The girl is an asset," he said. "A high-value asset, given what Quill's old contacts are whispering about under-court coinage. Winterbourne has committed theft against the Vellum Guild. The remedy is retrieval."

"The remedy is retrieval," I agreed. "Say the rest of it."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth. Not quite a smile. Corvane does not smile; he deploys the muscles adjacent to smiling, the way a merchant deploys a sample.

"The Guildmaster wants it handled quietly. No ward-flares, no blood on guild stone. And he wants the contract enforced to the letter." He drew a folded document from inside his coat. "Which means I'll need your seal."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about running a guild that trades in impossible things: the impossible things are the easy part. Borrowed years keep beautifully, if you store them cool. Bottled names don't even slosh. Second chances are more temperamental — they sour if you look at them too hopefully — but a firm hand and a wax seal manage most of them.

The hard part is the paper. Every transaction we broker rests on contracts, and every contract rests on the assumption that a signature means something. A name freely given binds tighter than any chain. That is not metaphor. That is the load-bearing wall of the entire enterprise. Tear out signatures, and the whole guild comes down on your head.

So when Corvane handed me the contract and said, "You'll want to verify the authority before I ride," I did what I always do. I read it.

It was the original instrument of Gideon Winterbourne's indenture — the one signed seven years ago, the day he walked into our hall soaked in rain and someone else's blood, asking for work and offering, as collateral, a soul he claimed was already forfeit. I had seen copies of it a hundred times. Copies are safe. Copies are just ink describing ink.

This was the original. And originals, in our trade, are *live*.

The moment my fingers touched the vellum, I felt it — that faint warmth, like holding a bird. Somewhere in the weave of the thing, a name sat coiled, and the name was awake, and the name knew it had been picked up.

I read the terms. I had read them before, but reading is not the same as seeing, and seeing is not the same as understanding. Seven years ago I had understood exactly enough to process the paperwork and not one syllable more. That is the guild's great gift to its officers: we learn to read without comprehending, the way a butcher learns to cut without thinking about the animal.

Now I comprehended.

The signature at the bottom was not *Gideon Winterbourne*.

Oh, it said Gideon Winterbourne. The letters were all there, in that careful clerk's hand I could have identified blind, from across a room, in the dark. But beneath the ink, the way a word sits beneath a watermark, there was another name. Older. Written in the same hand — which should have been impossible, because the older name predated the hand by decades — and written in a script I had only ever seen in two places: the restricted archives, and my nightmares, which are frankly better organized than the archives.

I knew that script. Every senior officer of the Vellum Guild knows it, the way every sailor knows the shape of a storm cloud. It is the script of the under-court. The mountain's own hand. And it does not appear on guild documents. Ever. There are standing orders about it. There are *bonfires* about it.

"Corvane," I said, and my voice came out steadier than I had any right to expect, "where did the Guildmaster get this?"

"From the deep vault. Where it has been since before your tenure."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the answer I was given." He watched me with those filing-cabinet eyes. "You've gone pale, Guildmistress."

"I'm always pale. It's the lighting." I set the contract down on Gideon's desk, deliberately, the way you'd put down a snake — gently, and while watching it. "Tell me what you know about the man whose name this is."

"Gideon Winterbourne. Former mercenary. Sworn to some minor god of debts or thresholds or whatever it is he mutters at on his knees every night. Competent. Honest, until last night. Fond of ledgers." A pause. "Is there something else he's fond of that I should know about?"

"No," I said. "That's rather the problem. There shouldn't be anything else at all."

Because here is what I did not say, what I have not said aloud in eleven years, what I will deny with my dying breath and possibly afterward: I recognized the name under the name. Not the script — the *name*. I had seen it once before, young and ambitious and full of the guild's own doctrines, when they walked me through the Founding Ledger as part of my elevation. One page. One entry, sealed in black wax, in that same mountain-script. A debt so old it predated the guild itself, contracted by the guild's founders with parties beneath the mountain, secured against —

Secured against a soul described only by function, not by name. *The Counterweight.* That was all the entry said. The Counterweight. Held in trust. To be called when the thirteenth moon turns.

I had asked my predecessor what it meant. She had looked at me the way you look at a child who has asked where meat comes from, and she had said: "It means the guild keeps its oldest promise. Pray you're retired before it comes due."

Then she had poured herself a drink with a hand that was not entirely steady, and I had learned the third lesson of the Vellum Guild, the one they never write down: the founders were not traders who sometimes dealt with the under-court. They were *agents* who sometimes dealt with the living.

* * *

By midmorning I had done three things, in ascending order of stupidity.

First, I pulled the retrieval warrant and sealed it. Duty is duty, and whatever rot lies at the root of the tree, the branches still bear fruit, and the clerks still eat. The girl was guild property by every instrument we held. Gideon had stolen her. Those were the facts as the paper saw them, and I have built my life on the principle that when the facts and your feelings disagree, you check the facts twice and then fire whoever forged them.

Second, I went down to the vaults and requested the Founding Ledger. The archivist — a desiccated creature named Mott who has been old for as long as anyone can remember and gives every indication of continuing indefinitely out of pure spite — informed me that the Founding Ledger was out for restoration.

"Restoration," I repeated.

"Moth damage," said Mott, without looking up.

"Moths. In a warded vault. Beneath six seals."

"Moths are persistent." He turned a page with one yellow fingernail. "Guildmistress. You look like a woman about to ask a question she'll regret. May I offer some professional advice? Don't."

"When did the order go down?"

"What order?"

"Mott."

He sighed, the sigh of a man burdened beyond endurance by the competence of others, and slid a chit across the desk. The Guildmaster's seal. Dated four days ago.

Four days. Two days *before* the child arrived at our door speaking a dead language. Before her words made our wards flare like a struck match. Before Gideon Winterbourne — honest, tedious, devout Gideon — looked at a copper coin and went the color of candle wax, as though remembering something he had never been told.

The Guildmaster had ordered the Founding Ledger hidden before the girl ever knocked. Which meant he knew she was coming. Which meant someone beneath the mountain had told him. Which meant the sale of that child's soul — the resale that landed her in our vault as collateral — was not a transaction.

It was an invitation.

Third, and stupidest: I went back up to Gideon's office, alone, and I touched the contract again.

I told myself it was procedure. Verification of authority, chain of custody, all the comfortable vocabulary of a woman who has spent thirty years making sure the machinery runs smoothly and has never once asked what the machinery is *for*. I laid my palm flat on the vellum and let the live name underneath feel my touch, and I did the thing that no officer of the Vellum Guild is supposed to know how to do, the thing my predecessor taught me in one drunken hour and swore me to secrecy over: I listened to it.

Contracts remember. That is their nature. Ink is just the body; the memory lives in the binding. And this contract remembered its signing — not the words, but the *moment*. The weight of a hand. The hesitation before commitment. And underneath the hesitation, something I had never felt in a document before, something that made the hair on my arms stand up and stay standing:

Fear. Not the signer's fear.

The *name's* fear.

Whatever Gideon Winterbourne was seven years ago, when he pressed his hand to this page, the thing being bound had known what it was being bound into. It had known, and it had signed anyway, and the desperation of that choice is soaked so deep into the fibers of the vellum that thirty years in the trade couldn't have missed it, and I had missed it, because I had read the contract without listening, the way I was trained to, the way we are *all* trained to.

We don't read souls at the Vellum Guild. We count them.

I lifted my hand. The warmth faded. The office was very quiet, and outside the rain had started again, the same soft gray rain that had been falling the night a mud-caked child appeared at our door and spoke a language older than kingdoms, and I understood — finally, uselessly, far too late — that the storm hadn't brought her.

She had been *sent*. The way you send a letter. The way you send a lure.

* * *

Corvane found me at dusk in the dispatch yard, supervising the saddling. Six retrievers, chosen for discretion, mounted on horses chosen for endurance. He was already armored, already armed, already wearing that attentive expression that makes strong men find reasons to be elsewhere.

"You've read the contract," he said. It wasn't a question. Nothing with Corvane ever is.

"I've read it."

"And?"

"And it will be enforced." I handed him the warrant, properly sealed, properly witnessed. My hand did not shake. I had rehearsed. "The girl comes back. Winterbourne comes back alive — the Guildmaster was specific on that point, and I want him specific in return. Whatever debts the man owes, he owes to the guild, and the guild collects its own. Dead men pay nothing."

"Understood." He took the warrant, and then he didn't move. Rain dripped off the eaves between us, patient as a metronome. "You're lying to me, Guildmistress."

"I am your Guildmistress. Lying to you is among my enumerated powers."

"Not about the warrant." His voice dropped, and for the first time in eleven years, Corvane sounded uncertain, and I discovered that his uncertainty frightened me more than his certainty ever had. "About the signature. You looked at that name under his name, and you recognized it, and you've been white as bone ever since. I've carried that contract for three days. I've felt it. It's *awake*, Sarelle. Contracts don't wake up. So tell me what I'm riding toward, because I've broken kneecaps for this guild and burned houses for this guild and I have never once asked why, but tonight, for the first time, I'm asking."

And there it was. The moment. Standing in the rain with the best weapon the guild owns, being asked a direct question by a man who has never asked one, with the Founding Ledger's ghost sitting in my chest like swallowed ice.

I could tell him. I could tell him about the Counterweight, about the thirteenth moon, about the invitation dressed as a child, about the growing suspicion that Gideon Winterbourne was never a man who joined our guild at all but a *debt* that our guild has been keeping warm for a hundred years, waiting for it to come due. I could tell him that the retrieval warrant in his saddlebag might be the oldest lie the Vellum Guild ever told, and that he and I are both characters in it.

Or I could send him down the road clean, carrying nothing but the warrant and his orders, because knowledge in our trade is not power. Knowledge is *liability*. Everything you know, the guild owns, and everything the guild owns, the mountain eventually collects.

"Bring them both back," I said. "That's all you need to carry."

He held my eyes a moment longer. Then he nodded, swung up into the saddle, and led his five retrievers out through the gate into the wet dark, and I stood in the yard and watched the lanterns shrink down the road toward the mountains, and I thought: *I have just sent the guild's sharpest knife after the only man who might survive what's coming, and I have sent him believing he's the hunter.*

I went back inside, past the wards, past the clerks, down to my office, and I unlocked the drawer where I keep the things I am not supposed to have. Inside was a copy — my copy, made eleven years ago, in defiance of standing orders — of a single page from the Founding Ledger.

I spread it on my desk beside a blank sheet of guild vellum, and I dipped my pen, and I began, in the old mountain-script, the one language the Guildmaster cannot read and Mott pretends not to:

*To the man called Gideon Winterbourne, or to whatever answers to that name —*

The pen stopped. Because that was the trouble, wasn't it. That was the whole rotten heart of it. I did not know who would answer. A mercenary. A constructed man. A counterweight. A soul the mountain sold to itself to bait a trap a century in the making. Perhaps all of them. Perhaps none.

Outside, above the rooftops, the twelfth moon hung low and bruise-colored, and I could see — plainly, horribly — how much thinner its light was than it ought to be. One turn left. Maybe less.

I finished the letter in nine words, sealed it, and gave it to the fastest rider in the stable with orders to run the retrievers down and put it into Corvane's hand before the border.

*The guild did not make you. It only kept you.*

Then I sat alone in the dark with my forgery of a conscience, and waited to learn whether I had just saved the guild — or signed its death warrant, in the only language that could never be countersigned.
