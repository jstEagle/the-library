## 12. Village of Borrowed Years

The village announced itself by smell before sight — woodsmoke and wet wool and something sweeter underneath, like honey left too long in the sun. We crested the last hill as the twelfth moon dragged itself above the treeline, thin as a paring of bone, and there below us lay a scatter of rooftops huddled around a well like children around a storyteller.

"They have bread," Wren said.

"We don't know that."

"Smoke. Ovens. Bread." She said it with the absolute certainty of an eight-year-old who had decided the universe owed her dinner, and honestly, I was in no position to argue. The last three days we'd eaten what the road offered, which was to say acorns, one suspicious mushroom I'd lost at dice with my own conscience, and a stream that tasted of iron and regret.

"Fine," I said. "Bread. But we're strangers with no papers and worse manners. Let me do the talking."

Wren looked up at me with an expression I was beginning to dread, the one that said she understood every word I said and found most of them charmingly stupid. She patted the copper coin through the pocket of my coat — *our* coin now, our single piece of evidence, our curse — and said nothing, which from her was practically a signed affidavit of agreement.

We went down into the village of borrowed years.

* * *

I noticed the wrongness before I could name it. That's a mercenary's gift, or a curse: you see the thing out of joint before you see the thing itself. A woman drawing water moved like a grandmother — careful, deliberate, each motion priced in advance — but her face was round and unlined, maybe twenty years old. A man splitting firewood had a boy's smooth cheeks and hands that shook between strokes. An old man sat on a bench outside the first house, and he was the only genuinely old person I'd seen, and even he looked wrong somehow, like a painting hung slightly crooked.

They all turned to look at us when we came down the lane. Not hostile. Worse than hostile: *hopeful*. They looked at us the way starving men look at a full table.

"You'll be wanting lodging," the old man on the bench said. His voice was gravel and patience. "Maren keeps the inn. Tell her Tam sent you." He paused, and his eyes — old eyes in a face that couldn't decide how old it was — flicked to Wren. "And tell her they'll want paying. Everything here wants paying."

"I'm familiar with the principle," I said.

"Mm." He smiled without much humor. "You've got a guild coat under that travel cloak. I can smell the vellum on you. So you know better than most: nothing is free, boy. Some places just make you count it out loud."

I did have a guild coat under my cloak. I also had a guild brand on my forearm and a guild oath in my mouth, and I was increasingly unsure which of those I regretted most. "We're not here on guild business," I said, which was true in the way that a knife isn't technically a murder until someone picks it up.

"No," Tam agreed mildly. "But business finds people like you anyway. It always has a nose for trouble." He gestured with his chin toward the inn. "Go on. And mind the girl doesn't touch anyone."

That stopped me cold. "Why?"

Tam's smile faded into something older and sadder. "Because some of us sold more than we meant to," he said, "and it's rude to remind a man of his own price."

* * *

Maren kept the inn the way a fortress keeps its gates — efficiently, and with visible suspicion of everyone who approached. She was perhaps thirty by her face and seventy by her posture, and when she counted out our room's price she did it in a whisper, sliding coins across the bar like a dealer in stolen goods.

"Two nights, bread, stew, water that won't kill you," she said. "Twelve coppers."

I put the coins down one at a time, keeping our special copper well away from the pile. Maren watched me do it and something in her face eased.

"Not selling anything tonight?" she asked.

"Do I look like a peddler?"

"You look like a man deciding whether to run." She took the coins. "The ones who come through here either sell or run. There's no third door." Then her eyes went to Wren, who was staring at the stew pot with naked longing, and Maren's whole manner changed — softened, cracked open like ice on a spring river. "Gods below. When did you eat last?"

"Yesterday," Wren said. "Mostly."

Maren filled two bowls without another word about payment, set them down hard enough to slosh, and stood over us while we ate as if guarding the meal against bandits. The stew was barley and turnip and something I chose not to identify, and it was the finest food I had ever tasted, or possibly I was just hungry. Hunger makes an honest critic.

The common room filled slowly around us as the evening wore on. Farmers, mostly, and a smith, and a woman who might have been a midwife. All young faces. All old movements. I watched a girl of perhaps sixteen carry a tray with the stooped caution of a woman of sixty, and I watched her set it down and rest her hand on the small of her back like the weight of years lived there.

"What happened to this place?" I asked Maren quietly.

She didn't pretend not to understand. "The blight came, six years back. Crops, then cattle, then the wells. People were dying slow, and slow dying is expensive." She wiped the bar with a rag that had seen more honest days. "Then a trader came through. Polite fellow. Soft voice. Offered to buy."

"Buy what?"

Maren's laugh had no floor under it. "What do you think? Years. Ten off the front of your life, paid out in grain and gold enough to replant. Everyone signed. Everyone." Her eyes went to the girl with the tray. "Liss gave fifteen. She was eleven. Do the arithmetic on what she has left and then don't, because none of us can bear to."

"And the trader?" I asked, though I already knew. You know a shape by its shadow. "Was he masked?"

"No mask. But he had ink on his fingers," Maren said, "and paper that never tore, and when he left, the road behind him smelled like a library." She leaned close. "You know the guild. Don't lie to me, I can smell the vellum. So tell me true: is what we signed real? Is it binding? Can it be undone?"

Every instinct I had said *lie*, because the truth was: yes, it's binding; yes, the Vellum Guild trades in borrowed years the way other merchants trade in salt; and no, in seven years of keeping their ledgers I had never once seen an entry reversed. But Wren was watching me with stew on her chin, and lying in front of her felt suddenly like a thing I could no longer afford.

"It's binding," I said. "As far as I know, it can't be undone."

Maren nodded slowly, unsurprised, and went back to her rag. Hope, I've learned, is a currency too, and these people had learned not to spend it.

* * *

It was later — the fire burned low, the room gone quiet except for Liss sweeping ashes — that everything changed.

Wren had fallen asleep against my side, and I was half-drowsing myself, when Liss stumbled near our bench. Her foot caught the hearthstone. The tray of dregs she carried went flying, and she pitched forward, and I reached out too late, and Wren — waking, confused, arms flung out to catch herself or catch Liss, I'll never know which — *touched her*.

Palm to wrist. Bare skin to bare skin.

The light that came off them wasn't light exactly. It was more like remembering — the visual form of remembering, gold and soft and moving backward, like smoke returning to a candle. It poured from Wren's small hand into Liss's arm and I heard Liss gasp, heard the sound of a body being *corrected*, and then it was done, and the ash tray lay broken on the floor, and Liss stood there straight-backed and blinking with tears running down her sixteen-year-old face.

"My back," she whispered. "My back doesn't hurt. My knees—" She pressed her hands to her own face like a blind woman reading it for the first time. "Five years. I got five years back. I *felt* them come home."

The room had gone absolutely silent. Every borrowed face was turned toward us, and every pair of eyes held the same expression, and I recognized it because I'd seen it on the road into town: hope. But this time it was hope with teeth.

Wren looked up at me, still sleep-soft, utterly unaware of what she'd done. "Her years were sad," she said simply. "They wanted to go back to her."

"Oh, child," I breathed, and pulled her close, and scanned the room over her head like a man counting exits.

Because here is what I knew, in the marrow the guild built me from: power like that does not go unnoticed. Power like that gets *ledgered*. Somewhere in the vaults beneath the guildhouse there was a shelf waiting for this exact eventuality, and somewhere beneath the mountain there was a court full of beautiful predators who had already sold this child's soul once and would crawl across broken glass to sell it again — or harvest it outright — once word spread that the soul itself could give back what had been taken.

Maren appeared at my elbow. Her face had gone carefully blank, the blankness of a woman doing rapid and dangerous arithmetic.

"That's a valuable girl you have," she said quietly.

"She's not a *valuable*," I said, harder than I meant to. "She's a child."

"In this village, everything valuable gets sold eventually. That's the lesson six years taught us." Maren glanced at Liss, who was laughing now, actually laughing, touching her own straight spine like a miracle — and it *was* a miracle, and miracles are just transactions nobody's negotiated yet. "You should leave at first light. Before word travels. Because it will travel. It always travels faster than you."

She was right, of course. And I was already reaching for my purse to pay for the broken tray, already calculating roads, already doing the grim mathematics of a fugitive — when the inn door opened and the cold came in with it.

A rider stood in the doorway, snow-dusted despite there being no snow, mud-spattered despite there being no rain. He looked at no one else in the room. He looked at Wren, and then at me, and he smiled with a great many teeth.

"Beg pardon," he said pleasantly. "I'm looking for a man who used to keep ledgers, and a little girl who speaks the old tongue. My employers miss them terribly."

His fingers, I noticed, were stained with ink.
