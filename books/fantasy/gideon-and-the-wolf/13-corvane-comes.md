## 13. Corvane Comes

The trouble with running from the Vellum Guild is that they keep excellent records.

I'd spent seven years among those ledgers. I knew what they wrote down: every debt, every default, every soul sold on a Tuesday and resold by Friday. Somewhere in a vault beneath the guildhouse there was a page with my name on it — or whatever name I'd signed before I was Gideon Winterbourne — and somewhere behind us, men were reading it aloud to each other like a recipe.

"You're doing the face again," Wren said.

"What face?"

"The one where you look like you swallowed a ledger."

She was walking backward ahead of me on the forest road, which is a thing eight-year-olds do because they have never once considered that the world contains consequences. The twelfth moon hung low and bruised overhead, its light gone thin as watered wine. Three nights until it turned. Three nights until the thirteenth moon rose over the mountain and whatever bargain had been struck in her name came due.

"I'm thinking," I said.

"Thinking looks different."

"Does it."

"Yes. Thinking you look sad. That face you look like someone's about to be hurt." She turned forward again, hands jammed in the pockets of the coat I'd bought her in the village of borrowed years, three days and one miracle ago. "Is it me?"

"No," I said, and meant it. "It's me."

That was the honest answer, and honesty was becoming a habit I didn't trust. For seven years I had been a simple man with a simple shape: mercenary turned clerk, sword hung up, oath sworn to a god who never answered, ledgers balanced nightly to the penny. A man could live inside a shape like that the way a coin lives inside a purse — safe, counted, going nowhere. But since the night Wren knocked on the guildhouse door, the purse had been emptying. Every mile toward the mountain shook something loose. My hands remembered things my mind did not. In the village, when the retriever's dog had lunged at Wren, I'd moved before thought — not like a clerk, not even like a soldier, but like something older and colder, something that had done terrible arithmetic with other people's lives and found the sums acceptable.

I was afraid of what was following us. But I was more afraid of what was walking beside it, wearing my boots.

* * *

We made camp in the ruin of a shepherd's cot, walls waist-high, roof long surrendered to the crows. Wren slept curled against the fire like a comma, and I sat up with my back to stone and watched the dark between the trees and did not sleep, because sleep lately meant dreams of towers and fires and a man signing his name in blood, and I'd had enough of other people's memories for one lifetime.

The birds went quiet first.

Not gradually — all at once, the way they do when something passes through the wood that the wood itself fears. Then the crickets. Then the wind, as if the whole night were holding its breath around a held breath.

"Wren." I put my hand on her shoulder and she woke the way she always woke — instantly, silently, eyes open and already looking at me. Whatever palace she'd been born in, whoever she'd been before adults sold her name, someone had taught her to wake like a soldier. Or she'd learned it after. I tried not to think about what waking like that cost a child. "Company."

"How many?"

"One," I said, listening. "Maybe two. Good ones."

"Good ones are bad ones," she said, which was the sort of proverb Quill would have charged money for.

I doused the fire with a sweep of my boot and took up the short sword I'd carried out of the guildhouse — guild issue, plain steel, the kind of weapon designed for closing accounts rather than opening conversations. It sat wrong in my hand. It had sat wrong for seven years, ever since I'd sworn my oath and hung up the trade. Tonight it sat like it had been cast from my own wrist-bone.

He came out of the trees without sound, which should be impossible for a man his size. Corvane stood better than six feet, built like something the guild had commissioned rather than born, his face the kind of handsome that has been maintained rather than lived in. He wore no guild colors. He wore nothing at all that would identify him, which identified him completely.

"Master Winterbourne." His voice was pleasant. That was the worst part. Corvane's voice was the voice of a man reading terms and conditions aloud at your bedside. "You've been difficult."

"Occupational hazard of having a conscience."

"Is that what it is?" He tilted his head, genuinely curious, or performing genuine curiosity so well there was no difference. "Sarelle said conscience. I said vanity. We had a small wager."

"And here I don't even get to collect if I win."

"You won't win." He stepped into the ruined doorway, filling it, moonlight failing to find anything on him worth glinting off. "Give me the girl. Walk back with me. Sarelle will plead mitigation; the fine will be ruinous but survivable. You'll balance ledgers until your eyes fail, and this will become one more entry neither of us speaks of."

"And if I say no?"

"Then I do the thing I'm actually here for." He sighed, and it was almost tender. "I'd hoped to avoid it. You're expensive, Winterbourne. Do you know what your contract specifies? What the guild paid for you? The retrieval clause alone—"

"Contract." The word came out of me flat and cold, and I heard — distantly, as if from another room — how flat and cold it was. "What contract."

Corvane smiled. And then he moved.

* * *

Here is what I remember of the fight, though *remember* is too tidy a word for it. Remembering implies I was present in any way that mattered.

He came fast for a big man, low, leading with a weighted chain he'd had coiled somewhere under that anonymous cloak, and the chain sang toward my knee with the specific intent of putting me down without killing me — retrievers want their assets breathing, both of them, though I suspected only one of us was still on the manifest as an asset. And my body answered. Not clumsily, not rustily, not the way a man answers who hasn't fought in seven years. My body answered the way a lock answers its key.

I was inside the chain's arc before it finished singing. My left hand caught it — caught a moving weighted chain barehanded, wrapped it twice around my forearm where the leather bracer ate its bite — and my right hand drove the pommel of the short sword up under his jaw, not hard enough to break it, exactly hard enough to make the next two seconds his problem instead of mine. He staggered. I turned with him, using the chain's own tension, and put him into the wall of the shepherd's cot hard enough to crack stones that had survived two hundred years of weather.

And through all of it, some quiet part of me that had no name was *narrating*. Not in words. In certainties. *His left side is weak — old injury, below the ribs, favor it.* *He telegraphs the second throw with his shoulder.* *He's holding back; he wants you alive; use that.* Each certainty arrived complete, pre-installed, like furniture in a house I'd never entered. I knew things about fighting Corvane that I had no way of knowing, the way I'd known the sigil on Wren's copper coin, the way I'd known the phrase in her dead language before she taught it to me.

Somewhere underneath, the man named Gideon Winterbourne — clerk, penitent, keeper of a silent god — was screaming at all of it to stop.

Because here was the horror, the true horror, the one I'd been outrunning since the toll bridge: it wasn't that I remembered how to fight. It was that fighting felt like *homecoming*. My body settled into the violence the way a tired traveler settles into his own bed. There was no fear in it. There was no effort. There was only a vast, familiar competence, and beneath the competence a pleasure so old and worn-in it barely registered as pleasure anymore — the way a drunk doesn't taste the drink, just the arrival.

Corvane got an arm free and hit me across the ear and the world rang white. I went down. He was on me, knee on my chest, chain looped for the throat, and his pleasant voice was in my ear, breathless now, less pleasant:

"Just the girl, Winterbourne. She's not yours. None of this is yours. You're a made thing playing at being a man, and we both know who made you—"

I broke his grip. I don't know how. Later, examining my memory of it, I found the gap where the technique should have been — a blank filled with result, like a ledger line where the sum appears without the working. My elbow found his throat. My thumb found the nerve behind his ear. And then I had the chain, and I had him face-down in the ashes of our fire, and I had the short sword's point resting in the soft place below his jaw where a man keeps everything he needs to keep living.

I could feel the moment balanced on the point. One ounce more pressure. The guild would write him off as a retrieval loss. No one would ask questions. Questions cost money, and the Vellum Guild hated spending.

"Do it," Corvane said. He wasn't afraid. That was somehow worse than everything else. "You want to know why I'm not afraid? Because I've read your file, Winterbourne. All of it. And men like you don't kill men like me. Men like you *used* to. Before. Ask yourself what happened seven years ago. Ask yourself why a god that never answers still gets your prayers. Ask yourself—" and here he laughed, blood on his teeth, delighted, "—ask yourself why you can't remember your mother's face, and whose fault you think that is."

The bridge-keeper. The toll I'd paid without flinching, the memory I'd sold like small change. He knew. They all knew. They'd known what the road would cost me and they'd let me pay it, because every piece of myself I spent made me easier to herd.

"You signed with us," Corvane said, "long before you swore to your god."

The words went in deeper than the sword would have.

I knelt there with his life under my blade and I searched myself for the man who would press down — and I couldn't find him, and that frightened me most of all, because I also couldn't find proof he wasn't there. Only a silence where the certainty should have been. The same silence my god kept. Maybe it was the same silence. Maybe it had been the same silence all along, watching, waiting, keeping its ledger of me.

Wren's hand touched my shoulder.

I hadn't heard her cross the ruin. She looked at me — at the blade, at Corvane, at whatever was happening in my face — with those old, old eyes, and she said one word in her language. The one from the road. The one that had cracked open the tower and the fire and the man signing in blood.

*Remember.*

"Not like this," I said. I don't know if I said it to her or to myself or to the god keeping its patient silence above us. I lifted the blade from Corvane's throat.

Then I hit him with the pommel, once, precisely, in the temple — a blow calculated by whatever lived in my hands to keep him unconscious until well past dawn — and bound him with his own chain to the one standing wall of the shepherd's cot, thorough knots, knots my fingers tied without consulting me.

"That was mercy," Corvane mumbled, already sliding under. "Sarelle won't... the next one won't..."

"The next one can bill me," I said.

* * *

We walked out of the wood under the failing light of the twelfth moon, and I carried the shaking with me like a passenger.

It hit about a mile out, the way it always does — after, never during. My knees wanted the ground. My stomach wanted to be rid of itself. Wren walked close without touching me, the way you walk near a spooked horse, and finally she said, "You were going to kill him."

"Yes."

"But you didn't."

"No."

"Why?"

And I gave her the truest answer I had, which was no answer at all: "I don't know yet whether it was because of who I am, or who I'm afraid I used to be."

She considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. "Both, probably," she said. "Grown-ups are usually both."

Ahead of us, far to the north, the mountain stood black against the stars, and above its peak — impossibly early, impossibly thin, a sliver of light where no sliver should be — I saw the first edge of the thirteenth moon beginning to rise.

Two nights. Maybe less.

And somewhere behind us, in the dark we'd left, Corvane's warning echoed in his pleasant dying voice: *the next one won't.*
