## 14. The Name Under the Skin

I had been a broker of names for nineteen years, and in that time I learned one iron law: every person is a document. Some are well-written. Some are forgeries. Most are drafts that never got finished. I made my living reading the marginalia.

So when a copper coin stamped with the under-court's sigil landed on my table — carried, if the rumors were true, by a mercenary clerk with a silent god and a stolen child — I did what any sensible exile would do.

I packed a bag.

Not because I wanted to help. Let's be clear about that. I was exiled from the Vellum Guild for the crime of being right at an inconvenient volume, and my affection for its employees runs somewhere between indigestion and old scar tissue. But I know what that coin means. I minted appraisals for three of them before they burned my office and my reputation along with it. A coin like that doesn't buy bread. It buys *people*. And when a coin like that surfaces in the world above ground, it means somebody down below has opened a ledger they'd promised to keep shut.

Somebody always opens it eventually. That's the trouble with ledgers. They itch.

* * *

I found them at a crossroads shrine two days out from the village of borrowed years — I'd followed the trail of small miracles and large lies, which is how you track anything worth tracking. The mercenary sat sharpening a knife he clearly didn't need to sharpen, the way men do when their hands want work and their heads want silence. The girl slept against his pack, breathing in the deep, even rhythm of someone who has finally stopped running.

He heard me coming, of course. Good instincts. He came up off the log with the knife low and his weight forward, and for half a heartbeat I saw what Corvane must have seen in the fight at the village: not a clerk. Something older wearing a clerk's coat.

"Quill," I said, raising both hands, palms out, the universal gesture of the harmless. "Formerly of the Vellum Guild. Currently of nowhere, which has excellent rent."

"I know who you are."

"You do?" That surprised me genuinely. My name had been scraped from the guild rolls so thoroughly that even I sometimes doubted I'd existed. "Then you're better informed than your employers would like."

"Sarelle mentioned you." He didn't lower the knife. "She said you were a liar with good penmanship."

"She flatters me. The penmanship was excellent." I let my eyes flick to the girl, then back. "Is she well?"

"Why do you care?"

"Because I helped write the contract that sold her, and I've spent nine years trying to drink away the memory, and neither effort succeeded." I said it flatly, without decoration, because decoration is how liars dress up truths they're ashamed of, and I have never been able to afford shame's wardrobe. "May I sit? I've walked a long way to ruin your week, and I'd rather do it comfortably."

He looked at me for a long moment. Then he sheathed the knife and gestured at the fire with something that wasn't quite hospitality but had stopped short of murder, which in my experience is as warm as mercenaries get.

* * *

I laid it out for him the way I used to lay out contracts for clients: plainly, in order, with the worst part last. It's a professional habit. You build the scaffolding before you drop the house.

"The coin," I began. "Court currency, under-mint, struck for transactions involving souls. Not metaphorically. There are exactly four things you can buy beneath the mountain, and three of them are versions of death. Your guild holds collateral against Wren — that much you know. What you don't know is the chain of title."

"There's a chain?"

"There is always a chain. Nothing in this world is owned outright; everything is held against something else. Souls especially. A soul is the only collateral that appreciates." I fed a stick to the fire. "Wren's soul was sold once — by adults in a palace who thought they were saving a kingdom, which is what cowards tell themselves when they're spending children. It went down the mountain. Then, six years ago, it was resold upward. As collateral. To the Vellum Guild."

His face did something complicated. I watched him arrive at it. "The guild owns her soul."

"The guild *holds* her soul. Ownership implies you get to enjoy it. The guild just sits on things until they ripen or rot." I took a breath. Here came the house. "But here's what kept me walking instead of sleeping, Gideon Winterbourne. Resales require a counterparty. Someone had to stand surety on the guild's side. Someone whose signature the under-court would accept without question. And there is exactly one signature in the Vellum Guild's entire archive that the Pale Regent honors on sight."

The fire popped. The girl stirred and settled.

"No," he said.

"Yes."

"That's not possible. I've been with the guild seven years. I signed on as a—"

"As a mercenary clerk. Yes. I've read your intake contract. Or rather, I read a copy of it, before they burned my office, and I noticed something that has been sitting in my chest like a swallowed stone ever since." I reached into my coat and drew out the thing I had walked through two storms and one very unfriendly forest to deliver. "Your intake contract is countersigned. Both parties. Yours and the guild's. Standard practice. Except."

I handed him the paper. He took it the way men take verdicts.

"Except your signature on that contract is dated," I said, "and the ink is *older than the date*. Whoever wrote 'Gideon Winterbourne' at the bottom of that page wrote it years before the page existed. Which means either the guild forged your signature with ink they aged deliberately — possible, but expensive and pointless — or—"

"Or I signed it before I remember signing it."

"Or," I said gently, because some houses deserve a gentle demolition, "you never signed it at all. Because you never existed."

* * *

He was quiet for a long time. I let him be quiet. This is another professional habit: after the worst news, silence is the only courtesy left that isn't a lie.

The girl woke during it. She looked at him, then at me, then at the paper in his hands, and she said something in that old language — soft, questioning. He answered her in it. Badly, with a traveler's accent, but he answered, and I confess the hair on my arms stood up, because I recognized perhaps one word in five and the word I recognized was *name*.

"He says—" she began in broken common, then stopped, frowning at the difficulty of it. "He asks. Is it true. That he is... written."

"It's true that he's *written about*," I said. "Whether he's written is the interesting question."

She considered this with the terrible seriousness of children, who have not yet learned to look away from hard things. Then she crawled over and put her hand flat on the paper, over the signature, the way you'd touch a wound to see if it still hurt.

"This is not his hand," she announced.

Gideon stared at her. "You can read signatures?"

"I can read *names*." She tapped the ink. "Names have weight. This one is heavy. Like it was carried a long way before it was put down here." She looked up at him, and whatever she saw in his face made her voice go smaller. "It is your name. But it is tired. Like it has been used many times before you."

"*Before* me," he repeated.

I leaned forward. "That's the other thing I brought. Sit down, Winterbourne. Actually — you're already sitting. Good. One less collapse to manage."

I took the paper back and smoothed it on my knee. "Seven years ago, a man named Gideon Winterbourne appears in the world with a soldier's scars, a mercenary's skills, and no history anyone can verify. No family. No debts — which, believe me, is more suspicious than debts. I pulled what records survived my disgrace. Do you know what I found?"

"Tell me."

"A hole. Not an absence — absences leave edges, neighbors, rumor. A *hole*. Records that should mention you simply don't. Parish rolls with pages missing. Muster lists with one line scraped clean. Even the god you swear to — what's his name, the Quiet One, the Listener — even his shrines show offerings in your hand going back exactly seven years and not one day further." I met his eyes. "People aren't born like that, Winterbourne. People are *messy*. You are immaculate. You are the cleanest piece of writing I have ever encountered, and I once catalogued a contract written on the inside of a dead man's skin."

"So what am I?" His voice was level. Too level. The levelness of a man standing on a ledge deciding whether the drop is worse than the staying. "A construct? The guild built me? Their god built me? A weapon? Collateral? Pick one, Quill, you're the expert."

"I'm the expert, so I'll tell you the truth experts hate: I don't know. The signature predates the contract. The Regent honors it. Those two facts together suggest you were made *for* this — for the bargain, for the child, for the mountain. Whether the guild made you or the god made you depends on which of them you think is capable of writing a man into the world." I shrugged. "For what it's worth, the guild can barely write a legible invoice."

"And the god hasn't spoken in years."

"Hasn't spoken to *you*, maybe. There's a difference between silence and absence. Ask any widow."

That landed harder than I intended. He looked at the fire for a while, and the girl moved closer to him, and I pretended to study my own boots and gave them the privacy of my bad eyesight.

* * *

Here is the thing I didn't say by the fire, because it wasn't yet the hour for it. I'll say it now, to this page, where no one blushes.

I have seen constructed people before. The guild made three, in my time — little ones, partial ones, a courier with no childhood and a witness with no fear. They worked. They functioned. And every one of them, given time, grew something the makers hadn't installed. The courier developed a fondness for rain. The witness taught herself to hum. Small rebellions of self, sprouting in the margins like weeds through flagstones.

A made self, in my long experience, becomes a real self the way a made bed becomes a slept-in one. The making is not the point. The lying-down is the point. The dreaming.

But I also know what it is to suspect your own pages were written by someone else's hand. I signed contracts for nineteen years that ruined strangers, and I told myself I was only the pen. The pen. What a comfortable word. What a coward's word. So when I watched Gideon Winterbourne sit with the knowledge that he might be nothing but ink, I did not offer him comfort, because comfort from me would have been hypocrisy with good posture.

What I offered instead was the paper.

"Keep it," I said, when he tried to hand the intake contract back. "Proof of forgery is still proof. If your name was written by another hand, then whoever holds the pen holds you — unless you take it. And there's a version of events, Winterbourne, where taking it back matters more than anything else on this road."

"What do you mean?"

"I mean the Regent honors your signature. Think about what that means. Think about what could be *signed* with it, down there, that couldn't be signed by anyone else." I let him arrive at it himself. He was quick. Clerks usually are; it's the honest ones who are slowest, because they check their work.

His face went pale in the firelight. "My name could close the contract. Whatever the original bargain was — my signature is the seal."

"Which is why you were made, I'd wager. Or why you were *kept*. A key that walks around and carries itself to the lock. Very economical. Very guild." I stood, joints cracking like green wood, and shouldered my bag. "So. Two roads from this fire. You can turn around, go home, and spend the rest of your life being a well-kept entry in someone else's book. Or you can walk down that mountain and find out what your name is *worth* — and to whom — and make them regret the price."

The girl tugged his sleeve and said something in the old tongue. He answered. I caught the word for *mountain* and the word for *together*, and something in my chest that I'd assumed was scar tissue turned out, inconveniently, to still be a heart.

"You're coming with us, then," Gideon said. It wasn't quite a question.

"I'm coming with you," I agreed, "because I burned my bridges with such thoroughness that the ashes are the only home I have left, and because someone on this road needs to read the fine print before you all sign your lives away. Again. In your case, possibly for the second time."

We broke camp in the grey hour before dawn. The twelfth moon hung low and thin over the eastern hills, its light gone the color of old vellum, and beyond it, far off, the mountain stood black against the stars — patient as a creditor.

As we walked, the girl fell into step beside me and asked, in her careful, broken common, whether it was true that a man could be written.

"Everything is written," I told her. "The question is who holds the pen next."

She thought about that for a hundred paces. Then she said, "He does. Now." And nodded toward Gideon, walking ahead of us into the failing light, a man carrying proof of his own nonexistence folded against his heart like a letter from home.

Behind us, far behind, on the road we'd taken, I heard — or thought I heard — hoofbeats. Many. Moving fast.

Sarelle had made her choice, then. I only prayed to a god I didn't believe in that she'd chosen the same side of the mountain we had.
