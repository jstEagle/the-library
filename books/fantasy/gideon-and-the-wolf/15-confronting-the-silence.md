## 15. Confronting the Silence

I had been praying to a dead god for seven years, and I want to be clear about something: I was very good at it.

Seven years of kneeling on cold stone before dawn, palms flat, head bowed, speaking into an absence the way a man speaks into a well and waits for his own voice to come back changed. Seven years of tithes and vigils and the small daily surrenders that my oath demanded — no lies spoken aloud, no coin kept past need, no name given lightly. The Vellum Guild had found me useful precisely because of it. A mercenary who answers to a god nobody's heard from in living memory is a mercenary who can be trusted with impossible inventory. Who would he tell? Who would believe him?

So when Quill's words were still rattling around inside me like dice in a cup — *Gideon Winterbourne never existed before seven years ago* — there was really only one place left to go. Not for comfort. I'd stopped expecting comfort somewhere around year three. I went for answers, and if the god wouldn't give them, then at least I could stand in one of its houses and shout at the furniture.

The shrine sat two days off the road to the mountain, up a goat track that Wren climbed with the sure-footedness of someone who had been walking these hills since before the hills had names. She hadn't wanted to stop. Every hour we lost, she reminded me without words — a tug on my sleeve, a look toward the peak where the thirteenth moon would soon complete its turn — that time was spending itself faster than we were.

"One night," I told her. "One night, and then we walk."

She studied me with those too-old eyes, then touched her own chest, then pointed at mine.

"I know," I said. "It's about me. That's the problem."

* * *

The shrine had been grand once. You could see it in the bones of the place: columns that some mason had spent a decade fluting by hand, a floor of pale stone worn into a shallow bowl by centuries of knees, an altar carved from a single slab of black granite that no cart in any kingdom could have hauled up this mountain. Now the roof was mostly sky. Moss had taken the columns. Someone — pilgrims, maybe, or just weather — had toppled the statue in the apse, and all that remained of whatever face my god had worn was a pair of feet and the hem of a robe, rendered in such loving detail that even the sandal-straps survived.

Wren walked the length of the ruined nave slowly, trailing her fingers along the fallen stones, and I watched her do it and felt something cold slide down my spine. Because she wasn't reading them. She was *reading* them. Her lips moved. Little sounds came out of her, half-voiced, the way a literate person murmurs when their eyes move down a page.

"What does it say?" I asked.

She looked up at me, and her expression did something complicated — pity and frustration sharing a small face — and she pressed her palm flat against a column and said a word. Just one. It sounded like water going over stones, like wind finding the gap under a door.

Then she pointed at me. And said it again.

"Don't," I said. "Don't do that. I'm not ready for whatever that is."

She rolled her eyes at me. Eight years old, soul sold twice over, hunted by an enforcer who could fold a man in half, and she still found room to roll her eyes at me. I decided, not for the first time, that whoever she had been before the adults got hold of her, they had made something remarkable and then thrown it away.

We made camp in what had been the priest's cell, a low vaulted room that still had half a roof, and I built a fire while she curled up on my bedroll like a cat deciding the territory was acceptable. She was asleep before the wood caught properly. Children sleep like that when they trust you, all at once, the whole body surrendering — and I stood there holding kindling and understood that she trusted me completely, and that this was now the single most important fact of my life.

I waited until her breathing went long and even. Then I took the copper coin out of my shirt, where it had lived against my skin since the night she pressed it into my hand, and I walked out into the nave alone.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about demanding things of gods: the architecture works against you. The bowl of that floor had been worn by ten thousand kneeling worshippers, and when I stood in its center, every line of the place — the columns, the broken apse, the black altar — converged on the spot where I stood, so that refusing to kneel felt less like defiance and more like standing in the exact center of a target.

Fine. I knelt. But I didn't bow my head, and I didn't close my eyes, and I didn't start soft.

"No," I said, and my voice went up into the roofless dark and came back to me off the far wall, thinner than it should have been. "No more of this. Seven years. Seven years of silence, and I gave you everything you asked for. I kept your oath when keeping it cost me. I kept it when it cost other people. I knelt in the rain and the snow and the dust, and I never once asked why you weren't answering, because I told myself faith doesn't ask."

The mountain wind moved through the broken columns and made a sound like breathing.

"Well, I'm asking now." I got to my feet. My knees ached. Everything ached; Corvane had left marks I was still discovering. "You want to know what I learned this week? I learned that I am not a man. I'm a ledger entry. Somebody wrote me. Seven years ago, right around the time I started hearing nothing from you — funny coincidence, that — somebody opened a book and put Gideon Winterbourne on a page, and here I came, walking around, swearing oaths, keeping ledgers, being *useful*. So either you made me, or the guild made me, or something older than both made me, and whichever it was—" My voice cracked, and I hated it, and I pushed through anyway. "Whichever it was, you owe me the truth. You owe me that much. I don't even care if it's bad. Do you understand? I'll take bad. I'll take terrible. I will take the worst thing you know about me over one more night of this."

Silence.

Of course silence. There was always silence. I had built a whole life on top of that silence, furnished it, hung curtains in it.

I laughed, and it came out wrong. "Right. Yes. Thank you. Very instructive."

I turned to go, and that was when Wren screamed.

Not a nightmare scream — I knew those by now, the small choked sounds she made most nights, the palace burning behind her eyes. This was different. This was awake and terrified, and it came from the priest's cell, and I covered the distance between us in maybe four strides with my knife already out because apparently my body had opinions about protecting this child that bypassed my mind entirely.

She was sitting bolt upright, staring past me, through me, at the doorway of the cell. Her hands were moving fast, shaping words in that old language, words tumbling over each other, and her eyes were wide and wet and fixed on something six inches to the left of my heart.

"Wren. Wren, look at me. What is it? What's—"

She grabbed my wrist. Her grip was shockingly strong. She pulled my hand up, pressed it flat against my own chest, over the coin, and held it there, and then she said the word again — the same word she'd said to the column, the word like water over stones — but slower this time, deliberate, syllable by syllable, the way you speak to someone learning.

And the coin burned.

Not hot. Not painful. It was more like the moment when a numb limb wakes — a flood of feeling rushing back into something that had gone quiet so long ago you'd forgotten it was ever supposed to feel like anything. The coin pulsed against my palm, once, twice, in a rhythm I recognized with a certainty that arrived from nowhere and everywhere at once:

A heartbeat.

And then the silence — seven years of silence, the great furnished silence of my life — broke.

It didn't come as a voice. I want to be honest about that, because I've heard men describe god-voices and I never believed them, and I still don't quite believe what happened to me. It came as *meaning*, arriving whole, the way you sometimes wake already knowing something. One word, in Wren's language, in the language of the fallen stones and the old hills, a word I had never been taught and understood perfectly:

*Remember.*

That was all. Remember. Not *you are forgiven*, not *here is your purpose*, not even *I am here*. Remember.

And underneath it, carried in the word like silt in a river, came something else — not words, but weight. The sense of a vast patience. Of attention that had never once wavered, not through seven years of vigils, not through a single dawn on cold stone. The feeling of standing in a locked room for years believing yourself abandoned, and then having the walls speak and say: *we were the walls the whole time. We were holding the roof up.*

"You were waiting," I said aloud, and my voice shook, and I didn't care. "You were watching me the entire time. All those mornings. You were *there*."

The meaning came again, gentler now, almost amused, in the way of old teachers: *Remember.*

"Why?" And here was the anger, finally, the real anger, seven years of it coming up out of the floor of me. "Why make me wait? Why let me kneel to a silence? Why let me think—" I stopped. Swallowed. Started again, quieter. "Why let me think I was alone?"

The answer, when it came, was not kind, but it was true, and I have since learned that this is the only currency my god deals in.

*Because you were not ready. Because a made thing must choose itself before it can be anything else. Because if I had told you what you are, you would have believed it was assigned to you — and a self that is assigned can be reassigned. It had to grow from the inside, Gideon. It had to become yours. And look—*

And the meaning turned, and I felt it looking with me, back down the seven years, and what it showed me was not the vigils. It showed me Wren asleep on my bedroll. It showed me hiding the coin from Sarelle, my first lie, my first treason, my heart choosing a child over my employers before my head even knew why. It showed me giving my mother's face to the bridge-keeper and walking on with a hole in me shaped like love, because a child needed crossing more than I needed remembering.

*I did not make you faithful,* the god said, or meant, or was. *I watched you become it.*

I sat down on the cold floor of my god's ruined house and I wept like a child, and Wren — awake, watchful, having witnessed the entire conversation from her side of it — crawled over and leaned against me, and stayed there, and let me.

* * *

Later, near dawn, we sat in the firelight in the priest's cell and I tried to explain, which was absurd, because how do you explain a word to the girl who gave it to you?

"It said remember," I told her. "Your word. The one you keep trying to give me. It said it back."

She nodded slowly, unsurprised, and then she did something new. She took my hand, turned it palm-up, and traced on it with one finger — deliberately, teaching-shapes, the way she'd begun doing on the road. A spiral. A line through it. Two marks below like feet.

"Remember," I repeated, and the shapes meant it, I could feel them meaning it, the way the coin had meant heartbeat.

She nodded again, fierce, and then pointed at herself, and then at the mountain visible through the broken roof, black against the graying sky. Then she held up her fingers. Thirteen. Folded them down one by one until three remained.

Three moons. Maybe less, the way the light was failing.

"We'll make it," I said. "I don't know how yet. But we'll make it."

And then, because the word was still sitting in me, still working its way down through the layers like rain into dry ground, I said: "There's something else. Something it showed me. When I remember — when I finally get all of it back — I think I'm going to find out who signed me into existence. And I think—" I looked at her, at this child whose soul had been traded like a sack of grain by people who should have died for her instead. "I think the ones who made me and the ones who sold you are the same people. Or the same *thing*. The contract Quill talked about, the original one. I think my name is written on it."

Wren went very still. Then she reached out and took the copper coin from my open palm, held it up between us so the firelight moved across the sigil stamped into it, and pressed it back into my hand, closing my fingers over it with both of hers.

She said one more word. Not *remember*. Something shorter, harder, with an edge to it, like a blade being drawn.

I didn't know that one yet.

But I understood it anyway, the way you understand a drawn blade, and when I looked at the eastern sky through the broken roof — gray now, then gold, the last moons fading as the sun came up over the road to the mountain — I found that the fear was still there, and the grief, and the hole where my mother's face used to be.

But underneath all of it, for the first time in seven years, there was solid ground.

My god had not abandoned me. My god had been waiting for me to be worth answering.

"Get your boots on," I told Wren, and my voice came out steady, and it was mine, whoever owned the rest of me. "We've got a mountain to climb, a court to rob, and a contract with my name on it to burn."

She grinned at me — quick, fierce, princely, eight years old — and laced her boots.

We were on the road before full light.
