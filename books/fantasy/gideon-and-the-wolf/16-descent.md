## 16. Descent

*Gideon*

The door in the mountainside was exactly where Quill's map said it would be, which was the first thing wrong with it.

Maps, in my experience, are instruments of optimism. They promise that the world is knowable, that distances behave themselves, that a dotted line means a path rather than an invitation to die somewhere picturesque. This one had been drawn on the back of a debt notice — Quill's idea of poetry, apparently — and it showed a crack in the rock above a frozen waterfall, with a single annotation in his cramped hand: *It opens for what it is owed. Don't knock. Knocking implies you think you might not belong.*

So we stood in the blue shadow of the mountain while the twelfth moon thinned overhead like a coin worn smooth by too many hands, and I did not knock.

"Maybe it's shy," Wren offered.

She had taken to speaking more on the road, her common tongue still arriving in pieces, words stacked in the wrong order like a child's tower of river stones. *Shy,* she had learned from me, along with several other things a ward of mine should probably not learn yet. She was eight years old and carried a prince's soul in her small chest, and she was eating a heel of bread with the concentration of someone defusing a bomb.

"It's a door," I said. "Doors aren't shy."

"The guild doors were shy."

"The guild doors were *locked*, and you picked them open with a hairpin, which is a different thing entirely." I crouched by the crack in the rock. It wasn't much to look at — a seam of darkness narrower than my hand, exhaling air that smelled of candle smoke and cold iron and something older underneath, something like the inside of a sealed room. "Wren. When we go through, whatever happens, stay behind me. If I tell you to run, you run. Not cleverly. Not bravely. Just run."

She considered this with the gravity she gave all my instructions, which was the gravity of a magistrate reviewing a petition she intended to deny. "You will not say run," she decided. "You will say stay. You always say stay."

"That's because staying near me has so far been survivable."

"You say that like it is a good record." She licked butter off her thumb. "It is a terrible record, Gideon. You have been stabbed twice since I met you."

"Once. The second time was a graze."

"You screamed."

"I *exhaled forcefully.*"

The seam in the rock breathed out again, and this time the breath stirred my hair, and beneath the candle-and-iron smell there came something else: a scent I knew without knowing how I knew it. Ink. Old ink, the kind the guild used for contracts, the kind that never quite washed out of skin no matter how many years passed. My hands remembered it before my head did. They curled slightly, the way they did when I held a quill.

"Well," I said quietly. "I suppose we're expected."

"What does it smell like?"

"Paperwork."

Wren wrinkled her nose. Then she stood, brushed the crumbs from her coat with great ceremony, took my hand — she only did that when she thought I needed it more than she did, which was a habit of hers I found both touching and deeply insulting — and looked up at me with eyes the color of the frozen waterfall.

"Ready?" she asked.

"No," I said honestly. "But I've been told honesty opens doors."

I pressed my palm flat against the seam, and the mountain opened like a ledger.

That is genuinely the best description I can manage. There was no grinding of stone, no dramatic rumble. There was simply a moment when the rock was closed, and then a moment when it was open, the way a book is closed and then open, pages parting to reveal something that had been waiting there all along, patient as ink. Cold light spilled out. Not torchlight. Not moonlight. Light the color of the space between words.

And a voice — not heard so much as felt, rising through my boots, through my bones, through the oath-scar on my wrist where my god had once marked me:

***Owed.***

The word settled over us like a hand on the shoulder. Wren shivered. I stepped through first, because if the mountain wanted to eat anyone, it could start with the man who had spent seven years filing its paperwork.

* * *

*Wren*

Inside the mountain it was winter that had been taught manners.

That is how I thought of it, though I did not have the words yet to say it to Gideon. Outside, winter is rude. It takes your fingers and your breath and does not apologize. But this cold was polite. It touched your face like a servant checking whether you were worth waking the master for. The corridor went down and down, and the walls were ice, but not dirty ice — ice cut into panels, each panel holding a picture, and the pictures moved when you were not looking directly at them.

I looked anyway. I am not supposed to look at things directly; that is a rule I made myself after the guildhouse, where looking at things directly got people pale and whispery. But these pictures — I looked, and they did not stop moving, and one of them stopped moving *for me.*

A feast. A long table under a ceiling of icicles like teeth. People sitting at it, dressed in clothes from before any kingdom I knew of, and every one of them turning their heads toward me at the same slow speed, like weeds following the sun.

"Gideon," I said.

"I see them."

"They are looking at me."

"They're looking at both of us." His voice had gone flat and quiet, the voice he used when he was frightened and had decided fear was a problem to be solved rather than felt. "Keep walking. Eyes forward."

"But they are *familiar,*" I said, and then wished I had not, because it was true in a way that made my stomach hurt. The faces at the table were not strange faces. They were faces I had seen before, or faces that had been seen before by whoever I used to be — the person with the palace and the name that adults had sold. One of them had my old nurse's chin. One of them had my father's hands, folded neatly on the table, except my father's hands had calluses from swords and these hands were smooth as eggs.

"Gideon," I said again, quieter. "Whose faces are those?"

He did not answer for eleven steps. I counted, because counting keeps the fear in a box.

"Paid-for ones," he said finally. "Come on."

* * *

*Gideon*

The corridor ended at the court, and the court was everything Quill had described and nothing he could have prepared me for.

Imagine a feast frozen at its most beautiful moment. Wine caught mid-pour, arcing silver. A dancer suspended between steps, her skirt a bell of frost. Musicians with bows raised, strings unbroken, notes visibly hanging in the air like insects in amber. Now imagine that none of it is dead — that the wine continues pouring forever without ever landing, that the dancer falls forever without ever reaching the floor — and you have some sense of the under-court. Beauty as a machine. Elegance as a trap that has already sprung and is savoring the closing.

And the nobles.

They moved among the frozen feast like the only living things in a painting, and they were beautiful, and that was the horror of it. Every face was flawless. Too flawless. Faces don't come without pores, without asymmetry, without the small honest accidents of having lived. These faces had been *chosen.* I'd spent seven years appraising impossible things for the Vellum Guild — bottled names, borrowed years, second chances in jars — and I knew quality when I saw it. These were the finest purchases I had ever beheld. Someone had bought youth and paid for it in whatever currency buys youth down here, and the transaction showed, the way good tailoring shows, the way a lie told often enough starts to fit.

A woman glided past us wearing the face of a girl perhaps sixteen, and the face was lovely, and the eyes above it were old in a way that had nothing to do with years. She smiled at Wren. The smile was perfect. Every tooth accounted for.

"Little coin," she said, in a voice like a bell rung underwater. "Returned to circulation at last."

"She's not a coin," I said, before my brain could file the appropriate caution.

The woman turned her purchased face to me, and her expression performed curiosity beautifully — brows lifting, lips parting — but the performance arrived a half-second late, like a translation lagging behind the original. "And you are the courier. How punctual. The Regent does so appreciate punctuality. So few of the living bother."

"We're not couriers. We're not delivering anything."

"Of course not." Her smile widened by a precise, measured degree. "You are merely walking, uninvited, into the court beneath the mountain, carrying a soul that appears in our ledgers, seven days before the moon completes its turn. Merely tourists." She drifted closer, and the cold came off her like the cold off a vault door. "I wore a face like yours once, little clerk. Worried. Watchful. Convinced I was a person." She leaned in, and her perfume was ink and candle smoke. "The Regent will see you now. He is so rarely patient, and he is being patient for you. Do try to be worth it."

She swept away toward the frozen dancer, and I noticed — because noticing is the one skill I never sold — that where her shadow fell across the ice, the shadow did not match her. It was the shadow of someone older. Someone stooped. Someone whose face had presumably been spent a long time ago.

Wren's hand found mine. Small, cold, gripping hard.

"Gideon," she whispered. "I remember this table."

"Don't," I said. "Not here. Not until we're somewhere they can't hear remembering."

"They can hear everything here. That is why I whispered."

Fair point. The child was better at this than I was.

We walked the length of the hall, between the frozen feast and the moving nobles, and I kept my eyes forward the way I'd told her to and broke my own rule within twenty paces, because one of the seated figures was not frozen. He sat at the far end of the table in a chair of black ice, and he was watching us come, and he was smiling the way a creditor smiles.

The Pale Regent. He wore white the way other men wore armor, and his face was the only face in the hall that looked like it belonged to him — which meant either that he had never sold it, or that he had bought it back, or that it had never been a face at all. Around him, the frozen feast glittered. Above him, the icicle-teeth of the ceiling caught the wordless light. And everywhere, everywhere, beauty — beauty laid on thick as gilt on a cheap frame, beauty doing its work, which was to keep you from looking at what was underneath.

I had audited ledgers long enough to know that anything this beautiful is hiding a number it doesn't want you to see.

"There he is," the Regent said, and his voice was warm, and that was the worst thing yet, because nothing else in this place was warm, and warmth that has no source is a lure. "The man who was owed. Come down from the daylight at last. Sit. Eat. We have all the time in the world — indeed, we have considerably more than that."

"I'm not hungry," I said.

"No," he agreed pleasantly. "But you are *owed,* Gideon Winterbourne. Or rather—" and here his smile deepened, folding in on itself like a contract signing itself, "—you are *owing.* And we should very much like to discuss the terms."

Beside me, Wren went very still, the way she did when she understood something before the adults did.

And the Regent turned his belonging-face toward her, and said, in a language older than any kingdom, a single word that I somehow understood without understanding:

***Welcome home, princeling.***
