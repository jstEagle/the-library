## 17. The Court Beneath the Mountain

The door that opened for souls already owed opened for us without a sound, which I found insulting. I had expected groaning stone, a grinding of ancient hinges, some acknowledgment that we were walking into the gullet of something old and hungry. Instead it simply wasn't there anymore — one moment a wall of black rock veined with moonlight, the next an archway breathing warm air that smelled of candlewax and cold iron.

Wren's hand found mine. Small fingers, mud still in the creases of her knuckles from three days on the road. She looked up at me with those eyes that had once belonged to a prince, and she said nothing, because there was nothing left to say that either of us could bear.

"Stay behind my left arm," I told her. "If anything happens, you run back up the stairs and you don't look back."

She squeezed my hand once, which in her language — in *our* language now, the one she'd been feeding me word by word across a hundred miles of dying country — meant *no*. It also meant *yes*, depending on where you put the stress. Children's languages are efficient like that.

We went down.

* * *

I had imagined the under-court as a place of darkness, because everything beneath mountains should be dark. What we found instead was light — too much of it, light with no source, falling from nowhere onto a hall vast enough to swallow the Vellum Guild whole and still have room for its regrets. Pillars of white stone rose into a ceiling lost in glittering haze. Long tables stretched away into the distance, laid for a feast, and every seat was filled.

That was the first horror. The second took longer to arrive, because my mind kept refusing it.

The court was beautiful. Every noble at those tables was beautiful — faces like carved saints, skin like candlelit porcelain, eyes that caught the sourceless light and held it. They were eating, or performing the idea of eating, lifting morsels to lips that never quite closed around them. And as Wren and I walked the long aisle between the tables — because walk it we must; there was no other direction the hall permitted — I began to notice what a man notices when he has spent seven years auditing ledgers full of impossible things.

Repetition. The same face, twice. Then again, four seats down. A woman's face worn by three different bodies, each wearing it slightly wrong, like a coat borrowed from a sister. A jawline I'd seen on a farmer in the village of borrowed years, on a corpse by the toll bridge, on a merchant who'd once tried to sell me bottled thunder.

They were wearing us. All of us. Every face above ground that had ever been sold, traded, collateralized, foreclosed — the under-court wore them to its eternal feast, and called it fashion.

"They're smiling," Wren whispered. "Why are they smiling? Nobody's happy here."

"Because they bought the smiles separately," I said. "From people who used to have them."

A herald stood at the far end of the hall, before a dais of black glass. He was the only figure not beautiful — deliberately so, I think, the way a servant wears livery. His face was plain as paper, his voice dry as one.

"The Guild's man," he announced, "and the asset. The Regent will see what is owed."

"I'm nobody's asset," Wren said, in her broken common tongue, loud enough for half the hall to hear.

Several nobles turned their purchased faces toward her, and I watched hunger move through them like wind through wheat.

* * *

The Pale Regent sat alone on the dais, which surprised me. I had expected a throne room crowded with schemers. Instead there was only him: a tall figure in robes the color of frost on a window, seated in a chair that might once have been a tree, and his face —

His face was the problem. Not because it was terrible. Because it was *familiar*. Not any specific face I knew. Familiar the way a word on the tip of your tongue is familiar, the way a dream is familiar ten seconds after waking. I had seen this face somewhere that no longer existed, in a life I had never been allowed to remember.

"Gideon Winterbourne," the Regent said. His voice was quiet, courteous, and absolutely certain of itself. "Keeper of ledgers. Sworn to a god who does not answer. Come all this way through rain and memory to bargain for a child."

"You've been well informed."

"I wrote most of it." He gestured to a seat opposite him — a plain wooden chair, insultingly ordinary amid all that splendor. "Sit. Bargaining goes better sitting. Standing, men feel they can leave."

"There's no leaving, though. Is there?"

"No," he said pleasantly. "But the illusion helps."

I sat. Wren pressed herself against my side, and I let her, and I noted with the detached part of my mind that keeps accounts even while the rest of a man is drowning — I noted that my hands were steady. Seven years of clerical work hadn't killed the mercenary after all. He'd just been waiting, patient as a debt, for the right creditor.

"Now," said the Regent, folding pale hands. "You've come to buy back a soul. You believe you have something to trade. Let me spare us both the theater of offers and counter-offers." He leaned forward, and the sourceless light did something unkind to his features, hollowing them, aging them, making him briefly look like every tired clerk I had ever worked beside. "You have nothing. You arrived with nothing. Everything you carry — your skill with a blade, your oath, your name itself — was issued to you. On credit. By parties whose interests I represent."

"That's a very grand way of saying I'm poor."

"It's a very precise way of saying you're *collateral*."

The word went through me like a blade finding the gap in armor. Not because I didn't know. Quill had told me. The proof had told me — seven years ago, a man named Gideon Winterbourne simply began, with a mercenary's scars and a god's oath and no history deeper than a rain puddle. I had known since the shrine, since the single word my silent god finally gave me: *Remember.*

Knowing a thing and hearing it said aloud by the thing that made you are different animals entirely.

"Say it plainly," I said. "I've read enough contracts to hate the ones written to be misunderstood."

Something flickered across the Regent's borrowed calm — respect, perhaps, or the memory of it. "Very plainly, then. Sit comfortably. This is a long entry."

He rose and walked to the edge of the dais, and as he spoke, images rose in the air above the hall like breath in winter — scenes rendered in pale light, a ledger's illustrations come alive.

"Two hundred years ago, there was a kingdom above this mountain. Its royal line held a gift: souls that could be divided and survive, souls that could be pawned and redeemed, souls worth more than kingdoms because they could *carry* kingdoms. Your ward's line. Her blood." An image: a palace, towers, a child with Wren's eyes standing at a window. "The last prince of that line was born with a soul too large for one body. The court below coveted it. The court above feared it. And so a bargain was struck — but bargains require collateral, and what do kings own that gods do not?"

The image shifted. A tower burning. A man at a desk signing a contract in blood — I knew that scene, I had *seen* it on the road, torn out of my own half-memories like a page from a book I wasn't supposed to read.

"A hostage," the Regent continued. "Not the prince. Something else. Something the guild could hold against the day the contract came due. A soul, unformed, unclaimed, sold before it ever had a name — because a soul with no name cannot be missed, and cannot fight, and cannot be ransomed by anyone who loves it." He turned, and those too-familiar eyes fixed on me. "It needed a shape. A container. Skills, oaths, habits, a devotion to keep it docile. So the guild built one. They gave it a sword-hand and a ledger-hand and a god to pray to — a god sworn to guard it until collection. And they named it Gideon Winterbourne."

The hall was very quiet. Even the feast had stopped, hundreds of beautiful faces turned toward the dais, watching me learn what I was with the polite attention of creditors at a reading of a will.

"You," the Regent said gently, "are the collateral. You always were. The child's soul was sold to us — yes, truly sold, the guild's brokers are thorough if nothing else — but the sale was never the point. The sale was *bait*. A soul that large, sold so wrongly, would eventually draw its guardian down the mountain. Contracts of this age require completion in person. Signatures in presence. Blood in witness." He smiled, and it was the smile of a man closing a ledger at year's end. "You brought yourself to me, Gideon Winterbourne. You walked two hundred miles to deliver the collateral to the collections office. I have rarely seen such diligence from an asset."

* * *

I want to tell you I raged. That I leapt from the chair, drew steel, did something magnificent and doomed.

What I actually did was worse. I laughed.

It came out of me ugly, one short bark that echoed off pillars built to flatter whispers, and half the court flinched — flinched! — as though laughter were the one currency they'd never learned to counterfeit.

"Of course," I said. "Of course. Seven years of praying into silence. Seven years of keeping books for monsters, auditing trades in borrowed years and bottled names, telling myself the work was neutral, that a ledger doesn't choose what it records." I stood. My hands were still steady. I remember being grateful for that, and hating myself for being grateful about anything in that moment. "And the whole time, I was the entry. I was the *line item*. Somewhere in your vaults there's a page with my name on it and a value assigned, and I've spent seven years filing pages exactly like it under *Assets, Miscellaneous*."

"Vault Three," said the Regent. "Shelf nine. Blue binding. Would you like to see it?"

"No." And then, because honesty is a habit even in hell: "Yes. Later. If there is a later."

Wren tugged my sleeve. She'd understood perhaps a third of it — the language of courts is not the language of children — but she'd understood the shape of it, the way children always understand the shape of a thing before its details. She looked up at me and said, in the old tongue, the phrase she'd taught me on the road, the one that had cracked open my half-memories by the fire:

*"Your name is yours."*

"Not according to shelf nine," I said.

"Names can be stolen," she said stubbornly, in common, mangling every second word. "Quill said. Stolen things can be stole *back*."

The Regent heard her. I saw him hear her — saw something move behind his face, quick and involuntary, like a man remembering he once had a heart and wondering where he'd put it.

"The child speaks truer than she knows," he said. "Which brings us to terms. You came to bargain. Very well. Here is the bargain as it stands written, two hundred years old, coming due with the thirteenth moon." Light gathered above us again, and this time it showed me the contract itself — vast, hanging in the air like a banner, columns of script in the old language, and I could read more of it than I should have been able to, Wren's lessons lodged in me like seeds. "One soul of the royal line, delivered whole, in perpetuity. Against which: the collateral is released. Freed. Given back its name, its history, its seven missing years and two hundred missing years besides. You may be a man again, Gideon Winterbourne — or whatever name the man beneath you answers to. Whole. Real. *Owed to no one.*"

"And she becomes a lamp for your table. Fuel for your faces."

"The court must eat."

"The court could stop."

"The court could stop," he agreed mildly, "the way a river could stop. Now — you have until the thirteenth moon completes its turn. One night. Above us, even now, it is rising." He reseated himself, folded his hands, and regarded me with the patience of stone. "Consider. Most collateral, offered freedom, weeps with gratitude. Most guardians, offered their own souls, sign before the ink dries. You are not most assets, Gideon Winterbourne. That was, I am told, an oversight in your construction. Someone loved the idea of you a little too much."

"Who?" The question tore out of me before I could weigh it. "Who built me? Who signed?"

The Regent's expression did something I had not seen it do. It closed. Like a door.

"That," he said, "is not in my ledger to tell. But it *is* in someone's. And when the moon turns, and the bargain seals one way or the other — you may yet meet them. They do so love to attend closings."

Above the mountain, faint through stone thick enough to bury armies, I felt — did not hear, *felt* — the twelfth moon begin to fail, and the thirteenth begin to rise.

Wren's hand slipped into mine. In the old language, the one older than any kingdom, older maybe than the thing on the throne, she said the word for *stay*.

And for the first time since a knock came at my door on a rain-slicked night, I knew exactly what I was going to do.

The trouble was, I was fairly sure it was going to cost everything — and I was equally sure the Regent hadn't told me the worst of it yet.
