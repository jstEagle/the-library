## 18. Sarelle's Choice

I have burned many things in my forty years with the Vellum Guild. Ledgers, mostly. Letters that should never have been written. One unfortunate apprentice who transcribed a bottled name onto the wrong page and had to be *unwritten* — but we don't speak of Aldous anymore, and I paid for his funeral out of my own purse, which is more than the guild ever did for anyone.

So when I say I know fire, you may believe me.

The contract in my satchel was not meant to be burned. It was meant to be *presented*. That is what Corvane believed as he walked beside me down the mountain road, and what the retrievers behind us believed, and what the guild's masters in their masked convocation believed when they gave me the writ of enforcement and told me, in that tone they use for matters already decided, to bring back the asset and the thief or bring back proof of their deaths.

Corvane carried the enforcement copy. I carried the original.

That distinction would have meant nothing to me three weeks ago. It means everything now, because three weeks ago I had not read the original contracts. Three weeks ago I still believed the Vellum Guild traded in impossible things honestly — which is to say, cruelly, but by the letter. There is a comfort in cruelty by the letter. You can sleep at night. You can tell yourself the ledger balances and that balance is a kind of mercy.

Then I sat in the archive at Vellum House with a lamp and a key no one remembered giving me, and I read what we actually held.

* * *

You should understand something about guild contracts before I go further. They are not paper. Paper burns too easily, and the guild has enemies who own matches. A true contract is written on vellum scraped from the hide of an animal that died owing something — don't ask what; even I stopped asking years ago — and the ink is mixed with a drop of whatever was pledged. A year of life. A memory. A name. The contract *tastes* its collateral. Hold one to your tongue and you can tell whether it is genuine, the way a banker bites a coin.

I did not taste this one. I didn't need to. I could feel it through the satchel leather, warm as a sleeping bird, all the way down the mountain.

It was old. Older than the guild's founding charter, which is supposed to be our oldest document. Older than the kingdom whose succession crisis everyone pretends not to remember. The seal at its foot was pressed in wax the color of drowned flesh, and the signature above it made my hands shake so badly I had to set the lamp down on the archive table and breathe like a woman in labor until they stilled.

Because I knew that signature. Every senior clerk knows it. We practice it, in fact, in our second year of training — the guild teaches us the hands of the great debtors the way a seminary teaches scripture. Gideon Winterbourne's signature, flowing and confident, dated seven years before Gideon Winterbourne, by every record we possess, existed.

There was a second signature beneath his. Smaller. A child's careful letters, guided by an adult hand: *Wren, called also by her true name, which is withheld per clause nine.*

Clause nine. Withheld names. The guild's oldest protection — if the debtor's true name is never written, the debt can never be discharged by naming. Clever. Ancient. Monstrous.

And between those two signatures, in the Regent's spidery hand, the terms. I read them four times, hoping each time the words would rearrange themselves into something defensible. They did not.

The prince's soul, sold by his uncle to the court beneath the mountain. The court's claim on the prince's soul, resold — *not transferred, resold, with interest accruing* — to the Vellum Guild as security against a prior and greater debt. And the prior debt: one soul, male, adult, self-pledged, signature attached, to be delivered to the mountain upon recovery of the secondary collateral.

We were not the child's owners. We were the *bait*.

Somewhere beneath the mountain, a thing wearing a pale crown had arranged for a mute child washed in old blood to walk out of a storm and knock on the door of the one man in the world whose soul it already held. The thirteenth moon wasn't a deadline for the girl. It was a deadline for *him*. She was the hook. Winterbourne was the fish. And the guild — my guild, my life's work, the only institution I had ever served without shame — had held the rod and called itself a neutral broker of impossible things.

Neutral. I laughed alone in the archive until the lamp guttered, and then I sat very still for a long time, listening to the building breathe around me, and understood that I had two choices, and that both of them ended my career, and that only one of them let me keep calling myself Sarelle afterward.

* * *

"You're quiet," Corvane said, three days later, as the mountain filled half the sky ahead of us. "Quiet even for you."

"I'm composing," I said. "Enforcement requires rhetoric. You wouldn't know; you just break things until the paperwork agrees with you."

"Paperwork always agrees with me eventually." He smiled the way he does, which is to say the way a door does when it opens onto a room you wish you hadn't seen. "The thief won't get past the gatekeeper. Nothing gets past the bridge-keeper without paying, and he hasn't got anything left worth taking. I've read his file."

"His file is seven years long," I said. "Think about that."

"I think about the writ." He patted the enforcement copy inside his coat, and I felt the original shift against my ribs like a second heartbeat. "Sarelle. If you're planning something—"

"I'm always planning something. It's why the masters send me instead of you. You're the hammer, Corvane. Hammers don't plan. They fall."

He grunted, satisfied, or pretending to be, and I watched the mountain grow, and I thought about Aldous again, poor unwritten Aldous, and about how every institution keeps one grave it doesn't mark, and how I had spent thirty years telling myself ours was necessary.

* * *

The under-court is worse than the stories, and the stories are bad enough. The door opened for us the way it opens for everything owed — Corvane carries debts the way other men carry knives — and the cold that came out was not weather-cold but *ledger*-cold, the chill of a page that has been waiting a very long time to be written on.

Inside: the frozen feast. I had heard descriptions. None of them prepared me for the light, which comes from nowhere and flatters nothing, or for the nobles, who wear their purchased faces the way fashionable women wear borrowed jewels — beautifully, and with the constant small anxiety of the unowned. A duchess with a shepherd's laugh lines around eyes that had never once looked at a sheep. A young lord with a soldier's broken nose set into a face soft as candlewax. They turned to look at us as we crossed the hall, and every one of them looked *hungry*, and every one of them looked afraid, and I realized those were the same expression here.

The Pale Regent received us on a throne of stacked contracts. I am not being poetic. Actual contracts, tens of thousands of them, pressed into a seat and back and armrests, and I swear on whatever I still believe in that the throne *breathed*.

"Guildmistress." Its voice was dry paper turning. "You've come to enforce."

"I've come to conclude," I said, and beside me Corvane straightened, sensing — too late, dear hammer, always too late — that the falling was going to be done by someone else.

Across the hall, near the dais, stood Gideon Winterbourne with the child's hand in his, and I will note for the record that he did not look surprised to see me. He looked tired, and resolved, and older than seven years, and when our eyes met he inclined his head slightly, mercenary to mercenary, one professional acknowledging another walking into a job she can't walk out of.

"Sarelle," he said. "Come to collect?"

"Among other things." I unslung the satchel. My hands were steady now. Isn't that always the way — steady at last, at the exact moment steadiness stops mattering? "Before any collection occurs, the guild's claim must be verified against the original instrument. Guild law, article one. Even here, I assume, contract precedes possession."

The Regent's smile was a fold in pale cloth. "You assume much, guildmistress."

"I'm paid to." I drew out the original contract, and the hall changed. I felt it change — the hungry faces went still, the false light bent toward the vellum in my hands like iron filings toward a lodestone, and somewhere in the throne of stacked paper something whimpered. "This is the primary instrument. Dated before the founding of my guild. Bearing two signatures." I turned it so the light could read it, though I suspect the light had been reading it since I entered. "One belongs to the man standing beside the child. The other belongs to her. Clause nine withheld her name from every copy — including mine, including the enforcement writ, including, I'd wager, your own working ledger. Which means that for the entire term of the resale, *no party to this transaction except you has known what was actually being bought.*"

"Guildmistress," Corvane said quietly, "what are you doing?"

"My job," I said. "Finally."

* * *

Here is the thing about treachery: it looks exactly like duty right up until the moment it doesn't. I had rehearsed this speech on the road, in the dark, while Corvane snored, and in rehearsal it had sounded like madness. Standing in the frozen light with the vellum warm against my palms, it sounded like the first honest sentence I'd spoken in thirty years.

"The resale is void," I said. "Not contested. Void. The guild's copy was executed without disclosure of the primary instrument, under coercion of concealment, and — this is the part I want everyone to hear —" I raised my voice, and the purchased faces leaned in, nobles and servants and things too old for titles, all of them starving for words the way their owners had once starved for souls, "— *the collateral described in the resale was never the guild's to sell.* You cannot pledge a hostage twice, Regent. Even beneath a mountain. Even with a crown. The child's soul was taken by force and fraud, and every instrument built upon that taking is rotten from the root. I burn the guild's copy, the resale dies, and you are left holding only the original contract — which names *him*—" I pointed at Winterbourne, "—and *only him*, and which he came here of his own free will to answer."

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight and temperature.

"You understand," the Regent said softly, "that the guild will disown you. Unwrite you. There will be no record that Sarelle Vosk was ever born."

"There's barely a record now," I said. "I sold the guild my twenties when I signed on. Did you know that? Twenty years, wholesale, for a clerkship and a pension. I've been sixty-one years old since I was thirty-nine." I laughed, and it came out wrong, wet at the edges. "You want to take the rest? Take a number. Everyone else has."

"Sarelle." Corvane's hand was on his blade now, and his voice had the flatness he saves for executions. "Step away from the contract."

"No."

"It's not too late. Give it to me. The masters will be lenient. You're valuable."

"I'm *thorough*, Corvane. There's a difference, and the difference is that thorough people read the documents." I met his eyes, and I made myself smile, because if I was going to die in a hole under a mountain I was going to do it with better manners than the guild ever taught me. "You carry the enforcement copy. Burn yours too, or don't. But if you draw that sword, know that you'll be enforcing a fraud, and that somewhere in that stack of paper on the throne, your own name is written, and I promise you the Regent reads its clauses more carefully than you do."

His blade stayed where it was. Long enough. Long enough was all I needed.

I struck the flame from the little fire-box I'd carried for twenty years and never once used — guildmistresses don't burn things themselves; that's what clerks are for — and touched it to the corner of the original contract.

Vellum cut from a debtor's hide burns slowly. It wants to live. It argued with the flame, hissing, curling, and as it burned I heard things — a child crying in a palace corridor, a man's voice reciting terms in a language Wren speaks, the sound of a quill signing, signing, signing — and I stood there and let it argue until there was nothing left of the guild's claim but ash drifting up toward a ceiling that had no sky above it.

The whimpering in the throne stopped. All across the hall, the purchased faces turned toward the child, and something in the air shifted — the particular shift of a debt transferring, of weight moving from one column of a ledger to another.

"Well," said the Regent, and its voice had lost the dry-paper patience and gained something underneath, something old and annoyed and, I thought, faintly *relieved*, the way a gambler is relieved when the last card forces his hand. "Now there is only the original bargain. One soul, freely pledged, against one soul unlawfully held." Its dead-flesh eyes found Gideon Winterbourne. "You heard the guildmistress, thief. The moon turns within the hour. Do you accept the terms as read?"

Winterbourne looked at the child. The child looked at him. Whatever passed between them needed no translation, which is more than I can say for anything else that has happened in my life.

"I accept," he said.

And I — traitor, arsonist, the last honest clerk of the Vellum Guild — stood in the frozen light and began, at last, to hope.

Which is when the mountain shuddered, and the throne of contracts sighed open like a book, and I understood that the Regent had never intended to lose — that burning the resale had not closed the trap but sprung it — and that the final clause of the original contract, the one I had read four times and thought I understood, was written in a hand I had not yet learned to see.

The thirteenth moon began to turn.
