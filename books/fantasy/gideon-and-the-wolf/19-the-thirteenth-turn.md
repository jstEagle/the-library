## 19. The Thirteenth Turn

The Pale Regent's court did not breathe.

I noticed it the way you notice a wrong note in a hymn — not at first, but all at once, and then you cannot unhear it. The nobles of the under-court sat in their frozen feast-hall with goblets raised and laughter caught mid-throat, faces like candlewax poured over skulls, and not one ribcage among them rose or fell. The only breath in that whole glittering cavern was Wren's, small and quick beside me, and my own, which I was working very hard to keep steady.

Above us, through a shaft in the mountain's crown, the thirteenth moon hung fat and white and nearly finished turning. Its light fell down the shaft like milk poured slow, pooling on the obsidian floor between me and the Regent's throne of fused ledgers. I could see pages in the throne's construction. Contracts, pressed flat and lacquered into stone. Somewhere in there, I suspected, was a page with my name on it.

Or whatever my name was.

"Terms," the Regent said.

Its voice was the sound a quill makes when it scratches out a word — a soft, wet erasure. It wore a face today. That was the courtesy of the under-court: they wore the faces of whoever you loved best, so you would look at them while they ruined you. This one had chosen a face I didn't recognize, which told me something bleak about myself. A woman, perhaps forty, kind around the eyes, with flour dusted in her hair as though she'd been baking bread a moment before eternity interrupted her.

I decided not to think about who she might have been.

"One soul for one soul," the Regent said. "That is the old rate. It has not changed since before your kingdoms learned to count."

"And which souls are we discussing?" I asked. "You'll forgive me if I want the inventory named aloud. I keep ledgers for a living. Ambiguity is where the guild skins people."

Something moved behind the Regent's borrowed eyes — amusement, maybe, or hunger wearing amusement's coat. "The child's soul, held by this court against a debt of blood and name. And yours, Gideon Winterbourne, sworn to a silent god, seven years old as a man and empty as a drum before that." It spread its hands, flour-dusted and gentle. "One for one. You may take hers and leave yours. Or leave hers and take yours back — the man you were before the guild wrote *Gideon Winterbourne* across the vacancy. His memories. His name. His sins. All of it returned, whole, tonight."

The hall was quiet enough that I could hear the moonlight.

That was the trap, of course. There is always a trap; I'd audited enough contracts to know that a bargain without one is like a prayer without a god behind it — technically possible, historically rare. The Regent was offering me everything I'd been clawing toward since Quill put that copper coin in my palm and went white as tallow. My past. The tower, the fire, the man signing in blood. The answer to Corvane's taunt, to Sarelle's fear, to the single word my god had finally spoken after seven years of silence.

*Remember.*

And the price was a child.

"I imagine," I said, "there's a third option. There usually is, in contracts written by clever people. The third option just isn't advertised."

"The third option," the Regent said, "is that you leave here with nothing and we keep what we are owed."

"Then let's talk about what you're owed. Because I've read the original contracts now — Sarelle was good enough to burn the guild's copy in front of you, which I'm told voids a resale, but the *original* still stands. And the original names collateral." I took a step forward onto the moonlit floor. My boots made no sound. Neither did anything else here. "The original doesn't name the prince's soul as collateral. It names mine. Wren's sale wasn't a sale. It was bait. You strung a child on a hook and cast her into the world because you knew — somehow you knew — that a man with no past would come for her. That I'd walk every mile of this road, pay every toll, spend every memory I had, and arrive at your door ready to sign anything."

"Flattering," said the Regent. "But inaccurate. We do not lure. We wait. There is a difference, though I understand mortals prefer 'lure,' as it permits them to feel clever rather than merely doomed."

"Then correct me," I said. "Lay the contract on the table. All of it. Every clause, every rider, every footnote in whatever language this court uses when it thinks no one living can read it."

Wren's hand found mine. Small, cold, certain. She looked up at me and said something in the old tongue — the phrase she'd taught me on the road, the one that had cracked open the tower and the fire and the blood-signed page. It meant, roughly, *I know what you're going to do, and I forbid it.* Eight years old, forbidding me. If I lived to be a thousand I would never meet anyone braver.

"*Aethen sul,*" I answered. *I hear you.* Which was not the same as agreeing, and we both knew it, and her grip tightened like she could hold my future shut with eight small fingers.

The Regent gestured, and a ledger rose from the throne — one page peeling free, drifting down through the milk-light to land at my feet. I picked it up. The vellum was warm. It is never a good sign when a contract is warm.

I read. I am good at reading contracts; it is the one thing the Vellum Guild ever truly gave me. And there it was, in clauses older than any kingdom, in ink that shifted like oil when I tried to look at it directly:

*Collateral: one soul, unnamed, held in trust against the completion of the exchange. Should the bearer present himself willingly before the turn of the thirteenth moon, the debt may be discharged by substitution of equivalent value.*

Equivalent value. Not identical. Equivalent.

"You don't want two souls," I said slowly. "You never wanted two souls. Souls are currency to you, and currency is only useful when it moves. What you want is the *transaction*. The completion. The moment the ledger balances and the court gets to feel, for one more century, that the books are closed." I looked up. "So balance them differently."

"Name your substitution," the Regent said, and every frozen noble in that hall leaned in without moving, which is a trick I hope never to see again.

I had rehearsed this on the road. In the dark, in the rain, walking beside a sleeping child, I had turned the words over until they were worn smooth as river stones. But rehearsal is nothing. Rehearsal is the mercenary sharpening his blade the night before and telling himself he won't be afraid. When the moment came, the blade shook in my hand all the same.

"My name," I said.

The Regent tilted its borrowed face.

"Gideon Winterbourne," I said. "Seven years of him. Every morning he woke in the guildhouse and prayed to a god who never answered. Every ledger he balanced. Every lie he told Sarelle, every ward he walked past, every mile of this road, every memory he paid at the toll bridge — including his mother's face, which I want noted, Regent, because that one hurt, and I want the pain counted as part of the price. Take the name. Take the history. Take the man entire. Everything Gideon Winterbourne is, was, or might have been — that's my equivalent value. One constructed soul, complete, freely given." I swallowed. My mouth tasted of copper and cold air. "In exchange, the girl walks out of this mountain with her own soul, her own name, and no claim against her ever again, by this court, its heirs, its assigns, or its masked cousins above ground. Clean entry. No riders."

Silence. Even the moonlight seemed to pause.

"You understand," the Regent said softly, "what you are offering. Not death. Death is simple; death is a door. You are offering *unmaking*. The name goes, and everything the name holds. You will stand outside this mountain tomorrow morning with no memory of why you are standing there. You will not know the child. You will not know the god you swore to. You will not know that you were ever brave, or that it cost you anything, because the man who was brave will have been spent to purchase a stranger's freedom." Its flour-dusted face leaned close, and for one horrible moment I thought I saw pity in it. "She will not even thank you. She will not know there is anything to thank."

"I know," I said.

"She will grieve a man she cannot remember losing."

"I know," I said, and my voice cracked on the second word, and I let it, because some things should crack.

"And you do it anyway. Why?" The Regent sounded genuinely curious, the way an auditor sounds curious — not compassion, arithmetic. "You were made, Gideon Winterbourne. Written into being by powers you never met, for purposes you never learned. A ledger entry given flesh. Why spend a false coin so lavishly?"

And there it was. The question underneath all the other questions, the one that had been waiting for me since chapter one of my miserable existence — whether a made self can be a real one, whether seven borrowed years outweigh a stolen childhood, whether a man assembled from ink has anything inside him worth the spending.

I thought about Wren asleep against my shoulder on the road, her weight real as bread. About the village of borrowed years, and the old man with a child's face weeping with gratitude when she gave him back a decade. About my mother's face dissolving at the toll bridge, and how I'd paid it without hesitation and grieved it anyway, because grief doesn't check provenance. About the silence of my god, which had never once felt empty — only patient, only *watchful*, like a father listening at a door.

"A coin doesn't ask if it's minted true," I said. "It spends itself on what it's for. That's the whole theology of it, Regent. I was made, fine. Made things have purposes. Here's mine."

I set my hand on the contract.

Wren made a sound — small, furious, heartbroken — and hit me in the leg with her fist, once, hard, and then buried her face against me and held on. I put my free hand on her hair. "Hey," I said. "Hey. Listen. You remember the word I asked you? On the road?"

She nodded against my ribs.

"Say it to me tomorrow," I said. "I won't know what it means. Say it anyway. Promise me."

She pulled back and looked at me with red-rimmed eyes, and she spoke in the old tongue, the whole sentence, and I understood maybe half of it and treasured all of it. Then she stepped back, because she was a prince, whatever else she was, and princes know when the signing has begun.

The moon finished its turn.

I felt it happen — not saw, *felt*, the way you feel a bone set. Light came down the shaft like a bell being struck, and the contract warmed under my palm, and the Regent spoke the terms aloud in a language that made my teeth ache, and I spoke my assent in common, plain words, because I wanted the record clear:

"I give the name Gideon Winterbourne, and all it contains. Freely. Completely. For the girl."

The ink moved up my arm.

That is the only way I can describe it. Not pain, exactly — pain would have been simpler. It was *subtraction*. Beginning at my fingertips, a quieting, like snow falling on a field, covering things one by one. I thought: *the guildhouse, the smell of ink and tallow.* And then I thought: *what is a guildhouse?* And the question didn't frighten me, which frightened me, briefly, and then that fright went too, gently, like a lamp carried out of a room.

I heard Quill's voice somewhere, saying *court currency, minted for souls,* and I didn't know who Quill was, and then I didn't know that I didn't know.

I heard a bridge-keeper's price, and my mother's face — gone already, gone at the toll, and now the grief for it went too, and I stood in a strange hall holding a stranger's hand and could not remember why I was crying.

I heard a god say *Remember,* and I reached for the word the way you reach for a stair in the dark, and found nothing, and kept reaching, because some part of me — the part deeper than name, deeper than ledger, the part they couldn't write or erase — kept reaching, kept saying *this matters, hold onto this, hold—*

The last thing to go was her.

Not her face. I suspect no contract can take another person entirely; we are each other's unrecorded property. But the meaning of her went — the reason my chest ached looking at her, the seven years' worth of fierce and terrified love compressed into eight days of knowing her. She became, in the space of a heartbeat, a small mud-caked girl standing in a glittering hall, crying, holding the hand of a tall man who looked at her with polite, blank kindness and said:

"I'm sorry — do I know you?"

And the moon's light went out of the shaft, and the court exhaled its first breath in a century, and somewhere far above, dawn began considering the mountains.

* * *

They walked us out through a door that opened only for souls already owed, and I suppose mine qualified, in the way an emptied purse still qualifies as a purse. The passage was long and grey and smelled of stone and old water, and the child held my hand the entire way, and I let her, because she clearly needed it, and because there was no reason not to. Strangers are permitted kindness. It costs nothing.

We came out onto a hillside in early light. The world was absurdly ordinary — grass, wind, a hawk turning circles over the valley — and I stood in it feeling like a page torn from a book, edges rough, nothing written on either side.

The girl stopped. She looked up at me for a long time, searching my face the way you search a house you used to live in, checking whether anything of you is still there behind the windows.

Then she spoke. One word. The word she'd promised.

I didn't know it. I didn't know what it meant, or what language it was, or why my chest hurt suddenly, sharply, like a scar remembering it was a wound.

But I didn't let go of her hand.

"Come on, then," I said, because someone had to say something, and the words came from nowhere, from the part of me that remained when everything else was spent. "Whatever this is — wherever we're going — we'd better start walking."

She wiped her face with her sleeve, nodded once, princely even in rags, and together we went down the hill into a country where I knew no one, remembered nothing, and owed — for the first time in my life —

nothing at all.

Except, I would learn later, one small thing. One word, kept safe somewhere I couldn't reach, in a silence that was not empty.

Waiting.
