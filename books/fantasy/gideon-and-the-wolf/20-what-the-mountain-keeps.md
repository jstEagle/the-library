## 20. What the Mountain Keeps

The first thing I knew was grass.

Real grass, wet with morning, tickling my cheek the way it does when you've been sleeping on the ground and not on a throne of frozen glass. I lay there a while with my eyes shut, because I was afraid that if I opened them I would still be under the mountain, and under the mountain you are never allowed to finish sleeping. Under the mountain sleep is like a coin someone keeps taking out of your hand to look at and then putting back wrong-side up.

But the grass kept being grass. And there was wind — real wind, wind that smelled of rain and pine and distance — and somewhere below us a river was talking to itself in the way rivers do when they think no one is listening.

I opened my eyes.

The sky was enormous. It went on forever in every direction, grey-gold at the edges where the sun was thinking about coming up, and there were no ceilings on it anywhere. No vaulted dark. no faces made of other people's faces leaning down to look at me. Just sky, all the way up, like a door left open by accident for a thousand years.

I sat up too fast and the world tilted, and then it settled, and then I remembered everything at once, which is how remembering works after something like that. Not slowly, politely, piece by piece. All of it, like a drawer dumped out on a floor.

The palace. The bargain. My father's face when he signed. The men who came with scales. The cold room beneath the mountain where they keep souls in jars with labels written in a language that has no word for *sorry*. The thirteenth moon turning overhead like a key in a lock. Gideon standing before the Pale Regent with his hands open, saying words I didn't understand until I understood them completely.

*One soul for one soul.*

He gave them his name. He gave them everything he was. Every year of his life, every memory, every scar and every story, poured out of him like water from a cup, and he did it smiling, or almost smiling, because that's what Gideon did instead of crying.

And then we were here. On a hillside. Above ground. Whole.

Well. One of us whole.

I turned my head and he was lying beside me, three arm-lengths away, flat on his back with his eyes open, watching the sky the way I had been afraid to watch it. His chest went up and down. That was good. That was the important part. Quill had told me once, in his crooked house full of stolen paper, that as long as a man breathes, everything else can be argued about later.

"Gideon," I said.

He blinked. Slowly, like an owl deciding whether the day was worth it. Then he turned his head and looked at me, and my heart fell down inside me and landed somewhere around my boots.

Because I knew that look. I had seen it in mirrors, back when I had a mirror and a face worth looking into. It was the look of someone waking up in a room they have never seen, wearing clothes that fit but aren't theirs, in a body that works perfectly and belongs to no one.

"I don't know that name," he said.

His voice was his voice. That was the cruelest part. The mountain had taken everything except the sound of him, so that when he spoke it was exactly like hearing Gideon Winterbourne speak, except there was nobody home behind the words. Just a man-shaped space, tidy and empty, waiting to be filled.

"It's your name," I said. "It was."

"Was." He tried the word out, rolling it around like a pebble. He sat up, and I watched him take inventory of himself the way soldiers do — hands first, then feet, then the horizon, then me. Mercenary habits live deeper than memory, apparently. The mountain hadn't thought to dig those out. Or maybe it couldn't. Maybe some things aren't stored in the head at all.

"Do you know who I am?" I asked. I tried to make my voice small and casual, the way you ask a question you're terrified of.

He studied me for a long moment. Wind moved his hair. Somewhere behind us, the river kept chattering.

"No," he said. "Should I?"

"You should." I hugged my knees. "You spent eleven days teaching me to swear properly in Common. You said I had a talent for it. You carried me across a river when my legs were too short for the current. You paid a bridge-keeper your mother's face so we could get to the mountain in time." My voice cracked on that last one, and I hated it, because now he would never know what I meant, and neither, in a way, would I. The bridge-keeper had eaten her face. She was gone from the world twice over — once to time, once to a toll.

He listened to all this without blinking. When I finished, he said, "That sounds like something a man should remember."

"It is."

"And I don't."

"No."

He nodded slowly, accepting it the way you accept weather. That was the worst thing yet, somehow. Gideon — the real one, the one who existed last night — would have raged, or joked, or gone very quiet and dangerous. This man just nodded, polite as a clerk, because there was nothing in him left to rage *with*. A person needs a past to be angry about losing it.

"What do I know?" he asked. "If you don't mind my asking. What's left?"

I wiped my nose on my sleeve. "You know how to fight. You know roads. You know how to read people and count coins and mend things. You know—" I stopped, because the next part was going to hurt, and I decided to say it anyway, because he deserved true things even if they were sharp. "You know my language. Some of it. I taught you on the road. Words. Phrases. You were terrible at the vowels."

"I was terrible at the vowels," he agreed, and there was the ghost of something in his face — not a memory, nothing so kind as that, just the shape where one used to be, like a dent left in a cushion.

We sat there while the sun came up properly. I cried a little, quietly, into my knees, where he couldn't see well. It seemed rude to cry where he could watch, since none of it was his grief anymore. It was mine alone now, the whole weight of it: the palace, the sale, the road, the tower fire, the man signing a contract in blood — all of it lived in me and nowhere else. I was eight years old and I was the only historian of two entire lives.

When I was done crying, I did what needed doing.

* * *

Here is a thing I learned under the mountain, in the cold room with the jars: a name is not a word. Everyone thinks it's a word. It isn't. A word is just the handle you use to carry the name around. The name itself is a *thing*, heavy and warm, and if you say it right — really right, in the old language, the language from before kingdoms, the language the mountain speaks when it thinks no one is listening — you can hand it to somebody. Put it right into their hands.

The Regent had done it backwards. It had reached into Gideon and pulled the name out through his mouth, and he had let it, and that was the price, and prices are prices. But pulling out leaves a hole. And holes can be filled. Not with everything — the mountain keeps what it takes; that's why it's called a mountain and not a market — but with something. With one true thing, maybe. If you have one true thing to give.

I had one true thing.

It wasn't much. It was four days' walk of lessons beside a dying moon, a tired mercenary and a sold prince sitting by fires, and me teaching him my language word by word because it was the only treasure I had left to share. Most of it was gone from him now, rinsed away with the rest. But phrases go deep. Phrases lodge. And there was one phrase I had taught him near the end, on the last night before the mountain, when he'd asked me what the old word was for *choosing*, because he wanted to be sure, when the moment came, that everyone understood — the Regent, the guild, the silent god, himself — that this was a choice and not a trick.

I had taught him the phrase. He had repeated it badly. He had gotten the vowels wrong, and I had laughed, and he had laughed, and then he'd said it again, correctly, and the meaning had settled into him like a stone settling into a riverbed.

*I chose this.*

In the old tongue it is shorter than in Common. Three sounds. It doesn't just mean *I chose this* — it means *I saw the cost, and I paid it, and I am not a victim, and no one owes me, and I am free.* It is the sentence our people say over graves. It is the sentence you give someone so they can begin honestly.

I knelt in front of him in the wet grass. He watched me come without fear, which broke my heart a little more, because the Gideon I knew would have been suspicious of anyone kneeling in front of him with a serious face.

"I'm going to teach you a word," I said. "One word. It's the only one I can give back. Do you understand? The mountain took the rest, and the mountain doesn't give refunds. But this one I put somewhere it couldn't reach. I put it in the lesson. And lessons go deeper than ledgers."

"Ledgers," he repeated, and frowned, faintly, at nothing. Something stirred far down in him, a fish turning over in a deep pond, and then was still. "I don't know why that word feels like work."

"Never mind ledgers." I took his hands. His hands were steady. Mine weren't. "Say what I say. Your vowels will be terrible."

"They usually are," he said, and I nearly lost it right there, because that was such a *Gideon* thing to say, proof that some sediment of him remained, silt at the bottom of the emptied riverbed.

I said the phrase.

He frowned. Tried it. Got the vowels wrong, exactly as predicted, mangling the second sound so thoroughly that the phrase briefly meant *I ate this,* which is a different sentence entirely and a much sillier one.

"Again," I said.

He tried again. Closer.

"Again."

Third time, he got it. And I felt it happen — felt it the way you feel a key turn in a lock you're standing next to. The phrase went into him, into the hole the mountain had left, and it fit the way a keystone fits, not filling the arch but making it possible for the arch to stand at all. His eyes changed. Not with memory — I couldn't give him that, and I wouldn't lie to him by pretending — but with *meaning*. He suddenly knew something true about himself, and it was this: whatever he had been, whoever he had been, he was not a man things had merely happened to. Somewhere in the vanished country of his past, he had stood up and chosen, with open eyes, and the choosing was still his even though the reasons were gone.

He exhaled, long and slow, like a man setting down a load he hadn't known he was carrying.

"That's true," he said wonderingly. "Whatever it means. I don't know what it means. But it's *true*. It's the truest thing in me." He looked at his own hands. "Did I choose something? Did I choose—"

"Me," I said. "You chose me. Over yourself. That's what it means. That's the whole of what it means."

He was quiet for a long time. The river talked. The sun cleared the hills and laid a stripe of gold across the grass between us, and steam began to rise off the wet ground like the world breathing.

"Then I made a good choice," he said finally.

"You made a stupid choice," I said, and my voice broke, and this time I let it break where he could hear. "You made the stupidest, best choice anyone ever made, and I'm going to spend the rest of my life being worth it, so help me, I will. You'll see. I'll be so worth it."

"I believe you," he said simply. And then, because he was still in some deep unremembered way himself: "Do I owe you money?"

I laughed. It burst out of me sideways, ugly and wet and real, the first laugh since the mountain, and it scared birds out of the hedge below us, a whole gray flurry of them rising into the enormous open sky.

"A little," I said. "You owed me a copper coin once. You never paid it back."

"Then I'll owe you," he said. "A debt seems like a fine thing to build a man on. Better than most foundations." He stood, brushing grass from his knees, and looked down the hill toward the river, where — far off, past the tree line — a thread of chimney smoke rose from some small human settlement. A town. A harbor town, by the glint of water beyond it. "We'll start there. Whoever we are."

"We," I said, and held onto the word with both hands.

"We," he agreed, and offered me his hand up the hill, and I took it, and we walked down toward the smoke and the water and the beginning of whatever came next.

Behind us, on the crest of the hill, the grass bent in the wind in long slow waves, and if you had known how to look — if you had learned the old letters, the ones older than any kingdom — you might have seen that the waves spelled something. A single word, written over and over across the hillside in moving green:

*Remember.*

Not a command. An invitation. The silent god, keeping watch over what it had saved, keeping safe the one thing the mountain could not take: the fact that he had once been loved enough to be bought back.

I saw it. I didn't tell him. Some words you teach a man one morning at a time.

But as we walked, I noticed him glancing back at the hill, once, twice, with a small crease between his brows, like a man trying to recall a dream. And when we reached the bottom, he stopped, and looked at the town, and said quietly, to himself or to the wind or possibly to something listening:

"I feel like I've been here before. Not this place. Just... this. Beginning."

"You have," I said. "This is your first time. But not your last."

He considered that, and nodded, and shouldered an invisible pack out of pure habit, and we walked toward the harbor together — a girl with a kingdom waiting for her and a man with nothing but a single true sentence — while high above, faint and patient, the last sliver of the thirteenth moon hung pale in the morning sky, refusing quite yet to set.
