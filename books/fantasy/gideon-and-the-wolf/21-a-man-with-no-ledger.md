## 21. A Man With No Ledger

The nets come to me torn, and I make them whole again. It is honest work, the kind that leaves salt in the creases of your hands and no questions in your head worth losing sleep over. Most mornings I wake before the gulls do, walk down to the water, and mend whatever the sea broke yesterday. The sea breaks a great many things. It keeps me busy.

I don't know my own name.

I want to be clear about that up front, because people in Saltmere assume things. They see a man of maybe forty, broad through the shoulders, scarred across the knuckles in a way that suggests he once did something other than mend nets, and they invent a history for him. Brannick, the harbormaster, decided within a week of my arrival that I was a discharged soldier from the border wars. Old Maren at the smokehouse swears I'm a runaway priest. The children — who are the only honest people in any town — just call him Netmender, because that is what I do, and children have never once been wrong about what matters.

Netmender suits me fine. A name is a coat you wear, and I've learned you can go bareheaded through life without catching cold.

What happened to my name is not something I know either. There is a hole where that knowledge should sit, and here is the strange part: the hole doesn't hurt. When I press my thumb against the memory of it — the way you'd press a bruise to check if it's still tender — there's nothing there. No ache. No edges. Just smooth absence, like the socket of a tooth pulled so long ago the jaw has forgotten it ever held anything.

I remember how to tie seventeen kinds of knots. I remember that rain coming off the water smells different than rain coming off the land. I remember a phrase in a language nobody in Saltmere speaks, four syllables that rise and fall like something being set down gently on a table, and when I say it to myself while working, my chest does something complicated and warm that I have decided not to examine too closely. Some furniture you don't move. You just dust around it and are grateful it's there.

* * *

Tuesdays I drink one ale at the Drowned Gull, which is one more ale than I need and exactly as many as I want. Tam the barkeep waters them, everyone knows it, and nobody minds, because Tam also lets fishermen run tabs through three bad seasons and never once asked for a ledger.

That word still snags something in me. *Ledger.* I said it aloud once, early on, testing it like a coin bitten for gold, and got such a strange look from myself that I haven't repeated the experiment. It's not fear. It's more like walking past a door in your own house that's always been locked, and realizing one day that you've stopped wondering what's behind it. Not peace exactly. Something adjacent to peace. Peace's quiet cousin who lives out past the harbor and doesn't come to festivals.

It was a Tuesday, gray and low-tide smelling, when the stranger came in.

I noticed him the way you notice weather — peripherally, then all at once. He had the look of a man who'd spent years indoors arguing with paper: ink-stained fingers, a stoop like a question mark that had given up on getting an answer, and eyes that went around the room cataloguing exits before they ever settled on a face. When those eyes found mine, he went very still, the way a man goes still when he's spotted something he'd convinced himself was extinct.

He sat down across from me without asking. Tam brought two ales, unbidden, which meant the stranger had ordered before approaching, which meant this was planned, which meant I should probably be worried, and instead I felt mostly curious, with a small side of resigned. That's the thing about having no past: it leaves you nothing to defend. You can't ambush a man who isn't carrying anything.

"You don't know me," the stranger said.

"No," I agreed. "Should I?"

"Probably not. I was never important enough to be remembered by anyone whose opinion mattered." He turned his glass in a slow circle. "My name is Quill."

"That's a trade, not a name."

"It started as a punishment and became a profession. The order may surprise you." He looked up then, and his eyes were wetter than a man drinking watery ale had any right to expect. "You really don't remember anything. Sarelle said you wouldn't, but I confess I hoped she was wrong. She's usually wrong about hopeful things."

"Sarelle," I said. The name landed somewhere and didn't echo. "Another friend of yours?"

"She was our employer. Once." He drank, grimaced at the watering, drank again anyway. "Do you mind if I tell you a story? You're in it, but you won't recognize yourself. That's rather the point of telling it."

I signaled Tam for another round. If a man is going to tell you that you're in his story, the least courtesy you can extend is a full glass.

* * *

He talked for a long time. I'll give it to you the way it settled in me, which is to say sideways, the way light comes through dirty glass.

There had been an organization called the Vellum Guild. They traded in impossible things — years out of lives, names out of mouths, second chances bottled and shelved like preserves. Quill had been a broker for them until he brokered something he shouldn't have and was exiled for the crime of conscience, which, he noted dryly, was the guild's least popular commodity.

And there had been a mercenary. Sworn to a god that had gone silent, keeping the guild's ledgers, hollowed out by devotion and calling it purpose. This mercenary had found a child at his door one storm-struck night — a mute girl speaking a language older than kingdoms, carrying a copper coin stamped with a sigil from a court beneath a mountain. Her soul had been sold. Sold twice, actually; Quill's voice cracked on that part, and he took a moment with his ale. Sold to the under-court, then resold as collateral to the very guild that employed the mercenary, because the guild, it turned out, was not a neutral trader in impossible things but a predator with excellent bookkeeping.

The mercenary stole her back. Fled toward the mountain. Paid a bridge-keeper in memories. Fought an enforcer named Corvane and spared him, which Corvane never forgave him for, sparing being the one insult a professional cannot abide. Learned, from a disgraced broker — Quill raised his hand here, sheepish — that the mercenary himself might never have existed before seven years ago. That he might have been a ledger entry given flesh. Collateral, or a weapon, or possibly a gift from a god who hadn't gone silent at all but had simply been waiting for its man to be ready to listen.

At a ruined shrine, the story went, the god finally answered. One word, in the child's old language. *Remember.*

Then the mountain. The court beneath it, glittering and terrible, all its nobles wearing faces purchased from the living. A Guildmistress named Sarelle who arrived to enforce a claim and burned the contracts instead, choosing principle over survival, which Quill described with the reverence other men reserve for miracles. And a bargain struck under the thirteenth moon: one soul for one soul. The mercenary gave everything he was — his name, his history, every memory that made him himself — to buy back the child's.

"He walked out of that mountain empty," Quill said, "and she walked out whole. And the last thing anyone saw him do, before he forgot how to be seen, was—" He stopped. Started again. "There was a phrase. In her language. Four syllables. Do you know it?"

My chest did the complicated warm thing.

"*Sera-veth-ka-lune,*" I said, and the vowels came out shaped right, worn smooth like river stones, like I'd been saying them for years. "It means—" I frowned. "I don't know what it means. But I know how it means it."

Quill put his face in his ink-stained hands and shook for a while, and I gathered this was laughter and grief sharing a coat, taking turns outside.

"It means *you chose this,*" he said finally. "She gave it back to him. One memory, so he could begin honestly. And then he vanished, and Sarelle spent months looking, and I spent months looking, and here you are mending nets in a town so small it isn't on maps we could afford." He wiped his eyes. "Gideon—"

"Don't," I said.

It came out sharper than I intended. He blinked. So did I, honestly. I set down my glass and considered the feeling underneath the word, the way you'd consider a strange sound in a wall.

"I'm not angry," I told him, and found it was true. "But that name — whoever wore it — he's not me. Or I'm not him. Or..." I turned my hands over, net-scarred, salt-cracked, honest hands. "Look. If a man gives away everything he is to save a child, and then some well-meaning friend hands it all back to him wrapped in a story — hasn't the gift been wasted? He paid for her future with his entire past. The least the rest of us can do is let the transaction stand."

Quill studied me for a long moment. "Does it frighten you? Being empty?"

"Frighten?" I laughed. "Friend, I don't think you understand what you're describing. Every man I've ever mended a net beside carries ghosts. Regrets with teeth. Debts that follow." The last phrase came out of me oddly familiar, and I chose not to chase where it came from. "Me? I owe nothing to yesterday. Yesterday and I are square. Do you know how rare that is? Men spend fortunes trying to buy what I got for free."

"It wasn't free."

"No," I said quietly. "I imagine it wasn't. Which is why I intend to enjoy it thoroughly, on behalf of whoever paid."

* * *

We walked out along the seawall after, because the ale was done and Quill seemed a man who needed walking as much as talking. The tide was coming in, patient as anything, folding itself over the mudflats in long silver sheets.

"The guild collapsed," he said. "After Sarelle burned the contracts, the whole edifice came apart. Turns out an empire of debts only stands while everyone believes the debts are real. She guards the archives now. All of it — every ledger, every bottle, every stolen year — locked away where no one can ever trade in them again. She says someone has to keep watch over the evidence of what people will do to each other when they start pricing souls." He smiled thinly. "She also says, and I quote, 'Tell him the accounts are closed. All of them. Tell him he owes nothing.'"

"I don't know who 'him' is," I said. "But that sounds like good news for him."

"She'd want you to have it anyway."

"Then I'll hold onto it for him." I fished a flat stone from the wall and skipped it out across the water — five skips, clean, a skill my hands apparently kept even when everything else went. "What about the girl?"

"Wren." Quill said the name the way you set down something fragile. "Restored. Whole. By rights she's a prince of a bloodline most kingdoms forgot existed — there are people who would crown her tomorrow, and others who'd prefer her dead, and she wants nothing to do with either lot. Last I heard she'd chosen a guardian. A nameless man. They live simply. She's teaching him words."

"Words?"

"One each morning. Of the old language. He lost everything, you understand — but language isn't memory, exactly. It's closer to music. It stays in the body." Quill glanced at me sidelong. "She says he's a slow student but an earnest one. She says—" He hesitated.

"Go on."

"She says sometimes he says words nobody taught him. Just comes out with them, watching the water or mending a net. Last month it was the word for *harbor*. Before that, the word for *stay*." Quill's voice went soft. "She thinks something of him is still kept somewhere safe. That the silent god holds what he surrendered and leaks it back to him drop by drop, like a tide returning what it borrowed."

I stood at the rail a long time, watching the water come in.

There was a phrase in my chest, four syllables, meaning *you chose this*, though I couldn't have said how I knew. And now, standing over the gray-green water with an ex-broker breathing the sea air beside me, another word surfaced — unasked-for, untaught, rising like a fish to inspect the light. Two syllables, round and warm, and I knew with absolute certainty that it meant *home*, and I knew with almost-equal certainty that I'd never learned it from anyone living.

"Huh," I said.

"What?"

"Nothing." I smiled, and it felt like a door opening somewhere far away, in a house I couldn't picture, in a room I'd never seen. "Just remembered a word."

Quill opened his mouth — I could see the question assembling itself behind his eyes, all his careful broker's instincts lining up to ask *which word, what does it mean, what else comes back* — and then, to his credit, he closed it again. Some ledgers, he seemed to decide, were better left unopened.

"Will you stay here?" he asked instead. "In Saltmere?"

"Tides come in, tides go out." I shrugged. "For now, the nets need mending, and I'm the man who mends them. That's a whole identity. More than some people get."

He laughed — a real one this time, rusty but functional — and we stood together while the sun went down over water that owed neither of us anything.

He left the next morning. I helped him load his mule, and at the last he gripped my forearm like an old comrade, which puzzled both of us, and said, "If she ever finds you — the girl — be kind to her. She carried more than any soul should."

"I'm always kind to strangers," I said. "They're the only people I know."

He rode out east, toward wherever ruined guilds keep their ghosts. I watched him go, then went back down to the water, because the herring boats would be in by noon and their nets would be a disaster, and disasters, I have found, are the one crop that never fails.

That night, before sleep, I said the four syllables to the dark ceiling of my little room above the smokehouse. *You chose this.* Whoever *you* was. Whatever *this* had been.

And from somewhere — below the floorboards, below the bedrock, below the mountain roots of the world — something answered. Not in words. In warmth. Like a banked fire acknowledging a hand held near it.

*Not yet,* the warmth seemed to say. *But soon.*

I slept better than any man with no name has a right to.
