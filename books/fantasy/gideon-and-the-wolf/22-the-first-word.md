## 22. The First Word

The morning I taught Gideon Winterbourne his first word, the harbor smelled of fish and tar and the particular salt-sweetness of a town that has never once been important enough to conquer. I had decided this was my favorite kind of place. Important places had taken everything from me twice — once when I was a prince with a name too heavy to carry, and once when I was nobody at all. Unimportant places only asked that you mend your nets and mind your own business.

I was minding my own business, which happened to be him.

"Say it," I told him.

He frowned at me across the breakfast table, the way he always did now, like a man trying to read a letter written in water. His hands were big for the work they did — rope-burned, knuckles scarred from years he could not remember earning those scars. He held his bread in one of them and forgot about it, which was also usual. Bread went cold around Gideon. Everything went a little cold around Gideon, waiting for him to come back to it.

"Say what?" he asked.

"The word. *Sethra.* Say it."

He tried. Of course he tried; he was the sort of man who tried at things even when trying hurt, which was most things, lately. His tongue stumbled over the old sounds the way a cart horse stumbles on stairs. "*Seh... thra.*"

"*Sethra.* The *th* is soft. Like breath on a window."

"Like breath on a window," he repeated, and then, softer: "*Sethra.*"

And there it was. The word means *morning* in the language beneath the mountain — the language I had spoken before I could speak anything else, the language they took out of me along with my name and sold to men who wore masks over their masks. It is a small word. It does not sound like much. But when Gideon said it, wrong-shaped and clumsy as it was, something in my chest turned over like a boat righting itself in a swell.

Because here is the thing no ledger ever recorded, not even the Vellum Guild's impossible ones: words are the only currency that grows when you spend them. Give away a coin and you are poorer. Give away a word and you have made two people richer. I had learned this in the worst classroom there is, and I intended to spend every word I had.

"That's good," I said. "That's terrible, actually. We'll do it again tomorrow."

He almost smiled. He did that a lot now — almost-smiling, like a man practicing an expression he'd read about in a book. "Tomorrow," he agreed. "What's tomorrow's word?"

"You'll see." I tapped the side of my nose, which is a gesture I learned from a fishwife named Marta who had sold eleven years of her life to feed her grandchildren and regretted none of them. "A teacher doesn't give away the lesson."

"A teacher," he said, "who makes her student learn a dead language one word per day could teach him for forty years and get nowhere."

"It's not dead," I said, more sharply than I meant to. "It's sleeping."

He looked at me then — really looked, the way he sometimes did, with those gray eyes that held nothing behind them and somehow still managed to hold everything. "No," he said quietly. "I suppose it isn't. Sleeping things wake up."

I want it written down somewhere, even if only in the margin of this account, that I did not cry. Eight-year-olds — or whatever I am now, age being another thing the under-court misfiled — do not cry over breakfast. Princes especially do not. I had been a prince once. I remembered the rules, even if I remembered nothing else worth keeping from that life.

Mostly.

* * *

You should understand how we came to live in a room above a net-mender's shop in a town whose name I will not write down, because names are precious and this one deserves to stay small.

After the mountain — after the thirteenth moon finished its turn and the bargain sealed and the Pale Regent got exactly what he wanted, which was everything Gideon Winterbourne had ever been — I woke on the hillside above the shrine with the dawn in my face and my soul sitting inside me like a lamp someone had finally lit. Whole. Named. I could feel my name again, the true one, the princely one, folded up small at the center of me where I kept it safe. I have not said it aloud since. Some days I think I will. Most days I think I'll let it sleep until I'm grown and can decide what to do with a birthright that was bought and sold before I could walk.

Beside me on the hillside lay a man with no name at all.

That is not poetry. I mean it literally. I reached into him the way you reach into a pocket, gently, the way Quill had taught me souls can be touched, and found — nothing. Not emptiness the way a cup is empty. Emptiness the way a page is empty. Blank, and smooth, and waiting.

He opened his eyes while my hand was still hovering near his face, and he looked at me with perfect courtesy and no recognition whatsoever, and he said, "Hello. Do I know you?"

And I, restored prince of a drowned court, heir to bargains and blood and a palace I half-remembered from inside someone else's soul, sat down in the wet grass and told him the truth, because it was the only thing I had left to give him.

"No," I said. "But you're going to."

* * *

Quill came in the autumn, as I knew he would. Disgraced brokers cannot stay away from other people's business; it is like asking a gull not to follow a fishing boat.

He arrived on the mail packet looking thinner than when I'd last seen him, which was saying something, wearing a coat that had been good ten years ago and a hat that had never been good at all. He brought news the way he brought everything — wrapped in complaints.

"The Guild has collapsed," he announced, dropping into the chair in our little room while Gideon mended nets below us, humming a tune he didn't know he knew. "Collapsed. Sarelle burned the master contracts before the Regent's own throne, you know. Voided the resale, forced the new bargain, and then walked out of the mountain and back up to the guildhouse and started burning everything with a signature on it. Corvane helped her. Corvane! The man carried that contract with your friend's true name on it for seven years like it was scripture, and then one day he just... put it down."

"People do that," I said. "Put things down. When they finally look at what they're carrying."

Quill gave me a long look. For a moment I thought he might say something about princes and burdens, but he was kinder than his profession, in the end. He just cleared his throat and went on.

"Sarelle keeps the archives now. What's left of them. Says somebody has to guard the vault so no one else gets ideas about trading in children. She asked after you." A pause. "Both of you."

"What did you tell her?"

"That the prince was well and keeping his own counsel, and that his guardian was..." Quill waved a hand toward the window, toward the harbor, toward the man below us laughing at something Marta had shouted across the docks. "...occupied."

"He doesn't remember any of it," I said. "The guild. Sarelle. The bridge. Any of it. He doesn't remember his mother's face — he paid that at the toll, before any of this, and the mountain kept it even after everything else burned. He doesn't remember the road, or the village of borrowed years, or teaching me to whistle, or—" My voice did something I disapproved of, so I stopped talking for a moment.

Quill waited. He was good at waiting. It is most of what brokers do.

"And he minds less than you'd think," I finished. "That's the strange part. I watch him some mornings, watching the water, and he's not sad. He's not grieving. There's just... room in him. Room where all of it used to be. And he fills it up with new things. Nets. Tides. Marta's curses. My lessons."

"Is that a tragedy or a mercy?" Quill asked, and it wasn't cruel; it was a real question, broker to the bone.

"I don't know yet," I admitted. "Maybe it's both. Maybe it's neither. Maybe it's just what's true, and truth doesn't have to pick a team."

Quill snorted. "You sound like Sarelle."

"I'll take that as the compliment it secretly is."

Before he left, he paused at the door, and his voice dropped into the register he used for the rare occasions he wasn't performing. "The god, boy. The silent one. Gideon's god. Anyone hear anything? After everything?"

I shook my head. "Silent as ever."

"Hm." He turned his hat in his hands. "Seven years of silence, then a word at a ruined shrine, then silence again. If I were still a broker, I'd say that pattern smells like patience. Gods don't go quiet because they've stopped listening. They go quiet because they're waiting for the payment to mature."

Then he was gone, down the stairs and onto the packet and out of our story, mostly. And below us, Gideon hummed his unknown tune, and I wrote the day's word in my book, and the sea did what the sea does.

* * *

Winter came, and with it the routine that became our whole liturgy.

Every morning, one word. That was the rule, and I enforced it with the iron discipline of someone who had once ruled a court, or would have, if anyone had let me rule anything besides my own patience.

*Sethra.* Morning.

*Nevva.* Water — and he learned that one fast, living where we lived; by midwinter he'd point at the gray swells beyond the breakwater and murmur it like a prayer.

*Ossa.* Bread. He laughed the first time he said that one, a short surprised bark, and said it sounded like a sneeze, and I informed him that in the old courts, mocking the bread-word was punishable by a week of silence, and he said that explained why the old courts fell, and I hit him with a crust, and it was, I think, the best morning either of us had that month.

Some words were harder. *Vaelith* — grief, or the shape grief leaves in a room after it goes. He struggled with that one for three days, his mouth refusing the shapes, and on the fourth day he said it perfectly, once, staring out at the winter sea, and I did not ask him what he was thinking, because I already knew: he was thinking about the mother whose face he had paid away, the hole where she should be, the debt he couldn't even feel anymore. He said *vaelith* and I said nothing, and we drank our tea, and that was its own kind of lesson.

"Why these words?" he asked me once, in early spring. "Why not useful ones? Boat. Fish. Money. You're teaching me a language like you're building a house — foundation stones first. Who builds a house one stone a day?"

"Somebody who wants the house to last," I said.

He considered that. He was better at considering things now than he had ever been as the mercenary, I suspected — all that room in him, all that blank page, and nothing to do with it but think. "Whose language is it? Really? Yours?"

"Mine," I said. "And yours. Once."

He didn't ask what I meant. He never asked what I meant. That was the strangest mercy of the man without a past: he trusted the future I was building him more than he mourned the one he'd lost, and he never once pried at the locked door of before. Other people in town asked questions — where he'd come from, who the girl was, why a grown man let a child run his lessons. He'd just smile that almost-smile and say, "She found me. As far as I know, that's the whole story."

As far as he knew, it was.

It was not the whole story. But I had decided — and this decision cost me more than the mountain ever cost Gideon, though no ledger anywhere records it — that the rest of the story was mine to keep until he was ready. Or until I was. Whichever came first. Souls keep their own time, Quill liked to say. They are the one commodity that ripens instead of rotting.

* * *

On the last morning of spring, I taught him the hardest word I knew.

Not the longest. The longest ones are easy — old languages grow long words the way harbors grow barnacles, layer on layer, and Gideon had gotten good at climbing them. No, the hardest word I knew was four letters and a breath, and I had been saving it since the beginning, because I am a prince, and princes understand that the best gifts are the ones you make people wait for.

"*Doma*," I said.

He repeated it. Perfectly, of course. By now his tongue knew the way even when his mind didn't; the language lived in his mouth like a bird that had returned to an old nest.

"Do you know what it means?"

"No."

"It means *home*." I watched his face carefully. "Not house. Not harbor. Not the place you sleep. *Home.* The place that knows you're coming before you arrive. The place that was keeping a light on the whole time you were gone. In the old courts, you didn't say it to places. You said it to people. It meant — *you are where I belong.*"

The kitchen was very quiet. Down at the harbor, the gulls were arguing about something, the way gulls do, and the tide was turning, and Marta was probably cursing her grandsons in three languages, and none of it touched the little room where a man with no past sat holding a word like a live coal.

"Doma," he said again. Slower. Testing the weight of it.

"Yes."

"And who would I say it to?" he asked. "If I said it. Who would it mean?"

And I opened my mouth to answer — I had an answer ready, I had carried it all spring, I was going to say *me, you great lump, obviously me* — and that was when he said the word.

Not *doma*. Not the word I'd taught him.

Another one.

It came out of him quiet as breath on a window, in a voice that was and wasn't his, and it was a word I had never taught him and no one had ever taught him, because it was not a word from the language beneath the mountain at all. It was older. It was the word the mountain itself was built on, the word the Pale Regent had worn as a mask, the word that had been waiting seven silent years in whatever vault gods keep for the things they refuse to lose.

I don't know how to spell it. I have decided that no one needs to. But I know what it meant, because meaning poured off it like heat off a forge:

*I kept it. All this time, I kept it. Your home was never lost. It was only waiting for you to be ready.*

Gideon blinked. Looked down at his own hands as if they belonged to someone else — which, in fairness, they had. Then he looked up at me, and for the first time since the hillside, since *do I know you*, there was something behind his eyes. Not memory. Not quite. More like the place where memory would live, swept clean and warmed, a hearth laid and waiting for a match.

"I don't know what I just said," he told me.

"I know," I said.

"Did you teach me that one?"

"No," I said, and my voice cracked straight down the middle, princely composure and all, and I found I did not care. "Nobody taught you that one."

He nodded slowly. He picked up his bread — cold, as always, forgotten, as always — and bit into it anyway, and chewed, and looked out the window at the harbor light coming up gold on the water, at the nets and the boats and the unimportant town that had never once been conquered, and he said, in common, plain as porridge:

"Well. Whoever's keeping it — tell them thank you. And tell them I'm in no hurry."

Below us, the tide turned. Above us, the gulls wheeled against a sky going blue at the edges. And somewhere — in the silence between heartbeats, in the space where a god had waited seven patient years — I felt something that was not an answer and not a word and not a name, but was unmistakably, impossibly, a light being left on.

I got out my book. I turned to a fresh page. And I began, at the top, in careful letters, in a language that was sleeping no longer:

*Lesson one.*
