## 1. The Ledger Opens Itself

The trouble with impossible things, Jonah Nystrom had learned, was that they arrived at inconvenient hours and always expected to be entered neatly.

It was nearly the ninth bell when the summer came in. Not the season itself — the Guild had stopped dealing in whole seasons after the Incident with the Monsoon of 1187, which was still spoken of in the counting house only as *the Dampening* — but a summer, lightly used. Three months of it, secondhand, the seller claiming it had been worn only on weekends.

Jonah set down his pen and looked at the slip of vellum on his desk. It smelled, faintly, of cut grass and warm stone. Somewhere in it, he was almost certain, a bee was still going about its business.

"Lightly used," he repeated, to no one.

"Lightly used," confirmed the broker, a narrow woman named Fenn with the particular patience of someone who had sold stranger things before breakfast. "Owner passed on. His heirs want it liquidated before the frost takes an interest. You know how frost is."

Jonah did know how frost was. Frost, in the Guild's ledgers, was a creditor. It waited. It compounded. It took names first and fingers later, and it never, ever wrote anything down, which Jonah privately considered the mark of a poor bookkeeper.

He turned the slip over. The provenance was clean, the seals intact, the summer's edges only slightly foxed at the corners — a June gone brown, a July with a thumbprint of rain in the middle of it. The August was pristine. Someone had clearly been saving it.

"All right," Jonah said, and dipped his pen. "Lot number?"

"Forty-one."

"Condition?"

"Good. One owner. Stored in a cedar chest, which I'm told accounts for the—" she waved a hand at the slip, at the smell of cut grass, "—the *ambience*."

Jonah wrote: *One (1) summer, lightly used, condition good. Provenance: estate of M. Calloway, deceased. Notes: cedar-stored. Bee included, unverified.*

The Guild's counting house was a long stone room at the top of Vellum Street, and it was Jonah's particular misfortune — or calling; he had never settled which — to be its keeper of ledgers. There were eleven of them on the great shelves behind him, each the size of a tombstone and each with opinions. The Ledger of Borrowed Years had a habit of sighing when someone took out more than they could repay. The Ledger of Traded Names rustled when it was lied to. The Ledger of Weather, Sold by the Season had once bitten an apprentice, though in fairness the apprentice had been trying to sell it a hailstorm of his own manufacture.

Jonah got along with the ledgers because Jonah listened. That was his gift and his burden both: bone remembers, and ivory — carved, worked, held close to a warm hand — remembers loudest of all. He wore a small carved charm at his wrist, a sparrow his grandmother had made him when he was seven, and through it he had heard the dead his whole life. Not sentences, usually. More like weather. A pressure. A mood moving through the grain.

Tonight the ledgers were quiet, which should have been his first warning.

He finished the entry, sanded it, and was reaching for the blotter when the twelfth ledger turned its own page.

Now. It must be said that Jonah had worked in the counting house for nine years, and in that time he had seen a ledger do many things. He had seen the Ledger of Borrowed Years slam itself shut on a man's fingers. He had seen the Ledger of Traded Names weep ink, actual weeping, over a child sold out of her own name. He had seen the Weather Ledger dream, visibly, of rain.

He had never seen one turn its own page. Ledgers were patient things. They waited to be read. That was the whole covenant of paper and hand.

But the twelfth ledger — the old one, the one nobody used, the one shelved highest and dusted least, its spine stamped in letters so worn that Jonah had spent nine years assuming it said *PROPHECIES, MISCELLANEOUS* when it might, for all he knew, have said *RECIPES* — the twelfth ledger turned its page the way a person turns to look at you.

And then it began to write.

The pen was still in Jonah's hand. He felt it move — not his hand moving the pen, but the pen agreeing to be moved, the way a door agrees to be opened. The nib touched the twelfth ledger's page and ink came out of it in a hand that was not his. It was a fine, careful hand, old-fashioned, with a slight backward slant, the writing of someone who had learned letters when letters were expensive.

The hand wrote today's date.

Then it wrote, beneath the date, in that same patient backward-slanting script:

*The bone-carver is listening. Good. We have been so quiet for so long.*

Jonah did not scream. He was proud of this later. What he did was set the pen down with the exaggerated care of a man handling something that might be asleep, and then he said, in the reasonable voice he used on brokers and on ledgers both:

"You have the wrong book. This is a counting house. We record things. We don't — " he gestured at the page, at the date, at the sentence sitting there like a cat that had let itself in, "— *correspond*."

The ink dried. The page waited.

Then, slowly, the whisper came.

It came up through the paper the way cold comes up through floorboards. Jonah had heard the dead through bone all his life — a hum, a pressure, a mood in the grain — and this was that, but *more*, and *arranged*, and it was not one voice but many, layered, the way a choir stacks a chord: low voices holding the floor of it, high voices turning above like weathercocks. They were not singing yet. They were tuning. Somewhere very far away and very close, something was clearing its throat, and the throat was a century wide.

*The covenant,* the whisper said, or the whisper's ghost did, or the page did, and the word came through Jonah's wrist-charm and up the bones of his arm like a struck bell. *The tithe. The songs. Who has kept them? Who has kept count?*

"I keep count," Jonah heard himself say. His voice sounded very small in the long stone room. "That's — that's rather the point of me."

*Then count this,* said the page, and the pen, unbidden, wrote one more line before it stilled:

*Tonight the ledger opens. Tomorrow the girl arrives with her mother's voice. After that, the winter that has no bottom.*

The ink sank into the page and was gone — not dried, *gone*, drawn down into the paper like water into a drought-stricken field, and the twelfth ledger sat there being an ordinary book again, smug in the way of all books that know more than they say.

Jonah sat for a long moment. The bee-smell of the summer drifted up from the slip beside him, absurd and green and impossibly far away.

"Right," he said finally, to the room at large. "I'm going to need a larger blotter."

He should have fetched the guildmaster. That was procedure. Impossible entries went to the guildmaster, who initialled them, and the guildmaster's initials made impossible things official, and official impossible things were, by some alchemy Jonah had never fully understood, safe. That was the whole architecture of the Guild: you wrote it down, someone important signed it, and reality agreed to be bound.

But when Jonah stood and turned toward the guildmaster's door at the end of the counting house, he found himself looking instead at the great shelves. At the eleven ledgers with their opinions and their moods. And he thought, with a clarity that arrived the way cold air arrives under a door: *the ledgers are honest. It's the hands that sign them I'm not sure about.*

The whisper had said *who has kept count*, and it had not sounded like a question. It had sounded like an indictment wearing a question's clothes.

Jonah sat back down. He opened the day-ledger to a fresh page, and in his own small, careful hand — the hand of a man who had been keeping honest books for nine years in a building he was suddenly no longer sure of — he wrote:

*Tonight, ninth bell. A summer, lightly used, entered and sealed. Lot forty-one. And one (1) prophecy, unsolicited, regarding a girl, a voice, and a winter. Filed under: things I did not agree to.*

He considered, then added: *Bee included, unverified.*

The ledgers behind him were quiet. But as he snuffed the lamp and gathered his coat, Jonah's wrist-charm hummed against his pulse — that old familiar pressure, that weather in the grain — and for the first time in his life, the dead did not sound like weather.

They sounded like they were waiting for someone.

He was halfway down the stairs, keys in hand, when the knock came at the counting-house door below. Three knocks. Firm. The knock of someone who had traveled a long way in bad weather and had not come to apologize for it.

Through the sparrow at his wrist, faint as a memory of warmth, Jonah felt the dead lean in.

*Tomorrow,* the page had written.

The ledgers, Jonah was learning, kept excellent time.
