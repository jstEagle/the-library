## 2. A Girl with Her Mother's Voice

The Vellum Guild's counting house had a knocker shaped like a closed mouth, which Hazel Ashgrave considered the most honest piece of architecture in the city. She stood before it in the grey of early morning, her mother's debt papers wrapped in oilcloth against her chest, and knocked the way you knock when you intend to be heard over the sound of your own fear: three times, hard, with the heel of her hand.

The door opened on a clerk so young he still had ink on his knuckles from yesterday.

"We're not open," he said.

"Then I'll wait inside where it's warm." She stepped past him, and he made a noise like a kettle deciding against boiling.

The counting house smelled of tallow and cold paper, that particular dryness of a room where everything valuable is kept away from moisture, including feeling. Ledgers lined the walls in their thousands, spines cracked, corners soft with handling. Somewhere above, a clock ticked with the smugness of a thing that has never once been wrong about anything that mattered to it.

"I'm here about Maren Ashgrave," Hazel announced, to the clerk, to the room, to whatever powers kept books in such a place. "She's been gone eleven months. Her accounts are owed settlement. Debts honored, holdings returned, per guild law, section—" she unfolded the paper she'd copied out by lantern light three times to get it right, "—fourteen, subsection six."

The clerk blinked at her. "You've read the statutes?"

"I've *memorized* them. There's a difference. Reading is what people do when they have someone to read to."

He opened his mouth — probably to explain that guild law was more of a suggestion the guild made to itself — and that was when the older man came down the stairs.

Hazel knew him at once, though they'd never met. Not because anyone had described him, but because he moved like a man carrying something fragile up a long flight of steps he climbed twenty times a day. Bone-carver's hands, ink-stained to the second knuckle. A face that looked like it had been designed for kindness and then left out in weather.

Jonah Nystrom. Her mother had written his name twice in the margins of her letters, always small, always underlined once, as if even in ink she didn't want to say it too loudly.

"You're the ledger-keeper," Hazel said.

"I'm the bone-carver," Jonah said. "The ledgers keep themselves, mostly. It's one of the things nobody warns you about this job." He looked at her the way a man looks at a door he's sure he locked and finds standing open. "You're Maren's daughter. You have her voice."

"Everyone says that."

"They're wrong, usually, when they say it. They mean you sound alike. You don't. You *are* alike — there's a weight in how you speak, like every word gets weighed before it's allowed out." He said it plainly, without flattery, and it landed harder than flattery would have. "What do you want?"

"What I said. Her debts honored."

"Maren Ashgrave had no debts."

"She had *accounts*," Hazel said, and unwrapped the oilcloth. "And these."

* * *

Her mother's papers were not debts in any way the clerk could enter into a book, which became clear within minutes. The first was a receipt for one borrowed winter, repaid in full, signed by a hand that wasn't her mother's. The second was a promissory note for a name — just the name, no explanation of whose, or why anyone would trade one — marked PAID IN SONGS, which meant nothing to Hazel and visibly a great deal to Jonah, who went very still in the way of a man hearing a floorboard creak somewhere below the ground floor.

The third paper was the prophecy.

Half of one, anyway. The page had been torn down the middle with a violence that didn't match anything else in her mother's careful hand, because the writing wasn't her mother's hand at all. It was Hazel's.

That was the part she hadn't told the clerk. That was the part she'd carried through the dark streets at dawn telling herself she was mistaken, that grief did strange things to the eye, that everyone's handwriting looked like their own if you stared long enough.

It didn't look like her handwriting. It *was* her handwriting — the way she'd write in ten years, maybe fifteen, after her letters had settled into themselves. She recognized the slight forward lean she'd only recently begun to develop. She recognized the way she crossed her sevens, which she'd learned from her mother and which her mother had never done.

"When did you copy this?" Jonah asked quietly.

"I didn't."

"Hazel—"

"That's my hand." Her voice came out steadier than she felt, which was its own kind of frightening. "I know what my writing looks like. I also know what it will look like, because apparently I've already seen it. That page is half of something, and the other half is missing, and my mother tore it, and then my mother disappeared, and I want those two facts explained by someone who keeps books on impossible things."

Silence. The clock ticked. Somewhere in the walls, faintly, something that might have been wind and might have been humming shifted its weight.

Jonah took the page with both hands, the way he'd take a bone from someone's family, and held it near the lamp. He read it once silently. Then his lips moved, reading it again aloud without sound, and stopped.

"Read it," Hazel said.

"It's not mine to read first."

"My mother's missing. Read it."

So he read it:

*"When the ledger turns to its own day,*
*the Nystrom boy will hear what's owed.*
*The Ashgrave girl carries the other half —*
*her mother's voice, her own hand,*
*and between them, the line unbroken:"*

And there the page ended. Torn.

"The line unbroken," Hazel repeated. "Between us. What line?"

Jonah set the page down very carefully, as though it might bruise. Then he walked to the wall of ledgers, ran his finger along the spines with the familiarity of long habit, and pulled down a volume so old its leather had gone the color of tea. He carried it to the table, opened it to a ribbon marker, and turned the book toward her.

"This is the covenant ledger. It predates the guild. Nobody writes in it anymore — or rather, nobody admits to writing in it, because entries appear that no one claims." His finger traced down a column of names in a dozen different hands, some faded to whispers, some sharp as yesterday. "Look at the last lines."

Hazel leaned in. The ink was old, brown-black, but the letters were clear:

*Maren Ashgrave, who sang the tithe and was not paid.*

And beneath it, in fresher ink that seemed almost embarrassed to be there:

*Hazel Ashgrave, who carries the other half.*

And beneath *that*, in a hand she now knew better than her own future:

*Jonah Nystrom, who hears what's owed.*

Their names. Three names, stacked like stones in a wall, sharing a single line of entry as though the book had run out of room for separate lives.

"That's impossible," she said. "I've never been here. I've never touched this book."

"No," Jonah agreed. "You haven't." He was looking at the page with an expression she couldn't read — not fear exactly, more like recognition arriving ahead of understanding. "But it's been expecting you. Or you've been expected. With old books it's hard to say which comes first." He closed it gently. "Your mother sang the tithe. Do you know what that means?"

"No. And I'm starting to suspect that not knowing is going to cost me."

"It costs everyone eventually. The question is whether you pay knowing or unknowing." He rubbed his eyes with the heel of his hand, and for a moment he looked less like a keeper of impossible ledgers and more like a tired man who wanted very much to be an ordinary one. "Maren used to come here. Once a season, for years. She'd sing in the back room — just songs, nothing anyone would call payment. Old ones. Winter songs. And the ledgers would... settle. Everything balanced. I never understood it. I thought she was eccentric."

"And now?"

"Now I think she was paying something. And I think eleven months ago, she stopped being able to." He met Hazel's eyes. "The night she disappeared, the covenant ledger wrote today's date on a blank page. By itself. In ink that isn't ours."

* * *

They sat across from each other at the counting table, the torn prophecy between them like a border neither wanted to cross alone. The clerk had retreated to a corner with a stack of receipts he was alphabetizing with the desperate industry of a man pretending not to exist.

"Tell me about her," Jonah said. "Not the missing. The her."

Hazel found, to her surprise, that she wanted to. "She hummed while she cooked. Always the same tune. I used to think she'd forgotten the words, but now I wonder if she was keeping them somewhere safe." She turned her mother's ring on her finger — plain silver, worn thin at the band. "She told me once that voices don't die, they just go where they're needed. I thought it was comfort-talk. Mothers say things like that." She laughed, short and unamused. "I think she was giving me instructions."

"She gave you more than instructions. She gave you half a page in your own handwriting." Jonah tapped the prophecy gently. "Which means somewhere, sometime, you finish writing this. Which means somewhere, sometime, you learn what the rest of it says."

"Or I tear it myself, and give the other half to someone else's daughter, and we can all enjoy this same conversation in thirty years."

Despite himself — she saw it happen, the corner of his mouth losing a brief war — Jonah smiled. "You argue like her too."

"People keep saying that."

"I mean it as a compliment. Mostly."

The lamp flickered. Not the way lamps flicker when drafts move — the flame bent toward the covenant ledger, sitting closed on the table, and held there, leaning, like a listener.

Neither of them spoke. The flame straightened slowly, deciding, perhaps, that it had said enough.

"It does that," Jonah said quietly. "More lately."

"How much more?"

"Every day since the date appeared." He laid his palm flat on the ledger's cover, and Hazel watched his face change — watched him listen to something through the leather, the way she imagined a man might listen through a door to a conversation he wasn't invited to. When he lifted his hand, his fingers were trembling, very slightly, and he curled them closed as if embarrassed by them.

"There's singing," he said. "Under the pages. Under all of them. It's been getting louder for months, and I told myself it was the building settling, and I am done telling myself things that aren't true." He looked up at her, and whatever wariness had sat between them all morning had burned down to something plainer and more useful. "Your mother's voice is in there. I think some of it is hers."

The words went through Hazel like cold water finding a crack. She put her hand on the ledger too — because of course she did, because her mother was possibly on the other side of the leather, and no statute in fourteen subsections was going to stop her — and felt nothing but old paper and cold air, and hated the nothing, and kept her hand there anyway.

"I don't hear anything," she whispered.

"You wouldn't. Not yet. Hearing is my trade; singing is yours. We're two halves of the same bad news." He drew the ledger toward himself, then paused, struck by something, and flipped to the page with their three names. "Wait. Look."

Beneath Jonah's line, fresh ink was surfacing the way frost surfaces on glass — slowly, letter by letter, in no hand either of them recognized, unless it was the book's own:

*The other half waits where the dead trade warmth.*
*Come before the frost finishes falling.*

Hazel read it twice. "Where the dead trade warmth," she said. "That's a place?"

"It's a market," Jonah said, and his voice had gone carefully flat, the flatness of a man holding a door shut against something. "A border-market. I have..." He reached into his collar and drew out a small carved bone on a cord — white, worn smooth, no bigger than a thumb. As Hazel watched, it began, very faintly, to hum. "I have a guide. Apparently it agrees with the book, which is unsettling, because it's never hummed at anything before except me."

The clerk in the corner had stopped alphabetizing. "You're not actually going," he said. "To the border-market. Nobody goes there. People who go there come back *short*."

"Short of what?" Hazel asked.

"Yes," the clerk said, with feeling. "Exactly."

Jonah was already wrapping the ledger in cloth, moving with the decision of a man who has stopped negotiating with himself. "We take the book. We take the page. If your mother's other half is out there, we find it, and we find out what she was paying, and why it stopped." He looked at Hazel across the table, this stranger with her mother's voice and her own future handwriting, and offered her the only honest thing he had. "I won't pretend this is safe. I'll only promise you'll be lied to less than you would be staying home."

Hazel picked up the torn prophecy and folded it into her coat, over her heart, where important documents and worse decisions belong.

"Then let's go collect the rest of my sentence," she said.

The bone hummed. The ledger, wrapped and silent, seemed to lean toward the door — toward the grey morning, the falling frost, and the long road to wherever the dead kept their market.

Behind them, unnoticed by either, the covenant ledger's ribbon marker slipped loose and lay across the page like a finger pointing the way.
