## 3. What the Dead Charge

The bone had been humming since sundown.

It was a small thing, no longer than a thumb, carved from the shin of a goose that had died before Jonah was born. He kept it in the third drawer of his desk, beneath a stack of receipts for traded laughter — the guild did brisk business in laughter, mostly from children whose parents needed rent money — and for eleven years it had never made so much as a whisper. Bones spoke when they were worked. This one had been finished decades ago.

Now it hummed like a struck tuning fork, and every time Jonah tried to put it down, the hum climbed half a note higher, patient and insistent, the way his grandmother used to wait at the table when he'd said he wasn't hungry and both of them knew he was lying.

"All right," he told it. "All right."

He wrapped it in flannel, tucked it into his coat pocket, and let it steer him out of the counting house past midnight, through streets where the frost had begun to arrive early and stay late. The bone tugged left at the cooper's yard. It tugged him across the canal on ice that creaked but held. And when the houses thinned out and the road gave up entirely, becoming merely a suggestion of a path between black pines, the hum settled into something almost contented, a cat purring in a pocket.

Jonah Nystrom was forty-one years old and had spent his whole life listening to the dead through ivory and antler and the occasional stubborn tooth. He knew their voices the way a piano tuner knows middle C. But he had never once followed them anywhere. The dead asked; he answered. That was the arrangement. It was a good arrangement, tidy as double-entry bookkeeping, and he was about to walk straight off the edge of it into whatever waited at the end of a humming goose bone.

The trees opened onto a valley that should not have existed.

He knew this country. He'd walked it as a boy. There was no valley here, only a long gray slope of scree. Yet below him lay a market: stalls of pale canvas strung with lanterns that burned without heat or smoke, lanes packed with figures moving in that particular unhurried way of people who have nowhere else to be and all the time in the world to not be there. The light was wrong — soft, sourceless, the light of a memory of afternoon rather than an actual one. And the sound. The sound was singing, everywhere, woven through everything: vendors singing prices, lovers singing arguments, someone by a well singing what could only have been a complaint about her brother-in-law, set to a melody so lovely that Jonah stood on the ridge and forgot, briefly, how to breathe.

*The Choir,* he thought. Not the dead-as-he-knew-them, murmuring through carved whale tooth about unpaid debts and unkind last words. These were the dead *organized.* The dead in *harmony.*

The bone in his pocket hummed approval and pulled him downhill.

* * *

The market accepted him the way water accepts a stone. No one stared. A woman selling candied winter — actual winter, crystallized, each shard glittering with a different storm — sang him a greeting in three-part harmony with herself and waved him toward a stall near the center, where an old man sat behind a counter of black ice, mending a violin with fingers that were slightly, politely transparent.

"You're the ledger-keeper," the old man said. Not a question. His voice came out layered, two voices wearing one throat, an octave apart. "We've heard your handwriting. It's very neat."

"Thank you," said Jonah, because his grandmother had raised him to accept compliments even under circumstances of profound supernatural disquiet. "I'm afraid I don't know you."

"No," the old man agreed cheerfully. "You know our work, though. Or rather—" he tied off a string, plucked it, listened with his head cocked like a bird's, "—you've been *recording* it. Eleven years of entries. Borrowed years. Traded names. Weather sold by the season." He looked up, and his eyes were kind and terribly old. "Tell me, bookkeeper. Where do you imagine those things come from?"

Jonah opened his mouth. Closed it. In the counting house, goods arrived in crates with manifests. Summer came in barrels marked with the guild seal. Names arrived on paper. He had never once asked, because asking questions in a counting house is like asking the tide where it's been — the answer changes nothing and makes everyone uncomfortable.

"I record what crosses the desk," he said finally.

"Mm." The old man set down the violin. "And do you record what leaves?"

"I record sales."

"Do you ever wonder," the old man said, gently, the way you'd ask a child if they'd checked under the bed, "why the summer we sell you is always *used?* Lightly used, the manifest says. Why would the dead keep used summers, bookkeeper? What do you suppose we were doing with them first?"

The market sang on around them. Somewhere a fiddle struck up something in six-eight time, and a dozen voices caught it and passed it along like a bucket brigade.

"The tithe," Jonah said slowly. The word surfaced from somewhere deep — from the oldest ledgers, the ones locked in the cellar, the ones written in a hand so old the ink had gone brown. He'd catalogued them once. There had been a recurring entry, annual, for two hundred years, and then, sixty-one years ago, it simply stopped. He'd assumed the account closed. "The guild paid a tithe. Songs."

"Songs," the old man confirmed. "Every year, on the turning of winter, the living brought us songs. New ones. Made-up ones, sung badly, sung drunk, sung by children who got the words wrong. Do you know what a wrong word is worth over here?" His translucent fingers spread wide. "Everything. A wrong word means the song is *alive*, means it's still growing, still being carried by mouths that might drop it any moment. We kept winter gentle on that diet. Two hundred years of your clumsy, misspelled music, and the frost came when it was called and left when it was told."

"And then?"

"And then the guild decided songs were valuable." The old man's second voice dropped away, leaving just the one, thin and dry. "Decided that if the dead would trade warmth for music, the guild could buy the music cheap and sell the warmth dear. So they stopped paying the tithe and started *harvesting.* Every song that crossed their desks — bought, seized, appraised, warehoused. Sixty-one years of inventory." He smiled, and it was the worst smile Jonah had ever seen, because it held no blame at all. Only arithmetic. "Your ledgers are honest, bookkeeper. That's the joke of it. Every line true. Built on theft from top to bottom, and every line true."

The ground did not literally tilt, but Jonah's understanding of it did. He thought of the warehouse rows he'd inventoried — the crates of lullabies, the sealed jars of work songs, the entire shelf labeled *WEDDING MUSIC, ASSORTED, PRE-OWNED.* He'd assumed the guild bought them fair. The guild's books balanced. The guild's books *always* balanced. He had staked his whole quiet life on that.

"We didn't know," he said, and heard how it sounded, and winced.

"No," the old man agreed. "But the frost doesn't read ledgers. The Choir grieves, and grief has weather in it. You'll have noticed the winter arriving early." He picked the violin back up, turned it over. "Sixty-one years of stolen song, and now the covenant's broken thread is coming due. The dead are owed. Winter is collecting."

"What happens if nobody pays?"

The old man looked at him with genuine, gentle pity. "Bookkeeper. Look around you. What do you think all this *is?* We're not waiting. We're trading. Memories for warmth — the dead have plenty of both, and the living are short on one and freezing for lack of the other. Your villages are selling their names to stay warm through February. I sold a man his own wedding last week. He cried, and then he thanked me, and then he couldn't remember why he'd come." He sighed. "The market does what markets do. It's the covenant that stopped doing what covenants do."

The goose bone in Jonah's pocket went suddenly, urgently loud — not humming now but *singing*, a thin high thread of melody that cut through the market noise. Heads turned. Down the lane, several translucent figures paused mid-bargain.

The old man's eyebrows rose. "Well," he said. "That's not one of ours."

"It's a goose," Jonah said helplessly. "From my desk drawer."

"That," said the old man, "is a summons. And it isn't calling you, bookkeeper. It's calling *through* you. Someone on the far side wants a word with the one man alive who keeps honest books about dishonest goods." He leaned across the counter of black ice, and for a moment both his voices returned, harmonizing low. "Go home. Check your oldest ledgers. The brown-ink ones. Find the page where the tithe ends, and look at who signed the order to stop paying."

"Why?"

"Because names matter here," the old man said, "and because you're going to want to know whose family has been warm all winter while yours froze." He smiled again, that terrible kind smile. "And because she asked me to tell you. The one who sent the goose. She says you'll remember her voice."

The bone sang on. And beneath its thin high note, faint as frost forming on glass, Jonah heard — unmistakable, impossible, sixty years gone — his grandmother, humming.

* * *

He walked back up the ridge with the market's singing fading behind him like a tide going out, and the cold that met him at the treeline was no ordinary cold. It had intention in it. It pressed against his chest like a hand checking whether the door was locked.

In his pocket, the goose bone had gone quiet, satisfied, spent. But the tune it had carried stayed lodged in his head, and it took him most of the dark walk home to place it, and when he did he stopped dead in the middle of the frozen road.

It was a lullaby. His grandmother's lullaby — the one she'd sung him, the one he'd found *in the guild warehouse*, shelf nine, crate forty, jarred and labeled and priced.

They hadn't bought it. They'd taken it. And somewhere in the dark ahead, sixty-one years of stolen songs were waiting in the ledgers, itemized in his own neat handwriting, every line true.

He put his head down and walked faster, toward the counting house, toward the cellar, toward the brown-ink books and the name he was now afraid to find signed at the bottom of the page where the singing stopped.
