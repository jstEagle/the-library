## 4. Enforcers at the Door

Hazel had been in the counting house for three days, and in that time she had learned three things about the Vellum Guild.

First: the tea was terrible. Jonah Nystrom kept a tin of leaves so old they had given up on flavor entirely and settled instead for a kind of apologetic bitterness, like an apology from someone who didn't mean it. She drank it anyway. It was warm, and warmth was becoming a currency she understood the value of.

Second: bone-carvers hummed when they worked. Jonah probably didn't know he did it. He'd sit hunched over a sliver of ivory with his little knives, and a tune would leak out of him — something slow and minor-keyed that made the lamp flames lean toward him like listeners at a recital. When Hazel asked what it was, he'd said, "I don't know. The bones start it," and then looked embarrassed, as though he'd admitted to talking to his supper.

Third — and this was the thing she turned over at night on her cot behind the shelving, staring at the underside of a desk where some long-dead clerk had stuck forty years of chewing gum — third: the prophecy ledger did not like being ignored.

It sat on its lectern in the reading room like a cat pretending not to watch you. *The Annals of the Choir's Keeping*, said the spine, in gold gone brown. A century of prophecies, most of them wrong in the way weather reports are wrong — confidently, and by only a little. But the entry for tonight was neither wrong nor little.

*On this date,* it read, in ink that had dried a hundred years before Hazel was born but still smelled faintly of rain, *the daughter of the Keeper returns to the counting house, carrying half a song. Let the guild decide whether it is owed or owing.*

And beneath that, in a second hand — cramped, slanting, unmistakably hers, though she had never written it — the words she had shown Jonah on her first night here:

*Hazel and the Choir. One stays. One goes.*

"You keep looking at it like it's going to bite," Jonah said now, from the doorway. He had two cups of the terrible tea and the expression of a man who had brought them because bringing things was easier than saying comforting words.

"It already bit." Hazel tapped the page. "It wrote my handwriting, Jonah. My *future* handwriting. Do you know how rude that is? I haven't decided how I hold a pen yet."

"Maybe you'll develop the slant out of spite."

"Maybe I will." She took the cup. Their fingers brushed around it, briefly, and both of them pretended that hadn't happened, which was becoming a small tradition between them, like the tea.

Jonah set his cup down and came to stand beside her at the lectern. Up close, the ledger's pages had a texture she hadn't noticed before — faint ridges, like skin after a long bath. Like something waiting to be written on rather than something already full.

"The guildmaster knows we've been reading it," Jonah said quietly. "I filed the access slip. That's how it works here. Everything gets a slip."

"Even betrayal?"

"*Especially* betrayal. Betrayal is very well documented." He almost smiled. "If enforcers come, they'll come for the book. Not us. We're clerks. Clerks are expensive to replace."

"And if we're standing next to the book?"

"Then we should practice looking like furniture."

Hazel was about to tell him that he would make extremely unconvincing furniture — too many opinions in the shoulders — when every lamp in the counting house went out at once.

Not flickered. *Went out*, all together, like a held breath.

Then came the sound from the street below: boots. Many boots, moving in the particular rhythm of people who have rehearsed arriving somewhere unwelcome. Iron rang against iron — the guild's seal-knock, three strikes and a pause, three strikes and a pause, the rhythm of law itself coming up the stairs.

"They're early," Jonah whispered.

"For what?"

"For anything. Enforcers are always early. It's how you know they're enforcers and not guests."

He moved fast then, faster than she'd seen him move, blowing the lamps' wicks cold and sweeping the prophecy ledger off its lectern into his arms. And the ledger — Hazel felt it before she understood it — the ledger *leaned into him*. Settled against his chest like a child being picked up.

"It wants to go somewhere," she breathed.

"I know." His voice had gone strange and thin. "I hear it. Through the cover. It's — singing, under the vellum. Hazel, it's the same tune the bones—"

The door burst open.

There were six of them, which seemed excessive even for a guild that documented betrayal. They wore grey coats cut for movement and faces cut for nothing at all, and the lead one carried a writ on a rod of black iron, unrolled, official, and utterly uninterested in conversation.

"By order of the Guildmaster," the lead enforcer read, in the flat voice of someone who has read this sentence over many bodies, "the Annals of the Choir's Keeping are seized as evidence of irregular covenant activity. All persons present will stand aside. Resistance will be entered into the record."

"Entered into the record," Hazel repeated. "You write down resistance?"

"We write down everything." A pause. "It's the guild."

She could feel Jonah trembling beside her — not with fear, she realized, but with the effort of holding still while the book in his arms pulled toward the enforcers like a dog pulling toward another dog across a street. Or away from them. She couldn't tell which, and the not-knowing sat in her stomach like a swallowed stone.

"The writ says evidence," Jonah said. His voice was admirably steady. "Evidence implies a hearing. When is the hearing?"

"There is no hearing for covenant matters."

"Then it isn't evidence. It's theft with paperwork."

The lead enforcer looked at him for a long moment, and Hazel understood that this man was not stupid; he was simply paid to be a wall, and walls do not debate. "Step aside, carver."

And that was when the ledger opened itself in Jonah's arms.

Not flipped — *opened*, wide as a mouth, pages riffling backward through a century of dates with a sound like wind through winter reeds. The lamps stayed dead but the book gave its own light now, a pale glow rising off the margins, and Hazel saw that the margins were moving. The white space around each prophecy was rippling, deepening, going from paper-white to a white with depth to it, a white like snowfall at dusk, like—

Like a door.

"Hazel," Jonah said, and his voice cracked clean down the middle. "I think it's offering."

"That's insane."

"Yes. Probably. Also I think the alternative is those gentlemen." The enforcers had stopped advancing; even walls, apparently, hesitated at a glowing book. One of them was backing toward the stairs. "It's your mother's book. Your line. I don't think it will take me."

The lead enforcer recovered himself and lunged — and Hazel did the single least sensible thing of her life, which was saying quite a lot, because she had crossed a country on the strength of half a prophecy and a name.

She stepped into the margin.

* * *

Falling into a book, it turns out, feels less like falling and more like being *read*.

Hazel dropped through white space that pressed on all sides of her at once, gentle and enormous, and every inch of her skin felt like a page being turned. Words went past her — not past her eyes, past her whole self — centuries of them, prophecies in a hundred hands, and most slid by without touching her, but some snagged:

*a winter with no bottom,*

*the tithe unpaid, unpaid, unpaid,*

*and a girl who carries half a song, and a carver who hears what he cuts,*

—and one line, close and warm as breath in her ear, in the cramped slanting hand she was beginning to hate and love in equal measure:

*not yet, little keeper. further in.*

Then the whiteness tore open like a curtain, and Hazel fell out onto frozen grass under a sky she recognized and did not recognize — the same stars as home, but arranged with a confidence hers lacked, brighter, closer, as if the sky here had never learned to be modest.

Snow lay on the ground in drifts that were not snow. She knew this the way you know a dream is a dream: the drifts glittered, but the glitter was sound. Each flake caught light and gave back a note, tiny and clear, millions of them, so that the whole field shimmered with a music like the world's largest music box playing very, very slowly.

The border-market. Jonah had described it — the stalls of traded memories, the Choir trading warmth for songs — but description had not prepared her for the *cold*. Not the temperature. The cold was fine. The cold was the kind of honest cold that minds its own business. What chilled her was the quiet underneath the music-box shimmer: a silence shaped exactly like a choir that has stopped mid-hymn.

"Hazel."

She spun.

Her mother stood ten feet away among the singing snowdrifts, exactly as Hazel remembered her and entirely different — no shawl, no travel-worn boots, no tiredness worn like a coat. Her hair was loose. Her hands were empty. She looked, Hazel thought with a lurch that was half joy and half fury, *rested*, and there was something obscene about that, something that made Hazel want to shake her and hold her in the same motion.

"Mama."

"Don't come closer, love. Not yet. The margin holds you and the market holds me, and if we touch before the terms are right, the terms get *expensive*." Her mother smiled, and it was her mother's smile, crooked on the left side first. "You found the ledger. Of course you found the ledger. You're my daughter; you find everything. You found the hidden biscuits when you were four."

"You *left*." The words came out harder than Hazel meant them, sharper, and she let them stay sharp. "No note. No word. I followed your debts across half the country, Mama. Debts. Because they were the only trail you left."

"I know." Her mother's face did something complicated — grief and guilt and a fierce, stubborn love all trying to use the same features at once. "I'm sorry. I'm sorrier than sorry. But listen to me, because we may not get long, and the book brought you here for a reason, and the reason is wearing grey coats and climbing your stairs right now." She stepped nearer, stopping just short of the invisible line between them, and her voice dropped low and urgent. "The guild didn't lose the covenant, Hazel. They *ended* it. Deliberately. The tithe of songs — the payment that kept the Choir bound and winter gentle — your guildmaster's family voted it out, generations ago, and they've been harvesting what the dead used to be paid ever since. Borrowed years. Sold summers. Every impossible thing in those ledgers is stolen goods."

Hazel's stomach turned over. Three days of terrible tea and humming carvers and a book that leaned like a child — and underneath it all, theft, generations deep, dressed in slips and stamps and hearings-that-don't-exist.

"The law won't save you," her mother said. "The law *is* the theft. So you go around it. You and the carver. There's a way to remake the covenant — the eldest singer will tell you the terms, and the terms are honest, which is worse than unfair, so brace yourself. And Hazel—" her mother's voice caught, and for a moment she was just a woman who had abandoned her daughter and knew it, "—when you learn the price, and you will hate it, remember: freely given is the only coin they accept. Nobody can pay it for anybody else. Not even me. *Especially* not me."

"Mama, what price? What—"

Boots. Behind her, in the white. The margin was opening again, and through it she could hear Jonah shouting her name and the flat voice of the lead enforcer reading something official at a book that was ignoring him magnificently.

Her mother stepped back into the snowdrifts, and the singing flakes rose around her like applause. "Go back, love. Read the rest of the entry. The part in *my* hand." She smiled, crooked-side-first, eyes bright with tears that refused to fall in this honest cold. "And Hazel — the handwriting you're so offended by? Practice your slant. You're going to need it sooner than the book thinks."

The whiteness closed over Hazel like water.

* * *

She landed on the counting house floor with the ledger splayed beneath her like a caught bird, and six enforcers staring at the space where a girl had just poured herself out of a century-old book.

For a moment, nobody moved. Even the lamps seemed curious.

Then Hazel got up, brushed the margin-light off her skirts, and looked the lead enforcer dead in his wall of a face.

"The writ's invalid," she said, "because the book isn't guild property. It never was. It's a *creditor*." She put her hand flat on the open page, feeling the faint ridge-and-skin pulse of it under her palm, feeling it lean into her touch the way it had leaned into Jonah. "And it just showed me its accounts."

Jonah, still clutching his cold tea, mouth slightly open, managed: "Hazel. You were *inside* it."

"I know."

"For how long?"

"Long enough." She turned to the ledger, to the final entry, to the lines beneath the prophecy in her own future hand — lines that had not existed three days ago, written now in a cramped, slanting script she recognized with a jolt that went through her like the cold of the other side.

Her mother's hand. Fresh ink. Still smelling of rain.

*The tithe was ended by choice,* it read. *Find the guildmaster's fire. Burn what binds you. Then bring the carver to the ice — and ask the eldest singer what an anchor costs.*

Hazel looked up at Jonah, and the dread in her chest sharpened, wonderfully and terribly, into something with an edge. Resolve.

"Get your coat," she said. "We're done asking the guild's permission. We're going around it."
