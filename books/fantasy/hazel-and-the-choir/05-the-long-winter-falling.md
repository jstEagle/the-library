## 5. The Long Winter Falling

The frost came inland the way bad news does — slowly at first, so politely you almost thanked it, and then all at once, everywhere, with no respect for doors.

Jonah saw it first in the ledgers. That was how he saw most things. The Vellum Guild's books recorded weather sales by district, and for three weeks running the entries had gone strange: *frost, uncontracted, origin unknown* appearing in column after column like a stain spreading through clean linen. Weather that nobody had bought. Weather that had simply arrived, uninvited and unpaid for, which in Jonah's experience was the only kind that ever behaved badly.

"Read it again," said Master Corvane's clerk, a nervous man named Pell who chewed his quills when anxious and was therefore always chewing.

"*Frost, uncontracted,*" Jonah read. "*Origin unknown.* Eleven districts this week. Twelve." He turned the page. "Fourteen."

Pell chewed harder. "The guildmaster says it's a supply fluctuation."

"The guildmaster," Jonah said mildly, "also says the tithe of songs was discontinued due to administrative redundancy, and I have seen what happens when songs go unpaid." He did not say the rest — that he had walked into the border-market with a humming bone in his pocket and come back knowing his employer's honest arithmetic was built on theft from the dead. Some knowledge you kept behind your teeth until you knew what to do with it.

He closed the ledger. Outside the counting house window, the harbor had gone white at its edges, and it was only autumn.

* * *

The road north out of the city was lined with villages learning new manners.

In the first one, a woman sold her name for a coat. Jonah watched it happen from the doorway of the market-house, where he'd stopped to warm his hands over a brazier that burned colder than it should have. The broker was guild-licensed, brass badge and all, and he handled the transaction gently, the way you'd handle something breakable.

"You'll be called by your trade instead," the broker explained, writing in a ledger Jonah recognized as guild-issue. "Cooper. Everyone will know you as Cooper. It's not so different."

"It's everything different," said the woman, but she took the coat, because her children were cold, and she signed, and Jonah watched the name lift off her like steam off tea. Afterward she looked at him with a face that was still perfectly ordinary and perfectly empty in one small place, the way a room is empty after furniture is carried out.

"Do I know you?" she asked.

"No," Jonah said, which was true, and also the kindest lie available.

By the fourth village they weren't even selling names anymore. They were giving them away. A farmer pressed his grandfather's name into Jonah's hand outside an inn — literally pressed it, folding a scrap of paper into his palm with the desperate grip of a drowning man passing off a stone. "Take it. Take it somewhere warm. It's just sitting here getting frostbitten."

"I can't—"

"Please. It's a good name. Old. It kept three generations of us honest." The farmer's eyes were red-rimmed. "Nothing keeps anymore. You understand? Nothing holds. The cold comes right through the middle of a thing."

Jonah understood. The covenant had broken because the guild had stopped paying the Choir its tithe of songs, and without ritual — without the old singing that told winter where to stand and how far to come — the dead grieved unbound, and their grief was cold, and it was spreading inland like ink through wet paper.

He bought a room at the inn, paid double, and sat up most of the night listening to the walls. Not metaphorically. Jonah heard the dead through ivory and bone, and lately the walls themselves seemed to carry a sound underneath their silence: a vast, ragged, many-throated weeping, far off, like a choir rehearsing in a cathedral made of ice.

They weren't angry. That was the terrible part. He'd expected anger — vengeance, demands, the hot politics of the wronged. But the sound was grief, pure and patient and bottomless, the grief of singers whose song had been taken mid-verse and who could not stop reaching for the next line.

Somewhere in that sound, faint but unmistakable, was his grandmother's voice.

He'd known it would be. He'd known since the border-market, since the carved bone hummed against his ribs like a second heart. But knowing a thing and hearing it are different animals, and hearing it made him put his head down on the inn table and weep into his sleeves like a boy, quietly, so the innkeeper wouldn't hear.

* * *

The relic had been in his family for sixty years. It was a child's whistle, carved from elk ivory by his grandmother's own hands, given to Jonah when he was seven and too young to know it was anything but a toy. He'd never blown it. His grandmother had told him once, laughing, "Don't you blow that unless you mean it, little wren. Bone remembers breath."

Bone remembers breath. He turned the whistle over now on the inn table, lamplight sliding along its worn curves, along the tiny running-stag she'd carved on its side with more patience than the whole world deserved.

He was a bone-carver. It was the one thing he was entirely sure of being. And if the dead could speak through what he carved, then perhaps — perhaps — they could listen through it too. Every tool he owned was back at the counting house, but a carver's hands were his first tools, and the innkeeper lent him a knife for whittling fish-hooks, and the whistle itself was the finest stock he'd ever hold.

He worked through the small hours. He didn't reshape the whistle; that would have been desecration. Instead he opened it — cut thin listening-channels along its length, the way you'd open a window in a shut house, each groove tuned by feel and by the hum he could already sense rising under his fingertips like bees waking in a wall. His grandmother's cuts crossed his own, sixty years apart, two hands agreeing across time about where the grain wanted to go.

Near dawn, the thing in his palms was no longer quite a whistle and not yet whatever it was becoming. A listening-bone. He held it to his ear the way you'd hold a seashell.

And the winter spoke.

"—little wren—" 

Jonah's whole body went still, the way a forest goes still.

"—I know that hand. Sixty years and I know that hand. You've my patience in it, and your mother's stubbornness, and don't think I've forgotten how you cried at your father's funeral and then asked everyone practical questions about the coffin, because that's exactly right, that's exactly how a Nystrom grieves, usefully—"

"Grandmother." His voice cracked on it. "Grandmother, it's cold out there. The whole country's going cold. They stopped paying you."

A pause. When her voice came again it was quieter, and older, and it carried the weight of a hundred voices behind it, all leaning in to listen.

"We know why they stopped, little wren. We've always known. The tithe wasn't ended. It was *redirected*. Songs are power, and power goes where someone wants it to go, and there's a family in that city that hasn't aged past fifty in ninety years." Her voice hardened into flint. "We grieve, yes. But grief isn't all we're doing. Grief is just what leaks through the crack. Behind it we are *waiting*, and we are very old, and we remember every verse of the covenant, including the parts the guild hoped we'd forgotten."

"What parts?"

"The part about the anchor." Something moved through the listening-bone like wind through wheat — the sound of the many behind her shifting, murmuring, hope moving through the dead like warmth through a cold room. "Every covenant needs a living anchor, willingly given. No coercion. No trickery. That's why they never managed to bind us to their purposes — you cannot trick a willing heart into existing. But a willing heart, freely offered..." She let it hang.

"Grandmother, what are you asking me?"

"I'm asking nothing. That's the law of it. I can't ask, and you can't be pushed, and anyone who tells you otherwise is selling something." A pause, and then her voice gentled, went wry, went *hers*. "But I'll tell you what I see from here, little wren. I see a girl coming up the frozen road behind you. Prophecy-keeper's daughter, voice like a struck bell, carrying half a prophecy written in her own future hand. And I see that the two of you share a line in a book neither of you chose, and I see that winter has a face now — yours, mine, ours — and it knows you, and it has been waiting a long time for someone to answer."

Outside the inn window, the frost had reached the glass, feathering across it in patterns that were almost — not quite, but almost — musical notation.

"And Jonah," his grandmother added, softer. "When you find her — and you will find her, she's louder than a church bell and twice as determined — tell her the Choir remembers her mother. Tell her her mother sings with us. Tell her—" the voice caught, frayed, gathered itself, "—tell her we're keeping her mother's verses safe until she comes to sing them home."

The listening-bone went quiet in his hands, warm as a living thing.

Jonah sat in the grey dawn light with his grandmother's whistle opened into a window, and felt grief turn over inside him like a plow turning earth — the same dark soil, but suddenly ready for planting. This winter had a face. It had his grandmother's laugh lines around its eyes. And it knew him, and it was waiting, and down the frozen road, faint but unmistakable, he could already hear a girl's voice arguing with a toll-keeper about the price of passage.

He stood, wrapped the listening-bone carefully in cloth, tucked it next to his heart, and went out into the falling snow to meet his fate, which — being a Nystrom — he intended to question thoroughly about its terms.
