## 6. Terms of Reforging

The border-market smelled of woodsmoke and old paper, which Hazel found comforting until she realized the smoke was coming from memories being burned for warmth and the paper was people.

Not *people* exactly. Names, mostly, traded in bundles like kindling. A woman with frost in her eyelashes was selling her christening by the syllable — three coppers a word — and a boy no older than twelve had spread out his grandmother's laugh on a cloth, portioned into jars, each one labeled in a careful child's hand: *first kind*, *middle kind*, *the kind that made milk come out of Da's nose.*

Hazel had come here following a bone that hummed.

Jonah had pressed it into her hands at the edge of the frost line, his fingers blue, his voice steadier than she'd ever heard it. "It'll pull you toward the eldest singer. Don't bargain first. Listen first. And whatever you do—" he'd caught her sleeve, and for one unguarded moment she'd seen how frightened he was, not for himself but for her, "—don't offer anything you aren't prepared to lose."

"I'm not prepared to lose anything," Hazel had said. "That's rather the point."

"That," Jonah said, "is what everyone says before they meet the Choir."

Now the bone tugged her past the stalls, past a merchant weighing a summer afternoon against a barrel of lamp oil, past a stall where someone was auctioning the smell of rain on a hot road in lots. The market had no walls and no roof, but it had edges — she could feel them, the way you feel the edge of sleep. Beyond those edges the air went white and still, and somewhere in that whiteness, something enormous was singing very quietly, the way a kettle sings before it boils.

The bone grew warm. Then it grew *insistent*. Then it simply stopped humming and began, unmistakably, to sing along.

* * *

The eldest singer did not look old.

That was the first thing that unsettled Hazel. She had imagined something ancient and bent, a crone of frost and grave-cloth. Instead she found a woman sitting on an upturned rowboat at the market's white edge, mending a net full of nothing, her face smooth as a stone worn by a river. Her hair was black with one streak of white, and when she looked up, her eyes were the exact gray of the sky before snow.

"You're the prophecy-keeper's daughter," the singer said. Her voice was low and warm and slightly amused, as though Hazel were a joke the universe had told and she alone appreciated it.

"Hazel Vane."

"I know. It's written on you." The singer nodded at Hazel's chest, where, beneath her coat, the half-prophecy lay against her skin. "In your mother's hand. Which is also your hand. Which is also, if we're honest, my hand — I dictated it, a hundred years ago, to a keeper who took dictation badly and got paid anyway. Sit. You're letting the cold in behind you, and I've only just gotten comfortable with it."

Hazel sat on the ice beside the boat because there was nowhere else, and because some deep animal part of her understood that this woman decided where things ended, including conversations.

"You broke the covenant," Hazel said. No listening first. Well. She'd never been good at taking direction.

"We stopped being paid," the singer corrected. She drew the net through her hands; whatever she was catching, it wasn't fish. "There's a difference, and the difference is the whole reason you're standing on my doorstep freezing. Your guild owed us a tithe of songs. Every season's turning, the living would come to the border and sing us the year — births, harvests, quarrels, jokes, the whole ridiculous parade of being alive. We kept winter gentle in exchange. That was the covenant. Songs for softness. Memory for mercy."

"And then?"

"Then a guildmaster with excellent handwriting decided songs were inefficient." The singer's needle paused. "He worked out that if the dead are *hungry* instead of *paid*, they'll trade. Desperation sets a better exchange rate than gratitude ever did. So he ended the tithe, waited for the cold to bite, and opened the memory markets. Everything your guild sells now — borrowed years, traded names, weather by the season — it all comes from us. Bought cheap from the starving and sold dear to the living." She smiled, and it was the worst smile Hazel had ever seen, because it held no anger at all. Only arithmetic. "Your guild didn't break the covenant, girl. They *foreclosed* on it."

Hazel thought of the counting house. Of Jonah's careful columns, his honest ink, the way he'd said *I keep the ledgers true* like a man reciting a prayer. She thought of him hearing that truth crack under him and choosing, afterward, to walk toward the sound instead of away from it.

"The ledgers balance," she said quietly. "That's the horrible part. They balance perfectly. They just balance on theft."

"Now you understand the trade." The singer set down her net. "So. You've come to remake the covenant. Everyone always comes to remake the covenant eventually — guildmasters' daughters, kings' fools, one memorable bishop. Shall I tell you why none of them succeeded?"

"Because the guild won't pay."

"Because the guild *can't*." The singer leaned forward, and the temperature dropped in a way that had nothing to do with weather. "A covenant isn't a contract. A contract binds two parties who might cheat each other, so it needs enforcement, penalties, fine print. A covenant binds two parties who have *chosen* each other, so it needs only one thing: a living voice, given freely, willing to stand on the border and hold the door open from both sides. An anchor. No coercion. No trickery. No fine print, because fine print is just lying wearing spectacles." She tilted her head. "Do you see the problem?"

Hazel saw it. She felt it settle over her like the coat she wasn't wearing anymore, though she couldn't remember taking it off.

"The guild has never once done anything freely," she said. "Every gift they give is an investment. Every kindness is collateral. They couldn't anchor a covenant if their immortality depended on it—"

"—which it does," the singer said pleasantly. "Which it does. Their whole hoard, their borrowed centuries, their little dynasty of never-dying — it's all built on our hunger. Remake the covenant honestly, and every stolen year comes due. That's why they'll burn this market down before they let you sing. Not out of malice. Out of *accounting*."

* * *

They talked for a long time after that, or what felt like a long time; time at the border moved like syrup, slow and sweet and slightly dishonest about itself. The singer answered everything. That was the unnerving part — Hazel had expected riddles, tests, a dragon's maze of conditions. Instead she got straight answers, priced fairly, like a shopkeeper who respected you enough not to haggle.

"What happens to the anchor?" Hazel asked, finally asking the question she'd been carrying since the frost line.

"They stay," the singer said. "On this side. Alive — we don't take life, we're not monsters, we're *creditors* — but apart. Holding the door. Hearing both sides forever. It's not death. It's worse and better than death: it's *responsibility*, permanent." She studied Hazel with those gray eyes. "You're thinking of volunteering. I can hear it. It's written on you too, right under the prophecy."

"I'm thinking of everyone except me," Hazel said. "My mother is somewhere on your side of the border. I saw her. If I stay, I could—"

"No." For the first time, the singer's voice lost its warmth entirely. "An anchor cannot be someone with debts on the far side. Love that owes is love that can be leveraged, and a leveraged anchor is a broken covenant with extra steps. Your mother stays your mother. Grief stays grief. The anchor must come clean-handed or not at all." She resumed her mending. "Terrible, isn't it? Justice exists here. Real justice, honest prices, no cheating anyone, not even ourselves. And it costs *exactly* what it should, which is always more than anyone wants to pay."

Hazel wrapped her arms around herself and looked out at the white. Somewhere in it, faintly, she could hear singing — hundreds of voices, patient, hungry, gentle. Not demanding. Just waiting, the way winter waits, without malice and without mercy.

"Who would do it?" she asked. "Freely. Knowing the price. Who's ever been stupid enough?"

The singer smiled again, and this time it reached her eyes, and it was almost — almost — kind.

"Oh, one or two," she said. "Usually the ones who spent their lives keeping other people's accounts true. There's a bone-carver at your guild, I'm told, who hears the dead through the ivory. He's been listening to us his whole life, whether he knew it or not. Do you know what that does to a soul, girl? It *seasons* it. Makes it fit for the door." She stood, folding her empty net. "Go back to him. Tell him the terms. All of them. Don't soften a single price — he'd know, and he'd never forgive you, and neither would I."

"And if he refuses?"

"Then winter wins, and in a hundred years your grandchildren will sell their names for firewood, and I will sit here mending this net, and I will be very polite about it." The singer shouldered the boat as if it weighed nothing. "But he won't refuse. I've heard him carving. Even through the ivory, even through the dark — that man keeps time like a heartbeat. He was born to hold a door open."

Hazel walked back through the market with the humming bone against her palm and the terms sitting in her chest like swallowed stones. Behind her, the eldest singer's voice rose once, clear over the stalls, in something that might have been a blessing or might have been an invoice:

*Tell him the truth, prophecy-keeper's daughter. It's the only currency we've never debased.*

She found Jonah at the frost line, stamping feeling back into his feet, and watched his face as she came toward him — watched him read the answer in her walk before she said a word.

"There's a way," she said. "But Jonah — you need to hear the price first. All of it. Before you say anything."

Jonah looked at her for a long moment. Then he reached into his coat and drew out his carving knife, and the look on his face was the look of a man who had already balanced the ledger and found, to his own quiet horror, that it came out even.

"Tell me," he said.
