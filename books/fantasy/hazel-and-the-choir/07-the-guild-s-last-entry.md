## 7. The Guild's Last Entry

The counting house at midnight smelled of tallow, cold iron, and the particular dust that accumulates around money — a dry, papery scent, like the inside of an old man's fist. Jonah had worked beneath that smell for eleven years. He knew it the way a fish knows water.

He came in through the tradesman's door with Hazel's half-prophecy folded against his chest and the listening-bone humming in his pocket like a trapped wasp. The guildmaster's lamp still burned behind the inner office, which was itself unusual. Master Corvane Vellus was a man of strict hours; his ledgers closed at dusk, his conscience — such as it was — presumably on similar schedule.

Jonah had rehearsed this conversation on the walk over. He had planned to be reasonable. He had planned to present the evidence of the broken tithe like a clerk presenting an invoice: here is the discrepancy, sir, here are the missing entries, here is the century of songs the guild collected and never paid.

He got as far as the doorway before he saw what was on the guildmaster's desk.

It was a ledger. Not one of Jonah's — Jonah kept the trade books, the honest ones, the ones where summers changed hands and names were pawned and every entry balanced to the penny because that was the whole point of the Vellum Guild, that even impossible things could be accounted for honestly. This ledger was older. Its cover was vellum gone the color of weak tea, and it lay open, and the pages were moving.

Not fluttering. Moving. Writing themselves in a hand Jonah recognized from a hundred years of prophecy margins: small, patient, unhurried as frost.

"You're up late," said Master Vellus, without looking up.

"So are you," Jonah said. "Sir."

The guildmaster was not what people expected. He was a small man, neat as a pin-cushion, with spectacles he polished when he wanted to avoid eye contact and a voice like dry paper folding. He had once given Jonah a raise by sliding the notice across the desk face-down, so it wouldn't be awkward. Jonah had liked him for that, in the way you can like someone who is careful about small kindnesses while committing large crimes.

"Sit down, Nystrom." Vellus finally looked up. His eyes went to Jonah's coat, where the bone hummed. "And put that thing away. It's rude to bring a listening device into a private meeting."

"It's rude," Jonah said, "to stop paying the dead."

The silence that followed had texture. Somewhere in the walls, the building settled, or pretended to.

Vellus took off his spectacles. He cleaned them. He put them back on. "Ah," he said. "You've been to the market."

"I've been to the market."

"And you spoke with them."

"I heard them." Jonah set the listening-bone on the desk between them, where it shivered and sang a note too low for comfort. "They're grieving, sir. They've been grieving for a hundred years, and winter is coming down off the mountains because nobody taught them how to let go. Because we stopped teaching them. The tithe—"

"The tithe," Vellus said, "was ended by my grandfather. Deliberately. With great care. And I have spent my life making sure it stayed ended." He said it the way other men announced a marriage or a coronation. "Would you like to know why?"

"I'd like you to tell me anyway."

The guildmaster turned the old ledger around so Jonah could read it. The self-writing page had stopped. What remained was a record — dates, entries, sums. But the currency column didn't list coin.

It listed years.

*Received of A. Vellus: forty winters of song, held in trust. Received of A. Vellus: sixty more. Balance carried forward.* Page after page. A century of the Choir's payments, banked. Not stolen, exactly — the dead had *paid*, willingly, into the covenant, and the covenant had been meant to circulate their gift through the world like blood through a heart. Instead the Vellus family had dammed it.

"One family's immortality," Jonah said quietly. It wasn't a question. He'd seen enough ledgers to know a hoard when he saw one.

"My grandfather was dying at thirty," Vellus said. There was something almost tender in his voice now, which was worse than anger. "The Choir offered him warmth — that's all they ever offer, warmth, they're very generous that way — and he asked instead for time. They explained the terms. He accepted. And then he realized that if the covenant flowed freely, the gift would thin out across ten thousand hearths, and there would never be enough left over for a second withdrawal. So he stopped the flow. Closed the valve. Kept the pressure." He smiled faintly. "I am sixty-eight years old, Nystrom. I have the lungs of a man of fifty. My father lived to ninety-four. We are, I'm told, excellent value for money."

"You're a dam with a family name."

"A reservoir," Vellus corrected mildly. "Words matter in our trade. You taught me that."

Jonah looked at the ledger. At the bottom of the current page, in that patient frost-hand, a new line had appeared:

*Balance outstanding: everything.*

"They know," Jonah said. "They've always known."

"Of course they know. They're dead, not stupid." Vellus laced his fingers. "But knowing and collecting are different matters. The covenant requires a willing anchor — a living voice that binds what law cannot. That's why my grandfather chose accounting instead. Law can be bought. Anchors cannot. Believe me, we tried. Three generations of trying to buy, coerce, or trick some poor soul into standing on the border and singing us a permanent line of credit." He shook his head with genuine regret. "The dead are terribly scrupulous. It's their most irritating quality."

"And now Hazel."

"Now the prophecy-keeper's daughter, yes. Whose mother, incidentally, made the same discovery you did, and whose mother is currently enjoying the hospitality of our enforcers' far side arrangements." Vellus said it lightly, but his eyes didn't leave Jonah's face. "You should know, before you do anything dramatic, that there is a place for you in the arrangement. There has always been a place for you. Your talent with the ivory — hearing them, honoring them — that's rare. That's *valuable*. When the transfer completes, when the Choir's power passes formally into the family's keeping, I'll need a bone-carver who can keep the dead quiet. Comfortable, even. Well-paid. Warm."

"Warm," Jonah repeated.

"Everyone wants to be warm, Nystrom. It's the one honest desire."

Jonah thought about the border-market. About the Choir trading memories for heat, selling pieces of themselves like farmers selling seed corn in a famine year. About his grandmother's voice in the bone, saying *the ledger remembers what men forget*.

He thought about eleven years of honest columns. Every impossible sale recorded fairly. Every borrowed summer entered true. He had told himself the honesty of the books redeemed the nature of the trade. He had told himself a lot of things.

"Here's my problem, sir," Jonah said, and heard his own voice come out steady, which surprised him. "I keep ledgers. And your books don't balance. A hundred years of songs taken in and nothing paid out. In any honest house, that's not wealth. That's theft with good penmanship."

"The law disagrees."

"The law is your grandfather's handwriting."

For the first time, Master Corvane Vellus looked genuinely tired. "Yes," he admitted. "It is. I did hope you'd take the offer. You're the only employee I've ever respected, and I want you to understand that I say that without sentiment. You balance. In thirty years, you've never once fudged a column." He reached into his drawer and drew out a document Jonah knew intimately: his employment contract, signed at nineteen, wax seal intact, terms generous, exit clauses nonexistent. "So I'll give you a choice, since the dead are so fond of choices. Sign the extension. Or—" he set the contract on the desk, deliberately within reach of the ledger fire's brass grate, "—burn it, and become a man with no wages, no protection, no legal standing, and a warrant for conspiracy arriving by morning post. Choose."

Jonah looked at the fire. Small, domestic, cheerful. The kind of fire you warm your hands over while deciding someone else's fate.

He thought about severance. The word had always meant money to him — a payout, a closing of accounts. But standing there, he understood it differently. Severance was a cutting. Something severed. You didn't get to choose whether it hurt. You only got to choose what stayed cut.

Hazel was at the border-market negotiating with the eldest singer. She thought she might have to pay the price herself. She hadn't said so outright, but she had that look — the look of someone doing arithmetic they refuse to show you. And Jonah had done his own arithmetic, silently, all the way across town: a living anchor, willing, uncoerced. Someone who understood the dead because he'd spent his life listening to them through ivory. Someone whose bones were already half-accustomed to carrying voices.

Someone expendable, said the cowardly part of him, the part that liked wages and warm rooms.

Someone who keeps honest books, said the rest of him. And the dead deserve one honest clerk.

"Before I decide," Jonah said, "one professional question."

Vellus raised an eyebrow.

"When the transfer completes — when the Choir's power becomes yours — what happens to them? The singers themselves."

The guildmaster paused a moment too long. "Preserved," he said.

"That's not an answer."

"It's the answer that appears in the documentation."

Jonah nodded slowly. "Right." He picked up his employment contract. The paper felt lighter than it should have, considering it had held his entire adult life. Eleven years. A pension accruing. A room above a chandler's shop that smelled of tallow and was, he suddenly realized, the closest thing to home he had.

"Well," he said. "In my experience, when a man won't say plainly what happens to the merchandise, the merchandise suffers. And I don't keep books for houses like that."

He fed the contract into the grate.

The wax seal caught first, bubbling red like a small wound, then the vellum curled, blackened, and went up in a breath of flame that smelled — Jonah would remember this forever — like summer. Like a summer, lightly used, returned to its owner. The fire flared green for exactly one heartbeat, and in that heartbeat every ledger in the counting house rustled at once, like a flock of birds startled off a wire, and somewhere very far away and very close, something began to sing.

"There," Jonah said. His hands were shaking now that it was done. "Severance."

Master Vellus watched the ash settle. He did not look angry. He looked, horribly, like a man confirming a figure he'd already penciled in.

"I did respect you," he said, and sighed, and rang the small silver bell on his desk — the one Jonah had always assumed was for tea. "You understand I can't allow the audit."

"I'd be disappointed if you could."

The door opened. Enforcers — four of them, gray-cloaked, smelling of frost and iron. But Jonah had walked out of scarier places than this office lately. He had walked out of the border-market with his name still his own.

"Mr. Nystrom will accompany you," Vellus said. "Gently. He's earned that much." Then, as they took Jonah's arms: "One piece of professional advice, since you were my best clerk. The girl is negotiating with the eldest singer right now. The terms require a willing anchor. Do you imagine she hasn't already guessed who fits the description? Do you imagine she'll let you?" He almost smiled. "Love makes terrible bookkeeping, Nystrom. Everyone overpays."

"They also undercharge themselves," Jonah said. "That's the error you never caught, sir. Love isn't a bad ledger. It's just one where both parties insist on paying."

The enforcers marched him toward the door, and Jonah went, oddly light, ash still drifting from his sleeve like gray snow. Behind him the old prophecy ledger flipped its pages once, twice, and wrote a final line in its patient frost-hand, and though no one read it until much later, it said:

*Entry closed. Account transferred. The carver has chosen the other side of the book.*

By morning post, as promised, the warrant arrived. By midnight, Jonah intended to be past the reach of any post at all — out on the ice, where Hazel was singing, where winter leaned in to listen, and where a man with nothing left to lose and one honest column left to enter was going to offer the dead the only payment he had.

Himself. Fair market value. No haggling.

* * *

The snow started as they crossed the guild yard, fat flakes falling straight down, windless, deliberate — snow that fell like it was being sent rather than merely happening. One flake landed on Jonah's wrist and did not melt.

In the bone in his pocket, faint and clear as a bell rung underwater, his grandmother's voice said: *Run, boy. And mind the ice. It remembers everyone who crosses.*

Jonah ran.
