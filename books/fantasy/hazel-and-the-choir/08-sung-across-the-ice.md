## 8. Sung Across the Ice

The border had once been a river. Now it was a river's ghost — a white seam in the world where the water had frozen so deep and so long ago that even the ice had forgotten it was ever anything else. Hazel stood at its edge with her boots sinking into snow that squeaked like new leather, and she thought, absurdly, of her mother telling her that a song sung properly could hold up a bridge.

*Well,* she thought. *Let's find out if it can hold up a world.*

Jonah knelt beside her, unrolling his kit on a scrap of oilcloth: chisels, mallets, a whetstone worn to the shape of his thumb, and three bones wrapped in wool — two carved already, one still raw and pale as a held breath.

"You're shivering," he said, without looking up.

"I'm *concentrating.* It looks similar from the outside."

"It does." He glanced at her then, and there was that crooked half-smile of his, the one that made him look less like a guild clerk and more like the boy who'd once whittled a whistle from a chicken bone just to see if it would work. (It had. It had also summoned every dog within a mile, which was how they'd learned what his gift really was.) "For the record, I've decided the plan is terrible."

"You've mentioned. Eleven times."

"Twelve. You were humming during the last one." He set the raw bone down gently, the way one sets down something sleeping. "Terrible plans are the only kind that work, though. The good ones get anticipated. Nobody anticipates this."

Hazel looked out across the ice. Far off — too far to be near, too near to be far — the Choir was singing. Not the gentle winter-song of the old covenant, not anymore. This was grief given melody, vast and looping, the sound of ten thousand dead singing to an audience that had stopped listening a century ago. The snow shivered with it. Somewhere behind them, she knew, the enforcers were crossing the last ridge, torches bobbing like angry fireflies.

"They're coming," she said.

"I know."

"Say it anyway. I like hearing you be right about things. It balances out all the times you're wrong about whether I've eaten."

"You haven't eaten," Jonah said. "I packed you a bun. It's in your left pocket. It has been for six hours."

She checked. There was a bun. She ate it standing up, watching the ice, and it was somehow the kindest thing anyone had done for her all winter, which said a great deal about her winter.

* * *

The old covenant had words. Of course it did; her mother had spent half a lifetime hunting them, copying fragments onto scraps of vellum hidden inside lullabies and recipe books and one very suspicious embroidery of a duck. Hazel had inherited the scraps, and over the long months she'd stitched them into something whole — or whole enough. A covenant wasn't law, exactly. It was closer to a promise made in front of witnesses, except the witnesses were the dead and the promise was sung instead of signed.

"Again from the top," Jonah said. "The third verse keeps fraying."

"The third verse is *supposed* to fray. My mother's notes say the fraying is the point. It shows the Choir you mean it — that you'd keep singing even when the song fights back."

"That seems needlessly dramatic."

"She was a prophecy-keeper's daughter raised by prophecy-keepers. Drama was her native language." Hazel flexed her fingers against the cold. "Sing your part once more."

So Jonah sang — badly, honestly, in a voice like a door that needed oiling — the counter-melody the ledger had taught him, the part written in a century-old book by a hand that had turned out to be his own. The ice hummed under their feet. Out in the middle distance, the Choir's grief-song stuttered, the way a room goes quiet when someone says something true.

And then the torches crested the ridge.

There were nine of them, guild-black coats bright with frost, led by a broad silhouette Hazel recognized with a lurch: Vess, the enforcer-captain who had once held a door open for her mother and later swore he'd never seen her at all. His breath steamed like a kettle with opinions.

"Hazel Quill!" His voice carried flat across the ice. "By order of the Vellum Guild, surrender the covenant-text and step away from the border!"

"By order of *whom?*" she called back. "Your guildmaster's been burning books to keep one family young! Your wages are paid out of stolen songs!"

There was a pause. Somewhere among the enforcers, someone shifted their weight in a way that suggested the seed had landed in soil after all.

"Surrender the text," Vess repeated, but slower now, the way men repeat things when they're buying time to think.

"No," Hazel said pleasantly. "But thank you for coming all this way. You're actually rather useful. A covenant needs witnesses."

* * *

They walked out onto the ice together — because of course they did; the covenant had to be sung *at* the border, not beside it, and the border was the middle of the frozen river's ghost, and everything worth doing in Hazel's life had apparently been scheduled at the worst possible location.

The cold out there was different. Not weather-cold but *attention*-cold, as if the ice itself had opened one eye. Beneath their boots, far down, shapes moved: the Choir, rising toward the sound of living voices like fish rising to bread.

Jonah knelt and unwrapped the first carved bone. "Anchor one," he said. "North point." He pressed it to the ice and sang the setting-verse, and the bone sank through the surface without breaking it, the way a stone sinks through a dream.

"Anchor two. South point." Again the hum, again the slow settling. The grief-song overhead bent around them now, curious.

"Anchor three," Jonah said, and reached for the raw bone, and stopped.

Hazel felt it before she understood it — a wrongness in the pattern, a missing tooth in a comb. Two anchors held the song at north and south. The third had to come from the singer himself. Her mother's marginalia had been clear as a bell and twice as cruel: *the binding takes a living voice, freely given, rooted in bone.*

"Jonah," she said carefully. "Give me the chisel."

"No," he said, cheerfully, horribly. "It has to be me. Freely given, remember? No coercion, no trickery. If I hand you the chisel, that's coercion by proxy. The eldest singer explained it four times. She was very patient. Dead people have excellent patience."

"There has to be another way—"

"There isn't, and we both read the same notes, so let's not insult each other by pretending." He rolled up his sleeve. His hands, those careful carver's hands, were steady. "Rune-line runs along the radius. It'll hurt approximately as much as it sounds like it will. Sing the third verse while I do it — the fraying verse. Your job is to not stop, no matter what you hear."

"What will I hear?"

He smiled at her, and it was the smile from the counting house, from the market, from every small hour they'd stolen between catastrophes. "Me," he said. "Being honest for once."

* * *

She sang.

The third verse fought her exactly as promised — the notes kept sliding, the words kept rearranging themselves into doubts (*turn back, turn back, the guild will pardon you, the spring will come without you*) — and she gripped the melody by the throat and hauled it forward anyway, because that was the verse's whole meaning: *I sing even now.*

Beneath her voice, Jonah worked. The little sounds he made were worse than screaming would have been — small, disciplined exhalations, a craftsman's sounds, the sounds of a man treating his own arm as a difficult piece of stock. The chisel whispered. Once, only once, it cracked, and he breathed a curse in the polite register he saved for mislabeled ledgers, and started again.

The ice lit up beneath them. Lines of pale gold spread from the two anchors, racing toward the center, waiting for the third. And the Choir came up through the ice like dawn comes through a window — not shapes at first, just voices, hundreds of them, thousands, harmonizing with her verse and carrying it past where her breath gave out. Her grandmother was somewhere in that sound. Jonah's grandmother too, she realized; she could hear the resemblance, a certain stubborn warmth in the low harmony.

*He hears them always,* she thought, carving the thought into the melody. *He has heard the dead his whole life. And he chose to go to them singing.*

The gold lines met. The third anchor flared — runes glowing along Jonah's forearm like a window into a lit room — and the covenant took root with a sound like a great bell being struck underwater. The grief-song broke apart and reassembled, note by note, into the old winter-song, gentle and vast, and the cold changed key. Snow began falling upward. Somewhere behind them, nine enforcers stood very still with their torches guttering, and Vess took off his hat, which Hazel suspected was the first honest gesture of his career.

The covenant held.

The covenant held, and the eldest singer rose through the ice directly before them, tall and patient, her face like the last page of a favorite book, and she looked at Jonah with something terribly like tenderness.

"The anchor holds," she said. "The anchor stays."

* * *

The door opened in the ice — a doorway of dark water and warmer air, of lamplight and low singing, the other side showing itself the way a house shows itself when a friend leaves the lamp in the window.

One passage. That was the term. The anchor crosses or the anchor remains, and either way the door closes at the last note of the song, and the last note was coming, was always coming, was coming now.

"Walk through," Hazel said. "You've paid. You've more than paid. Let the runes root from the far side, it'll work, we can try—"

"Hazel." Jonah took her hands. His forearm was bandaged with strips of his own shirt, and beneath the bandage the runes glowed softly, patient as banked coals. "Listen to me. If I cross, the anchor is a memory of a man. If I stay, the anchor is a man. The covenant deserves the difference. So do you."

"That's not fair."

"No," he agreed. "It isn't. Fairness would be both of us growing old and arguing about whose turn it is to make breakfast. But justice priced this honestly, and I've spent my life keeping honest books. I know a fair price when I see one, even when it's mine."

"You could have told me. Before the ice. Before I—" Her voice cracked clean through the melody, and the Choir caught it and carried it for her, the way you carry a tired child.

"If I'd told you, you'd have found a way to stop me, and you'd have succeeded, because you're the most stubborn person I've ever met, and I include several centuries of dead people in that assessment." His thumb brushed her knuckles. "I have one request. One. Remember me accurately. Not kindly — accurately. I was fussy about ink. I hummed off-key. I packed buns into pockets instead of saying things out loud. Don't sand my edges smooth. The edges are the best part."

The last note was rising. The door stood open, lamplight spilling across the ice, and the eldest singer waited with her hands folded, and Jonah Nystrom stepped backward toward it the way a man steps back to admire finished work.

"Jonah—"

"Third verse," he said. "Don't stop, no matter what you hear."

And he smiled, and the note came down like snowfall, and the door began to close—

—and through the narrowing gap of light, clear and unhurried and impossibly warm, Jonah began to sing the fourth verse. The one that wasn't in any book. The one no one had taught him.

The one that sounded, unmistakably, like a beginning.
