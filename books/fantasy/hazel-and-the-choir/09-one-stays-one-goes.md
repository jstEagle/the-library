## 9. One Stays, One Goes

*Jonah*

The door held.

That was the first miracle of it, and Jonah had time to appreciate the strangeness of that word — *miracle*, which he had never once used in a ledger, because miracles did not balance. But there it was: a doorway standing open in the ice where no doorway should be, its frame stitched from old songs, its threshold glowing like the last coal of a hearth that refused, out of sheer stubbornness, to die.

Through it he could see the border-market as it must have looked a hundred years ago — stalls strung with lanterns, the Choir moving among them not as ghosts but as neighbors, their faces warm, their mouths full of melody instead of grief. The covenant was taking root. He could feel it in his own bones, quite literally, in the anchor-runes he had carved into them over the past three hours with hands that only shook a little. The binding hummed through him like a plucked string still deciding what note it wanted to be.

One passage. That had been the term. The eldest singer had been very clear about it, in the way of someone who has watched a thousand bargains sour and refuses to sweeten this one even for pity's sake. The door would hold long enough for one of them to walk home.

Hazel was looking at him the way people look at a ledger when the numbers have come out wrong and they are checking the arithmetic a fourth time, hoping the error is theirs.

"No," she said.

"Hazel—"

"No. You don't get to do this quietly. I know you. You were going to just — step through the other way and let me find out from the *book*, weren't you? From some entry. *Anchor retained, per terms.*" Her voice cracked on the last word, and somewhere behind her, one of the enforcers — frozen mid-lunge at the edge of the ice, caught between duty and terror — made a small involuntary sound of sympathy before remembering whose side he was supposed to be on.

"I hadn't decided how you'd find out," Jonah said, which was true, and also a lie, and they both knew it.

"You're a terrible liar for a man who keeps books."

"I keep honest books. Lying is a different trade entirely."

She crossed the threshold-space toward him — not through the door, but alongside it, close enough that he could see frost forming in her eyelashes, could see that she'd been crying and had done it efficiently, the way she did everything, budgeting even her grief. "Jonah Nystrom," she said, and his whole name in her mouth sounded like an entry being read aloud at a trial. "You told me once that every price in this market is honest. That's why you trusted it. So tell me honestly — is this your price, or are you paying mine?"

And there it was. The question underneath everything, sharp as a carver's blade.

Because it would have been easy — so easy — to say *it's the terms, it's the covenant, it's arithmetic*. To hide behind the eldest singer's law the way he'd hidden behind ledgers his whole life. But the dead had taught him something in these last weeks, listening through ivory and bone: that honesty is a muscle, and it aches exactly like any other muscle when you use it after years of neglect.

"It's my price," he said. "I chose it. Before we ever came to the ice, I think. Maybe the night I burned my contract. Maybe the night I heard my grandmother singing in a bone I carved from my own childhood." He smiled, and found, to his surprise, that the smile was real. "I've spent my whole life recording other people's debts, Hazel. This is the first one I've ever wanted to pay myself."

* * *

*Hazel*

She raged at him for eleven minutes. She knew it was eleven because she counted, the way her mother had taught her to count during hard things — not to make them shorter, but to make them *measured*, to prove they had edges.

She called him a coward, which was unfair and they both knew it. She called him a martyr, which was worse, because martyrs got to feel noble about it. She told him about spring — described it to him in detail, the mud and the crocuses and the way the guild's roof leaked in April right above the prophecy shelf, and wouldn't he want to be there to complain about it properly? She threatened to refuse to go. She threatened to sing the covenant backwards. She said, at the lowest point, the thing she would be ashamed of later:

"I'll forget you. I'll put your name in a ledger and sell it, and then I won't even miss you."

Jonah received all of it standing very still, the way he stood when a bone sang too loudly, letting the sound pass through him rather than fighting it. And when she finally ran out of fury — fury being, she understood suddenly, just love with nowhere to put itself — he said:

"You won't forget me. Not because you couldn't, but because forgetting isn't your style. You demanded your mother's debts be honored when every sensible person told you to go home. You hid inside a book. You negotiated with the oldest dead thing on this side of the ice and got better terms than the entire Vellum Guild managed in a century." His voice went gentle in a way that was somehow harder to bear than shouting. "You don't forget, Hazel. You carry. It's the family gift. Your mother carried prophecies; you'll carry this."

"That's not fair."

"No," he agreed. "It isn't. Most true things aren't."

The wind was rising behind them. She could feel the covenant settling into place around them both now, could feel the anchor-runes in his bones resonating against the song still hanging in the air from her throat — her mother's voice, her grandmother's voice, going back and back, all of it braided through the binding. The door pulsed softly, patiently. It was not impatient. Doors made of songs never were. But it would not hold forever.

"What do you want?" she asked finally, and her voice came out small. "When I go back. What do you want me to do?"

Jonah considered this with the seriousness he gave to every entry, and she realized that this, too, was a kind of love — that he would not answer quickly, would not give her a pretty nothing.

"Remember me accurately," he said. "Not kindly. Kindness smooths people off the page until there's nothing left but the outline. Remember that I was fussy about ink. That I liked my tea too strong and my evenings too quiet. That I was afraid almost all the time and did the necessary things anyway, which is the only courage worth the name. Remember that I got the arithmetic wrong sometimes and hated admitting it." He paused. "And remember that I loved you. Not sentimentally. Accurately. As a fact, entered twice, verified."

Hazel laughed — a wet, broken, entirely genuine laugh — because of course. Of course he would phrase it like a ledger entry. Of course the most romantic thing Jonah Nystrom had ever said to anyone would sound like bookkeeping.

"Entered twice," she managed. "Verified."

"That's all I ask."

* * *

*Jonah*

He walked her to the threshold, because some things you do even when doing them is the whole cost.

The border-market shimmered on the other side of the door, and the Choir had gathered near it — not crowding, never crowding, but present, the way family is present at departures. His grandmother stood among them. She had waited, he understood, a hundred years to see the tithe restored, and she was not sentimental about it either; she simply nodded at him, once, the nod she used to give when he'd finished a chore well, and it undid him more thoroughly than any farewell could have.

"The guild," Hazel said, gripping his hands. Her fingers were freezing. He warmed them anyway, out of habit, out of spite against the cold. "I'm reopening it. Fair tithes. Open books. Songs paid forward. You'd hate the paperwork."

"I'll audit it from here," he said. "I expect quarterly reports."

"On what? You'll have no eyes."

"I'll have a bone. You'll carve my name into it, and I'll listen, and if your figures don't add up I'll haunt you gently. Nothing dramatic. Just — sighing. Inconvenient sighs, at dinner parties."

She was crying again, and laughing again, and holding onto his hands like a woman holding onto a rope in weather. "Jonah."

"Yes?"

"Thank you for choosing it. I know the difference now. Between a price that's taken and a price that's given." She lifted her chin, and he saw her mother in her face, and her mother's mother, and all the carriers going back. "I'll pay mine the same way."

"I know," he said. "That's why I can go."

He let go of her hands first, because if she had let go first he would have known it forever as the moment she stopped holding on, and he could not have carried that accurately or otherwise. Then he stepped back from the threshold — one step, two — and turned fully toward the border-market, toward the lanterns and the singers and the long work of keeping winter gentle, and he heard her take a breath behind him, the deep breath of someone about to sing or about to scream, and he did not turn around, because turning around was not in the terms.

The door closed behind him with a sound like a book shutting gently, like a period at the end of a true sentence.

On the far side of the ice, already walking toward spring, Hazel felt the covenant settle over her shoulders like a coat — warm, heavy, hers to carry — and in her pocket, unaccountably, the small listening-bone she had taken from his workbench grew warm.

She took it out. It was humming.

Not a message yet. Just a hum. Just a man on the other side of everything, unhurried, checking that his name had arrived safely — and waiting, with a patience he had learned from the dead, to hear how the story would be entered next.
