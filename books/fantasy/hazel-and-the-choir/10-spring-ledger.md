## 10. Spring Ledger

The counting house smelled different in spring.

Hazel noticed it the first morning she unlocked the doors under her own name — or rather, under no name at all, because the new sign above the door read simply *The Ledger*, and beneath it, smaller, carved by a carpenter who had charged her fairly and been paid fairly: *All accounts open. All debts sung.*

Old Master Corvane's guild had smelled of tallow, dust, and the particular sourness of ink kept too long in a cold room — the smell of things hidden. Her mother had once described it to her, years ago, as "the smell of a room holding its breath." Now the windows stood open to a morning that actually felt like morning, and the air coming through carried wet earth, river water, and the green insistence of things growing whether or not anyone had traded for them.

That was the strangest part, still. The weather was free now.

Hazel set down her satchel on the counter Jonah had once worked behind — she'd had it planed smooth and re-oiled, though she'd left one deep scratch near the corner where his knife had slipped, because some marks deserved to stay — and opened the great ledger book to its first fresh page.

*Spring, Year One of the Reforged Covenant,* she wrote. Then, because the old ledgers had always begun with an inventory, she continued in the same careful hand:

*Item: one summer, arriving on schedule, unowned.*
*Item: one winter, ended early, forgiven.*
*Item: one choir, singing again, unpaid but thanked daily.*
*Item: one bone-carver, retained on the other side of the border, terms permanent, compensation ongoing.*

Her pen hesitated over that last line. It always did.

She had learned, these months, that grief did not get smaller. It just got better company.

* * *

The morning's first customer was a farmer named Odell Brask, who came in twisting his cap and would not sit down even when offered a chair.

"It's about my grandmother," he said. "Maren Brask. She died in the hard winter. Before the — well. Before."

"Before the covenant broke," Hazel said gently. "I know."

"She never got sung across. The Choir wasn't taking anyone then, were they? They were too busy grieving." He looked at his boots. "My mum says there's nothing to be done. Says the dead who fell in the bad winter are just gone, like snow off a roof."

Hazel turned the ledger around so he could see it. "Your mum is wrong, and I say that with love, because half this town is wrong in exactly the same way. The dead who fell unwintered weren't lost. They were held. The Choir doesn't lose people; it waits for them. That's practically all it does."

"So — what do I owe?"

"Nothing today." She slid a small card across the counter, printed in her own hand: *Songs Paid Forward.* "Here's how we work now. You can't pay me for your grandmother's crossing, because the crossing itself costs nothing. What you can do is this: within the year, sing something for someone else's dead. A neighbor's. A stranger's. Anyone. One song, freely given, entered here—" she tapped the ledger, "—and the account balances."

Odell stared at the card like it might bite him. "That's it? No borrowed years? No names?"

"The old guild priced everything in things people couldn't spare," Hazel said. "We price in things people have too much of anyway. Grief wants somewhere to go. We just give it directions."

He laughed — a startled, rusty sound, the laugh of a man whose town had forgotten laughing was allowed — and took the card with both hands, the way you'd take bread.

When he'd gone, Hazel made the entry: *Brask, Maren — crossing scheduled, dusk, north field. Cost: one song, forward-dated. Debtor: Odell Brask, solvent in joy.*

She was pleased with that last phrase. She'd been collecting them, little honest phrases the old ledgers would never have permitted. *Solvent in joy.* Jonah would have liked that one. He'd had a weakness for accounting language used tenderly — she remembered him explaining, that terrible winter, that every debt was really a relationship, and every payment a way of saying *I remember you.*

Which was, she supposed, the whole theology of the thing now.

* * *

At midday she went out back to the workshop, where the afternoon light came through the east window at the angle Jonah had always claimed was best for carving — he'd been right, she'd checked, resentfully at first, then with resignation, then with something warmer she didn't examine too closely.

On the bench lay a piece of bone, pale and clean, cut from the antler shed by the white hart that had appeared at the reforging and not been seen since. The Choir had sent it to her in the thaw, wrapped in moss, with no note, because the dead considered notes unnecessary when they could simply put a thought directly into your head at three in the morning, which they did, cheerfully, whenever they felt she was taking herself too seriously.

Today's thought had arrived with breakfast: *Carve something. You've been hiding behind paperwork for a week.*

The dead were gossips and meddlers. She loved them completely.

She picked up the graver. Her hands had gotten steadier over the months — Jonah had taught her the basics in those last frozen weeks, his fingers over hers on the tool, patient in the unhurried way of someone who has already decided how his time will end and therefore refuses to waste any of it being anxious. She'd been clumsy then. She was less clumsy now. Still not good. But the bone didn't seem to mind.

She carved the letters slowly. J-O-N-A-H. Then, beneath, because accuracy mattered more than kindness and he had asked for it explicitly: *He heard us. He stayed anyway. He was right about the light in the east window.*

The shavings curled away like tiny sleeping creatures. Outside, a blackbird was conducting its usual one-bird war against the concept of silence.

And when the last stroke was done — when the name sat complete in the bone, edges sharp, depth true — Hazel set down the graver, put her palm flat against the carving, and waited.

It didn't take long anymore. It never took long anymore.

"Well," said a voice through the ivory, warm and dry and faintly amused, the voice of a man settling into a chair he fully intended to occupy for a century. "Let's see what you've written about me. If you've flattered me, I'm filing a complaint. If you've slandered me, I'm filing two."

"Heard us. Stayed anyway. Right about the window."

A pause. Then, genuinely touched and trying to hide it and failing, the way he always failed: "You gave me credit for the window?"

"You were right about it. Accuracy, not kindness. Your words."

"My best words." She could hear him smiling. Through bone, somehow, you could hear smiling — a warmth in the resonance, like sun reaching the bottom of a well. "How's the ledger?"

"Balanced. Odell Brask just paid his grandmother's crossing with a forward-dated song. He sang badly, for the record. The Choir has already complained to me twice."

"We're very particular audiences. Tell him to practice. We're immortal; we have time to wait for improvement." A rustle in the sound, like pages turning on the far side of everything. "Anything else to report?"

Hazel leaned back against the bench and let herself look out the window — at the street, at the market stalls setting up without paying a weather-tithe, at children running past who had never known a winter that took names. "The guildmaster's family appealed the covenant again. Third time. The court ruled against them. Apparently 'my grandfather hoarded the songs of the dead' is not a strong legal position."

"It never was. Their lawyer should have consulted me. I keep excellent records over here."

"How is it over there?" she asked, the way she asked every day, and the way she would ask every day, because he had told her once that being remembered accurately required asking accurate questions.

"Warm," Jonah said. "Unhurried. My grandmother sends her regards and says your penmanship has improved. The Choir is learning forty new songs from a fisherman who drowned in '61 and turns out to have been sitting on quite a repertoire. And Hazel—" the warmth in the bone shifted, deepened, became for a moment almost unbearably gentle, "—the spring came through here too. Everything you planted on your side grows on mine. In case you wondered if it mattered. It matters."

Hazel wiped her eyes with the back of her wrist, briskly, the way you'd settle an account. "Good. Noted for the record."

"Noted," he agreed. "Go on, then. You have customers. And I have a rehearsal."

"One more thing." She picked up the bone again, turned it in the light. "The hart's antler had another piece. I'm going to carve it next week. I haven't decided what yet."

"Carve whatever you like," Jonah said. "But if it's a portrait of me, make the nose accurate. Everyone over here agrees it's my best feature, and I'd hate for history to undersell it."

She laughed — really laughed, the kind that surprised her own ribs — and set the bone down in the east-window light, where it glowed like something alive, which, in every way that counted, it was.

* * *

That evening she closed the ledger and stood a moment in the doorway of the counting house, looking at the sign, at the open windows, at the town going gold with sunset.

Her mother had taught her that inheritance was what got left to you — debts, prophecies, half-written futures in your own future hand. It had taken a dead man's sacrifice and a winter's worth of singing to teach her the correction: inheritance was also what you chose to carry on. A song, forwarded. A scratch in a countertop, preserved. A name in bone, answered.

She locked up — though honestly the lock was ceremonial now; half the town wandered in to read the open books, and she encouraged it — and walked home through the warm evening, humming.

Behind her, in the darkened shop, the ledger lay open on the counter. And in the margin of tomorrow's page, in a hand that was not hers, in ink that had not been there at closing, someone on the other side had written a single line:

*Item: one story, ongoing. Terms: excellent. Payment: spring.*
