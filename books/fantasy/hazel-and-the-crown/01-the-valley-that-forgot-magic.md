## 1. The Valley That Forgot Magic

The bees woke before Silas Crane did, which was the correct order of things, and he told them so every morning as though they might one day take it under advisement.

"Up you get," he murmured, lifting the first lid of the season. The hive exhaled its warm bread-smell into the cold morning air, and a few drowsy workers crawled over his knuckles with the absent dignity of old women at market. "Mind the frost on the east comb. And don't start with me about the clover. I know about the clover."

The bees, being bees, ignored him entirely and got on with the business of living, which Silas had always found instructive.

He was thirty-one years old and had lived in the valley of Hollowmere for nine years, and in all that time he had never once been struck by lightning. This was, for most men, not an achievement worth noting. For Silas it was a discipline, like prayer or beekeeping — something practiced daily, without fail, and never spoken of aloud.

The valley itself made the discipline easier. Hollowmere sat in a fold of the mountains like a coin dropped in a pocket, and magic could not follow a body down the switchback road into it. Whatever strange weather lived in a person's blood simply... quieted. Went to sleep. The old-timers called it the valley's mercy, and left offerings of bread and buttons at the standing stone by the mill, though none of them could have said what from. They only knew that here, nothing whispered in the dark, no charm ever worked, no curse ever took, and nobody's grandmother turned out to be something else wearing her face.

Silas knew what the valley protected them from, because he was it.

Beneath his collar, high on the left side of his chest where the heart's thunder gathers, he carried the storm-caller's brand: a spiral forked like a lightning-bolt, inked into the skin in silver that never faded and never warmed. In the lowlands it would ache when storms gathered three valleys off. Here it was just a scar that had forgotten its job, and Silas had spent nine years being grateful for that in the way other men are grateful for rain.

He avoided mirrors the way some men avoid old debts. It wasn't vanity — quite the opposite. It was that mirrors, even valley-mirrors, dead-magic mirrors, sometimes caught him wrong. He'd catch his own eye across a polished kettle or a still trough and think, just for half a heartbeat, *that man is going to be trouble*, and then the feeling would pass and he'd go back to scraping wax. So: no mirrors. He shaved by touch. His reflection in the honey vat was a smear of shadow and he preferred it that way.

The morning the peddler came up the lane, Silas was elbow-deep in hive number four, settling a quarrel between two queens' factions with the impartial diplomacy of smoke.

"Ho, the apiary!" called a voice like a cart wheel needing grease.

Silas straightened, wincing as his back registered its opinion of the cold. Coming up the track was a mule, a cart hung with pots and ribbons and birdcages containing no birds, and atop the driver's bench a small round woman wrapped in a coat that had been red once, in some other century, in some other country.

"Brida Quill," Silas said, and sighed the sigh of a man greeting weather he'd hoped would go around.

"The very same." Brida reined in, beamed with all the confidence of someone who had never once been welcome anywhere and had stopped caring around her fourth decade. She was the only peddler who made the switchback road twice a year, spring and autumn, and the only person in Hollowmere who looked at Silas Crane the way you look at a locked door out of habit rather than suspicion. "You look terrible, Crane. Have you been sleeping?"

"I've been wintering."

"You've been brooding. There's a difference. Brooding has posture." She swung down from the cart, joints crackling like green firewood, and peered past him at the hives. "How's the stock?"

"Strong. Mean. Good honey year coming if the frost holds off."

"Frost won't hold off." She said it lightly, shaking out her skirts, but something in Silas's chest ticked over, the way a clock ticks when it's about to be wrong. "Early winter this year. I can smell it. I've got furs to sell in Greyharbor before the passes seal, and I mean to be through them well ahead of the snow." She patted the cart affectionately. "Which brings me to why I've climbed a mountain to annoy you."

"You climbed a mountain to sell me something I don't want."

"I climbed a mountain," Brida said, "to sell you something you don't want *at a fair price*. There's a distinction, and it's the whole basis of commerce." She began rummaging in the cart's depths, moving a copper pot, a bundle of quills, a taxidermied stoat with an expression of permanent grievance. "I've got needles, nails, ribbon, salt, a book—"

"A book," Silas said flatly.

"Mm." She didn't look up. "Found it in an estate sale down in the fen country. Old house, older family, last of them died with the doors open and the fire lit, which I always think is a shame. Book was in a locked drawer. Under the floorboards under the locked drawer, if you follow me."

"I don't want it."

"Didn't say you wanted it. Said I've got it." She straightened, holding a battered volume wrapped in oilcloth, and for the first time since Silas had known her, Brida Quill wasn't performing. Her small eyes were steady on his face. "It's a strange one, Crane. Paper's a hundred years old if it's a day, but the ink—" she turned it over in her hands, frowning, "—the ink looks wet. Looked wet when I found it. Looks wet now. I've sold books to priests and scholars and one very frightening nun, and none of them would touch this one. Too heavy, they said. Books shouldn't weigh what this weighs."

"Then sell it to the miller. He reads."

"The miller reads almanacs and scandal sheets. This needs—" she hesitated, hunting for the word, and settled on it reluctantly, "—*company*."

The word went down Silas's spine like a drop of ice water. Behind him, inside hive four, ten thousand bees went abruptly, totally silent.

That should have been his answer right there. Bees go quiet for storms, for bears, for the shadow of a hawk — and Silas Crane had spent thirty-one years learning every language fear spoke. But the morning was bright, the frost was melting off the fence rails, and the brand beneath his collar was cool and dumb as a coin. Nine years of peace will make a fool of anyone. It had certainly made a fool of him.

"How much?" he said.

Brida named a price that was insulting to them both, and Silas paid it, because that was how she liked to do business — she'd feel she'd cheated you otherwise — and because some part of him, the part he kept below the waterline, already knew refusing wouldn't matter. Some things come up a mountain road whether you buy them or not. Better to have the receipt.

She handed the book over with both hands, oddly formal, and then clapped him on the shoulder hard enough to stagger a smaller man. "There. Now we're both rid of it. Honey next autumn, Crane, and none of your scowling — it curdles the cream."

She was gone down the lane before he thought of a reply, which was unlike him, and the silence of the bees lasted another full minute, which was unlike them.

* * *

He didn't open the book until evening, and he told himself the delay was chores rather than dread, and mostly believed it.

The house was small and clean and smelled of wax and woodsmoke, the way he'd built it to. One room downstairs, a loft above, shelves his grandfather would have approved of and a hearth his grandfather would have called ambitious. On the mantel, face-down, a hand mirror he hadn't uncovered in nine years. He lit two lamps, put the kettle on out of pure nervous habit, and set the book on the table between them like a guest he suspected of theft.

The oilcloth came away easily. The cover was leather gone soft and dark as old blood, tooled with a design he couldn't quite resolve — spirals, maybe, or eyes, depending on the light. No title. The weight of it in his hands was wrong, the way Brida had said: dense as river stone, warm as a sleeping animal.

"Don't be ridiculous," Silas told it, and opened it.

The pages were heavy rag paper, foxed at the edges, covered in a fine spidery script that looped and flourished like handwriting trying to become music. Margins crowded with little diagrams — stars, chains, a mountain drawn in cross-section with something coiled at its root. A book of prophecies, sure enough. He'd seen one before, long ago, in a life he kept folded up small and stored in a place even he didn't visit. Courts kept such books. Courts *wrote* such books.

He turned pages. Kings unnamed and kings foretold. A famine written in verse so bad it circled back around to good. A wedding, a war, a door that must not be opened until — the page tore there, deliberately, he thought, torn out and burned by somebody who'd read ahead.

And then, near the middle of the book, the handwriting changed.

It was still spidery, still fine, but suddenly *careful* — the way writing gets careful when the writer's hands are shaking and they're pretending they aren't. The heading read:

*Of the Storm That Was Sent Away.*

Silas's mouth went dry. He told himself it was a common enough thing, storms sent away, weather-omens, folk poetry. He turned the page because his hands did it without consulting him.

The page showed a man.

An ink drawing, fine-lined, masterful — a man standing in a doorway with bees in the foreground and mountains behind, rendered with such particularity that Silas recognized the crooked gatepost, the third rail missing from the fence, the chip in the doorframe from the year he'd misjudged a hive box. The man in the drawing stood the way Silas stood, weight on the left leg, head slightly bowed, one hand raised toward the hives.

His face was Silas's face.

Not similar. Not a cousin's resemblance. It was his own face looking back at him off a hundred-year-old page, down to the crease between the brows, down to the small scar on the chin from a fall he'd taken at seven years old off a roof in a country he would not name.

The room got very quiet. The kettle, halfway to boiling, seemed to hold its breath.

Below the portrait, the careful handwriting continued:

*He will hide where magic cannot follow, and the hiding will keep him, for a while. The valley loves him and cannot say so. Let him keep his bees; let him keep his small mercies; let him keep his collar closed.*

*But on the night the peddler brings the book, the silence begins to break.*

Silas read it three times. Then a fourth, slowly, the way you reread a letter that says someone has died, checking each word for a loophole.

*On the night the peddler brings the book.*

Brida had come up the lane that morning. The book had been in his house for six hours. His hands were on it now, and the paper was warm, and — he pressed his thumb to the corner of the page and it came away faintly dark, faintly damp, as though the ink had been laid down an hour ago instead of a hundred years.

"No," Silas said aloud, to the book, to the room, to whatever patient thing had carried this up a mountain to find him. "No, thank you. I'm owed a quiet life. I paid for it. I paid *dearly* for it."

The book, being a book, said nothing. But as he watched, the ink of the last line seemed to settle deeper into the paper, the way footprints sink into snow — not changing, exactly. Just becoming more true.

He did the sensible thing. He shut the book, wrapped it in the oilcloth, and put it in the iron stove, on top of a proper fire, and stood there while the flames licked at it with the satisfaction of a man solving a problem.

The fire refused it.

There's no better way to say it. The flames bent around the covers like water around a stone in a stream. The oilcloth scorched and curled to ash; the book sat in the heart of the fire untouched, unblackened, unbothered, radiating a warmth that had nothing to do with combustion. A single page lifted, as if in a wind that existed nowhere else in the room, and turned.

Silas stood with the poker in his hand for a long moment, watching his quiet life burn everything except the thing he needed burned.

Then, outside, far up the valley where the switchback road came down off the pass, a sound reached him that had no business reaching him at all — hooves, many hooves, riding fast, and beneath the hoofbeats something else, a thin ringing note like frost forming on glass, like the sound the brand used to make in the years before the valley, in the years when the sky answered him and he had not yet learned to be afraid of his own name.

The bees in all six hives went silent at once.

The fire went out.

And on the stove's cold iron, the book lay open to a new page, ink still glistening, that read:

*A stranger comes at dusk. Do not open the door.*
