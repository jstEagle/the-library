## 3. Hazel of the Under-Court

Hazel woke to the smell of beeswax and woodsmoke and the immediate, professional assessment that she was not dead.

This was good news. She catalogued it alongside her other assets: one head, aching; two hands, unmarked; one satchel, still slung across her body by its strap, which meant whoever had dragged her inside had been decent enough not to search her. Or too honest to know how. Or — she opened her eyes a slit and took inventory of the room — too poor for it to matter.

The room was small and clean in the way of places where someone had given up on beauty but not on order. Dried herbs hung from the rafters in tidy bundles. A shelf held eleven jars of honey, each labeled in a careful hand: *June, wild clover. June, meadow. July, late.* A window let in gray morning light, and through it she could see the edge of another building, a fence, a stretch of grass going gold at the tips.

No frost on the glass. That was the detail that made her chest go tight. No frost anywhere. She had forgotten what a room looked like without frost creeping up the corners like a debt collector.

She sat up slowly, because her body insisted on complaining about every joint individually, and took stock of herself the way she'd been taught to take stock of anything: precisely, without flinching, and while planning three lies ahead.

Her boots were still on. Good sign. Her coat was still buttoned. Better sign. The satchel's strap had been loosened, not cut — someone had slid it off her shoulder to lay her down and then put it back. She pressed her palm flat against the bag's side and felt the shape within it, hard-edged and cold even through the canvas, and something in her ribs unclenched by a single degree.

Still there. Still hers. Still stolen.

The floor creaked somewhere below, and Hazel did what any sensible runaway did when cornered in a stranger's house: she lay back down, closed her eyes, and prepared to be pathetic. Pathetic was useful. Pathetic got you soup instead of questions. She arranged her breathing into shallow exhaustion, let her hands go slack, and waited.

The door opened. Footsteps — heavy, deliberate, the tread of a man who moved carefully around his own furniture. He stopped beside the bed. She felt him looking at her, and she kept her face slack and her mind running like a well-oiled press.

*You are Hazel. You are from — no. Not yet. Let him talk first. Never spend a lie before you know what question it has to answer.*

"You're awake," he said. "I know you're awake. Your breathing changed."

Hazel opened one eye. It was, she reflected, a very good eye — wide, dark, suitably tragic. "Is it?" she said, and her voice came out rougher than she'd planned, because apparently some of the exhaustion was real. "Sorry. I'm — where am I?"

"A valley," he said. "My house. You collapsed at my door last night."

"Did I?" She pushed herself upright, wincing on schedule. "I'm so sorry. I don't — I've been walking a long time." This was true, which was important. Every lie needed a spine of truth or it wouldn't stand up under pressure. "There was trouble on the road. Riders."

Something crossed his face — quick, there and gone, but Hazel had spent six years reading faces for a living, and she caught it. Recognition. *He saw them too.*

"I'm Hazel," she said, before he could ask, because a name freely given bought more trust than a name extracted. "Hazel Thorn."

It wasn't her name. Her name was Hazel Vane, and she'd buried it outside the mountain's gates along with everything else she couldn't afford to carry. But *Thorn* was common enough, plain enough, and it had the advantage of being half-true: her mother had grown thorn roses along their fence until the winter killed them, and Hazel had learned to climb them barefoot without bleeding, which her mother had called a gift and which Hazel now understood had simply been the first lesson in a very long curriculum.

"Silas," he said. "Silas Crane." He said it the way people say their own names when they haven't had occasion to use them in years — testing whether it still fit.

"Well, Silas Crane." She swung her legs off the bed and stood, steadying herself on the bedpost, and made herself smile. Not a big smile. A tired, grateful, *I'm-no-trouble* smile, calibrated to the millimeter. "I'll be out of your hair as soon as I can stand straight. I don't want to impose."

"It's no imposition," he said, and then, because he was clearly a man who found lying uncomfortable and therefore did it badly: "It's a small imposition. I have work. Bees."

"Bees," Hazel repeated warmly, as though this were fascinating. "Of course. Please, don't let me keep you."

He didn't leave. He stood there with his hands in his pockets, looking at her with an expression she recognized from a hundred interrogations she'd transcribed — the look of a man circling a question he didn't know how to ask. She gave him nothing. She'd once watched the Court Scribe Prime sit through four hours of silence to make a prisoner crack, and she had learned from the best that silence was a tool, and tools could be picked up by anyone.

"You should eat," he said finally. "There's bread. And — I suppose you're not allergic to honey."

"I could never be allergic to honey," Hazel said solemnly. "That would be a moral failing."

Something flickered at the corner of his mouth. *Good,* she thought. *Like me. Laugh at me and you'll feed me.* It was an old trick and it worked on nearly everyone: be slightly funny, slightly pitiable, slightly less than a threat. Nobody interrogated the amusing ones.

* * *

The bread was dense and good and the honey was, frankly, transcendent, and Hazel ate with the controlled greed of someone who had learned that eating fast marked you as desperate and desperate people got robbed. Silas sat across the table and watched her over his own cup, and she let him watch, because watching cost him attention and attention was a resource she wanted aimed anywhere but at her satchel.

"So," he said, on the third piece of bread. "The riders."

"What about them?"

"How many?"

Hazel considered. Lying about numbers was amateur work; numbers could be checked, counted, corroborated. "Four that I saw. Maybe more behind the rise. They wore — " she frowned, as if searching for the word, though she could have drawn their livery from memory in her sleep, in the dark, upside down — "pale colors? Gray and white. Like frost. There were badges, some kind of crest, a mountain with — I don't know. I'm not from anywhere they'd bother with. I'm just a scribe. Was. Between positions."

"Scribe," Silas said.

"Mm." She buttered another slice with great concentration. "Ink and ledgers. Very dull. You'd be amazed how little anyone tells a scribe, considering how much we write down. People talk around us like we're furniture." She looked up and smiled her open, harmless smile. "Which is fine. Furniture eats better than you'd think."

"Where are you headed?"

"South," she said instantly, because *south* was the answer to every direction question and committed her to nothing. "Away from the winter. Aren't we all?"

He set down his cup. Outside the window, the light had strengthened, and she could see the valley properly for the first time: green slopes, a river braiding through the middle, hives stacked in white rows along a hillside. It looked, she thought with a strange lurch, like a painting of a place that had never heard of the long winter. Somewhere in her chest, the part of her that was still nine years old and had eaten pine bark to stop her stomach growling looked at the eleven jars of honey on the shelf and wanted to cry.

She did not cry. Crying was expensive and she was on a budget.

"This valley," she said instead, lightly, filing the observation away. "It's lovely. Very — quiet."

"Yes," Silas said, in a tone that suggested *quiet* meant considerably more to him than it did to her.

Interesting. She turned that over while she chewed. Everyone knew about the dead zones — the pockets where magic pooled wrong and went silent, where the court's reach went numb. Refugees spoke of them the way sailors spoke of safe harbors. But nobody she'd ever met had actually *lived* in one, because living in one meant living without, and most people would rather be hunted than be nothing.

And yet here was a man with a bee farm and a jar-labeling habit, hiding in a hole in the world's memory, who had seen frost-liveried riders on his road and gone pale in exactly the way a man goes pale when he recognizes the uniform.

*You're not just hiding here,* she thought. *You were put here. Or you put yourself here so thoroughly that you forgot the way out.*

She filed that away too. Hazel's mind was a ledger, always had been — columns and entries, debts and credits, everything entered neatly and nothing ever forgiven. It was the one inheritance from the court she couldn't burn.

"The riders," Silas said again, and this time the question underneath the question surfaced. "Were they following *you*?"

Here it was. The hinge. Hazel set down her knife, folded her hands, and deployed the truth the way she'd been trained to deploy it: partially, precisely, and pointed away from the wound.

"They were following *something*," she said. "I stole it from them."

His eyebrows went up. To his credit, he didn't interrupt.

"Not money," she said quickly. "Nothing like that. Documents. Papers. Things that — " she exhaled, and let a fraction of the real exhaustion show, because a confession with no cost attached reads as performance — "things that shouldn't belong to the mountain. Things the mountain took." She met his eyes. "I was a scribe in the under-court. Below the mountain, in the city that doesn't have a name on any map. I saw what was written there. Some of it I copied myself, which is its own sin, and I won't pretend otherwise. And when I ran, I took proof."

That was all true. It was also, she noted professionally, almost entirely useless — true the way a keyhole is true. It showed the shape of the thing without giving up the key.

"And they want it back," Silas said.

"They want *me* back," Hazel corrected. "The papers are an excuse. The court doesn't forgive desertion. It's not cruelty, exactly — it's bookkeeping. An unreturned asset is a line that won't balance, and the Mountain King cannot abide an unbalanced line." She smiled, thin and bitter, and let him see it. "I was very good at my job, Silas Crane. That's why they'll come for me. Mediocre scribes get replaced. Good ones get retrieved."

"Why tell me this?" he asked quietly. "You don't know me."

No, she thought. I don't. And you don't know me, and one of us is lying about nearly everything, and I genuinely cannot tell which.

"Because you pulled me off your doorstep in the night," she said, "and you didn't go through my bag, and you fed me honey I'm fairly sure is worth more per jar than I've earned in a month. That buys a person the benefit of the doubt." She paused, then added — and this part surprised her by being unscripted — "Also, you look like a man who knows what it is to be followed."

He went very still. Outside, a bee bumped against the window glass, twice, and droned off.

"I should check the road," he said, standing abruptly.

"Take the back path," Hazel said. "If they circled in the night, the front's the wrong place to look."

He stopped in the doorway and looked back at her, and for a moment his face did something complicated — surprise and suspicion and, buried deepest, something almost like relief. As if he'd been alone with his vigilance so long that having a second pair of eyes on it was a luxury he'd forgotten existed.

"You've done this before," he said. It wasn't quite a question.

"I've survived before," Hazel said. "It's similar, but the pay is worse."

* * *

He left, and Hazel did three things in rapid succession.

First, she checked the satchel. The key was there — wrapped in oilcloth, then in a spare shift, then in lies: a false bottom sewn with court-standard stitching, a decoy letter in cipher that would take a cryptographer two satisfying hours to break and yield nothing but a recipe for pickled eels, and, topmost, a perfectly innocent bundle of love letters addressed to a fictional aunt in Greyharbor, written in a hand deliberately two degrees sloppier than her own. She'd designed the whole arrangement herself, and she permitted herself ten seconds of professional pride before moving on.

Second, she examined the room. Under the mattress: nothing. Behind the shelf: nothing. In the cold hearth, however, curled in the ash like a dead moth — a scrap of paper, scorched at the edges, that refused to burn. She smoothed it out. Blank. But the paper was wrong, thick and faintly warm, with a grain that shifted under her thumb like something breathing, and her stomach dropped through the floorboards, because she knew that paper. She had milled its cousins by the ream in the Scriptorium beneath the mountain. Prophecy-stock. Scripted vellum, ensorcelled, expensive, restricted to the Office of the Scribe Prime.

Why did a beekeeper in a magic-dead valley own a page of prophecy-stock?

Third — and this was the mistake, she'd admit later, the first genuine error she'd made since fleeing the court — she sat down at his table, found his pen and ink (of course he had pen and ink; everyone had pen and ink; it was practically a law of nature), and began, purely as an exercise, purely to keep her hand from shaking, to draft a list.

*Assets,* she wrote. *One: shelter, temporary. Two: host, unknown quantity, probable fugitive, probable gentle. Three: valley null-field — confirm extent. Four:*

She stopped. Because the fourth entry, the one her hand had written without asking permission, read:

*Four: Tam.*

And beneath it, in smaller letters, as if trying to hide from itself:

*Sixty-one days to gate-sealing. Sixty-one days to buy him back or lose the receipt forever.*

Hazel looked at the words for a long time. Then she did what she always did with things that hurt: she blotted them, folded the paper into precise eighths, and tucked it into her sleeve, because a scribe throws away nothing, least of all her own confessions.

Tam. Tam, who had smiled when they came for him. That was the part she couldn't file, couldn't balance, couldn't enter in any column. She had expected him to fight. She had rehearsed his fighting — she'd lain awake nights constructing the argument, *you can't, you promised Mother, I'll find another way* — and instead he had looked at the contract in her hand, understood in one glance what it cost and what it bought, and smiled at her. Smiled, and squeezed her wrist, and said, *Winter's long, Hazie. Go be warm.*

And she had signed. In her own blood, per the custom, her signature looping elegant and damning across the line, and the clerk had stamped it, and the collectors had taken her brother's soul out through his mouth like a breath of smoke, and Tam's body had gone on sitting at the table, breathing, blinking, empty, and she had walked out of the under-court with passage papers and sixty pounds of guilt and had not looked back, because looking back was for people whose ledgers balanced.

*Guilt is a currency,* the Scribe Prime used to say, pacing the scriptorium with his ink-stained fingers laced behind his back. *Most people waste it feeling. Spend it working.*

Hazel had spent hers working for six years. She intended to spend the next sixty-one days the same way.

The door opened. Silas came in faster than he'd left, snow-melt on his shoulders — snow-melt, in a valley with no frost, which was its own wrong note — and his face had gone the color of the ash in his hearth.

"They're at the valley's edge," he said. "Four of them. Riding the boundary line back and forth like — like dogs that've lost a scent." He looked at her, and whatever he saw in her face made him add, flatly: "They're not leaving, are they."

"No," Hazel said. She rose, rolled her sleeves, and began, with the brisk competence that was both her armor and her prison, clearing the breakfast things. "But they can't come in. Can they? This valley — it keeps things out. That's why you're here."

"That's why I'm here," he agreed carefully.

"Then we have time." She set the plates down harder than necessary. "Time is the one thing I know how to manage, Silas Crane. Give me a week and I'll have us a route south that no rider's horse has ever smelled. Give me two and I'll have forged us papers good enough to walk through the King's own—"

"Hazel."

"—what?"

"You're doing the thing," he said, "where you talk very fast and organize things so you don't have to feel them. I do it with the bees."

Hazel opened her mouth to deny it with dignity, and found, to her considerable irritation, that her eyes were stinging.

She blamed the honey. It was clearly very strong stuff.

Outside, far off at the valley's rim, something glinted — pale armor catching the sun, four points of light riding a slow, patient circuit, like hands counting the walls of a house they intended to own. And in her sleeve, against her wrist, the folded list pressed its neat eighth-fold edges into her skin, sixty-one days ticking down inside it like a second heart.

"Tell me about the bees," Hazel said, when she trusted her voice again. "Start with the jars. Why does the July honey get its own label?"

And Silas Crane, fugitive, beekeeper, owner of impossible paper, looked at her for a long moment — and then, mercifully, told her.
