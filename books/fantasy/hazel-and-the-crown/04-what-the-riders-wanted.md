## 4. What the Riders Wanted

Hazel had woken in stranger beds than this one — the archive cot during the winter of the Great Inventory, a hayloft above a tollhouse whose owner had wanted payment in a currency she refused to name — but she had never woken in a stranger's bed while pretending to still be asleep so she could listen to him worry.

Silas Crane worried the way other men paced. He moved through his small house touching things: the kettle, the shutter, the window frame where he could see out without being seen. She heard him stop at the window and go very still, and she opened her eyes a slit and saw why.

Riders.

They came along the valley's rim like ink drawn around the edge of a page. Seven of them, maybe eight, on horses that were too pale to be horses — frost-liveried, manes like spun glass, hooves that left no prints in the grass but left the grass itself gray where they passed. They did not enter the valley. They circled it, patient as water finding a crack, and every time they reached the line where Silas's fields began, their horses shied and turned, and the riders wheeled them back along the rim again.

"The valley won't let them in," Silas said, without turning around. He'd known she was awake. Of course he had. "It doesn't let anything in. That's rather the point of it."

"Then we're fine," Hazel said, sitting up. Her ribs ached from three days of hard riding before her mare had foundered and she'd run the rest of the way on foot. "They can circle until spring."

"They've been circling since before dawn." He finally looked at her, and his face had that quality she was already learning to read — the storm-caller's brand hidden under his collar, the man built entirely out of careful ordinary things, and underneath it all something watching the sky. "Nothing circles that long unless it's waiting for an invitation. Or making one."

Hazel got up, found her boots by the door where she'd left them, and made herself useful by putting the kettle on, because that was what you did in someone else's house when you were trying not to be thrown out of it. The book lay on his table where he'd dropped it last night, the prophecy page still open to her own face — no, not her face, she reminded herself, a woman with her build and her jaw and her eyes, inked a hundred years ago by a dead scribe's hand. She kept her back to it.

"You should know," she said, "that those riders aren't here for me specifically. Probably."

"Probably."

"They're carrying a seal. A court seal. If I'm right, it's a warrant of recovery, which means they're not authorized to take anyone. They're authorized to take *something*." She poured the hot water over the leaves he'd left in the crock. "Warrants name the object, not the person. It's a legal distinction the court is very proud of. It lets them say they never hunt people. They only recover property."

"And are you property?"

"I," said Hazel, with the crispness of a court clerk correcting a misfiled document, "am a former employee."

* * *

She watched the riders from the doorway while Silas checked on his bees, because apparently even a man with lightning under his skin had priorities, and his priorities were bees. The riders had stopped circling. All seven stood at the valley's western edge now, in a line, facing inward, motionless as fence posts. Their leader sat slightly forward in the saddle, and even at this distance Hazel could see the banner unfurled across the horse's flank: white silk, and on it, pressed in wax the color of old ice, the seal of the under-court.

She knew that seal. She had pressed that seal, four hundred times a day, for six years. And beneath it, in the warrant's lower margin, there would be a line of authentication — the countersignature of the Scribe Second Rank who had verified the document's authority.

Her signature.

Not her real one. Her forged one — the hand she'd built out of the Court Scribe Prime's habits, the way he hooked his *r*s, the way he pressed too hard on descenders because his right wrist had been broken badly in his youth and healed crooked. She had spent two years learning that wrist. She had forged the Scribe Prime's name on eleven documents before the one that mattered, and the eleventh had gotten her out of the mountain with a key sewn into her coat lining and Tam's soul hanging in a vault behind her like a lantern she couldn't carry.

The riders were holding up a piece of paper with her lie written on it, and the lie was vouching for them.

"They're showing me something," she murmured.

"What?" Silas came up beside her, smelling of smoke and honeycomb, and followed her gaze. "I can't read it from here."

"It's a courtesy." Her mouth tasted like copper. "The court always shows the seal first. Parley custom. They're saying: *we come under law*. It's meant to be reassuring." She laughed, and it came out wrong, a dry little sound with nothing in it. "It's meant to make you open your door."

"Has it ever worked?"

"Once, probably. On somebody honest."

Silas looked at her for a long moment. Then he went inside and came back with the prophecy book, tucked under his arm like a man bringing a weapon to a negotiation, which, Hazel supposed, in a way he was.

* * *

The herald came at noon.

He did not ride down. He walked — dismounted at the valley's edge, handed his reins to another rider, and stepped across the boundary line the way a man steps into cold water, braced for it. Nothing happened to him. The null-field kept magic out; it had no quarrel with an ordinary man in an extraordinary uniform. He was tall and gray-faced, with the bloodless look of someone who worked underground, and he wore the frost-livery without its helmet, and his voice, when he called out across the stubble field, was pleasant and reasonable and made the hair on Hazel's arms stand up.

"Hazel Merrow," he called. "Scribe Second Rank, resigned. We request parley under the terms of the Concord of Doors. No hands raised, no bindings laid, words only. You have my oath on the seal."

"That's a real oath," Hazel said quietly to Silas. "If he breaks it, the seal burns him. They hate doing parleys for exactly that reason."

"Then why do it?"

"Because they think they don't have to break it." She squared her shoulders. "Stay by the door. If I run, don't follow. If I scream—"

"What do I do if you scream?"

"Be flattered. It'll mean I thought you could help."

She walked out into the field alone. Behind her she felt Silas hovering in the doorway like a bad conscience, and further back, absurdly, the bees humming in their boxes, keeping their own counsel about the weather.

The herald stopped ten paces away and bowed — correctly, precisely, the bow owed to a Scribe Second Rank, no more, and she hated how much she noticed that, how the hierarchy lived in her spine still.

"Scribe Merrow."

"I resigned."

"The court does not accept resignations. It accepts leaves of absence." He produced the warrant from inside his coat, unrolled it, and held it up — not toward her, but angled so she could read it, which was worse, which was intimate, which was the whole point. "You will observe the authentication."

She observed it. Her forgery looked back at her, flawless, beautiful, the Scribe Prime's crooked-wristed hand rendered better than the Scribe Prime himself could have managed on a bad day. Eleven years of training, and this was what she'd built with it: a perfect lie that gave seven riders the legal right to stand at a valley's edge forever.

"You forged the Scribe Prime's countersignature," the herald said. Not accusing. Admiring, almost. "The court reviewed the warrant for three days before anyone thought to check whether the Scribe Prime had actually signed it. He was flattered, incidentally. He says your *r*s are better than his."

"Tell him thank you," Hazel said. "Tell him I had a good teacher and a lot of time to practice, copying out harvest ledgers in a freezing archive while my brother—" She stopped. The herald waited, courteously, letting the silence do its work. That was training too. She recognized the technique from the inside, and knowing the trap didn't keep her teeth out of it. "What do you want?"

"The key."

"I don't have any key."

"Scribe Merrow." He sighed, gently, the sigh of a man who has done this many times and finds it tedious rather than tragic. "You crossed the passes in the last week of autumn with a foundered horse, no supplies, and no destination, moving like someone who knows exactly where she's going. You carried weight sewn into your coat — the coat you're wearing now, incidentally; the left lining hangs differently than the right, we noticed it in the archive inventory and we notice it now. And the night you fled, the soul-vaults recorded an access attempt at the third-tier seals. Failed, but attempted. With a key that fit the lock and turned halfway." He rolled the warrant closed. "You took something that belongs to the mountain. Return it, and the court's interest in you ends. The warrant names an object, not a person. Walk away free. That is not a trick. Check the law if you like — you're better at the law than most of us."

The wind moved through the stubble field. Somewhere behind her, Silas's door creaked as he leaned harder against the frame.

And Hazel stood in the middle of it all and felt the first crack go through her armor — not because the offer tempted her, but because it didn't, and she understood suddenly that it wasn't meant to. The herald knew she wouldn't give up the key. The offer existed to be refused, publicly, so that when the court's patience ran out, the record would show the thief was offered terms and declined them. She was being written into a document. She could feel it happening, sentence by sentence, her refusal becoming the next clause.

That was the thing about the court. It didn't need to beat you. It just needed everything you did to be admissible.

"If I return the key," she said slowly, "Tam comes home."

The herald's face did something very small and very final. Pity, filed correctly. "The contract was signed in blood and countersigned by the debtor. Your brother's soul is court property for the term of the agreement. The key opens vaults, Scribe Merrow. It does not amend contracts."

"Then the key stays lost."

"Then the court waits." He bowed again, the same precise bow, and turned to go, and then paused, and delivered the last line like a clerk initialing a page. "Winter seals the passes in forty days. After that, nothing goes in or out of the mountain until thaw. Whatever you mean to do, do it soon. The court would prefer you froze out here honestly rather than died in there dishonestly. It's tidier."

He walked back across the boundary line. The riders turned as one, unhurried, and resumed their circuit of the valley's rim, patient as arithmetic.

Hazel stood in the field until the cold got through her coat, and then she walked back to the house, and Silas held the door open, and she went in, and only then did her legs inform her they were done.

She sat down hard on his chair. The kettle was still warm. She wrapped both hands around it anyway, for something to hold.

"So," Silas said. "Something that belongs to the mountain."

"Yes."

"In your coat."

"Yes."

"And these forty days."

"Yes."

He pulled out the other chair and sat down across from her, and to her lasting surprise he didn't ask what the thing was. He poured her tea instead, pushed the cup across the table, and said, "My grandfather used to say that when a debt collector comes to the door, you count what he brought before you count what he wants. Seven riders, a real oath, and a warrant with your best forgery on it. That's not a retrieval party. That's a siege that hasn't admitted what it is yet."

"They can't cross," Hazel said. "The valley holds."

"For now." He glanced at the window, at the riders wheeling along the far rim, small as crows. "But I found my face in a century-old book last night, and the fire wouldn't burn it, and a woman fell through my door at dusk exactly like the page said she would. I've stopped believing in *for now*."

Hazel looked down into her tea. The truth sat in her chest where she'd packed it, sewn in tight as the key itself, and it shifted when she breathed. She had told him her name and nothing else. Names were cheap. Names were the coin you paid at every door.

But the herald had counted her coat linings, and the book had counted her face, and the winter had started counting down its forty days, and somewhere in all that counting she had run out of room to be only a name.

"There's a mountain," she said, "with a court under it. And the court keeps souls in vaults, like candles in a cellar. And one of them is my brother's, because I signed it away myself, in my own blood, to buy us through the last long winter — and it bought *me* through, and it didn't buy him anything at all except a hook to hang on." Her voice stayed level, court-trained, ledger-flat, and she despised it. "I stole a key. It opens the third-tier vaults. And in forty days the passes close, and if I'm not inside the mountain before they close, I will never get him back, and he will hang in the dark singing our mother's song to himself until the contract runs out in ninety-nine years."

Silence. The bees hummed. The kettle ticked as it cooled.

Silas set down his cup with great care, the way a man sets down something when his hands want to do something else entirely.

"Ninety-nine years," he repeated.

"Fifty-eight remaining. I've been counting."

"Of course you have." He rubbed his face. When he lowered his hand, he was looking at her with an expression she couldn't file anywhere — not pity, not judgment, something more dangerous than either. Recognition, maybe. A man who hid a brand under his collar, looking at a woman who hid a key in her coat, each of them seeing the same shape of secret. "And the riders can't cross the valley line, but you can leave whenever you like."

"Any time," Hazel said. "That's the joke. The safest place in the world, and it only works if I stay. And staying is the one thing I can't do."

Outside, the light was going long and gold over the fields, and on the valley's rim, all seven riders stopped at once — stopped mid-circuit, stopped together, and turned their pale horses to face the house.

Not the valley. Not the rim.

The house.

And far off, at the edge of hearing, under the ground, under the quiet, something in the valley's deep silence went *tick* — like a clock, like a lock, like a heart deciding whether to keep beating — and the air above Silas Crane's bee-fields shivered once, the way a candle flame shivers when a door opens somewhere in the house.

Hazel's hand went to her coat lining. Silas's hand went to his collar.

"Oh no," she said. "Oh, no, it's not waiting for me. It's waiting for *you*."
