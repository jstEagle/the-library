## 6. Terms of a Bad Bargain

The knock came at dawn, and it was polite.

That was the worst part, Silas thought later. Not thunder, not splintering wood — three measured raps, evenly spaced, like a neighbor come to borrow a cup of flour. He stood in his kitchen with a honey spoon halfway to his mouth and watched Hazel go white as the frost on his window.

"They can't cross," she whispered. "You said they can't cross."

"I said they couldn't *yesterday*."

He set down the spoon. The bees in the yard had gone silent, every hive humming at once in a low, uneasy chord that raised the hair on his arms. His grandfather's iron bell hung by the door, and it was ringing — not swinging, not touched, just ringing, a thin clear note like a struck fingernail.

Silas opened the door.

The herald stood in his yard as though the yard had been arranged for her benefit. She was tall and gray-robed, with a face like a well-made chair: correct, serviceable, and entirely without warmth. Frost-riders sat their pale horses at the valley's edge behind her, a full dozen of them, motionless as fence posts. They had not crossed the tree line. But they were *closer* than they had been yesterday, close enough now that Silas could see the breath of the horses steaming silver in the cold.

"Parley," said the herald, in a voice like paper being folded. "Under the old forms. I am sworn to no violence within the sound of your hearth." Her eyes flicked past him to Hazel. "Mostly."

"That's reassuring," Silas said. "Come in, then. Mind the dog."

"You don't have a dog."

"I'm considering one. It seemed wise lately."

* * *

Hazel's hands would not stop shaking, so she put them in her lap and pressed them flat against her knees, the way her mother used to do when the bailiff came counting. Breathe in for four. Hold for four. Out for four. Scribe's breathing — the first thing they taught you at the under-court, because a shaking hand makes a shaky signature, and a shaky signature is a broken contract, and a broken contract in that place was worth more than your life and cost more too.

The herald sat across from her at Silas's kitchen table, which was absurd. There was a bowl of plums between them. Someone — Silas, damn him, damn his terrible hospitality — had offered the herald plums.

"You may call me Vesper," the herald said. "I speak for the Mountain King in matters of debt."

"We don't owe you anything," Hazel said.

Vesper smiled. It was the kind of smile that had been explained to her by a tutor. "Everyone owes the mountain something, scribe. Most people simply haven't read their own ledgers." She turned the plum over in her long fingers without eating it. "But we needn't be tedious. A theft occurred. An item of the court's property left the archive beneath the mountain, carried out in the pocket of a runaway clerk. The King is reasonable. Return what was taken, and the matter ends here, this morning, over breakfast. Refuse—" she glanced around the kitchen, at the copper pots, the jars of honey, the iron bell by the door, cataloguing everything with the mild attention of an appraiser, "—and the matter ends when winter seals the passes. The court claims both thieves. The valley's little silence will not hold forever. Nothing holds forever. Ask the ice."

"The valley isn't yours to threaten," Silas said.

"The valley," said Vesper, "is not my concern. You are. Well." She rose, smoothed her robe. "I will return at dusk for your answer. Do think it over carefully. The King truly does prefer mercy. It is cheaper than the alternative, and he is — I want to say *frugal* — about expense." At the door she paused. "The plums are excellent. Pity about the company they keep."

Then she was gone, and the riders wheeled away from the tree line like a thought being withdrawn, and the bees started up again all at once, and Silas sat down heavily across from Hazel and said:

"So. What did you steal?"

* * *

Hazel looked at the table. She looked at the door. She calculated, briefly and honestly, whether she could be out the back and over the fence before he caught her, and knew she couldn't, and knew also — this was the worse discovery — that she didn't especially want to.

"My brother's name is Tam," she said.

She told it plainly, because there was no other way to tell it and no time to dress it up. The last long winter. The village where the snow came in October and never left, where the candle-guild's prices climbed until light itself was a luxury, where her father coughed himself thinner and thinner and the grain ran out in the second month of a winter that should have lasted one. The court's agents came through the fens in a black barge, buying. Not grain. Not gold. Souls — or rather, futures. A family could sell one member's soul to the under-court and receive passage, provision, warmth, survival. The rest lived. That was the arithmetic. That was always the arithmetic.

"They make it sound kind," she said. "That's the trick of it. The clerk who drew up our contract had a voice like a warm bath. He explained how Tam wouldn't suffer, how his soul would be *kept safe*, kept like a lantern in a vault, and how someday — someday! — arrangements might be made. And Tam was seventeen and hungry, and I was nineteen and desperate, and Papa was dying, and Mama had already stopped talking." Her thumb worried the edge of the table. "Tam smiled when he signed. Did you know that? He smiled. He thought he was being brave. He thought he was saving us. And the worst thing — the thing I can't get out from under — is that he *was*. We lived. Everyone else in that village who wouldn't deal, half of them didn't see spring."

Silas said nothing. He was good at saying nothing, she was learning. He just listened with his whole face, which was somehow harder to sit under than judgment.

"So I took passage south. Found work. Worked my way into the court's own scriptorium — they hire outsiders, they have to, they eat their own scribes' memories until none of them can remember how to write properly." A ghost of a smile. "Three years I copied contracts. Ledgers. Collection orders. I learned every seal, every cipher, every form. And eleven days ago, when they sent me down to the soul-vaults to update the index—" her voice caught, steadied, "—I saw him. Or I saw his lantern. A little dim light in a row of ten thousand dim lights, humming. Humming our mother's song. And I stole the key off the warden's belt while he slept, and I walked out of the mountain with it sewn into my coat, and I have been running since."

She reached into her collar and drew out a cord. On it hung a key no longer than her finger, made of some dark metal that seemed to drink the morning light, worked into the shape of a moth with folded wings.

"Not documents," she said. "Not evidence. This opens the vaults where they keep them. All of them. Every soul the court has bought or taken or harvested for — I don't even know how long. Centuries, maybe." She set it on the table between them, and her hand finally, finally went still. "So yes. They want it back. And no, I'm not giving it to them. And I'm sorry, Silas Crane, because you seem like a decent man with bees, and I have brought a war to your kitchen."

Silas looked at the key for a long moment. Then he got up, put the kettle on, and said, "Right. Tea first. Decisions after."

* * *

*Tea first,* Silas thought, watching the water heat. *Decisions after.* As if there were any decision at all. As if a man could hand a girl and a key to riders in frost-liveries and then go back to his hives and his quiet mornings and sleep a single night in that bed ever again.

His brand itched beneath his collar. It had been itching for two days, since the book arrived, since he'd seen his own face inked on a page dated tonight — *tonight*, which was now today, which was now dusk-shaped and coming fast. He hadn't told her about the book. He wasn't sure why. Cowardice, probably, wearing the mask of privacy. *My secrets and yours,* he thought grimly. *What a household.*

"You're staring at the kettle like it insulted you," Hazel said.

"It's thinking. Kettles take time." He poured. "Here's what I know. Those riders couldn't cross the valley line yesterday. Today their herald walked into my kitchen and drank my tea — well, declined my tea, which is nearly as intimate. Something's changed. The valley's protection is thinning, or being thinned, and I'd wager my bees it has something to do with why it protected me in the first place."

"You? The valley protects *you*?"

"There are things about me," Silas said, "that I keep under my collar, along with a scar I'll tell you about never. Let's say I came here to be somewhere magic couldn't find me, and it worked, for seven years, right up until a peddler sold me a book of prophecies with my own face in it. Since then things have been getting less quiet at a rate I don't love."

Hazel stared at him. "You've been sitting on that for two days?"

"I've been *processing* it for two days. Processing takes time."

"You absolute—" She laughed, sudden and helpless, and it transformed her whole face, and Silas thought, distantly, that it was a shame she didn't do it more often, and then thought, more distantly still, that he might be in a fair amount of trouble. "Fine. Fates all around. Yours in a book, mine in a vault. We're a matched set of disasters."

"Don't flatter us. Disasters imply scale."

* * *

Dusk came the way it comes in autumn — early, apologetic, sliding blue into the gaps in the curtains. Silas lit the lamps. Hazel sat with the moth-key in her fist and her spine straight as a ledger line. Between them on the table lay the prophecy book, closed, because neither of them wanted to look at it and neither of them could stop glancing at it.

At the exact moment the sun went down, the knock came again. Three raps. Polite as ever.

Vesper entered without waiting, and this time she did not sit, and this time she did not look at the plums. She looked at Hazel's closed fist, and something in her gray face shifted — not warmth, nothing so useful as warmth, but the sharpened interest of a fisherman seeing the rod bend.

"Well?" she said.

Hazel opened her hand. The key caught the lamplight and gave none back.

"Here is my answer," Hazel said, and her voice only shook a little, and Silas, listening, thought it was the bravest shaking he had ever heard. "Tell your King he can have his key back when I've used it. Tell him I'm going to open his vaults and walk out with my brother's soul and as many others as will follow me. Tell him the contract he bought from a starving family was signed under duress and written in bad faith, and that I know contract law better than any clerk he owns, because he trained me himself. And tell him—" she lifted her chin, "—that if he wants it, he can come and take it, and learn what it costs."

The silence stretched. Somewhere outside, the bees hummed their low chord. The iron bell by the door rang its thin note, unstruck.

Vesper bowed — precisely, formally, the bow of one professional acknowledging another's move on the board. "I will convey your message verbatim. The King appreciates clarity." She straightened. "For the record, scribe: no one has ever refused the parley before. You are the first. I confess I find it interesting." Her gaze slid to Silas, and lingered there a moment too long, on his collar, on the place beneath it. "Both of you. Interesting. Dusk tomorrow, then. After that, the passes begin to seal, and the court's patience seals with them."

She let herself out. Through the window they watched her walk to the tree line, unhurried, and the twelve riders turn as one, and the frost-light of them dwindle down the valley road toward the mountain that ate winters.

Hazel exhaled for what felt like the first time in a day. "Well. I've done it now."

"You've done it now," Silas agreed. He looked at the key, and at the book, and at the girl holding both her courage and her guilt in one small scarred hand. Outside, far off toward the peaks, lightning flickered inside a cloudless sky — once, twice, patient as a heartbeat. His brand burned like a coal.

"Hazel," he said slowly, "how much do you know about the way out of this valley? Because I have a horrible feeling we're going to need to leave it."
