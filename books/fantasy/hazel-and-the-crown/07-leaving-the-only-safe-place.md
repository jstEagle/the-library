## 7. Leaving the Only Safe Place

Silas had lived in the valley for eleven years, and in all that time he had never once seen the road out of it.

Not because the valley was hard to leave. There was a pass, a perfectly ordinary pass, with a perfectly ordinary road winding up through the pines and down the other side toward whatever lay beyond — fens, he'd been told, and after the fens a city called Greyharbor, and beyond that the whole wide world, going about its business without him. He could have walked out any morning before breakfast. He simply never had.

The bees didn't need him to leave. The hives needed tending twice a week in summer, once a fortnight in autumn, and not at all in winter, when they clustered together inside their boxes like small humming congregations and asked nothing of him but quiet. He was very good at quiet. He had made an art of it. Eleven years of quiet, layered one day over another like wax over comb, until the man underneath — the storm-caller, the exile, the thing with the brand beneath his collar — had gone soft and still as old honey.

He stood at his kitchen window that morning, watching Hazel sleep in his grandfather's chair, and understood with terrible clarity that all of it was about to end.

She'd slept there because she'd refused the bed. "You've done enough," she'd said, or rather mumbled, on her feet and swaying, insisting she only needed to sit a moment before deciding what to do next. That had been four hours ago. Now she was folded into the chair with her boots still on, one hand curled around the stolen key on its cord at her throat, her breath whistling faintly through a nose that had taken a knock during her collapse at his door. Even asleep she looked like someone composing an argument.

Silas put the kettle on. It seemed like the correct response to the end of the world.

* * *

"You can't stay here," Hazel said, around her third cup of tea. "I know you know that. I watched you figure it out last night while I was pretending to be asleep."

"You were pretending to be asleep?"

"I'm a scribe. We're trained to eavesdrop. It's half the job." She blew on her tea. "The other half is forging things badly enough to be blamed for later."

"The riders can't cross," Silas said. "You saw them. They circled the rim all night like crows around a dead thing they couldn't land on. Whatever keeps magic out of this valley keeps them out too."

"Keeps *you* out too," Hazel said. "That's my point. That's the whole point." She set the cup down and leaned forward, and Silas noticed again how young she was underneath all that competence — nineteen, maybe twenty, with chapped hands and ink stains worked so deep into her knuckles they looked like birthmarks. "Whatever's coming for me isn't going to stop at the valley's edge forever. Winter seals the passes. You told me yourself the peddler said it. When winter comes, the court doesn't need to send riders. They just need to wait. And then—"

"And then what? They walk in when the snow melts?"

"No. They don't need to walk anywhere." She tapped the table, where the prophecy book sat between them like a guest neither wanted to acknowledge. "I've read court documents my whole life. This book isn't a prediction, it's a *claim*. A lien. Your face is in it, dated to tonight. Tonight something happens, Silas. Something the mountain has scheduled. And if you're sitting here in your bee-field when it does—"

"Then what?" he asked, and hated how small his voice came out.

Hazel hesitated. She was many things — liar, thief, runaway, the most stubborn person he'd met in eleven years of meeting almost no one — but she was honest in her way, which was to say she lied constantly except when lying would be crueler than the truth.

"Then the valley stops protecting you," she said quietly. "Because protection isn't free. Somebody built that null-field, ages ago, on purpose, and everything built on purpose can be unbuilt. Or bought. Or broken. And when it goes—" she gestured vaguely at him, at his collar, at the brand underneath it that he'd shown her by lamplight, the storm-sigil seared into his skin like a signature on a contract he'd never signed, "—everything you've been holding back comes through at once. I felt it last night. One heartbeat of silence breaking, and the sky split open. You think the court did that? That was *you*, leaking."

Silas looked out the window. His bees were working the late asters, methodical and unbothered, and he envied them with his whole chest.

"I haven't left this valley since I was seventeen," he said.

"I know."

"I don't know what's past the pass. Not really. I know the words for it. Fens. Greyharbor. But I've never—" He stopped, started again. "When I cross that line, the magic will find me. Like water finding a crack. Eleven years I've been dry, and out there I'll drown in it. That's what it feels like. Drowning. Everyone thinks storm-callers love the storm, and maybe some do, but mine—" He pressed his palm flat against the table, against the wood his grandfather had planed smooth, and felt the faint itch under his skin that never quite went away, even here, even now. "Mine was taken from me and chained to a mountain, and it hates me for surviving without it. I can feel that too. Out there I'll feel all of it."

Hazel was quiet for a moment. Then she said, "My brother is in a vault under that mountain, hanging from a hook like a coat, and I put him there. So I'm going back regardless. With you or without you. The prophecy says we go together, and I hate prophecies, but I hate leaving people behind more, and I have left exactly one person behind in my life and I will not make it two."

"That's not fair," Silas said.

"No," she agreed. "It isn't. Most true things aren't."

* * *

He spent the afternoon doing the chores he would no longer have, and it was the worst afternoon of his life, which he recognized was a privileged sort of worst, and did anyway.

He fed the hives their winter syrup, tipping the little feeders into place while the bees crawled over his hands with the blind trust of creatures who had never once considered that a hand might mean anything but warmth. He talked to them, because he always talked to them, low nonsense in the register his grandfather had used: *good girls, easy now, plenty for the cold.* Queen Marigold's hive was strongest; he'd named her line after flowers for six generations of queens, and he caught himself wondering who would name the seventh, and had to stand very still for a while with his forehead against the hive box until the feeling passed.

He closed the chicken coop. He banked the fire. He took down his grandfather's tools and hung each one in its place on the pegboard, handle out, the way the old man had insisted, *so a tool knows its home,* though Silas suspected the real reason was that his grandfather liked looking at them.

And then he went to the shelf above the hearth, where the iron bell sat.

It wasn't much to look at. Fist-sized, black with age, cracked along one side, mute as a stone. His grandfather had carried it out of the mountains forty years ago, when the courts purged the storm-lines, and had rung it exactly once in Silas's hearing — the day they crossed into the valley, when Silas was seventeen and screaming, when the storm was being peeled off him like bark off a tree, when every mile of the road out had been lit by lightning answering his grief. At the valley's edge his grandfather had stopped, rung the bell, and said, *There. Now nothing can find you. Now you get to be nobody.*

Silas had spent eleven years learning to be nobody, and he'd gotten good at it, and the cruelest part — the part that made his eyes sting now, standing in his own warm kitchen — was that he'd *liked* it. He'd liked the bees and the quiet and the smallness of his days. He'd liked being a man whose biggest problem was a bad honey year. Somewhere along the way exile had stopped feeling like exile and started feeling like a life, and now he was going to have to grieve it while it was still technically available to him, which was, he decided, the most unfair order of operations imaginable.

He wrapped the bell in wool and put it in his pack.

Then he packed, in no particular order and with several false starts: honeycomb (for trade), a spare shirt, the good knife, a jar of salve, three books he would probably not read, and — after a long internal argument with himself — the prophecy book, because burning it hadn't worked and burying it felt like leaving a snake in his garden, and because some cowardly practical corner of his mind noted that a book which rewrote itself might be useful to have where he could see it.

At dusk he stood in his doorway and looked at his house. Two rooms, a crooked chimney, a porch swing he'd built himself and used perhaps nine times. He had painted the door blue because his grandmother's door had been blue. He locked it, then unlocked it, then left it unlocked, because if anyone from the valley came looking for him they should be able to get in and take the honey.

"Hazel," he called. "I'm ready."

She appeared at the barn's corner, where she'd been saddling the one mule he owned — a disagreeable animal named Duchess whom Hazel had somehow charmed within an hour, confirming Silas's suspicion that she could talk her way into or out of anything. She looked at him, and at the pack, and at the house, and she didn't say anything encouraging, which he appreciated. Encouragement would have broken him.

They walked out past the hives. He didn't say goodbye to the bees. Saying goodbye to the bees would have meant kneeling in the dirt and weeping, and there would be time for that on the road, presumably, when Hazel wasn't watching.

Except she was watching, of course. She was always watching.

"They'll be fine," she said. "Bees are older than courts. Older than kings. They've survived worse than winters."

"You don't know anything about bees."

"I know they've been around since before the mountain learned to be afraid," Hazel said. "That's enough."

* * *

The pass began at the old boundary stones, half-swallowed by moss, and Silas had walked to them a hundred times without ever crossing — a ritual he'd never examined, the way you don't examine the walls of a room you live in. Now he stood between two leaning stones with the evening wind coming down from the heights, carrying the first mineral smell of coming snow, and felt the hair rise on his arms.

"It's right here?" Hazel asked softly. "The line?"

"Right here." His mouth was dry. "Grandfather said you can feel it in your teeth. Like biting foil."

"And on the other side?"

"On the other side, everything I buried finds me." He shifted the pack on his shoulders. The bell clinked dully inside it, wrapped in wool. "Eleven years ago I came through here screaming loud enough to bring down half the pine slope. My grandfather rang the bell and the valley swallowed the sound, and I woke up three days later in that house, and I was *quiet*. Do you understand? For eleven years I have been quiet. Not peaceful. Quiet. There's a difference, and I only just learned it, and I hate that I learned it from a book that wants to eat me."

Hazel reached out and took his sleeve — not his hand, his sleeve, a scribe's precise contact, evidence rather than intimacy.

"We don't have to cross tonight," she offered. "We can camp on this side. Cross at dawn."

"If I camp on this side tonight I'll spend the whole night talking myself into staying. And tomorrow night. And the night after." He laughed, and it came out wrong, wet at the edges. "No. If I'm going to drown, I'd rather do it while it's still light enough to see the bottom."

He stepped between the stones.

The change was immediate and total, like walking from a dim room into noon. Every nerve he had — nerves he'd forgotten he had, nerves that had lain fallow for eleven years like his grandfather's fields — lit up at once. The wind wasn't just wind anymore; it had texture, intention, a long memory of every mountain it had ever poured down. The clouds overhead weren't weather, they were a *herd*, vast and slow and grazing on the cold front coming down from the north, and somewhere among them, far off, something turned its attention toward him the way a dog lifts its head when it hears its name.

The brand beneath his collar flared hot as a coal.

"Oh," Silas said, and his voice came out doubled, harmonized with itself, a chord instead of a note. "Oh, that's — hello."

"Silas." Hazel's grip moved from his sleeve to his wrist. "Silas, look at me. What do you need?"

"Nothing. I — it's just —" He was laughing again, or crying, or both; the air around him tasted like the minute before a summer storm, ozone and green and possibility. "It remembers me. Eleven years and it *remembers me*. Nobody's remembered me like that since Grandfather died."

Far away, over the shoulder of the mountain they were walking away from, thunder answered. Not near thunder. Patient thunder. The kind that could wait.

Hazel looked up at the sound, then back at him, and something in her face shifted — not fear exactly, or not only fear. Recognition. The look of someone seeing a debt come due.

"There it is," she said quietly. "The storm shall kneel. That's what the book says about you, eventually. I read it again this morning while you fed your chickens." She tightened her grip on his wrist and stepped across the boundary stones to stand beside him, on the outside, in the wide cold world where magic could follow them both. "So let's go teach it whose knees those are."

Behind them, at the top of the pass, the first snow of the long winter began to fall.

Ahead, down in the darkening fens, something that was not yet a lantern and not yet a bell began, very faintly, to ring.
