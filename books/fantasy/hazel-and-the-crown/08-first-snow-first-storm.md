## 8. First Snow, First Storm

Hazel had crossed the pass at Silas Crane's pace, which was to say slowly, apologetically, and with frequent stops to apologize to the mule they didn't have.

He wasn't a slow man by nature — she'd seen him move quickly enough when she collapsed on his doorstep, all instinct and caught elbows — but he moved through the world like a guest who might be asked to leave at any moment. He checked over his shoulder every hundred paces. He touched the iron bell hanging from his pack strap the way other men touched charms against ill luck, except that for Silas the bell *was* the luck, or what remained of it.

"Say something," she said, somewhere past the tree line, where the pines gave up and the wind took over the conversation.

"I'm saying something."

"You're apologizing to a rock."

"It was in my way." He stepped around it anyway, giving it room, as though the rock had feelings too and he hadn't wanted to bruise them.

Hazel pulled her stolen court cloak tighter and decided, not for the first time, that she had made a catastrophic error in trusting a beekeeper. She was a scribe of the under-court — former scribe, runaway scribe, *wanted* scribe — and her survival instincts were supposed to be excellent. They had gotten her through a winter that ate whole villages. They had gotten her out of Greyharbor's archive with a key sewn into her hem and a forged seal drying under her thumbnail. And then those same instincts had looked at a man who flinched from mirrors and thought: *yes, this one will do.*

The pass climbed. The air thinned. And behind them, always behind them, the valley sat in its bowl like a held breath.

She'd watched it shrink as they climbed — green, ordinary, stubbornly unmagical — and she understood now why Silas had stayed eleven years in a place where nothing could follow him. Not riders. Not prophecy. Not himself. The valley was the only room in the world where Silas Crane got to be nobody, and he had walked out of it because a woman fell bleeding on his doorstep and a book refused to burn.

She hadn't thanked him yet. She kept drafting the thanks in her head the way she drafted everything — three versions, ranked by honesty — and none of them survived contact with his face.

*Version one: Thank you for ruining your life for mine.* Too honest. He'd go back.

*Version two: I'll pay you back.* A lie. She had nothing.

*Version three:* She never got past the opening clause.

The snow started an hour before the summit. Not falling so much as arriving — flakes materializing out of grey air, fat and unhurried, the first snow of the long winter proper. Hazel knew this snow. This was the snow that came down the mountain every year like a landlord collecting rent, the snow that had stood outside her family's door the winter Tam stopped smiling.

Silas stopped walking.

"Hazel." His voice had gone strange. Careful. The voice of a man holding a full cup very level. "How far to the far side?"

"Half a day down, if the weather holds."

"And if it doesn't?"

She looked at him. He was standing very still in the falling white, and his shadow — thin, stretched by the low sun — seemed to be moving when he wasn't. Just at the edges. Just a shiver, like heat off summer stone, except it was the coldest hour of the year.

"Silas," she said. "When did you last feel your magic?"

"Eleven years ago." He swallowed. "It doesn't work like that. It isn't a thing you feel. It's a thing you *are*, and the valley made me... less. Quiet. Like a hand gone numb under you in the night." His fingers flexed at his sides. "We're past the boundary stones. I can feel it coming back."

"Is that bad?"

He laughed — one short, unhappy note. "It's like asking if drowning is bad. It depends entirely on whether there's water in your lungs."

The storm answered before she could.

***

It began, as far as Hazel could tell, with his heartbeat.

Later — much later, when she'd had time to think about it like a scribe instead of like a drowning woman — she would reconstruct the sequence. His fear rose. The storm heard it. The storm, having waited eleven years behind a wall of null-silence, heard its master's voice again and came running like a dog that had been shut out all night, and it did not come gently.

But in the moment there was no sequence. There was only the sky going from grey to bruise-purple in the space of four breaths, and the wind standing up off the mountainside like something rising from a bed, and Silas saying, in a small voice utterly unlike the man who apologized to rocks:

"Oh no. Oh, no, no — *down*, get down—"

Hazel dropped flat behind a granite shelf as the first lightning hit the ridge above them. Not near them. *Above* them — close enough that the thunder arrived inside her teeth, inside her ribs, inside the fillings of her thoughts. Snow leapt off the rocks in a white gasp. Somewhere below, a pine cracked like a snapped bone.

"He can't control it," she realized aloud, stupidly, uselessly. "You can't control it."

"I told you!" He was on his knees beside her, hands pressed flat to the stone as though holding the mountain down. Rain had begun — sideways rain, needling, half ice. "I told you what I was! You said the court would unmake the valley either way, you said—"

"I said a lot of things while packing!"

Lightning again, closer, and this time she felt it *answer* him — felt the hair on her arms lift toward his body like iron filings finding their lodestone. The storm wasn't attacking them. That was the horror of it. The storm was *greeting* him, wrapping around him like a long-lost hound, and it had no idea how big it was. Wind screamed down the pass. The temperature plunged so fast her breath froze mid-air and drifted as glitter.

And Silas Crane knelt in the middle of it with his eyes rolled white and his brand blazing through his collar — she could see it now, the storm-caller's mark, a spiral like a lightning bolt trying to become a circle — and he was *terrified*, and his terror fed the wind, and the wind fed his terror, a wheel turning faster and faster with both of them strapped to it.

*This is how he dies,* she thought. *On a mountainside, killed by his own inheritance, and I die next to him because I trusted a beekeeper.*

Then the scribe in her — the part of her that had spent nine years learning the under-court's breathing disciplines, the ones you used to keep your hand steady when writing contracts in blood, the ones that kept panic from leaking into ink and ink into law — the scribe in her stood up inside her chest and said: *no.*

She crawled to him through the horizontal rain, grabbed his face in both frozen hands, and shouted directly into it:

"SILAS. LOOK AT ME. FOUR IN. HOLD. SEVEN OUT."

His eyes found hers. White receding. Storm still screaming.

"Four in," she roared, exaggerating her own chest, making it enormous, theatrical, impossible to ignore. "One-two-three-four. HOLD. One-two-three-four-five-six-seven. OUT. Again!"

"Why—" he gasped.

"Because it works! Because I used it the night I signed my brother away and it's the only reason my hand didn't shake and ruin the contract! Four in, seven out, *breathe with me or we both die on this rock!*"

And he breathed.

Badly, at first — ragged, fighting it, the way a drowning man fights the rope thrown to him because grabbing it means admitting he's drowning. But she kept her hands on his face and her count in his ears, four in, hold, seven out, four in, hold, seven out, and slowly — grudgingly, like a debt being paid under protest — the storm began to breathe with him.

She felt it happen. There was no other word for it. The wind's scream bent downward in pitch, softened, found a rhythm. Lightning still flickered but stopped striking; it hung in the clouds like veins of silver, restless, waiting. The rain gentled to sleet, sleet to snow, snow to a fall so soft it seemed embarrassed about the earlier noise.

And Silas Crane opened his eyes, and looked at her, and whispered:

"How."

"Scribes learn to keep the body quiet so the hand stays true." Her own heart was hammering hard enough to contradict her. "Apparently it works on weather."

"It's never—" He sat back on his heels, shaking, soaked, alive. "Eleven years ago I flattened half a village. Nobody talked me down. My grandmother's letters said the storm answers feeling, and I felt everything, and there was no one to—" He stopped. Started again. "You counted. It listened to you."

"It listened to *you*. I just gave you something to listen to." She was shivering violently now that the adrenaline was draining, cold flooding in where courage had been standing. "That's most of my job, historically. Giving people things to listen to."

***

They found shelter by following the storm's mood, which sounded mad and worked anyway.

Silas explained it afterward, wrapped in both their cloaks inside a shallow cave above the treeline, while Hazel fed twigs to a fire she'd built with the last dry match and a great deal of profanity. The storm, he said, wasn't a creature exactly. More like a tide. It had pressure and preference. It wanted open sky here, wanted to avoid the granite spine of the ridge there, and if you stopped fighting it and started reading it—

"—like marginalia," Hazel said.

He blinked at her. Water dripped off his nose. "What?"

"Marginalia. Notes in the margins of a text. You're not reading the main page anymore, you're reading what someone scribbled around it." She poked the fire. "My master used to say the margins are where the truth lives. The main text is what people want remembered. The margins are what they couldn't help noticing."

Silas considered this with the total seriousness he brought to everything, as though she'd handed him a puzzle box. "So the storm is... margins."

"The storm is the biggest margin in the world, and you're the only one who reads it."

Something happened to his face then — a loosening, a lightness, like a knot slipping. He laughed. Really laughed, short and startled out of him, and the fire flared in sympathy, and he winced and made a conscious effort to laugh more quietly, which somehow made it worse, and soon they were both laughing — hysterical, post-adrenaline, alive-laughing — while the snow came down gentle as flour outside their cave.

*Oh no,* Hazel thought, in the middle of it. *I like him.*

She filed the thought away in the place where she kept dangerous documents.

***

They learned each other's rhythms the way you learn anything in the mountains: because the alternative was dying.

She learned that his counting was different from hers — he counted in fives, always fives, she suspected from years of hive frames — and that if she matched her breathing to his fives instead of forcing her fours, the storm settled twice as fast. She learned that his magic ran ahead of his awareness by about three seconds, so the trick was watching his hands: when his fingers spread, wind; when they curled, pressure dropping; when he went still and his jaw tightened, lightning, get behind stone.

He learned that she hummed when she was frightened — an old song, wordless, the one their mother sang, though she didn't tell him that — and that the humming steadied him more than any exercise, because apparently a storm-caller's magic keyed to *other people's* calm even better than his own. He learned that she catalogued exits compulsively, that she slept facing the cave mouth, that she could sharpen any stick into a weapon in under a minute and had done so twice already without appearing to notice.

"You're always braced," he observed, on the second evening, watching her sit with her back to the rock and her knees drawn up like a fortress.

"I'm efficient."

"You sleep like a soldier awaiting cavalry."

"I *was* awaited by cavalry, as of four days ago."

"That's not what I meant." He turned his hands palm-up over the fire, studying them as though they belonged to a stranger — which, Hazel reflected, they sort of did, these days. "In the valley, I was braced too. Eleven years of bracing. You keep waiting for the thing you are to catch up with you." He glanced at her. "It catches up eventually. Usually on a mountain."

Hazel didn't answer. The fire popped. Outside, the snow fell in the deep silence of high places, and for once the silence didn't feel like the valley's null-hush or the court's dream-harvest. It just felt like snow.

"My brother hummed too," she said finally, because the silence had gotten honest and she hadn't the strength to make it lie. "Tam. Before. He'd hum when I was upset, badly, on purpose, so I'd have to laugh at him instead of crying." She pulled her knees tighter. "The court has his soul in a vault under the mountain, and I'm told the souls there keep doing what they loved. So somewhere down there, in the dark, he's probably still humming. Badly. On purpose."

Silas was quiet a long moment. Then, instead of pity — she'd have bitten through pity like wire — he said:

"When we get him back, I'd like to hear it. The bad humming. For comparison purposes. I've been told my own talents are wasted on bees."

Despite everything — the wet wool, the aching cold, the frost-riders surely combing the roads behind them — Hazel smiled. It felt strange on her face, underused, like a door opening into a room she'd sealed.

"You realize," she said, "that according to the prophecy book, you kneel before a king and the storm serves the mountain. Every step we take toward Tam is a step along a road someone else wrote."

"Then we'll walk it badly," Silas said. "Off the margins. I hear that's where the truth lives."

She threw a twig at his head. He caught it, grinned, and fed it to the fire, and his fingers curled slightly as it burned — and far above them, through miles of cloud, Hazel saw a single vein of lightning flicker, soft and brief, like a nod.

***

They descended the far side of the pass the next morning into a world already sealing itself for winter.

The first farmstead appeared at noon: doors painted with soul-sigils, windows shuttered with wax and ribbon, the whole house sleeping its harvested sleep behind symbols Hazel had *written variants of herself*, in a warm archive, in steady ink. She stood in the yard and felt the sigils look back at her.

Silas touched her shoulder. "Whose hand is that?"

She didn't answer. Because on the doorpost, beneath the sigil, was a collection ledger nailed up for the court's tally-men — and the column headings, the neat, upright, disciplined lettering, were hers. Copied, trained into a hundred junior scribes across the province. Her master's hand, passed down through hers, now nailed to a starving family's door like a warrant.

Behind them, far up the pass they'd just descended, something glinted white and moving. Riders. Many. Not following their trail — sweeping the heights, methodical as a search algorithm, closing the net one grid-square at a time.

"We need to get off the road," Hazel said. "Now. Is there anyone between here and the fens who'd shelter two fugitives?"

Silas's face did something complicated. "There's a ferrywoman," he said slowly. "Maren. One eye, worse temper, and a debt she owes my grandfather that's older than both of us." He looked back at the glinting heights, then at the sigiled door, then at Hazel — really looked, the way he was learning to. "She also owes a favor to a woman named Sandoval. Or she did. If that blood runs true, we might survive the week."

Hazel stared at him. "My mother's name was Sandoval."

"I know," said Silas Crane, and started walking. "Why do you think I took you in?"
