## 9. The Tollhouse of Small Mercies

Maren had one eye and eleven rules, and she'd tell you the eye was the more negotiable of the two.

She was mending a net on the tollhouse porch when they came down off the fen-causeway — two figures, one tall and moving like a man carrying something breakable inside his own ribs, one small and walking like the road owed her money. Maren had been watching the causeway since noon, because the gulls had gone quiet in that particular way gulls do before weather or trouble, and on the fens those were usually the same thing.

"Evening," she called, not looking up from her net. "Toll's three coppers, or a story, or labor. I don't take silver. Silver's how they find you."

The small one stopped dead. The tall one nearly walked into her back.

"You're joking," the small one said.

"Do I look like a woman who jokes? Look at this face." Maren pointed at it with her netting needle. It was, admittedly, not a face built for comedy — wind-scoured, sun-cured, the empty socket beneath her patch folded into permanent skepticism. "Forty years of ferrying fools across the Merrow Fen, and this is what the wind did to me. There's no humor left in it. Only tolls."

"We don't have coppers," the tall one said. His voice was low and careful, like he was used to being listened to and didn't enjoy it.

"Then you'll be wanting option two or option three."

The small one turned around slowly. She had mud to the knee, frost-burn on her cheeks, and eyes that went through Maren like an auditor through a ledger. "Maren," she said. "Of the tollhouse. On the Merrow crossing."

"That's me. Who's asking?"

"My mother," the girl said, "was Sefa Vane. Of Greyharbor. Before she married."

Maren put down the net.

Well. That was that, then. Forty years, and the debt came walking up her own causeway wearing a scribe's ink-stained fingers and a dead woman's stubborn chin. Maren had known it would come someday — debts like hers always did, they were patient as ice — but she'd hoped for at least another decade, long enough to forget the exact shape of what she owed.

"Sefa Vane," Maren said. "Hah." She stood, joints cracking like green kindling, and looked the girl over properly. Thin. Too thin. The kind of thin that came from running rather than fasting, which was worse, because runners forgot to eat out of guilt instead of poverty, and guilt was a much harder habit to feed. "You have her eyebrows. The ones that judge people. Sit down, both of you, before I decide to charge you for standing."

* * *

The tollhouse was one room and a loft, built on stilts over the fen's black water, and it smelled the way Maren liked her houses to smell: peat smoke, tar, fish, and the lavender bundles she hung against damp and despair respectively. She put them at her table — a plank on two barrels, polished by forty years of elbows — and fed them before she asked a single question, because that was rule four: *feed first, interrogate after; hungry people lie badly and eat worse.*

The tall one ate like a man who'd forgotten food existed and was rediscovering it with religious awe. The girl — Hazel, she'd said, Hazel Vane, and wasn't that a name to carry — ate fast and neat, watching the door between bites.

"Nobody's coming," Maren told her. "The fen doesn't let riders cross easy. Horses sink. Men sink faster. The only way onto my causeway is the way you came, and I'd hear them a mile off, complaining about the mud."

"They crossed the valley," Hazel said quietly. "Where nothing was supposed to be able to follow."

"Ah." Maren ladled more stew into the tall one's bowl without asking. "So it's started."

The tall one looked up. "You know about the valley?"

"I know about everything that happens within a day's walk of my door, and half of what happens beyond it, because travelers talk when they're warm and I listen when they're stupid." She sat down across from them with her own bowl. "Last week a tinker came through swearing the sky over Crane's Valley split open at midnight like a struck bell. Said lightning fell where no cloud was. Said the quiet there — the famous quiet, the magic-dead quiet — broke for a heartbeat and everyone's chickens laid eggs with double yolks for three days after."

"Is that true?" the tall one asked.

"The eggs? Aye. I bought six. Made a fine custard." She pointed her spoon at him. "The rest I believe too, because here you sit, storm-caller, smelling of ozone and terror, and there she sits, court-trained if I ever saw it, and neither of you will tell me your business, which means your business is bad."

Silence. Good silence, though — the thinking kind. Maren ate her stew and let it work.

It was the tall one who cracked, eventually, the way big honest men always do. "We're going to the mountain," he said. "To the under-court. To take something back."

Maren set down her spoon.

* * *

Here is what Maren knew about the under-court, and she knew it the way you know a dog that bit you: personally.

She'd gone down once herself, thirty-one years ago, young and furious and grieving, with a sister's soul-price burning a hole in her chest. She'd come back up with most of what she went for and less than she'd counted on. She still couldn't remember her wedding day. Not dimly, not hazily — *not at all*. There was a man she'd loved for nine years and she could recall his hands, his laugh, the way he sang off-key in the boat, but the day she married him was a smooth blank in her mind, like a stone worn featureless by water. He'd never blamed her. That was somehow the cruelest part.

"Rule seven," she said now, into the firelight. "No one goes down the mountain twice."

"We only need once," Hazel said.

"Everyone says that." Maren poked the fire harder than it deserved. "Listen to me, girl, because I knew your mother, and your mother would skin me alive if I let you walk into that place ignorant. The under-court runs on memory. Not gold — gold's for the surface world, for kings and merchants and other children. Down there, memory *is* coin. They buy it, sell it, spend it, burn it in their candles. A strong memory — a wedding, a birth, the taste of your mother's bread — that's worth a season's passage. A whole childhood, well-preserved, can buy you a title."

"I know," Hazel said. "I was a scribe there. I wrote the purchase contracts."

Maren stopped. Looked at her. Looked at the tall one, who was studying his stew with the expression of a man learning something he'd already feared.

"You were *court*," Maren said.

"I was property with good handwriting," Hazel said, and there was flint under it. "There's a difference, and the difference is exactly why we're going back."

"Hm." Maren chewed on that along with her turnip. "And you, giant. What are you going down for?"

"He's going for me," Hazel said, at the same moment the tall one said, "Her brother's soul."

They looked at each other. Something passed between them — quick, wordless, the kind of thing Maren had spent forty years observing in couples and almost never in strangers. Her empty eye ached the way it did when weather was coming, or truth.

"A soul," Maren said. "From the vaults."

"Yes."

"And you know what the vaults cost to enter?"

"No," Silas said. "Tell us."

* * *

Maren got out the bottle then. Plum brandy, her own making, fierce enough to strip barnacles. She poured three fingers each and made them drink before she talked, because some talks go better with a burn in the throat to match the one in the chest.

"When I went down," she said, "I paid my passage in memories. That's the standard toll — you pay as you descend, gate by gate, and the wardens choose what they take. You don't get to pick. That's the first cruelty. They took my wedding day, my father's face, and the name of the village I was born in. I found the village later, on a map. Stood outside it. Felt nothing. It was like reading about a stranger's home." She turned her cup slowly. "The second cruelty is what comes back *with* you. Or rather, what doesn't. Everyone who descends comes back missing something. Always. Even the ones who pay full price and steal nothing. The mountain takes a tithe on exit — a memory, a year of your life, the ability to cry, once a woman came back unable to taste anything but salt. Nobody knows how it chooses. The court calls it 'administrative fees.'"

"That's cheerful," Silas said faintly.

"I'm not done. Third cruelty." She leaned forward, and even the fire seemed to lean in. "The memories they take don't die. They're *used*. Rendered into light, burned in the halls, woven into dreams for the sleeping court. Somewhere under that mountain, right now, my wedding day is burning in a sconce, keeping a warden's corridor warm. And sometimes — this is the part that keeps me up nights, so I share it freely — sometimes the memories remember their owners. Sailors on the fen say that on still nights you can hear the mountain humming songs nobody taught it. Songs from up here. From lives it swallowed."

Hazel had gone very white. "Tam hummed," she said. "When he worked. All the time. Drove our mother mad."

"Then you know what to listen for," Maren said gently. "That's worth more than any map."

* * *

Later, when the tall one — Silas, though he'd given the name reluctantly, like handing someone a knife handle-first — had climbed to the loft and started snoring with the deep embarrassment of a large man asleep among strangers, Hazel stayed at the table, turning her cup.

"Say what you're going to say," Maren told her. "You've been winding up to it since the stew."

"How much do you know about my mother?"

"Enough. We ran cargo together, before her knees and my eye went. She pulled me out of the Merrow with a boat-hook when the fen tried to keep me — I was twenty-two and certain I could outrun a flood tide, which tells you everything about being twenty-two." Maren smiled despite herself. "She laughed at me for ten minutes. Then she made me promise that if anyone of hers ever came down my causeway needing help, I'd give it free and ask questions never. So." She spread her hands. "Ask your questions never. I'm giving it free."

Hazel's jaw worked. Whatever she'd rehearsed was clearly not surviving contact.

"She sold things," Hazel finally said. "Near the end. The bad winter. Memories, trinkets, anything the peddlers would carry south. Did she ever... did she ever talk about what it cost?"

Ah. There it was. Maren set down her cup and gave the girl the whole weight of her one good eye.

"Your mother sold a memory to a passing trader once — a little one, a summer afternoon, she said it barely mattered. And afterward she sat on my porch and cried until the candles drowned, because she couldn't remember why she was crying, only that she'd lost something and couldn't even mourn it properly." Maren kept her voice level. "She made me promise two things that night. One: never sell to the traders, no matter the winter. Two: if I ever broke promise one, I should write down what I sold *before* I sold it, so at least I'd know the shape of the hole." She tapped the table. "There's a book in that chest behind you. Every memory I've got, written out, dated. Thirty-one years of entries. Because the mountain isn't the only thing that eats a life piece by piece, girl. Poverty does it too. Grief does it. The difference is the mountain gives you a receipt."

Hazel was crying now, silently, the way scribes cry — efficiently, without smudging anything important.

"And you're telling me this because—"

"Because you're going to trade with them. Don't insult me, I can see it in your ledger-keeping face. You've already priced yourself out." Maren reached across and gripped the girl's wrist, hard, the way you grip someone on a slippery deck. "So hear the last of it. When they offer you a price down there — and they will, they're *reasonable*, that's the horror of them — you check it against the book. You ask yourself: is this memory worth what I'm buying? And you remember that the mountain always rounds *up*. In its favor. Every time. Your mother knew it. Now you know it."

Hazel wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, and nodded, and Maren pretended not to see, because that was mercy too.

* * *

The storm woke near midnight.

Maren heard it before she understood it — a pressure change, a wrongness in the fen-water, the reeds all leaning east at once. She was up with her boat-hook before her boots were on, and by the time she reached the main room, the windows were flickering blue and the tall one was standing in the middle of the floor with his arms wrapped around himself like a man holding his own coat shut against a gale.

Lightning crawled under his skin. Actual lightning — Maren had seen storm-work before, from a distance, from the safe side of history, and this was that, untrained and leaking.

"It's the fen," he gasped. "The water — it remembers storms, it's *calling*—"

"Maren," Hazel said sharply, awake and armed with nothing but a candlestick, "what do I do?"

"Nothing! Both of you, nothing!" Maren planted herself in front of Silas, boat-hook and all, one eye blazing. "Boy. Look at me. Just me. What did you do, back in your valley, when it wanted out?"

"I — there was nothing to do, the valley was quiet—"

"So you've never held it. Never once." She clicked her tongue. "Right. New lesson, and I'm charging you for it later. Rule eight: you don't fight weather, you *give it a job*. Storms are like sailors — idle hands, mutiny. Find it work." She grabbed his wrists and pressed his palms flat against the window frame, old salt-hard wood, tarred and nailed and *useful*. "Feel that? Solid. Boring. Utterly indifferent to you. Give the lightning somewhere dull to go. Ground it in the ordinary. Think of — I don't know — mending nets. Counting knots. The most tedious thing you know."

His breath hitched. The blue under his skin stuttered, flickered — and then, slowly, like a cat deciding the lap would do, it settled. Outside, the fen-water flattened. The reeds straightened up, embarrassed.

"There," Maren said, stepping back. "Now you owe me for a lesson *and* a window frame."

"I didn't break the window frame."

"You were about to. Intent counts. Everything counts, down the mountain especially." She looked at him a long moment — this big frightened gentle man with a weapon in his blood and no idea how to hold it — and felt the old ache start up behind her patch, the one that meant she was about to care about somebody again, which at her age was practically a diagnosis. "Get some sleep, storm-caller. Tomorrow I'm taking you across the Merrow myself, as far as the Old Ford. After that you're on your own, and the fen gets honest, and the mountain gets close."

* * *

Dawn came up gray and cold over the water, and Maren stood on her dock checking the barge lines while the two of them packed — the girl folding everything into precise squares, the boy clutching an iron bell like a talisman, which, fair enough, it probably was.

"One more thing," Maren said, not looking up from the knots. "Before you go down. Each of you, pick one memory. Your best one. The one you'd refuse to sell at any price. And hold onto it like a rope in a riptide. When they come counting — and they count *everything* — you press that one down deep and you think of my ugly face saying *not this one*. Understand? The mountain takes what you surrender easiest. So surrender nothing easily."

"And what was yours?" Hazel asked. "The one you kept?"

Maren tested a knot. Pulled it tight.

"The laugh," she said. "My sister's laugh. They offered me triple for it — said laughter rendered bright, burns longer than grief. I told them no. First and last time I ever told that court no." She finally looked up, and grinned with all her ruined face. "Still got it. Want to hear?"

She laughed then — a great bark of a sound, rusty and real — and after a startled second, Hazel laughed too, and then the boy, and the three of them stood on a rotten dock in the freezing dawn laughing like fools, which Maren considered excellent tactical preparation for the hell they were sailing toward.

She pushed off from the dock and poled the barge out into the gray water.

"Rule eleven," she called back over her shoulder. "Come back *missing nothing*. I'll be checking."

Behind her, on the far shore, the mountain waited — patient as a debt, humming a song it had stolen from someone's mother.

Maren rowed toward it anyway. Some promises you keep with your whole face, both eyes or one.
