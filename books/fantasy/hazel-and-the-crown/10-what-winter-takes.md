## 10. What Winter Takes

The fens began on the third morning out of Maren's ferry-house, and they began the way most bad things begin: gradually, and then all at once.

First the trees thinned. Then the ground went soft, then spongy, then frankly treacherous, so that Silas walked ahead testing each step with his grandfather's iron bell like a man probing a soup for bones. Then the reeds came, tall as two men, whispering in a wind that never seemed to touch anything else. And then the cold arrived — not the ordinary cold of late autumn, which Hazel had grown up with and could respect, but a cold with *intent*, a cold that pressed against her cheeks like fingers checking whether she was ripe.

"The court's weather," Hazel said, pulling her scarf up over her nose.

"You can tell whose it is?"

"Everything has handwriting, Silas. Weather included." She nodded at the frost creeping along the reed-tops in long, orderly filaments, each one identical to its neighbor, each one placed with the precision of a ruled line. "See? That's court-frost. It doesn't drift. It *files* itself."

Silas looked at the frost for a long moment. "That's the most unsettling thing you've ever said, and last week you told me your old master used to sign his executions with a flourish."

"He did. Left-facing loop. Very elegant." Hazel stepped over a pool of standing water that was already skinning over with ice. "You learn to read a lot of hands when you're a scribe. Mine included." She said it lightly, the way she said most true things — tossed out sideways, at nothing, hoping it would land somewhere far away from anyone who might pick it up.

Silas, to his credit, didn't pick it up. He just grunted and kept walking, bell swinging, and Hazel loved him a little for that, in the way you love someone for not asking questions you're not ready to answer.

They were three days into the fens when they found the first village.

* * *

It sat on a low rise where the causeway forked — a dozen stone houses with turf roofs, a well, a shrine to some small local god whose name had been worn off the post by weather. Smoke should have been rising from the chimneys. Lamplight should have been showing in the windows. It was the hour when villages are loudest, supper-time, dogs and children and somebody shouting about somebody else's goat.

The village was silent.

Not empty-silent. Not abandoned-silent. Hazel had seen abandoned villages before; they have a particular quality, an untended quality, weeds in the doorways and shutters hanging crooked. This village was *tended*. The paths were swept. Firewood was stacked under eaves in neat cords. A child's wooden horse stood upright in a yard, positioned carefully beside the door as though set down just a moment ago.

And every single door was painted.

Hazel stopped walking so abruptly that Silas nearly trod on her heels. She knew those marks. Of course she knew them. She had *drawn* them, or ones exactly like them, hundreds of times, in the scriptorium beneath the mountain, her pen moving through the motions while her mind wandered anywhere else it could reach. Soul-sigils: interlocking circles crossed with the vertical line of harvest, the little hooks at the terminals that meant *collected*, the fine inner spirals that meant *dreaming*.

"They're asleep," she said. Her voice came out strange, flattened, like sound heard through a wall. "All of them. They've sealed themselves in for the long winter."

"Sealed themselves how? Those marks—"

"Are contracts." She was walking now, fast, toward the nearest door, and Silas followed because he always did, though she could feel him wanting to grab her sleeve. "Household contracts. Standard form. The family signs, the sigil is painted, and the court takes their dreams for the season. In exchange—" she recited it in the flat cadence of boilerplate, the words worn smooth from repetition, "—the household receives warmth without fuel, food without labor, and freedom from want until spring thaw. All debts payable upon waking."

She put her hand on the door.

It wasn't locked. It swung open at her touch, gently, obediently, and warm air breathed out at them smelling of bread and tallow and sleep.

Inside, a family lay in their beds. Grandmother by the hearth, mother and father in the big bed, two children tucked together in a trundle like spoons in a drawer. Their chests rose and fell. Their faces were peaceful. Above each of them, faint as breath on glass, hung a thin ribbon of light — silver-grey, shot through with color when it moved — rising from their foreheads and passing up through the ceiling, through the roof, into the darkening sky.

Harvest-lines. Hazel had read about them in ledgers. She had never seen one.

"They're dreaming," she whispered. "Right now. Whatever they're dreaming, it's going up there, and something underneath the mountain is drinking it."

Silas stood in the doorway behind her, very still. When he spoke, his voice was careful, the way you speak near a sleeping child. "Is it hurting them?"

"No." The word came out before she could stop it, automatic, reflexive — the scribe's answer, the company line. "That's the whole point of the contract. It's painless. It's *kind*. They wake in spring rested, fed, having lost nothing but—"

"But what?"

She looked at the children in the trundle. One of them, a girl of perhaps six, smiled in her sleep. Her harvest-line pulsed gently, and for half a heartbeat Hazel saw color move through it — gold, and green, and a bright specific red that could only be the memory of berries picked on a summer day.

"—but everything," Hazel finished quietly. "Every dream they'd have had. Every dream they *did* have. The court takes the dreams they've already dreamed too, Silas. The memories. That's the currency. You don't lose sleep. You lose the summers. You lose your mother's voice. You lose the taste of bread from your own oven, and come spring you'll eat it and it'll be like the first time, and everyone will say isn't it wonderful, and no one will remember why it used to mean more."

The grandmother by the hearth sighed in her sleep. The harvest-line above her was thinner than the others. Fainter. Hazel had seen figures like that in the ledgers too, in columns marked *residual yield, aged households* — and she had copied those columns faithfully, in her own neat hand, and had never once let herself think about what the numbers were made of.

She backed out of the house and pulled the door shut behind her, softly, as if closing it on a sickroom.

* * *

They found the collection ledger in the shrine.

It sat on the altar where the nameless god's offering bowl should have been — a fat volume bound in grey leather, chained to a stand, its pages protected from damp by a cantrip Hazel knew the making-words for. Beside it lay a quill and an inkpot, frozen solid, waiting for whoever collected here next.

"It's a waystation," she said. "Riders come through, update the accounts, move on. The shrine god gets evicted for the season. Very tidy. Everything about the court is tidy."

Silas watched her open the book with the expression of a man watching someone poke a sleeping bear. "Do we need to read it?"

"No."

"Do you *want* to?"

She wanted to burn it. She wanted to walk away and never think about the fens again. And she opened it anyway, because guilt is a kind of appetite, and hers had been starving for years.

The ledger was beautiful. That was the worst part. Court script-work was beautiful the way a blade is beautiful, and this was masterwork — columns flowing in perfect parallel, sigils rendered with a precision that made her own hand look like a child's scrawl, marginal annotations in the tight, dry shorthand of the Scribe Prime's office.

She turned pages. Households entered, yields assessed, contracts renewed or lapsed. Village after fen-village, sealed and sleeping and drunk from. The numbers marched past her, and she read them the way she'd been trained to read them, fluently, effortlessly, and then—

Then she reached a page dated four winters past, and the hand changed.

She knew that hand. She had practiced that hand for six years, copying it under her master's eye, being corrected for letting her flourishes get lazy. She knew the slight forward lean of it, the way the ascenders hooked left, the distinctive double-cross on the letter that meant *soul, contracted*.

It was her own handwriting.

Not similar. Not close. *Her own.* Four winters ago, in the scriptorium, pen in her fist, Tam already gone below, her belly full of the bread the court paid her with — she had filled page after page exactly like this one. Household by household. Village by village. She had thought of it as copying. She had told herself it was copying. But the ledger didn't lie about what it was, and neither, anymore, could she.

"Hazel?" Silas said.

"I wrote this." Her finger rested on the entry without touching it. *Fenhold Rise. Twelve households. Yield: good. Renewal recommended.* Her signature sat beneath it, small and neat and complete. "I mean — I wrote dozens of these. Hundreds. I told myself I was just a copyist, that the words weren't mine, that I was a pen someone else was holding. But it's my hand, Silas. It was always my hand. Nobody held the pen for me."

Silas was quiet for a moment. Outside, the wind moved through the reeds with its long whispering sigh, and somewhere overhead a harvest-line of light climbed invisibly toward the mountain.

"What happens to these people in spring?" he asked.

"They wake up. Fed. Warm. Grateful, probably." She closed the ledger, and her hand lingered on the cover. "That's the trap of it. That's why nobody fights. The court doesn't take things *from* you. It makes a bargain so comfortable you thank it. My family signed because we were starving, and I signed because I was clever enough to get a better rate, and I have spent every winter since telling myself that cleverness was the same thing as innocence."

"And is it?"

"No." She laughed, and it came out cracked down the middle. "No, it really isn't."

Silas took the ledger out of her hands — gently, the way he handled everything, bees and books and frightened runaway scribes — and set it back on the altar. Then he did something she didn't expect. He took the frozen inkpot and, with the edge of his palm, broke it cleanly in two against the altar stone.

"That won't stop them," she said. "They'll bring more ink."

"I know." He brushed the shards aside. "But it'll slow down whoever comes next, and right now slowing them down is the only weapon we've got, and I'd rather use a small one than none." He glanced at her, and his eyes were kind and tired and entirely without judgment, which was somehow harder to bear than judgment would have been. "You sold your brother's soul to keep your family alive through a winter. I hid in a hole for ten years pretending I wasn't what I am. Neither of us gets to throw stones. Come on. We should find shelter before full dark."

"Silas." He paused at the shrine door. "Thank you."

"For breaking an inkpot?"

"For not looking at me like I'm what's written in that book."

He considered this. "Hazel, I've spent ten years avoiding mirrors. I'm the last person alive qualified to look at anybody like that."

* * *

They sheltered that night in the shrine's back room, under the nameless god's faded gaze, and Hazel slept badly and dreamed — actually dreamed, her own dreams still her own, and she clung to that fact like a rope over a drop.

Near dawn, a sound woke her: hooves. Many hooves, moving at speed along the causeway, accompanied by the particular crystalline ringing of court-harness.

She was on her feet with her hand over Silas's mouth before he finished inhaling. Through the shutter-crack they watched them pass: eight frost-riders in their pale liveries, breath streaming, spears topped with banners of woven ice. Not searching. Not hunting. *Collecting.* They drew rein at the shrine, and one dismounted, stamped snow from his boots with the weary familiarity of a tradesman, and pushed open the door.

A moment later his voice drifted out, mild and irritated:

"Somebody's broken the inkpot again."

His companion snorted. "Third village this month. Vandals, probably. Or wights." There was a pause, the sound of a satchel opening, fresh ink being uncorked. "Doesn't matter. Ledger's due at the mountain by first deep-frost. Write it up."

Hazel felt Silas tense beside her, felt the air pressure shift the way it did when his brand woke — the storm under his skin leaning toward the riders like a dog toward the door. She pressed his wrist, once. *Not yet. Not here. Not over an inkpot.*

The riders mounted and rode on eastward, toward the mountain, their banners crackling, and the hoofbeats faded into the whisper of the reeds.

Hazel let out her breath slowly. "They're heading the same way we are."

"I noticed."

"And they'll reach the gates before winter seals them. Which means the court will know exactly where we are, and roughly when we arrive, and—" She stopped. Something was nagging at her, some detail from the ledger, some figure that hadn't fit. She closed her eyes and let it surface.

The renewal dates. Every contract in that ledger renewed for the same term — not one winter, not five, but *this* winter specifically, flagged in the margin in the Scribe Prime's shorthand. And she knew that shorthand mark. Everyone in the scriptorium knew it. It appeared only once a generation, on documents of the highest priority.

It meant *harvest-year*.

"Silas," she said slowly, "the court isn't collecting the usual tithe this winter. Every household in the fens has been renewed for a full harvest. Everything they have. Every dream, every memory, down to the floorboards of their childhoods." She met his eyes in the grey pre-dawn light. "This is the winter the passes seal forever. They're not stocking the larder. They're stripping the land bare before they lock the doors."

Outside, the first true snow of the long winter began to fall — thick, silent, and patient, filing itself neatly to the ground.
