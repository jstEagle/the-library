## 12. Riders on the White Road

The causeway appeared out of the fens like a promise: a long white road of packed chalk and old shell, raised above the black water, running straight toward the distant smudge of Greyharbor. After three days of slogging through reeds and sucking mud, it looked like mercy.

"It's too straight," Hazel said.

"It's a *road*," Silas said. "Roads are straight. That's rather the point of them."

"Roads built by fen-folk curve. They follow the dry ground. This one was built by people who didn't care what the water thought." She stood at the causeway's edge, boots muddy to the knee, and looked down its whole pale length with the flat expression she wore when she was doing arithmetic she didn't like. "People who don't care what the water thinks are people who don't expect to be here long."

Silas wanted to argue. He had gotten reasonably good at arguing with Hazel over the past week — it was one of the few activities that made him feel like himself, or at least like someone adjacent to himself. But his left palm had been itching since dawn, deep under the skin, in the place where the brand lived when it wasn't flaring, and he had learned that the itch was usually right about things.

"Fine," he said. "We go around."

"We can't go around. The fens widen past this point. Maren said so." Hazel sighed and stepped up onto the chalk. "We go fast, then. Eyes up. If you see frost on the shells — frost in *autumn* — you say so immediately."

They walked. The causeway rang faintly under their boots, a sound like a struck bell buried too deep to hear properly. On either side the water lay perfectly still, mirroring the grey sky so completely that Silas felt they were walking along the seam of the world, balanced between two identical greynesses. It should have been beautiful. Mostly it was unnerving.

"You know," he said, mostly to fill the silence, "when I imagined leaving the valley — which I did imagine, occasionally, usually while mucking out the bees — I pictured more inns. Inns with stew. I feel the literature of adventure has misled me badly."

"The literature of adventure is written by survivors," Hazel said. "Survivors skip the mud."

"And the fear?"

"Especially the fear." She glanced sideways at him. Something in her face softened by a degree, which for Hazel was practically an embrace. "Your hands are shaking."

"They're not."

"They've been shaking since the tollhouse. You hold them still when you think I'm looking, and then they start again." She stopped walking. The causeway stretched ahead of them, empty and bright and wrong. "Silas. When the riders come—"

"*If* they come—"

"When," she repeated, with the patience of someone who had catalogued every lie told in three kingdoms, "they come. What will you do?"

He looked at his palms. Turned them over, as if the answer might be written there. "I don't know," he said honestly. "That's the truth of it. I spent eleven years learning how to be a man who doesn't do anything. The storm answers me now — it comes when I'm frightened, it comes when I'm angry — but I don't know how to aim it at a person. I don't know if I can."

Hazel was quiet for a moment. Then she reached into her coat and drew out the little knife she carried, the scribe's knife, the one she used for cutting quills and trimming pages. She held it out to him, handle first.

"What's this?"

"A better answer than lightning." Her voice was very even. "A storm doesn't care who it hits. A blade does. If it comes to killing, I'd rather it be done small and precise than large and sorry."

"I'm not taking your knife."

"You're not taking anything. I'm lending it. There's a difference, and the difference is that I intend to get it back." She pressed it into his hand and closed his fingers over it. Her hands were cold. They were always cold; he had noticed it the first night and never mentioned it. "And if you won't use it, then keep it anyway, because holding something sharp makes people braver, and brave is going to matter more than strong today."

The itch in his palm flared — and then he realized it wasn't the itch at all. It was the brand, waking, and the air above the water had gone strange and glittering, the way air goes before snow.

"Frost," he said quietly. "On the shells. All along the edge."

Hazel turned. Far behind them, at the causeway's southern end, the mist had begun to move against the wind.

* * *

There were six of them, maybe seven — it was hard to count shapes made of horse-steam and hoarfrost, riders whose livery was the blue-white of deep ice and whose faces were the smooth blankness of a frozen window. Their horses made no sound on the chalk. That was the worst part, Silas thought later. Six riders bearing down at a gallop, and not one hoofbeat.

"Run," Hazel said. "Don't fight, don't talk, don't be clever. Run for the far end. There'll be hills past the causeway — hollow ones, Maren said, the old barrow-kind. Wights don't let riders in hollow hills. Even the court's riders."

"How do you know that?"

"Because I filed the treaty that says so." She was already running. "I *wrote* the treaty!"

Silas ran. The causeway rang beneath them, louder now, a real bell-sound, and he understood with a sick lurch that the road itself was warning whatever lived nearby — that the white road had been built to sing, and it sang only one song.

The riders gained. Of course they gained. Horses of frost do not tire.

Twenty lengths. Fifteen. Silas could hear them now after all — not hoofbeats but a sound like breath drawn through a keyhole, thin and whistling, six mouths breathing winter. His brand burned. The sky overhead began to churn, clouds knotting themselves into a fist, and he felt the storm lean down toward him like a dog hearing its name, eager, enormous, *ready*—

*No,* he thought. *Not here. Not with Hazel beside me. Not where I can't choose what it touches.*

He stumbled. Half a step, no more, but on a causeway half a step is the whole world. His boot skidded on frost-slicked chalk — frost spreading outward from the nearest rider like spilled milk — and he went down hard on his left hand, the knife flying from his grip, the chalk biting through skin.

And the rider was there.

It swung down off its horse without dismounting, somehow both, and reached for him with a hand like a glove full of needles. Silas threw up his arm. Lightning came — he felt it come, felt it gather in his shoulder and scream down his arm, all his terror given a voice at last —

—and died two feet from the rider's chest, swallowed, eaten, gone, as if the frost were a mouth.

The rider paused. Tilted its smooth head. And then, deliberately, it pressed one needle-finger into the raw scrape on Silas's palm.

The pain was brief and total. Three drops of blood welled up, black-red against the white chalk — and the rider touched each one, and each drop rose from the stone like a bead on a string and vanished into the frost of its palm.

Then the world went white and sideways. Thunder without lightning cracked the sky. The causeway heaved — actually *heaved*, like the back of a waking animal — and Silas, blind with pain and fury and humiliation, felt the storm tear loose of him entirely, unaimed, unasked, a whipcrack of pressure that flattened the reeds for a hundred yards in every direction and hurled the nearest rider from its horse into the fen water, where it sank steaming.

"Silas!" Hazel's hand closed on his collar. "Up! *Up!*"

He came up running. The remaining riders were regrouping, their formation broken, their horses screaming steam — and one of them was down, and not getting up, because Hazel had stopped, turned, and put her scribe's knife — snatched from the chalk where it had fallen — into the gap beneath the second rider's helm, the place where a jaw would be if frost had jaws.

She had done it the way she did everything: precisely, quickly, without wasted motion.

Then she saw what she had done, and her legs stopped working.

* * *

Later — much later, huddled in the dark of a hollow hill with Odd the grave-wight chattering somewhere near the entrance — Silas would understand what those thirty seconds had cost her. But in the moment there was only this: Hazel standing over a dissolving shape of frost and empty livery, the knife dripping nothing at all, her face absolutely white, her breath coming in the short shallow pulls of someone drowning on dry land.

"Hazel." He touched her elbow. She flinched hard enough to hurt them both. "Hazel, we have to move."

"I killed—" Her voice came out tiny. She cleared her throat and tried again, reaching for the court-scribe tone, the competent voice, the armor. "I killed a man. Or. Whatever that was. Was that a man? Underneath, was there a man?"

"I don't know."

"That's worse." She laughed, one broken note. "If it was a man, then I killed a man. If it wasn't, then I don't even know what I am now. Scribes aren't — we're not supposed to touch the ledger and the knife both. There are *rules*. I wrote half of them." Her knees buckled.

Silas caught her. For a moment she let herself be caught — forehead pressed against his shoulder, fists knotted in his coat, shaking like the last leaf of autumn. The remaining riders wheeled at the far end of the causeway, regrouping, but the storm had torn a stretch of the chalk away behind them, and the white road now ended in black water, and the horses of frost would not cross the gap. Small mercies. Ugly ones.

"There," Silas said, pointing. Fifty yards off the causeway, a hill rose out of the fens — round-topped, green, with a dark doorway in its side like a pupil. A hollow hill. "Maren's barrow-kind. Your treaty. Can you walk?"

"Yes." She pushed off his shoulder and stood, wiping her face with the heel of her hand, briskly, as though her face belonged to someone else. "Yes. Don't look at me."

"I have to look at you. You're my—"

"Don't." Sharper than she meant it; he heard her hear it. "Not yet. Later you can look at me. Right now just walk."

They walked. Behind them the riders paced the broken edge of the causeway, patient as winter, and one of them raised its needle hand — the one that held his blood — in something that was either a salute or a promise.

* * *

The inside of the hollow hill was warm, which Silas had not expected, and lit, which he had expected less. Torches of bog-oak burned in iron brackets along a passage lined with names — thousands of them, carved in scripts dead and living, climbing the walls like ivy.

"Tally of visitors," said a voice like a drawer full of spoons. "Everyone who sleeps here gets carved. House rule. Very old house."

Odd the grave-wight unfolded from an alcove — tall, gaunt, courteous, with eyes like coins at the bottom of a well and an expression of tremendous, barely contained delight at having guests.

"Two live ones!" Odd clasped their long hands. "Oh, it's been *ages*. Sit, sit. Tea — well, hot water with things in it, the things being a mystery even to me. You look terrible. Both of you. Especially you, madam, if you don't mind my saying, you look like someone who has recently done violence for the first time, which is a specific look, I've seen it perhaps four hundred times, and it never improves."

Hazel sat down abruptly on a stone bench, as if her strings had been cut again.

"She's right," Silas said quietly. "About the look."

"Mm. And you, sir, are bleeding on my floor, which is fine, blood is traditional, but may I ask — and forgive the directness, I collect gossip the way other folk collect buttons — why the mountain's frost-riders took your blood instead of your head? Because they don't do that. They take heads. Blood-taking is *ritual*, and ritual means orders, and orders mean someone upstairs knows your name."

Silas looked at his palm. Three small punctures, already closing, already scabbing — but the skin around them was cold, faintly, persistently cold, like a coin kept too long in a pocket.

"They got what they came for," he said.

"Mm," said Odd again, and poured the mysterious tea, and smiled with far too many teeth. "Well. Stories, then. House rule number two: everyone who sleeps in my hill tells one true story a night. Payment in kind. And I think, friends, I think tonight's stories had better begin with *why the mountain wants you* — because from where I stand, watching my doorway, those riders haven't left. They're sitting out there in the fog like cats at a mousehole. And cats," Odd added cheerfully, settling onto a bench across from them, folding their long hands, "can wait a very long time. So. Who's first?"
