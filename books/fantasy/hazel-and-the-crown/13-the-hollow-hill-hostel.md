## 13. The Hollow Hill Hostel

Odd had been dead for four hundred years, give or take a decade, and in all that time the best thing that had ever happened to him was a pair of thieves falling into his barrow.

He heard them coming long before they arrived — which was not difficult, as grave-wights hear everything within a hill's radius, and these two were loud in the particular way of people trying very hard to be quiet. The man's boots squelched. The woman's breathing had the ragged, hitching rhythm of someone who had recently done something terrible and was still carrying it around like a hot coal. And beneath both of them, faint but unmistakable, hummed the smell of storm.

Odd loved storms. He'd been buried under one, back when he was alive and foolish and standing on the wrong side of a lightning-struck oak. It was, he often told his bones (who were poor conversationalists), the most interesting thing that had ever happened to him until now.

They came down through the hill's mouth at dusk — the little door in the turf that looked like nothing, the one Odd kept disguised with a growth of brambles he'd spent decades training to look uninviting. The man went first, sword drawn, which was sweet. The woman followed with a satchel clutched to her chest and blood on her sleeve that Odd sincerely hoped wasn't hers.

"Oh, *finally*," Odd said, from the dark just past the entrance. "Do you know how long I've been waiting for visitors? Don't answer. Rhetorical. Also, mind the third step, it's my cousin."

The woman screamed. The man raised his sword higher, which was sweeter still.

"Put that down," Odd said, shuffling forward into the glow of their little lantern. He knew what he looked like: a lean gray figure wrapped in grave-clothes gone soft as old linen, eyes like coins left too long in a fountain, a smile with rather more teeth than strictly necessary. He'd had four centuries to practice looking harmless, and he'd gotten quite good at it, mostly by talking. "I'm a wight, yes, a barrow-wight specifically, this is my barrow, and before you ask — no, I don't want your bones. Well. Not *yours* particularly. Yours are all knobbly." He gestured at the man with a finger like a birch twig. "No offense."

"None taken," said the man, who did not lower his sword.

"Some taken, actually, but we'll work through it. Names! I'm Odd. Yes, really. My mother had a sense of humor and my father had a sense of doom, and between them they made me. You're the storm-caller and the thief, you've been walking since dawn, you fought frost-riders on the causeway — don't look surprised, the whole hill heard it, hills hear *everything*, it's our one hobby — and you're bleeding, and you're shaking, and you're both about three hours from collapse. So." He spread his arms, grave-clothes billowing dramatically. "Welcome to the Hollow Hill Hostel! Rates negotiable. Payment accepted in stories."

The woman found her voice first. She had a scribe's voice, clipped and careful even when frightened. "Stories?"

"True ones. One each, every night you stay. That's the price. No coin — what would I spend it on? No souls — had mine appraised centuries ago, the market's flooded. Stories. Fresh ones. Do you have any idea how boring eternity is? I've memorized the roots. All of them. There are forty-one major ones and they all go down."

Silence. The two thieves looked at each other in the lantern light, having one of those entire conversations people have with only eyebrows. Odd waited. Waiting was another thing he was excellent at.

"The third step is your cousin?" the man said finally.

"Adopted. Long story. Which, incidentally, will cost you."

* * *

They stayed. Of course they stayed. Outside, the snow was coming down thick enough to write on, and the frost-riders were combing the white road for a man whose palm had given up a few drops of blood to their scrying, and there was nowhere else in the world warm.

That was the other thing about the barrow, the thing Odd never mentioned upfront because it sounded like boasting: it was *warm*. Old warmth, deep warmth, the kind that seeped up from somewhere below the bones of the world. The court beneath the mountain hated it. Couldn't touch it. A hollow hill was one of the last places in the north where the mountain's long winter simply didn't reach, and Odd guarded his barrow the way a dragon guards gold — jealously, and mostly out of habit.

He fed them, too. Barrow-wights keep larders; it's a point of pride. There was honey-bread from a trade with a hedge-witch sixty years back (still good — time moved strangely in the hill), dried apples, a hard cheese that had achieved a kind of philosophical maturity. The storm-caller ate like a man who'd forgotten food was allowed. The scribe ate slowly, watching Odd over every bite, cataloguing him. He could see her doing it. He approved. Scribes were the best kind of guests — they noticed things, and noticing things meant they'd eventually *say* things, and saying things was the raw material of story.

"So," Odd said, settling cross-legged in the air an inch above the floor, because sitting on furniture was for the living and also his knees had been dust since the reign of a king nobody remembered. "Rules of the house. One: the inner chamber is mine. You may look at the door. Admiring the door is encouraged. Opening the door is discouraged in terms I'll elaborate on if you try. Two: the warmth stays. Don't ask me how it works, I don't know, I found it here and I'm not giving it back. Three—" he leaned in, coin-eyes gleaming, "—stories at sundown. True ones. I'll know if you lie. Lying has a flavor."

"What flavor?" asked the storm-caller, Silas. He had a voice like a door that hadn't been opened in years.

"Like copper and wet wool. Terrible. You'll want to avoid it." Odd tilted his head. "You first tomorrow. I already know your outline — storm-caller, exile, valley, bees, book of prophecies, dreadfully dramatic — but outlines aren't stories. Tomorrow night, I want the middle bits. The parts you'd skip."

"Why?" Hazel asked. Always the direct questions, this one. Odd liked her enormously already.

"Because I'm going to guide you into the under-court," Odd said, and enjoyed the way both of them went still, "through the back ways, the servant passages, the routes the court doesn't watch because the court forgot they exist — and I can do that because I died before the court finished building its walls and I remember where the doors used to be. But guiding is a contract, and contracts need consideration, and my price is that by the time we arrive, I'll know exactly who I'm guiding and why. Wights don't walk into that mountain blind. The last wight who tried came back as a hat rack. Very stylish. Deeply undignified."

"You know the way in," Hazel said. Not a question. Her fingers had gone tight on her satchel — the one with the key inside it, the key to the soul-vaults, though she hadn't said so aloud yet and Odd was politely pretending the hill hadn't told him. Hills tell you everything. It's exhausting, honestly, being this well-informed.

"I know the way in," Odd agreed. "And the way through. And eleven ways out, of which seven still exist. Whether you come back out is between you, your planning, and whatever gods you insult along the way. But I'll get you to the doors." He smiled his too-many-teeth smile. "For stories."

"That's it?" Silas said. "Stories? That's your price?"

"Silas." Hazel's voice had gone flat and careful. "Nothing costs only stories. Ask what he's not telling us."

Odd pressed a hand to his chest, wounded. "She suspects me. Four hundred years dead and *still* suspected. Fine. Fine! The truth, since you're both so grim about it: stories are how I stay. A wight who stops being remembered becomes a rumor, and a rumor who stops being told becomes nothing. Every true story you give me, I keep. I'm a hostel and I'm a ledger and I'm — well. I'm the last person alive who remembers half the people in these hills, and when you two are done with your grand tragedy and gone wherever grand tragedies go, someone ought to remember you properly. That's the whole price. Memory. It's not much." He picked at a thread on his grave-clothes. "It's everything."

The fire cracked. Somewhere far above, snow hissed against the turf roof.

"One story a night," Hazel said at last. "True ones. Starting tomorrow."

"Starting tonight," Odd said. "You've been here an hour and I'm already starving."

* * *

Hazel went first, because Hazel always went first — Odd could see it in her, the oldest kind of guilt, the kind that volunteers for pain to feel useful. She gave him the small story first, testing him: how she'd learned letters by copying her mother's recipes, how she'd once forged a holiday permit to get extra flour and gotten away with it, how the flour had tasted like victory and chalk.

"Better," Odd said, when she finished. "Now the real one."

"There isn't—"

"Copper and wet wool," Odd reminded gently. "You lied just then. Small lie, but it counts. Save the big one for when you trust me. I'm patient. It's my second-best skill after talking."

Something flickered across her face — relief and resentment sharing a chair, which was, Odd thought, going to be the theme of this entire journey.

Silas's story, when it came, was about bees. Odd had expected storms, exile, prophecy — the dramatic scaffolding. Instead Silas talked about a hive he'd kept in his fourth year in the valley, a swarm that should have died in the late frost but didn't, and how he'd sat with them through the cold night with a lamp, doing nothing, because there was nothing to do, and how they'd lived anyway. He told it badly, haltingly, like a man reading a language he'd almost forgotten. But his hands moved while he spoke — shaping hexagons in the air — and Odd watched them and thought: *there's the whole man, right there. The storm is the loud part. This is the true part.*

"That one's free," Odd said quietly, when Silas finished. "That one I'd have paid for."

* * *

On the third night, Hazel finally told the true story — the brother, the contract, the soul sold smiling — and Odd listened without interrupting, which for him was heroic effort, and afterward he said only: "Thank you. I'll keep it safe. That's what I do with the good ones."

And on the fourth morning, when the snow eased and the white road called and the two thieves packed their satchels with honey-bread and courage, Odd stood at the barrow's mouth in his soft grave-clothes and announced:

"Right. I'm coming with you."

Silas stared. "You said you'd guide us."

"I said I'd get you to the doors. I've reconsidered. Partially because you'll die without me — accurate, verifiable, no offense — and partially because—" Odd paused, and for once in four hundred years, chose his words with care. "Because you two are the most interesting thing to happen to me since the lightning, and I have been *so bored*, and if you think I'm letting the best story of my afterlife walk into the under-court without me, you have profoundly underestimated how selfish a wight can be."

Hazel was already smiling, small and reluctant, the first one Odd had seen from her. "You're coming for gossip."

"I'm coming for *material*," Odd corrected. "There's a difference. Gossip is idle. Material has structure." He pulled his hood up, tucked his cousin's favorite root cutting into his sleeve as a travel snack, and stepped out into the snow, which did not touch him, because snow knows better. "Now. Before we reach the drowned chapel road, there's something you should see. Something I found in the fens last century, and never told a living soul, because there weren't any worth telling."

He led them east, toward the low hills, chattering the whole way about fen-drains and forgotten kings, until they crested a rise — and stopped.

Below, half-sunk in the black water, lay a chapel. Drowned to its windows. And on its threshold, frozen mid-struggle in the peat, preserved perfectly, was a skeleton in a scribe's robes, one arm outstretched toward the door, fingers still clutching something pale and rolled.

"A hundred years dead," Odd said softly. "And still reaching for the vaults. Sound familiar?"

Beside him, Hazel had gone very still, and Silas had gone very quiet, and Odd thought: *oh, this is going to be such a good story.*
