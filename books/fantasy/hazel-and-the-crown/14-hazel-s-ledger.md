## 14. Hazel's Ledger

The hollow hill kept its own hours. There was no window to argue with the sun, no draft to carry away smoke, and so the fire Odd insisted on building — a wight's fire, green-edged and slow — burned at whatever pace it pleased while the three of them sat around it like travelers waiting out weather that had already stopped mattering.

Odd had told his story for the night. It had involved a duke, a stolen femur, and a wedding that went wrong in ways Hazel was fairly sure were anatomically impossible. Silas had laughed twice, which she counted as progress; he'd been holding his laughter the way some men held their breath, letting it out only when he forgot to guard it.

Now Odd folded his long hands under his chin and looked at her with his one good eye and one eye that had been replaced by something polished and patient.

"Your turn," he said. "A story each night, true ones. That was the bargain."

"I told you about the ferry crossing," Hazel said. "That was true."

"You told me about a ferry crossing. You left out the part where you paid the toll with someone else's coin." Odd's voice wasn't unkind. Wights, she was learning, didn't do cruelty for its own sake; they did honesty because they'd run out of everything else. "I can smell a ledger on you, scribe. You've been carrying it since I met you. Tonight's story is the ledger."

Silas looked up from the whetstone he'd been running along his knife without any real conviction. He didn't say anything. That was worse than if he had.

Hazel pulled her knees up and considered the fire. The green light made her hands look like someone else's hands, which was, she supposed, the point of sitting near wight-fire when you had something terrible to say. If the hands weren't hers, maybe the words wouldn't be either.

"All right," she said. "But you should know how this ends before I start. It doesn't end with anyone forgiving me. I've had years to work on the ending, and that's still where it lands."

"Historically," Odd said, settling back against a barrow wall that was softer than stone had any right to be, "the best stories are the ones that land where the teller doesn't want them to."

* * *

"The winter I sold my brother started three winters earlier," Hazel said. "That's the part nobody understands about starving. It doesn't happen all at once. It happens in decisions. Small ones. Sensible ones. You sell the good blanket first, because you're proud of it and pride doesn't keep you warm. Then the copper pot. Then your mother's ring, but only the setting, because the stone had cracked years ago and nobody would pay for a cracked stone anyway."

She heard herself doing it — the scribe's cadence, itemized, orderly — and made herself stop. Ledgers were how she hid. She knew that. Knowing it didn't make it easier to put the ledger down.

"My father died in the second winter. Not dramatically. He went to sleep in the chair by the hearth and didn't wake up, and honestly it might have been the kindest thing that happened to our family that decade. My mother lasted until the third winter. Tam was fourteen."

She glanced at Silas. He was watching her now, the whetstone forgotten, and his face had the particular stillness of a man standing very close to a cliff edge he'd been pretending wasn't there.

"You've seen what the long winter does," she said. "Out here, past the fens, it's bad. But we lived in the shadow country, between the mountain and the lowlands, where the court's reach is longest. The frost comes down the valleys like it has somewhere to be. Crops don't fail there — crops never get planted. The third winter, we ate the seed grain in November and both of us knew exactly what that meant, and neither of us said it out loud, because saying it would have made it arithmetic instead of hope."

"What did it mean?" Silas asked quietly.

"It meant February was going to ask us a question we couldn't answer." She turned her hands over, palms up, and studied the calluses there. Scribe's callus on the middle finger of her right hand. A burn scar from a candle gone over. Nothing dramatic. Hands that had done ordinary things right up until they hadn't. "There's an office in every market town in the shadow country. You've walked past them. They look like tax houses, because that's the disguise. A clerk behind a desk, a brazier, a ledger open to a fresh page. And a sign painted small and neat: *Provisions Available. Terms Negotiable.*"

"Souls," Silas said.

"Souls," Hazel agreed. "But not the way the stories say. The stories have demons with contracts and hellfire clauses. It's not like that. It's worse, because it's *bureaucratic*. You sit down. The clerk offers you tea. Real tea, with honey — do you understand? After three winters, honey. And he explains, kindly, reasonably, that the court beneath the mountain maintains the long winter, yes, but the long winter maintains the world, or so they claim, and souls are the fuel, and wouldn't you rather contribute a loved one painlessly than watch them starve slowly? There's even a sliding scale. Children yield more. Elders less. There's a *chart*. I remember thinking, as he turned the chart toward me, that someone had designed that chart. Someone had sat at a desk and chosen the typeface and decided the numbers looked friendlier in blue ink."

Her voice had gone flat and precise again. She let it. Some parts of the story could only be carried in that voice.

"We'd already buried our parents. It was just Tam and me, and Tam was fourteen and growing, which meant he needed more food than me, which meant the hunger hit him harder, which meant I watched my little brother get eaten alive by arithmetic. So I sat in the nice warm office drinking honeyed tea, and I did what I do. What I'm *for*. I read the contract."

Odd made a small sound. Not quite a laugh. "Of course you did."

"Nobody reads the contract," Hazel said. "That's the whole architecture of it. People come in starving and ashamed and they sign where the clerk points because reading feels like bargaining and bargaining feels like ingratitude. But I was apprenticed to a scrivener by then — I'd been copying deeds and wills since I was eleven — and I read every line. Twice. And here's what I found, down in the subclauses, in language so old the clerk probably couldn't parse it himself: the contract requires *consent of the soul given*. Not the family. Not the guardian. The soul itself must agree, freely, or the taking fails and the provisions are forfeit."

Silas frowned. "A fourteen-year-old boy consented?"

"He did more than consent." Her throat closed around it. She took a breath, the way she'd taught Silas to breathe in the squall on the white road — four counts in, hold, four counts out — and hated that her body reached for the lesson at exactly this moment. "I went home and I told him. All of it. The chart, the terms, the clause. I laid it out like a briefing because if I let myself feel it I'd never get through it. And Tam listened, and asked two questions. The first was whether the provisions would cover me through spring. The second—"

She stopped. The fire popped. Somewhere deeper in the hill, water dripped with the patience of centuries.

"The second was whether I'd be safe," she said. "Whether *I'd* live. Not whether it would hurt him. Whether it would save me. And then he smiled — and I need you to understand, Silas, that I have replayed that smile ten thousand times and it never changes, it's always the same smile, the one he used to give me when he'd finished his chores and mine too, without telling me, so I wouldn't feel bad — and he said, 'Then write it up proper. You always said sloppy contracts are how people get hurt.'"

The words ran out. She'd known they would. There was a place in the story where the scribe's voice failed, and past that place there was only the girl who had signed her name in her own blood because the clerk said blood-ink bound truer, and who had watched two men in frost-grey livery take her brother's hand gently, so gently, like dancers, while Tam waved at her over their shoulders.

"He hummed," she said. "On the way out. Our mother's song. The lullaby about the river that forgets its name. He hummed it so I'd know he wasn't afraid. And I stood in the doorway of that warm office with a sack of provisions on my shoulder and I *let the door close*, and I have been on the wrong side of that door ever since."

* * *

For a long moment nobody spoke. Even the fire seemed to lean in.

It was Odd, of all of them, who broke the silence, and he did it gently, which Hazel hadn't known wights could do. "How long did he last?" he asked. "In the vaults. Do they... fade quickly?"

"That's the thing." Hazel wiped her face with the heel of her hand, briskly, businesslike, refusing to make a ceremony of it. "They don't fade fast. Souls in the vaults are fuel, and the court rations its fuel. Tam's been burning slow for six years. When I worked in the archive — after, when they took me in as a scribe because I was useful and grateful and broken in a way that made me obedient — I requested access to the collection ledgers. Standard archival work, nothing suspicious. And I found his entry. Entry number—" she closed her eyes, "—forty-one thousand and six. Condition: stable. Last drawn upon: the Feast of Falling, three years ago. Notes in the margin, in the Court Scribe Prime's own hand: *Subject sings. Recommend no further harvest; resonance interferes with adjacent holdings.*"

"He sings," Silas said. His voice came out rough.

"He hums our mother's song. Six years in a lantern-cage underground and he's still humming it, and the court flagged it as an *interference problem*." Something hot moved through her chest, and for once she didn't file it away. "That's when I stopped being their scribe and started being their thief. Because I'd learned things in that archive. I'd learned that the vaults have keys. Individual ones, cut to each soul's warding, kept in the Scribe Prime's cabinet. And I'd learned that the Court Scribe Prime — Master Vess, the man who taught me script-magic, who praised my penmanship, who once told me I had the finest contract-hand he'd seen in thirty years — that man wrote the prophecy book."

Silas went very still. "The book that found me."

"The book that found you. Though I suspect—" she caught herself, shook her head. "No. That's tomorrow's story. Tonight's story is mine, and I'm not done, and the worst part isn't even the selling. The worst part is what came after."

She drew a breath and made herself look at him straight on, because this next part deserved eye contact, deserved to cost her something visible.

"They gave me a position. Warm rooms. Ink that didn't freeze. Three meals a day. And I took all of it, Silas. I didn't gnash my teeth and plot rebellion from a cold garret. I said thank you. I did excellent work. For six years I filed the paperwork of soul-harvest with clean margins and tidy cross-references, and I slept well, and I told myself that staying inside was the only way to ever get Tam out, and maybe that was even true, but here's the truth underneath the truth: I stayed because the archive was warm. I collaborated with the people who owned my brother because they gave me honey in my tea, and I would have kept collaborating until I died if I hadn't found his ledger entry and felt something crack loose that I couldn't file away anymore."

"So you stole the key," Silas said.

"I stole the key. Vess's master cabinet, third drawer, false bottom — I built the false bottom myself two years prior, for storing restricted sigils, which tells you everything about how thoroughly I belonged there. And I forged my own transfer orders, signed them with a seal I cut from wax salvaged off a dead courier, and I walked out of the mountain during the Feast of Falling with a key to my brother's cage in my boot and my heart going like a rabbit's." She almost smiled. "And then I ran into a valley where magic can't follow, because I figured even the court's writ goes thin somewhere, and I knocked on the door of the one man in the world whose face was printed in a prophecy dated that same night, and I lied to him about all of it for two days."

"Not all of it," Silas said. "You told me your name."

"I told you my name because it was the only thing I had left that was true." She hugged her knees tighter. "So. That's the ledger. Line by line. A dead father, a dead mother, a sold brother who forgave me before I even asked, six years of comfortable complicity, one stolen key, and one lie of a friendship that got you run out of the only safe place you had." She spread her hands, empty, palms up. "Judge away. Everyone else has. I'll wait."

* * *

Silas didn't answer right away. He fed a stick to the green fire and watched it catch, and Hazel recognized the gesture for what it was — a man buying time, the way she bought time with ledgers and he bought it with bees and both of them with silence.

"You want me to say it was monstrous," he said finally. "What you did. Signing him over."

"It was monstrous."

"It was." He said it simply, without heat, the way you'd confirm the weather. "And you want me to say it, and then you want me to leave, or hate you, or do something that lets you stop carrying it, because punishment would be a relief. Punishment closes the account."

Hazel stared at him. "That's not—"

"It's exactly what you're doing. I've watched you do it since the night you collapsed at my door. Every kindness I offer, you try to convert into a debt. I give you soup, you inventory my pantry. I stand between you and the riders, you start calculating what you owe me. You'd rather owe me your life than accept it free, because a debt can be paid and then it's over, and then you can go back to believing you deserve the cold." He set the stick down. "I'm not going to help you with that."

"Silas—"

"I'm not done." But his voice gentled. "Here's what I actually think. I think a monster doesn't read the contract. A monster doesn't spend six years filing the harvest papers and then crack when she finds her brother singing. A monster doesn't steal a key she can't use alone and walk into the dark with a stranger whose face she found in a cursed book. You did a monstrous thing in a monstrous winter, and you have been trying to make it the whole of yourself ever since, and I watched you talk a storm out of killing us both with nothing but breathing exercises, and I watched you count out Maren's payment to the copper farthing because you can't bear to owe anyone, and I am telling you, Hazel, as a man who spent seven years hiding from a mirror: the person you think you are and the person sitting by this fire are not the same person. I know the difference. I *am* the difference."

The fire crackled. Hazel discovered she was crying, quietly, without drama, tears just falling out of her like the hill had tilted, and she didn't wipe them away because wiping them away would be tidying, and for once in her life she wanted something untidy.

"You didn't forgive me," she managed.

"No," Silas said. "I don't forgive you. Forgiveness is yours to find, someday, maybe, and it won't come from me and it shouldn't. I just stay. Those are different things. Staying is the part I can do."

From across the fire came a wet, honking sound. They both turned. Odd was dabbing at his single eye with a handkerchief monogrammed with someone else's initials.

"Oh, don't mind me," the wight said thickly. "I've been dead two hundred years, I'm allowed to cry at the good ones. This is going straight into my rotation. The boy who smiled. The honey in the tea. *Sloppy contracts are how people get hurt* — I mean, honestly, that child." He blew his nose with a sound like a bagpipe failing. "Two hundred years of dukes and femurs and this is the finest story I've been told in this barrow, and I include the one about me."

Hazel laughed — a cracked, surprised, ugly-wonderful laugh — and found that the ledger in her chest had not closed, exactly, but had at least been opened to a new page, and new pages were her native country.

Silas nudged the whetstone aside and, wordlessly, poured a third cup of the chicory brew he'd been nursing, and handed it to her, and that was the whole ceremony. Staying, in a cup.

She drank it. It was terrible. She drank all of it.

* * *

It was much later — the green fire banked to embers, Odd snoring in a manner that suggested wights dreamed, and dreamed loudly — that Hazel lay awake staring at the barrow ceiling and felt the thought arrive that had been circling her all night like a moth.

Vess had written the prophecy book. Vess had written *the storm shall kneel*. Vess had lost a key — her theft — and a scribe, and had sent frost-riders, and had let a prophecy do his hunting for him.

Which meant every step they'd taken since the valley had been taken on Vess's paper.

She rolled over and found Silas awake too, watching the embers, the brand at his collar catching their light.

"Silas," she whispered. "When we get to Greyharbor — the temple records, your grandmother — there's something I need to check in the script. In the prophecy. Before we go another mile."

He looked at her. "What kind of something?"

"The kind that tells us whether we've been following a map," Hazel said, "or walking inside someone's trap with the lid closing a little more every day."

Above them, far beyond the hill's warm dark, snow began to fall on the white road — and somewhere south, in a city of candles, a merchant was already counting what their route might fetch.
