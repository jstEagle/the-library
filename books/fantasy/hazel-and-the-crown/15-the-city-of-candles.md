## 15. The City of Candles

They smelled Greyharbor before they saw it.

That was the first strange thing. Silas had expected woodsmoke, fish, the sour breath of a harbor town — but what came down the road to meet them was something sweeter and stranger: tallow and beeswax and something darker underneath, like a candle burned too long in a closed room. Hazel stopped on the crest of the last hill and went very still.

"What is that?" Silas asked.

"Light," she said. "Being sold."

The city sat in a bowl between the hills like a cupped hand holding an ember. It was not large — Silas could have walked its length in half an hour, he judged — but it glowed. Every window burned. Every doorway held a lantern. Strings of candles hung between rooftops like festival garlands, except this was no festival; the light was steady, disciplined, rationed, and around the edges of the city, where the glow thinned, the dark pressed in like a tide against a seawall.

"Last free city," Hazel said. "That's what they call it. Free meaning: nobody's soul has been sold here yet." She said it lightly, but her hand drifted to her satchel, where the key slept wrapped in oilcloth, and Silas noticed she'd been doing that more often since the fens. Since the sleeping villages. Since she'd found her own handwriting in the collection ledgers and hadn't spoken for two hours afterward.

"Free," Silas repeated, looking at the walls of light. "It doesn't look free. It looks besieged."

"It's both. That's rather the point of it."

* * *

The gate was a tunnel of lanterns, and passing through it felt like diving underwater into warm honey. A gate-warden in a wax-stiffened coat looked them over — Silas's beekeeper's clothes, Hazel's ink-stained fingers, Odd's absence (the wight had parted from them at the fen's edge, muttering about consecrated ground and promising, cheerfully, to "catch up the unpleasant way") — and then held out a small brass dish.

"Candle tax," the warden said. "Two bits each, or one hour of memory per night of stay. Guild rates."

"Memory?" Silas said.

The warden shrugged with the weariness of a man who answered this question forty times a day. "The guilds buy. Small things, mostly. The name of your first pet. The taste of a fruit you liked. Nobody misses them, is the official line." He wiggled the dish. "Coin's fine."

Silas paid coin. He had a little, sewn into his collar next to the brand he kept hidden, and it hurt to spend it, but watching the warden's bored face he understood that most people who came through this gate did not have coin. Most people paid in themselves, a spoonful at a time.

Inside, the city was loud and warm and wrong. The streets were crowded — refugees mostly, Silas realized, families with painted trunks and hollow eyes, come in from the fens before winter sealed everything — and every one of them moved through pools of candlelight as if the light were a currency they were spending just by standing in it. Which, he quickly learned, they were.

"The guilds meter it," Hazel explained as they walked. She spoke in the low, quick voice of someone reciting from an archive, which was how she coped, Silas had learned. Facts as armor. "You buy a candle, you burn it in your own room. Public light — street lanterns, gate-lanterns — is taxed by presence. Stand under a lamp for more than a quarter hour without paying, and a lamplighter comes and moves you along. They're called *shepherds*." Her mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "I wrote some of the ordinances. Early days. Before Tam."

"You wrote laws for this place?"

"I wrote laws for whoever paid. That was the job." She turned down a narrow lane. "There's an inn called the Wick & Wane. Scribe-friendly. We can afford one night if we don't eat much."

"One night? Hazel, we need—"

"I know what we need. I'm doing arithmetic." She glanced back at him, and for a moment the archive-voice cracked and he saw the girl underneath, the one who had signed away her brother in blood because the alternative was watching him starve slowly instead of quickly. "Everything here costs. Get used to it fast, or it'll take you apart before the court does."

* * *

The Wick & Wane was warm in the way of places that charged by the degree. Its common room glowed with a hundred candles, and Silas watched the patrons with growing unease: a fisherman eating alone, his plate lit by a stub so short it guttered; a family of four sharing a single lantern at their table, their faces all leaning toward it like plants toward sun; an old woman in the corner who had bought, judging by the crowd of candles around her, either great wealth or great fear.

The innkeeper, a broad woman with forearms like hams and a voice like a landslide, took their coin and gave them a room "with light included, since you've got honest faces, which is rarer than money these days."

Silas thanked her, then couldn't help himself. "The old woman in the corner. All those candles. Is she—"

"Paying for company," the innkeeper said. "Buys three seats' worth of lamplight every evening so folk will sit near her. Been doing it since her son went up the mountain." She said *up the mountain* the way other people said *into the sea*: a direction you didn't come back from. She leaned in, lowering her voice to something almost gentle. "New here, aren't you. Valley boy, by the look of your hands. Listen. In Greyharbor we've got a saying: *the dark is patient, but the guilds are not.* Mind your purse, mind your memories, and don't sign anything."

She slapped two keys on the counter and stumped off, and Silas stood there feeling as though he'd been shown the machinery behind a wall he'd always thought was solid.

Upstairs, in their small room with its one permitted candle, Hazel laid out their assets on the bed like a surgeon laying out instruments: eleven coins, a coil of good rope, Silas's grandfather's iron bell, her scribe's kit, the oilcloth bundle.

"And the key," Silas said.

"And the key." She didn't touch the bundle. "Which is worth more than everything else on this bed combined, and which I would thank you not to mention in public."

"Hazel. I mentioned it *upstairs.*"

"You mentioned it in a whisper in a locked room, which in this city counts as discretion." She rubbed her eyes. Ink stained her fingers to the second knuckle; it seemed permanent now, part of her, like the brand was part of him. "We need passage east of the city, gear for the deep cold, and information about the drowned chapel approach. That's food, rope, coats, and bribes. Eleven coins buys us maybe the coats."

"So we sell something."

"We are not selling the key."

"I didn't say the key." He hesitated. "What do the guilds pay for memories? Actually pay?"

Hazel looked at him for a long moment. "No," she said.

"It's just — the valley. My bees. The summer I learned to swim in the mill pond. I've got sixty years of nothing much, Hazel. Ordinary things. If a few of them buy us coats and a boat—"

"*No.*" She said it harder than he'd ever heard her say anything, and then, catching herself, softer: "You don't understand what it's like, giving pieces away. You start with the taste of plums and you think, well, that's nothing, who needs that. And it works, so you give the name of your first friend. And that works. And one day you reach for a memory and find a hole shaped exactly like your mother singing, and you can't even grieve it properly because you can't remember why it mattered." She was gripping the edge of the bedframe. "I watched people do it in the court archives. Sold-down scribes. Bright eyes going dim one transaction at a time. It doesn't hurt, Silas. That's the horror of it. It never hurts until it's far too late."

The candle flame leaned, though there was no draft. Silas thought of the old woman downstairs, buying company by the hour.

"All right," he said quietly. "Not memories."

"Not memories," Hazel agreed, and unrolled her scribe's kit instead. "But there's another market here. Copywork. Legal documents, letters home, wills. The guilds need literate hands and they pay in coin, not candlelight, if you're good enough to demand it." A spark of the old competence came back into her face, the armor going back on plate by plate. "I'm very good. And you—" she eyed him, "—can hold a light and keep your mouth shut. Between us we might make passage money in three days."

"Three days." Silas lay back on the narrow bed and stared at the ceiling, where the candle threw a trembling circle of gold. Three days in a city that ate its citizens by the spoonful, while winter sealed the passes and the court beneath the mountain counted down toward the Feast of Falling. He thought of Tam's soul hanging in the dark, humming a lullaby. He thought of the prophecy book, packed at the bottom of his bag, which had been quiet since the fens but which he no longer trusted to stay that way.

Three days. He fell asleep doing arithmetic, and hated it.

* * *

The copyhouse of the Candlewrights' Guild occupied a former chapel, which told Silas most of what he needed to know about the city's priorities. Pews had become desks. The altar was now a ledger-shelf. Where a god might once have hung, there was instead a chandelier of white candles so vast it had its own pulley system and two full-time attendants, and beneath it fifty scribes bent over their work in perfect silence, each desk lit by a single tall candle marked with the guild's seal.

Hazel walked in like a woman coming home, which was perhaps the saddest thing Silas had seen all week.

The master of the copyhouse was a thin man named Quillon with ink to his elbows and spectacles thick as bottle-bottoms. He tested Hazel the way a horse-trader tests a mare: a legal clause in Old Court script, a mercantile contract with a deliberate error buried in the fourth paragraph, a line of cipher the guild used for internal accounts. Hazel caught the error before she finished reading, corrected the clause from memory, and deciphered the cipher while making pleasant conversation about the weather.

Quillon removed his spectacles, polished them, and put them back on, which Silas gathered was the equivalent of applause.

"You'll do," he said. "Piece rates. Clean work, no questions about content, and if any document leaves this room in your head, the guild's remedy is spelled out in your induction papers, which you will now sign." He slid a contract across the desk.

Hazel read it — actually read it, every line, while Quillon grew visibly impatient — and then set down the quill. "Clause nine gives the guild first claim on any 'items of scriptural significance' found in a worker's possession. Define *scriptural significance*."

"Anything with the mountain's seals on it," Quillon said blandly.

"Then strike clause nine or find another scribe." Hazel's voice was pleasant. Her hand, Silas noticed, had gone white-knuckled around the quill. "I've worked for worse employers than you, Master Quillon, and I outlasted them all by knowing exactly what I'd signed."

There was a silence in which the chandelier above them shed tiny beads of wax like slow rain. Then Quillon smiled with genuine pleasure, struck the clause, and initialled it. "Ohh, you're court-trained," he said. "Should have guessed. They breed you lot ruthless." He waved them to two empty desks. "Start with the widow's petitions. And boy — you. Hold the light steady. That's a real skill, whatever she tells you."

So Silas spent his first day in the last free city holding a candle over other people's griefs. Widow's petitions: formal requests to the guild relief fund, each one a small masterpiece of desperation dressed in polite language. *My husband was taken up the mountain in the harvest of the ninth year. I request consideration for my daughter, aged six, who cannot sleep unlit.* Another: *I have already sold the taste of bread, the smell of rain, and my wedding day. I request consideration for anything at all.*

He copied them out in his careful beekeeper's hand and found, to his shame, that his eyes kept blurring, and that Master Quillon docked his piece rate for smudges.

* * *

On the second day, the merchant found them.

Silas noticed him at lunch — a meal of grey bread and thin soup eaten at their desks because leaving cost time and time cost coin — a round, comfortable man moving through the copyhouse like a barge through reeds, greeting scribes by name, pressing coins into palms with the ease of long practice. His coat was good wool, his rings were modest gold, and his smile was the smile of a man who had made peace with every compromise he'd ever made and would be delighted to help you make yours.

"That's Vessic," Hazel murmured, not looking up. "Candle-merchant. Middle guild. Buys oddities, resells to collectors. Why is he coming over here."

"Why is he smiling at *us*?"

"Because we're new and desperate. New and desperate is his favorite vintage."

Vessic arrived at their desks trailing warmth and the smell of expensive tallow. "Mistress Scribe! Master — candle-holder. Forgive me, I don't mean to interrupt genius at work, but word travels in this house, and the word is that a court-trained scribe has washed up on our shores with a *very* interesting traveling companion." He beamed at Silas. "Valley-born, yes? I can always tell. You people have a certain — stillness. Like ponds."

"We're busy," Hazel said.

"Of course, of course. Busy. Winter's coming, everyone's busy." Vessic produced, from nowhere, a small candle — white, unmarked, smelling of honey — and set it burning on the corner of Hazel's desk with a conjurer's flourish. "A gift. Best wax in the city, and I include the guild cathedral in that claim. Burn it while you consider a proposition. Nothing sinister. I hear — and I am only a humble merchant, I hear many things, accuracy varies — that a certain traveler came down out of the high passes carrying items of historical interest. Items of *vault-related* interest." His eyes, small and bright as currants, flicked once to Hazel's satchel and back. "I pay better than the guilds. I ask fewer questions. And unlike the guilds, mistress, I never report my purchases to anyone wearing frost."

The soup in Silas's stomach turned to ice water. *Frost.* Frost-riders. The court. This comfortable man was telling them, in the middle of a crowded copyhouse, that he knew what they carried and knew who hunted them.

Hazel's face did something remarkable: it became completely blank, the face of a woman thinking about lunch. "Master Vessic," she said pleasantly, "if I had such an item, selling it would defeat the entire purpose of having stolen it. And if I didn't have such an item, entertaining your offer would waste both our afternoons. So which disappointment would you like?"

Vessic laughed, delighted, a big rolling laugh that turned several heads. "Oh, *wonderful*. Quillon was right, they breed you ruthless." He tapped the burning candle. "Keep the gift. It burns for twelve hours and costs me nothing. When you change your mind — and in Greyharbor everyone changes their mind eventually, it's practically a civic custom — ask any lamplighter for Vessic of the Middle Row." He bowed, wished them profitable darkness, and rolled away, pausing only to press a coin into the widow-petition scribe's palm and murmur something that made the young man laugh shakily.

Silas exhaled. "He knows."

"He suspects. There's a difference, and right now it's the width of a knife blade." Hazel stared at the gifted candle as if it might bite. "He'll sell the suspicion itself if he can't sell the key. Merchants like Vessic trade in *maybes*, and maybes are cheaper to carry and spoil slower." She pinched out the flame with two fingers, unhurt, unbothered, and dropped the candle stub into her satchel beside the oilcloth. "We work faster. Two days becomes one. Tonight I finish the widow petitions, tomorrow I take the guild's cipher contracts — double rate — and the day after we're gone."

"And if he sells us before then?"

Hazel looked at the chandelier, at the fifty bowed heads, at the altar of ledgers where a god used to be.

"Then we'd better hope," she said, "that the storm in you is worth more than the key is. Because one way or another, Silas, this city is going to put a price on us. It puts a price on everyone." She pulled the next petition toward her — *I have already sold the sound of my children laughing* — and dipped her pen. "The only question is whether we get to set it ourselves."

Silas picked up his candle and held it steady over her page, and outside the former chapel's windows the true dark came down over Greyharbor, patient and enormous, while inside the city bought and sold its little flames, and somewhere across the rooftops a comfortable man in a good wool coat was already counting what a valley-born stranger and a runaway scribe might fetch, and from whom.

That night, for the first time since the fens, the prophecy book turned its own pages — softly, twice, like something clearing its throat — and when Silas checked, a fresh line had surfaced in the margin of the old verse, in ink still wet enough to smear:

*In the city that sells the sun, the thief will be offered a fair price. She should refuse it. The storm should listen to why.*
