## 16. An Old Storm's Grave

The temple of Greyharbor smelled of tallow and old paper, which was to say it smelled like every honest building in the city, except that here both were holy.

Silas had never been inside a temple before. The valley had no gods worth the walk — his grandfather used to say the closest thing they had was weather, and you didn't worship weather, you *apologized* to it. So he stood in the doorway a long moment, dripping snowmelt onto the flagstones, feeling like a man who had wandered into someone else's kitchen and forgotten what he came for.

"You're blocking the light," said a small voice at knee height.

He looked down. A child in an acolyte's gray robe was polishing the floor with her whole body, sleeves rolled, hair escaping its braid in every direction. She squinted up at him with the frank appraisal of someone too young to have learned tact.

"Sorry," Silas said, stepping aside.

"Are you here to pray or to read?"

"Is there a difference?"

"Enormous." She stood, wringing out her rag. "Prayers go in the front hall. Readers go down the archive stairs. Prayers are free but nobody listens fast. Reading costs a candle but you get answers." She held out a grubby palm. "Two coppers."

"That's extortion."

"It's *tithing*," she said, with the serene confidence of the well-trained. "Sister Verity says so."

He paid. It seemed wiser than arguing with theology.

* * *

The archive beneath Greyharbor's temple was older than the city above it, if the stones were to be believed, and stones are terrible liars but excellent witnesses. Shelves climbed into darkness where the candlelight gave up. The air had that particular stillness of places where people had been quiet for centuries on purpose.

Hazel went straight to the catalog like a hound scenting dinner. She'd been doing that all morning — since the rooftop escape, since the storm, since she'd watched him pull lightning out of the sky with his bare hands and then apologize to it afterward. She kept looking at him sideways, the way you look at a door you've just discovered was unlocked the whole time.

"The court keeps its records sealed," she murmured, running one finger along the shelf labels. "But Greyharbor's temple has been collecting everything the mountain throws away for six hundred years. Executions. Exiles. Heresies." A thin smile. "Trash is history, if you keep it long enough."

"And we're looking for...?"

"Your grandmother." She said it gently, but not *too* gently, which he appreciated. "You said the name your grandfather wouldn't say. You said it last night in your sleep, actually, which was less gentle."

"I don't talk in my sleep."

"You recite beekeeping advice in your sleep. Very firmly. Something about the queen needing space." She pulled a ledger from the shelf and blew dust off it in a gray plume. "Storm-caller. That's the title the old records would use. Here — *Registry of Censures, Volume Nine.*"

"Censures," Silas repeated. "That's a polite word for something, isn't it."

"It's a very polite word for executions."

* * *

They found her two hours later, by candlelight, in a hand copied from a hand copied from a hand — the way truth degrades as it travels, each scribe sanding off a little more of the grain.

**BRANWEN OF THE HIGH FELLS, called STORM-CALLER.**

Silas sat down on the floor when he read the name. Not dramatically. His legs simply decided the matter without consulting him.

Hazel crouched beside him and read aloud, because some things need a voice:

*"Censured for refusal of tithe. The mountain required the winter be fed; the storm-caller declined, stating — and I quote the transcript — 'I will not pour children into that hole to keep a king comfortable.' She was taken at the pass of Dunmarrow in the year of the Long Frost. Sentence carried out by the Court Scribe Prime, who unbound her from her storm before the sentence, per custom, that the power might return to the mountain's keeping."*

Silas read the next line himself, because Hazel's voice had gone careful and he needed to do this part alone:

*"Note: the storm did not disperse. Unprecedented. It withdrew into itself and could not be rendered. By order of the Mountain King it was chained in the deep vaults pending study. Location: the Storm Room, third gate below the soul-vaults."*

Chained. Not destroyed. Chained, and filed away like a disputed invoice.

"She refused," Silas said. His voice came out strange, flat, like a coin dropped on stone. "She told them no. To their faces. And they—"

"They took her storm first," Hazel said quietly. "Because a storm-caller without her storm is just a woman. Easier to kill. Easier to forget." She closed the ledger with a soft thump. "That's why the court fears your family, Silas. Not because you're dangerous. Because you can say no and mean it."

He stared at the candle flame until it doubled and trebled. He thought about his grandfather, who had never once spoken her name, who had taught him to hide the brand under his collar and flinch from mirrors and call the valley's silence *peace*. All those years Silas had believed the silence was mercy. Now it looked more like a scar tissue. His grandfather hadn't been protecting him from the storm.

He'd been protecting him from becoming the kind of person who got executed for kindness.

"There's more," Hazel said. She'd gone back to the shelf and come down with a thinner volume, its cover cracked like dried mud. "This is a personal effects inventory. What they confiscated." She opened it to a ribbon-marked page and turned it toward him.

The list was short. One iron bell, engraved. One coat, mended at both elbows. One letter, unsent, addressed to *my son, if he lives.*

Silas's throat closed. His grandfather had owned an iron bell. It hung on a nail above the door of the cottage in the valley, and Silas had rung it every evening of his childhood to call the bees home, and his grandfather had never once explained why a man who hated magic kept a bell made of cold-forged iron, the only metal storms couldn't touch.

It was hers. It had been hers, and somehow it had traveled from an executioner's inventory table to a nail above a door in a valley where magic couldn't follow, carried by a son who couldn't say her name and a grandson who didn't know he was ringing his dead grandmother's dinner bell every night of his life.

"I have it," Silas said. "The bell. It's sitting on a mantel four hundred miles from here, holding up nothing."

Hazel put her hand over his on the open book. She didn't say anything, which was, he was learning, her highest form of eloquence.

* * *

They stayed until the candles burned low. Hazel copied everything — she was a scribe down to the marrow, and even fugitive, even hunted, her handwriting stayed beautiful, each letter standing up straight like it had somewhere to be. Silas read. The archive had more: fragments of trial transcripts, a guard's complaint about the prisoner who sang in her cell, a temple marginal note in faded brown ink: *the storm-caller asked whether the children of the fens were fed before she was taken. The scribes did not answer.*

She'd been worried about other people's children at the end. Silas pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes and breathed until the candle stopped swimming.

When they finally climbed the stairs, the acolyte was waiting at the top, arms crossed, having clearly aged several years in authority since morning.

"Find what you wanted?" she demanded.

"No," Silas said honestly. "We found what we needed. It wasn't the same thing."

She considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. "Sister Verity says that's how it usually goes." Then, because she was perhaps eight and mercy comes naturally at that age: "The candle's on the house. You look like you got robbed anyway."

* * *

Outside, Greyharbor was lighting itself against the early dark. Candle-guild runners moved door to door with their brass trays, selling little flames at prices that would have bought a week of bread in the valley, and people paid, because the alternative was the dark, and everyone in this city knew what lived in the dark now. Silas watched a mother count out coins with numb fingers for three candles that would last until midnight, and understood, suddenly and completely, why his grandmother had said no.

Hazel walked beside him with her hood up and her shoulders tense. She'd been tense since the rooftops; being recognized by frost-riders will do that. But tonight there was something else under it, a thought working behind her eyes like a mouse behind a wall.

"Say it," Silas said.

"Say what?"

"Whatever you've been not-saying since the archive. You get a crease." He tapped the spot between his own eyebrows. "Right there. It means you're composing."

She almost smiled. "You've known me three weeks."

"Long enough for the crease."

She was quiet for half a street. Then: "The Court Scribe Prime executed her. Did you catch that? The same office that wrote the prophecy book. My old master's office." Her breath fogged. "Silas, the court didn't just kill her. They *kept* her storm. They've been sitting on it for sixty years like a dragon on a hoard. Why? Storms disperse. Everyone knows that. Hers didn't. It waited."

"For me," Silas said. The words felt enormous in his mouth. "Or for someone. Blood calls to blood — my grandfather used to mutter that about the bees. Maybe it's true about weather too."

"Which means your inheritance isn't buried. It's *imprisoned*. In the same vaults where Tam is." She stopped walking and turned to face him, and the candlelight caught the sharp bones of her face and the exhaustion underneath them. "Do you understand what that means? Everything they took from my family and everything they took from yours is in the same room. Behind the same doors. We don't have to choose between the errands. They're one errand."

Silas looked out over the city — the gutters running with meltwater, the towers wearing crowns of smoke, ten thousand windows each burning its small purchased flame against a winter that had lasted longer than any winter should. Somewhere north, beneath a mountain, a storm that had once been his grandmother's rage hung in chains, waiting sixty years for someone with the right blood to come and let it loose.

His whole life he had thought of the brand under his collar as a disease. A thing exiled along with him. Tonight, for the first time, it felt like something else entirely.

A debt. An inheritance. A woman's unfinished *no*, waiting for a voice.

"Hazel," he said slowly, "when we get into those vaults — and we are going to get into those vaults — I'm not just stealing your brother back."

"I know," she said.

"I'm taking back what's mine. And I don't think I'm going to be gentle about it."

Behind them, unnoticed, the acolyte's candle stub guttered in the temple doorway — and relit itself, twice, though there was no wind.

Above the northern mountains, far past the edge of the lamplight, thunder answered.
