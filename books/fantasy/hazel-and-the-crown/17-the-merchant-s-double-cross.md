## 17. The Merchant's Double Cross

Hazel had been a scribe long enough to know that the most dangerous lies were the ones written in beautiful hands.

Verrin the candle-merchant had beautiful hands. Long fingers, ink-stained at the cuticles — no, not ink, she realized later, wax — and a smile he wore like a signature he'd practiced in a mirror. He'd found them on their second morning in Greyharbor, materializing beside their table at the Guttered Wick with the effortless courtesy of a man who had never once been told no.

"The key," he'd said, without preamble, setting down two candles that smelled of honey and something older. "The one in your coat pocket. I'm told it opens doors beneath the mountain."

Hazel had kept her face still through long practice. Beside her, Silas went carefully blank, which was his version of panic.

"I don't know what you mean," she said.

"Of course you don't." Verrin had smiled wider. "But if you did, I'd want you to know that I pay better than the frost-riders do for silence, and I ask fewer questions than they do about anything else." He'd slid a small pouch across the table, heavy with coin. "Consider it a retainer against future conversation."

They hadn't sold him the key. They hadn't taken the coin either, which in hindsight Hazel recognized as the mistake. A man like Verrin didn't offer retainers; he made investments. And when an investment refused to bear fruit, men like Verrin harvested what they could.

Which was how, on their third night in Greyharbor, Hazel came to be sprinting across the rooftops of the candle quarter with her lungs full of cold smoke and three frost-riders behind her.

* * *

It started, as catastrophes often did, with a small kindness.

They had gone back to Verrin's shop — against Silas's judgment, which Hazel should have weighted more heavily, because Silas's judgment was the cautious, beekeeperly sort that had kept him alive in a valley where caution was the only crop — to return the unopened pouch and end the negotiation cleanly. The shop smelled of tallow and myrrh, shelves crowded with candles in every color of grief: white for the newly dead, black for the long winter, a strange blue-grey that Verrin called *deeplight* and would not discuss.

"You came back," Verrin said, pleased, as though they were old friends. "That's rare. Most people who refuse me once stay refused forever."

"We're returning your money," Hazel said, setting the pouch on the counter. "And we're leaving Greyharbor tonight. If anyone asks—"

"If anyone asks, I'll tell them exactly what I saw." He picked up the pouch, weighed it, and set it down again. "Two travelers. One carrying a key that hums when the moon is out. One—" and here his eyes went to Silas, and something in them sharpened like a blade being tested against a thumb, "—one who flinches every time the wind changes."

Silas said nothing. But Hazel felt him go still in a way she'd learned to read, the stillness of a man holding a door shut against something enormous.

"There's a storm coming," Verrin said softly. "Not weather. Weather is honest. This one has a name, and the mountain pays very well for names." He tapped the counter twice, a little rhythm, almost cheerful. "I sold the route an hour ago. You have perhaps until the bells. I'd start running now."

For one absurd heartbeat, Hazel almost admired him. It took a particular kind of craftsman to betray someone so politely.

Then the first bell rang, and admiration became irrelevant.

* * *

They went out the back, because going out the front was for people who wanted to die in a doorway.

Greyharbor's rooftops were built for this, though nobody admitted it. The city had spent two centuries crouching under the mountain's shadow, and its architecture had developed the hunched, conniving posture of a thing trying not to be noticed: low roofs nearly touching, drainpipes thick as a man's wrist, laundry lines strung between chimneys like the rigging of a desperate fleet. Hazel had grown up in the court beneath the mountain, where every corridor was stone and every stone was watched. Rooftops, she decided, were freedom's compromise — exposed, exhausting, and gloriously unregulated.

"This way," she gasped, hauling Silas over a gap between a chandler's warehouse and a dyer's loft. Below, the street was already filling with torchlight. Frost-riders moved through crowds the way knives move through bread — no hurry, total confidence.

"How many?" Silas called.

"Three on foot. Maybe more on the causeway." She risked a look back. "They're not chasing us, Silas. They're herding us."

He understood a half-second before she pointed it out. The riders weren't following their actual path; they were spreading along the streets below in a widening arc, funneled inward toward the harbor square, where the white road began and the open country ended. Somewhere ahead, she'd bet her forged seal on it, more riders waited at the top of the only stairs down.

"They took blood from you?" she asked. "In the ambush on the causeway?"

"A palm's worth. Why?"

"Because blood tells them where you are. Court scrying. It fades after a few days, but yours is fresh enough to follow." She cursed under her breath — a scribe's curse, grammatically impeccable. "We need to break the trail or outrun it, and we can't outrun horses."

"So we break it." Silas landed beside her on the dye-house roof, breathing hard, and there was something in his voice she'd heard only once before — the night the valley's silence broke, when lightning had split the sky and his brand had burned through his shirt. Terror. But underneath the terror, a terrible patience, like a held breath.

"No," she said, before he could say whatever came next. "No, absolutely not. You've barely touched it since the fens. You nearly drowned us both in a squall you didn't even mean to raise."

"That squall was panic." He looked out over the city, at the torches converging below, and his jaw set in a way that made her stomach drop. "This wouldn't be."

"Silas—"

"Hazel." He turned to her, and up close she could see the brand at his collar, faintly luminous, like a coal remembering fire. "There are six ways off these roofs and riders at all of them. I can spend the next hour running until my legs give out and then explain myself to the Mountain King's men, or I can spend thirty seconds doing the one thing I was born to do." A ghost of a smile. "You taught me breathing exercises. Let me use them."

Below them, a rider shouted. Boots on a ladder. Two roofs away, maybe less.

Hazel thought about Tam, hanging dim in a vault like a lantern running out of oil. She thought about the prophecy book, and the line Ellery had written in fresh ink: *the storm shall kneel.* She thought about how, her whole life, power had been something other people held over her — contracts, kings, winters — and how she had never once watched someone hold power *carefully*, as if it were a living thing that deserved gentleness.

"Thirty seconds," she said. "And if you so much as singe my eyebrows, I will write you into a contract so binding your grandchildren inherit the debt."

"Fair terms." He knelt on the rooftop tiles, laid both palms flat against them, and closed his eyes.

* * *

Later — much later, when she had time to write it down and rewrite it and burn the draft — Hazel would try to describe what happened next, and she would fail, because language was built for describing things that obeyed rules.

It did not begin with thunder. That was the first impossible thing. She had expected thunder, expected the sky to tear open the way it had over the valley, all fury and accident. Instead, the air simply *changed*. The pressure dropped. Every candle flame in the candle quarter leaned east at once, ten thousand little fires bowing toward a kneeling man on a dye-house roof, and the hair rose along Hazel's arms, and she understood — with the clarity of a woman reading her own handwriting — that the storm had not come *to* Silas.

He had called it. By name. Quietly. The way you call a dog you raised from a pup.

Clouds gathered over Greyharbor in a spiral so precise it looked drawn with a compass. Rain began — but only over the harbor square, only over the white road, only where the frost-riders stood. Sheets of it, hammering, blinding, dousing torches and turning the cobbles to glass. Lightning followed, and this was the second impossible thing: it struck nothing. It struck *beside* things. Bolt after bolt walked down the length of the causeway road, each one landing a precise, contemptuous yard from a rider, close enough to fill their visors with light and their hearts with arithmetic. Not one horse fell. Not one man burned. Every single one of them ran.

"Oh," Hazel whispered.

The riders on the ladder stopped climbing. The ones in the square broke formation entirely, wheeling their horses through the flooded streets, and the beautiful terrible truth of it was that they weren't fleeing Silas. They were fleeing the *accuracy*. Anyone can fight a storm. No one knows where to stand against a storm that has opinions.

Silas opened his eyes. They were grey-white, lit from inside, and for a moment Hazel looked at him and saw the page of the prophecy book — the inked figure with lightning crowning his head — and felt fear and awe braid themselves together so tightly she couldn't tell which strand was which. This was what the court had exiled. This was what the mountain feared. This was what her master, Scribe Prime, had spent a hundred years of prophecy steering toward some purpose of his own.

And then Silas swayed, and the awe won, because he caught himself on his hands and laughed — a small, stunned, disbelieving laugh, the laugh of a man who has just discovered the limb he thought was amputated still answers when he calls.

"It listened," he said. "Hazel. It *listened*."

"Don't get sentimental," she managed, because her voice needed something to do besides shake. "You're leaking rain on my boots."

Above them, the spiral clouds unraveled with the same tidy precision they'd arrived, releasing the city back to its ordinary drizzle. Below, the last rider vanished down the white road, and the bells of Greyharbor rang the storm-hour in tones of genuine confusion — the bell-ringers had no protocol for weather with manners.

Silas slumped against the chimney, soaked, gray-faced, grinning like a fool. "Thirty seconds?"

"Forty-one," Hazel said. "I counted. You owe me eleven seconds."

"I'll repay them with interest." He flexed his fingers, watching sparks crawl between them like curious insects, and his grin faded into something quieter. Something almost frightened, but in the good way — the way you're frightened by a door opening in a wall you'd thought was solid your whole life.

Hazel sat down beside him on the wet tiles. Her heart was still hammering, partly from the run, mostly from the sight of him kneeling in the rain with the sky arranged around him like a crown being fitted. *The storm shall kneel,* the book had said. She'd assumed it meant kneeling to the mountain. Watching him now, she wondered if it might mean something else entirely — a storm that chose where to strike, and therefore a man who chose who to be.

"You were magnificent," she said, and then, because sincerity made her itch: "Don't let it go to your head. Magnificent people die of it constantly. There's an entire shelf of records about it in the court archive."

"Noted." He tipped his head back against the chimney brick. "Where do we go? They'll regroup. Verrin sold them our route, which means they know we're headed for the coast road, which means—"

"Which means we need a route Verrin doesn't know." Hazel pulled her coat tighter and stared out over the drowned, glittering city. "Back ways. Underways. Some path into the mountain that isn't on any map the court bought."

Silas snorted. "You say that like such a thing exists."

"It does exist." She hesitated. She had been saving this card, the way she saved everything, hoarding information like the candle-guilds hoarded light. But the riders knew their road now, and hoarding was a luxury for people with time. "When I was in the archives, I read a deposition. From a ferrywoman. About a barrow on the moor road where a grave-wight guards the entrance to the deep ways — tunnels older than the court, running all the way under the mountain. The wight lets travelers pass."

"For a price?"

"For stories, according to the deposition. True ones." She glanced sideways at him. "Apparently it's terribly lonely, being dead in a hill."

Silas considered this. Rain dripped off his nose. Somewhere below, Greyharbor was lighting its candles again, one stubborn flame at a time, and the ordinary world reasserted itself around two fugitives on a roof.

"A ghost who wants gossip," he said at last. "That's the strangest thing I've heard since I met you."

"You met me collapsing in your doorway. The bar for strange was underground from the start." Hazel stood, knees protesting, and offered him her hand. "Come on, storm-caller. We have a moor to cross and a dead person to charm."

He took her hand and let her pull him up — and neither of them noticed, in the dark and the drizzle, the small blue-grey candle burning in Verrin's shop window below. Deeplight. The color the merchant would not discuss. The color, though they had no way of knowing it yet, that burned only when someone was watching from somewhere deeper than the mountain.

And far beneath the city, in a drowned chapel where the water never stirred, something ancient turned its attention toward the surface — and began, patiently, to listen for footsteps.
