## 18. Odd's Bargain Renegotiated

Odd had been dead for four hundred years, give or take a decade of forgetting, and in all that time he had learned one great truth about the living: they were terrible at paying their debts.

Not because they were dishonest. Because they were *busy*. They got distracted by weather, by love, by the urgent business of staying alive, and somewhere between one sunrise and the next, a wight's little arrangement would slip their minds entirely. Odd had once guarded a barrow for a farmer who promised him a story every solstice. The farmer told exactly one — a good one, about a pig that could count — and then died owing Odd thirty-one more, which was, in Odd's professional opinion, the rudest thing a man had ever done.

So when the girl with the scribe's hands came to him on the third night, sat down cross-legged on his barrow like she owned it, and said, "I need to renegotiate," Odd was not surprised.

He was, however, delighted. Renegotiation meant conversation. Conversation meant stories. And stories were the only currency Odd had ever cared about, because you couldn't spend gold underground, but a true tale told well would keep a body warm through any number of centuries.

"Renegotiate," he repeated, rolling the word around like a pebble in his mouth. His voice came out the way it always did — like wind moving through a keyhole, thin and whistling and far too pleased with itself. "That's a fancy word for *I want something else from you*."

The girl — Hazel, she'd said her name was, though Odd suspected there were at least three other names folded up inside her like letters in a drawer — didn't flinch. He liked that. Most living folk flinched at him. It wasn't his face; well, it was partly his face, what remained of it, all grave-grey and cheekbone. Mostly it was the way he moved, which was less like walking and more like remembering how walking worked every few steps.

"The storm-caller and I need to get into the mountain," Hazel said. "You know the back ways. You said so."

"I said I knew *a* back way." Odd held up one finger, then a second, then lost track of what he was counting and started over. "One. Singular. And here's my first question, scribe-girl, and think carefully before you answer, because I'll know if you lie — I've been lied to by better liars than you, and they're all buried within shouting distance of where I'm sitting."

Hazel's jaw tightened. "Ask."

"Why does a runaway court scribe want into the under-court? Nobody breaks *in*. People break out of it. That's practically its founding principle. There are songs about it. Bad songs, mind, the court doesn't fund good ones, but still."

Silence stretched. Above them, the hollow hill breathed its warm dark breath, and somewhere deeper in the barrow, Odd's cousins rustled in their sleep — the old bones, the quiet ones, the ones who'd stopped wanting stories centuries ago. Poor sods.

"Her brother's soul is in the vaults," Silas said quietly, from where he stood at the mouth of the barrow with his arms crossed like a man guarding a door that only existed behind his ribs.

"*Silas.*" Hazel's voice cracked like a whip.

"What?" The storm-caller shrugged. "He asked. And you're bad at lying to things that live in graves."

Odd clapped his hands together, which made a sound like two shovels meeting. "Oh, *wonderful*. A soul-theft! I haven't heard of one of those since—" He paused, riffling through his memory, which was organized the way a magpie organizes a nest: by shininess rather than sense. "Since Ellery. Oh, oh, oh. You don't know about Ellery yet, do you? No, no, your faces say no. Excellent. I'm saving that one. A storyteller doesn't spend his best coin on the first night."

"We're not here for stories," Hazel said.

"Everyone's here for stories. They just don't know it yet." Odd leaned forward, and his shadow leaned with him, a half-second late, the way shadows do when they belong to someone who died holding a grudge against the sun. "But fine. Business first. Gossip after. Here is the shape of your problem, scribe-girl, and I want you to appreciate it properly, because I've been waiting four hundred years for someone to have this exact problem so I could explain it with dramatic pauses."

He paused dramatically.

Hazel waited. Silas shifted his weight.

"You're both terrible audiences," Odd said, wounded. "Right. The back way into the mountain runs through the Drowned Chapel."

*

He watched the word land on them. *Chapel.* It always landed. Living folk had a particular relationship with holy ground — they treated it like a grandmother they visited twice a year and feared the rest of the time.

"There used to be a monastery up on the mountain's shoulder," Odd went on, warming now, pacing the way he did when a story had hold of him, "back before the under-court, back before the long winter, back when the mountain had a different name that nobody remembers because names die faster than people. Good monks. Quiet monks. Terrible singers — I heard them from down here, through forty feet of earth, and let me tell you, the dead complained. We wrote a petition. It was ignored, because we're dead, which is the whole trouble with being dead."

"The chapel flooded," Silas said. Not a question. The storm-caller had that look he got sometimes, the one where his eyes went distant and grey like weather coming in over water.

"Flooded, drowned, sank — pick your verb, they all fit. When the court built its winter, the waters rose to meet it, or the mountain sank to escape them, or both, depending on which of my cousins you ask, and I have some very argumentative cousins. The point is: the chapel's down there now, under the fens, under the roots of the mountain, and the back door of the soul-vaults opens right through its crypt. Consecrated ground, every stone of it. Blessed by men who meant it, which matters. Blessings fade when the blesser stops believing. Those monks believed down to their marrow."

"And you can't cross it," Hazel said.

Odd spread his arms wide, an actor accepting applause that never came. "*Cannot.* Not won't. Can't. It's not like a locked door, girl, it's like asking me to be alive. I set one toe on consecrated earth and everything I am goes — poof. Four hundred years of excellent gossip, gone. My cousins would mourn me. Well. Two of them would. The rest would argue about who gets my chair, and it's a terrible chair, that's the insult of it."

"So we go around," Silas said.

"You can't go around. That's the *point* of a back way. The front ways have gates and wards and frost-riders and a king who talks nicely, and the side ways have worse. The chapel is the only road in that isn't watched, because nothing that serves the court can walk it either. Consecrated ground doesn't care whose side you're on. It cares whether you're *dead*, and the court is nothing but dead wearing crowns." Odd stopped pacing. He looked at Hazel, and for a moment he let the jester fall off his face, because this next part deserved a straight face. "There is a way. There's always a way. But it costs, and I want you to hear the price with your whole ears before you decide anything."

Hazel lifted her chin. Odd had noticed she did that — braced herself like a woman stepping into cold water, as if every hard thing could be gotten through faster if you walked into it on purpose. He'd known a lot of people like that. They usually died tired.

"Name it," she said.

"A bone-barge." Odd gestured toward the deep dark of the barrow, where something long and pale rested under a shroud of old linen. "My boat. Rib-bones of a whale that beached itself a thousand years ago grieving its calf — don't make that face, it volunteered, grief makes creatures do strange things, ask me how I know. The barge can carry the living across the chapel waters. It has done it before. Twice. Both times ended badly, but not because of the barge, and I'll tell you those stories another night, they're *good* ones. The trouble is the barge doesn't move itself. It needs a dead hand on the tiller. My hand. And I can't touch the water inside the chapel bounds without burning up like a candle in a gale."

"So the barge carries us, but you can't steer it through," Silas said slowly.

"Now you're seeing it. Unless—" and here Odd smiled, and he knew it wasn't a comforting smile, he'd been told, repeatedly, by the two cousins who spoke to him, "—unless I borrow something. A piece of the living, carried outside the body. Something small enough to hold in a dead hand and heavy enough to pay a toll. The chapel takes payment at the waterline, see. Always has. Holiness is a toll-road, and the toll is proof you're real. I'd carry your coin across for you. Your proof-of-living. And when we reached the far shore, I'd give it back."

"Give it back," Hazel repeated.

"If you wanted it back," Odd said gently, and watched her understand.

*

Here is what Odd knew about memories, and he knew it the way a fisherman knows tides: the under-court bought and sold them, everyone knew that, the whole economy of the mountain ran on harvested dreams and rendered recollection. Candles made of somebody's wedding day. Soup made of somebody's childhood summer. He'd seen traders pass through the fens with carts full of little glass vials, each one glowing faintly, each one humming a tune nobody else could hear.

But that was the court's trade. Crude. Industrial. They took memories the way loggers took forests — everything, indiscriminately, whatever burned.

What Odd was proposing was different. Smaller. Older. A single memory, freely given, held outside a living soul, carried across holy water in a dead man's hand. The chapel wouldn't take it — the chapel took *proof*, not payment, and gave it back at the far shore, that was the covenant, that was the whole ancient deal. But the crossing would change it. Water remembered. Holy water especially. Whatever went across came back touched.

And a memory of a mother's face, Odd thought, watching Hazel's expression do arithmetic she clearly hated — a memory like that didn't come back untouched. It came back *weathered*. Like a letter left out in rain. Still legible. Never quite the same.

"There are other memories," Hazel said. Her voice was very even, the way voices get when they're holding a roof up. "I have hundreds. Thousands. I memorized entire archives, I can give you—"

"You could," Odd agreed. "And I'd take them, and I'd enjoy them, and somewhere around year sixty I'd realize I'd spent your best coin on pocket change." He crouched down to her level, which for a wight meant folding up like a deckchair. "Girl. I've been dead a long time. Do you know what I miss? Not food. Not sun. Not even gossip, though don't tell my cousins. What I miss is *her*. My mother. I can't remember her face anymore. I remember that she hummed when she baked. I remember her hands were strong. I remember she called me a name that wasn't Odd, and I cannot for the life of me — ha — recall what it was. Everything else about her has worn away like a footprint in tide-sand. So when I say your mother's face is worth more than your archives, I'm not flattering you. I'm telling you what I'd trade if I had anything left to trade."

Silence. The barrow breathed. Somewhere in the dark, one of the quiet cousins turned over in the earth with a sound like a page turning.

"My mother's dead," Hazel said finally. Flat. Factual. Scribe's voice, recording the entry. "Three winters ago. The fever, the same winter I sold Tam. I keep waiting for her face to start fading too, and it hasn't, and I check every morning like checking a lock. It's the last—" She stopped. Started again. "It's the last thing I have that's hers that isn't a debt."

"That's why it's the price," Odd said softly. "Cheap tolls don't open holy doors."

"And if it changes? If the water wears it?"

"Then you'll have a mother's face with rain on it instead of without. Still hers. Still yours." Odd tilted his head. "Or — third option, and I offer it because I'm generous and also because I want to see what you do with it — you refuse. We find another way. There's always another way. It'll be worse, and someone will probably die, but you keep your memory whole and I keep my conscience, such as it is, which is mostly decorative."

Hazel looked at Silas. Odd watched the look travel between them — that new thing growing between the scribe and the storm-caller, still green and awkward as spring grass pushing through snow, but rooted already, deeper than either of them seemed to know. The storm-caller shook his head slowly. *Your choice,* the shake said. *I won't carry this one for you.*

Which, Odd noted approvingly, was exactly right. Some weights you offered to share and some you stepped back from, and knowing the difference was most of what passed for wisdom among the living.

Hazel closed her eyes. Odd saw her lips move — not words, he realized, but handwriting. She was writing something in the air, some private ledger-entry, some line in a book only she kept. *Paid out:* something. *Received:* something else.

When she opened her eyes, they were dry, and Odd understood he was looking at a person who had already survived the worst day of her life and filed it under *proceeding*.

"All right," she said. "How do we do it?"

*

The taking of the memory was, Odd had to admit, stranger than he'd expected, and he had expected it to be strange indeed.

Hazel sat in the center of the barrow with her hands palm-up on her knees, breathing the way scribes apparently breathed — four counts in, seven held, eight out — while Silas knelt behind her with one hand hovering over her heart, not touching, close as a held note. The storm in him stirred as he did it. Odd felt it in his teeth. Four hundred years underground and he'd forgotten what weather felt like from below, and now his whole barrow hummed like a struck bell, and the quiet cousins woke up and *listened*, all of them, dozens of old bones turning their attention upward through the soil toward the living boy full of thunder.

"Think of her," Silas murmured. "Just her face. Hold it steady."

"She's laughing," Hazel whispered. "She's — it's midwinter, we're laughing at something stupid Tam said, the fire's low, her cheeks are—" Her breath caught. Held. Released on the eighth count. "Steady. It's steady."

"Odd."

"Here." Odd knelt opposite her and cupped his dead hands together, making a bowl of them. Grave-cold. Patient. "Whenever you're ready, girl. Or never. Never is also a fine answer."

Hazel looked at her own cupped hands. Then, deliberately, like signing her name, she leaned forward and breathed out — one long slow exhale, warm, alive — into Odd's palms.

And there it was.

A light, small and gold, no bigger than a moth. And inside it — Odd caught his breath, an old habit he refused to abandon — inside it was a face. Round-cheeked. Laugh-lines deep as furrows. Eyes the same stubborn brown as the girl kneeling in front of him, and a smile that said *I know exactly what mischief you've been at, and I love you anyway.*

"Oh," Odd said. All his cleverness gone, just for a heartbeat, just a dead man saying *oh* at a moth-sized sun. "Oh, she looks like she told *good* stories."

"She did," Hazel said. Her voice only shook a little. "Better than yours."

"*Everyone's* better than mine, that's why I collect." Carefully, so carefully, Odd closed his fingers over the light. It didn't burn. It warmed — actually warmed, heat soaking into hands that hadn't been warm since the reign of a king nobody remembered. He tucked it beneath his tongue, where dead men keep the things they can't bear to hold, and felt it settle there like a coal in a banked fire. "There. Paid. Now listen, both of you, because the rules matter: the barge leaves at moonset tomorrow. Sleep tonight — real sleep, the kind you'll miss soon. Eat whatever Maren packed you, even the smoked eel, *especially* the smoked eel, it's better than it deserves. And when we're on the water, you do exactly what I say, when I say it. The chapel is holy ground and holy ground has manners, but manners aren't mercy. Understood?"

"Understood," said Silas.

Hazel nodded once. Then, quieter, not looking at him: "If it comes back wrong—"

"It won't come back wrong," Odd said. "It'll come back *lived-in*. There's a difference, and someday you'll thank me for it. Probably. I'm a wight, not a prophet. Prophets are worse than us, they charge more and they're wrong on purpose."

He shuffled off toward the deep dark where the bone-barge lay sleeping under its linen shroud, and as he went he heard, behind him, low voices — the scribe and the storm-caller, talking in the warm dark of a hollow hill, two frightened people building a small brave thing between them out of trust and eel-smoke and borrowed courage.

Odd settled his hand on the whale-ribs and patted them, the way you'd wake an old friend.

"Up you get, lovely," he murmured. "We're going to chapel."

And beneath his tongue, small and gold and warm, a mother laughed at something her children said, midwinter, firelight, a hundred miles and a lifetime ago — and Odd carried her down into the dark, humming a tune he almost remembered, toward water that remembered everything.
