## 19. The Drowned Chapel

The back way into the mountain began, as most terrible ideas do, with Odd being cheerful about it.

"Down through the drowned chapel," the grave-wight said, leading them along a ledge above a black lake that had no business existing inside a hill. "Lovely spot. Very atmospheric. The dead adore it. Well — the dead who *stay* dead adore it. The others get a bit territorial."

Silas kept one hand on the rope rail Maren had rigged for them at the fen's edge and tried not to look at the water. The water looked back anyway. It had that quality — a stillness so complete it felt like attention.

"How deep?" Hazel asked. She had her ledger-satchel strapped tight to her chest, the way she'd carried it since Greyharbor, since the merchant's betrayal, since Silas had learned what his hands could do when he stopped apologizing to them.

"Deep enough," said Odd. "The chapel flooded two hundred years ago when the court rerouted the under-river to fill their cisterns. Very rude of them. Nobody consulted the residents." Odd gestured at the barrow-dark around them with evident grievance. "That's the trouble with the living and their cousins below. They build first and apologize never."

"And you can't come all the way with us," Hazel said. It wasn't a question. She'd been turning it over since the hollow hill, Silas could tell, the way she turned over everything — contracts, clauses, exits.

Odd's lantern-jaw dimmed, just slightly, like a candle considering itself. "Consecrated ground. Even drowned, even abandoned, even *forgotten* — which is the rudest part, honestly, we worked very hard on those hymns — the chapel remembers what it was. Wights can't cross. It's not pain, exactly. More like..." Odd searched for the word with both hands. "Like being uninvited from your own name."

"I'm sorry," Hazel said.

"Don't be sorry, be *entertaining*." Odd brightened again with the effort of someone determined to die — or whatever wights did — in good humor. "You owe me a story tonight before you go down. A true one. That was the bargain, and I'll note for the record that you've been paying in installments like a merchant's debt, which is *very* scribe of you."

Hazel almost smiled. It was small and crooked and gone in a moment, but Silas had learned to catch them. He'd gotten better at catching a lot of things lately: the way her breathing changed before she lied, the way she touched the key hidden against her sternum when she thought no one saw, the way she watched him now whenever lightning lived too long behind his eyes.

He was still learning what to do about that last one.

They made camp on a shelf of dry stone above the waterline, and Odd demanded payment. Hazel told the story of the winter she'd learned to whistle through her teeth — how Tam couldn't, how she'd taught him forty times on the frozen steps of their tenement, how he'd finally managed it the same week the court's men came, and how she hadn't been able to whistle since without tasting iron.

It wasn't funny. But Odd laughed anyway, at the right places, softly, the way you laugh at grief when laughing is the only respectful thing left to do with it. Then the wight curled up against a wall like a cat made of shadows and opinions, and said, "Sleep. Tomorrow you go where I can't follow, and I intend to complain about it extensively when you return. Which you will. I've decided. Wight's privilege."

Silas lay awake a long while, listening to the black water breathe.

* * *

The descent began at dawn, if dawn meant anything a hundred feet underground, which Silas doubted. Odd led them to a seam in the rock where old stairs spiraled down into the lake itself — stairs that went *through* the water without disturbing it, each step dry as bone, the flood held back by something older than the chapel's drowning.

"Preservation wards," Hazel murmured, running her fingers along the step's edge without touching. "Court work. Old court work." Her voice did the thing it did when she recognized handwriting. "This is pre-King script. This might be older than the King."

"Everything here is older than somebody," said Odd. "In you go. Mind the third landing, the warding flickers there and you'll feel a hand on your shoulder. It's only the chapel checking your pockets. Be polite and it'll let you keep everything but your dignity."

"Comforting," said Silas.

"I'm a grave-wight, dear. Comfort is not my department. Company is."

And then Odd stopped, at the exact line where stone gave way to consecrated dark, and could go no further, and stood there wringing shadow-hands until Hazel turned back and hugged the wight, which was either very brave or very foolish, and Odd made a sound like a bell full of bees and pretended afterward that it hadn't happened.

"Third landing," Odd called after them. "Politeness! And if you meet anyone down there who's been waiting a hundred years — *listen to them*. The dead are terrible gossips but excellent teachers."

The stairs took them down into silence.

Not valley-silence. Silas knew valley-silence the way he knew his own heartbeat — the soft null hush that had held him safe for seven years, magic-proof and storm-proof, the quiet he'd mourned leaving. This was different. This silence had *weight*. It pressed on the ears like deep water. It was the silence of a room where something had once screamed and the walls had agreed, out of politeness or exhaustion, never to repeat it.

The third landing flickered exactly as promised. Silas felt the touch on his shoulder — light, curious, impersonal, like a customs officer's pat-down — and then a warmth passed over him and moved on, satisfied or disappointed, hard to say.

"It took stock of us," Hazel whispered.

"What did it find?"

She was quiet a moment. "I don't know. I hope nothing worth taking."

Below the third landing, the stairs opened into the chapel.

Silas had imagined drowned chapels before — everyone raised on border-farm ghost stories had — and he'd imagined wreckage: collapsed pews, drifting hymnals, silt. What he found instead stopped him on the last step with his hand clamped on the rail.

The chapel was intact. Perfectly, horribly intact. The flood had never touched it; the preservation wards held the water back in a great curved ceiling overhead, so that they walked down the center aisle beneath a hanging ocean, fish moving slow and silver through the inverted sky, light filtering down green-gold and trembling. Candles that should have drowned a century ago still burned along the walls, flames upright and patient, fed by nothing.

And in the pews, kneeling in the aisles, frozen mid-reach toward the altar —

People.

"Hazel," Silas said, and his voice came out very small in all that holy space. "Hazel, don't—"

But she was already walking forward, because of course she was, because she was Hazel, and caution was a language she read fluently and spoke never.

They were preserved. Not skeletons — not yet, not all of them — but caught, every one, in the moment of their failing. A man in rusted mail reached for the altar with fingers that had been reaching for a hundred years. A woman in a scholar's robes clutched a crowbar of white iron, her face turned up, mouth open around a word the wards had kept fresh on her lips. Two figures held hands even now, even here, mid-run toward a door that had never opened for them.

"They came for the vaults," Hazel breathed. "All of them. Soul-thieves. Rescuers. Whatever you want to call them." She moved down the aisle slowly, reading them like documents. "Look at the liveries — that one's wearing a hedge-knight's colors, that one's a fen-witch, that's — Silas, that's a *child*, or was. They've been coming for centuries. And the wards just—" She swallowed. "They don't kill you. They *keep* you. Mid-struggle. Forever. The court doesn't need to punish thieves. It just needs to leave them here as an argument."

Silas's skin crawled. He thought of the prophecy book, of pages that turned themselves, of ink that knew his name. *You were sent down a road other people died on.* He thought of Ellery's line — *the storm shall kneel* — and wondered, not for the first time, whose hand had written it and why.

One of the preserved figures was different.

Near the altar, apart from the others, knelt a slight figure in scribe's grey — the same cut of robe Hazel wore, or nearly, faded to the color of old ash. The posture was wrong for struggle. No reach, no run, no desperation. This one knelt neatly, spine straight, hands folded around a leather-bound book held to the chest like something precious. The skull's jaw was closed. Composed. Whoever this had been, they had chosen their last position with care.

Hazel made a sound he'd never heard from her.

He turned. She had gone white to the lips, and her hand had risen halfway to her mouth and stopped there, and she was staring at the scribe's skeleton the way you stare at your own reflection in a window when the street behind you shows something you didn't expect.

"Hazel?"

"That's a Master's robe," she said. "Court grey, senior rank. The clasp — see the clasp? Scribe Prime. There's only ever one." Her voice had gone flat and careful, the voice she used for reading aloud things that must not be misread. "Silas. That's the Scribe Prime's insignia. From a hundred years ago."

"The one who wrote the prophecy book."

"The one who *trained my master*, who trained me." Hazel approached the kneeling skeleton the way you approach a sleeping animal. "Ellery Vane. We have her marginalia in the archive — I copied it as an apprentice, I used to complain about her handwriting, I complained about the handwriting of a woman who—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "She came down here. A hundred years ago. The Scribe Prime herself, the highest scribe in the court, and she came down into her own chapel with a book and she *knelt*."

Silas came to stand beside her. Up close, he could see what the skeleton held. The book was swollen with damp-less centuries, its cover cracked, its pages fanned just enough to show that something was tucked inside — folded paper, many sheets, pressed flat.

A map, maybe. Or a letter.

"We shouldn't," Silas said.

"We absolutely shouldn't," Hazel agreed, and knelt, and with the gentleness she used for everything except herself, eased the book from the skeleton's arms.

The bones did not resist. But as the book came free, the skull tipped — slowly, deliberately, it seemed — until the empty sockets faced Hazel straight on. Dust sifted from the jaw. And in the green-gold light of the hanging sea, Silas would have sworn, would swear on his grandmother's grave and his own, that the sound that moved through the chapel then was not the water overhead, not the candles, not any settling of old stone.

It was a breath. In. Held. Released.

Like someone who had waited a hundred years to stop holding it.

"Hazel," Silas said again, and this time she heard him, and stepped back, and the three of them stood there — thief, storm-caller, and the patient congregation of the failed — while in Hazel's hands the dead scribe's book fell open on its own to a page near the end.

The writing was court-copperplate, beautiful, precise, and utterly alive. The ink gleamed wet.

*If you are reading this,* it said, *then the book found you, and the book is never wrong about people — only about outcomes. I am sorry for the trap. I built it to be broken, not believed.*

*Come closer. I haven't much left to haunt with.*

Behind them, at the far end of the aisle, in the pew where no one had been sitting, a candle flame bent sideways — as if someone had just sat down beside it.
