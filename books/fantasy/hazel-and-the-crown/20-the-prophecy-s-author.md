## 20. The Prophecy's Author

I had been dead for a hundred and six years, and in all that time, no one had come to visit.

Not that I blamed them. The drowned chapel was not on anyone's list of pleasant destinations. The water came up to the second pew on good days and the altar rail on bad ones, and the ceiling dripped in a rhythm I had long ago memorized as a waltz — one-two-three, drip, one-two-three — because a scribe without anything to write will find patterns in anything at all. I had counted the fish. There were eleven. I had named them. I regret to report that I gave them all courtly titles, which tells you something about how my century went.

So when the living came splashing down into my crypt — a storm-caller with his collar clutched like a rosary, a runaway scribe holding her stolen key above the waterline as though it might dissolve, and behind them the sound of frost-riders giving up at the chapel's threshold — I did what any reasonable ghost would do.

I tidied up.

There is not much tidying available to a ghost, but there is some. I straightened the bones in my own alcove, tucked my hands — or rather, arranged the bones of my hands — over the book in my lap, and composed what I hoped was an expression of dignified welcome. Ghosts cannot actually compose expressions; we borrow whatever the light is doing. But the light cooperated, filtering down through the flooded skylight in a way that made me look, I thought, wise. Perhaps even serene.

Then the girl looked directly at me and said, "Oh, you've got to be joking."

Which is, I will note for posterity, *not* how most people greet their predecessors.

* * *

Her name was Hazel, and she was a scribe, and the moment she said so I felt something in my incorporeal chest that I can only describe as professional delight. She held herself the way we are trained to hold ourselves: spine straight from habit rather than pride, ink stains worked so deep into the cuticles that they read as birthmarks, eyes that catalogued everything in the room before deciding whether to be afraid of it. She catalogued me. I watched her do it. Her gaze went: skeleton, book, chains of office rotted to lace, the wax seal still clutched between my fingerbones. Then she did the thing every true scribe does when confronted with a mystery.

She reached for it.

"Don't," said the storm-caller, whose name I already knew, because I had written it down a hundred years before he was born.

Hazel froze with her fingers an inch from my seal. "It's court wax," she said. "Prime grade. Nobody's used this since—" She stopped. Looked at the seal again. Looked at the skeleton. Looked at the book in its lap, the book she had been carrying across half the country, the book that had turned its own pages and rewritten itself in fresh ink and generally behaved like a document with opinions.

"Oh," she said, very quietly. "Oh, you're *her.*"

"I am," I said, "and I would very much appreciate it if you'd stop dripping on my pews."

The storm-caller — Silas, tall and careful and wearing terror the way other men wore cloaks — made a sound that was half laugh and half prayer. Hazel sat down in the water without ceremony, which told me more about her upbringing than any confession could have. Scribes who have served the under-court learn to sit anywhere, at any time, because the work does not wait for chairs.

"You're Ellery," she said. "Scribe Prime Ellery. They took your name off the rolls."

"They would have," I agreed. "I resigned rather badly."

"Resigned." Silas's voice was dry as old vellum. "You died in a crypt."

"Yes, well. My letter of resignation was somewhat longer than theirs preferred. Four hundred pages, if memory serves. With appendices." I let the light shift, let them see the shape of my smile. "They never read past the first page. Kings rarely do. It is their single greatest weakness and I built an entire prophecy out of it."

* * *

Here is what I told them, there in the water, while the fish — Lord Bassington, Lady Minnow, the rest of my small court — circled us with the indifference of bureaucrats at a funeral:

I had been Court Scribe Prime for thirty-one years. I had written the harvest ledgers myself. Every winter, the mountain took souls to feed the shield, and every winter I wrote down how many, in whose hand, against whose debt, and I told myself it was arithmetic, that someone had to keep the numbers honest even if the numbers themselves were monstrous. That is the scribe's oldest lie, and I tell you now, whoever reads this: *the ledger is never neutral.* A record is a choice about what deserves remembering, and choosing is the beginning of everything, both sin and salvation.

In my thirty-first year I found the entry that broke me. A village called Aldermoor, forty-one souls, taken in a single night — and beside it, in the King's own hand, a note: *surplus. hold for expansion.*

Surplus. As if souls were grain. As if the people were a crop the mountain had planted.

I began writing the book that night. Not a prophecy — I want to be very clear about this, because the word has done damage — but a *letter*. A letter across time, addressed to whoever came after me with enough stubbornness to finish what I started. Prophecies fail, you see. That is their nature. But a letter can be *answered*, and an answer requires a reader, and a reader requires a choice, and choice — real choice, uncoerced, unwritten — is the one thing the King's magic cannot digest.

"The book isn't predicting," I told Silas, and watched him go pale in the green water-light. "It never was. It's negotiating. Every line it adds is the court trying to close the argument, and every time you refuse to act as written, you amend it. You've been co-authoring your own fate since the peddler put it in your hands. He didn't find you by accident, by the way. I bribed three generations of peddlers. The last one finally got it right, though he did try to charge you double, which I found distasteful."

"He did charge me double," Silas said faintly.

"He charged everyone double. It wasn't personal."

Hazel laughed — a short, startled sound, quickly smothered, the laugh of someone who has been punished for laughing. I resolved, in the way of ghosts, to make her do it again before she left my chapel.

* * *

But then the conversation turned, as conversations with me always must, to the part I had dreaded telling for a hundred years. And I will set it down here honestly, because honesty is the only inheritance I have left.

"The storm can unmake the soul-vaults," I said to Silas. "That is true, and it is the whole of the hope I have to offer you, and I am sorry, because it comes with teeth."

He stood very still. Storm-callers, I have observed across two generations, go still the way water goes still before it remembers it can drown things.

"Tell me," he said.

"When your grandmother's storm was caged beneath the mountain, the court bound it with scripture — literal scripture, words of law woven into iron. The same law holds the vaults. One law cannot easily break another, but a storm is not *bound by* law, it is *made of* opposition to it. Lightning is simply the sky refusing to be quiet. If you take up the storm — fully, as its caller, blood and bone — you can tear the vaults open. Every soul inside goes free. Tam. Isolde. Ten thousand winters' worth of harvested lives, released in a single hour."

"And the cost," Hazel said. She had gone scribe-pale, the color of paper. "There's a cost. There's always a cost. What is it?"

I looked at Silas Crane, who had spent seven years teaching bees to trust him, who flinched from mirrors, who had crossed a country terrified of his own skin, and I told him the truth because he deserved it and because lies, in my experience, curdle.

"The vaults are held together by the same force that holds the storm's cage. When they break, the breaking wants a shape. A vessel. The storm will pour through the gap, and it will need somewhere to go, and the nearest suitable vessel will be the man standing in front of it holding the door open." I spread my translucent hands. "You may survive it. Your grandmother's mother survived her calling. But the odds, as I recorded them in the appendix nobody ever read, are not generous. Roughly speaking: the storm unmakes the vaults, or the vaults unmake you, and the difference between those outcomes is a margin thinner than the hair of a scribe's brush."

Silence, except for the waltz of the drips. One-two-three.

"That's your hope?" Silas said at last. His voice did not shake, which surprised me; I had expected shaking. "Your great letter across time? 'Here is how to save everyone: possibly die.'"

"It is hope *with teeth,*" I said. "Hope without teeth is just wishing, and wishing has never once moved a mountain. I wrote you a weapon, young man, not a lullaby. Weapons are honest. They tell you exactly what they'll cost going in, which is more than kings ever do."

He was quiet a long moment. Then — and this is why I chose him, why I waited a century in the dark counting fish — he nodded slowly and said, "Okay. But if I'm possibly dying, I want to know what happens to the bees."

Hazel made the startled laugh again. Louder, this time. I counted it a victory.

* * *

She stayed behind when Silas went up to stand watch at the flooded stair — a habit of his, I gathered, watching doors, guarding sleep, carrying vigilance like a second spine. Hazel remained in the water at my feet, turning my hundred-year-old book over in her hands with the reverence of her profession and the suspicion of her experience.

"You wrote the line," she said. "*The storm shall kneel.*"

"I did."

"The court thinks it means he kneels to the King."

"The court," I said, "has never once understood its own paperwork. No — listen, child, because this is the hinge of everything. Kneeling is not surrender. Kneeling is what you do when you choose to receive something too heavy to take standing up. A coronation is a kneeling. So is a proposal. So is planting a seed. The King has spent ten thousand years mistaking the posture for the meaning, and that mistake is the crack in his foundation, and I have spent a hundred years widening it with a pen." I leaned forward, or did the light-thing that passed for leaning. "When the moment comes — and it is coming, sooner than my margins predicted — he will have a choice dressed up as a command. Everyone will tell him what the storm must do. And the only way through is if he stops, and kneels, and *asks.* Asks the people beside him. Surrenders the authorship. Do you understand? The prophecy breaks the moment he chooses freely, because a free choice cannot be foretold — not by me, not by the court, not by any book. That blankness at the end? That is not an omission. That is the entire point. The last page belongs to whoever refuses to let me finish the sentence."

Hazel was crying, I realized. Quietly, the way scribes cry — without disturbing the documents.

"She tried too," she said. "A hundred years ago. The skeleton — you — you came down here to do what we're doing."

"I came down here and I failed," I said gently. "I got as far as the drowned chapel and the water found me, and I died with the map unfinished and the appendix unwritten, and the court erased my name and shelved my letter in a peddler's discount bin. That is what failure looks like, Hazel of the Under-Court. It looks like a hundred years of waiting for someone better. And then one night the door bangs open and two soaked, frightened, magnificent people splash into your crypt arguing about tolls, and you understand that failure was only ever the long road to them." I let the light warm. "You are the better someone. Both of you. Heaven help the mountain."

She wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, smearing nothing, since tears and ghosts mix poorly, and asked the question I had been waiting for — the scribe's question, the only question that matters.

"What do you need us to carry?"

* * *

What I needed them to carry was this: the map's final third, which existed nowhere but my memory, because I had burned the draft. The vaults lay below the Feast Hall, below the King's sleeping court, reachable during the Feast of Falling when the whole mountain dreamed. The storm-room lay deeper still, behind a door that answered only to consent — the founding charter's oldest clause, the one loophole the King had never dared close because closing it would admit his crown was borrowed. And between here and there lay the wight-road, the bone-barge, the drowned crossing where consecrated ground ended and Odd's kind began.

I gave them all of it. I gave Hazel the vault wardings — three lines of counter-script, drawn in the air, that would buy them thirty heartbeats once the alarm rang. I gave Silas the truth about his grandmother's cage: that the chains were scripture and scripture could be *read aloud wrong*, deliberately, lovingly mispronounced until the law choked on itself. And last, because a letter should end with something soft, I gave them this:

"When you doubt — and you will, both of you, in the dark places ahead — remember that a dead woman bet a hundred years on you. I do not bet poorly. Ask the fish."

Lord Bassington swam past with tremendous dignity, which helped.

Silas came back down the stair as I finished, water streaming off him, and stood beside Hazel in the flooded nave, and for a moment the three of us were simply quiet together — the dead scribe and the two thieves, in a chapel where the god had drowned along with everything else, and yet somehow the air felt less like a tomb than it had in a century.

"One more thing," I said. "The book. When you reach the end of what I wrote, there will be a page that tries to write itself. Fresh ink, the court's hand, my old master's cadences — the Scribe Prime who replaced me has been amending my letter for decades, steering it toward the King's ending. Whatever it offers you, whatever future it promises, however reasonable it sounds —" I fixed them both with the full weight of a century's attention. "Do not sign it. Do not speak it aloud. And above all, do not believe that the version of events where everyone obeys is the version where anyone survives."

"And if we can't stop it from writing?" Hazel asked.

"Then you do what scribes have always done with documents that lie." I smiled, and the light did its best to show it. "You annotate."

Above us, faint through stone and water, came a sound that did not belong to my chapel's waltz: a horn, far off, from the direction of the fen road. Then another, answering. The frost-riders had not given up at the threshold after all. They had only gone looking for another way in.

Silas and Hazel exchanged a glance — the glance of people discovering they can already communicate without words, which is either love or logistics, and in my long experience the distinction hardly matters.

"The back way," Hazel said. "Odd's road. We have to move."

"Go," I said. "And take the book. It was always yours. It was always going to be yours. That is the only prophecy I will permit myself."

They waded for the stair, water streaming from their clothes, the battered book clutched against Hazel's chest — my letter, my century, my hope with teeth. At the top step Silas paused and looked back, and I saw that he wanted to say something courteous, something fitting, some farewell worthy of the occasion.

What he actually said was: "If we live, I'm naming a hive after you."

And I confess that a dead woman laughed for the first time in a hundred years, and the sound went up the stair after them like a blessing, and the drips kept their waltz, and the fish kept their titles, and I settled back into my alcove to wait — no longer dreading the waiting, for the first time, because now I knew how the letter ended.

Or rather, I knew it didn't.

That was the whole point.

Far above, muffled by stone and flood, the horns sounded again — closer now, and coming down.
