## 21. Gates Beneath the Mountain

The drowned chapel had a back door. Of course it did — everything under the mountain had a back door, because the court had been built by people who were terrified of being caught, and that fear had fossilized into architecture.

Hazel stood at the threshold of the servants' passage and pressed her palm flat against the stone the way she used to when she was nine years old and late for her lessons. The rock was warm. It remembered her.

"Is it supposed to be warm?" Odd whispered. The wight had to whisper; consecrated ground still prickled along its edges like a wool sweater worn inside out, so Odd waited in the barge while Hazel and Silas stepped through into the dry dark beyond. "Stone's not supposed to be warm. Stone is my whole profession, I'd know."

"It remembers body heat," Hazel said. "A thousand years of servants walking this corridor. The mountain drank it in." She didn't say: *it remembers me.* She didn't have to. Her hand knew the exact place where the mortar bulged, where a mason named Corvin had gotten lazy in the reign of some king or other, and she felt a sob build somewhere behind her ribs like a sneeze she refused to permit.

Silas touched her shoulder. He'd gotten good at that — knowing when to touch and when not to, which was more than most men ever learned about anything. "You drew this," he said quietly. "Every turn. You've had it in your head for three years."

"Four," Hazel said. "I added the east stair last winter. They built it after I left. I heard them talking about it through the archive wall." She swallowed. "That's the horrible part, Silas. I know this place better than I know my own mother's face." She thought of the memory she'd paid Odd, the soft blank place where her mother's smile used to be, and pushed the thought down deep where it couldn't reach her hands. "And I love it anyway. That's the *worst* part."

"That's just home," said Odd, from the dark behind them, wistful. "Nobody chooses their homes sensibly."

* * *

They came up out of the passage into a gallery, and Hazel stopped breathing for a moment, because the gallery was lit.

Not by lamps. By candles — thousands of them, ranked along the walls in iron sconces, each flame a slightly different color, a slightly different height, flickering in rhythms that no fire follows. Some burned steady as a held note. Some guttered and gasped. One near the ceiling wept little sparks that fell upward.

"Those are—" Silas began.

"Memories," Hazel said. "Rendered down. A good childhood makes about six months of light. A first love, maybe a season." She kept her voice flat, scribe-flat, the voice she'd used to read out sentences and tax levies without letting any of it land on her. "The court calls it *tithing*. The light has to come from somewhere, and the mountain doesn't get sun."

Silas was staring at the nearest candle with an expression she recognized from mirrors — the look of a man doing arithmetic he wished he'd never learned. "Whose memory is that one?"

"I don't know." She took his arm and moved him along. "Don't try to find out. That way lies standing in a hallway crying until the riders find you, and I've done it once already tonight."

"You haven't."

"I did it in advance," she said. "Emotionally. I'm running a deficit."

That got the ghost of a laugh out of him, which was the point. Gentle humor was how they'd survived the fens and the causeway and the hollow hill, and Hazel had come to think of it as a tool as legitimate as any lockpick: you could joke a person across a chasm that no rope would span.

The gallery ran toward the great doors of the outer hall, and the doors stood open, because it was the Feast of Falling.

* * *

She'd forgotten the sound of it. That was the thing nobody warned you about — not the sight of the Feast but the sound. The whole court slept on this night, every lord and lady and clerk and cook, laid out on their couches of state with their mouths slightly open, while the mountain reached into them and harvested the year's dreams. And the sound they made, all of them together, was a low tide-like sighing, rising and falling, hundreds of chests keeping a slow shared time. It filled the halls like weather.

"They're all asleep?" Silas whispered.

"All but the harvesters, and the harvesters don't see thieves. They see dreams leaving bodies. We're the wrong kind of interesting." Hazel peered around the doorframe into the outer hall. "Stay behind me. Step where I step. If anyone wakes—"

"What do we do if anyone wakes?"

"We were never here. I still remember the protocols. Thieves who apologize are thieves who hang."

The outer hall was vast and cold and beautiful, and Hazel hated how beautiful. Banners of frost-livery hung from rafters. The King's throne sat empty beneath a canopy of frozen breath — actual frozen breath, plaited and hung like garlands, exhaled by generations of courtiers awed enough to waste their air on wonder. Along both walls, the honored dead and the honored living watched from their frames.

Hazel had planned for the portraits. She'd known they'd be there. What she hadn't planned for was finding hers.

It hung in the third rank, between the Master of Keys and a woman she vaguely remembered dying of a cough. Hazel, aged nineteen, in court blue, holding a ledger against her chest like a shield. The painter had flattered her jaw and given her eyes a steadiness she hadn't possessed, and beneath it ran the inscription in gold leaf:

*HAZEL OF THE UNDER-COURT — SCRIBE OF THE SECOND RANK — HER HAND NEVER ERRED.*

"My hand erred constantly," she hissed, before she could stop herself. "I misspelled *chancery* twice in my first year. There's an entire contract somewhere with *chansery* in it and everyone pretended not to notice because correcting me was more trouble than the error."

Silas looked from the portrait to her and back. "You're famous here."

"I'm *furniture* here. That's worse. Furniture gets dusted." But her throat had gone tight, because there was something obscene about seeing yourself rendered in oil paint by a court you'd betrayed, honored by a king you'd robbed, smiling a smile you'd never worn. Homecoming as horror wasn't the burning-down-of-the-house kind. It was the discovering-that-the-house-kept-your-room-exactly-as-you-left-it kind, complete with the little clay bird you'd made at eleven, except now the bird was gilded and labeled and lying about you in gold leaf.

Odd's head appeared around the doorframe — the wight had crept in despite the prickle of consecrated ground, drawn by gossip like a moth to the only candle worth having. "Ooh. Is that you? You were *pretty*. No offense meant to the current arrangement of your face, which has more character."

"None taken. Character is what happens after the portrait."

* * *

They crossed the outer hall in the sighing dark, between sleeping alcoves where junior courtiers dozed in shifts even on the Feast night — someone had to mind the candles. Hazel counted the sleepers out of habit. Fourteen. Two would wake if a candle within thirty feet guttered out. She steered them wide.

The inner corridors were hers. Here the plan stopped being Ellery's map and became muscle memory: left at the cistern, past the scriptorium — she pressed two fingers to the door as they passed, an old habit, a scribe's benediction, *may your ink never freeze* — down the stair where the seventh step sang a faint note underfoot because Corvin had cut it thin, and Corvin again, four hundred years dead, still tripping the unwary.

Silas noticed. "You're humming."

"I'm not."

"You are. Under your breath. It's the same tune Tam was—" He stopped himself.

Hazel stood very still on the singing step. "Our mother sang it while she worked. I didn't know I was doing it." She started down again, faster. "The mountain does that. It keeps things. You think you've buried a song and then the walls hand it back to you at the worst possible moment, and you realize the mountain has been *keeping* it, the way it keeps everything, the way it kept my portrait, the way it keeps souls in lanterns—" Her voice cracked on the last word and she hated it, hated the crack, hated the mountain for hearing it.

Silas caught her elbow at the bottom of the stair. In the candlelight his face was pale and set, the brand at his collar showing a faint blue through the cloth — the storm sensing the mountain's heart the way a compass senses north. "We're going to get him," he said. Not *we'll try.* Not *if the wards allow.* Just the plain declarative sentence, and she loved him a little for refusing to hedge.

"And then what?" she asked, because she was a scribe and scribes read the fine print even in moments of tenderness. "When we have him. When we're running. Your storm, Silas. It's down here too. Your grandmother's storm, in the heart of this mountain, and the book says—"

"The book negotiates," he said. "It doesn't decide." But his hand had gone to his collar, and Hazel saw that he was afraid, and that he was carrying the fear the way she carried her guilt: competently, quietly, where it wouldn't slow the walking.

* * *

The Feast of Falling reached its deep hour, and the sighing of the sleepers changed.

Hazel felt it before she understood it — a pressure shift, like ears popping — and then the candles all along the corridor leaned. Every flame in a hundred yards of hallway bent sideways at once, toward the mountain's heart, as though inhaling. The harvested dreams were coming home. Somewhere below, the vaults were drinking.

"Now," she breathed. "While the wards are busy swallowing. Move."

They ran — softly, absurdly, a sprint conducted on the balls of the feet through a palace of sleepers. Past the Hall of Tithes where the year's collected memories hung in bundles like drying herbs. Past the nursery wing, where Hazel firmly did not look at the small beds. Down, always down, following warmth in the stone, following the pull of the brand on Silas's neck, which glowed brighter with every landing until it threw blue light across the walls like moonrise underwater.

At the last junction before the vault stairs, Hazel stopped so suddenly that Silas walked into her back.

The corridor ahead was blocked by a single figure: a harvester, awake, robed in grey, its hood full of drifting motes of light it had apparently paused to count. Between its fingers it turned a candle-end no bigger than a thumb — a scrap-memory, a fragment, the sort of thing too small to bother rendering properly.

The harvester looked up. Light moved inside its hood like fish under ice.

Hazel's mind, trained in three years of forgery and four years of bureaucratic survival, produced exactly one asset: her name. She straightened, lifted her chin, and put the entire weight of the second rank into her voice.

"You're counting wrong," she said coldly. "Motes per candle should be tallied in sevens, not fives. Second-rank protocol. Honestly, has no one trained you?"

The harvester went very still. Then it bowed — a deep, servile bow, the bow of a servant to a superior — and shuffled aside, and stood with its head down while three thieves walked past it into the deepest dark of the mountain, one of whom was officially furniture.

Only when the vault stairs opened beneath them did Hazel let out a breath that shook.

"Gentle humor," Silas murmured. "As a lockpick."

"Shut up and descend."

Below them, faint through miles of stone, a soul was humming its mother's song. Above them, the candles leaned and drank. And somewhere between the two, in the dark of the vault stairs, Hazel's own handwriting waited on ledgers she had sworn never to read again — because the collection records for the long winter, every name, every tithe, every family sealed behind painted doors, bore a signature she knew better than her own heartbeat.

Her own. Or one indistinguishable from it.

The stairs went down, and down, and the mountain kept everything.
