## 22. The Soul-Vaults

The door to the soul-vaults was not locked.

Hazel had spent three days of her life — three days she would never get back, three days that could have been spent eating or sleeping or kissing Silas Crane, though she hadn't known that last one was on the table yet — memorizing the vault's security protocols from the archive copies. Triple-warded doors. Blood-seals. A rotation of jailer-wights who changed watch at the hour of the deep bell. She had drawn diagrams. She had planned contingencies for eleven separate failure points.

The door was ajar.

"It's *ajar*," she whispered, as if saying it louder might make it less insulting.

"Maybe they're expecting us," Odd said cheerfully. The grave-wight had folded himself down to roughly half his usual size to fit through the servant corridors, and now looked like a large, lumpy shadow wearing a hat made of other shadows. "Courts do love an ambush. Very traditional."

"They're asleep," Silas said quietly. He had one hand flat against the stone, and Hazel saw the faint blue flicker under his skin where the storm lived now, coiled and waiting. "All of them. The whole mountain. Can't you feel it? It's like... standing inside a held breath."

She could feel it, actually. That was the worst part. She'd grown up in this mountain's shadow, and she knew its rhythms the way a farmer knows weather: the Feast of Falling, when the court lay down in their finery and slept, and the harvest came up through the floors like sap. Ten thousand souls dreaming, and every dream siphoned upward to feed the winter.

And somewhere below all that, in a room she had signed papers about and never once been allowed to see, Tam.

"Eleven contingencies," she muttered, and pushed the door open.

* * *

The stairs went down farther than stairs should. That was the first thing. The second thing was the light.

It wasn't torchlight. It wasn't candlelight, though Greyharbor had ruined her for candles forever — she'd never again be able to look at one without calculating how many memories it cost. This light came from above, from the ceiling of the vast cavern at the stair's foot, and when Hazel stepped off the last step and looked up, she stopped breathing.

Souls hung from the dark like lanterns.

Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. Each one a soft, shifting glow in its own color — amber and rose and green-gold and one extraordinary violet far off to the left — suspended on nothing, drifting in slow, gentle orbits, like a night sky someone had bottled and hung upside down. They pulsed faintly, in and out, in and out, and Hazel realized with a lurch that the rhythm was breathing. Or something like it. The nearest lantern glowed warmest when it drifted close to its neighbors and dimmed when it drifted away, and she thought: *they're lonely.* She thought: *they've been lonely for years.*

"Oh," said Odd softly. For once, the wight had nothing funny to say. His hat-shadows drooped. "Oh, that's *dreadful*. And lovely. Both at once. Like a wedding I attended in 1304."

Silas put his hand on her shoulder. He didn't say anything. He didn't need to; his hand said *I'm here*, which was, Hazel was learning, most of what love actually consisted of. Not the grand declarations. The hand on the shoulder while you looked at ten thousand stolen souls and did arithmetic you couldn't finish.

"I filed requisitions for this room," she said. Her voice came out strange and flat. "Wax allocations. Lantern-oil metaphors in the inventory ledgers. I wrote *storage, miscellaneous luminous, qty. 10,000-ish* in my own handwriting and I didn't—" Her throat closed. "I didn't let myself think about what the luminous things were."

"You were nineteen and starving," Silas said.

"I was a *scribe*. Words are supposed to be my whole job. Knowing what they mean." She wiped her face with the heel of her hand, briskly, the way you'd shoo a fly. "Come on. Vault records say the older souls are kept deeper. Tam's only been here four years. He'll be near the entrance tiers."

"How will you know him?" Odd asked. "Among all these?"

Hazel looked up at the drifting lights, and something in her chest that had been clenched for four years finally, finally loosened its grip enough to answer.

"He'll be humming," she said.

* * *

Finding Tam took two hours, and it was the strangest two hours of Hazel's life, which was saying something, because the previous week had included a rooftop chase, a talking skeleton, and the moment Silas Crane had turned lightning into a precision instrument and she'd forgotten how knees worked.

They moved along the tiers of hanging souls, and Hazel did what she'd done her whole life: she read. Souls, it turned out, were legible if you knew the script. Court training covered soul-script in the third year — purely theoretical, they'd said, purely administrative — and now every hour of those dry lectures paid itself back with interest. The amber ones near the entrance hummed in old farmer's cadences; she could pick out fragments of planting songs, wedding toasts, a man who appeared to have spent forty years of afterlife mentally re-plowing the same stubborn field. A green-gold soul nearby carried a child's rhythm, quick and skipping, and Hazel had to stop and press her fist to her mouth for a moment before she could keep walking.

"They don't hurt?" Silas asked quietly. "Being here?"

"They're asleep. Mostly." She kept her voice level by main force. "The court calls it *preservation*. The dreams get harvested but the core stays intact. In theory." She touched the edge of a ledger-knowledge she'd never wanted. "In theory they could be returned. If the body's still living. If it's been less than..." She stopped.

"Less than what?"

"The contract I signed said seven years." She stared straight ahead. "Tam's got three left. After that the soul starts to... fray. Unravel into the general light. They use the frayed ones for the big candles. The ones that burn in the throne hall."

Nobody said anything for a while. Above them, ten thousand souls breathed in and out.

Then, drifting around a rib of rock that jutted from the cavern wall like the mountain's own collarbone, Hazel heard it.

Humming.

Faint. Thread-thin. Rising out of the general murmur of sleeping souls the way one voice rises out of a crowd — and she knew it instantly, the way you know your own name, the way you know the floor of your childhood house by the creak of the third board. It was a lullaby. Their mother's lullaby, the one about the fox who wanted to be the moon, the one their mother had sung off-key on purpose because Tam laughed every time she hit the wrong note, and so she hit the wrong note *every time*, deliberately, a whole song built out of love disguised as mistakes.

"TAM!" 

The word tore out of her before she could stop it, loud in the silence, and both Silas and Odd grabbed her arms, but she barely felt them. Because there he was.

He hung a little apart from the others, the way he'd always stood a little apart from crowds, watching. His soul was dimmer than the rest — four years of harvesting had taken their toll, worn him thin as winter ice over a stream — and he flickered. Not steadily. In little stutters, like a candle in a draft, like a boy trying to stay awake past bedtime. But the color was right. Warm brown-gold, the color of bread crust, of hazelnuts, of the exact light through the kitchen window on the morning their mother made pancakes. And the humming went on and on, patient, faithful, the fox-and-the-moon song cycling through him like a heartbeat he refused to put down.

"He stayed," Hazel whispered. "Four years and he *stayed*. He could have frayed out and stopped hurting and he *stayed* and he kept singing—"

"Hazel." Silas's voice, very gentle. "Slowly. Remember what Ellery said about the wards."

But she was already moving, already reaching up, already stretching her fingers toward that warm brown-gold light, and some part of her — the part that had survived a starvation winter, the part that forged signatures and sold souls and did whatever ugly arithmetic the world demanded — that part was screaming *wait, wait, check the ward-lines first, you wrote the protocols yourself, you KNOW*—

And the rest of her, the nine-year-old who used to ride on Tam's shoulders, said: *it's my brother.*

Her fingertips touched light.

* * *

It was warm.

That was the first thing, the thing she would remember afterward when everything else had gone to smoke: it was *warm*, warmer than any fire, warmer than the kitchen window light, and it knew her. The soul flared under her touch — brown-gold blazing up toward white — and the humming broke off mid-verse, and then a voice that was not quite a voice, that was more like a memory of a voice, said:

"*Hazey?*"

Four years. Four years of guilt ground down into competence, four years of forged seals and steady hands and never letting herself cry where anyone could see, and it all came apart in one syllable. "Tam," she sobbed. "Tam, I'm here, I'm sorry, I'm so sorry, I came to take you home, I've come to take you *home*—"

"*You shouldn't have,*" the light said, and even flickering, even worn thin, even after everything, her brother's soul managed to sound exactly like a teenage boy being embarrassed by his sister in public. "*Hazey. You have to go. It knows you're here now.*"

"What knows—"

The ward woke.

Hazel felt it before she heard it — a shudder through the stone, deep under her feet, like the mountain clearing its throat. Then light: cold blue lines igniting across the cavern floor, racing outward from beneath Tam's lantern in branching veins, script crawling along them faster than she could read, and she *tried* to read it, scribe to the bone, even now, even sobbing — *intrusion, intrusion, tier seven, lineage trace active, lineage trace active*—

"No no no no—" She snatched her hand back. Too late. The blue lines had already reached her feet, curled up around her ankles like cold water, and she felt them *take her measure*: her blood, her name, the contract she'd signed, cataloguing her in three heartbeats. "It's tracing me. Silas, it's tracing my *lineage*, it'll find the contract, it'll wake the—"

The alarm rolled.

There was no better word for it. Sound came up through the mountain like a bell the size of the sky being struck once, and then again, and then again, each toll deeper than the last, and with every toll the sleeping souls overhead stirred. Lights flickered on in waves, tier after tier, ten thousand lanterns waking confused and afraid, and the cavern that had been a held breath became a held scream.

"Odd!" Silas had his arm around her waist — when had he gotten there? — hauling her back from the ward-lines. "Is there a way out that isn't the way we came?"

"There is always a way out that isn't the way you came," Odd said, and for the first time since Hazel had met him, the chatty grave-wight sounded dead serious. "Question is whether it's a way out or a way *further down*. In my professional experience those are frequently the same hole."

"Tam." Hazel twisted back toward the lantern-soul, which was straining on its invisible tether, flickering hard, the lullaby gone silent. "Tam, listen to me. Three days. I have a key, I have a plan, I'm coming back for you, I swear it on Mom's name—"

"*Go,*" her brother's soul said, and the warmth of it wrapped around her one last time, brief as a hug. "*Take care of her, storm-man. She forgets to eat.*"

"I noticed," Silas said.

Then the far end of the cavern lit up — not blue but gold, torchlight, many torches, and the sound of boots, dozens of boots, coming down the processional stair that Hazel had never been allowed to use and now understood why. Jailer-wights. Honor guard. And behind them, descending with terrible unhurried calm, a figure in frost-livery whose crown caught the light of ten thousand frightened souls.

"Up," Silas said. "NOW."

* * *

They ran.

Odd led, uncoiling back to full unsettling height and flowing along passages Hazel hadn't known existed — service channels, drainage ways, the mountain's secret plumbing, because every palace has plumbing and nobody guards the plumbing. Behind them the bell tolled and tolled, and the King's voice rolled through the stone itself, not shouting, never shouting, just *present*, everywhere, reasonable as ever:

"*Thieves in the vaults. Thieves among the lanterns. Come back to me, little scribe, and we'll discuss your contract like civilized people.*"

"Don't listen," Silas gasped, keeping pace beside her, his hand finding hers in the dark. Under his skin the storm was flickering faster now, agitated, and the walls sweated frost wherever they passed. "It's a recording. It's a trick. It's—"

"It's my handwriting on every door we pass," Hazel said bitterly. "I know."

They burst out of a maintenance hatch into a corridor Hazel did know — the Gallery of Honored Servants, her own painted portrait smirking down at her from the wall, twenty-one years old and already damned — and skidded to a stop, because the corridor ahead was full of frost-riders.

And the corridor behind them was filling with blue ward-light.

Trapped. Properly, textbook trapped. Somewhere in her head, Instructor Vess's voice recited: *a cornered agent does not fight; a cornered agent negotiates.* Hazel opened her mouth to try.

Silas stepped in front of her.

The storm came off him like heat off a forge — not loose, not wild, but *ready*, and the frost-riders, to their considerable credit, took a collective step backward. Lightning crawled along the gallery floor and lit every portrait, including hers, including the Mountain King's enormous official likeness, from below, the way children light their faces with torches to tell ghost stories.

"New plan," Silas said. "Anybody got one?"

Above them, muffled by a hundred feet of stone, the bell tolled again — and this time, Hazel, scribe to the bone, heard what was woven into the toll. Not just alarm. *Summons.* The court was waking early. All of it. The Feast of Falling was over, and somewhere above, a king in a crown of frozen breath was getting dressed to receive guests.

"Actually," Hazel said, grabbing Silas's sleeve, "yes. Follow me. And whatever happens — whatever you hear, whatever you see — do *not* let go of my hand."

"Why? What's down there?"

"The one place in this mountain," she said, already running, "where even the King can't follow."
