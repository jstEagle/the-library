## 24. The King's Arithmetic

They did not throw Silas into a cell.

That was the first thing he noticed, and the thing that frightened him most. Cells were for people the court wanted to forget. He was walked — gently, almost ceremonially — up through the mountain's bones by two frost-riders who had surrendered their spears at a doorway and now escorted him like ushers at a wedding neither of them approved of. The corridors grew warmer as they climbed, which felt like an insult. He had expected the under-court to be cold. It was not. It was *cozy*, in the way a hearth is cozy without asking what the fire is burning.

"Where are you taking me?" he asked for the third time.

The taller rider glanced at him with something that might have been pity, if pity were allowed on duty. "The King likes to talk," she said. "He talks to everyone. It's how he wins."

"That's reassuring."

"It isn't meant to be," she said, and opened the door.

* * *

The room at the top of the mountain was small.

Silas had imagined a throne hall — vaulted, echoing, cruel. Instead there was a round chamber with a low ceiling of living stone, a table set for tea, and one window cut into the rock that looked out onto nothing but white. Snow, endless snow, falling upward past the glass in slow spirals, as though the mountain were exhaling winter rather than wearing it.

The Mountain King sat at the table pouring tea.

He looked, Silas thought wildly, like somebody's grandfather. A broad, comfortable man in grey robes, silver-bearded, with a crown of frozen breath hovering above his brow — a circlet of mist that never melted, curling and reforming with each slow exhalation. His hands were steady. His eyes were kind. That was the worst part. Kindness radiated off him like heat off a stove, and every instinct Silas had learned in ten years of hiding told him that things which seemed warmest were often the ones eating something.

"You must be Crane," the King said. "Sit. The tea is willowbark and honey. I know you valley folk distrust anything sweeter than survival, but indulge me."

"I'm not hungry."

"You're not. But your hands are shaking, and holding a cup gives them somewhere to be." The King slid a cup across the table anyway. "I find that's most of hospitality, really. Giving shaking hands somewhere to be."

Silas sat. He didn't drink. But he held the cup, because his hands needed it, and hated himself a little for how right the man was.

"You have Hazel Vane," Silas said. "And Tam's soul. And me. You don't need to soften me up before whatever this is."

"Soften you." The King smiled, and the frozen breath above his brow curled like a question mark. "Young man, I am going to show you the truth. Truth doesn't need softening. It needs *witnessing*. There's a difference, and everyone who hates me has spent their whole life refusing to learn it." He turned his chair toward the window. "Come look."

Silas stayed seated. "I've seen snow."

"Not from here." Something in the King's voice shifted — not menace, exactly. Weariness. Ancient, geological weariness, the kind that gets pressed into stone and comes out as strata. "Ten thousand years I have watched it fall. Come and see what I see, or sit there and keep your ignorance like a candle you're afraid to spend. Either way, you'll listen. Nobody ever fails to listen. It's the one law that holds everywhere."

Silas got up. His legs decided before his pride could object.

* * *

The window was deeper than it looked.

That was the only way he could describe it afterward. He leaned toward the glass and the glass was not glass — or not only glass — and suddenly he was looking down, down through the mountain itself, through chambers and vaults and veins of frozen fire, down past the soul-vaults where lanterns hung in their thousands, down to a floor of darkness that was not stone.

Something moved in it.

It did not move the way animals move, or weather moves, or even the way nightmares move. It moved the way *hunger* moves when hunger has been waiting so long it has forgotten it ever wanted anything specific. It was vast in a direction Silas's eyes refused to report accurately. When he tried to measure it, his mind offered him instead the memory of the last long winter — the sound of his own stomach, the neighbors' doors painted shut, the silence of a village deciding who would eat.

"That," the King said quietly, "is why the mountain exists."

Silas stepped back from the window. His shoulder blades found the wall and pressed against it as if the wall might vouch for him.

"What is it?"

"No one knows. Not its name — it may not have one. Not its age. The first storm-callers found it sleeping when the world was young and warm, and they understood, the way you understand a wolf track outside the sheep pen, that it would wake eventually and rise, and everything above it — every field, every valley, every bee-kept hillside — would go into it the way a log goes into a fire." The King poured himself tea now, unhurried. "So we built a lid. The mountain is the lid. And a lid, Master Crane, requires weight."

"The souls." Silas's voice came out flat. "You burn them to hold the lid down."

"Burn is such a blunt word. We *spend* them. Memory, dream, warmth, will — all of it goes into the deep workings, all of it becomes pressure against that—" he nodded at the window, "—that appetite. Ten thousand winters I have kept the long cold turning, because a cold world with people in it is infinitely preferable to a warm world with none. Every soul in my vaults is a brick. Every harvest is mortar. I do not enjoy it." He said it simply, without performance, and that was the horror: he clearly didn't. "But arithmetic does not require enjoyment. Only accuracy."

"And the valley." Silas's mouth had gone dry. "The magic-dead valley. That's yours too."

"A pressure valve. A place where the lid is thin and quiet, where stray power can pool and dissipate harmlessly. Your bees, Master Crane. Your mirrors. Your ten years of peace." The King looked at him over the rim of his cup. "You thought you escaped me. In truth, I sheltered you. The exile was a sentence; the valley was a mercy. I have been paying for your grandmother's defiance out of my own stores for a decade, and I have never once sent riders across that border. Ask yourself why."

Because the prophecy, some traitorous part of Silas whispered. Because the book. Because you need him. But another part — the part that had tended hives through ten safe springs — heard *mercy* and wanted it to be true so badly it ached.

"Why tell me this?" Silas managed. "Why show me the window? What do you want?"

"Your consent."

The word landed in the room like a dropped bell.

"My grandmother wouldn't give it," Silas said slowly. "That's why she died."

"Yes." No flinch. No apology. "Ellery wrote her pretty book about it. Refusal makes wonderful poetry and terrible policy. She died, her storm went into my keeping, and the lid held another century. That is the sum of her martyrdom: one dead woman, one imprisoned storm, and ten thousand winters of everyone else alive." He set down his cup. "I am offering you the same choice, but I am offering it honestly, which is more than fate ever does. Your storm is in my mountain. Legally, honorably mine — taken in lawful judgment from a condemned traitor. I will return it to you freely. Full inheritance. You will walk out of here the most powerful storm-caller in a thousand years."

"In exchange for?"

"Hazel Vane stays. Her crime stands — theft from the soul-vaults, forgery of court seal, attempted extraction of a sealed asset. The law is clear and I do not bend it, because the day I bend it for sympathy is the day the whole charter rots." The King's voice remained gentle, reasonable, terrible. "And the asset — her brother — remains in the vaults, where he keeps the lid weighted along with the rest. You leave. You take your storm. You go back to your bees, and no one follows you, and the world above goes on believing spring is a right rather than a subsidy."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I keep everything. The storm, the scribe, the soul, and you. And the lid holds a little longer on someone else's warmth." The King spread his hands. "I want to be very clear, because everyone always thinks there's a third door: there is no option in which nobody pays. There is only the question of *who*, and *how much*, and whether the payer consents. I built a kingdom on consent because consent is the only currency that doesn't devalue. Your grandmother's refusal cost me dearly. Don't repeat her mistake out of sentiment. Sentiment is how lids fail."

Silas looked at the window. Down through the mountain. At the dark that dreamed of eating.

"You say 'we,'" he said. "The first storm-callers. Built the lid. Were you one of them?"

For the first time, the Mountain King paused.

The pause lasted perhaps two heartbeats. In it, Silas watched something move behind the kind eyes — not guilt, exactly. Something older and sadder. The expression of a man looking at a photograph of himself from before.

"I was present at the founding," the King said at last. "Yes."

"You don't look old enough."

"Neither does the winter," said the King, "and yet."

* * *

They gave him a room, not a cell. A good room, with a real bed and a brazier and a window that faced inward, at rock, so there was nothing to look at and nothing to look in. The door was not locked. It did not need to be. The mountain was the lock.

Silas sat on the bed and did the arithmetic himself, because if he let the King do it, the King would win.

One soul = one brick. Thousands of bricks = one lid. One lid = one world, unfrozen, un-eaten. Hazel = one brick. Tam = one brick. Isolde — the princess whose name he'd heard whispered by the jailer-wights with a strange tenderness — presumably the cornerstone.

Against that: one woman who had sold her brother to survive and had spent every day since trying to buy him back. One grave-wight who liked gossip. One ferrywoman who owed a debt. One valley full of people who painted their doors and slept through winters they believed were weather.

The math was clear. The math was *airtight*. That was the trap of it — the King hadn't lied to him, hadn't needed to. He'd just held up the ledger and let Silas read it, and the ledger balanced, and balanced ledgers are the most seductive objects in any world.

His hands were shaking again. No cup to hold.

"Grandmother," he said aloud to the empty room, "you absolute mule. What did you know that he doesn't?"

The brazier crackled. Somewhere far below, faint as a heartbeat through stone, something turned over in its sleep.

And then — softly, from inside the wall, as though the mountain itself had cleared its throat — came a knock. Three knocks. Pause. Two knocks. A rhythm Silas didn't recognize, but someone clearly expected him to.

The stone beside the headboard bulged, gently, like dough rising. A seam appeared. Through it, a woman's voice, low and dry and amused:

"Well," said the voice of the King's daughter. "You're harder to convince than the last forty storm-callers. Good. I hate repeating myself, and I've been rehearsing this speech for nine thousand years. Are you decent? More importantly — are you *listening*? Because the King showed you his arithmetic, and I've come to show you where he hides the remainder."

The seam in the wall widened a hand's width, and a pair of ancient, patient, rain-colored eyes looked out at him from the dark.
