## 25. Cells of Salt

The cell was made of salt.

Hazel discovered this the hard way, by licking the wall in the dark, because she was a scribe and scribes tested their materials. It tasted of old tears and something mineral and patient, like the mountain itself had wept for so long it had crystallized. The salt was packed into blocks fitted without mortar, and when she pressed her palm against it, her skin came away stinging and faintly dry, as though the wall had drunk a little of her.

She sat on the floor — there was no bed, only a shelf of the same salt worn smooth by other prisoners' despair — and took inventory, because inventory was what she did when everything else failed. One runaway scribe. One stolen key hidden in the lining of her boot, which they had somehow not found, or perhaps had found and decided she was more useful believing she still had it. Zero allies. One storm-caller somewhere else in this mountain, presumably also in a cell, presumably alive, because the Mountain King did not seem like a king who wasted assets.

Her hands were shaking. She folded them in her lap and made them stop, mostly.

The door had no lock. It didn't need one; the blocks were eight feet thick, and besides, Hazel suspected that anyone who tried to scratch their way out would simply be absorbed, slowly, into the seasoning. Instead the door simply opened when it wished to, which was how she learned that the jailer-wight existed.

It came in sideways, the way water comes through a bad window seal. It was tall and thin and gray as ash, with a face like a thumbprint in dough — features suggested rather than committed to — and it wore a ring of keys at its belt that jingled even when it stood perfectly still, which it usually did not, preferring instead to seep.

"Prisoner Hazel," it said. Its voice was like pages turning underwater. "You are awake. Good. The King dislikes conducting business with sleeping persons. He says it feels like theft."

"I'm not conducting any business," Hazel said. "I'm a prisoner."

"You are a prisoner *currently*," the wight agreed, settling into the corner opposite her, folding up like a carpenter's rule. "Business is a matter of time. Everything down here is a matter of time. Time and salt." It tilted its featureless head. "You were Court Scribe."

It wasn't a question, but Hazel treated it like one anyway. "I was a girl who copied ledgers."

"You were Court Scribe," the wight repeated, with the mild insistence of someone correcting a misfiled document. "I have been jailer here for six hundred years. I remember your predecessor. I remember Ellery." Something moved across its doughy face that might, on a face with more commitment, have been a smile. "You hold your pen the same way. Like it might bite you, and you might let it."

Hazel said nothing. Her heart was doing something embarrassing.

"The King will make you an offer eventually," the wight went on. "He makes everyone offers. That is how he rules. Not with fear — fear is crude, fear is for frost-riders — but with arithmetic. Everyone has a number. A price at which their duty and their desire shake hands." It unfolded one long finger. "But I am not the King. I am only the jailer. And I have watched ten thousand prisoners sit where you sit, and I will tell you what I told none of them, because none of them asked nicely."

Hazel swallowed. "What did you tell them?"

"That the cells of salt do not hold bodies," the wight said. "They hold *yes*. Every day you spend here, the salt takes a little of your no. You will wake one morning and find you have agreed to things. You will not remember agreeing. That is how the court fills its offices. Nobody chooses forever. Forever is chosen for them, one grain at a time."

The silence stretched. Somewhere far above, muffled through tons of stone, Hazel could hear music — the Feast of Falling continuing without them, the whole court dreaming its harvest-dreams while two thieves rotted in the walls.

"My brother," Hazel said quietly. "Tam. Is he—"

"In the vaults. Lantern four hundred and twelve." The wight said it without cruelty, the way you'd read out a shelf location. "He hums. Most souls flicker or scream or go quiet. Yours hums. It is very annoying. I have complained about it for three years."

Despite everything — despite the salt and the dark and the arithmetic closing in — Hazel laughed. It came out cracked and wet, but it was a laugh.

"You complained about my brother's soul."

"To the Office of Auditory Nuisances. Twice." The wight produced, from somewhere inside its gray robes, a small ledger. "Which brings me to why I am here. You are a scribe. I am a jailer. Jails generate paperwork. Six hundred years of it. And the last person who could draft a proper writ of transfer died before your grandmother was born." It set the ledger on the salt floor between them with a soft, final tap. "I have a proposition."

"I don't make deals with jailers."

"No," the wight agreed. "You make deals with kings, and look where it got you. With me, at least, the terms will be written honestly. I am constitutionally incapable of dishonest paperwork. It gives me hives." It gestured at its featureless skin. "Metaphorically."

Hazel looked at the ledger. Then at the door that opened when it wished. Then at her own hands, which had stopped shaking, because they wanted the pen. That was the terrible truth of her, the thing she'd never said aloud: give her a contract and a crisis, and her hands went quiet. Guilt wore competence like a coat, and right now the coat fit.

"What's the proposition?" she asked.

"The storm-caller," the wight said. "Silas Crane. The King intends to offer him his grandmother's storm in exchange for you. This is not secret; the King believes all his bargains are generous and therefore publicizable. But the King's offers always have a second page. The storm he offers is real, but the freedom that comes with it is not. Silas Crane will accept, and he will spend eternity holding up winter thinking he chose it, and you will spend eternity in these walls watching him believe it." The wight folded its fingers together. "Unless the paperwork changes."

"Changes how?"

"Contracts require breach clauses. Every honest contract does. What happens if a party fails? What happens if terms are broken? The King's contracts never include them, because the King never intends to fail, and because a breach clause is a door, and the King hates doors." The wight leaned forward, and its thumbprint face seemed, briefly, almost kind. "Draft me a proper contract, Prisoner Hazel. Your service as Court Scribe — true service, binding, forever, mine to command — in exchange for the storm-caller's release. Full terms. Full clauses. Including what happens when *you* break it."

"And why would I ever break it?"

"You won't," the wight said. "For a hundred years, perhaps two. But you are the sort of person who sold her brother's soul to survive a winter and has regretted it every single day since, and people like you always break eventually. People like you are *made* of breaking. I want the breach clause drafted correctly when you do." It slid the ledger toward her. "Because when you break it, Prisoner Hazel, I intend to be standing somewhere else entirely."

Hazel stared at it. "You're planning your own escape."

"I am planning my *retirement*," the wight corrected. "There is a difference. Escapes are dramatic. Retirements are properly documented." It rose, unfolding upward like smoke deciding to be tall again. "The pen is in the drawer. The ink is your own blood; it always is, down here. Take your time. The salt is patient, but I find I am not."

* * *

Three corridors over and one level down, Silas Crane was learning that salt cells had opinions about storms.

His brand ached. That was the first thing — a deep, low throb under his collarbone, like a bruise on the bone itself, and every hour it grew louder, until he could feel the storm beneath the mountain answering it. Call and response. His grandmother's prison, humming to the last of her line through six feet of consecrated salt, and the salt doing its best to keep the conversation polite.

He'd tried talking to it. That was what you did with bees, and bees were the closest thing to weather he'd managed in seven years. *Easy,* he thought at the ache. *Easy now. I'm here.* The brand flared once, warm as a hand on his chest, and settled.

Then the door opened, and the King's daughter came in.

He knew her immediately, though no one had told him. She was beautiful the way frost on a window is beautiful — precise, patterned, and cold all the way through — except her eyes, which were brown. Warm, ordinary, human brown, set in a face that had forgotten everything else. She carried a candle that burned with a pale, sweet-smelling flame, and when she set it on the floor between them, Silas realized with a lurch that the flame was *singing*, faintly, a half-remembered lullaby.

A soul-candle. Someone's harvested memory, burning to light her way.

"Isolde," she said. "That was my name. I'm told it still is." She sat down across from him with the unhurried grace of someone who had nowhere to be for ten thousand years. "You're the storm-caller. You have her chin. The grandmother. I watched them bring her down here, a hundred years ago. She spat at the King. Nobody spits at the King. I liked her immediately."

Silas pulled his collar higher, useless gesture. "Did he send you?"

"He doesn't know I'm here. Or he knows and permits it, which down here is the same thing and neither is safe." Isolde turned the soul-candle idly, and the singing shifted keys. "I'm going to tell you something, and then you're going to tell me whether you're the man the prophecy wants or the man the valley made, because those are different men, and I've met both kinds."

"I'm a beekeeper," Silas said.

Isolde smiled, and for a moment the frost fell away from her face entirely, and she looked like what she was: a girl who remembered rain. "Good answer. The prophecy-men always lead with destiny. Beekeepers lead with bees." She leaned closer. "Here is my something. My father — the King, the crown of frozen breath, all of it — was not always the King. He was the first storm-caller. Did you know that?"

Silas's mouth went dry. "Ellery's book says—"

"Ellery's book says what Ellery needed it to say. She was cleverer than all of us." Isolde's voice dropped. "He fled down here when the deep thing first stirred. He built the winter as a shield. He took the first soul — mine — to light the first candle, and he has been taking them ever since, one winter at a time, telling himself each one is necessary. And maybe some of them were. That's the horror of it, storm-caller. The arithmetic is *real*. The mountain does hold the deep thing down. Every soul burned is a wall held up. He'll show you. He showed me, ten thousand winters ago, and I believed him for nine thousand of them."

"And now?"

"Now I have watched enough winters fall to know the difference between a wall and a cage." She reached into the folds of her gown and drew out something small and black and heavy: a key, wrought in a style older than anything Hazel carried, older than the vaults themselves. The master-key. It seemed to drink the candlelight. "This opens everything. The vaults. The storm room. The little door behind the throne that even the jailer-wight has forgotten." She pressed it into his bound hands, and it was cold, cold as the space between stars. "And I can tell you where your grandmother's storm sleeps, because I helped chain it. I was human, then. I cried while we worked. My father thanked me for being brave."

Silas's throat worked. "Why? Why give me this?"

"Because you're going to be offered a choice soon," Isolde said. "My father will be kind about it. That's the worst part. He'll be reasonable, and fair, and honorable, and every word will be true, and if you take his bargain the world above freezes a little slower and nothing ever, ever changes." Her brown eyes held his, and in them he saw ten thousand winters stacked like rings in a tree. "I remember rain, Silas Crane. I remember what it sounded like on leaves. I have been frost for so long that remembering is the only rebellion I have left. So I am spending it on you."

She stood, smooth as ice forming, and retrieved her candle. At the door she paused.

"One more thing. The jailer-wight. Gray, chatty, complains about humming?" She glanced back, and her smile was small and fierce. "Six hundred years, and it has never once opened a cell early. If it visits you, ask yourself what it wants. Wights don't do kindness. They do *transactions*." 

The door closed behind her. The salt drank the silence.

Silas sat alone with the master-key in his lap, heavier than iron had any right to be, and listened to his brand hum against the stone — his grandmother's storm, calling to him through the walls, patient as weather.

Somewhere above, the Feast of Falling played on. Somewhere below, the deep thing slept. And somewhere between, in a cell of salt, Hazel was dipping a pen in her own blood, writing *forever* in a hand that shook only a little — while three floors down, her jailer counted the hours until a contract broke, and smiled its unfinished smile.

Two sacrifices, converging in the dark like rivers under ice.

Neither of them knew yet what the other had paid.

Only the salt heard both, and the salt kept count.
