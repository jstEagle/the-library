## 26. The Princess Who Remembered Rain

Isolde had learned, over ten thousand winters, that the trick to being forgotten was to be useful.

She carried the salt-cell keys. She trimmed the wicks in the memory-candles that lined the King's private corridor. She poured his wine at the Feast of Falling and stood at his shoulder while he slept, because a king who dreams his harvest wants someone watching when he wakes weeping and pretending he hasn't. Nobody guards against the daughter. The daughter is furniture. The daughter is the crown's shadow, and shadows are never searched.

So when the jailer-wight came shuffling down the salt corridor with his ring of little keys and his face like a wet hymnal, Isolde was already standing at the cell door, already holding a tray of bread and thin soup, already wearing the expression she had practiced for ten millennia: mild, dutiful, faintly bored.

"His Majesty's mercy," she said. "The storm-caller is to be fed."

The wight peered at her. Wights see poorly in the bright sense and well in the dark one, and in the dark sense Isolde glowed like a banked coal — the first soul ever taken, the oldest flame in the mountain, and even the dead gave her a wide, respectful chill.

"The King didn't say—"

"The King doesn't say most of what I do," Isolde said pleasantly. "That's rather the arrangement."

He grumbled, but he had ledgers to falsify elsewhere, and a princess with a soup tray is a difficult thing to argue with when you cannot remember whether you were ever allowed to argue with princesses. He shuffled off. Isolde waited until his footsteps dissolved into the mountain's slow heartbeat — that vast, patient *thrum* beneath everything, the sound of the shield holding, the sound of the deep thing turning over in its sleep.

Then she opened the cell.

The storm-caller was sitting against the wall with his knees drawn up, and he looked, she thought, exactly like what he was: a man who had been handed his inheritance in the form of a bill. His brand was still faintly visible at his collar, a pale weal like an old scar, and when she entered he flinched from her the way people flinch from cold water.

"You're the daughter," he said.

"I'm the daughter." She set the tray down between them and sat, folding her hands, because the first rule of smuggling mercy out of a fortress is to look like you have all the time in the world. "Isolde. I'd say it's a pleasure, but we're in a salt cell, and you've just refused my father's best offer, so let's skip the pleasantries and get to the interesting part."

"Which is?"

"Which is that you looked at the Mountain King wearing his crown of frozen breath, in his hall of ten thousand candles, and you said no." Her voice did something she hadn't intended — warmed, quickened. "Do you know how long it's been since anyone said no to him in that hall? I've watched kings kneel there. I've watched heroes weep. I watched your grandmother stand in that same spot, and she said no too, and they took her storm for it and her head besides."

Silas went very still. "You knew her."

"I knew *of* her. I know of everyone now. That's what ten thousand winters does — it turns you into an archive." She smiled, and it was an old, tired smile, worn smooth as a river stone. "Maerlin Crane. Storm-caller of the western ridges. She used to make it rain in summer, real rain, warm rain. I remember rain." She said it simply, the way other people say *I remember my wedding day.* "That's the first thing they took from me, you know. Not my soul — my soul they took second. First they took the weather. I was fifteen, and the first long winter came down, and my father went beneath the mountain to hide from the hunger, and he took the sky with him. I haven't felt rain in ten thousand years. Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

"No," Silas said honestly.

"Good. Honest. My father would have said *yes, Highness, of course, Highness* and understood nothing." She leaned forward. "I'm telling you that I am the oldest prisoner in this mountain, that I have watched the Feast of Falling ten thousand times, that I have poured wine for my father while he slept the sleep of the harvest, and that I have decided — quite recently, quite firmly, and with no intention of revisiting the decision — that it ends. Even if it ends me."

* * *

He stared at her for a long moment. In the salt-cell light — which was no light at all, only the faint grey seep of the mountain's pulse — his face did the arithmetic she'd seen a thousand faces do in that hall: *what does this cost, what does this cost, what does this cost.*

"That thing below," he said carefully. "The one the shield holds back. My father — the King said—"

"The King said that every soul burned is a wall held up, and that if you refuse, everyone freezes." Isolde waved a hand. "And it's true. That's the horror of him. He's never lied to anyone. He doesn't need to. He just shows you the arithmetic and lets you do the coward's math yourself." She paused. "But he leaves things out. He's very good at leaving things out. He left out that the wall was never meant to be permanent. He left out that the hunger grows *fed* on fear as much as on souls — that ten thousand years of hiding have made it stronger, not weaker. He left out that there was once a plan. There were storm-callers, in the old days, whose whole craft was the fighting of that thing. My father was one." Her mouth twisted. "He was the best of them. And then he looked into the dark one time too long, and he decided that living behind a wall of other people's souls was the same as winning."

"You want me to fight it."

"I want you to *have the choice.*" She said it with sudden fierceness, and the candles in the corridor beyond guttered, because Isolde's feelings had always leaked; a soul kept in a jar for ten millennia develops cracks. "That's all anyone has ever asked of him and been denied. Not victory — no one can promise you victory. Choice. He took the sky's decision away from everyone, including the sky. I would like, before I end, to put it back."

Silas was quiet for a while. Somewhere above them, muffled by a hundred fathoms of stone, the Feast of Falling went on — the court asleep, dreaming their harvested dreams, the whole mountain fat and slow and vulnerable.

"The scribe," he said at last. "Hazel. She signed a contract with a jailer-wight. Her service, forever, for my release. She's going to—"

"Breach it. Deliberately. Yes." Isolde smiled again, and this time it had teeth in it, bright and wicked and young. "I read the draft. She thinks she hid her clause well. She hid it *adequately.* A true scribe would have buried it in the subvention on damages. I left her an inkpot where she could find it. I've been leaving inkpots in useful places for three hundred years. It's remarkable what a person can accomplish with an inkpot and a reputation for harmlessness."

* * *

She rose then, and held out her hand.

"Come. The King sleeps until the feast ends, and the feast ends at dawn, and dawn is ours. But there are things you must have, and things you must know, and only I can give you either."

He took her hand and she pulled him up, and for a moment — just a moment — she let him feel what she was. Not the princess, not the furniture, not the crown's shadow. The first soul. The oldest flame. Ten thousand winters of banked fire, and beneath it, impossibly, still: a girl of fifteen who had loved the rain.

He breathed in sharply. "You're *burning.*"

"I'm *waiting,*" she corrected. "It's not the same thing, but it's close enough for government work. Come."

She led him through corridors only she knew — past the wick-trimming stations, past the archive of rendered memory where the court's currency sat in rows of little labelled jars (she did not let him look long; some things a man should not see before he must), down a stair that was not on any map because she had scratched it into the stone herself over four thousand years, one fingernail's width per century, patient as erosion.

At the bottom was a small iron door, and hanging on a nail beside it, a key.

It was not a large key. It was not ornate. It was, in fact, a deeply disappointing object — iron, pitted, the size of a man's thumb — and Silas looked at it the way men look at gods: with suspicion.

"The master-key," Isolde said. "To the soul-vaults. All of them. Every ward, every lock, every chain of scripture. My father gave it to me the first century, because he trusted me, because I was his daughter and the taking of my soul had broken something in him he never let heal. He never asked for it back. Asking would have meant admitting I might use it." She pressed it into Silas's palm and closed his fingers over it, and her hands were cold as a cellar and steady as a smith's. "It's been in my keeping for nine thousand nine hundred years. It's heavy. Not the iron. The waiting."

"Why give it to me? You don't know me. I could be exactly what the prophecy says. The book said the storm would kneel — to him, maybe. Maybe I kneel tomorrow."

"You might," Isolde agreed cheerfully. "People are disappointing at truly impressive rates. But here is what I know about you, Silas Crane. I know you said no in the great hall when saying yes would have bought you your grandmother's storm, legally, honorably, with paperwork. I know you came down here for a scribe's brother, whom you have never met, on a scribe's guilt that isn't yours. And I know that when my father showed you the true arithmetic — the wall, the hunger, the freezing world — you looked sick, and you looked afraid, and you did not look *convinced.*" She patted his cheek, gently, the way one pats a large and dangerous animal that has decided, provisionally, not to bite. "Cowardice always looks convinced. That's how you spot it."

"And the storm? My grandmother's storm. The King offered it to me. Where is it truly?"

"Below," she said. "Below the vaults. Below the shield. At the mountain's heart, where the first storm-caller chained it when he built his winter — because he could not destroy the storms, and could not bear to let them roam free above while he hid. Your grandmother's storm has been down there for a hundred years, coiled in a cage of chains and scripture, *waiting for its blood.*" Her voice dropped. "The storm-room is past the deep fissures. Past the places where the mountain has begun to crack. You will feel the hunger down there, Silas. It dreams, and its dreams leak, and the leaking feels like drowning in cold honey. When you feel it, you must remember three things."

"Which are?"

"One: it lies. It is very old and very hungry and it lies the way my father does — reasonably, kindly, terribly. Two: the souls are the weapon. Freed souls burn hotter than any lightning; my father has never understood this, because he thinks of souls as fuel, and fuel is for burning, not for *asking.* Three—" and here Isolde stopped, and for the first time in ten thousand winters her composure cracked, just a hairline, just enough to show the girl underneath, "—three: when it ends, I end with it. I was the first taken. My soul is the keystone of the shield. When the vaults open, I come apart. I have made my peace with this. I am telling *you* so that you do not waste my last hour trying to save me. Promise me."

Silas looked at her for a long time. "No," he said.

She blinked. "No?"

"No. I don't make promises I intend to spend the rest of my life regretting. Ask me to try. Ask me to *want* to save you. But don't ask me to promise, because I've read enough of Hazel's contracts to know that a promise extracted under grief is just a trap with better handwriting." He turned the disappointing little key over in his palm. "I'll try. That's the whole offer."

Isolde laughed.

It was not a princess's laugh. It was a fifteen-year-old's laugh, startled out of her, bright and cracked and rusted with disuse, and it echoed up the secret stair and out into a mountain that had not heard it in ten millennia. She laughed until she had to lean on the wall, and when she straightened, her eyes were wet, and she was, for one unguarded moment, radiant — the way a candle is radiant in the last inch of its burning.

"Try," she said. "Yes. All right. *Try.* That's — yes. That's the best anyone has ever offered me." She wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, briskly, businesslike, the princess reassembling herself piece by piece. "Now go. The feast ends at dawn. The scribe's contract breaches at dawn. When the mountain starts screaming, you go down — down through the vaults, down past the fissures, and you follow the cold that *hums.* Storms hum, in their cages. My father never noticed. He has no ear for music."

She pressed something else into his hand then — small, folded, sealed with a plain drop of wax. "For the scribe. She'll understand it. It's the subvention on damages, from my father's own founding charter, the clause no one has read aloud in nine thousand years." Her smile turned sharp and sweet. "The one about consent."

Silas tucked it away. At the iron door he paused, and looked back at her — this ancient girl in her ancient dress, standing in her secret stair with her secret key finally given away, lighter by one impossible weight and heavier by everything else.

"Isolde. The rain. If we win — if it ends —"

"Then it rains," she said simply. "Somewhere. On someone. And I won't feel it, but I'll have *known* it was coming, and that, storm-caller, is more than I've had in ten thousand years." She flapped her fingers at him, shooing. "Go. And Silas — when you meet my father again, and he offers you his kindness, remember that I loved him once. Remember that he was the best of us. And then remember that the best of us built a wall out of children, and called it mercy, and slept soundly behind it for ten thousand years."

* * *

He went. She heard his footsteps fade down the secret stair, quick and light, a man running toward his fate with the particular gait of someone who has finally stopped being dragged.

Isolde stood alone in the dark a while longer. Then she straightened her dress, composed her face into its mild, dutiful, faintly bored expression, and climbed back up toward the feast hall, where her father slept his harvested sleep and the candles of rendered memory burned in their thousands, each one a person, each one a winter, each one a wall.

Dawn was coming. She could feel it the way she had once felt weather — as a pressure, a promise, a change in the air.

Above her, far above, through a hundred fathoms of stone, something in the mountain's heartbeat *stuttered.*

Once. Twice.

Then, from the direction of the salt cells, faint but unmistakable, rising through the stone like smoke through floorboards: the sound of a contract tearing, and a woman's voice, clear as a bell, reading her own name aloud.

Isolde smiled, and began, at last, to run.
