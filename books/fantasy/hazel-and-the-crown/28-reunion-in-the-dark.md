## 28. Reunion in the Dark

The vaults came apart the way a held breath comes apart — all at once, and everywhere.

Silas felt it before he heard it: a shudder in the stone that traveled up through his boots and settled somewhere behind his teeth. Then the first soul-lantern tore loose from its chain overhead and went spinning into the dark, trailing light like a comet with nowhere to be.

He ran.

The corridor was chaos in both directions. Souls that had hung for decades — some for centuries — were wrenching free of their moorings as Hazel's hidden clause chewed through the vault's architecture from the inside. He could hear them now, really *hear* them, which no one was supposed to be able to do. Not wailing. Worse. *Humming.* Each one humming a different fragment of a song it had once known on the surface — a lullaby here, a harvest chant there, a drinking tune that dissolved halfway through because the man who'd sung it had forgotten how the chorus went.

A lantern-soul drifted past his shoulder, close enough that its light washed over his hands, and inside it he saw an old woman's face, eyes closed, mouth moving. She looked, Silas thought wildly, like she was dreaming of kneading bread.

"Sorry," he said to her, because there was nothing else to say, and kept running.

The map Ellery's ghost had burned into his memory said the east gallery curved back toward the central stair — toward where Hazel had been taken when the salt cells swallowed her. Toward Tam.

The stone groaned again, deeper this time, and somewhere behind him a whole rack of souls let go at once with a sound like a chandelier surrendering. Light flooded the corridor, blinding after the dark, and Silas threw up an arm —

— and collided with someone at full sprint, and they went down together in a tangle of limbs and swearing.

For one absurd heartbeat he thought he'd run into another fleeing soul-thief, some rival from a century ago shaken loose by the collapse. Then he registered the ink-stained fingers, the scribe's calluses, the smell of lamp-oil and panic, and his heart did something complicated and painful in his chest.

"Hazel."

"Silas." She was already scrambling up, hauling him with her, her eyes doing a rapid inventory — face, hands, ribs, *there*, checking he was whole. "You're alive. Good. That's — good, we can be relieved later, right now —"

"The clause worked."

Her grin was wild and terrified and the most beautiful thing he'd seen in weeks. "Of course it worked. I wrote it." Behind her, down the corridor she'd come from, cell doors stood open along the wall like a row of pulled teeth, salt dust still drifting out of them in pale clouds. "Breach of contract triggers immediate dissolution of all subsidiary holdings. I told you I'd read the founding charter."

"You told me you'd *skimmed* the founding charter."

"I skimmed it very thoroughly." She grabbed his wrist. "Come on. The vaults are coming down and the King is —"

The King's voice arrived before she could finish naming him.

It didn't echo. Echoes bounce off walls and grow tired. This voice came *through* the walls, through the floor, through the marrow of the mountain itself, so that it seemed to speak from inside their own skulls, courteous as ever:

"*Thieves. You have stolen from the cradle of winter. Every soul you loose is a stone pulled from a wall, and when the wall falls, you will be beneath it.*"

Silas's skin crawled. Hazel's grip on his wrist tightened until it hurt.

"He's angry," she whispered.

"No," Silas said, listening to the terrible evenness of it. "He's *reasonable*. That's worse."

* * *

They ran.

The corridors of the under-court had been built to be walked slowly, reverently, by subjects awed by their own smallness. At a dead sprint they were a labyrinth designed by something with too many elbows. Hazel led — she'd drawn these halls from memory a dozen times in her head during the long winter, and her feet remembered what her fear had made her memorize — while Silas brought up the rear with the storm coiled tight under his breastbone, ready to spend itself against whatever came around the next corner.

Nothing came around any corner. That was somehow worse. The King didn't need riders anymore. The mountain *was* his rider now.

"*Hazel Underwood.*" The voice found her name and rolled it around like a jeweler assessing a flawed stone. "*You signed my ledger in your own blood. There is no clause in you that I did not write.*"

"There's one," Hazel panted, taking a corner so hard her shoulder scraped the wall. "Page nine hundred and forty. Subsection twelve. *All contracts void upon breach of good faith.* You lied to me about Tam's terms. I checked. Twice. In triplicate." She shot a glance back at Silas, breathless, almost giddy. "I filed the objection while they were dragging me to the cells. A jailer-wight witnessed it. Wights are *terrible* gossips but *excellent* notaries."

Something that might have been the mountain equivalent of a laugh moved through the stone — or might have been another section of ceiling giving way. With the King, Silas was learning, it was difficult to tell.

"*You would drown in paperwork the world,*" the voice said, and for the first time there was something underneath the courtesy. Something old and tired and furious. "*To save one flickering light?*"

"Yes," Hazel and Silas said together, and then looked at each other, startled, as if each had expected to be the only one foolish enough to mean it.

The east gallery opened ahead of them — a vast chamber where the souls hung thickest, hundreds of lanterns now swinging on half-snapped chains, the whole room lit like a storm of trapped fireflies. And there, third row from the far wall, exactly where Hazel had left it hanging in her nightmares:

Tam.

His lantern-soul had come half loose, swinging on a single frayed chain, dimmer than Silas remembered from the vault's first horror — but not out. Not out. Inside the glass-and-light of it, a young man's shape turned slowly, and his lips were moving, and though no sound crossed the boundary, Silas knew the shape of the words. He'd watched Hazel mouth them often enough in her sleep.

*The song their mother sang.*

"TAM!" Hazel's voice cracked across the chamber, and every soul within earshot flickered — startled birds indeed — and Tam's lantern swung toward her voice like a flower toward sun.

She was already climbing. There was a maintenance ladder bolted to the wall, scribe's scaffolding for the clerks who tended the collection, and she went up it hand over hand with a speed that made Silas's stomach drop. Below her, the chamber floor was beginning to tilt as the vault's geometry lost its argument with the charter dissolving through it.

"Hazel, the chain —"

"I see the chain!"

"It's going to —"

"I SAID I SEE THE CHAIN, SILAS."

The chain chose that moment to snap.

Time went strange. Silas's hand came up without consulting him, and the storm answered — not lightning, not yet, just a rope of wind and pressure that caught the falling lantern three feet above the tilting floor and held it, trembling, turning gently, like a caught moth.

Hazel came down the ladder twice as fast as she'd gone up it, which should not have been possible, and took the lantern out of the wind with both hands, the way you'd take a sleeping child.

For a moment she just stood there, holding it, and the light of her brother's soul washed over her face, and Silas saw her lips form the words *hi, Tam, sorry I'm late,* and saw the shape inside the lantern press its glowing hand to the inside of the light, right where her cheek was.

Then the mountain screamed.

Not the King's reasonable voice — the mountain itself, stone grinding on stone, as somewhere above them the Feast of Falling ended early and ten thousand dreamers woke at once to find their harvest burning.

"*RUN,*" said the King's voice, and this time it wasn't a threat. It sounded, horribly, like advice.

* * *

**HAZEL**

Later — if there was a later — Hazel would try to explain to someone what it felt like to run through a collapsing underworld carrying your brother's soul in your arms, and she would fail, because the honest answer was: *it felt like joy.*

That was the shameful part. The vaults were dying around them, the King's voice was pouring through the walls reciting the exact text of her contract back at her in a tone of judicial disappointment, chunks of ceiling the size of oxen were coming down, and Hazel Underwood — who had not properly laughed since the last real summer — was grinning like an idiot.

Because Tam was *warm*. She hadn't known a soul could be warm through its lantern, but he was, faintly, the way a stone stays warm after the fire, and every few strides he flickered against her arms in a rhythm she recognized. Their mother's song. He was keeping time with her footsteps.

"Left!" Silas shouted, and she went left, ducking under a falling chain that whipped past where her head had been. A freed soul swooped through the gap after it — a young man in a shepherd's coat, laughing, actually *laughing*, cartwheeling through the air like the first day of spring — and Hazel thought: *that's what we're doing. All of us. We're all just running for the same exit.*

"The stair's gone," Silas reported, skidding to a halt at the junction where the central stair used to be. It was now a waterfall of rubble. "Ellery's map — is there another way up?"

Hazel's mind, trained by seven years of the court's archive, flipped through possibilities like pages. "The record-annex has a ventilation shaft. Scribes used it to smuggle out —" she caught herself, "— *documents*. It's narrow."

"How narrow?"

"You'll fit."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only answer I have!"

He looked at her — at Tam's lantern in her arms, at the dust sifting down, at the whole mountain holding its breath around them — and then he did the thing that made her chest ache in a way she refused to examine until they were safely above ground: he smiled.

"Narrow shafts it is. After you, scribe."

The King's voice followed them into the annex, gentler now, which was its most frightening register. "*Isolde gave you the key,*" it said to Silas, and Hazel felt him flinch beside her. "*My daughter. My first theft. You carry her mercy like it costs you nothing. Ask her, thief, what it cost* her."

"She told us," Silas said quietly, climbing. "Everything ends. Even mountains. Especially cowards."

The silence that followed had weight. Somewhere far below, something that was not the King shifted in its sleep, and every lantern-soul in the mountain flickered at once.

Hazel climbed faster.

* * *

The ventilation shaft was exactly as narrow as advertised, which meant Hazel went first with Tam held against her chest, wriggling upward through warm dark air that smelled of candle-tallow and centuries, while Silas braced himself below and pushed when she needed pushing and complained about neither the squeezing nor the indignity, which she counted among his finest qualities.

Halfway up, wedged in a bend of the shaft with her brother's soul humming against her collarbone, Hazel started to laugh.

"What?" Silas's voice came up the shaft, muffled and wary. "What's funny? Nothing about this is funny."

"My master," she gasped, "the Court Scribe Prime — he used to say the archives' greatest security was that no thief could resist reading what they stole. That curiosity always stopped them in the stacks long enough to be caught." She wheezed. "He never accounted for a thief who'd already read everything. Twice. Out of *boredom*."

Above her, Silas laughed too — short, startled, real — and the sound bounced up the shaft ahead of them like it was scouting the route.

Tam flickered. Brightened. And for one impossible moment, through the glass of his lantern, Hazel heard — not imagined, *heard* — a thread of sound escape him. Two notes of their mother's song, thin as paper, drifting up the shaft.

Silas went still below her.

"You heard that," she said. Not a question.

"I heard that."

"Souls aren't supposed to make sound outside the vaults."

"Neither are storms supposed to kneel," Silas said, "and the book's been wrong about me since page one. Maybe breaking a prophecy breaks other things too. Maybe he's getting *louder*."

Hazel pressed her forehead gently against the warm glass. "Hold on, Tam," she whispered. "We're getting you above ground. There's a valley. There are bees. You're going to hate the bees."

The lantern flickered in a pattern she chose to interpret as *no I won't.*

* * *

They came out of the shaft into a records gallery three levels up, into air that tasted of smoke and waking court, and found the corridor full of fleeing servants — wights and grey-clad clerks and one extremely dignified candle-guild factor — all streaming away from the mountain's heart with the blind unanimity of a kicked anthill.

Nobody stopped them. Nobody even looked. The under-court, Hazel realized, had spent so many centuries believing itself eternal that it had no protocol whatsoever for ending.

"This way," she said, pulling Silas against the current. "Isolde's directions. The heart of the mountain is below the throne, and the throne is —"

"— directly ahead of everyone running the other way," Silas finished grimly. "Naturally."

They fought upstream through the crowd, past a wight clutching an armful of ledgers it had apparently decided to save from the apocalypse on principle, past two frost-riders who had abandoned their horses-shaped-of-frost and were arguing bitterly about whose fault this was. The King's voice rang through the stone overhead, no longer addressing them — addressing *everyone*, issuing orders that fewer and fewer things obeyed, and beneath his words Hazel could hear the other sound growing. The deep, patient scrape of something below turning over in its sleep and finding the blankets gone.

Then the last corner, and the great doors of the throne hall standing open, and beyond them the descent Isolde had described: stairs going down into a heatless red glow, toward the heart of the mountain, toward a cage of chains and scripture, toward a storm that had waited a hundred years for blood of its blood.

Hazel shifted Tam's lantern higher against her chest. Inside it, her brother turned his glowing face toward the red-lit stairs, and hummed.

Silas looked down at the stairs, then at her, and squared his shoulders like a man picking up something heavy that he intended to carry the rest of the way.

"Together?" he said.

"Together," said Hazel.

And the mountain, groaning, let them pass.
