## 30. What the Prophecy Meant

The storm fit him like a coat he'd been measured for at birth and only now allowed to try on.

Silas stood in the heart of the mountain with lightning running through his veins like his own blood had been waiting all these years for permission, and the first thing he thought — the first coherent thought in a mind otherwise full of thunder — was: *Grandmother, you could have warned me it itches.*

It did itch. Not unpleasantly. More like the feeling of a limb waking up, pins and needles from crown to heel, except the limb was everything.

"Silas." Hazel's voice came to him from very far away and very close at once, the way sound worked now that he could hear the mountain's pulse beneath it. "You're glowing. I want you to know that. In case nobody's told you yet. You're *glowing*, and your hair is doing something I can only describe as *defiant*."

He looked down at his hands. Arcs of pale blue crawled between his fingers like tame eels, or eels that *believed* they were tame, which was not quite the same thing. "Is it bad?"

"It's the most beautiful terrifying thing I've ever seen," Hazel said. "And I grew up reading the Mountain King's tax ledgers, so my standards are admittedly strange."

Odd, pressed against the far wall of the storm room with all of its grave-wight dignity hanging by a thread, raised one bony hand. "For the record, the wight finds it *also* beautiful-terrifying, but would like it noted that wights are flammable, and would appreciate it if the beautiful-terrifying thing stayed at least six feet away."

Behind them all, Isolde stood at the mouth of the corridor, holding the fissure closed with her dissolving body, her back to them, her shoulders trembling with effort she refused to show. She did not turn around. She didn't have to. Silas could feel her now — feel everyone, everything, every soul in the mountain like candles seen through frosted glass — and Isolde burned the brightest and the thinnest, a candle down to its last hour, still choosing to stand in the draft.

The storm room itself was a cathedral of chains. His grandmother's storm coiled in the center of it, vast and furious and *familiar*, a serpent of cloud and lightning bound in links of scripture — actual scripture, he realized now, each chain-link inscribed with words in the old court-hand, the same hand Hazel used when she was being careful. The storm had poured into him when he stepped inside the cage, or he into it; the distinction had stopped mattering. What remained of the cage held nothing now but chains and the smell of rain on hot stone.

And in his head, quiet as a book opening, Ellery's last line finally made sense.

*And the storm shall kneel.*

He had read those words a hundred times since the peddler sold him the book. He had assumed they meant surrender. Submission. The storm-caller brought low before some king or god or winter. He had spent months dreading them the way you dread a diagnosis.

But standing here, full of sky, he understood. Kneeling wasn't what happened *to* you. It was what you did when you chose where your strength went. A storm that knelt was a storm that aimed. Every storm-caller before him — the King himself, once, ages ago — had read that line as a threat because they could not imagine power that bent on purpose. Power that asked.

Power that said: *where do you need me?*

"Silas?" Hazel again, gentler now. She had crossed the room without him noticing — another thing about having a storm for a heartbeat, it made time go strange — and stood beside him, looking up at his face with an expression he was beginning to know well: fear and love sharing a chair, neither willing to give up its seat. "You've gone somewhere. Where'd you go?"

"I understood the book," he said. His voice came out doubled, his own voice and something older underneath it, rolling like distant thunder. He winced. "Sorry. I understood the book," he tried again, quieter, and this time it was just him. Mostly. "Ellery wrote it as instructions, not predictions. 'The storm shall kneel' — it means kneel to *choose*. To ask. Not to a king."

Hazel exhaled slowly. "So the whole prophecy, the whole horrible trap we've been caught in—"

"Was a letter," Silas said. "From a woman who died a hundred years ago trying to do what we're doing. She couldn't break the court herself, so she wrote a key instead and hid it in a story and waited a century for someone stubborn enough to pick it up." He almost laughed. It came out as a rumble that rattled dust from the ceiling. Odd yelped. "Sorry."

"You're going to have to work on that," Hazel said. "The involuntary seismic commentary. Very unnerving."

* * *

But there was a bill attached. There is always a bill.

He felt it the moment he reached, experimentally, toward the vaults — felt the souls hanging there like lanterns, thousands of them, ten thousand winters' worth of harvested dreams and names and mothers' songs — and felt how easily the storm could shatter their cages. One breath. One gesture. He could free every soul in the mountain before the Mountain King finished drawing breath to object.

And he felt what else would happen.

The shield.

The King had shown him the arithmetic in the throne hall, and Silas had hated how much sense it made: the long winter fed the mountain, the mountain's cold kept the seal, and the seal held down the thing below — the hunger older than the court, the reason the first storm-caller had fled underground rather than fight. Every soul burned as candlelight was a stone in a wall. Break the wall and the stones come loose, and behind the wall—

Behind the wall, something rolled over in its sleep.

Silas felt it even now, even through the storm's roar: a pressure from below, patient and enormous, like the ocean leaning against a door. The freed souls rising through the fissures were light going *up*. Something else was coming up after them.

"Hazel." He turned to her, and the arcs around his hands dimmed, because some things deserved his full attention undecorated. "I can break the vaults. All of them. Every soul goes free — Tam, Isolde's people, everyone the court took since before Ellery. But the moment I do, the shield dies. The mountain stops being a prison and starts being a hole. And the thing below—"

"I know." Her jaw was set. Scribe's jaw, decision-made jaw. "I did the accounting while you were busy becoming weather. Freeing them costs us the wall. Keeping the wall costs them everything, forever, and us too — the King just doesn't phrase it that way." She looked at the chains, at the empty cage, at him. "What does the storm say?"

That was the question, wasn't it. Everyone had opinions — the King's duty, Ellery's hope, Hazel's arithmetic, Odd's survival instincts. But the storm was his inheritance, his grandmother's legacy, older than the court's charter, and it had been underground longer than any of them. It might know things.

Silas closed his eyes and knelt.

Not dramatically. Not the way kings demanded kneeling, all ceremony and humiliation. He simply went down on one knee on the warm stone floor of the storm room, the way you'd kneel by a garden bed, the way you'd kneel to speak to a child, and he asked — out loud, because it seemed polite, and because Hazel was watching and someone ought to narrate —

"What do we do?"

The storm answered the way storms answer: all at once, and sideways.

Images, mostly. Rain on a valley he knew — *his* valley, the bees, the bell — and rain on fens where sealed villages slept, and rain on Greyharbor's rooftops, and under all of it a memory that wasn't his: a woman with his face and grey in her hair, standing on a mountaintop, refusing. *No,* she was saying, to someone in a frozen crown. *You don't get to buy the world with other people's souls and call it shelter.* And below her feet, the mountain, and below the mountain, the hunger, and her storm crackling around her like a drawn bow.

She hadn't fought the deep thing. She'd offered to. They'd executed her for the offer, because an offer to fight meant admitting the fight existed, and the court's whole architecture rested on pretending the wall was permanent.

But the offer was the answer. The storm showed him: the wall was never meant to be the *only* plan. It was meant to buy time until a storm-caller strong enough — and brave enough, and foolish enough — came along and aimed the sky downward instead of hoarding it. The shield wasn't sacred. It was scaffolding. And scaffolding is supposed to come down when the real building stands.

The real building was choice. The real building was kneeling to ask, and aiming together.

Silas opened his eyes. He was crying, a little, which was embarrassing, and the tears sizzled faintly on his cheeks, which was more embarrassing.

"Well?" Hazel crouched in front of him. "Don't keep a girl in suspense. I've breached contracts faster than this."

"The wall was scaffolding," he said. "My grandmother figured it out. The storm was always meant to fight the deep thing — with the freed souls as its lightning. That's why the vaults and the storm are both down here. Ellery saw it. The whole design was waiting for—" he gestured vaguely at himself, at the glow, at the general catastrophe of his existence, "—this."

"For you."

"For *us*," he corrected. "Because here's the part I can't do alone. When I aim the storm down, I'll need every soul in the vaults to rise *through* it — willingly, burning bright, all at once. Ten thousand lanterns becoming one bolt. And souls don't move on command. They move on—" he hesitated, then smiled crookedly, "—on invitation. On words. On somebody who knows how to write a summons so compelling that the dead volunteer."

Hazel stared at him. Then she started laughing — wet-eyed, slightly hysterical, wonderful laughter. "You want me to write the dead a letter of invitation to a battle."

"A very persuasive one."

"Silas Crane, I forged the Mountain King's signature at nineteen. I once wrote a procurement complaint so vicious it got a steward reassigned to a fen. I can absolutely write ten thousand souls into volunteering." She was already patting her pockets for ink she didn't have, out of pure habit. "I'll need something to write on. And with. And probably a moment alone to cry about my brother, but that can be scheduled."

"Tam first," Silas said. "We grab Tam's lantern, we get him out, and then—" 

Then the floor moved.

Not shook. *Moved* — a slow, grinding tilt, like the whole mountain shifting its weight, and from far below came a sound that Silas felt in his new storm-bones rather than heard: a long, unhurried scrape, the sound of something enormous turning over and, this time, not settling back to sleep.

Dust rained from the ceiling. One of the scripture-chains snapped and hit the ground with a sound like a slammed hymnal. Odd made a noise that no living creature should have been able to make and clung to the wall.

"That," Odd said, with tremendous composure, "is the sound of our appointment arriving early. Wights hate early appointments."

At the corridor's mouth, Isolde staggered. The fissure behind her glowed now — not with her fading light but with something else, something hungry and red-gold and wrong, pushing up through the cracks like roots in reverse. She braced herself against the stone and looked back at them, and even half-dissolved, even worn down to her final hour, she managed a smile.

"He's waking," she called. "Whatever you're going to do — do it soon. And Silas?"

"Yes?"

"When you aim that storm — aim it like my father never could. Like you *chose* it." Her voice cracked, and the mountain cracked with it. "Make it mean something."

Silas rose. The storm rose with him, filling the broken cage, spilling out through the shattered doorway, and far above he could hear — distantly, muffled by miles of stone — the Mountain King's voice beginning to shout orders, and boots, and bells, and the particular chaos of a court whose founding lie has just gotten up off the floor.

Hazel took his hand. Hers was steady. His crackled.

"Together, then," she said. "You bring the weather. I'll bring the paperwork."

Below them, the deep thing began, patiently, joyfully, to climb.
