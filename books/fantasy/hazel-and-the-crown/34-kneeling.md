## 34. Kneeling

The storm did not feel like weather.

That was the first thing Silas understood, standing in the mountain's heart with his grandmother's lightning coiled around his arms like a living thing that had waited a hundred years to be held. It felt like a heartbeat. Like a second heart, enormous and impatient, beating against the inside of his ribs and asking — not demanding, asking — *where? what? whom?*

Every story he'd ever heard about storm-callers had them pointing. The old woodcuts in Greyharbor's temple showed his grandmother with her arm flung skyward, hair whipping, mouth open in a shout of command. Command. That was the word everyone used. *Command the storm.*

But it wasn't commanding him. It was waiting on him the way a hound waits on a hunter who has just realized the quarry is a child.

Below his feet — below all their feet — the deep thing was waking. He could feel it now through the soles of his boots, through the stone itself: a slow, vast attention turning over in its sleep like a whale rolling in dark water. The fissures in the vault floor had stopped crackling and started breathing. Each exhale carried up a cold that had nothing to do with winter, a cold from before winters were invented, and every freed soul in the shattered vaults was rising away from it as fast as lantern-light can climb.

And above, at the far end of the storm room, the Mountain King was picking himself up off his frozen throne, and his face had finally, fully, come off.

"Boy," the King said, and his voice no longer bothered with kindness. It was the voice of an avalanche deciding where to stop. "You will kneel to me, or you will kneel to nothing."

Silas looked at him. He thought about kneeling. He thought about how easy it would be — one bend of the knee, one word of surrender, and maybe the King would let Hazel live, let Odd scuttle back to his barrow, let Isolde keep whatever scraps of herself remained. The old bargain. The reasonable bargain. The King had been making it for ten thousand years, and it had always sounded almost fair right up until the moment it closed around your throat.

"No," Silas said.

It came out smaller than he'd meant it to. His voice cracked on the vowel, because he was twenty-three years old and terrified and holding enough lightning to crack a continent, and the King laughed — a sound like a glacier calving.

"Then die standing. It changes nothing. The shield is failing, the hunger is waking, and your little light show will feed it faster than ten thousand souls ever—"

"I said no." And this time the storm said it with him. The whole mountain shuddered. Chains of scripture hanging from the ceiling of the storm room rattled and snapped, link by link, each broken verse falling as ash. "I'm done taking orders. From kings. From books. From dead grandmothers." He swallowed. "From myself, most of all."

He turned his back on the King.

It was possibly the stupidest thing he had ever done, and he had once tried to burn a book that refused to burn. Behind him he heard the King draw breath to speak again — or to strike; by then they may have been the same thing — but Silas was already looking at the three people who had walked into hell with him, and the storm was roaring in his ears like a tide going out, and he understood, suddenly and completely, what Ellery's last line had meant.

*The storm shall kneel.*

Not to a king. Not to fate. Not to the mountain or the winter or the hunger underneath.

Kneeling meant choosing. Kneeling meant putting down the weight. Every storm-caller before him had stood alone on the high ground and decided for the world, and every one of them had been wrong — his grandmother executed for refusing, the King fled underground rather than fight, ten thousand years of solitary men and women holding lightning like a secret until the secret crushed them.

Silas Crane got down on both knees.

The stone was cold. Somewhere below, the deep thing shifted, and dust sifted from the ceiling like grey snow. He knelt anyway, in front of Hazel, in front of Odd, in front of Isolde, and he set the storm down between them — not literally, though the lightning did pool on the floor around him in slow blue rivers, which was disconcerting — but in the only way that mattered.

"I don't know what to do," he said.

Hazel made a small sound, half laugh, half sob. Of everything she might have expected him to say — and she was a scribe; she could have written a ledger of expectations — that wasn't on any page.

"The King says the shield has to fall or the world freezes," Silas went on. His voice was steadier now. Kneeling helped. It turned out that was the whole trick, the inheritance nobody had written down: the storm didn't want a master, it wanted someone honest enough to admit when they were lost. "Ellery says I can shatter the vaults and free every soul, and the shield dies with them. The King says if I aim this at him, the hunger eats us all by morning. Everyone I've ever trusted to know things is telling me something different, and the only people I actually trust—" his voice caught, and he let it catch, "—are standing right here. So. You've followed me into the dark. I'm asking you to lead me out of it. How do we spend the storm?"

Silence. The kind of silence a valley makes — and he thought of home, absurdly, of bees and bell-flowers, and found he wasn't homesick, because home had apparently been something you carry, not somewhere you go.

Hazel spoke first, because of course she did. She was already pacing, already talking with her hands, ink-stained fingers sketching arguments in the air.

"You can't shatter the vaults outright. Ellery told you why — the shield dies, the thing comes up, and we trade ten thousand prisoners for everyone above ground. But the King's way is worse. His way is the same way it's always been: feed it slowly, forever, and call the feeding mercy." She stopped pacing. She looked at him the way she'd looked at the contract she'd rewritten in the cells of salt — the look of a woman finding the flaw in a document everyone else believed was perfect. "But contracts have clauses. Storms must too. What does the storm actually *do*, Silas? When it strikes?"

"It... finds the path of least resistance," he said slowly. "Between the cloud and the ground. Between high and low. It doesn't destroy things, exactly. It *equalizes* them. Everything it touches ends up at the same potential."

"And what's the difference in potential," Hazel said, "between the deep thing and everything above it?"

Odd made a noise like a kettle achieving enlightenment. "Ohhh. Ohh, that's *lovely*. That's nasty and lovely, that is. She wants you to ground it."

"Ground it?" Isolde's voice was thin as frost on a windowpane. Ten thousand winters had worn her down to nearly nothing — a girl-shaped memory in a gown of dissolving starlight — but her eyes were still sharp, still human, still remembering rain. "Ground *that*?"

"A lightning rod," Hazel said. "Not a weapon. A drain. Don't kill it — I don't think anything can kill it, and I don't think we should bet the spring on trying. Equalize it. Take everything the storm is, everything the vaults are, all that stolen power the court's been hoarding since before my grandmother's grandmother, and push it *down* — not to destroy the hunger, but to fill it. Feed it so completely, so fast, that it goes back to sleep. Not starving anymore. Not hungry anymore. Just... full. Sated for another age. While we figure out what to do about it properly, with time, with everyone awake instead of asleep in sealed houses having their dreams harvested."

"That would require more power than the storm holds," Isolde said quietly. "Even yours. Even hers." She nodded upward, toward the cage of chains and scripture where the storm had lived for a century. "Ten thousand souls' worth more."

And then the silence changed, because everyone arrived at the same thought at the same time, and the freed souls overhead — thousands of them, drifting up through the fissured dark like reverse snowfall, like embers returning to a fire — seemed to pause, as if they'd heard it too.

"They're already free," Hazel whispered. "We broke the wards. They're not prisoners anymore. They're just... leaving." Her hand went to her mouth. "They get to choose too."

Tam's soul was somewhere in that rising river of light. Silas had seen it — dim, flickering, humming their mother's song — before the alarms rolled and the world came apart. He looked at Hazel and saw her doing the same arithmetic he was doing, and saw her arrive at the answer he dreaded and hoped for in equal measure.

"They won't all go," she said. Her voice shook. "Some of them are tired, some are frightened, some just want to rise and be done with this place. But some of them—" She pressed her fist against her sternum, over the hollow where a brother used to live. "Some of them have been down here so long they've forgotten what sky looks like. If someone asked. If someone asked *properly* — not commanded, not harvested, not contracted. Asked."

"Nobody's ever asked them anything," Isolde said. There was wonder in it, and grief, and something that might have been the beginning of peace. "I was the first. Do you know what I would have given, ten thousand years ago, for someone to ask me?" She smiled, and it was a terrible, beautiful smile, the smile of a princess who remembered rain. "Ask them."

* * *

So Silas Crane, kneeling in the heart of the mountain, opened himself entirely to the storm and asked it to do the one thing no storm had ever done.

Not strike. *Speak.*

Lightning is a language, it turns out. It is the oldest language — the crackle that was there before words, before courts, before anyone thought to build a wall against the dark and call the wall civilization. Silas didn't shape it into thunder-words; he simply opened the door of himself and let the storm pour its question outward and upward, into the dark where the freed souls drifted:

*Will you help us?*

Not: will you serve. Not: will you burn for the mountain, or bleed for the valley, or hold up the sky. Just: *will you help us.* Will you spend yourselves one more time — freely, gladly, knowing exactly what it costs and where it goes — to put the hunger back to sleep?

For a long moment, nothing. The souls rose in silence. Silas felt the storm's hope guttering in his chest like a candle in a draft, and beside him Hazel had gone very still, and Odd had, for the first time in Silas's acquaintance, absolutely nothing to say.

Then, from high above — from the top of the rising column of light, from the direction of the surface, of the sky, of spring — one soul flickered brighter.

And answered.

It was not words. It was better than words. It was a feeling poured directly into the storm's great open ear: the sensation of a long winter ending. Of ice cracking on a pond in March. Of a door painted with soul-sigils opening from the *inside*.

One soul turned. Then ten. Then a hundred, then a thousand, a cascade of small bright decisions, each one a person — a farmer, a ferrywoman, a child, a scribe, a king's forgotten daughter's forgotten friend — choosing, for the first time in centuries, to give rather than be taken.

They came down like snowfall reversed twice over. They came down singing.

"Oh," said Odd, in a very small voice for such a large wight. "Oh, that's the most beautiful thing I've ever seen, and I once watched a lich apologize to a goose."

"Hold on to them," Hazel breathed. "Silas — hold on to them gently. They're not ammunition. They're volunteers."

"I know," Silas said. And he did. The storm knew. The storm gathered them the way a valley gathers snowmelt — carefully, gratefully, every drop honored — and the lightning in the storm room turned from blue to white to a color that has no name because it only appears when ten thousand souls agree on something, and the color was *warm*.

Above them, the Mountain King screamed.

"You fools! You short-sighted, sentimental FOOLS! Without the shield the hunger wakes fully, it climbs, it DEVOURS—"

"Yes," Silas said, standing at last, and the storm stood with him, and his shadow on the wall had wings made of other people's courage. "And then it gets *full*."

He aimed downward.

* * *

Aiming a storm at the bottom of the world is not like throwing a spear. It is like opening a vein and trusting the blood to find its way home. Silas felt the path form beneath him — through the vault floor, through the fissures, down past the roots of the mountain, past the King's ancient cowardice fossilized into law, down to the vast slow turning attention of the thing that had slept since before sleep was invented — and he felt it notice him.

It noticed him the way the ocean notices a raindrop.

*Small,* it thought at him, in a voice that was not a voice, in a cold that was older than cold. *Small thing. Small light. I have eaten lights greater than mountains.*

"I know," Silas said aloud, and his voice only shook a little. "Here's ten thousand more."

He let go.

The storm went down through the mountain like dawn going down through a valley — not violently, not at first, but inevitably, filling every crack, every hollow, every dark place the court had ever carved or hidden or fled into. The freed souls rode the lightning down willingly, singing their small songs — harvest songs, lullabies, a mother's tune about a boy named Tam — and where they struck the deep thing's waking attention, they did not wound it.

They *fed* it.

The hunger recoiled — not in pain, but in astonishment. In ten thousand years it had been fed drop by drop, rationed, hoarded, drip-fed through candles and contracts and harvested dreams, kept hungry on purpose because a hungry thing is a controllable thing. No one had ever simply *given* it a meal. No one had ever trusted it with abundance.

It ate. God help them all, it ate — the storm, the souls, the stored power of a century of imprisoned lightning, the whole hoarded treasury of the under-court's stolen light — and as it ate, the vast attention slowed. The breathing cold eased. The fissures' exhalations grew shorter, softer, until the deep thing settled back into the dark like a guest finally full at a table that had starved it politely for ages, and its last feeling as it sank toward sleep was not gratitude, exactly, but something adjacent to it. Something that might, given time and better neighbors, learn to become gratitude.

The shield died. Hazel had been right about that — Silas felt it go, felt the great dome of frozen breath over the world above dissolve like morning mist. But the hunger did not wake to fill the gap. It slept, sated, and the gap was only sky.

Sky. Open, undefended, ordinary sky.

Silas came back to himself on his knees again — he didn't remember falling — with Hazel's hands on his face and Odd's enormous paw thumping his back hard enough to crack a lesser man's spine, and the storm room filled with a light that was fading now, gently, the way a bonfire fades when everyone it warmed has gone safely home.

"You did it," Hazel was saying, laughing and crying in the same breath, which Silas would later learn was simply how she talked when she was happy. "You absolute beekeeping maniac, you *did* it—"

"We did it," Silas said. "I knelt. You aimed."

"Details." She kissed him, quick and fierce, and then the floor moved.

Not trembled. *Moved* — a long, groaning shift, deep in the mountain's bones, and dust cascaded from the ceiling, and somewhere far above them iron rang against iron as the under-court's architecture, built on ten thousand years of stolen winter, began to renegotiate its lease.

"The mountain's letting go!" Isolde cried, and even now, even dissolving, even smiling, she sounded less afraid than relieved. "Everything the King held frozen is thawing at once — the vaults, the passes, the throne itself—"

At the far end of the room, the Mountain King rose from his knees — when had *he* fallen? — and there was nothing kingly left in his face at all. Only a very old man in a crown of melting breath, watching his life's work come down around him, and reaching, with the last of his terrible patience, for the one thing left that he could still break.

"If I cannot keep the winter," he said, "I will keep the *debt*."

His eyes fixed on Hazel. On the contract-scar that glowed faintly on her wrist — the deal she'd struck in the salt cells, the clause she'd hidden, the breach she'd bought with her own freedom. And Silas saw, with a lurch, that the King had read her fine print after all — and had found, buried in the procedural wreckage of his collapsing court, one line of the old law that still had teeth.

"The thief's debt transfers," the King said, and began to walk toward her through the falling mountain, each step cracking the floor like lake ice in April. "Blood for blood. Soul for soul. The charter demands its balance, girl, and I am the last of the court, and while I stand, the law stands—"

Behind him, unnoticed by everyone except a grave-wight with an eye for endings, the last unbroken chain of scripture in the storm room gave a soft, tired *snap* — and fell.

And the mountain, freed of its oldest law, began — very gently, very enormously — to sing.
