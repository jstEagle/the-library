## 35. The Battle Above and Below

### Silas

The storm came out of him like a held breath finally released.

Silas had spent eleven years learning to be small — to fold his shoulders in, to keep his voice low, to walk past mirrors. Now the mountain's heart roared around him, chains of scripture snapping one by one, and every folded-up inch of him unfolded at once. Lightning lived behind his ribs. It had always lived there. He had simply been taught by exile to call it a cough.

"Silas!" Hazel's voice cut through the thunder, thin but certain. "Down! Aim *down*!"

He aimed down.

The deep thing rose through the fissure like a black tide given ambition — a hunger older than the court, older than the winter, older perhaps than the idea of *up*. It had no face, so it wore the absence of one: a vast wrongness in the stone, all teeth where there should have been geology. The freed souls swirled above it, thousands of lantern-lights loosed from their hooks, and when Silas pulled, they came to him like iron filings to a lodestone.

They became his lightning.

He had expected them to burn. He had braced for screams. Instead the souls *sang* — a soundless chord he felt in his teeth, in the old scar of his brand, in the place where his grandmother's storm now coiled obedient as a hound that remembered being loved. Every bolt he threw was somebody's stolen life thrown back at the thing that had taken it, and the deep thing recoiled from each strike as if scalded.

"That's it," Silas breathed. "That's — Hazel, it doesn't like—"

"I see it!" She was on her knees on the shattered flagstones, the charter spread before her, ink flying from her pen faster than any human hand should move. "Keep it busy! I'm dissolving its landlord!"

A tendril of dark lashed up through the fissure toward her. Silas caught it with a fistful of lightning and held on, arms shaking, teeth gritted, laughing despite everything because — because this was what he was *for*. Not hiding. Not beekeeping. This.

The storm agreed. The storm had always agreed. It had only ever been waiting for him to ask.

* * *

### Hazel

The charter of the under-court was, in Hazel's professional opinion, the worst-drafted legal document she had ever read, and she had once transcribed a contract written by a drunk goblin advocate on the back of another contract.

*Let the Mountain endure while the Winter is fed,* the founding clause ran, in script so old the letters had grown little roots into the vellum. *Let the Court hold authority over all beneath and all it shelters.*

There it was. *Beneath.* The King's whole claim, four hundred years of frozen breath and harvested dreams, resting on a preposition.

"You can't do this," the Mountain King said. He stood at the edge of the fissure, crown steaming, and for the first time since she'd known him he looked less like a monarch than like a man watching his house burn while insisting it was a controlled demolition. "The charter is not yours to amend."

"No," Hazel agreed pleasantly, dipping her pen in lightning-light, because Silas's storm had gotten everywhere and frankly it made an excellent ink. "It's mine to *dissolve*. Different verb. Look it up — oh wait, you burned the reference library in year two hundred and six. I found the requisition order. Sloppy."

She wrote: *The office of Mountain King is hereby dissolved, effective immediately, retroactive to the moment its holder first chose hiding over duty.*

The words sank into the vellum and the vellum shuddered. Somewhere far above, frost-riders stopped mid-charge as the liveries melted off their shoulders like spring snow. Somewhere below, the deep thing screamed — not in rage, Hazel realized, but in *confusion*, its ancient grievance suddenly missing its counterparty.

"You were never fighting it," she told the King, and her voice cracked, because underneath the scribe's competence there was just a girl who had sold her brother and could not stop doing arithmetic about it. "You were keeping its books. Four hundred years of balanced ledgers. That's all your kingdom ever was."

"Hazel." His voice went soft, reasonable, terrible, the way it had been in the throne room. "If you strike my name from that page, the shield falls with me."

"The shield already fell," she said. "Look down."

And he looked down, which was the first honest thing he'd done in centuries, and saw Isolde.

* * *

### Isolde

Isolde had been the court's first soul, and she had learned over ten thousand winters that a soul does not break all at once. It wears away, like a stair worn smooth by feet. What remained of her was mostly memory and stubbornness, and both were enough.

She lay across the fissure.

It was not graceful. Grace had been scraped off her long ago, along with most of her name and the taste of rain. She simply stretched herself across the crack in the world — this girl-shaped remnant, this princess-shaped hole — and let the hunger push against her, and did not move.

It hurt the way drowning hurts, if drowning took years.

Below her, the deep thing pressed upward. Above her, Silas's storm raged, and the freed souls wheeled overhead like a sky full of startled stars, and Odd — the grave-wight, the chatterbox, the strangest friend she'd made in ten millennia — stood at the fissure's lip telling stories downward.

Isolde had heard every story the mountain had. She had not heard this one.

"—and then," Odd was saying, in that bright gravel voice, leaning over the edge with the enthusiasm of someone describing a neighbor's scandalous hedge, "the deep thing got *sealed*. Right here. By a storm-caller who knelt. And everyone lived, and the winter broke, and nobody remembers why they were ever afraid of the dark, because the dark lost. That's how it ends. I've seen it. Wights are very good at endings; we stand next to them professionally."

The deep thing paused.

It was, Isolde realized, working very hard to digest the story. It had eaten ten thousand years of dreams and memories and it had never once been told how *it* ended — because no story about it had ever existed. It was a hunger with no narrative. It had no ending to look forward to or fear, and so it had never had a reason to stop.

Now it had one. Odd had handed it one, gift-wrapped, in a cheerful voice, like a peddler selling doom at a village fair.

The pressure against Isolde's body eased — just slightly, just enough.

"Oh," she whispered, to no one, to the storm, to the girl she had been. "*Oh, that's clever.*"

* * *

### Odd

Odd had told stories in the dark for six hundred years, and had developed strong opinions about audience.

Bad audiences fidgeted. Good audiences wept. The *best* audiences — and Odd counted exactly three in six centuries: a dead knight, a lost child, and now, unexpectedly, an eldritch hunger older than the court — listened.

"It didn't want to hear the ending at first," Odd explained later, though no one asked, because Odd explained things regardless. "Kept pushing. Kept gnawing at Isolde, poor love, and growling at the lightning man. But you can't argue with a story that's *true*, and wights don't lie about endings. We're in the business. Reputation matters."

So Odd kept talking. Odd told it about the seal, and the kneeling, and the spring — *spring*, a word the deep thing had certainly never heard, and which it turned over and over like a coin of unfamiliar mint. Odd told it about the valley where magic couldn't follow, and the bees, and the woman who wrote her way out of a prison with a hidden clause. Odd told it the one story in all the world shaped exactly like itself:

*A great hunger wakes beneath a mountain. It rises. It is met. It ends.*

"And here's the trick of it," Odd confided to the darkness, patting the fissure's edge fondly, the way one pats a large dangerous dog that has decided, provisionally, not to bite. "Stories need somewhere to *go*. You've never had an ending, so you've had nowhere to go, so you've just gone *up*, forever, hungry. But now you've got one. All you have to do is arrive."

The deep thing shuddered. The fissure's edges crumbled. And slowly — with the terrible reluctance of a creature setting down a weight it had carried so long it had forgotten it wasn't part of its body — it began, at last, to move *toward* its own conclusion instead of away from it.

"There's a good horror," Odd said warmly. "Off you pop."

* * *

### Silas

The last hour of the battle, Silas would remember in pieces, the way storms remember coastlines.

He remembered Hazel on her knees in the wreckage of the court, rewriting reality in her own hand, muttering contract clauses like prayers, and at one point yelling "*Objection sustained!*" at a mountain.

He remembered Isolde across the fissure, going translucent, holding, holding, her face turned up toward the storm with an expression he'd only ever seen on people watching the first thaw after a killing winter.

He remembered Odd, of all creatures, standing at the edge of the abyss doing what Odd did best, which was talk until the universe gave in.

And he remembered the moment the souls sang loudest — when Tam's small flickering light rose among them, still humming their mother's song, and lent itself to the lightning without being asked, and the bolt that followed struck the deep thing square in its absence-of-a-face and *hurt* it, truly hurt it, and Silas understood that he had never once in his life wielded this storm alone.

It had always been made of everybody.

"Now!" Hazel's voice, ragged. "Charter's void! The seal's nothing but a bad habit! Somebody put a period on this sentence!"

Silas raised both hands. The storm gathered — his grandmother's fury, the valley's borrowed quiet, ten thousand stolen lives given freely back to the fight — and he thought, absurdly, of bees. Of how a hive defends itself: not with rage, but with total, coordinated, unhesitating commitment. One body. Many wings.

He brought the storm down into the fissure like a door closing.

The light was white and total. The sound was the sound of a story arriving at its last word. And when it faded, the deep thing was sealed — not caged, not chained, not fed, but *ended*, folded into the ending Odd had told it, sleeping now the way mountains sleep: completely, and without appetite.

The silence afterward rang louder than the thunder had.

Then Hazel made a noise that was half sob and half laugh, and threw her pen — her pen! — into the fissure after the darkness, and Isolde smiled up at them all with the last of herself, and Odd burst into tears, which for a wight involved something deeply unsettling that Silas politely did not examine.

"We won," Hazel said, as if checking the math twice. "We actually — Silas. Silas, we won."

He started toward her. The storm hummed in his chest, victorious, enormous, more himself than he had ever been.

And that was when he felt it — the pull. The vaults below, cracked open, empty of their lanterns. One hollow shape left among the broken hooks, waiting, patient as a coat on a hook. A body that had lain in the dark for three years, breathing shallowly, waiting for its soul to come home.

Waiting for *something* to come home.

The storm stirred inside him, and Silas understood — with the strange calm of a man reading the last line of a prophecy — that winning had sent him the bill.

* * *

### Hazel

Afterward there was dust, and ringing ears, and Odd loudly narrating the victory to anyone within earshot including several rocks. Hazel sat down hard on a fallen pillar and laughed until she cried, which she noted distantly was the second time in two days, and she was beginning to worry it was becoming a habit.

Tam's lantern-soul drifted down out of the swirling lights and settled beside her, dim and flickering, humming.

"Hello, you," she whispered, cupping it in her hands like a candle flame in wind. It leaned into her palms. Three years. Three years of ledger entries and guilt and a name she couldn't say aloud, and here he was, small and warm and still humming their mother's song, a little off-key even as a soul, which would have made her laugh if she hadn't been busy crying again.

"He'll want to know what you traded," Tam's light seemed to say, in the way of souls, which communicate mostly in accusation.

"Nothing I regret," Hazel lied gently.

Across the chamber, Silas stood very still at the mouth of the vault stairs, looking down into the dark where the empty body waited. The storm rolled visibly under his skin now, silver in the seams of him, and his expression was one Hazel recognized from mirrors: a person doing arithmetic they don't want to finish.

"Silas?" she called softly.

He turned. He smiled at her — that crooked, beekeeper's smile, the one that had survived exile and prophecy and a war — and he said, "Haze. Can you tell me about the healing wards? In the old charters. The ones that let a soul... settle back in?"

The question landed in her stomach like a stone.

Because she knew the old charters. She knew exactly what rites could press a soul back into waiting flesh. And she knew — scribe to the bone, clause-reader to the marrow — what else those rites required, and what the storm-caller standing in front of her, glowing like a bottled dawn, was quietly asking her to help him give away.

Behind him, Tam's light flickered. Hopeful. Waiting.

Hazel opened her mouth, and found that for the first time in her life, she did not know what to write.
