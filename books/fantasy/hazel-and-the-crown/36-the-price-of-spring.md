## 36. The Price of Spring

The silence afterward was the loudest thing Hazel had ever heard.

The deep thing had gone down like a lamp guttering — not with a scream but with a long, reluctant exhale, the way a fire gives up when someone finally covers it. The fissures in the vault floor had sealed themselves with a sound like a thousand books closing at once. Isolde was dust and a smile. Odd was sitting on a broken pillar, patting their own skull gently, muttering, "Well. That's told him," to nobody in particular.

And the storm was still in Silas.

That was the problem. That was the whole problem, sitting in the mountain's heart like an unexploded candle.

Hazel watched him from across the ruined chamber. He stood where his grandmother's cage had been, chains puddled around his boots like shed snakeskin, and lightning moved under his skin the way blood moves under skin — visible, alive, *too much*. His hair floated. His eyes were two small weather systems. When he breathed, the air tasted of ozone and rain-on-hot-stone.

He was magnificent. He was also dying.

She could see it the way she'd learned to see everything in the court beneath the mountain: in ledgers, in balances, in the arithmetic of what goes in and what must come out. A vessel built for one storm had taken on two — his inheritance and his grandmother's, braided together now, crackling against each other like argumentative siblings sharing a bed. Every few seconds a spark jumped off his shoulder and burned a small black star into the stone.

"Silas," she said carefully.

"I know." His voice came out doubled, harmonized with itself, thunder underneath. "I can feel it. It's like—" He laughed, and the laugh rolled around the chamber and came back to them from three directions. "It's like being asked to hold the ocean in a teacup. And I keep thinking, any moment now, the teacup's going to have opinions."

Odd raised a hand. "Speaking as someone who has been dead for four hundred years, I would like to register that 'the teacup having opinions' is how most hauntings start."

"Not helping, Odd."

"Wasn't trying to."

* * *

They found Tam where they'd left him.

The soul-vaults were quieter now — emptier, most of the lantern-souls risen through the fissures during the battle, streaming upward like reverse snowfall, like sparks returning to a fire. But one lantern still hung in its alcove, dim and stubborn, humming a lullaby in a voice like a struck glass. And below it, laid out on the cold stone shelf where the court had kept him all these years, was Tam's body.

Hazel knelt beside it and did not let herself cry, because she had done her crying already, years of it, compressed into the worst winter of her life, and what was left was something harder and more useful. She took his hand. It was cool but not cold. The court kept its investments well.

He looked older than she remembered and exactly the same. Both things were true. He'd been fifteen when she signed him away; the body hadn't aged a day, but her memory of him had grown up without him, and now the two versions lay side by side and refused to agree.

"He's still here," Silas said behind her. She felt him more than heard him — the air pressure changed when he entered a room now, a faint drop before rain. "The lantern. I can hear it. It's faint, but it's holding on. Souls don't leave while the body's warm, and the court never let him get cold."

"Then we put the lantern back and he wakes up," Hazel said. "That's how it works. That's how it's supposed to work."

Silence. The bad kind. The kind with arithmetic in it.

"Say it," she said, without turning around. "Whatever you're not saying. Say it out loud so I can argue with it."

"The lantern will go back in," Silas said quietly. "And the body will wake. For about a minute. Because there's nothing in this mountain anymore to feed a soul, Hazel. The court *was* the furnace. We broke the furnace. A soul coming back to a body down here is a candle lit in a room with no air." A pause, and then, gentler: "Unless the room gets air. Unless something fills the space first. Something with enough heat in it to keep a soul burning until it remembers how to burn on its own."

Hazel turned around very slowly.

Lightning moved under Silas's skin. Two storms' worth. A vessel built for one.

"No," she said.

"Hazel—"

"*No.* You don't get to do this. You don't get to stand there glowing like a saint in a stained-glass window and do arithmetic at me. Find another way."

"There isn't one." He said it simply, no drama in it at all, which was somehow worse. "I've been looking since the fissure closed. Believe me. I've turned over every stone in my head twice. The storm has to go somewhere or it tears me apart, and if it tears me apart it doesn't just kill me — it scatters. All that power, loose in a dead mountain, with one flickering soul and one empty body standing in it." He spread his hands. Sparks dripped off his fingertips and died on the floor. "Or I aim it. One vessel, one storm, one soul that needs a hearth. It's not even clever. It's just the only door in the room."

"You said—" Her voice cracked and she hated it. "In the valley. You said the valley gave you seven years of being nobody. Of being *quiet*. You said you loved it. You said your hands remembered bees better than lightning even back then—"

"They did." He smiled, and it was the smile from the valley, the beekeeper's smile, the one that had nothing to do with storms at all. "That's the point, Hazel. That's the whole point. I got seven years of being a man who tended bees. Some people don't get seven minutes. Tam hasn't gotten *any*. He's been a lantern in a drawer for six years because his sister loved him enough to damn herself." He crouched down next to her, next to Tam, close enough that the static lifted the fine hairs on her arm. "I'm not giving anything up. I'm spending it. There's a difference. Ask any merchant — oh wait, you're a scribe, ask any ledger. What's the difference between losing money and spending it?"

Hazel looked at him for a long time.

"Losing money is what happens *to* you," she said finally. "Spending it is what you *choose*."

"And?"

"And I hate that you've been reading my ledgers."

* * *

Odd insisted on being present. "I've attended four hundred years of funerals and not one single resurrection," the wight said, arranging themselves on a broken pillar with the air of a critic taking the best seat at the theater. "If I miss this one I shall be insufferable about it for another four hundred years, and none of us wants that."

Isolde was gone, but she had left instructions, in the way of princesses — scratched into the wall of the storm room in a hand that had gone shaky near the end:

*The storm knows its blood. Let it choose. Do not force it. Forced gifts break.*

So they didn't force it.

Silas knelt on the stone shelf beside Tam's body. Hazel held the lantern-soul in both hands — it weighed almost nothing and everything, and it hummed her mother's song against her palms, and she had to breathe through the scribe's exercises three times before her hands would stay steady.

"How does it work?" she asked. "Technically. I want it technically. I want it like a contract."

Silas huffed — a small thundercloud of amusement. "Of course you do. Fine. Clause one: I open myself. All the way. Both storms, everything I am, everything the exile left in me. Clause two: I pour it into him — slow, steady, like filling a cup, not like throwing a bucket. Clause three: you set the lantern in his chest at the same time, and the storm holds the shape of him open while the soul climbs back in." He paused. "Clause four."

"Go on."

"Nobody lets go until it's done. Whatever happens. Whatever I say. Even if I scream. Especially if I scream." His eyes — those small weather systems — found hers. "Because I'm probably going to scream, and I'd hate for it to be for nothing."

"That's a terrible clause," Hazel said. "Contracts should have exit terms."

"This one doesn't." Gently. "Some things shouldn't."

She wanted to argue. She was, by trade and temperament and six years of surviving the under-court, an arguer of the first water. She had argued with kings, jailers, wardens, and once, memorably, a contract that tried to argue back. She opened her mouth and every argument she owned lined up behind her teeth —

— and then she looked at Tam's face, and the arguments all sat down.

"All right," she said. "On three."

"On three," Silas agreed. Then, softer, almost to himself: "You know what's funny? I spent seven years hiding from this. Built a whole life around avoiding it. And now that it's leaving—" He shook his head, hair drifting. "Now that it's going, I finally feel like myself. Isn't that a stupid joke?"

"It's a terrible joke," Hazel said. "Tell it again at dinner sometime."

"Dinner," he repeated, tasting the word. "Yes. Good. Hold me to that. Hold me to dinner."

"One," said Hazel.

"Two," said Silas.

"Three," said Odd, who had never once respected a count in four hundred years, and it was probably better that way, because if they had waited Hazel might have lost her nerve, and instead the world simply began.

* * *

Here is what Hazel saw, and she wrote it down afterward, because writing things down was the only prayer she knew:

Silas opened like a book.

Not his flesh — nothing so gruesome. Something deeper, some page of him that had been pressed shut for seven years under the weight of exile and valley-quiet and careful, careful living. It opened, and what came out was not lightning first. It was *weather*. It was the smell of a valley before rain, the green-black pressure of a summer sky deciding, the first fat drop hitting hot dust. His grandmother's storm rose in him and met his own and they didn't fight — they braided. Old storm and young storm, wound together like rope, like a river remembering it used to be two streams.

Then he leaned forward and put his forehead against Tam's forehead, and poured.

The light went through Tam like dawn through a window. Hazel saw the frost of six years leave his skin in one long breath, saw color climb back up his throat like sap rising, saw his chest lift toward a breath that wasn't there yet — waiting, waiting, an inhale suspended at the top of a cliff—

"Now," Silas ground out, through teeth that sparked. "*The lantern. Now.*"

She set it against his sternum. The glass melted like sugar. The soul inside — dim, flickering, still humming — sank through skin and bone like a stone dropped into deep water, down and down, and for one horrible heartbeat nothing happened, and Hazel's entire future stood in that heartbeat wearing her brother's face—

Tam gasped.

It was the ugliest, wettest, most beautiful sound Hazel had ever heard. His back arched. His eyes flew open — brown, ordinary, human, *his* — and his hand shot up and grabbed a fistful of Silas's shirt with the strength of a drowning boy, and he said, in a voice rusted from six years of disuse:

"...Hazel. You look *terrible*."

And Hazel laughed and sobbed in the same breath, which she would deny in all subsequent tellings, and pressed her face into her brother's chest while his heart — his actual, beating, storm-warmed heart — thumped against her ear like someone knocking on a door after a very long journey home.

Behind them, softly, Silas made a sound.

She turned in time to see the light go out of him.

It didn't hurt him — that was the mercy, and she checked, she checked for years afterward in her memory, frame by frame, and it didn't hurt. It was more like watching a tide go out. The glow drained from his skin. The floating hair settled, strand by strand, onto his shoulders. The double voice collapsed into a single, plain, mortal voice mid-word — she never learned what word — and Silas Crane swayed, and caught himself on the edge of the stone shelf, and looked at his own hands.

Ordinary hands. Beekeeper's hands. A little scarred from hive work. Trembling now, but trembling the way anyone's hands tremble.

"Oh," he said. Very quietly. Very simply. Like a man setting down a load he'd carried so long he'd forgotten it had weight. "Oh, that's — huh."

"Silas?" Hazel scrambled over. "Are you — talk to me. Are you *you*?"

He flexed his fingers. He listened to the mountain — to the absence of the mountain, to the sudden enormous quiet where the storm had lived. Hazel watched him do it, watched him reach for something that wasn't there anymore, the way a tongue returns to a missing tooth.

"Yeah," he said. And then, wonderingly, with a laugh that was just a laugh, singular, unaccompanied by thunder: "Yeah, I'm me. Just — the regular-sized version." He looked up at her, and his eyes were just eyes, gray-green, tired, crinkled at the corners. "Hazel. I can't hear the weather."

"I know."

"I *can't hear the weather*. Do you understand? Seven years I pretended I couldn't, and now I actually can't, and it's—" He stopped. Started again. "It's quiet in here. It's so quiet. It's like the valley followed me out."

Hazel took his face in her hands. His jaw was stubbled and ordinary and slightly static-burned, and she kissed the corner of it, because some clauses don't need to be written down.

"Welcome back," she said, "to being nobody."

"Thank you," said Silas Crane, exiled storm-caller, retired teacup, "it's good to be back."

From the pillar, Odd blew their nose — a horrifying sound, given that they appeared to have no nose — and announced, "I'm not crying, I'm leaking grave-moisture, it's a medical condition," and Tam, propped up on his elbows, blinking like a cat in sunlight, looked from Hazel to Silas to the wight and said:

"So. Whose funeral did I sleep through, and why does the new guy smell like a thunderstorm?"

* * *

They walked out slowly, because Tam's legs had forgotten their job and Silas's knees had opinions about stairs, and because none of them, in their hearts, minded taking the long way through the halls of the court that had broken all of them.

The mountain was changing around them as they climbed. Hazel felt it in the walls — a loosening, a letting-go, like a fist unclenching finger by finger. Candles that had burned rendered memory for centuries guttered out one by one as they passed, and where each flame died, the memory it held drifted free: a taste of bread, a mother's voice, a summer afternoon, little ghosts of ordinary life rising through the stone like bubbles through honey. The freed souls had gone ahead of them, up through the fissures, and the mountain was following them out.

At the great gates — the ones that sealed each winter, the ones Hazel had sold her brother's soul to pass through — they stopped.

The gates were open. Not broken. Open, the way a house opens its door when it hears family on the road.

Beyond them, far below, the passes stretched north toward the valley, and the snow on them was — Hazel squinted, disbelieving her own eyes, disbelieving them a second time, a third — *sliding*. Sliding off the slopes in great wet sheets. Melting. In the middle of the long winter, in the season the court had enforced for ten thousand years, the world outside was thawing.

"Spring," Tam said. He said it like a foreign word he was trying on. "Is that — is that allowed?"

"Nothing about today was allowed," Hazel said. "That was rather the point."

Silas stood in the gate with the wind — a real wind, an ordinary wind, a wind with no lightning in it at all — moving his hair like it moved anyone's hair. He had his eyes closed. Listening, Hazel realized, to weather he could no longer hear, the way a man who has gone deaf might turn his face to music anyway.

"What's it like?" she asked him. "Being on this side of it?"

He considered for a long moment.

"Cold," he said. "A little scared. Kind of free." He opened his eyes and grinned at her, and it was the grin from the valley, from the hives, from the seven quiet years — the grin of a man with no destiny whatsoever. "Mostly hungry, honestly. Is that normal? Do ordinary people just walk around being hungry all the time? How did none of you mention this?"

"Welcome to mortality," said Odd. "The snacks are the whole compensation package."

And they stepped through the gates together — a scribe, a beekeeper, a boy with a storm behind his eyes, and a chatty wight carrying a bone-barge pole like a walking staff — out of the mountain that had let them go, and down toward a spring that no book had ever prophesied.

Behind them, deep in the dark, something vast and old sighed, settled, and slept.

Ahead of them, the snow was coming down off the passes in rivers.

And somewhere along the road home — though none of them noticed it until much later — the last page of a battered prophecy book turned itself over, and waited, blank, for whatever came next.
