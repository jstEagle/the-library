## 37. The Mountain Lets Go

The charter died the way old laws always die: loudly at first, then all at once.

Hazel felt it go through her quill-hand before she heard anything — a shudder in the ink itself, as if every contract ever signed beneath the mountain had been one long sentence and someone had finally set down the period. The frost-liveries on the fleeing riders grayed to plain wool. The candles lining the great hall guttered, then burned ordinary and honest, mere tallow doing its humble work.

And the Mountain King's crown of frozen breath — the crown he had worn for a thousand years of reasonable, terrible arithmetic — cracked like river ice in April.

He did not scream. That was somehow worse. He simply looked down at his hands, which were becoming something older than a king, something that had once run from a storm instead of fighting it, and said, in a voice gone small and human: "I only wanted it to stop."

"It stopped," Hazel said. "You just didn't recognize what stopping looked like."

Then the fissures opened wide, and the mountain began to give back everything it had taken.

* * *

Silas stood in the storm room with lightning still singing in his teeth, and watched the first soul rise.

It came up out of the vaults below like a bubble finding the surface — a soft gold light, no bigger than a fist, trailing little ribbons of memory behind it. It paused in front of him. He had the absurd feeling of being inspected.

"Go on," he told it. His voice was wrecked from shouting over thunder. "You're free. Up and out."

The soul drifted closer and touched his forehead, feather-light, and for half a heartbeat Silas knew things that were not his: a kitchen with blue shutters. A dog named after a saint. The smell of bread on a morning so ordinary it hurt. Then it was past him, up through the crack in the mountain's roof, and out.

He turned, and the whole chamber was full of them.

They rose through the fissures in their hundreds, their thousands — lanterns unhooked from their hooks, ascending through the broken stone in slow spirals, streaming upward like snowfall run backward through time. Some shot straight for the surface like arrows. Some dawdled, circling rooms they'd been kept in, saying goodbye to nothing. One very small soul stopped to investigate Odd, who was sitting on a fallen pillar eating a biscuit he'd produced from somewhere wights keep biscuits.

"Oh, don't you start," Odd said to it. "I know I'm fascinating. Everyone does. But you've got somewhere to be." The soul bobbed once, almost politely, and went. Odd wiped his eye with the back of his hand. "Dust," he announced. "Terrible dust down here. Always has been."

"You're crying," Hazel said.

"I'm *mourning the architecture*, actually. This place was going to be lovely once they finished the east colonnade. In another thousand years." He sniffed hugely. "Also possibly crying. Both can be true. I contain multitudes; it's mostly bones."

Silas laughed — a raw, surprised sound, the laugh of a man discovering his face still remembered how. Around them the mountain groaned and settled, shedding its winter the way an old coat slips off shoulders that have finally come home.

* * *

Isolde was waiting by the fissure where the light poured through.

She had held it closed with her own dissolving body, and now there wasn't much left of her — a silhouette of gold dust shaped like a young woman, brightening at the edges, already more morning than person. She was looking up at the column of rising souls with an expression Silas would carry for the rest of his life: not triumph, not peace exactly, but *recognition*. Ten thousand winters, and here at last was spring arriving inside her own house.

"You should go up too," Silas said. It came out clumsy. Everything he'd said to her had been clumsy. "There's room. There's nothing holding you here anymore."

"There never was," Isolde said. "That was rather the tragedy." She turned her dust-bright face toward him. "Storm-caller. You knelt. Do you know how long I waited to see a powerful creature kneel? My father never knelt once. Not to the deep thing, not to the dark, not even to sleep. He thought kneeling was dying." Her edges shimmered. "Kneeling is just knowing where you actually stand."

"Hazel taught me where I stand. Mostly by yelling."

"A good education has many forms." She lifted one fading hand toward the light. "Tell the sun I said thank you. We were close, once, before my father built his winter between us. Tell it Isolde remembers rain."

And she let go.

She didn't fall apart so much as *open* — the gold dust of her loosening, rising, joining the stream of freed souls pouring through the fissure, until she was indistinguishable from the others except that when she passed Silas, the air smelled briefly of rain on hot stone, and he heard, or dreamed he heard, a girl laughing in a summer that predated every winter since.

Odd took off his hat. All the way off. Wights did not do that for anyone living, dead, or royal.

"Well," Odd said, putting it back on. "I have exactly one story about that, and I'm telling it at your dinner party whether you invite me or not."

* * *

Above, the world was ending in the best possible way.

The Feast of Falling had been underway when the vaults broke — the whole under-court asleep at its tables, dreaming their harvested dreams, fat and safe and blind. Now the sleepers woke to alarms that weren't alarms anymore but bells rung backwards, ringing *out*, ringing *done*. They woke to candles burning honest tallow. They woke to a hall full of rising light pouring through cracks in the ceiling of the world, and to a charter dissolving off the walls in letters that had held them for a thousand years.

A steward-wight dropped an entire tray of candied plums and did not pick up a single one.

Frost-riders pulled off their helmets and found underneath faces that hadn't seen weather in centuries. Courtiers clutched ledgers that were, page by page, going blank — every debt, every harvest tally, every name written in the court's script-magic unwriting itself, because the authority that made the writing true had been dissolved by a runaway scribe with a stolen quill and a grudge.

In the middle of the great hall, Hazel stood on a table — because if you're going to dissolve a government, you may as well be comfortable — and read the final lines of the amended charter aloud while the ink evaporated behind her voice.

"—and the office of Mountain King is hereby retired, with thanks for services rendered, none of which we can currently locate—"

Scattered, disbelieving laughter. It moved through the crowd like warmth through cold hands.

"—and the soul-vaults are closed, sealed, and converted to storage for turnips, or whatever people store now—"

More laughter. Someone cheered. An elderly duchess who had been dreaming the same harvested dream for four hundred years sat down hard on the floor and wept with joy into a napkin.

"—and the long winter is concluded, effective immediately, by order of nobody, because nobody is in charge anymore, and honestly? It suits us."

She stepped down. The hall erupted — not in the court's old applause, which had been a transaction, but in the messy, undignified noise of people who have just been handed back their lives and don't know where to put them.

Tam found her in the crush.

He was still unsteady — a body newly inhabited walks like a borrowed coat — but he crossed the hall with Silas steadying one elbow and grabbed his sister around the middle and held on, and Hazel, who had faced kings and storms and the arithmetic of her own guilt without flinching, made a sound like a hinge that needed oiling and cried into her brother's shoulder.

"You're heavy," she managed.

"You fed me one biscuit in three days," Tam said into her hair. His voice was his own, roughened slightly, and underneath it, faint as distant weather, something rumbled. When he laughed, the candle flames all leaned toward him. "I have complaints. I have a *list* of complaints. Also I appear to be full of lightning?"

"That's Silas's fault."

"It's true," Silas said. "I'm sorry. It seemed better than the alternative."

Tam pulled back and looked at him — really looked, the way you look at a man who has given you the thing you cannot repay — and whatever he saw made him grin, and when he grinned, a small clean arc of static jumped between his teeth.

"We'll call it even when you teach me to aim this away from the good furniture."

* * *

They surfaced near dawn.

Not the mountain's false dawn, which had been a rumor of light filtered through a thousand feet of frozen stone, but actual dawn — raw, unfiltered, indecent. The survivors came up out of the fissures and the old gates blinking like newborns, hundreds of them, riders and duchesses and wights and one extremely smug grave-wight riding on Tam's shoulder because his legs were tired and technically he was luggage.

The sky over the mountain's shoulder was doing something no one alive had seen.

It was being *blue*.

Not the bruised violet of the long winter, not the white blindness of snow-glare, but blue — deep, ridiculous, extravagant blue, with clouds in it, actual clouds, fat and white and going about their business. And the sun — the sun was up, shameless, pouring heat onto snowpack that had forgotten what heat was.

The melting started immediately. It started everywhere at once, all across the mountainside, a sound like ten thousand small whispers: *drip, drip, drip.* Snow slumped off branches. Ice groaned in the valleys and gave way to water. Somewhere below, a river that had been silent for generations cleared its throat and began, tentatively, to sing.

An old frost-rider took off his glove and pressed his bare hand flat against a rock that was warm. Just warm. He stayed like that a long time.

"The last time I felt this," he said, to no one, to everyone, "I was a boy. I thought I'd dreamed it."

"You didn't dream it," Hazel said. "It was waiting."

* * *

They walked down out of the mountain through a world coming apart in the gentlest way.

Water ran in every channel, every rut, every old scar in the land. Green things were happening at the edges of the snow — tentative, doubtful green, the color of things asking permission. Freed souls streamed overhead all day, heading wherever souls go when the ledger closes: some toward villages, some toward the sea, some simply up, up, into the blue, home to whatever home meant now. Occasionally one would dip low over the travelers, curious, and Tam's chest would glow in answer, and the soul would bob a greeting — *one of us, walking around, wearing weather* — before continuing on.

By afternoon the snow was slush. By evening it was memory. They camped on bare ground, actual ground, brown and soft and smelling of mud and possibility, and Maren — who had come up from the fens with the thaw, poling her ferry through channels that hadn't flowed in years — found them by following the smell of Odd's cooking, which was considerable, and mostly intentional.

"So," Maren said, dropping onto a log with the ease of a woman collecting a debt. "You broke winter."

"We had help," Hazel said.

"You broke winter, killed a king with paperwork, adopted a corpse, and put a storm in a boy." She counted on her fingers. "Your mother owed me a favor. I'm declaring it paid. Anything else would be greedy."

"Maren," Silas said, "you sheltered two strangers in a tollhouse in the dark of the year and asked nothing."

"And look what it got me. Front-row seats." She accepted a bowl of stew from Odd, sniffed it suspiciously, ate it anyway. "Anyone who descends comes back missing something, I told you. What'd you lose?"

They considered. Hazel had lost her mother's face to a bone-barge, though she'd gotten something back in the trade — Ellery's letter, the blank page, the knowledge that she was not the first and would not be the last to sign herself away and claw back. Silas had lost a storm, which was to say an identity, which was to say the wall he'd built himself out of. Tam had lost years. Odd had lost nothing but had gained gossip enough for a century, which for a wight was the same as losing weight.

"Ask me in the spring," Silas said finally. "Ask me what I miss. Right now I only know what I've got."

Maren snorted. "That's the healthiest thing anyone's said under this sky in a thousand years. I'm putting it on the boat."

* * *

Night fell — a real night, brief and mild, full of stars that had spent the long winter hiding — and around the fire the found family arranged itself in the particular geometry of people who intend to keep finding each other.

Tam slept first, deeply, a boy-shaped heap with occasional flickers of lightning chasing themselves through his dreams. When he turned over in his sleep, the grass beneath him sprouted two inches.

"He's going to be a menace to agriculture," Hazel observed fondly.

"He's going to be a menace, full stop. He asked me today whether storms get bored." Silas poked the fire. "I said sometimes. He said good, because he gets bored too, and maybe they could be bored together."

Odd made a sound that in a human would have been a sob and in a wight was probably indigestion. "I'm writing that down. That's going in the barrow records. 'Year One of Spring: the boy and the weather agreed to be bored together.'" He pointed his spoon at each of them in turn. "Which brings me to business. I've been thinking. Barrows are damp, gossip is scarce, and I find I've grown accustomed to certain people." He said it aggressively, as though accusing them. "There's a hill in your valley. Nice hill. Good acoustics. Dead neighbors, quiet ones, no drama. I could relocate. Supervise. Provide narrative continuity."

"You want to move in with us," Hazel translated.

"I want to *supervise* you. There's a difference. It's a professional arrangement."

"You want to have dinner with us every night."

"...The dinners are incidental."

Silas smiled at the fire. His hands, he noticed, were doing the thing they'd started doing — turning toward small tasks, the way a compass turns north. Finding sticks. Feeding flames. Remembering bees. The storm was gone out of him, and what remained was quieter than he'd been in twenty years, and lighter, and terrified, and glad.

Tomorrow they would walk on. Down out of the high country, through the fens running loud with meltwater, past the tollhouse and the hollow hill and the drowned chapel where Ellery's ghost had finally, gratefully, stopped writing. Home to a valley that had forgotten magic and might, now, remember it gently — a valley with a nice hill and good soil and room at the table.

But tonight there was stew, and stars, and Tam murmuring in his sleep to a storm that murmured back, and Hazel leaning against Silas's shoulder with the blank page from the prophecy book spread carefully on her knees, quill in hand, thinking.

"What are you going to write?" he asked.

"I don't know yet." She turned the page toward the firelight. Blank as new snow, waiting. "That's rather the point, isn't it? Ellery wrote a thousand years of prophecy and left one page empty, because the only honest thing you can predict is that somebody will choose something." She tapped the quill against her lip. "I'm deciding what we choose."

Above them, a freed soul drifted past, paused, and hovered over the blank page as if reading ahead.

Whatever it saw there, it liked. It spun once — a small golden pirouette, unmistakably delighted — and rose away into the spring-dark sky, toward the valley, toward home, toward a table being set somewhere in the future for everyone who had ever been lost and found again.

Hazel smiled, dipped her quill, and began.
