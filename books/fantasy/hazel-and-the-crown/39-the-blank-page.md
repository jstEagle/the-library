## 39. The Blank Page

The book did not want to burn.

Hazel had expected it to go up like any other paper — she had burned enough contracts in her life, some of them legally, most of them not. But the prophecy book sat in the fire pit behind Silas's cottage and simply *refused*, the flames curling around its edges like water around a stone, licking at the cover without ever quite touching it.

"It's sulking," Odd said. The grave-wight was sprawled on a bench that had been dragged out of the kitchen for the occasion, chin propped on one bony hand, watching the fire with the expression of a theatergoer whose play had stalled between acts. "Books do that, when they've been written by someone stubborn. My barrow's got a ledger like that. Three hundred years dead, the man who wrote it, and it still argues with me about rent."

"Ellery wasn't stubborn," Hazel said. She crouched beside the pit, poker in hand, feeling the heat press against her face. "She was patient. There's a difference. Stubborn people push. Patient people wait for you to come to them."

"And which are you?"

"Both. It's a terrible combination. Ask anyone."

Tam laughed from the doorway — a real laugh, unpracticed, still new enough to him that he sometimes seemed startled by it himself. He came out carrying a basket of bread rolls wrapped in cloth, and he set them down on the bench next to Odd, who immediately began arranging them into what appeared to be a defensive formation.

Hazel watched her brother move and did the thing she still did every time, which was look away quickly so she wouldn't stare. Four months since the vaults. Four months since Silas had knelt in the storm-room and poured everything he was into Tam's empty body, and her brother had opened his eyes with lightning banked behind them like coals under ash. He hummed now, constantly. Their mother's song, mostly — the harvest song, the one about the wheat and the waiting — but sometimes other things, melodies none of them knew, that Hazel suspected were old storms remembering themselves.

He was alive. That was the arithmetic she could not stop doing: alive, alive, alive, set against everything it had cost.

"Hazel." Tam's voice pulled her back. "You're burning your sleeve."

She looked down. The edge of her cuff was smoking gently. She beat it out with the poker, and Odd applauded politely, and Silas came around the side of the cottage with an armful of kindling and stopped dead when he saw the book sitting untouched in the middle of the fire.

"It's still doing that," he said.

"It's still doing that."

"I could call a bit of wind. Fan it along."

"You have no wind to call," Hazel reminded him gently. "That was rather the point of the whole affair."

Silas grimaced and dropped the kindling by the pit. Watching him was another thing she'd learned to do carefully — watching him reach for magic that wasn't there anymore, the little flinch of a man patting his pockets for keys he'd given away. He grieved it. He would always grieve it; you didn't lose a limb and stop noticing. But his hands had found other work. Last week he'd shown her how to tell a failing hive from a thriving one just by standing quietly and listening, and when she'd asked where he'd learned patience like that, he'd said, "From a valley that spent ten years teaching me to be nobody," and then he'd gone red and pretended to check on the bees.

"The last page," she said now. "I want the last page."

Odd sat up. "Oh, we're keeping a piece? Excellent. I love a relic. I've got a shelf."

"Not a relic." Hazel reached into the fire with the poker, hooked the book, and dragged it out onto the stones. It lay there steaming, cover scorched, every page inside burned to lace and ash — except, as she'd known it would be, the final one. Blank. White as first snow. Ellery had left it that way a hundred years ago, and no fire in the world seemed willing to take it before someone wrote on it.

"A letter across time needs somewhere for the reply to go," Hazel said. "She told me that. In the chapel. I thought she was being poetic. Scribes are always being poetic; it's how we avoid being honest." She turned the blank page over in her hands. "But she meant it literally. She left room."

"For what?" Tam asked.

Hazel thought about all the things it could be for. A new prophecy. A warning. Her own name, signed in blood, the way the court had taught her — ink as binding, writing as law. She thought about contracts, about clauses, about the careful terrible power of putting words down in the right order.

Then she went inside and got a pen.

* * *

They made her do it properly, of course. This was the trouble with found families: they developed opinions.

"That's my good table," Silas protested, as she spread the page flat across it and weighted the corners with salt cellars.

"It's the only table."

"It's the only table because it's my good table."

"Then it's honored," Hazel said, dipping the pen. "Tables should be used for important things or they get ideas above their station."

Odd leaned over her shoulder, radiating curiosity the way other people radiated body heat. "What are you writing? Is it a prophecy? Please let it be a prophecy. I've never been in a prophecy. Well — there was the incident with the pilgrim and the barrow, but she was clearly making it up as she went, and frankly her rhymes were weak."

"It's not a prophecy."

"Then what's the point of a magic page?"

"The point," Hazel said, "is that it isn't magic. That's rather the whole lesson of the last year, if anyone was taking notes." She thought for a moment. Then she wrote:

*You are invited to dinner.*

She paused. Considered. Added, beneath, in her best court hand — the hand that had once forged seals and freed souls and dissolved a kingdom:

*No prophecy will be discussed. Attendance is mandatory. Bring nothing but yourselves; everything else is provided. The valley, the first evening of spring, one year from tonight. All pages of this invitation are final.*

She signed it: *Hazel, of the Under-Court, formerly. And her family.*

"There," she said, sitting back. "Now it's a contract."

Silas read it upside down, frowning. "A contract with whom?"

"With whoever needs it." She lifted the page carefully, feeling the strange living warmth of it, the way it seemed to lean toward her hand like a plant toward light. "Ellery wrote a hundred years ahead because she couldn't reach anyone sooner. So we write forward too. Somewhere down the line there's going to be another person holding this book — or one like it — another storm-caller or runaway scribe or grave-wight with too much gossip, standing exactly where we stood, convinced the future is already written and their part in it is fixed." She smiled, and it surprised her how easily it came. "And they're going to turn to the last page looking for their fate, and instead they're going to find dinner."

"That's not a prophecy," Odd said slowly, with dawning delight. "That's a *trap*. A lovely trap. You've weaponized hospitality."

"I'm a scribe. We weaponize everything eventually."

Tam took the page from her with both hands, the way you hold something sleeping. Lightning moved faintly behind his eyes as he read it, and the air smelled the way it does before rain — clean and green and full of promise. "One year from tonight," he said. "Spring festival. Everyone comes here."

"Everyone," Hazel confirmed. "Maren's ferrying people upriver — she owes me a debt and I intend to spend it shamelessly. Isolde—" she caught herself, the way she still did, grief and gratitude sharing a chair inside her chest, "—Isolde would have wanted the good cups used. So we'll use the good cups. Odd is bringing stories."

"True ones," Odd said solemnly. "It's the only kind worth telling. Well. Except the ones I make up. Those are better."

"And you," Hazel said to Silas, "are providing the honey."

"I'm providing the honey," he agreed, "and everyone else is providing everything else, and somehow this makes me the host."

"It's called delegation. The court taught me that too."

"The court taught you *tyranny*."

"Same curriculum."

Tam laughed again, and set the page down in the center of the table, and for a moment they all just looked at it — the blank page that wasn't blank anymore, the invitation traveling forward through time like a hand reaching back through dark water. Outside, the bees droned in the late light. Somewhere down the valley, Odd's new barrow stood open and swept, waiting for its first guests, because wights apparently entertained. The mountain to the north was only a mountain now, snow-capped and ordinary, its gates open, its halls empty, its long winter broken forever.

Hazel picked up the burned husk of the rest of the book and fed it back into the fire, piece by piece, and this time it went willingly, almost gratefully, ash rising straight up into the windless sky like something finally allowed to leave.

* * *

Dinner that night was not the festival dinner. The festival dinner was a year away and already being planned within an inch of its life — Maren had sent word by river-post that she was bringing a barrel of fen-plum wine and "a surprise," which everyone agreed was ominous. Tonight was just the four of them, and the good table, and stew, because Tam had insisted on making stew and no one had yet found a way to tell Tam no about anything, partly out of love and partly out of fear of the weather.

He cooked the way he did everything now: humming, absent, half-listening to something vast and patient moving behind his eyes. The stew was excellent. The windows frosted anyway. Silas kept having to get up and crack them open while apologizing to the room.

"It does that when he's happy," Hazel explained to Odd, who was eating with the focused intensity of someone who hadn't tasted anything in three centuries and intended to make up for lost time.

"Frost when happy," Odd mused. "My last roommate wept when happy. Much damper household. You've got it easy."

"He also hums when he sleeps," Silas said. "Whole house hums. Bees don't know what to make of it. I caught them clustering on his window yesterday like they were taking notes."

"They were," Tam said mildly, ladling more stew. "Bees are very interested in resonance. Your grandfather's bell — the iron one — it's the same principle. Everything that holds a storm learns to sing eventually. Or everything that sings learns to hold a storm. I forget which way round the books put it." He said it lightly, the way he said everything now, but Hazel saw Silas go quiet at *your grandfather's bell*, and saw him decide not to say anything, and loved him a little for both.

That was the shape of their evenings now. Grief and gratitude passing dishes back and forth. Silas mourning the storm even as he marveled at it living in Tam; Hazel flinching at memories of the court even as she built a home with tools the court had given her; Tam carrying lightning he'd never asked for and a second chance he couldn't remember earning, cheerful about both. None of them had forgiven anything yet, quite. Forgiveness was a long road and they'd only just laced up their boots. But they stayed. Staying, Hazel had decided, was most of it. Maybe all of it.

"So," Odd said, around a roll. "Tell me a story. It's night. Those are the terms."

"You extorted those terms from us under a hill," Silas said.

"And you agreed to them, which makes them binding. I learned contract law from a ghost with a grudge; I'll hear no appeals."

Hazel wiped her hands and thought. Around the table the candle — a real candle, bought in Greyharbor at ruinous price and hoarded ever since, lit tonight because some days deserved it — guttered pleasantly. Tam's frost crept a little further up the window glass, delicate as ferns.

"Once," she said, "there was a scribe who thought the future was a document. Something already drafted, already sealed, waiting only to be read and obeyed. She spent her whole life copying it out faithfully — other people's fates, other people's endings — and she was very good at it, and it nearly killed her, and it killed people she loved." She looked at the page lying on the table, at her own handwriting drying on it. "And then one day she learned that the future isn't a document at all. It's a blank page. And the only thing written on it is whatever you're brave enough to put there — and the only rule is that you have to share the table while you write."

Silence, the good kind. Tam's humming had gone soft and low, the harvest song, the wheat and the waiting.

Odd sniffed. Actually sniffed, which for a wight was roughly equivalent to weeping. "That's cheating," the wight said thickly. "That's a true one *and* a good one. Now I owe you a story back, and mine's about a barrow dispute, and it can't possibly compete."

"Tell it anyway," Hazel said. "We've got all spring."

Outside, the first true evening of the season settled blue and mild over the valley. The bees went quiet. The frost on the windows began, very slowly, to bloom outward into shapes that were almost — if you squinted, if you were generous, if you were family — flowers.

And on the table, in the candlelight, the last page of the prophecy book waited for the ink to dry, and then, very softly, turned itself — as if settling in, as if getting comfortable, as if it already knew every name that would one day sit down at this table, and had saved them all a seat.

* * *

Later — much later, when Odd had told the barrow dispute twice (the second version longer and substantially less accurate) and Tam had fallen asleep mid-sentence with his head on his arms and the candles frosting themselves a protective halo — Hazel stepped out into the yard alone.

The stars were thick. The mountain stood black against them, harmless, home to nothing but stone and memory. She breathed the cold sweet air and felt the ache in her chest that never entirely left — for her mother's face, sold to carry a bone-barge; for Isolde, dust on the wind and smiling; for the girl she'd been who signed a contract in blood because winter gave her no better page to write on.

From inside, faintly, she heard Silas moving around the kitchen, banking the fire, covering Tam with a blanket, doing the small unglamorous work of keeping people. From the barrow down the lane, a thread of lamplight and the murmur of a wight talking to its collections. From the hives, the low contented hum of ten thousand small lives holding their storm through the night.

Hazel looked up at the blank sky over the valley — no prophecies in it, no futures foretold, nothing written anywhere at all — and found, to her considerable surprise, that she wasn't afraid of it.

She went back inside to set the table for morning.
