## 40. Spring, Unprophesied

The valley had never held a festival before. It had held harvest suppers and a grudging midwinter feast where everyone complained about the cold in the tone of people who had agreed to be cold together, but a spring festival — with bunting, with dancing, with an actual fiddler hired from over the pass — was new. The bunting had been Maren's idea. The fiddler had been Odd's, though Odd could not attend in daylight and had instead sent a list of songs it wanted requested, all of them funeral marches, which the fiddler declined to play on the grounds that it was spring.

Silas spent the morning of the festival doing what he did every morning: tending bees.

He had tried to explain this to Hazel once — that after everything, after storms and soul-vaults and a king unmade by his own charter, his greatest ambition was to stand very still near a box of stinging insects — and she had looked at him for a long moment and said, "You know what, yes. That tracks." She understood better than most. She had been a scribe at the court beneath the mountain, where every hour was measured in ink and dread, and now she kept a garden and argued with the goats, and she said the arguing was the best part.

The bees were calm today. He liked to think they knew the season had turned properly at last — not the thin, apologetic spring the valley used to get, but a real one, fat and green and loud with birdsong. A year ago the long winter had broken mid-feast, the under-court dissolved into dust and paperwork, and the freed souls had risen through the mountain's fissures like snow falling upward. Some of those souls had passed through this valley on their way to wherever souls go. Silas had been standing in his yard when they came, and he swore ever after that one of them had paused above him, warm as a hand on his shoulder, before continuing on.

He still didn't know if it had been Tam's. Tam himself didn't remember anything from the years between the contract and the waking. But Tam hummed their mother's song while he worked, and there was a storm behind his eyes — Silas's storm, poured out of him like the last of a flask — and sometimes, when the weather turned rough, Tam would go quiet and look east, and say, "Something's coming," two days before it came.

It was a useful talent on a bee farm.

"Silas!" Hazel's voice carried up from the house, sharp with festival-day urgency. "If you're going to bathe, bathe now! Maren's coming early and she'll comment!"

"She always comments."

"Then give her something new to comment on!"

* * *

By noon the meadow below the village green had filled with people. Not many — the valley was small and had spent a century perfecting the art of minding its own business — but enough. Maren arrived with her one eye bright and her ferry-pole repurposed as a maypole anchor, having decided without consulting anyone that the festival required a maypole. Odd sent word via a very confused mole that it would attend after dark and requested its usual seat be placed "somewhere with good acoustics and no consecration whatsoever."

Tam ran the cider stall. He was twenty-six years old and roughly four months old, depending on how you counted, and he had thrown himself into living with the ferocious appetite of someone making up lost time. He had learned to whistle in March. He was currently learning to flirt, badly, with the fiddler, who found him hilarious, which Silas suspected was the whole strategy.

And Hazel — Hazel wore her good dress, the blue one, and stood at the edge of the dancing with a cup of cider she wasn't drinking, watching everything with the expression of a woman cataloguing exits out of pure habit.

"You could dance," Silas said, arriving beside her.

"I could also stand here assessing structural risks."

"The maypole is anchored by a ferrywoman who has drowned three rivers. I think we're safe."

Hazel smiled despite herself. A year ago, Silas had learned, she hadn't known how to smile without calculating what it would cost. Now she did it like breathing. Mostly. There were still nights — he'd heard her, some evenings, talking softly to the empty chair where her mother's memory used to sit, because she'd traded her mother's face to a grave-wight for passage across consecrated ground, and grief without a face is a strange animal to feed. But Odd, in its roundabout way, had become part of the payment plan. Every night, a story. True ones. And slowly, in Odd's barrow in the valley's edge, Hazel was rebuilding her mother from the ground up: her laugh, reconstructed from Hazel's memories of sound; her hands, from the way she'd kneaded bread; her stubbornness, which needed no reconstruction at all.

"There," Hazel said suddenly, nodding toward the road. "Don't look. Peddler."

Silas looked immediately, because he had never once managed *don't look* in his life.

The peddler came up the valley road with a pack on his back and a smile already deployed, a broad man with sunburned cheeks and the particular cheerfulness of someone whose livelihood depended on strangers wanting things. Silas felt the old recognition settle over him like a coat. He knew that walk. He knew that pack.

"I know him," he said.

"You can't."

"He sold me the book, Hazel. The prophecy book. Right here, almost exactly here, a year and a week ago."

"That's — " Hazel stopped. Frowned. "Huh."

The peddler reached the edge of the green, set down his pack with theatrical weariness, and spread his wares on a blanket: ribbons, tin whistles, honey-cakes, and — Silas's stomach dropped with a familiarity that felt almost funny at this point, almost like a joke between old enemies — books.

"Prophecies!" the peddler called out, cheerful as a rooster. "Genuine prophecies! Futures foretold! Love, fortune, weather, doom — doom slightly extra!"

The villagers, to their credit, mostly laughed. One or two bought ribbons. Tam bought a honey-cake and attempted more flirting. Nobody approached the books except, inevitably, Silas and Hazel, who crossed the green side by side like two soldiers walking back onto a battlefield they'd sworn off.

The peddler saw them coming and his smile changed — softened, or sharpened, Silas couldn't tell. Up close he looked older than he had a year ago, or perhaps Silas had simply gotten better at reading faces since the year he'd learned that faces could lie for a hundred years at a time.

"Well now," the peddler said. "The storm-caller. And the scribe." He tipped an imaginary hat. "I wondered if I'd find you."

"You sold me a book," Silas said. "A book of prophecies. With my face in it."

"Did I?" The peddler's eyes crinkled. "I sell a lot of books."

"It turned its own pages. It rewrote itself. It was written by a dead woman trying to save the world across a hundred years."

"Mm." The peddler considered this. "That does narrow it down."

"Why are you here?" Hazel asked, and her voice had the flat court-scribe edge to it, the one that made jailer-wights reconsider their careers.

The peddler shrugged, easy as anything. "Same reason as always. Things need delivering. Roads need walking." He gestured at the blanket. "Buy a book?"

"No," they said, together.

"We'll buy the whole stock," Silas amended, because he had thought about this — actually thought about it, in the nights after the mountain, lying awake next to a woman who had once signed away her brother's soul and was determined never again to leave a contract ambiguous. "Every prophecy book you have. Name your price. We're buying them for firewood."

The peddler blinked. Then he laughed — a real laugh, surprised out of him, big enough that Tam looked over from the cider stall. "Firewood!"

"They don't predict," Hazel said. "They negotiate. They herd. They take the future and fold it down small until it fits in somebody's plan, and then they charge you your life for the privilege of reading it. We've seen how they're made." She tapped the blanket. "So. Price."

The peddler looked at her for a long moment. Then at Silas. Something moved behind his eyes — something old, something that made Silas think, absurdly, of Isolde, of ten thousand winters watched from behind a kind face.

"Three coppers each," he said quietly. "There are nine."

Silas paid. Twenty-seven coppers, and the peddler wrapped the books in brown paper like a butcher wrapping cuts, and handed the bundle over with both hands, almost ceremonially.

"For what it's worth," the peddler said, shouldering his empty pack, "the last page of every one of those is blank. Never could figure out why. Enjoy your festival." And he walked back down the valley road, whistling, and did not look back, and Silas did not follow him, which was possibly the hardest-won victory of his entire life.

* * *

They burned the books that evening, after the dancing, in the great fire pit on the green while the fiddler played something slow and the first stars came out. Odd attended openly for once, wrapped in a traveling cloak that fooled absolutely no one, sitting close to the flames because wights run cold, telling anyone who'd listen that this was the finest public execution it had witnessed in three centuries.

"One page at a time," Odd insisted. "Make it last. I want to see whether they scream."

"Books don't scream," said Tam.

"You haven't met these books."

They fed the fire one book at a time. Eight of them burned like ordinary paper — curled, blackened, gone. Hazel read snatches aloud as they burned, futures for strangers in far-off towns, small herding prophecies designed to nudge farmers toward particular fields and merchants toward particular roads. Each one she read, then tore out the blank last page and tucked it into her satchel before consigning the rest to the flames.

"What are you doing?" Silas asked.

"Ellery left her last page blank," Hazel said, feeding another book to the fire. "For whoever broke the prophecy. For us. Blank pages are the only honest thing these books have to offer. I'm collecting them." She glanced at him sideways, firelight on her face. "I have plans. Stationery-related."

The ninth book was different.

Silas felt it before he touched it — a hum against his palms, faint and familiar, like the memory of a storm. When he opened it, the pages were blank. All of them. No prophecies, no futures, no folded-down fate. Just clean paper, waiting.

Except the last page. The last page had writing on it, in fresh ink, in a hand Silas recognized because he had dreamed about it for a year afterward: the looping, urgent script of Scribe Ellery, dead a century, author of the letter-across-time that had dragged two fools down a mountain and out the other side into spring.

*To the ones who broke it,* the page read. *If you are reading this, the book found you again, which means the roads still need walking and the world still needs its odd deliveries. I could not write you a future. Nobody can. But I can leave you a question, which is nearly as good.*

*What will you do with the spring nobody predicted?*

Silas read it twice. Then he showed Hazel. Then, because it seemed right, he showed everyone — Tam leaning in with cider on his breath, Maren squinting with her one eye, Odd craning its hooded head and making a sound like a kettle taking personal offense.

"A question," Odd said disgustedly. "A hundred-year-old ghost and she leaves a *question*. Where's my doom? Where's my riddle with teeth?"

"That is the doom," Hazel said softly. "Don't you see? She's asking us to answer it. Every day. Forever." She took the page — gently, the way she handled everything that mattered — and did not burn it. Instead she sat down cross-legged by the fire, pulled out her pen, and smoothed the blank page on her knee.

"What are you writing?" Tam asked.

"An invitation," Hazel said. "To dinner."

She wrote it right there, by firelight, while the fiddler tuned up for another round and the valley's first spring festival spilled warm and loud around them:

*You are invited. Spring, next year, and the year after, and every year after that. Bring nothing but yourself. There will be bees' honey and bad flirting and a wight who tells stories and a woman who is learning, slowly, to be forgiven. No prophecy required. Attendance optional. Hope mandatory.*

*— H., S., T., & co.*

When she finished, the ink shimmered — just once, just faintly, the way the old book used to — and then settled, ordinary and permanent. Somewhere down the valley road, Silas would have sworn he heard a peddler laugh.

* * *

Later — much later, when the fire had burned to embers and the dancers had gone home and even Odd had shuffled off toward its barrow complaining that the acoustics had deteriorated — Silas and Hazel walked out past the bee-yard to the low hill where they could see the whole valley laid out under the stars. His valley. The place that had forgotten magic so he could survive, and which had welcomed magic home again once the storm went to live behind Tam's eyes.

"Do you miss it?" Hazel asked. She asked sometimes. She never pushed.

Silas considered, as he always considered, honestly. He missed the storm the way you miss a limb in cold weather — a phantom ache, a reach for something not there. On the night the deep thing stirred and he had knelt, not to a king but to his family, and aimed everything he was downward at the hunger older than the court — on that night he had been the largest thing in the world. He would never be that again. His hands remembered lightning the way they remembered his grandfather's iron bell: distantly, tenderly, uselessly.

But his hands also remembered bees. And this morning the bees had been calm, and the honey would be good this year, and Hazel was standing beside him with a blank page full of dinner invitations in her satchel, and somewhere behind them Tam was probably teaching the fiddler to whistle.

"Some nights," he said. "Most mornings, no."

Hazel nodded. Then she frowned, tilting her head at the sky. "Silas. Do you see that?"

He followed her gaze. Over the eastern ridge — the pass, the road, the wide world beyond — clouds were gathering. Not storm clouds. Nothing like storm clouds. Just a few soft shapes, moonlit, drifting against the wind.

And as they watched, the clouds parted — neatly, deliberately, like a curtain drawn by a considerate hand — and the stars behind them arranged themselves, briefly, unmistakably, into a shape.

Into letters.

Hazel had her pen out before Silas finished exhaling. "I'm just saying," she said, already writing, already smiling, "if the sky is going to start leaving messages, someone responsible ought to be taking dictation."

Above the ridge, the stars rearranged themselves again — slower this time, patient, playful — and Silas laughed, a real laugh, surprised out of him, big enough that the bees, safe in their hives in the dark, shifted and hummed.

The sky was writing. And for the first time in his life, Silas Crane intended to write back.
