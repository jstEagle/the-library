## 1. The Taster's Tongue

The stew was wrong before it ever reached my spoon.

I knew it the way a blind woman knows a door is open—by the air moving through it. The kitchen had sent up venison stew in a silver tureen, and the steam rising off it carried something underneath the rosemary and browned bone. Something green. Something sweet in the way that sweetness turns your stomach sideways when you know what to listen for.

I stood at my station behind the king's right shoulder, hands folded, eyes down, and let three seconds pass. Three seconds is nothing. Three seconds is also enough time for a taster to decide whether she wants to live through the afternoon.

"Your Majesty." I kept my voice flat, servant-plain. "The stew is off."

King Aldric the Continuer looked up from his dispatches with the expression of a man interrupted by weather. "Off."

"Turned," I said, which was kinder than *poisoned*, and safer for everyone in the room.

The chamberlain made a small sound like a hinge. The captain of the guard did not move, because captains of the guard never move until someone tells them whom to point at. I lifted the ladle myself—no one else would touch it now—and poured a measure into the tasting bowl, and drank, and stood very still while the nightshade introduced itself to my blood.

It was a light hand. That was the interesting part. Atropa belladonna, steeped long and low, folded under the fat of the broth where most tongues give up. A heavy dose would have killed me inside an hour; this one wanted the king sick by evening, weak by morning, pliable by week's end. Someone in this palace wanted him *managed*, not dead. That narrowed the list considerably, and widened it in ways I didn't like.

My mouth went dry. My pulse climbed and then, trained as a hound, lay back down. Twelve years of measured doses had taught my body every poison in the crown's ledger the way a clerk knows his columns: belladonna, foxglove, hemlock, arsenic in its slow gray patience. I could name each one by the shape of its arrival. This one arrived like a guest who'd been told where the sideboard was.

"Well?" the king said.

"A mild bitterness at the finish," I reported. "Nightshade, prepared by someone patient. Not enough to kill. Enough to soften."

Silence, of the particular quality that means everyone is recalculating their afternoon. Then the king waved two fingers—the royal gesture for *make it someone else's problem*—and the chamberlain swept the tureen away, and the captain finally moved, going to find whoever had carried the stew upstairs.

I went back to standing still.

That is the whole art of my position, if you're wondering. Stand still. Look dull. Be furniture with a tongue. The court calls us food-tasters *the king's spoons* behind our backs, and they think we don't hear, and we make sure they keep thinking it, because a spoon that hears things gets melted down.

But I will tell you what twelve years in this palace actually taught me, since no one asked: it taught me exits. There are four from the great hall—the main doors, the servants' stair behind the arras, the wine passage that runs to the buttery, and the window over the east cloister that drops eleven feet into ivy if you don't mind scratches. There are six from the kitchens. There are two from my sleeping cell, and one of them is mine alone, cut with a stolen chisel over four months of nights, hidden behind a shelf of salt crocks. I have never used it. I check it weekly anyway. Hope, in my experience, should be maintained like a blade: oiled, sheathed, never flourished.

* * *

Afterward, in the corridor outside the hall, Tam found me. Tam always finds me. He's a kitchen boy, sixteen or so, all elbows and flour, with a way of materializing at my elbow like steam off a pot.

"You saved him again," Tam said. "That's twice this season. They'll give you a medal."

"They'll give me extra salt rations and a longer shift," I said, walking. He fell into step, because he always falls into step. "Medals are for people whose names go on things."

"That could be you. Lior Hartwell, Hero of the King's Table."

"Don't use my name in corridors."

He blinked. "It's just your name."

"It's just a knife if someone picks it up." I didn't look at him. In this palace, names are handles, and handles are for pulling. I had watched a laundress lose her post because a chancellor learned her daughter worked in the mint; I had watched a gate sergeant hang because he'd once shared bread with the wrong cousin. My name appeared on exactly one document in the kingdom—the taster's registry—and I had read that document so many times I could recite the ink smudge on the fourth line.

Tam walked with me anyway, past the tapestry of the Founding, past the cold hearth in the west gallery that hadn't been lit in anyone's memory. The palace had a hundred hearths and half of them stood dark, swept and stacked with wood nobody burned. Economy, the steward said. But fires are cheap and coal is cheaper, and I had stopped believing the steward's arithmetic years ago. Some rooms you don't warm on purpose. Some rooms the palace wants cold.

"You eat lunch?" Tam asked. "There's barley soup. Real barley, not the weevil kind."

"I ate."

"You didn't. You never eat. You taste everything and eat nothing, like some kind of—" he groped for it, "—like a lock that only opens outward."

I stopped at the junction where the servants' stair met the gallery, which is a good place to stop, because from there I can see both directions and the window. "Tam."

"Yeah?"

"Why do you keep doing this?"

"Doing what?"

"This. Walking with me. Talking at me. Soup diplomacy." I turned and looked at him properly, which I try not to do, because faces are debts. He had flour on his jaw and a burn scar on his wrist from a kettle, and he looked at me the way nobody in this building looks at anything—straight on, unafraid, like I was a person instead of a procedure.

"Because you're the only one here who tells the truth about the food," he said simply. "And because somebody should know you're more than a spoon."

Something in my chest did a thing I didn't authorize. I filed it under *indigestion* and moved on.

"Eat your soup," I said. "Stay away from the east wing tonight. And don't know me, Tam. It's safer for you, and it's better for me, because people who are known can be *used*. People who are known can be *found*." I heard how it sounded even as I said it—like a lesson, like a wall—and hated the lesson and built the wall higher. "Go."

He went, but slowly, looking back once, and I pretended not to see it, and we were both lying, and that was the closest thing to friendship either of us could afford.

* * *

That night I sat in my cell with a candle stub and did what I do every night: inventory.

Body first. The nightshade was metabolizing cleanly; by morning there'd be nothing but a headache and a faint sweet taste at the back of my throat, my body's little receipt. I pressed my thumbnail into my forearm and watched the color come back—two heartbeats, normal. I flexed my hands. Steady. The ledger balanced.

Then the room. Candle, cot, blanket, the false brick behind the salt shelf. The knife sewn into the mattress seam. The three copper coins I kept not for spending but for remembering what money felt like. On the floor beneath the cot, loose under a board, a thing I never let myself look at too long: a sprig of hazel, dried brown, snapped from a tree that no longer existed anywhere I could prove.

I had taken it the day I left home. I was nine years old. I don't remember much of that day—that's the mercy or the crime of it, I've never decided which—but I remember running, and I remember the hazel hedge along the lane, and I remember that I took a piece of it because taking a piece of a place is how children carry places. Ashenmere. Even the word feels like it belongs to someone else now. Villages burn and villages stand; either way they stay on maps. Mine had done neither. It had simply stopped being spoken of, the way a name stops being spoken after a scandal, except there had been no scandal, and no grave, and no survivors' society, and no letters returned undelivered. Just silence, arranged neatly, official.

I put the sprig back under the board. I did this every night and told myself every night it was sentiment, and every night the word fit worse.

The candle guttered. Down the corridor, a door closed somewhere, soft as a held breath. I listened the way I always listen—not for sounds, but for the absence of them, the shape of the quiet, because in this palace the quiet has a texture and lately the texture had changed. Fewer footsteps in the east wing. More wax-sealed couriers leaving at odd hours. The Ministry of Continuity had been given new offices above the old chapel, and men in gray went in carrying ledgers and came out carrying nothing visible at all, which is the worst thing a man can carry.

I blew out the candle and lay down and performed my final ritual, the one no one trained me in, the one I invented out of pure stubbornness: I named myself. *Lior Hartwell. Taster. From—* and here the ritual snagged, as it always snagged, on the village, the way a key snags in a rusted lock. *From the mountains. From the north. From.*

From a place I could walk to and not find.

Sleep came the way it comes to soldiers and spoons: fast and shallow, one ear open.

And in the dark behind my eyes, just before sleep took me, something happened that had never happened in twelve years of careful, poisoned, invisible life. A warmth moved through me—not the nightshade, nothing like the nightshade, this was the opposite of poison, this was the feeling of being a child standing too close to a hearth—and it seemed to say, in a voice like coals settling,

*Lior.*

Not Hartwell. Not taster. Just the name itself, spoken whole, the way you speak a name when you've been waiting a long time to use it.

I sat up in the dark with my knife already in my hand, heart slamming, and the cell was empty, and the candle was cold, and far below the floors of the palace, in whatever cold rooms the Ministry keeps, I swear on the hazel sprig under my floorboard—I swear something answered back.
