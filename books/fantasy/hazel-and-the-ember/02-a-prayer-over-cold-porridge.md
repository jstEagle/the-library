## 2. A Prayer Over Cold Porridge

The garrison at Caelwatch sat so high the air tasted of iron, and the porridge they served tasted of nothing at all.

Lior stood in the provisioning hall with her sampling spoon and her ledger face, watching a quartermaster's clerk count sacks of barley that had already begun to rot. Cold storage. Bad seals. Someone up here was skimming coin off the king's mountain bread, and someone down in the capital would want to know who. That was the work. Not tasting—*accounting*. Twelve years of swallowing small deaths had made her less a servant than an instrument, and instruments got sent where fingers were needed.

"Try it," the clerk said. He was young, chapped-lipped, nervous in the way of men who knew what was in the sacks. "You're the taster. That's your whole purpose, isn't it? Tell us it's fit for the garrison."

It wasn't. She could smell the mold from three paces, sweet-rot under the barley dust, the kind that didn't kill you quick but filled your lungs like wet wool over a winter. But she dipped the spoon anyway, because refusing would mean explaining, and explaining meant being noticed.

The porridge was cold. Not cool—cold, the temperature of something left out overnight by someone who'd stopped caring whether anyone came home to eat it. It sat on her tongue like paste. Her body catalogued it automatically: mold spores, trace ergot, nothing lethal, nothing worth reporting beyond negligence. Her tongue had twelve years of poisons folded into its memory, each one a small scar she'd earned on schedule, administered by the palace physicians in measured doses until her blood became a ledger no assassin could read.

She swallowed. The hall was empty except for the clerk and one old soldier dozing by the hearth, and the hearth itself was dying—green wood, badly split, more smoke than flame.

And because no one was watching, because the porridge was cold and the room was gray and she was nine hundred feet above the life she'd carefully refused to live, Lior did the thing she never did in front of witnesses.

She prayed.

Not really. That was the lie she told herself. It wasn't prayer; it was habit, a child's rhythm worn into her mouth like a groove in a stair. *Ember warm me, Ember keep me, Ember know my name.* Four words and a name, murmured over cold food since before she could write, back when names still meant something and every village had a hearth-god squatting in its fire like a fat orange cat. The old gods were ash now—all of them, purged in the Reformation before she was old enough to remember their faces—and praying to them was technically heresy, which was why she only ever did it alone, over food no one else would eat, in rooms where the silence was thick enough to smother the words before they reached any ear that mattered.

*Ember warm me, Ember keep me, Ember know my—*

"Warm enough?"

Lior's spoon stopped halfway to her mouth.

The voice came from the hearth. Not from the soldier snoring beside it—from *in* it, from the coals, low and cracked like a log settling. She told herself it was the wind in the flue. Mountain garrisons had bad chimneys. Wind made sounds. Wind did not make words.

"You've been saying that all your life," the hearth said. "Every cold meal. Every empty room. I counted."

The spoon rang against the floor. Lior took two steps back before her body remembered it was supposed to be invisible, and then she stopped, because running was visible, screaming was visible, and the old soldier hadn't stirred—he heard nothing, saw nothing, and some deep animal part of her understood that whatever was speaking was speaking *only* to her.

"I'm not—" she started, and her voice came out as a whisper, which was worse. "There's nothing there. You're ergot. Ergot makes you see things. I read the report myself."

"Ergot," the fire said, amused, "doesn't know your name."

"My name is Lior Hartwell." Flat. Court-trained. A name was just a label; labels were safe.

"That," said the hearth gently, "is what you call yourself. I mean your name. The one your mother burned into the first loaf she ever baked you. The one spoken over your cradle in Ashenmere, in the old way, with salt and smoke. *Liora Ember-Hart.* Daughter of the hearth-tenders. Marked."

The word went through her like a blade through wet bread.

Ashenmere. She hadn't heard that word in twenty years, not from anyone's mouth, because she had trained herself never to say it and everyone who might have said it to her was gone or scattered or dead. She'd been six when the hunters came. She'd been six when she walked out of those mountains alone. Whatever name had come before *Lior Hartwell*, food-taster, had been buried so deep she'd convinced herself she'd invented the memory—the salt, the smoke, the feeling of being *known* by a fire.

"No record says that," she whispered. "I've looked. I looked for years. There is no record of me before the capital. No birth entry. Nothing."

"Because records burn," the hearth said, "and I am what's left when everything else does."

The coals shifted. And Lior saw—or thought she saw, ergot, tell yourself it's ergot—a shape in the embers, small and hunched, like a figure sitting too close to its own warmth. One ember brightened, dimmed, brightened again. Breathing.

"They told us you were dead," she said. "All of you. The Reformation. They burned the groves and broke the standing stones and the gods just... went out."

"Some went out." A crackle, like a shrug. "Some were eaten. Some ran. And some—" the ember pulsed, "—were too small to bother with. A hearth-god of one village, in a country of ten thousand villages. I was beneath their notice. So I did what small things do. I hid."

"Where?"

"In the only place they couldn't search." The warmth in the room seemed to lean toward her. "In a prayer. In a habit. In four words a frightened child kept saying over cold food long after the god they addressed was supposed to be gone. You kept the door open, little taster. Twenty years, you kept it open. I've been waiting inside your memory all along—curled up small, living on scraps of attention, growing fat on every 'Ember keep me' you never meant."

Her back found the wall. She let it. The stone was cold through her uniform and the cold felt honest, something to stand against while the world tilted.

"This is a trick," she said. "Poisoners dream. The physicians warned me—nightshade residue causes—"

"Liora."

Just the name. Just the old name, in the old cadence, the way it must have been sung over a cradle with salt and smoke, and Lior's eyes stung before she could stop them, and that—that was the terrifying part. Not the talking fire. The *relief*. Somewhere under twelve years of careful invisibility, under the ledger-body and the ledger-face, a six-year-old girl heard her name spoken by a hearth and wanted, with a shame that scorched worse than any poison, to walk into the warmth and be known.

She hated herself for it. She hated the fire for making her feel it.

"What do you want?" she managed. "Gods want things. Worship. Sacrifice. I don't have anything. I'm a taster. I taste things and I die slowly and I keep my head down."

"You have the one thing I need." The ember brightened, steadied. "You remember. Not everything—not yet—but more than anyone left in this country remembers about the places that are gone. And Liora, listen to me now, because the road down from this mountain is going to feel longer than the road up." The fire guttered, and for a moment the shape in the coals looked less like a hunched creature and more like something starving. "They are not finished. The ones who ate the great gods. They've learned to eat smaller. Villages. Names. Whole towns, unwritten. And the next place on their table—"

The hearth-flame bent sideways, though there was no draft.

"—is a village called Ashenmere. If it still exists. If it hasn't already been eaten and we're both remembering a ghost."

The floor did not move, but Lior felt it tilt anyway. Twenty years of careful smallness, of ledger-blood and ledger-face, of being no one from nowhere—and the nowhere had a name, and the name was scheduled for erasure like a line struck from an account book.

"Why me?" she asked. "Why wait twenty years inside a child's stupid prayer?"

"Because you were marked," the fire said simply. "Because your mother gave you to me the way other mothers give children to rivers or roads. And because, little taster, of everyone left alive in this kingdom—you are the only person whose body has been trained to survive exactly the kind of feast they're planning."

Somewhere down the corridor, boots. The clerk returning, keys jangling. The old soldier snored on.

Lior straightened off the wall, retrieved her spoon from the floor, and put her ledger-face back on like a mask she'd worn so long it had grown roots. When the clerk came in, she was writing in her report: *Barley stores compromised. Recommend replacement. Garrison provisions unfit.*

Fit for nothing, she wrote, and meant the whole country.

That night, packing her sample case for the descent, she caught herself murmuring the four words over a heel of garrison bread, and this time she did not pretend it was habit.

*Ember keep me,* she thought. *Tell me the rest on the road.*

The coal she'd smuggled out of the hearth, wrapped in her spare shift at the bottom of her case, glowed faintly through the linen—like something patient, finally fed, beginning to wake.

* * *

They gave her a mule and a two-man escort for the descent, which meant they expected her back, which meant the Ministry's paperwork had already decided her return was a fact. Lior rode with her hands loose on the reins and her mind tight around the coal warming her saddlebag like a second heartbeat.

Below Caelwatch, the valley opened gold and ordinary: harvest carts, chimney smoke, a bell ringing noon in a town whose name she'd never bothered to learn. Everything in its place. Everything accounted for.

Except that as they rounded the third switchback, the coal pulsed once—hard, urgent, a fist knocking from inside the linen—and a voice like banked heat spoke against her spine:

"Count the chimneys, Liora. Count them, and then count them again on the way past."

She counted eleven chimneys smoking in the town below.

On the switchback after, she counted again.

Ten.
