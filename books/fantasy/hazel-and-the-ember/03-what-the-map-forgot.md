## 3. What the Map Forgot

The road down from the garrison took four days, and for four days the warmth in my chest said nothing.

I told myself that was a mercy. I told myself a lot of things on that road—that I'd imagined the voice in the hearth-smoke, that cold porridge and altitude and twelve years of poisons had finally cooked something loose in my skull, that a god who knew my name was no more real than the king's mercy. The body is good at lying to itself when the truth would kill it. Mine had practice.

But at night, when I built my small fire the way I'd built ten thousand small fires, the flames leaned toward me like dogs toward a whistle. And once—only once—a coal cracked in a rhythm I almost recognized, two beats and a pause, the way my mother used to knock on the door of our house so I'd know it was her and not the tax men.

I put the fire out early that night and slept cold.

* * *

Carrowgate's gates came into view on the morning of the fifth day, and something was wrong before I could name what.

The city sat where it always sat, gray and sprawling against the river, smoke rising from its thousand chimneys. But the traffic on the south road was wrong. Caravans come and go; this was not caravans. This was people walking. Families, whole villages of them by the look—the dust on their hems, the bundles on their backs, the particular exhausted shuffle of people who have left everything and are pretending they meant to. Dozens. Then scores. A river of them flowing toward the gates, and none of them talking.

Refugees don't travel quiet. Refugees argue, weep, pray, haggle over the last of their bread. These people moved like mourners at a funeral for someone whose name they couldn't recall.

I fell into step behind an old man leading a goat. Up close he smelled of woodsmoke and river mud, and his hands were a carpenter's hands—I know hands; reading them is half my trade—and there were splinters under the skin of his right thumb that had been there long enough to heal over.

"Bad harvest?" I asked.

He looked at me with eyes like a man waking from surgery. "Pardon?"

"The road. Everyone walking. What happened?"

He opened his mouth. Closed it. His brow worked like a man trying to lift something far too heavy. "We're going to the city," he said finally, with the flat certainty of a memorized line. "We live—we lived—" He stopped. Looked back down the road he'd come, at the hundreds of others, and I watched something move through his face that I have only seen once before: on the face of a fellow taster the morning after they told him what he'd eaten.

Grief without an object. Mourning with the corpse erased.

"I had a daughter," he said, suddenly, urgently, gripping my sleeve. "I'm nearly sure I had a daughter."

I did what I do best. I made myself small, murmured something kind, and slipped away into the crowd while he stood calling after me—not after me, after the shape of a thought already dissolving.

* * *

At the gates, the queue moved fast because the guards barely looked at anyone. That was wrong too. Carrowgate's gate guards inspected every cart and every face; I'd spent years cataloguing their boredom, their bribes, their blind spots. Today they waved people through like shepherds counting sheep they already owned.

Above the gatehouse, where the royal arms had hung for as long as I'd been alive, hung a new banner instead. White field. A black circle, closed, seamless. Beneath it, fresh-painted over older paint:

MINISTRY OF CONTINUITY — ALL THAT IS, ENDURES

I read it three times. The words didn't change. That felt like a small victory.

Inside the walls, the city was Carrowgate and it wasn't. The streets were the same; the smells were the same—tar, bread, horse, the river's green rot. But people kept stopping mid-stride, staring at buildings as if waiting for them to introduce themselves. A woman outside the chandlery asked her husband which street they lived on. He didn't answer. Neither of them seemed frightened. That was the worst part. They wore the same expression as the old man with the goat: not terror, but a kind of polite blankness, like guests at a party where they've forgotten the host's name and decided it would be rude to ask.

I went to ground the way I always did—kitchen doors, service alleys, the back stairs of places that fed the palace. My body knew these routes better than my head did. And it was in the palace kitchens, three days later, wearing a borrowed apron and sampling a venison pie that needed more salt, that I heard the word for the first time.

"Ashenmere," said a scullery girl to another, giggling. "You know. Ashenmere. Where the—"

"There's no Ashenmere," the other said, not unkindly. "You're thinking of Merrowfield."

"No. No, I—Ashenmere. It had a bell tower. It had—"

"It had nothing, silly. There's no such place. Never was."

And the first girl laughed, easily, agreeably, and said, "No. Never was," and went back to her scrubbing, and I stood very still with a spoon in my hand while the floor tilted under me.

Because I knew Ashenmere.

Not well. Not the way you know a home. But every taster knows the provisioning ledgers, and Ashenmere had been on them—cheese, wool, river eels, a hard dry cider the mountain garrisons loved. Twelve years I'd read that name in neat clerical script. Twelve years it had existed because ink said so.

I found a ledger. The supply office in the kitchens kept five years of them stacked under a broken table. I went through them with hands that did not shake, because my hands never shake, and Ashenmere appeared in year one, and year two, and year three, and then—in the fourth year's spring ledger—there was simply a gap. Not crossed out. Crossed-out things leave ghosts, a shadow of ink, a scar. This was a gap, smooth as unwritten paper, as if the clerk had skipped from Merrowfield directly to Dunlow and no one had noticed because there was nothing to notice.

Except me. Except whatever had spoken to me out of a garrison hearth and called itself by a name I refused to say aloud.

* * *

*They rang bells,* the fire said that night, in the brazier of a guardroom I was cleaning in exchange for a corner to sleep in. *Ashenmere. On festival days the children rang the bell tower until the priest threatened to melt it down for candles. You don't remember that. But I do.*

I kept scrubbing the grate. "You're not real," I said, under my breath, in the tone I used for correcting junior kitchen staff.

*I'm the realest thing in this country, Lior Hartwell. Everything else is being edited.*

"Things don't get edited."

*Look out the window.*

I looked. Across the yard, the Ministry of Continuity had taken over the old census hall, and carts were coming and going at midnight, which ministries do not do. The carts were covered. From under the canvas edges came a light. Not lamplight—lamplight flickers and breathes. This light was steady and white and hungry, and where it leaked onto the cobbles, the cobbles looked *less*. I can't describe it better than that. Less worn. Less real. Like a drawing of cobblestones done by someone working from memory.

*Villages,* Ember said, and the warmth in my chest guttered like a thing in pain. *That's what the Maw eats now. Not just gods. Places. Names. The crown learned a century ago that a god's power can be bottled, but it spoils—it needs feeding. And a village that everyone remembers is a little congregation, isn't it? A little church of shared memory. Devour the memory, drink the devotion, and spend the power keeping the rest of the country exactly as it is. Continuity.* The word came out of the fire tasting of ash. *They call it peace. It's digestion.*

"Why are you telling me this?" I whispered. "Why me? Why does any of this land on me?"

Silence, then, long enough that I thought the thing had gone. When it spoke again its voice had changed—smaller, older, careful, the way you speak to someone standing on a ledge.

*Because you're from there, Lior. Because Ashenmere is your village, and you're the only soul in this country who still carries it—and you don't even know how much of it you carry. Because when they came for the names, yours was hidden inside me, and I hid it deeper. And because—*

"Don't." I said it sharply enough that a sleeping guard stirred. I crouched by the brazier, close enough to feel its heat on my face, and lowered my voice to almost nothing. "Don't tell me why. Don't tell me what I am to you. I taste things for a living. I know what happens when you swallow something sweet before you know what's in it."

*That,* said Ember softly, *is the wisest thing anyone has said to me in a hundred years.*

* * *

I should have run. I want that recorded somewhere, in whatever ledger survives: I knew, three days later, standing at the south gate watching the second wave of blank-faced refugees shuffle past the black-circle banner—I knew exactly what was happening, and every survival instinct I owned screamed at me to take my poison-proofed body and my smuggler's roads and go north, go anywhere, be small, be nothing, be invisible, the way I'd been invisible for twelve years and alive because of it.

Instead I stopped a young woman from the crowd. She had flour still on her forearms, a baker's arms, and she was holding a wooden doll she clearly did not recognize.

"Where are you from?" I asked.

She frowned at me, then at the doll, turning it over. "I don't—" She laughed, embarrassed. "Isn't that strange. I was sure I'd remember by the time I reached the walls." She held up the doll. "Do you know her? I think she's mine. I think I made her for—" Her face did the thing the carpenter's face had done, the lifting of the too-heavy weight. "There was a bell tower," she said. "Wasn't there? There was a bell tower and I can't—"

"Ashenmere," I said.

The word hit her like water hits a drought field. Her eyes went wide, wet, blazing—she grabbed my wrists with both flour-white hands and for one heartbeat she *blazed*, whole and present, a person with a village inside her.

"Yes," she breathed. "Yes, that's—yes—"

And then it passed through her and out, like light through glass, and I watched it leave. Her grip loosened. Her eyes cleared into that polite blankness. She smiled at me, thanked me politely for directions I hadn't given, picked up her doll, and walked through the gate into a city that had already forgotten her, humming a tune I realized—with a vertigo so total I had to lean against the wall—was a tune I also knew, from a place I also came from, and had also never been able to name until tonight.

The black banner snapped above the gate. Below it, in the census hall across the yard, the white hungry light flared once, like a swallow.

They weren't erasing strangers. They were erasing my country one village at a time, and I was the last copy of the first one they'd ever taken.

I turned from the gate and went to find Tam, the kitchen boy who kept following me, and the soldier who couldn't remember his war, and every other scrap of the forgotten I could gather—because Ember had told me one more thing before dawn, in a voice like a dying coal:

*There's a Door, Lior. Past Thornfell. And it's the only way out of a country that is being eaten—but it won't stay open forever, and neither will I.*
