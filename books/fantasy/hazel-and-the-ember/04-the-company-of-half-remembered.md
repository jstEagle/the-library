## 4. The Company of Half-Remembered

Lior found the soldier on the third day, sitting at the edge of the refugee camp outside the capital's west gate, cleaning a sword he could not name.

He was a big man gone soft at the edges, the way men go when they've been fed by charity too long. His uniform had been picked clean of insignia—someone had torn them off, or he had—but the tailoring was officer's work. Good wool. Reinforced shoulders. A sword meant to be swung from horseback.

"You're sitting wrong," Lior said.

He looked up. "Beg pardon?"

"A cavalryman rests his blade across his knees, edge out. You're holding it like a kitchen knife." She didn't know why she knew that. She knew a great many things she couldn't account for lately; Ember had lit something in her memory that burned sideways, illuminating rooms she'd never entered.

The man stared at the sword as if seeing it for the first time. "I was in the war," he said. It sounded like a sentence he'd been given by someone else, a line learned for a play. "The border war. I know that much. Ask me which border." His laugh came out broken. "Ask me who we fought. Ask me if I killed anyone. There's nothing there. It's like the whole thing happened to a man with my face."

"What's your name?"

"Brannic." He said it fast, before it could leave him. Then, quieter: "I think. I think that's still my name."

Lior sat down beside him on the cold ground because her legs decided it before she did. She had come looking for him—Ember had shown her a fragment, a burning standard, a hill with a dead tree—and finding him felt less like discovery than like recognition. Two pieces of a set.

"There's a place," she said, "where things that are forgotten can be carried instead. I'm going there. I don't have room for many people."

"That's not an invitation. That's a riddle."

"I'm not good at invitations."

He studied her the way soldiers study terrain. "You're the taster," he said suddenly. "The one who caught the nightshade. Everyone talked about it for a week and then forgot you entirely. Isn't that right? Isn't that how it works now?" He shook his head slowly. "I remember hearing about you. I don't remember your face. I'm looking straight at you and I can't hold onto it."

"Then don't," Lior said. "Hold onto the sword. Hold onto the war you can't recall. Hold onto Brannic. That's enough to start."

* * *

The midwife came to *them*, which frightened Lior more than any search would have.

Her name was Sera Voss—no relation, though the shared surname made Lior's skin prickle in a way she refused to examine—and she found their fire on the second night outside the walls, following a warmth only she could see. She was sixty, maybe older; her hands were the hands of a woman who had caught a hundred children, steady and scarred and impossibly gentle.

"They took my face," she said, without greeting, without preamble, as if she'd been rehearsing this speech against a wall for weeks. "My children. My own children. Three of them, all grown, all living in the capital, all alive—I checked, I walked past their doors, I saw their washing on their lines. And they look through me. Their wives look through me. The little one, my granddaughter, she called me *the rag woman* and offered me a coin." Her voice cracked on the coin. "She offered me a coin, like a beggar. Like I was nothing that had ever held her whole weight in two hands before her mother even saw her."

Lior poured her tea she hadn't offered. Sera drank it without noticing the kindness, which told Lior everything about the shape of her hunger.

"The Ministry did this?" Lior asked.

Sera's eyes went strange. "No. That's the terrible part. Nobody did it. Nobody *took* anything. It's more like—" She pressed her fingers together, searching. "It's like a debt being collected. Like someone spent us. Spent our names, our faces, our years, somewhere far away, and now the bill has come due and we're what's left after the payment clears." She looked into the fire. "There's a warmth in this flame. Do you feel it? It knows me. It said *Sera who stitched the breach*, and I haven't told anyone that word since the winter the twins came early, twenty-nine years ago. Twenty-nine years, and it said it like it had been waiting."

Ember had been waiting. Ember waited inside everyone the country had begun to spend, hoarding their fragments the way a miser hoards coins bitten and bent, because bent coins were still gold.

"Come with us," Lior said, and hated how much it cost her to say it, and hated more that some traitor part of her was glad.

* * *

Tam's chapter of it, as he would later tell it—and telling it was the only way he had left to keep it—began with bread.

He had stolen a heel of black bread from the palace kitchens the night Lior fled, wrapped it in cloth, and followed her out the postern gate before his fear caught up with his feet. He remembered doing that. He was almost sure he remembered doing that. But when he tried to remember *why*—what she was to him, what she'd been, whether they had ever spoken a hundred words or a thousand—the memory came apart in his hands like wet paper.

He knew her face. That was the horror of it: everyone else's faces slid off him now, his mother's, his brother's, the cook who'd taught him to fold dumplings, but Lior's face stayed. Stuck. The one nail left in a wall where every other picture had been taken down.

So he followed the nail.

He caught up with them on the Thornfell road, four days out, walking with the stubborn plod of someone who has decided that stopping would mean thinking. Lior saw him coming and felt something twist in her chest that she refused to name, because naming it would make it true, and true things could be taken away. Everything true got taken away now. That was the whole architecture of the age.

"You shouldn't have come," she said.

"I know." Tam set down his pack. He was seventeen and all elbows and earnestness, and his eyes were red-rimmed from days of crying he probably couldn't explain anymore. "I couldn't tell you why. I tried on the road. I wrote down reasons and then read them back and they were about someone else, some other boy following some other girl for reasons that made sense. But my feet kept knowing where you were. Isn't that mad? My feet remember. My head doesn't."

"Yes," Lior said. "That's exactly how it works now."

"Is it? How do you know?"

Because Ember had told her. Because Ember had shown her the shape of the mechanism: the Ministry of Continuity did not erase people directly. It *withdrew* them—their names, their bonds, their histories—drawing the substance of memory out of the population like water from a well, and pouring it into the Maw beneath the palace, where it fed something old and hungry that kept the kingdom's maps current and its history smooth and its king eternal. What remained after withdrawal was a person-shaped absence. Feet that remembered. Hands that remembered stitches. A sword-arm that remembered a war it could no longer name.

But fragments survived. Fragments always survived, in the body, in the habits, in the places the withdrawal-needles couldn't reach. And Ember could read fragments the way Lior once read poison—a trace on the tongue, a bitterness, a direction.

* * *

They made camp that night in the ruins of a waystation on the old smuggler road, a place Lior had used a dozen times in another life, and Ember flared so brightly in the dead hearth that Sera gasped and Brannic swore and Tam simply stared, openmouthed, the way boys stare at miracles.

"Tell me what you carry," Ember said—not in words, quite, but in a warmth that arranged itself inside their heads until it *became* words, each of them hearing it in a different voice. To Brannic it spoke like a drill sergeant. To Sera like a lullaby. To Tam like a child's imaginary friend, which, Lior suspected, was close to the truth of what gods were.

Brannic gave it the hill with the dead tree. He couldn't say where the hill was, only that his boots ached when he thought of it, ached along the exact line of a climb he'd made under arrow-fire, and the ache pointed north-northwest, into Thornfell.

Sera gave it the stitch. *The breach*, she called it, the word she'd used once, in a snowbound farmhouse, when a laboring mother was tearing and death stood in the room wearing its quietest coat. The memory lived in her hands. When she spread her fingers a certain way—an old way, a forgotten way—the warmth in the fire leaned toward her like a plant toward sun, and the leaning had a direction, and the direction was northwest.

Tam gave it the least and somehow the most: a smell. Woodsmoke and hazel and cold river water, the smell of a morning he could not otherwise describe, a morning that had made him follow a girl out of a palace. Ember took the smell and turned it over like a jeweler turning a stone, and pronounced it *Ashenmere*, and Ashenmere, wherever it had gone, whoever had eaten it, lay beyond Thornfell. Toward the Last Door.

"And me?" Lior asked the fire, when the others had finished. "What do I carry?"

Ember answered carefully, the way you answer someone standing on a ledge. "You carry the roads," it said. "Every smuggler's cut, every goat path, every ford that lies about its depth. You carry twelve years of poison, mapped vein by vein. You carry a name no record holds. You carry enough, Lior Hartwell. More than enough. But not yet everything. Something is still missing from you, and I will not say what, because you would not survive hearing it tonight."

Lior didn't ask twice. She had survived twelve years by never asking questions whose answers could kill her. Some disciplines kept you alive long past their usefulness.

* * *

Around the fire, late, when the others slept, Sera sat awake and watched Lior watch the dark.

"You keep yourself apart," Sera observed. "Even now. Even here. You sit facing outward while the rest of us face in."

"It's habit," Lior said. "Tasters sit facing doors."

"Mm. Or maybe you're guarding us already and haven't decided to admit it."

Lior bristled. "I'm not guarding anyone. I'm going to Thornfell because Ember needs the Door and the Door needs Thornfell and I'm the only one who knows the roads. You're all cargo I happen to be carrying."

Sera smiled, and it was the smile of a woman who had talked a thousand laboring mothers through their worst hours, the smile that says *I've heard worse denials from people in real pain*. "Cargo doesn't get a name spoken to it by a god," she said. "Cargo doesn't get followed across a kingdom by a boy who's forgotten why. Cargo doesn't sit facing the doors all night so the rest of us can sleep. Call yourself what you like, girl. The company has already decided what you are."

Lior said nothing, because the truth was that being decided-for terrified her more than the Ministry did. Names were anchors. Anchors got you found. Everything she had ever survived, she had survived by staying unanchored, unnamed, unfound.

And yet.

And yet when Tam woke in the grey hour before dawn and said, groggily, "Did I dream it, or did the fire talk?"—and when Brannic answered, "It talked, lad, it said we'd reach the pass in six days if the mountains hold still"—and when Sera laughed, actually laughed, for the first time since her granddaughter had offered her a coin—

Lior felt something in her chest that was not vertigo and not grief and not terror. Something warm. Something with a hearth-shape.

She hated it. She catalogued it like a poison anyway: onset gradual, warmth spreading from the sternum, no antidote known, prognosis uncertain.

On the sixth day, if the mountains held still, they would cross into Thornfell. On the seventh, if the roads still existed, they would walk routes only she remembered.

On the fourth night, a rider's torch appeared on the ridge behind them, moving with the unhurried confidence of someone who already knew where they were going.

Lior counted the torches. Seven.

She started counting exits.
