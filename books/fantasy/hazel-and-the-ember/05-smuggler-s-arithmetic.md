## 5. Smuggler's Arithmetic

The first pass was gone by morning.

Lior stood where the Widow's Stair should have been—a switchback trail cut into the mountain's flank, wide enough for two mules, narrow enough that smugglers crossed it in single file with their shoulders to the rock. She had carried contraband salt up it at fourteen, at sixteen, at nineteen. She had kissed a boy on its third landing and pushed him off the edge in the same year, when he tried to sell her name to a customs man. The Stair had been as fixed in her memory as the scar on her palm.

Where it should have been there was cliff. Unbroken, unweathered, unclimbed cliff, running smooth as poured wax from the scree below to the crags above. No seam. No scar of old road. As if the mountain had never been climbed at all—as if no one had ever needed to climb it, because no one had ever lived on the other side.

"Maybe we took a wrong turn," Tam said behind her.

"We didn't."

"How do you know?"

Because I remember, she almost said, and stopped, because remembering was exactly the problem. In the capital, memory had been taken from people. Here, it seemed, memory was being taken from the land itself. The Ministry's hunger did not stop at village gates. It ate roads. It ate passes. It ate the proof that anyone had ever walked here before.

Ember pulsed against her ribs, inside the iron lantern she'd bought from a peddler who no longer remembered what lanterns were for. "Ask me," the god said, his voice like coals settling. "Ask me what I see."

"What do you see?"

"A road." A flicker of light through the lantern's seams, gold as yolk. "A road worn into the stone by ten thousand feet. I can see it because they can't unmake what I hold. Their erasure slides off a lit thing like rain off a hot pan."

Lior looked at the cliff. Looked at the lantern. Did the arithmetic she'd been doing since Ashenmere: every mile Ember burned brighter, the company walked faster; every mile the company walked faster, the torches behind them gained.

"All right," she said. "Show me the road."

* * *

The company was seven now, not counting her or the god: Sera the midwife, whose children had forgotten her face; Dain the soldier, who could not recall his own war; Maren and her brother Osk, refugees from a village whose name slipped out of the mind like water out of a fist; Hesk, a toll-keeper whose bridge no longer existed and so, apparently, had never existed, nor had he; a girl called Wren who would not say what had been taken from her; and Tam, who had followed Lior out of the capital and could no longer quite say why.

They were, Lior thought, watching them pick their way along a road only Ember could see, the least impressive army ever assembled. Half of them flinched at their own shadows. Two of them cried in their sleep, calling names that came out wrong—calling *someone*, calling *the one who*, calling nothing at all, just the shape of a name with the sound scooped out.

But they remembered each other. That was the strange arithmetic of it. Sera couldn't recall her children's faces, but she remembered Dain's limp, and dosed it with willow bark each evening without being asked. Wren couldn't say what she'd lost, but she'd started walking beside Hesk on the bad days, silent, which was apparently a language they both spoke.

And every time one of them gave a true memory away—to Ember, to each other—the lantern burned hotter, and the road ahead held firmer under their boots.

It was Osk who figured it out first, on the second night in Thornfell, huddled at a fire that was mostly lantern-light. He'd been telling the story of his village's harvest dance, badly, the way you tell something you're afraid to finish, and the flame had leapt so high it threw shadows up the pines. When he stopped talking, the fire sank. When he forced himself through the last part—the part where his sister had danced with a boy whose name he'd already lost—the fire roared.

"It eats the sad ones best," Osk said, staring at it. "The true sad ones."

"Don't call it eating," Ember said, from the lantern. His voice was gentler than Lior had heard it. "Call it feeding. There's a difference. What you give me, I keep. Nothing that comes into a hearth is lost. It becomes warmth. It becomes the thing that lets the next person through the cold."

"And if we stop giving?" Lior asked.

"Then I gutter. And then the road goes." A pause, coal-crackle soft. "And then the country finishes forgetting you."

Nobody spoke after that. But around the fire, one by one, they began to talk—not to the god, Lior noticed, but to each other. Sera told about the first baby she'd caught, and how the mother had named her Sera without being asked, and how she'd carried that borrowed name like a coin for forty years. Dain described a battle he still couldn't place—the mud, the banners, the order to hold—and for once nobody corrected him that he didn't know which war it was. It was true anyway. True things didn't need their labels.

The lantern burned like a small sun. And in the morning, the road was still there.

* * *

On the third day, the mountains began fighting back in earnest.

Lior led them down a route she knew better than her own hands: the Hollow Way, a smuggler's cut through the spine of Thornfell, three days' travel, invisible from any crown road. By noon of the first day the walls of the cut had begun to lean. Not fall—lean, slowly, like bookends pressing a row of books shut. By dusk the gap had narrowed by half. By midnight, when Wren shook her awake, the walls had met.

They stood in the lantern-light before a wall of living granite, seamless, and Lior felt something crack open in her chest that had nothing to do with fear.

She knew this stone. She knew the lichen on this stone—the pale green kind that only grew on the north face, that old Tamsin Hollis had scraped and sold as fever-medicine until the customs men burned his stores. She knew the chisel marks near the base where the original cutters had signed their work, three initials and a date, 402 After Ember—

"After Ember," she said aloud, and laughed, and it came out wrong. "The calendar. The whole calendar is named after your kind. They're erasing you and the years are still counting you."

"They'll fix that too," Ember said quietly. "Give them time."

Grief, Lior thought, was a poison like any other. It had an onset, a spread, a peak. Twelve years of tasting had taught her to hold anything on her tongue without swallowing. So she stood before the wall that had eaten the Hollow Way and the initials and old Tamsin Hollis and the girl who had run salt over this path with her hood up against the sleet, and she held it, and she did not swallow, and she did not spit it out either. She just let it sit there, burning, cataloguing itself.

Then she turned to the others.

"I need memories," she said. "Not for the god. For me. Everything anyone remembers about these mountains. Every pass, every ford, every shepherd's hut, every stupid story about every stupid rock. If it happened here, I want it."

"Why?" Maren asked.

"Because they can't erase what more than one person holds." She looked at Ember's lantern, then away. "That's the flaw in their arithmetic. One keeper is a candle. Seven is a hearth."

So they sat in the shadow of the wall that had swallowed the Hollow Way, and they remembered Thornfell at each other. Hesk knew the fords—he'd collected tolls on the river for thirty years and had memorized every depth by season. Dain had marched through the western passes in a war he couldn't name and had kept, for reasons he couldn't explain, a map drawn from memory on the inside of his coat. Wren, it turned out, had been born in Thornfell, in a valley whose name made her go white when she finally said it—and when she said it, the lantern flared so bright that Osk swore and covered his eyes, and somewhere above them, faintly, a slope of scree shifted and settled into a shape that had steps in it.

Step by step, story by story, they rebuilt the mountains out of each other.

It cost them. Lior watched it cost them—watched Sera give away the memory of her mother's kitchen, watch her face while she did it, hungry to keep it and choosing to spend it, the way you'd spend your last coin on someone else's fire. That was the part the Ministry had never understood, Lior thought. They treated memory as property, hoarded and taxed and seized. But memory spent freely didn't diminish. It compounded. Every story given to the fire made the next story easier to tell.

By the fourth dawn they had a route again: south along the ridge Hesk remembered, down through the valley Wren had named, toward the deep Thornfell country where even the maps had always been guesses.

Behind them, on the ridgeline, the torches were seven again. Closer.

* * *

They made the valley by late afternoon, and Lior called a halt in the lee of a ruined waystation—one of the old smuggler caches, dry-walled stone, roof fallen in, but the walls standing, which was more than most things were managing these days.

While the others saw to the fire, she climbed to the station's broken parapet and looked back the way they'd come.

Thornfell spread below her in the long gold light, and it was beautiful, and it was dying, and she had spent twelve years telling herself she had left it. That was the trick she'd played on herself in the capital: that the taster's life was exile, that she'd cut the country out of her like a splinter, that a body trained to feel nothing could feel nothing about a place. She had been so proud of that numbness. She had called it survival.

But standing here, watching the light move across valleys that were forgetting themselves—watching a lake she'd swum in as a child sit perfectly still and perfectly empty, its fishing village gone, its smoke gone, its very shoreline beginning to blur at the edges like wet ink—she understood that she had never left at all. You didn't leave a country by leaving it. You left it by forgetting it, and she had never once managed that, not in twelve years of practiced numbness. All her poison-craft, all her discipline, and the country had simply waited inside her, patient as a seed, for someone to warm the ground.

"You're crying," Ember said, from the lantern at her hip.

"Tasting," she said. "There's a difference."

"There is," he agreed. "Yours is the first honest thing anyone's said in this range all week."

Below, the company had gathered around the fire, and Tam was looking up at her—not with suspicion, not with the blank confusion that had been creeping over him since the capital, but with something older and simpler. Recognition, maybe. Or the memory of recognition. He raised a hand, awkward, and she found herself raising hers back before she could decide not to.

Then she saw the other thing.

Smoke. Far to the east, past the valley mouth, past the ridge where the torches had been: a thin column rising from the direction of the lowland road. Too steady for a campfire. Too deliberate for an accident.

And beneath it, faint but unmistakable, carried on the evening wind the way sound carries in cold air: bells. Crown bells. The kind the inquisitors rang when they wanted you to know they had come, and wanted you to know there was nowhere left to run.

Seven torches on the ridge. Bells in the valley. And between Lior's company and the Last Door, three days of mountains that were being rewritten faster than anyone could remember them.

Ember's flame leaned eastward, like a finger pointing.

"They've stopped following us," the god said softly. "Now they're herding us."
