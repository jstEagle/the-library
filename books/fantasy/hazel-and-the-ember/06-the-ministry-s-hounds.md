## 6. The Ministry's Hounds

Tam had been counting the things he still remembered, the way other men counted coins.

It was a short list. His mother's kitchen, though not her face. The smell of tallow and onions, which meant the palace scullery, which meant six years of his life. Lior Hartwell refusing his friendship eleven hundred times—no, that one he remembered perfectly, because she'd done it again that very morning, pressing a heel of bread into his hand without looking at him and saying, *Eat it before you drop it.*

He was almost sure he'd followed her out of the capital. He could no longer remember why.

That was the part that frightened him. Not the hounds behind them, not the mountains rewriting themselves overnight like a scribe with a grudge. It was the hole where his reason should have been. He knew he'd chosen this—the cold, the hunger, the company of people who introduced themselves twice a day because their names kept sliding off each other—but the choosing itself was gone, scooped out of him like pulp from a fruit.

So when the hounds caught them on the fourth night, some traitorous corner of him thought: *finally, someone will tell me who I am.*

* * *

They were camped in a hollow below a ridge that had been a road two days ago. Ember sat in their little fire ring, burning low and gold, and the god did something gods apparently did now, which was talk.

"You're doing the counting again," Ember said. "I can hear it. It sounds like a man stacking stones in an empty room."

"Leave off," Tam said.

"I'm only asking what you'll do when you run out of stones."

The midwife, Sera, slept sitting up, her arms crossed over her chest as if holding something invisible—a bundle, maybe, or the memory of every child she'd ever delivered, all of whom had forgotten her face. The soldier, Dane, slept the way soldiers do, flat and total, though he couldn't recall a single battle he'd fought in. And Lior sat apart as always, back to a stone, tasting the dark air like she tasted everything, waiting for it to poison her.

Tam couldn't sleep. He'd stopped trusting his own dreams; half of them featured a life he wasn't sure was his.

He heard the dogs before anyone else—not because his ears were good, but because he'd spent six years listening for the head cook's footsteps, and fear sounds the same in every kitchen. A belling far down the valley. Then another, closer.

"Lior." His voice cracked. "Lior, they're here."

She was awake instantly, of course. She was always awake. That was the thing about Lior Hartwell—he'd understood it even at fifteen, hauling slop buckets past her in the corridor: she never fully slept because sleeping meant being somewhere you hadn't mapped first.

The others woke. Ember flared, and the hollow filled with shadows that leaned away from the light.

"How many?" Sera whispered.

"Two packs," Tam said. "Maybe twenty men. Inquisitors' hounds—they don't bark at people. They bark at memories." He didn't know how he knew that. He just did, the way he knew the weight of a full slop bucket. Another stone for the pile, from a room he couldn't find.

"They've been following us since the ford," Lior said quietly. "I let them get close."

Dane was on his feet with his hand on a sword he barely remembered how to use. "You *let* them—"

"I wanted to see who leads them." Lior's face was unreadable in Ember's light. "Now I have."

* * *

Chancellor Veyra came up the slope alone.

That was the first wrong thing. Twenty hounds and handlers below, torches strung along the ridgeline like a fallen constellation, and the Chancellor of Continuity walked into a hollow full of fugitives with her hands visible and empty. She was perhaps fifty, gray at the temples, dressed plainly for her office—and she moved the way Lior moved. Watching exits. Tasting the air.

"Master Tamwell," she said, and Tam's whole body went cold.

Nobody had used his full name in months. He'd half believed he didn't have one anymore.

"You were second scullery, west range, under Head Cook Brannic," Veyra continued, conversational, climbing. "You burned your left forearm on a kettle when you were nine. You once stole honeycomb for a girl who refused to eat it." She smiled slightly. "You've forgotten all of that. I haven't. The Ministry keeps what the country loses. That is rather the entire point of us."

"Stay back," Dane said, sword out now, though his grip looked uncertain, like a man holding someone else's tool.

Veyra ignored him. Her eyes found Lior and stayed there, and something passed across her face that Tam recognized, because he'd seen it in his own reflection lately: hunger shaped like grief.

"Hartwell," she said. "Twelve years in the tasting chair. Nightshade, foxglove, water hemlock, all of it laid into your body grain by grain until you became the ledger yourself. Do you know what you are? You're the finest instrument the crown ever built. And we built you for *this*."

"For what?" Lior said. "Say it plainly. I taste everything anyway."

"The Maw beneath the palace has an appetite we can no longer feed with villages alone. Ashenmere bought us a decade. But the Door beyond Thornfell opens soon—it opens *once*—and if it opens for the wrong self, everything the Ministry has preserved goes through it and is lost forever. Unless—" Veyra spread her hands, reasonable, terrible, "—the right person stands before it. A vessel. Prepared since birth. Immune to everything except what we pour into her."

Tam watched Lior's face while Veyra talked. He saw nothing move in it, which frightened him more than any expression would have.

"And my price?" Lior asked.

"Immunity. Full pardon. Restoration." Veyra's voice softened into something almost kind. "Your name spoken in every record again. Your village returned to the maps. Ashenmere, Hartwell, all of it—restored, whole, remembered. All we ask is the heart-flame of the little hearth-god you've been carrying. One ember, surrendered. The country stays intact. Everyone forgets a little more, but everyone always does. That's just arithmetic, Hartwell. You of all people understand arithmetic."

The silence stretched. Ember burned very small and very steady, the way a fire does when it's listening.

"That's a lie," Tam heard himself say.

Everyone turned. He hadn't decided to speak; his mouth had simply gone ahead of him, the way it used to in the kitchens when Brannic screamed and Tam's mouth defended whoever stood nearest.

"It's a lie," he said again, stronger. "I don't remember much anymore. Most days I can't remember why I'm here or what my mother looked like or whether I ever stole honeycomb for anybody. But I remember—" he groped for it, the stone at the bottom of the pile, "—I remember that forgetting doesn't work like she says. It isn't arithmetic. When they took Ashenmere, the refugees didn't lose a little. They lost *everything*, and then they lost knowing they'd lost it. That's not subtraction. That's the whole sum going to zero."

Veyra regarded him with mild interest, the way one regards a dog that has learned a trick. "And what would you know of sums, scullery boy?"

"Nothing," Tam admitted. "That's the point. I'm the least of anyone here. And even I can taste the poison in that offer."

Something flickered across the Chancellor's face—there and gone, fast as a flinch. Recognition, maybe. She had been a taster once; Lior had said so on the ridge above the drowned pass, saying it like a warning. Tasters learned early that the deadliest dishes were the ones that smelled like home.

"No matter," Veyra said. "The offer stands until dawn. Hartwell. Think about what you were made for." She turned, paused. "All of you were made for something. The Ministry simply remembers what."

Then she went down the slope, and the torches along the ridge began to withdraw, unhurried, patient, like a tide that knows it owns the shore.

* * *

They didn't sleep after that. They sat around Ember in a tight circle, and nobody spoke for a long time.

It was Tam who broke first, because the question had been eating him since Veyra said his name.

"Why did I follow you?" he asked Lior. "Out of the capital. I keep reaching for it and there's nothing there. Did I—was there a reason? Did I owe you? Did you save me? I'd like to know what I am before they tell me."

Lior looked at him for a long moment. In Ember's light her eyes were the color of a banked fire, and for once she didn't look away.

"You followed me," she said slowly, "because three years ago I found you crying in the larder over a letter you couldn't read anymore. Your mother had written it. The words kept fading while you held it. And I sat with you all night and read it to you from memory, over and over, until you could hold it in your head instead of on the page." She hesitated. "I told you never to thank me. I told you friendship was a debt I couldn't afford. But you kept bringing me bread anyway. You kept *seeing* me. And when I ran, you ran too, because someone had finally remembered you completely, and you couldn't bear to go back to being furniture."

Tam's throat worked. "I don't remember any of that."

"I know."

"But it's true?"

"It's true."

"Then keep it," he said. "Keep it for both of us. That's what you're for, isn't it? That's what you've always been for." He laughed, wet and cracked. "Gods, Lior. We're all just ledgers, and you're the only one who writes straight."

Ember flared gently, gold washing over all their faces. "Now you understand," the god said softly. "What she offers is safety. What you have is worse and better. It's each other."

Tam looked at the little fire that spoke, at Sera holding her invisible bundles, at Dane gripping a sword for wars he'd lost the memory of, at Lior—who was staring into the flame with an expression he'd never seen on her before, because it was hope, and hope looked like pain on a face that had practiced not having any.

He thought about Veyra's offer. Immunity. Restoration. A name in every book.

And he thought about the larder, and the letter, and a voice reading his mother's words aloud in the dark until they were safe inside him.

He reached into his shirt and pulled out the crust of bread Lior had given him that morning, stale now, saved without knowing why. He tore it in half and handed half to her.

"Eat it before you drop it," he said.

Lior took it. For a moment, just a moment, her mouth did something that was almost a smile.

Then Ember went suddenly tall and thin, flame stretching toward the north like a finger pointing, and the god's voice changed—urgent, crackling, afraid.

"She's not withdrawing," Ember said. "The hounds are circling wide. She gave you the night not as mercy but as measurement—to see which of you would run to her by morning. And there's more. I can feel it through the ground now, the way I once felt it through hearths." The flame guttered, steadied. "The Maw isn't waiting for your answer, Lior. It's already moving. It's coming up through Thornfell to meet us, and it is *hungry*, and between us and the Last Door there is a pass that will be full of its teeth by tomorrow's dusk."

Lior stood, brushing crumbs from her hands, and Tam saw the taster come back into her face—the stillness, the readiness, the woman who survived by knowing exactly what was in the dish.

"Then we walk tonight," she said. "And nobody eats anything I haven't tasted first."

They broke camp in the dark, and behind them, far down the valley, the hounds began to sing.
