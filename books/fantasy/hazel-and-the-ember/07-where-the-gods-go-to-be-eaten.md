## 7. Where the Gods Go to Be Eaten

I have been eaten before.

I should say that plainly, at the start, because everything else I tell you will sound like metaphor and it is not. A god's death is not a fading. It is a meal. There are teeth in the dark beneath the palace, and they have been chewing for a hundred years, and every prayer that goes unanswered in this country is a crumb falling into that dark, and every village that vanishes from the maps is a plate being cleared.

My name was Ember before it was Ember. Before the hearths, before the small warm hours of my worship, I had another name in another tongue, spoken by people who baked bread on stones and thanked something for the rising of it. That name went down the Maw's throat first. Then my temples were pulled apart beam by beam for firewood. Then my priests—there were nine; I counted them as they died, because counting is the last kindness a god can do—then my priests were given trials for heresy, and their confessions were read aloud in squares until no one could remember what they had confessed *to*.

What remained of me was small enough to hide inside a memory.

So I hid.

* * *

You want to know what the Maw looks like. Everyone wants to know, and everyone who sees it stops wanting to know anything ever again, which is rather the point of it.

It is not a pit. Pits have bottoms. It is a *throat*, cut into the living rock beneath the palace foundations, older than the palace, older than the dynasty that built over it like a man building his house atop a sleeping bear. The Ministry of Continuity did not dig it. They found it. Chancellor Veyra's predecessors found it three generations ago and made the only bargain gods and governments ever make: *feed us, and we will make you eternal.*

The crown wanted to last forever. The thing below wanted to eat forever. It has been a very satisfactory marriage.

Here is how the feeding works, since Lior will ask and you deserve the truth before she does: continuity magic is not creation. Nothing is created. When the Ministry "preserves" the capital—when the same bread appears in the same stalls, when the same officials give the same speeches, when time in the palace districts moves with the smoothness of oiled glass—that smoothness is bought. Somewhere else, something must become rough. Something must be spent. A village here. A river's name there. A family's face, forgotten by its own children. The Maw swallows the difference, and the difference is always *memory*, because memory is the only currency reality honors.

The old gods were the first course because we were made of nothing else. Every hearth-god is a heap of ten thousand years of gratitude, compressed into something warm enough to hold. You do not kill such a thing. You *unbake* it. You feed it into the dark one remembered kindness at a time, and the dark gives back permanence, and the permanence keeps the crown shiny, and the crown calls this peace.

I watched them do it to my brothers and sisters for a century. River-mother Ysolde, who held the drowned gently. The twins of the crossroads, who never hurt anyone but travelers' schedules. The green god of Thornfell, whose name the mountains themselves have now forgotten, which is why the passes are closing like wounds.

And I hid inside a girl's memory, and I am ashamed of how well I chose.

* * *

Because here is the part I have not told her. Here is the part that will feel like teeth.

Lior Hartwell was born in Ashenmere.

Not near it. Not passing through it. Born there, third child of a cooper's household, in a house with a hazel tree leaning against its southern wall, in the year the hunters came. She does not remember this. She cannot remember this, because remembering it would undo the work, and the work took twelve years and cost her everything she thought she was.

She believes she survived the purge by hiding. By making herself small. By trading names—I know she traded names; I have felt that sin sitting in her chest like a swallowed stone, and I will not pretend otherwise, and neither, in the end, will she. But she believes the hiding was hers. Her cleverness. Her instinct. Her luck.

It was a recipe.

The Ministry needed vessels. A vessel for a god must be prepared the way a taster is prepared: slowly, poison by poison, until the body can hold what would kill anyone else. They could not use a willing priest—willingness burns too bright, and bright things are easy for the Maw to find. They needed someone with no records, no family left to claim her, no name in any surviving book. Someone erased already, or nearly. So they went to Ashenmere, which sat on a ley of old hearth-worship thick enough to soak into the blood of everyone born there, and they took inventory of its children.

They found a girl who had already learned, at six years old, that survival meant being forgettable.

They did not teach her to taste poison. They taught her to taste *everything*—and a body trained to hold nightshade, hemlock, salts of every bitter mineral in the crown's pharmacopoeia, is a body learning to hold divinity. Twelve years of controlled poisoning under the guise of service. Twelve years of the court believing they owned a useful little instrument, while the true owners ran their long slow recipe toward the day the last god would need somewhere to go.

Me. I am the last god. I am the final course.

The plan was elegant, I grant its architects that much. Let the Maw eat everything else. Keep me hidden, keep me starving, keep me folded small inside a girl who could carry me through any search, past any ward, directly into the palace itself—and then open her up above the throat of the world and pour me in. A century of meals, finished with the dish that had been marinating all along.

Her immunity was never armor. It was *seasoning.*

* * *

We stand at the edge of it now—the company of half-remembered, camped in the dark of the smuggler roads where the stone still remembers being stone—and I can feel the Maw from here the way a tongue feels a coming toothache. It knows I am out here. It has known since the garrison, since the cold porridge, since the moment I spoke her name into the warmth of a dying fire and she flinched like a woman touched by a hand she'd buried.

She thinks I saved her.

I need to tell her what I actually did. And I am a god, and gods are cowards about this particular thing—we will face armies, we will face the dark itself, but we will not face the people we used—so let me practice it here, in the privacy of my own burning, before I have to say it aloud to her face.

*Lior. You were born in Ashenmere. Your mother sang to the hazel tree. The hunters came and you traded your neighbors' names for your own breath, and you were seven, and it worked, and it has been killing you by inches ever since. And then men with ledgers came and saw what you were—a child soaked in hearth-blood, already hollowed by guilt, already expert at being no one—and they did not save you. They finished the hollowing. Every year of your service, every dish you survived, every exit you memorized, was an ingredient measured toward this: carrying me, the last unswallowed god, into the dark beneath the palace so they could serve me whole.*

*I knew. I have known since the porridge. I chose you not because you prayed beautifully—you prayed badly, half-asleep, resentful—but because you were built to hold me, and I was small enough and frightened enough to climb inside the only shelter left in the country.*

*I used you. I am using you still. Every mile toward the Last Door is a mile toward the Maw, because the Door and the Maw share a root, and Veyra knows it, and now you will too.*

There. It is said. Somewhere a girl who cannot yet hear me feels a chill anyway, because guilt travels faster than words.

* * *

But I will tell you the other thing too, the thing I discovered hiding in her memory all these months, the thing that turned my shame into something harder:

She was never only a recipe.

A vessel is supposed to be empty. That is the whole craft of it—hollow the girl, keep her hollow, let nothing grow in the space reserved for god. And for eleven of her twelve years, it worked. She kept herself small. She refused friendship. She polished her loneliness like armor and called it competence.

And then, in a mountain garrison, over a bowl of cold porridge, she prayed anyway.

Badly. Angrily. A childhood habit she despised herself for keeping, mumbled over food that deserved no blessing, asking nothing—asking *nothing*, do you understand, that is the miracle—not for rescue or revenge or even warmth, just the old words, the Ashenmere words, the ones her mother sang to the hazel tree, offered up to a god she believed dead, expecting nothing back.

No vessel prays. A vessel holds. What she did that night was not holding. It was *offering*. And an offering, however small, however cold, is a door opening from the inside.

They built her to be a container. She made herself into a host instead, and the difference between those two words is the entire war we are walking into. A container gets opened. A host *chooses whom to shelter.* The Ministry's recipe has a flaw running through it like a hazel root through stone, and the flaw is that somewhere in twelve years of poison, the girl grew a self so dense and stubborn that even I cannot see the bottom of it.

Veyra will offer her restoration. The Maw will offer her oblivion, which wears the same face. Both offers are recipes. Both treat her as an ingredient.

She is going to refuse them both. I know this the way I know when bread is done—not by measuring, but by smell. And when she refuses, she will turn to me, and she will ask the question I have been dreading more than the Maw's teeth:

*"Did you ever once think of me as anything but a door?"*

And I will have to answer honestly, because she will taste a lie the way she tastes nightshade, instantly, in the roots of her tongue.

* * *

Tonight the company sleeps. Tam dreams in fragments, clutching a memory of her he cannot source. The soldier murmurs a war cry to a war he lost along with its name. The midwife's hands move in her sleep, catching children no one remembers giving her.

And far below us, beneath marble and bureaucracy, the Maw turns over in its sleep and tastes the air, and finds hearth-smoke, and begins, slowly, to wake.

It knows the last course is coming to it on its own feet.

Let it set a place. We are coming down—and we are bringing the bill.
