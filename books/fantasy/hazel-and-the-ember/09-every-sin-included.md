## 9. Every Sin Included

The memories came like a dam breaking.

Not gently. Not in order. Ember poured everything back into her at once, and Lior Hartwell drowned in her own life.

She was six years old, and the hearth in Ashenmere sang to her. She was nine, learning which mushrooms killed and which only blinded. She was eleven, hiding under the floorboards while the first hunters came through the village with their iron censers and their lists.

*The lists.*

Lior screamed, or thought she did. Her body knelt on the stone before the Door, hands pressed flat against it, but inside she was eleven years old again, crouched beneath her mother's bed with dust in her teeth, listening to boots on the floor above.

The hunters had caught old Marta the midwife's son first. They'd strung him up in the square as an example, and they'd said, very reasonably, that any child who came forward with names would be fed and housed and spared. Any child who didn't would be taken to the capital for *questioning*, and everyone knew what questioning meant now.

Lior had come forward.

She remembered the exact weight of the bread they gave her—dense, dark, still warm from a communal oven she'd betrayed. She remembered eating it slowly, deliberately, while she recited names. Her neighbors. The woman who taught her letters. The family who'd shared their winter stores when hers ran out. Names given up one by one, in a clear child's voice, because she wanted to live, because she was eleven and hungry and afraid, and wanting to live had never once felt like a sin until this moment, standing here, feeling all of it at once.

She had traded forty-one names for a loaf of bread and a place in the hunters' cart.

And then—the part she'd buried deepest, the part even Ember had kept from her until now—they hadn't just taken the named. They'd taken *everyone*. Because a village that harbored old gods' faithful couldn't be left half-cleansed. Ashenmere burned three nights later, and Lior rode out of it in a crown wagon, chewing the last of her bread, watching the glow on the horizon and telling herself it wasn't her fault, it wasn't, the hunters would have found out anyway—

But they wouldn't have. Not so fast. Not so completely. Forty-one names had been the key that unlocked the whole village.

"That's not true," Lior whispered. Her voice cracked against the Door. "Ember. Tell me that's not true."

The god's warmth flickered inside her chest, guttering low, almost out. He had spent nearly everything pouring her back into herself. What remained of him felt less like fire now and more like a coal cupped in bare palms, protected from the wind by sheer stubbornness.

*I can't tell you that,* Ember said, gentle as banked embers. *I can only tell you what I've always told you: you were a child. And children are not recipes. They are what survives the cooking.*

"I killed them." The words came out flat, factual, the way she reported poison to the king. "I killed my whole village."

*You survived your whole village,* Ember corrected. *There is a difference, and the difference is everything. But I will not lie to you, Lior Hartwell. You want me to take this memory from you, the way the Ministry takes villages. I could do it. I could burn it out of you and leave you clean enough to walk through that Door believing yourself innocent.*

Her breath stopped.

*That is what Veyra offered you,* he continued. *Continuity without cost. A self with the ugly parts edited out. And you already know what that makes. It makes Ashenmere. It makes a blank place on the map where people used to be.*

Behind her, the pass was quiet. Too quiet. Sera and Dain had bought them hours with their lives, hours that were bleeding away with every heartbeat Lior spent kneeling here. Somewhere down the mountain, Veyra's hounds were climbing, patient as arithmetic.

Tam stood a few paces off, holding the hazel sprig from Ashenmere that the midwife had carried across half a country. His face was swollen from grief he could barely remember the shape of. He didn't speak. He just watched her with those eyes that had followed her out of the palace without knowing why, and Lior understood suddenly that this was why. Some part of him had always known she was running from something. Now she finally knew what.

"Will it hurt?" she asked Ember. "Saying it?"

*Yes.*

"Good." She pushed herself upright. Her knees ached. Her whole body ached, every scar from twelve years of controlled poisoning singing at once, the ledger of her survival balanced at last. "Then it'll be real. And if it's real, maybe—" Her voice broke. She steadied it the way she'd been taught to steady her hands over the king's plate: completely, professionally, at terrible cost. "Maybe it counts."

She turned to face the Door.

It was older than the country. Older than the gods, maybe. A seam in the world shaped like a doorway, filled with nothing—not darkness, which is something, but absence, the same colorless erasure the Ministry fed its villages into. Except the Door's absence was honest about itself. It didn't pretend to be continuity. It simply waited, the way death waits, the way truth waits, for someone willing to walk through it whole.

Lior opened her mouth.

And found she couldn't.

Twelve years of training rose up in her like a wall. *Stay small. Stay invisible. Never give them anything true.* Every instinct that had kept her alive through a hundred poisoned meals and a decade of court intrigue screamed at her to stop, to find another way, to be clever, to be careful, to be *nobody* one more time.

"No," she said aloud, to herself, to the wall of habit. "I'm done being nobody. Nobody is how they got me. Nobody is how they got all of us."

She planted her feet like a woman bracing against a storm, and she spoke.

"My name is Lior Hartwell. I was born in Ashenmere, in the house with the blue door, third from the well. My mother mended nets. My father was dead before I could name him. When I was eleven years old, the king's hunters came for the old faith, and I gave them forty-one names to save my own skin. I ate their bread while I did it. I tasted every bite."

The words fell into the silence like stones into a well.

"The woman who taught me letters was called Mistress Ovelin. She smelled of chalk and lavender, and she died in the burning because of me. The family who fed us that winter was the Corren family; there were five of them; the youngest was a baby called Pip who laughed when you blew on his stomach. I killed them. I killed old Marta's son before the hunters ever touched him, because my testimony confirmed their suspicions. I have spent twelve years being a food-taster because I deserved to swallow poison every day of my life, and some part of me has always known it."

Tears cut hot lines down her cold-flushed face. She didn't wipe them away.

"And I am telling you this," she went on, louder now, shouting at the Door, at the country behind her, at the eleven-year-old girl still hiding under the floorboards in her heart, "because it's TRUE. Because they took everything else, but they don't get this. They don't get my guilt. They don't get my shame. It's MINE, and I'm carrying it out of here, and someday someone is going to hear the whole story of Ashenmere—including the worst part, including ME—and that's how it comes back. Not clean. Not edited. TRUE."

The Door did not open.

For one horrible moment, nothing happened at all. The wind moved. Somewhere below, faint as a dream, Lior heard dogs.

Then Ember stirred inside her—not guttering now, but flaring, blazing up along her ribs and throat like dawn coming early, and his voice was no longer a whisper but a roar of joy:

*YES. Oh, you foolish, brave, impossible girl—THAT'S IT. That's what it eats! Not purity—TRUTH! It was never going to open for someone spotless. Spotless people are the Ministry's favorite meal!*

Light poured from the seam. The absence at the heart of the Door kindled into something else—not fire exactly, but recognition, the warmth of being seen completely and not turned away from. Lior felt it wash over her like her mother's hands, like the communal oven's heat, like every hearth in Ashenmere burning all at once in welcome rather than destruction.

The Door began to open.

And Ember began to go out.

She felt it happening and grabbed for him the way you grab for a blanket sliding off a bed in winter. *No—wait—you're coming too, you have to—*

*Silly taster.* His voice was smiling, and fading, and smiling. *I'm a hearth-god. Hearth-gods don't travel. We stay where we're needed until we're ash, and then we stay in the ash. You are carrying all of them now—Ovelin, the Correns, little Pip, Marta, every name you gave and every name you didn't. That's the hearth now. You're the hearth. Keep it warm.*

"Don't," she whispered. "Please. Everyone leaves. Everyone gets erased. Don't you leave too."

*Child,* Ember said, and his last warmth wrapped around her heart like a hand, *I have been waiting inside your memory since before you could talk. Do you truly believe a little thing like running out of fire could make me forget you?*

He went out.

Not dramatically. Hearths don't do drama. One moment there was warmth in her chest; the next, a hearth-shaped space where warmth used to be, and the memory of warmth, which is its own kind of ember if you tend it right.

Lior stood alone before an open Door, crying without sound, holding a country inside her ribcage.

Down the pass, the dogs were closer. She could hear Veyra's voice now, thin with distance, calling her name in that reasonable tone that had talked a hundred villages into oblivion. Behind her, Tam made a small wounded noise and started forward—

"No." She turned. Took his face in her hands. His cheeks were wet; she hadn't realized he was crying too. "Listen to me. Tam of the palace kitchens. You burned my porridge on purpose the first week, to see if I'd notice. You notice things. That's your whole gift, and it's worth more than mine ever was. Remember that. Remember *me*. That's the job now—all of us who remember anything. We're the ark."

"I can't—" His voice shattered. "I don't even know why I followed you."

"You will." She pressed her forehead to his. "That's the hook I'm leaving in you. Come find me when it pulls."

She kissed his brow, the way you bless a child, the way you seal a letter, and stepped back before either of them could break.

Then Lior Hartwell—taster, traitor, vessel, ark—turned to the Door, held every name she carried against her heart like a shield made of guilt and love, and walked through.

The absence closed around her like water.

And somewhere behind her, in a country being unmade, a girl with a hazel sprig in her fist stood at the top of a pass, holding a memory that was starting to pull.
