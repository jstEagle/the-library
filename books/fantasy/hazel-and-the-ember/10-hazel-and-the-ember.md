## 10. Hazel and the Ember

The ground beyond the Door was not soft. That was the first thing Lior learned about exile: it did not cushion you. It simply held you up, indifferent as any floor, and let you decide what to do with yourself.

She had stepped through alone, and for three days she had done nothing but walk.

The borderland had no name she recognized, which was fitting, because names were what she carried now—all of them, a whole country's worth, stacked inside her skull like cordwood. The land here was a long gray valley stitched together from the edges of other places: terraced fields that climbed toward mountains no one had planted them for, a river that ran with the accent of somewhere else, roads that forked in ways that suggested they'd been argued into existence rather than built. Exiled nations, Ember had called them once, in one of his last warm murmurings. Countries too small or too stubborn or too true to be allowed to continue. They came here when the world ran out of room for them.

They did not come here often. But they came.

On the third day, Lior stopped walking at the base of a hill where the gray grass gave way to darker soil, and she knelt, and she opened the bundle she had carried through the Door pressed against her ribs like a second heart.

A hazel sprig. Cut from the tree behind Maren's cottage in Ashenmere, the one whose nuts the children used to fight over, the one Lior herself had climbed the autumn before the hunters came. She had taken the cutting without knowing why, back when her memories were still arriving in her like floodwater, and now she understood: some things you carry because you cannot yet explain them, and the explaining comes later, or it doesn't, and either way you keep carrying.

She dug with her hands. The soil was cold and honest about it. She set the sprig in, packed the earth around its thin stem, and sat back on her heels.

*Grow,* she thought. Then, because she was who she was—a woman who had spent twelve years learning that hope was just poison you hadn't swallowed yet—she thought: *or don't. I'll still be here tomorrow.*

But she watered it anyway, from her flask, every day.

* * *

The people of the borderland found her the way people find anything: slowly, then all at once.

First there was the old man from a drowned duchy whose name had been reclaimed by the sea—he told her this himself, cheerfully, the way exiles learn to tell hard things—and he asked what she was growing. "Hazel," she said. He nodded as if that answered everything and walked on.

Then a woman with her nation folded into a single embroidered shawl, every village of it stitched in thread, who stopped longer and asked where the hazel was from. And Lior opened her mouth to say *a tree, a hill, a place you've never heard of* and instead heard herself say:

"Ashenmere. It was a village in a country called Thornmere, in the shadow of the Thornfell mountains, and it had forty-one hearths and a bell that rang flat on cold mornings, and it is gone—not destroyed, *erased*, and I am the only person alive who remembers it."

The woman listened. All of it. The taster's tongue, the purge, the Ministry of Continuity, the Maw beneath the palace, the Door. When Lior finished, hoarse and shaking, the woman touched the hazel's first leaf and said, "That's how it starts. A telling is a seed. My grandmother said so, before our maps were taken." She tapped her own temple. "You plant them in people. Ground is just ground. People are soil that walks."

Lior told the story again the next day, to a pair of brothers who herded goats along the ridge. Again the week after, to a gathering that had grown to eleven listeners, then nineteen. Each time it came easier and each time it cost more—the memories were real, they had weight, and speaking them meant lifting them—but each time, she noticed something strange.

The story grew.

Not changed—*grew*. Details she hadn't consciously known surfaced as she spoke: the smell of Ashenmere's tanneries in August, the way the midwife Sela hummed when she worked, the exact pitch of the flat bell. As if remembering out loud gave the memory permission to finish itself. As if the country, spoken, was reassembling.

And once—only once, on a night when twenty-three exiles sat around her fire listening—she felt a warmth in the flames that was not heat. It turned toward her the way a face turns. It did not speak. It didn't need to. She knew that warmth the way she knew her own pulse.

"Ember?" she whispered, after the others slept.

The fire crackled. Somewhere very far off—or very close; distance worked strangely here—she felt an answer like a banked coal accepting ash: *not gone. kept.*

She wept, briefly, quietly, the way she did everything, and then she went to sleep, because grief was a chore now, scheduled between chores, and chores kept a person alive.

* * *

He came in the spring.

She was at the market square of the nearest settlement—a sprawl of tents and timber houses that the exiles called, in six different languages, simply *Here*—trading a bolt of dyed cloth for salt, when she heard a voice behind her that made her drop the salt.

"You always did haggle like someone insulting the merchant personally."

She turned. Tam stood in the crowd, thinner, sunburnt, his left arm bound in a sling that was clearly decorative by now, and he was looking at her with an expression she had spent twelve years making sure no one at court ever wore while looking at her: recognition, whole and unguarded.

"How," she said. Her voice failed on the second word and she started over. "How did you find me? The Door closed. The pass fell. I watched the mountain take it."

"You left me a memory." He dug in his shirt and produced a small tin, battered, and inside it a hazelnut—one nut, cracked slightly at the seam. "After Veyra took the pass. You were crying and pretending you weren't, and you put this in my hand and you said—" he closed his eyes, reciting, careful, the way he'd clearly recited it a thousand times on the road— "*'Ashenmere's orchard hill, third row from the wall, the tree with the lightning scar. If you forget everything else, keep this.'*"

"I don't remember saying that."

"You wouldn't. It wasn't yours anymore—it was mine. That's how Ember did it, I think. He split the remembering. Gave me one seed of it so I'd have a compass." He shrugged with his good shoulder. "I followed the nut. Don't ask me how. It got warmer when I went right and colder when I went wrong, and I have never been so grateful to be a kitchen boy with nothing better to do."

"The others," she said. "Sela. Captain Dree. The soldier who couldn't remember his war."

"Sela stayed at the last village before the pass. There were births coming—there are always births coming, she says, it's the one thing erasure can't stop. Dree—" Tam's face did something complicated. "Dree found his war. Part of it. Enough. He's raising a company of the half-forgotten out of Thornfell's bones. Says somebody has to hold the door *from* the other side, in case anyone else comes looking for it." He met her eyes. "In case anyone else needs out."

Lior looked down at the salt scattered on the cobbles and did not pick it up. Twelve years of staying small, of being a ledger and not a life, of refusing friendship because friendship was a handle someone could grab—and here was the kitchen boy, standing in the middle of a borderland of the erased, holding a hazelnut like a lantern.

"You could have sold me to Veyra," she said. "She'd have paid. Immunity. Restoration."

"She offered me that too." Tam smiled, and it was the same crooked smile from the palace kitchens, from before everything. "I told her I already knew who I was. Took me a while to mean it. But I meant it."

* * *

They planted the second sprig together—Tam insisted, and Lior discovered she did not mind being insisted upon—and then a third, from cuttings of the first, and by the following winter there was a row of seven hazels on the hill above Here, and children who had been born in exile climbed them as if climbing trees were the oldest thing in the world.

Lior told the story every week now. It had become a kind of liturgy, though she refused to call it that; liturgies, in her experience, were things institutions fed on. She called it *the telling*, and she told it plainly, without ornament, because she had learned that plainness survived translation and ornament did not. Every sin included—that was the rule she had bought the Door with, and she kept it. She told them about trading the names. She watched the parents flinch and the children listen hardest, because children always know which part is the load-bearing truth.

And each telling, the row of hazels grew a little faster than trees should grow. And each telling, somewhere in the borderland, another hearth warmed without fire—she collected the reports the way other exiles collected rumors: a widow two valleys over whose cold stove had begun cooking on its own; a shepherd who swore his fire leaned toward him when he spoke of home. Ember, distributed. Ember, seeded through the telling, living now in a hundred small flames instead of one dying god's chest.

*It's better, this way,* she told him one night, alone at the fire. *Being many small fires instead of one big one. Harder to eat a thing that's everywhere.*

The flames leaned in. *harder to forget, too,* the warmth said, smug as a cat, and she laughed for the first time since the Door.

* * *

On the first anniversary of her crossing, Lior climbed the hill at dawn with Tam, as had become their custom, to check the trees. The frost was thick, the sky the color of unpolished pewter, and the seventh hazel—the newest, the one the borderland children called the Story Tree—was bearing its first nut.

Tam picked it, weighed it in his palm, and frowned.

"Lior," he said slowly. "This one's warm."

She took it. He was right. Warm as a held coin, warm as a hearthstone hours after the fire dies, and through the shell, faint but unmistakable, she felt a pulse that was not Ember's—something newer, smaller, stranger. A warmth with a different signature. A warmth that felt, absurdly, *expectant*.

And far to the east, past the edge of the borderland, past the gray hills where the exiled nations huddled, there was a light on the horizon that had not been there yesterday. Not dawn. Dawn came from the other direction.

Tam saw it too. "Is that—"

"That's Thornfell," Lior said. Her taster's tongue, trained to detect poison in anything, detected it now in the air itself: a taste like hot metal and burnt prayer. "Something's happening at the Door."

The nut pulsed in her palm, patient as a seed, insistent as a name.

Somewhere behind that light, a country waited to be spoken back into being. And it seemed the telling had finally been heard.
