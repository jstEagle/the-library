## 1. The Ninth Dish

The arsenic came in on the venison, as arsenic usually does—riding the fat, sweetening itself into the glaze of blackcurrant and juniper where no one thinks to look. I knew it before the fork touched my tongue. I knew it by the smell, faintly bitter under the fruit, like a coin left too long in a well. And I knew it because I had asked for this dish to be prepared twice: once for the king's table and once, an hour earlier, in a plain copper pan in the kitchens below, where I could meet it alone.

I put the fork in my mouth anyway.

Some tasters spit. The clever ones do, and they live long dull lives in comfortable houses, and nobody writes songs about them. I swallow. That is the whole of my craft, if you want to be unkind about it: I swallow things that would kill other men, and then I stand very still and count.

One. Two. Three.

The warmth arrived on schedule, blooming low in my belly like a swallowed coal. Not pain, exactly. Arsenic's warmth is almost companionable after twelve years—it has the manners of an old acquaintance who arrives uninvited but knows when to leave. I let it sit. I breathed through it while the hall roared on around me, two hundred courtiers laughing at some joke made by a man with a voice like a landslide, and King Aldric of Valdrenn watched his ninth course approach with the appetite of a man who has never once wondered whether his dinner loves him back.

Seven. Eight. Nine.

The warmth crested, peaked, and began its slow retreat, the way a tide goes out and leaves the sand darker. My hands did not shake. My face did nothing at all, which is its finest skill. I set down the fork, looked to the steward, and gave him the smallest nod—the nod that says *live*, that says *eat*, that says *your king may proceed with his evening*.

The hall exhaled without knowing it had been holding its breath. Aldric took up his knife.

"Ravensworth," he said, around a mouthful of venison, and I confess my heart performed a small, traitorous stumble before my face caught up. "You look pale."

"I always look pale, Majesty. It's the profession."

He laughed. Kings love a joke that costs them nothing. "How many is that for you? Poisonings?"

"Dishes, Majesty. I don't count the others."

More laughter, rolling down the long tables like a barrel let loose on stairs. I bowed and withdrew to my station against the wall, where tasters stand: close enough to reach the king's cup, far enough to be furniture. Furniture with a stomach. In twelve years I have learned exactly how much presence a man can shed and still draw wages. It is more than you'd think. A good taster is a ghost with excellent digestion.

The rest of the banquet passed the way banquets do, which is to say slowly and then all at once. I tasted the tenth dish—a honeyed custard, innocent—and the eleventh, and the wine, three times decanted because Aldric trusts no one, including, on certain sleepless nights, himself. By the time the candles had burned down to their sockets, the arsenic was a memory in my blood, one more stone in the cairn I've been building inside myself since I was nineteen years old.

I do not recommend the cairn. But it holds.

* * *

The city below the citadel is called Greyharbor, though there hasn't been a harbor worth the name in a century; the sea walked away and left the mudflats blinking. What remains is smoke and slate and nine thousand people who have learned, the way people do, to want quietly. I moved through them in my second skin—not the taster's livery but a dockhand's oilskin two sizes wrong for me, hood up, shoulders slouched into a shape that invited no second glance. This is the other half of my trade, the half that pays better and kills slower.

In the tannery district, behind a door that opens only for a knock like rain on a coffin lid, a woman named Sorrel counted out my fee in clipped silver and pushed a parcel across the scarred table. It was the size of a prayer book, wrapped in waxed linen, tied with green cord.

"You know what's in it?" she asked. She asks every time. It's not curiosity; it's ritual, the way some men tap a barrel twice before tapping it a third time.

"No," I said, which was true, and also the answer she wanted. Smugglers who know what they carry become owners of it, and ownership is a weight that gets you hanged. I carry things. What they are belongs to whoever waits at the other end.

"It goes over Thornfell," she said. "The high pass. There's a shrine at the saddle—you'll see a cairn, an old one, stones older than the road. Leave it under the east face. Someone will come for it before dawn."

"And if no one comes?"

She shrugged. "Then you've had a walk in the mountains."

I took the parcel. It weighed less than its trouble, which is the best kind of cargo. At the door she caught my sleeve—not grabbing, just touching, the way you'd steady something you weren't sure was yours to steady.

"Ivo," she said. She is one of perhaps four living people who use the name, and I've never decided whether that makes her a friend or a liability. "They say the shrines up there are waking. Candles lit themselves at Wren's Rest last week. Old ones, cold fifty years."

"People say a lot of things."

"People said the sea would never leave Greyharbor," she said, and let go of my sleeve.

* * *

The pass begins where the road gives up pretending. I climbed out of the last shepherd's hamlet as the light went bronze and then ash, and by full dark I was walking under walls of stone so old they had stopped being scenery and become company. Thornfell country. Officially it isn't anything at all—on the maps it's a blank shoulder of the Valdrenn crownlands, marked with contour lines and the occasional surveyor's apology. Unofficially, everyone within a hundred miles still calls it Thornfell, in the lowered voice people reserve for the dead and for debts.

There used to be a kingdom here. That sentence sounds like the start of a fireside tale, and maybe it is, but it's also land registry, more or less. A country with terraced gardens climbing its valleys like green staircases, with gods who answered prayers the way neighbors answer doors, with a royal house whose name I will not write here because writing it feels like stepping on a grave. Then came annexation—not conquest, which at least leaves ruins you can point at, but erasure. Treaties unsigned, records burned, names struck from rolls and maps and gravestones until the whole country became a rumor with borders. The Valdrenn histories call it *the Reconciliation*. I have always admired the nerve of that word.

My grandmother came from those hills. I know this the way I know a few other useless, dangerous things: fragments, kept deliberately fragmentary. A garden above a pass. A song in a language I was told was "just old talk." An instruction repeated like a catechism: *be no one, be no one, be no one.* She died when I was small, and whatever else she carried went into the ground with her, or so I believed for thirty years.

I believed it right up until the moment, an hour past midnight, when I saw the light.

It stood at the saddle of the pass where Sorrel had said it would—or rather, where the cairn stood, the shrine, the heap of stones older than the road. Candlelight. One flame, burning in a niche worn smooth by centuries of weather, in a place where no traveler had reason to pause and every sensible smuggler gave the stones a wide berth out of professional superstition. The flame didn't gutter in the wind. The wind, in fact, seemed to bend around it politely, the way crowds part for a man carrying something breakable.

I should have turned back. Twelve years of being no one teaches you the grammar of turning back. Instead I stood in the shadow of the rocks with a dead man's parcel under my arm and watched a candle burn against the wind, and felt—for the first time in longer than I can say—that the dark was looking back at me.

I did what I'd come to do. Kneeling felt absurd and inevitable at once, like bowing to a king you don't believe in. I tucked the parcel beneath the east face of the cairn, wedged it among stones that were warm. Warm, in October, at altitude, past midnight. Stones should not be warm. I told myself it was geothermal. I have never once in my life believed a lie so quickly or so badly.

As I rose to go, the flame leaned toward me. Not flickered—*leaned*, deliberate as a dog lifting its head at a familiar footstep. And from somewhere inside the cairn, inside the mountain, inside the marrow of the world, came a sound I can only describe as attention. The particular silence of something vast deciding to notice you.

Then a voice—not aloud, not in the air, but in that place behind the breastbone where you keep the things you don't say—spoke three words in the old language my grandmother called *just old talk*. I shouldn't have understood them. I understood them perfectly.

*Ravensworth. Come home.*

I ran. I am not ashamed of it. I ran down the pass with the wind at my heels like a hand between my shoulder blades, and I did not stop until the shrine was a mile behind me and the valley opened below, gray and ordinary, smoking with morning. Only there did I permit myself the luxury of shaking hands and the indignity of being sick over a wall.

Then I sat on the wall until my legs agreed to hold me, and I made myself think, because thinking is the one tool no one can confiscate.

Possibilities. One: someone knows who I am and is playing an elaborate, expensive, lethal joke. Unlikely; jokes require witnesses, and my life has none. Two: I imagined it. Also unlikely—I have hallucinated under poison before, and hallucinations are never that lucid or that brief. Three—

Three was the thing Sorrel had said. *The shrines are waking.* Candles lighting themselves after fifty years of cold. And if the shrines were waking, they were waking for a reason, and reasons in Thornfell country had been buried along with everything else, including, apparently, a name I had spent my whole adult life burying deeper than any grave.

*Ravensworth.*

Not my name. My name is Ivo Marsh, journeyman taster, third guild rank, no family, no history, no debts worth collecting. Ravensworth was a name from a story my grandmother half-told, a name that belonged to a house that no longer existed anywhere except possibly underneath the language itself. A name that, if spoken in the wrong room in Greyharbor, would have me in chains by nightfall and in a shallow hole by spring.

And yet the mountain had said it like a key turning.

I got off the wall. I washed my face in a horse trough, because dignity is a habit worth keeping even when no one is watching—*especially* then, my grandmother would have said, if my grandmother had ever said anything so useful. The sun was coming up over the Valdrenn lowlands, gilding the smoke of Greyharbor, and somewhere in that smoke was a summons I hadn't received yet but could feel approaching like weather: Lady Vessa Corrane, spymaster of the crown, keeper of the king's secrets, collector of rumors about waking shrines. If candles were lighting themselves all over Thornfell, she would already know. If a god had spoken a name over the mountains, she would soon.

And when she sent for her taster—the quiet, reliable, forgettable man who swallows poison for his king and asks nothing but his wage—what would I do? Say no? Say *actually, my lady, the mountains appear to remember me*?

I started walking toward the city, because running looks suspicious and walking looks like nothing, and being nothing is the one art I have truly mastered.

Behind me, high on the pass, I would have sworn the candle was still burning. I didn't look back. Looking back is how ghosts get made, and I intended—at least that morning, with the arsenic warm in my blood and the god's words cooling in my chest—to stay alive a little longer, whatever it cost.

Which, as it turned out, was everything.
