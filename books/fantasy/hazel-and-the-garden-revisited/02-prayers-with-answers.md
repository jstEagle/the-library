## 2. Prayers with Answers

The parcel was small, which was the first lie about it. Small parcels are the ones that kill you. A man will carry a bale of uncut lace through a checkpoint with a song on his lips, but hand him something wrapped in oilcloth the size of a prayer book and he'll sweat through three shirts and start composing his own funeral in his head.

I knew what was in it before I took it. I always know, or I make sure I don't know, which is a different skill entirely and one I've practiced longer. This time I knew. Brannoc's-mark coins, pre-Annexation minting, forty of them, going up to a shrine that officially did not exist in a pass that officially had no name. The buyer was a stonemason in Lowmark who paid me double to ask no questions and triple to forget his face, and I'd have done it for single, because forgetting faces is the one trade I've never had to practice.

Twelve years as the king's taster teaches you things about your body that no one should know. That arsenic arrives like a warm hand on the back of your neck. That hemlock starts in the teeth. That the body is a country with its own borders, and mine has been invaded so many times it's stopped posting sentries and started issuing citizenship. The court physician calls my constitution "remarkable." The Guild calls it an asset. I call it twelve years of being the most useful corpse-in-waiting in Aldric's palace, and I would trade all of it for one afternoon where nobody needed me to swallow anything.

But that's not the arrangement. The arrangement is: I am no one, I am from nowhere, and if I die at table it will be recorded as a stomach complaint. In exchange I get coin, passage, and the particular freedom of a man whose absence would be noted only as an inconvenience.

So: the parcel, the pass, the shrine.

* * *

The Thornfell passes don't appear on maps anymore. That's not smugglers' romance; it's policy. When the crown annexed the old kingdom—twelve years before I was born, though the ink on that erasure is older than my grandmother—they didn't just redraw the borders. They redrew the *paper*. Roads were renamed, shrines were quarried for courthouse stone, and the mountain itself was given a new name in the new tongue, a name so bland that cartographers yawned writing it down. You cannot erase a mountain. But you can make everyone agree to call it something else, and after two generations, agreement does the work of armies.

Smugglers never agreed. Smugglers keep the old names the way other people keep ashes—in boxes, in corners, taken out only when no one's watching. On our routes we still say Thornfell when we mean the high passes, still swear by Brannoc when we drop a crate, still leave a coin at certain cairns without quite knowing why. It's not faith. It's ballast. Tradition is just cargo that got too heavy to throw overboard.

The shrine sat above the tree line where the pass narrowed to a throat of grey stone—a hollowed-out boulder, really, older than any kingdom, with a spring running out of its base and a lintel carved with knots that the chisels of the Annexation never reached this high to deface. There was a man inside it.

I smelled the trouble before I saw him. Blood has a smell in cold air, iron and something sweeter, and this was the sweet kind, the deep kind. He was curled against the boulder's inner wall, a big man gone soft-looking with blood loss, and I recognized him the way you recognize anyone on the routes: by silhouette and gait and the particular way he'd tied his pack. Doss. Ran wool and tin down the western passes for years. We'd shared fires maybe six times and never once exchanged true names, because that's the covenant of the road—you give a road-name, a route, and nothing else.

He'd been gutted. Not stabbed—gutted, torn low across the belly, the kind of wound that comes from rockfall or a boar or something with opinions about trespassers. His hands were pressed to himself and they were shaking, and there was so much blood soaked into the stone beneath him that the spring water running past it came out pink.

"Ivo," he said.

That stopped me. Not the knowing my name—I'm known on the routes, in the way a bridge is known—but the *way* he said it. Like it mattered. Like it was worth spending his last breath on.

"Doss." I knelt. My hands were already moving, doing the arithmetic of wounds—the depth, the heat, the smell of the gut contents, the pallor of his lips—and the arithmetic came back the way it always comes back for wounds like this. *No.* "Who did this?"

"Boar. Below the scree." He laughed, and the laugh cost him, and I watched the cost land. "Came up here to pray. Can you believe it? Forty years on the roads and I come up to pray."

"To who?" I said it lightly, the way you do. Nobody answers that question seriously anymore. The old gods were struck off along with everything else—Brannoc of the thresholds, Hazel of the gardens, the nameless ones of the springs. You can worship a god who's been erased, I suppose, but it's like posting letters to a house that's been demolished. Somewhere a postman stands puzzled in an empty lot.

"Brannoc," Doss said. "Keeper of doors. Opener of ways." His bloody hand left his belly and fumbled at his neck, and came out holding a coin—one of the ones I was carrying, I realized, same mint, same worn knotwork. "My father's. Carried it forty years. Never prayed once. Thought—" His breath hitched. "Thought it was just a habit. Just ballast."

"Doss, listen to me. I can get you down to Lowmark. There's a bone-setter—"

"There's no time and you know it." He said it without fear, which frightened me more than the wound did. "Help me sit up. Against the stone. Please."

And here is the thing I have thought about every day since: I helped him. I put my shoulder under a dying man and moved him against the carved stone of a god who officially does not exist, because he asked, and because some part of me—the part that tastes poison for a living and knows exactly how much of death a body can hold—understood that this was the one thing left I could actually give him. Comfort is also a delivery. I've carried stranger cargo.

He sat. The spring ran pink past his boots. Above us the wind came through the pass making the sound wind makes in high narrow places, which is either nothing or a voice, depending on how long you've been alone.

"Brannoc," Doss said, and his voice changed. I've heard men pray at executions and men pray in brothels and men pray over cards, and it's always performance, always a bid for an audience, even when the audience is only themselves. This wasn't that. This was a man setting down a pack he'd carried forty years. "I never asked you anything. Not once. Not when the snow took the Kestrel Road. Not when the revenue men burned Marek's barn with him in it. I figured you were gone, or you were never there, and either way a man carries his own weight." His hand found the coin again. "But I'm asking now. Not for me. For the boy in Rillhollow. My sister's boy. He doesn't know me, and that's how it should stay, but there's money buried under the third hearthstone, and if I die here it stays buried, and he grows up with nothing, and that's the whole prayer. That's it. Open that door for me. One door."

The wind came through the pass again.

And then it stopped.

You have to understand what that means in a place like that. Wind in the high passes is not weather; it's the permanent condition, the way wet is the condition of a riverbed. It has blown since before the Annexation, since before the old kingdom, since before names. It stopping is like the sea deciding to hold its breath.

In the silence, the spring ran clear.

Not gradually. Not fading from pink to pale. It went from blood to clean water between one heartbeat and the next, as if the mountain had blinked. And the light changed—not brightened, exactly, but *warmed*, the way light changes when a door opens in a dark house somewhere behind you. And the smell. Cold stone and blood, and then underneath it, rising like bread from an oven: loam. Turned earth. Green growing things. In a shrine above the tree line, in the dead season, in a pass where nothing blooms until midsummer, I smelled a garden.

Doss made a sound I will hear until I die. Not a word. A sound like a man who has been carrying something heavy for forty years finally being told he can set it down.

The wound closed.

I watched it. I want to be precise about this, because precision is the only religion I have left: I watched torn flesh draw together like water finding its level. I watched blood that had soaked into stone darken and dry and flake away as if the years of its aging were being returned. I watched color come back into a face that had been the grey of old candle wax, and I watched a man's belly, opened to the air in the cold, seal itself smooth under my eyes with only a pale line remaining, like a scar from some ordinary childhood fall.

Brannoc did not appear. There was no figure, no voice, no burning bush. There was only the sense—overwhelming, physical, undeniable—that something enormous had turned its attention toward this hollow of stone, considered a small dishonest request about a hearthstone in Rillhollow, and found it *good*. The way a gardener considers a seedling. The way a taster considers a dish.

Then the wind resumed, the light went ordinary, and the spring ran clear and cold and slightly amazed.

Doss looked down at himself. He touched the new scar with two fingers, the way you touch a tooth that shouldn't be there. Then he looked at me, and his eyes were wet, and he was laughing, quietly, helplessly, the laugh of a man whose entire understanding of the world had just been repossessed.

"Forty years," he said. "Forty years of ballast."

"What did it cost you?" That was my question. Of course it was. I taste for a living; my first instinct toward any gift is to ask what's in it.

"Cost?" He shook his head, still laughing. "It didn't cost. That's the thing, Ivo. It *gave*. Like it had been waiting. Like the debt was owed to *me*." He stood, testing his legs, and I noticed—he noticed me noticing—that he moved like a man twenty years younger. "Third hearthstone, Rillhollow," he said, half to himself. "I suppose I'd better go dig it up."

"You're not going to tell anyone."

It wasn't a question and he knew it. He looked at me then, really looked, and for the first time in our six fires and ten years of routes, I felt *seen*, and I want to tell you that being seen by a friend feels like warmth, but I'd be lying. Being seen feels like standing in an open doorway with winter at your back.

"No," he said gently. "I'm not going to tell anyone. But Ivo—it's not just me. Ask yourself why the gods picked *now* to wake up. Ask yourself what they're waiting for." He shouldered his pack—the same pack, the same worn straps, but somehow it looked lighter on him. "There's a reason the old prayers are all addressed to keepers of doors."

He went down the pass toward Rillhollow, walking like a man with an appointment. I stayed in the shrine a long while after, because my legs had decided independently that they weren't ready to carry me anywhere.

I tasted the air. Old habit. Arsenic is warm at the back of the neck; hemlock starts in the teeth; whatever filled that hollow of stone had been neither. It had been the opposite of poison. It had been the thing poison is defined against—the undamaged original, the clean vintage, the taste of something before it was ever tampered with.

Here is what I understood, sitting alone in a shrine that officially did not exist, next to a spring that had run with a dying man's blood and then decided otherwise:

Answered prayers are not miracles. Miracles are exceptions. Answered prayers are *systems*—a god awake, attending, keeping accounts. And systems can be mapped, and mapped things can be hunted, and the crown did not spend two generations erasing Thornfell—its names, its shrines, its royal line, its gods—to allow the erasure to start unraveling because a smuggler mentioned a hearthstone.

If Brannoc was answering prayers, then the great forgetting was failing. And if the forgetting was failing, then someone, eventually, would come asking what else had been buried under it. Names. Lines. Heirs.

I did not think of my own name then. I want to be honest: I did not. I thought of logistics. Routes, witnesses, the mason in Lowmark, the forty coins in my pack that suddenly weighed differently. I thought: *this must not reach the court.*

I thought: *no one can ever see me near this place.*

And then, because I am who I am—a man whose profession is swallowing danger so that others needn't—I picked up the parcel, walked to the lintel stone, and left one of Brannoc's own coins in the hollow of the carved knotwork. Ballast. Habit. Payment.

The wind took it, or the shrine did. I didn't wait to see which.

Three days later, in Lowmark, a message reached me through channels that don't exist: Lady Vessa Corrane, spymaster to King Aldric, requested my presence at court. The summons gave no reason. They never do.

But folded inside it, in her precise, unhurried hand, was a single line:

*They say the old god spoke a name over the mountains. Find out whose.*
