## 3. A Face in the Crowd

The summons came the way bad weather comes to smugglers: first a stillness that felt wrong, then the smell of it on the wind, and by then it was already too late to be anywhere else.

In my case, the wind smelled of beeswax and ink, which is how I knew the message was genuine. Forged orders from Lady Vessa Corrane would have been simpler—she was not a woman who wasted wax. The seal pressed into it was the spymaster's own: a crow holding a key in its beak, rendered so finely I could count the feathers. Whoever had carved that die had been given time and money and no deadline at all.

*Come to the Gray Gallery at the ninth bell. Come alone. Come as yourself.*

That last line had kept me awake longer than it should have. It meant nothing. It could not have meant anything. Nobody in the palace knew who I was—I had spent twelve years and no small amount of pain making certain of that—and yet the words sat in my chest like a swallowed stone. *Come as yourself.* As if there were such a person. As if "myself" were an outfit I kept folded in a drawer somewhere, next to my other disguises.

I went, of course. You do not ignore a summons from Vessa Corrane; you simply choose whether to arrive early or dead.

I chose early.

* * *

The Gray Gallery ran along the eastern face of the palace, a corridor of tall windows and taller pretension, hung with portraits of monarchs who had won their thrones by outliving their brothers. At the ninth bell the light came through low and gold and turned every dust mote into a small burning planet. I had walked this corridor a hundred times as furniture—a taster does not exist until he is needed, and even then he exists only as a mouth. I knew how to walk so that guards' eyes slid off me like rain off oiled leather.

Today I walked the same way, and it did not work.

It began at the far end of the gallery. A maid with an armful of linens looked up as I passed, and her eyes caught on my face for a full second—one heartbeat, maybe two—before she remembered herself and dropped her gaze. A second later a guard by the third window turned his head. Not sharply. Just the idle turn of a man noticing another man, which is a thing that had not happened to me since I was nineteen years old.

I did not break stride. You learn that much in my trade: panic is just poison you make yourself, and I had sworn off self-administered doses years ago. But something under my ribs had begun to hum, low and wrong, like a wineglass feeling out the note that will crack it.

By the time I reached the door at the gallery's end, three people had seen me. Three. In twelve years, I could count on one hand the number of people who had *looked* at me, and now they were doing it in broad daylight, unbidden, as if my face had decided to stop being a rumor.

The door opened before I knocked.

"Master Ivo." The secretary was a narrow man named Pell, whose entire personality had been distilled down to the ability to open doors at the correct moment. "She's waiting."

"She always is," I said, and he almost smiled, which for Pell was a religious experience.

* * *

Vessa Corrane worked in a room with no windows, which told you everything about her before she said a word. Spymasters are supposed to favor shadowy chambers with hidden doors and listening tubes in the walls, and most of them do, because most of them believe their own theater. Vessa had chosen a plain square room with one lamp, one desk, two chairs, and walls of bare gray stone, because she understood that the best place to hide a secret is not in the dark but in a room so boring that no one bothers to look twice.

She was not looking at papers when I came in. She was looking at me. That was the first wrong thing. People look *through* me, or *past* me, or at the space where they think I'm standing. Vessa looked at me the way an apothecary looks at a specimen she suspects has been mislabeled.

"Taster," she said. "Sit."

I sat. The chair was deliberately uncomfortable. I approved. Comfort makes people talkative, and neither of us wanted that.

"You survived the venison course last week," she said.

"The king enjoyed it. The arsenic less so."

"Don't joke about the arsenic."

"It's the only way I can joke about anything else."

Something moved at the corner of her mouth—not a smile, more the memory of one, filed away for later use. She was perhaps forty, with iron-gray hair cut short for practicality and hands that never fully settled, always turning a ring she wore on her right thumb, an old silver band worn nearly smooth. I'd noticed the ring over the years the way you notice weather. Today her thumb worried it constantly.

"I'm going to tell you a thing," she said, "and then I'm going to watch your face while I tell it, and you should know that watching faces is what I do instead of sleeping."

"That explains the eyes."

"My eyes are fine."

"They're excellent. That's the problem."

She let that sit, then leaned forward, and the lamplight found the lines around her mouth, the ones earned by delivering bad news to powerful people.

"Three weeks ago," she said, "a shepherd on the high slopes above Coldmere heard voices in the wind. Not wind-voices—the kind shepherds hear all winter and learn to ignore. Actual voices. Old ones. Speaking a language the crown banned sixty years ago, in a dialect nobody under fifty should know." She paused. "And among the words the wind carried, clear as a bell across four valleys, was a name."

I kept my face very still. This is the one skill I have never had to practice; stillness is just what's left when you've removed everything else.

"Ravensworth," she said.

The word landed in the room like a coin dropped in a well. I listened for the splash. There wasn't one—or if there was, it happened somewhere below hearing, deep in me, where I keep the things I don't examine.

"Old name," I said. My voice came out level. Twelve years of tasting have taught me that the voice goes level on its own when the body is busy elsewhere. "Border family. Landholders, once. Before the annexation."

"You know your history."

"I know everyone's history. It's safer than knowing my own."

Her thumb stopped moving on the ring. Then started again.

"There's more," she said. "Two days ago, a shrine on the Thornfell side of the pass—Brannoc's shrine, the little one under the split rock—was found swept clean and relit. Fresh offerings. Bread, salt, a cup of small beer. Someone climbed up there in winter and tended a god that hasn't legally existed since before either of us was born." She tilted her head. "And yesterday, a dying smuggler walked out of the same mountains with a wound that should have killed him, healed shut. He's telling anyone who'll listen that Brannoc answered his prayer. Half of them laugh. The other half are already walking up into the hills with offerings of their own."

"And the crown cares because?"

"The crown cares," Vessa said, "because answered prayers mean the annexation isn't finished. You don't erase a country by drawing new borders. You erase it by killing its memory, its language, its gods—and we did all three, thoroughly, or we believed we did. If the gods are waking, then the erasure is unraveling, and if the erasure is unraveling—" she spread her hands, elegant, terrible, "—then everything that was buried comes back up. Names included."

"Names," I said.

"Ravensworth," she said again, softer this time, watching me the whole while. "The old royal line of Thornfell. Poisoned out of existence, according to the histories. All of them. Root and branch." She smiled without warmth. "You'd appreciate that, taster. It was poison that finished them. The guild's own founding texts mention it—the Ravensworths were the reason the Taster's Guild received its charter. Find the poisons, find the poisoners, find the blood. Your profession was born to hunt that family into the ground."

Somewhere in my chest, the humming glass note got louder. I thought of the mountain shrine, the dying man, the god's hand closing over him like a father's over a candle flame. I thought of the word carried on the wind across four valleys, and how names are like seeds—they wait decades in dark soil, and everyone believes the field is empty.

"What do you want from me?" I asked.

"Investigate," she said. "The shrines. All of them, the whole length of the pass. You move through Thornfell country better than any agent I have—you're a ghost on those roads, half my reports call you the Gray Man of the passes—and ghosts hear things soldiers don't. I want to know who's tending the shrines. I want to know if the healing was real or fever-dream. And I want to know—" her eyes narrowed a fraction, "—whether the name in the wind means anything, or whether it's mountain madness and wishful thinking from people who miss being a country."

"And if it means something?"

"Then the king will need to know what woke it, and why, and whether it can be put back to sleep." She said it flatly, without cruelty, which somehow made it worse. Cruelty you can hate. Flatness you have to carry.

I stood. My legs worked, which felt like an oversight.

"You'll have your report," I said.

"One more thing." She rose too, and for a moment we stood across the desk from each other, two professionals in a windowless room, and she did the thing I will remember longer than anything else she said that day: she looked at my face. Fully. Deliberately. The way no one had looked at it in twelve years. Her gaze moved across it like a hand smoothing cloth, taking the measure of it, and I felt every year of my invisibility peel back like bark.

"Do you know," she said quietly, "that in nine years of correspondence and a dozen debriefings, I have never once been able to describe you afterward? I sit down to write my reports and your face is gone from my memory like water off glass. I assumed it was a trick of yours. Some smuggler's glamour." Her thumb stilled on the ring. "This morning I tried again. I still can't. Isn't that strange?"

"It's a professional asset," I said. "Ask the Guild."

"Mm." She released me from her gaze, and the room came back—lamp, stone, dust. "Go to the shrines, taster. And Ivo—" she used my name deliberately, the way you set a trap with good bait, "—if you find anything about that name in the wind, anything at all, you'll bring it to me first. Before anyone. Before the gods themselves, if they'll stand still long enough."

"As yourself," I said, before I could stop it.

Her eyebrows rose a fraction. "What?"

"Nothing. A figure of speech."

I left. Behind me, in the windowless room, I imagined her sitting back down, pulling paper toward her, and stopping—pen hovering—because once again there was nothing to write about my face.

* * *

I made it as far as the Gray Gallery before the vertigo took me properly.

It came on between one window and the next, the gold light lying along the floor in long bars, and suddenly the corridor seemed to tilt, gently, the way a ship tilts when the swell changes. I put my hand against the cold stone and stood there breathing while courtiers flowed past me like water around a rock—which was the problem, wasn't it, the rock part, the fact that today the water noticed the rock, today the maid had looked, the guard had turned his head—

Because the gods had said a name over the mountains. *Ravensworth.* Across four valleys, clear as a bell.

And names, spoken aloud by old gods, are not words. They are hooks. They go out into the world and they catch on things, and what they catch on, they pull. I had spent twelve years sanding myself smooth of every hook—no surname on any ledger, no birthplace in any record, a face that memory itself declined to hold. I had done it so well that I had half convinced myself there was nothing underneath the smoothing. No boy. No garden. No grandmother saying my true name into my hair in a language the crown had drowned.

But the gods didn't say a name into the wind for no reason. Gods are economical. They say what is true.

Which meant somewhere under all my careful nothing, the truth was still there, intact, waiting—had been waiting all along, patient as a seed—and the moment it was spoken aloud by something older than the crown, every eye in the world had begun, slowly, without knowing why, to slide toward me and *catch.*

The maid in the gallery. The guard at the window. Vessa's pen hovering over blank paper.

My invisibility wasn't failing. It was cracking—hairline fractures spreading out from a name, and through each fracture, light was getting in. Or getting out. I honestly couldn't tell which, and that was the worst of it. Because if light was getting out, then someone would eventually see what was inside me, and what was inside me was a country.

I straightened. Walked on. Out through the servants' gate, past the guard who did not quite look at me, down into the city where I was no one, which had always been my safest country and was starting, I realized, to feel like a cell.

There was work waiting. There was always work waiting—a parcel to run through the passes, the ordinary honest dishonesty of my days. And now there was also an order from the spymaster of the realm: go into the mountains, taster, and find out what woke the gods.

What she didn't know—what no one knew—was that I already had.

I had watched a dying man healed by a god with my own eyes, and run from it like a thief. And higher up, above the pass, there was a terraced garden cut into the mountainside that I had not visited in twelve years, because visiting it meant remembering, and remembering meant being someone.

I bought passage on a wool cart going east and did not look back at the palace.

But all the way out of the city, past the gates and the gallows tree and the last rooftops, I could feel it behind me like weather: the sense of being looked at. Not by Vessa. Not by the crown.

By the mountains themselves.

They had said a name into the wind. And the name was mine.
