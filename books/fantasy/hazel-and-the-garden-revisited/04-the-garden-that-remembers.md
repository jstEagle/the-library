## 4. The Garden That Remembers

There are three rules to being no one, and I had broken all of them by noon.

Rule one: never return to a place that remembers you. Rule two: never walk toward the thing you're afraid of; circle it, let it come to you, be somewhere else when it arrives. Rule three—Hazel's rule, though she'd have denied making rules at all—*never love anything you can't carry out of a burning house.*

The terraced garden above Greywater Pass remembered me. I was walking straight toward it. And as for what I loved—I'd find out soon enough that I'd left it up there twelve years ago, growing wild in the dark, waiting for me like an unpaid debt.

I told myself it was reconnaissance. The shrine at Brannoc's Knee had healed a dying man two nights past, and Lady Vessa Corrane would want a report eventually, and between now and *eventually* I needed to know how far the waking had spread. The garden sat above the pass like a held breath. If the old magic was leaking back into Thornfell, it would pool in high places first. Places that had been watered with something other than rain.

That was the story I told my boots, anyway. Boots are easy to lie to. They only care about direction.

* * *

My grandmother's name was Hazel, and she was the sort of woman who made even her name sound like an accusation.

"You've your grandfather's way of standing," she told me once, when I was seven or eight, "like furniture pretending not to be in the room." She said it while thinning carrots, not looking up, dirt to the wrist, and I remember thinking it was the cruelest thing anyone had ever said to me and also the first time anyone had ever looked at me properly, which is a confusing pair of gifts to receive at once.

She taught me the garden the way other people teach scripture. Not the flowers—the flowers were vanity, she said, pretty faces for passing trade—but the rows. The food rows. *A garden isn't a picture,* she said. *It's an argument. You're arguing with winter, and winter cheats.*

I can still map those terraces with my eyes shut. Seven levels cut into the mountainside above the pass, stone-faced, each wall fitted without mortar because mortar, Hazel said, was for people who didn't trust their own hands. On the lowest terrace she grew the practical things: roots, brassicas, beans that climbed strings she strung from stakes cut on the dark of the moon, because of course they were. The middle terraces were herbs, and not the polite kind. There was a bed of things she never named aloud, and when I asked, she said, "Those are for arguments you lose," and I understood even then that she meant poison, though neither of us used the word.

The top terrace was empty. Always empty. Turned soil, raked clean, nothing planted, year after year.

"What grows there?" I asked her once.

"Nothing yet," she said. And then, quieter, in the voice she used for things that were true: "Nothing yet."

I thought she meant she was old and tired. Children are generous interpreters of grief. What she meant, I understand now, was that something had been *taken out* of that soil, and she was keeping the space honest until it could come home.

The last time I saw her I was seventeen. Annexation had already eaten Thornfell whole—there wasn't a Thornfell anymore, officially, just the northern reaches of Aldric's kingdom, its name struck from maps with the tidy efficiency of a line through a debtor's ledger. Soldiers came up the pass road in their grey-and-crimson, and my grandparents went down to meet them, and I watched from the top terrace because Hazel had put me there with her hands on my shoulders and said, *Whatever you hear, whatever you see, you stay on this terrace until the sun clears the eastern ridge. Promise me.* I promised. It's the only promise I've ever kept without wanting to break it.

I heard very little. A cart. Voices, low, unhurried, almost gentle. My grandfather laughing once—a short, strange bark of a laugh, like a man agreeing to a joke he doesn't find funny. Then the cart going away down the pass, and the sun clearing the ridge, and the terrace under my feet warm as a living animal.

They never came back. No bodies, no trial record, no grave. In the new king's registers, the Ravensworths simply stopped, the way a word stops appearing in a language after the last speaker dies. I learned to do the same to myself. It seemed like the family trade.

* * *

The climb took most of the morning, which suited me. I don't trust arrivals that happen quickly.

Greywater Pass in early spring is all meltwater and mud and the particular silence of places where the wind has been shouting so long it's worn itself hoarse. The smugglers' trail branches off the shepherd path at a cairn I'd built myself at fourteen, and I noticed with distant irritation that someone had added stones to it over the years. Smugglers do that—mark a route that works. But this cairn had been built *up*, carefully, into a small tower taller than my knee, and someone had set a sprig of hawthorn on top, fresh, green, impossible this early in the season.

I stood there a long moment doing arithmetic I didn't like.

Then I climbed.

You feel the garden before you see it. That's not poetry; it's temperature. The air coming down off the terraces was warmer than the air around it by several degrees, soft, smelling of turned earth and green growth and—underneath, faint as a signature—hawthorn blossom. In early spring. Before the snowmelt had finished running off the high shoulders of the mountain.

I came around the last bend of the trail and stopped.

Every terrace was blooming.

Not overgrown—that's the thing I need you to understand, because overgrown I could have explained. Overgrown is just neglect wearing a green coat. Neglect I know intimately; I've been neglect's business partner for years. This was not neglect. This was *tending*. The bean strings were strung taut and fresh. The carrot beds were thinned to proper spacing. The herb rows were weeded to a knife-edge precision I recognized the way you recognize a dead parent's handwriting. The low walls stood clean of frost-heave, every stone seated exactly where Hazel's hands—or hands taught by Hazel's hands—had set them.

And the top terrace, the empty one, the one that had grown nothing for thirty years—

The top terrace was planted.

Rows of something I didn't recognize at first, waist-high, silver-green leaves, and here and there a flower like a white star with a heart of gold. I knew it the way you know a face in a crowd you haven't seen since childhood: not with the eyes first. With the body. With some older organ of knowing that lives behind the sternum.

I'd seen it pressed flat in the back of Hazel's seed book. One specimen, brown, brittle, labeled in her cramped hand with a single word in the old tongue. The word for *homecoming*, if the stories were true. The word for *Thornfell itself*, if they were truer than that.

It should not have been alive. It should not have been *growing*. Every botanist in the capital would tell you the species was extinct, catalogued, closed, a footnote in a chapter about a country that no longer existed. And here it stood in the mountain cold, drinking snowmelt, blooming weeks out of season, tended by no one.

By no one.

I walked the terraces slowly, the way you walk through a house where someone has just died, checking each room against your memory of them alive. Everything matched. Everything matched *too well*. There was a watering can by the second-terrace wall, cedar staves bound with iron, and I knew that can—I'd carried it when I was too small to carry it, dragging it between my feet like a stubborn pet. It was full. The iron bands were bright. Somewhere in the last day, someone had drawn water from the spring and carried it up these stairs.

But there were no footprints but mine. The melt-mud at the terrace edges held a single set of tracks, going up, and they were mine, and I checked twice, because a man whose profession is noticing things learns to distrust his own eyes before he distrusts the world.

"Right," I said aloud, to the garden, because talking to empty gardens is exactly the sort of thing a man does right before his life stops being manageable. "Who's minding you?"

The garden declined to answer. Gardens are worse interrogators' subjects than smugglers. I've known men hold out against thumbscrews; I've never known a parsnip crack.

So I did what I always do when a mystery won't confess: I worked. I found the hoe leaning where it always leaned, and I thinned a row of onions on the middle terrace, and the work went through my hands like water through a channel cut long ago, muscle remembering what the mind had spent twelve years burying. Hazel's voice lived in my wrists. *Thin to a hand's width. Don't mourn the ones you pull; the row is stronger for the choosing.* I hadn't thought of that sentence in a decade. It arrived whole, uncorroded, in her exact cadence, and I had to stop and put my hand flat on the cold stone wall and breathe until the feeling passed through me like weather.

Grief is a lot like arsenic, if you want to know. Small doses, regularly administered, and the body builds a tolerance. It's the big single dose that kills you. Twelve years of careful daily forgetting had made me proof against ordinary sorrow. This garden was administering the large dose.

Because here is what I had not let myself know, all this time, in the way a man doesn't let himself know he's lonely by never once using the word: I had believed the garden died with them. I had needed it to die with them. Because if the garden lived—if the garden went on being tended, season after season, by no one, for no one, in a country that officially did not exist—then their disappearance hadn't been an ending. It had been a *leaving*. And people who leave intend to come back, or intend for something to come back, and I had built my entire self on the foundation stone of *nothing is coming back, nothing is owed, no one is looking for you, and thank all the quiet gods for that.*

The gods, I was beginning to suspect, had not stayed quiet.

* * *

I found the carving near dusk, on the third terrace, in the face of the retaining wall.

I'd knelt to check the drainage channel—old habit, Hazel drilled the drains into me before the letters—and my thumb caught on a groove in the stone that shouldn't have been there. Smooth-edged. Weathered. Cut deep enough to hold shadow.

I brushed the grit away with my sleeve.

Letters. Child-cut letters, wobbling, uneven, the R backwards the way children do, the strokes gouged rather than carved because the hand that made them had been small and impatient and armed with a nail or a knife-point and more certainty than skill.

**IVO**

My own name, cut into my grandmother's garden wall, in a child's hand.

My hand.

I sat back on my heels in the dirt of the third terrace and looked at it for a long time. The light was going amber and then grey, and the silver-green plants on the top terrace caught the last of it and held it like lamps. Somewhere below, meltwater ticked in the channels. The hawthorn smell thickened as the air cooled, sweet and slightly sharp, the smell of thresholds, the smell of doors.

I remembered cutting it. Barely—it surfaced the way things surface from deep water, distorted, trailing weed. I was five, maybe six. Hazel had been showing me how the wall stones were fitted, no mortar, trust your own hands, and I'd wanted—children want terrible things—to leave a mark, to prove I'd been there, to make the garden *mine* the way it was hers. And she'd caught me at it, nail in the stone, and instead of scolding she'd gone very still, and then she'd knelt beside me and helped me finish the letters, guiding my hand on the hard ones, and afterward she'd said, "There. Now the garden knows you," and I'd laughed because I was six and didn't know that everything adults say is either a lie or a prophecy.

Now the garden knows you.

The garden knew me. The garden had known me for thirty years, through annexation and erasure and twelve years of my professional commitment to being forgettable, and it had gone on keeping my name cut in its wall the way a mother keeps a tooth, and now that the old gods were waking and prayers were being answered over the mountains, the garden had—what? Sent for me? Bloomed at me? Strung the bean rows and filled the watering can and waited, patient as soil, for the last heir of a struck-off country to climb the pass and finally, finally read his own handwriting?

I am not a man who cries. Crying is visibility, and visibility is death; I learned that at seventeen on the top terrace with the sun climbing the ridge. But I will report, in the interest of accuracy, that something happened to my face in the dusk on the third terrace, involving water, and that I let it happen for exactly as long as it takes to thin a row of carrots, and no longer.

Then I stood up, because standing up is also a tolerance you can build.

* * *

Here's what I know about myself, and I know it the way a taster knows poison: precisely, professionally, and too late to be useful. I know ninety-one ways a man can be killed at table. I know the weight of every border seal in three kingdoms and the smell of every forgery. I know how to be forgotten by a room in four seconds flat.

I do not know what to do with being *remembered*.

The stars were out by the time I finished walking the terraces a final time, touching nothing. At the head of the stair I paused, because the top terrace was glowing faintly—not the plants, I realized, but the soil between them, a shimmer like moonlight stored underground, rising, breathing, keeping time with something. With someone. The garden wasn't tended by no one.

It was tended by everyone Thornfell had ever lost, all at once, patiently, from the other side of something.

I went down the pass in the dark, taking the smugglers' trail at speed, because I had just understood two things and both of them required a library.

First: the waking wasn't spreading outward from the shrines. It was spreading outward from *here*. From this garden. From the top terrace where nothing had grown for thirty years and now everything did.

Second: a name spoken over the mountains by waking gods—a name Vessa Corrane would be hearing reports of within the week—was a name I shared. And Lady Vessa Corrane, spymaster, keeper of the crown's restricted archives, did not strike me as a woman who would stop at rumors.

If I wanted to know what the Ravensworth name meant before she did—if I wanted to know why a guild of royal poison-tasters had trained me so thoroughly, so specifically, so *expensively*—I was going to have to go into the archives myself.

I'm good at getting into places I'm not supposed to be.

I'm about to find out whether I'm good at surviving what I find there.
