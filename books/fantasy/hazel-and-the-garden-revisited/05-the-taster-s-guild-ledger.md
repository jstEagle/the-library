## 5. The Taster's Guild Ledger

There are three ways into the Taster's Guild archive, and I have earned all of them honestly, which is to say through poison, patience, and a talent for being forgotten in rooms where I am technically present.

The first way is the front door on Candlemakers' Row, used by guildmasters and their clerks, who walk past me with the particular blindness of men who have decided I am furniture. The second is the cellar stair that comes up from the wine vaults, used by tasters carrying ledgers too sensitive for daylight. The third is a gap behind the shelving of the apothecary's reference section, wide enough for a man built like a rumor, which I am.

I went in by the third way, an hour past midnight, because the second way has a door that clicks, and the first way has a clerk named Ossory who sleeps badly.

The archive smells the way old paper always smells: like something sweet that died politely. Candles are forbidden down here—fire and parchment being old enemies—so I worked by the light of a small lamp with a green glass shade, the kind the guild issues for reading in the dark without being read. I had stolen it from myself, years ago, which is the cleanest kind of theft.

* * *

Let me explain what I was looking for, and why a man whose entire survival depends on leaving no trace had spent three days arranging to leave one.

Ravensworth. The name spoken over the mountains by a god with no reason to know it. The name Vessa Corrane had said aloud in her office while watching my face, the way a jeweler watches a stone she suspects is paste—or isn't. She hadn't suspected me. Not yet. But names are like arsenic: harmless in the air, lethal once ingested, and Lady Corrane had already swallowed hers and was waiting to see where it settled.

So I needed to know what the name meant before she did. That was the whole strategy. Stay ahead of the woman whose profession is staying ahead of everyone.

The restricted shelves were organized by a dead guildmaster with a sense of humor, or possibly just a bad memory: annexation-era documents filed under *Trade Disputes, Resolved*. Thornfell had been many things—a kingdom, a granary, a garden—but on the crown's books it had been, officially, a trade dispute. You can erase a country by reclassifying it. I have seen men do the same to themselves.

I pulled the ledger at random, or what felt like random, though I no longer believe in that word. It was bound in grey calfskin, unmarked except for a number stamped at the spine: 114. The spine cracked when I opened it, softly, like a knuckle.

*Founding Charter, Guild of Royal Tasters, Year One of the Union.*

I almost put it back. Founding charters are where institutions lie about themselves—the part where they claim to have been invented to protect kings out of pure devotion, rather than because someone was paying. Every guild has one. The Cooks' charter claims their oaths go back to the first hearth. The Smugglers' doesn't exist, which is the most honest document in the city.

But the ink on the first page was good ink, expensive, the kind you use when the words matter, and so I read.

*Whereas the Kingdom of Thornfell did persist beyond its lawful dissolution—*

I stopped. Read it again. *Did persist.* Not *did resist*, not *did rebel*. Persisted. Like a fever. Like a debt.

*—and whereas certain bloodlines of the former royal house were not accounted for in the settlement of said dissolution; and whereas such remnants may be expected to persist likewise, concealed among the commonalty; be it enacted that there shall be established a Guild of Royal Tasters, sworn to the protection of the King's person, and charged further with the identification of persons of remnant blood, by whatsoever means the Guild shall devise.*

*By whatsoever means the Guild shall devise.*

I sat down on the floor. Not dramatically—I don't do dramatic; dramatic gets you noticed—but my knees made the decision independently and I let them have it.

I want to tell you I misunderstood at first. That I thought it was coincidence, or metaphor, or some clerk's florid style. But I have spent twelve years learning to read documents the way I read dishes: looking for the thing underneath the thing. And underneath this thing was very simple.

The guild that trained me. The guild that fed me poisons in graduated doses until my body learned each one like a language. The guild that taught me to detect hemlock by the numbness in my gums and arsenic by its warmth and nightshade by the dream it puts behind your eyes—all of it, every lesson, every scar, every year of my life measured out in milligrams—

It wasn't founded to protect kings.

That was the cover story. The *front*. The legitimate face of the operation, the way my smuggling routes have legitimate faces: a wool factor here, a candle merchant there. Protect the king's person. Yes. And also—and primarily, if you read the charter in order, and charters are always written in order of importance—find the people who survived the erasure of Thornfell, and identify them.

Identify. Such a gentle word. Charters don't say *kill*. They say *identify*, and then they say *by whatsoever means*, and then they stop talking, because the stopping is the sentence.

* * *

I should have left then. I know that now. A wise man takes one wound at a time and goes home to bleed privately. But I have never claimed wisdom; I claim competence, and competence wanted the rest of the ledger.

Because there was more. Of course there was more. Ledger 114 was the founding charter plus forty years of annotations, and the annotations were in different hands across the decades, and hands tell you things. Early entries were cramped, bureaucratic, satisfied. Later ones grew uncertain. One hand, around Year Thirty, pressed so hard the nib tore the page:

*No remnant located. Query: does remnant exist?*

And beneath it, another hand, older, in faded brown ink:

*Remnant exists. Remnant always exists. We found none because we taught them our own methods. Note for successors: the best-hidden heir will be the one we trained ourselves.*

I read that line four times.

Somewhere in the dark behind the shelving, the building settled, a beam ticking as it cooled, and I heard it as clearly as a bell. Because I understood, suddenly, with the cold clarity that arsenic warmth was always a rehearsal for—that the guild had done exactly what the note predicted. They had gone looking for hidden heirs and failed, and so they had done something cleverer. They had built a machine that would find heirs whether or not anyone was looking.

Think about how a taster is chosen. I remember my own selection, or the shape of it: an orphan, no family records, no name that anyone could trace—which was presented to me as a mercy. *No attachments*, the guildmaster said. *Clean blood, unencumbered.* They tested us for tolerance, dosed us, watched which children survived the curriculum. Twelve started. Three finished. I never asked what happened to the other nine, because asking questions is a habit tasters are cured of early.

But now run the logic backward, the way you'd work out a dish from its aftertaste. Who would you recruit if your true purpose was finding remnant blood? Not random orphans. You would recruit orphans *of uncertain origin*—children whose parentage was conveniently missing, whose bloodlines were blank pages. You would dose them with the old poisons, the Thornfell-specific compounds, the ones developed during the annexation wars against a people whose bodies carried the covenant of their gods in their very cells. And you would watch.

You would watch for the ones who didn't die.

Because here is what the charter's cold arithmetic implies, and what the brown-ink note confirms: resistance to those old poisons isn't luck. It's inheritance. The same blood the gods marked, the same covenant written into the body—the body remembers what the maps were ordered to forget. A child of Thornfell's royal line wouldn't just survive the guild's curriculum. He would *excel* at it. His immunity would look like talent.

They weren't training tasters. They were running a sieve. Every graduating class was a list of candidates, ranked by how much poison they could hold.

I finished top of my class.

* * *

There is a moment, after you taste something fatal, when you know it and your body doesn't yet—the gap between knowledge and symptom where you get to pretend, briefly, that you're still the person you were before you swallowed. I lived in that gap for the rest of that night.

I kept reading. I'm telling you I kept reading the way a man keeps drinking after the doctor's verdict: precisely because stopping would make it real.

Ledger 115 covered the purge itself. I had always known the outline of it—everyone knows the outline, the way everyone knows the shape of a country that no longer appears on maps, which is to say not at all, which is to say the outline is the point. Thornfell fell in a season. Annexed, absorbed, renamed in three separate treaties because the first two names didn't take. Its king surrendered terms, not territory: the lives of his people in exchange for the disappearance of his house. No execution. No exile recorded. The royal family simply *left the record*, and the record, obediently, closed around the space where they had been.

I had assumed, in the vague way one assumes about other people's tragedies, that the family had fled south, or died quietly, or scattered into gentry under new names. Assumption is what the comfortable call ignorance.

The ledger told it differently. There was a deposition, taken from a kitchen servant of the fallen court, dated eight days after the annexation. Her testimony ran three pages. Most of it was fear and food—the last meal she cooked for the royal household, which I read with professional attention despite everything: quail in bramble glaze, a bread of hazelnut flour, honeyed pears. A farewell dinner. She had known, cooking it, that it was a farewell, though not for the reason history assumed.

*They did not flee,* her testimony said. *They went into the mountain. The old one said the Door would keep what was given to it. The young queen wept but the old one was calm as a gardener. "A country is not its land," she said, "a country is its people, and its people will live inside the Door until there is someone to open it." Then they went up through the terraces and did not come down, and the soldiers came the next day, and we told them the family was dead, and showed them the empty graves we dug, and the soldiers wrote it down because soldiers write down whatever ends their work.*

Empty graves. A door in the mountain. An old woman calm as a gardener saying *someone will open it someday.*

My grandmother tended terraces above the pass. I know, because I visited them as a child, before—before everything, before the orphanage, before the guild, before I learned to be no one. I had stood in those gardens three days ago and found them blooming untended, and found a child's carving of my own name in the wood of the trellis, and I had run from it like a sensible man.

*A country is its people, and its people will live inside the Door.*

The Ravensworths hadn't fled. They had walked into the mountain with their kingdom's soul under their arm, and sealed it in, and let themselves be erased—poisoned out of history, the phrase formed itself in my head and I hated how well it fit—so that the erasure would protect the people instead of destroying them. The charter proved the crown never believed the graves. Forty years of guild annotations hunting a remnant that the founding document knew existed. The crown's own paranoia was the only surviving map to the truth.

And the tool they built to finish the hunt—the sieve, the machine, the guild—

was me.

* * *

The lamp burned low. Green light makes everything look drowned, and I suppose I looked it, sitting on the archive floor with a dead institution's diary open across my knees.

Here is the arithmetic I did, because arithmetic is what I do instead of weeping. Twelve years of service. Hundreds of dishes tasted. Dozens of poisons absorbed, catalogued, survived. If the guild's curriculum was a sieve, then my entire career—every toast I drank for Aldric, every suspect plate I cleared, every time I nodded to the king and said *safe, Majesty*—had been a test I was taking without knowing it. And every year I survived, every year I excelled, I confirmed the one thing the guild was built to find.

Somewhere in the current guild rolls, there is a file with my name in it. Or worse—there is a file with no name, just a description: *orphan, untraceable origin, exceptional tolerance, recommend continued observation.* I was never the guild's weapon. I was its specimen. The long experiment. The mouse that learned to run the maze so well it mistook the maze for a career.

And the worst of it—not the worst, but the part that sat in my chest like a swallowed stone—was this: they had been right. The method worked. Whatever runs in my blood, whatever let me survive the ninth dish last week with nothing but a familiar warmth, it answered the poisons the way Thornfell answers bells. My immunity wasn't armor I built. It was a birthright I'd been dosing myself with, one protected meal at a time, for twelve years.

I closed the ledger. The sound was very small in the dark.

Then I did the thing I am best at, the thing twelve years of practice had perfected: I erased myself from the room. Lamp out, gap behind the shelving, cellar stair, the clickless route, out through the wine vaults where a sleeping porter dreamed of vintages he'd never afford. By the time the sky went grey over the rooftops I was three streets away, walking like a man with nowhere particular to be, which is the safest way to walk in this city.

Except.

Except that I was no longer no one. That was the change, and I felt it the way you feel a key turning somewhere deep in a lock you didn't know your chest contained. For twelve years I had been invisible by craft, and craft had held. But the god over the mountains had spoken my name into the air, and the guild's own ledger had just confirmed that my blood was a matter of state, and Lady Vessa Corrane was awake in her tower somewhere, doing exactly what I had been doing all night: reading, connecting, moving one step closer to a conclusion.

She had ordered me to investigate the shrines. She had handed the hunt to the quarry, because she didn't yet know what she was holding. Which meant I had a gift she hadn't counted on: time. A few days, perhaps. Perhaps less.

Time enough to do what, though? Run. I could run—my roads were good, my network better, and no border in the world could hold a man the crown had never successfully described. Running was the competent choice. Running was what the ledger's authors would never expect, because their note had said it themselves: *the best-hidden heir will be the one we trained ourselves.* Trained men stand and fight or trained men investigate. Trained men do not panic, and therefore trained men are predictable.

Or.

Or I could go back up the pass, to the terraced garden blooming under no one's care, and stand in front of a child's carving of my own name, and stop running from the only question that had ever actually mattered.

Who opened the Door, grandmother? And what did you leave inside it for me?

I turned toward the mountain gates as the sun came up, and behind me, in the waking city, a bell began to ring the hour—and I swear on every poison I've ever survived that it rang nine times, and stopped, and then, distinctly, rang one more.
