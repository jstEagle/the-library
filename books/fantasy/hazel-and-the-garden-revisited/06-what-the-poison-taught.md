## 6. What the Poison Taught

The first sign was the tea.

I'd been drinking poison for twelve years, which sounds like a confession, and I suppose it is. Not all at once—no one survives that—but in the small, disciplined doses a taster administers to himself. A grain of arsenic here, a sip of hemlock there, the slow education of a body taught to treat murder as weather. The guild calls it *seasoning*. The older tasters call it something else, said only after the third cup of wine: they call it *paying rent*. You borrow your life from every toxin you master, and one day, they say, the landlord comes.

I had always assumed the landlord would come as a fever, or a shaking, or my own blood deciding it had opinions. What I hadn't expected was the tea.

It was a Tuesday, because catastrophes keep merchant's hours. I was in my rooms above the chandler's shop on Candlewick Lane, doing what I did most evenings: nothing, expertly. The parcel from the Thornfell passes had gone out with the miller's cart that morning. My hands were clean. My face was forgettable. And I had put the kettle on for black leaf tea, because I am a man of simple vices.

The problem was the leaves. Someone had dusted them with monkshood—aconite, if you want the pretty name, wolfsbane if you want the honest one. Enough to stop a horse's heart. Enough that even I should have felt it, because aconite is not one of my regulars; I keep my tolerances narrow on purpose. A taster who can survive everything is a taster who gets asked to taste everything, and then he is a dead taster with excellent credentials.

I drank half the cup before I noticed the taste—or rather, before I noticed the absence of one. Aconite has a bite. This had none. And yet I knew it was there, the way you know someone is standing behind you in an empty room. I set the cup down and looked at it for a long moment, and then, because I am either very brave or very stupid and have never managed to determine which, I finished it.

Nothing happened.

Well. Not nothing. Something happened, but it happened *inward*, in a place I don't have good maps for. It felt like rain falling on ground that had been dry so long the water went straight down instead of pooling. Like the poison arrived in my blood and found no purchase—found, instead, something already waiting for it. Something that took it up the way soil takes up water, or the way a creditor takes up coin.

Something that *thanked* it.

I sat very still for a while, listening to my own heart do its patient work, and thought about the shrine in the mountains. About Brannoc answering a dying man's prayer with light that came up out of the stone like sap. About the name spoken over the peaks—a name I had spent twelve years pretending I hadn't heard, the way you pretend not to hear your own name called from a house you swore you'd never enter again.

*Ravensworth.*

I washed the cup. I dried it. I put it back on its hook, because habits are the load-bearing walls of a life like mine. And then I sat by the window until dark, watching Candlewick Lane fill and empty, and tried to think about what I was.

Here is what a taster knows that a scholar doesn't: poisons are not things. They are *arguments*. Arsenic argues that your gut should forget itself. Hemlock argues that your nerves should stop talking to each other. Aconite makes the most persuasive argument of all—that your heart should simply resign. Every poison is a sentence of rhetoric delivered to your body, and immunity is just your body learning to answer back.

For twelve years I had thought I was learning rebuttals. Guild technique. Discipline. The mundane chemistry of a man who takes his doses like medicine.

But bodies don't learn arguments this well. Bodies don't hold arsenic the way mine held it—not neutralized, not flushed, but *kept*, filed away somewhere behind my ribs like correspondence in a drawer. I had felt it for years without naming it: that faint warmth after a dose, the sense of being slightly more than I'd been that morning. I'd assumed it was health. Vigor. The smugness of a surviving organ.

It wasn't vigor. It was *accumulation*. Every poison I had ever taken was still in me—not killing me, but *living* in me, converted into something else, the way a moneylender converts debt into interest. And whatever that something else was, it had just drunk a lethal dose of aconite and said *more, please*.

There are old stories about Thornfell. Even erased countries leave crumbs, and smugglers collect crumbs the way magpies collect anything shiny. The story that mattered—the one I'd heard a hundred times around a hundred fires and never once believed—was about the covenant. How the old kings of Thornfell were not rulers but *vessels*. How the land poured itself into its royal line, and the line poured itself back, and the gods walked the terraced gardens like gardeners walking their rows, and everything lived because everything was given away. How the last queen, when the annexation came, chose erasure over slaughter—and how the land withdrew into some hidden place, waiting.

Waiting for what? I'd always assumed the story ended there because the teller got bored.

Now, sitting in the dark with a belly full of monkshood and a heart beating like a metronome in an empty church, I considered the possibility that the story ended there because it wasn't finished. That the covenant hadn't died. That it had been *banked*—held in trust, in blood, in the one vessel nobody would ever think to look at, because nobody looks at a taster. Nobody remembers a taster. That was rather the point of me.

The old magic wasn't waking up in the mountains because the erasure was failing.

It was waking up because it had somewhere to go. And I was sitting in the dark, full to the brim, a cistern that had spent twelve years filling without noticing it was a cistern.

I laughed. It came out wrong—too loud, too jagged, the laugh of a man who has just realized the joke was on him the whole time. Somewhere below, the chandler's dog started up, and I put a hand over my mouth like a child caught swearing.

Because here was the truly funny part, the part that made my eyes sting: I had built myself to be evidence-proof. No papers. No marks. A face like a closed door, a name used so rarely it had gone soft in my mouth. Twelve years of being no one, done so thoroughly that even I sometimes had to check whether anyone was home.

And all along, the evidence had been *inside me*. Literally inside me, pooled in my blood like lamp oil, waiting for a spark. I could burn every document, forge every record, bury every Ravensworth under six feet of plausible deniability—and none of it would matter, because my own heartbeat was a signed confession. Because some hedge-witch with second sight could press two fingers to my wrist and read twelve years of the king's dinner table written there in letters of arsenic and old godlight.

You cannot smuggle yourself. Believe me. I've thought about it.

* * *

I gave myself three days to be rational about it.

Day one, I told myself it was coincidence. Bad leaves, weak batch, maybe the aconite had degraded in storage. Poison does that; it's the one honest thing about the trade. I bought more tea from three different merchants and dosed myself with a thimbleful of prepared extract, alone in my rooms, watching my own face in a cracked mirror like a man daring his reflection to flinch.

My reflection didn't flinch. My reflection looked, if anything, *smug*.

Day two, I tested the theory properly, which is to say I did something no sane taster would do: I stopped holding back. In the guild's cellar school they teach you tolerance the way a swimming instructor teaches drowning—gradually, with rails. I skipped the rails. Foxglove, which slows the heart until it walks. Nightshade, which sets your eyes burning and your mind wandering off without leaving a note. I took them in doses that would have dropped a bull, and I sat through each one the way you sit through a sermon you've heard before, feeling them arrive in my blood and be *received*, absorbed, added to the account. By midnight I was pacing my room with my skin humming like a struck bell, and I understood two things with perfect clarity.

First: I was not immune. Immunity is a wall. What I had was a *harbor*—things sailed into me and anchored, and became part of the fleet.

Second: I had no idea what the harbor was *for*. And that frightened me more than the aconite had. A wall keeps things out. A harbor means something is expected to arrive.

Day three, I got sloppy, because fear makes smugglers sloppy, and fear makes tasters worse. I went out. I went to the archive.

Not the restricted stacks—I'd been there once already, and once was enough to learn that the Ravensworth file had been pulled by someone with court seal, which meant Vessa Corrane knew the name too, which meant the clock was running whether I liked it or not. No, day three I did something quieter and stupider: I went to the herb-market, to old Maren the apothecary, who has sold antidotes to every poisoner in the city for forty years and never once asked a question, because asking questions is bad for business and Maren is very fond of her business.

I asked her to read me. Just that—hold my wrist, close her eyes, tell me what she felt. She's half-hedge-witch on her mother's side; she pretends otherwise, but her fingers know things her ledger doesn't.

She held my wrist for a long time. Longer than politeness. Her eyes stayed shut and her thumb moved over my pulse in little circles, like she was smoothing wrinkles out of cloth, and her face went through several expressions I didn't care for, ending at one I cared for least of all: recognition.

"You've been *fed*," she said.

"Maren—"

"Twelve years, I'd guess. Maybe more." She opened her eyes and looked at me, and for the first time in our long acquaintance she looked *through* me instead of past me, which is a violation of the first law of the city and we both knew it. "That's not tolerance in you, love. Tolerance wears out. What you've got is—" she paused, hunting for a word among her jars, "—*standing*. Like the poisons came in and asked whose house this was, and something in you answered, and now they all answer to it."

"What answers?"

She let go of my wrist and wiped her hand on her apron, which stung more than any diagnosis. "Something old," she said. "Something that was here before the maps changed. And I'll tell you something else for free, since you're paying anyway: word's going round the market that the crown's men are buying up every drop of hellebore and aconite in the quarter. Paying triple. Asking after tasters, too." She turned back to her scales, deliberately, the way you turn your back to end a conversation. "Whatever you are, Ivo, be it somewhere else by Friday."

* * *

They followed me home from Maren's. Two of them, good ones—one ahead, one behind, dressed in the gray-brown that says *nobody* as fluently as my own wardrobe did. I spotted them within a street, which should have comforted me and didn't, because if I could spot them, they wanted spotting. Shadowing a man badly is a message. The message was: *we see you, and soon everyone will.*

I led them a merry loop through the tannery district anyway—habit, pride, call it what you like—and lost them at the rendering yards, where the smell strips the patience out of any pursuer. Then I stood in the shadow of the glue-works and had the conversation with myself that I'd been putting off for three days.

The facts, laid out like dishes on a tasting table:

One. The old gods were awake and answering prayers, and they had spoken my family's name over the mountains like a toast at a wedding.

Two. My blood was no longer merely mine. Something had been accumulating in it for twelve years—something that answered to a covenant older than the kingdom that paid my wages.

Three. The spymaster of that kingdom had pulled the Ravensworth file, and her agents were buying poison in bulk and following tasters through the streets, and one of those tasters was currently standing in the reek of the glue-works realizing that he had spent his whole career preparing himself, dish by dish, dose by dose, to be exactly the thing he was now.

A living relic. A walking reliquary. Evidence that breathed.

And the worst of it—the part that made me grip the wall—the worst of it was that I couldn't even regret the twelve years. That was the trap's true genius, if traps have geniuses. Every dose had been a choice, freely made, for sound professional reasons. Seasoning myself had kept me alive through a thousand suspicious dinners. And every dose had also been a deposit, made without my knowledge, into an account I hadn't known existed. I had been building the evidence against myself with my own hands, one careful spoonful at a time, and calling it competence.

Vessa Corrane didn't need to find documents. She needed only to find *me*, and hand me a cup.

I almost laughed again. Didn't. The dog next door had suffered enough.

Instead I did what I do. I went to ground, and I made plans, and I told myself the plan was simple: get out of the city, get over the passes, get to the shrine, and ask the one-eyed god of the small doors what, precisely, he intended to do with a cistern full of stolen poison and a man who had never asked to be one.

But plans require sleep, and sleep requires believing tomorrow will resemble today, and I had just stopped believing that. So I lay on my cot in the dark above the chandler's shop, listening to my own blood move through me like a river that remembered being a sea, and I understood—with the cold clarity that comes only after the third impossible thing—that I was no longer hiding from the past.

I was the past. Hiding from itself.

Somewhere across the city, in a tower with a good view of the gallows, Lady Vessa Corrane was reading a report about a taster who survived what no taster survives. And somewhere in the mountains, an old god was tending his rows, patient as weather, waiting for the harvest he had planted twelve years ago, one poisoned dish at a time.

In the morning, there was a note under my door.

No seal. No signature. Just eight words, in a fine clerk's hand I recognized from a dozen forged manifests:

*"His Majesty requests your presence at supper."*
