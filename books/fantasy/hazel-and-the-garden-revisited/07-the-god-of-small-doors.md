## 7. The God of Small Doors

I went back to the garden because I couldn't stop thinking about the carving.

That's the honest version. The version I told myself was that a smuggler always scouts his routes twice, and my grandmother's terraces sat above the best unwatched pass into the old Thornfell interior, and if the crown was going to come sniffing at shrines—Lady Vessa's orders, delivered with that particular smile she uses when she's handing you a rope and calling it a ladder—then I needed to know what was up there before her agents did.

Both versions were true. That's usually how it goes with me.

* * *

The climb took most of a day. The terraced garden clung to the mountainside like something grown rather than built—seven levels of stone retaining walls, each one older than the kingdom that had swallowed it, planted in my grandmother's time with hazel and gooseberry and those bitter little plums nobody outside Thornfell ever learned to love. Twelve years since anyone had tended it. Twelve years since anyone had tended *anything* on this mountain, because the annexation had emptied these valleys the way you'd empty a house before burning it.

The garden was in bloom.

Not overgrown. Not gone wild, which would have made a kind of sense—nature reclaiming, etcetera, very poetic, very sad. No. It was *tended*. The rows were straight. The hazels were pruned back to three leaders apiece, the way my grandmother did it, the way she'd taught me before I was old enough to hold anything sharper than a spoon. Weeds pulled. Paths raked. Gooseberries fat and green as coins.

And in the corner of the third terrace, where the soil went down deepest, stood a child's carving on a hazel stake: **IVO**, cut in a hand I recognized because it had been mine.

I hadn't slept much since finding it. I'm telling you that now so you understand the state of me when he arrived.

* * *

He came up the path at dusk, and here is the thing I want recorded accurately, because later accounts got it wrong: I heard him coming. A one-eyed gardener with a limp does not move silently unless silence is a thing he can switch on, and this one switched it off again deliberately, halfway across the second terrace, so I'd hear the crunch of his boots and have time to decide whether to run.

I didn't run. Partly because running from a god seemed pointless—I'd seen what Brannoc's answer to a dying man's prayer looked like, the wound closing like water smoothing over a dropped stone—and partly because I was tired. Tired in the marrow way. Twelve years of being no one wears a groove in you, and lately something kept dragging me out of that groove, and I was starting to suspect the something had a name.

"You're the gardener," I said, because he carried a hoe, and because stating the obvious has always been my best defense.

"I'm the god of small doors." He tipped an imaginary cap with the hand not holding the hoe. He was ordinary in the way that made ordinariness feel like a costume—a stocky man of indeterminate age, grey-stubbled, mud to the knees, one eye bright hazel and the other a smooth seam of skin. "Brannoc, if you want the long form. Though the long form's mostly for priests, and we're fresh out."

"Gods don't usually introduce themselves to smugglers."

"Gods don't usually get answered prayers either." He set the hoe against the terrace wall with the care of a man putting away a tool he'd owned for centuries, which, presumably, he had. "You watched me heal Tam Weller at the shrine. Don't look surprised. You watch everything. It's your whole personality."

"It's my profession."

"It's your inheritance," he said mildly, "wearing a profession as a coat. Sit down, Ivo Ravensworth. Your legs are shaking and I'd rather you hear this sitting."

I sat on the retaining wall because my legs had, in fact, been shaking for two days, and there's no dignity in pretending otherwise in front of someone who can see through coats.

* * *

"The Verdant Door," Brannoc said, without preamble, the way gods apparently do things. "You've heard the name."

"In songs. In the passes, when the smugglers drink too much. A gate out of Thornfell. The one road the annexation never closed, because no one could find it."

"No one could find it," he agreed, "because it isn't a road out. It's a door, and doors open both ways, and this one—" he tapped his chest, right over the heart, "—opens inward."

"Meaning what?"

"Meaning that on the night your country died, it didn't die." He said it flatly, no drama in it, which somehow made it worse. "Your grandfather—Aldous Ravensworth, last king of a name the maps forgot—stood where you're sitting and made a bargain. The armies at the border were three days out. He could fight, and every soul in Thornfell would be put to the sword or the soil, and the old gods with them. Or he could do what he did."

"He erased us."

"He *withdrew* us." Brannoc picked up a fallen hazelnut, turned it in his fingers. "There is a place behind the world, the way a cellar is behind a kitchen. The Door opens onto it—the surviving heart of Thornfell, its soul, its covenant, its people's truest selves. Aldous opened the Door and led his kingdom through it—not their bodies. Their bodies stayed, scattered, married into the conquering bloodlines, forgot themselves, learned new names. But the *souls*, Ivo. The souls went through the Door and waited. Every person in these mountains who carries even a drop of Thornfell blood carries a piece of themselves on the other side of that gate, locked out of their own heart, and doesn't know why they've always felt half-homesick for a country they've never seen."

The wind came up the valley and moved through the hazels, and I swear on whatever I still believed in—which at that moment was approximately nothing—that the trees leaned toward him.

"Why tell me this?"

"Because the Door is sealed. It opens from either side—but only for an heir. Only for a Ravensworth who claims the inheritance fully. Name, grief, crown, all of it, spoken aloud, meant." His single eye fixed on me. "You are the last. And you have spent twelve years making yourself impossible to find, which was wise, and twelve years drinking poison on a king's behalf, which was not, and both of those things are about to matter enormously."

"I don't want a crown."

"Good. Crowns are heavy and everyone wants to take them off your head with an axe." He smiled, and for a flash the smile was ancient, the smile of something that had watched valleys fill with glaciers and unfill again. "But you misunderstand me if you think I'm offering you a throne. I'm offering you a *door*. There's a difference, and the difference is everything."

* * *

Here's what I should have said: *No. Leave me alone. I am a taster and a smuggler and a ghost, and ghosts survive by staying ghosts.*

Here's what I actually said: "If I opened it—if such a thing were possible, which I'm not conceding—what happens?"

"To you? Unknown. Doors change the ones who walk through them." He shrugged. "To Thornfell? Everything. The souls come home. The half-people become whole. The gods wake all the way instead of in scraps and prayers. The gardens bloom. The language comes back up out of the ground like water finding its old channel." He paused. "And the crown notices. The crown has spent two hundred years being very certain that Thornfell is dead, and dead things that stand up make kings nervous, and nervous kings do what King Aldric's grandfather did to your grandfather's country."

"So opening it starts a war."

"Or ends one. Depends on who's standing where when the Door swings." He crouched, pressed his palm flat to the soil of the third terrace, and the ground under his hand *warmed*—I felt it through the stone I sat on, a pulse like a heartbeat slowed to the pace of seasons. "You've felt it already. Don't lie to a god about your own blood. When you stood among these rows, you felt the garden know you."

I had. I had felt it the way you feel a hand on the back of your neck—recognized, claimed, *remembered*. I'd told myself it was the altitude.

"What do you want from me?" I asked. "Really. Gods don't climb mountains to chat. What's the price?"

"There it is. The smuggler's question." He stood, joints cracking like green wood in a fire. "The price is that you stop being invisible. That's all. That's the whole price, and it will cost you more than any poison ever has. You've built a life out of not being seen, Ivo. Out of faces forgetting you, names sliding off you, walking through rooms like smoke. The Door doesn't open for smoke. It opens for a man standing in full light saying *here I am, this is my name, this is my grief, this is mine.*" His voice gentled, which was somehow more frightening than the rest. "You'd have to be *seen*. By the crown. By the gods. By yourself. Most men would rather die."

"Most men haven't practiced dying professionally for twelve years."

"No," he agreed. "They haven't. Which is why it might be you."

* * *

He walked me down as far as the first terrace, then stopped, because gods apparently have boundaries even when they're wearing a gardener's body, or perhaps just good manners.

"One more thing," he said. "The immunity. The poisons you've taken for the king—arsenic, nightshade, the compounds they don't even name in the guild ledger. You think your body's simply grown tough. It hasn't. It's been *transmuting*. Old magic pools in blood that's been asked to refuse death nightly for a dozen years. You are, at present, the most concentrated vessel of Thornfell's covenant alive. A living relic. The Door will taste you before it opens, and it will find its own blood in you, changed but recognizable." He looked at me with the one eye, and the kindness in it was unbearable. "It also means the protections are borrowed, boy. Nothing holds forever. But that's a conversation for another evening, and you look like a man who needs sleep and a strong drink, in whichever order gets you through the night."

"Will I see you again?"

"Every time you prune a hazel," he said, "every time you pull a weed, every time you give something living what it needs instead of what you want from it. I'm the god of small doors, Ivo. Kitchens and cellars and garden gates. I'm not hard to find. I'm just easy to overlook." He grinned suddenly, and the grin was young. "Which, I'm told, used to be your specialty too."

Then he limped off down the path, and the sound of his boots faded, and the sound of the birds came back, and the mountain was only a mountain again—or wore itself convincingly as one.

* * *

I sat on the wall until full dark, doing sums.

Here's what the sums came to. Lady Vessa Corrane had ordered me to investigate the shrines, which meant the crown was already afraid, which meant the clock was already running whether I touched the Door or not. Witnesses had heard the old gods speak the name Ravensworth over the mountains. Somewhere in the capital, clerks were pulling records, and somewhere in the restricted archives I'd already found the guild's founding charter with its ugly little mandate, and none of it was going to stay buried just because I wanted to be buried with it.

There was a way out. That was the temptation, and I'll own it plainly: for one whole hour, sitting in my grandmother's garden with the stars coming out over terraces that remembered my name, I let myself imagine it. Walking through. Leaving the king, the guild, Vessa's clever eyes, the gallows-trees, the whole poisoned apparatus of a country that had eaten mine—all of it shut behind me like a door on winter. A country on the other side where nobody had ever taught me to be no one.

An exit. After twelve years of exits, one that actually worked.

But Brannoc had said *inward*, and the longer I sat with the word, the less it sounded like escape. A door that opens inward doesn't lead away from anything. It leads *into*—into the heart, into the root-cellar of the world, into the place where the souls of half the people in these mountains were waiting for someone to turn the key. Opening it wouldn't be leaving Thornfell.

It would be becoming it. Publicly. Permanently. Under the eyes of a king who had every reason to kill the last Ravensworth and a spymaster whose smile I still couldn't read.

I came down the mountain in the dark, by the smugglers' paths, moving fast and silent, excellent at being no one, and for the first time in twelve years the excellence felt like a cage rather than a craft.

At the bottom of the pass, where the trail met the old drover's road, a rider was waiting with a lantern hooded. I knew the horse before I knew the woman—Vessa Corrane rode a grey mare with a mean mouth, and she sat her like she'd been born angry at horses specifically.

"Taster," she called softly. "You're late reporting. And you've been up the mountain six hours longer than any shrine investigation requires."

She lifted the lantern. Light climbed her face, and her eyes did the thing they always did—looked *at* me, actually at me, which almost no one did and which I had never once enjoyed until recently, and now couldn't decide if I dreaded or craved.

"Walk with me," said Lady Vessa Corrane. "Tell me what the shrines say. And Ivo—" the lantern dipped, casual, deliberate, "—do try to remember that I already know you're lying before you start."
