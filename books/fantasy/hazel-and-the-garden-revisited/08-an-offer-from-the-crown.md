## 8. An Offer from the Crown

There are three ways to tell a man he is going to die.

You can send soldiers. Soldiers are honest but loud, and loud men start riots before the paperwork is finished. You can send a letter with the royal seal on it, which is quieter, but paper can be burned, and I have known too many condemned men who never read their own death sentences because they used them to light their pipes.

Or you can invite him to dinner yourself, pour the wine with your own hands, and watch his face while you say it.

I chose the third way, which tells you something about me that I would rather it didn't.

* * *

Ivo Ravensworth came to my rooms at the hour I named, which was the first thing wrong with him. Guilty men come early, to seem eager. Innocent men come late, to seem careless. He came at exactly the ninth bell, knocked once, and stood in my doorway smelling faintly of mountain rain and horse and nothing else—no perfume, no fear-sweat, no poison, though with a taster you can never rule out the last one as a courtesy.

"Spymaster." He inclined his head the precise degree owed to my rank. Not a hair more. Twelve years in the king's service and he had never once flattered me, which I had always counted among his virtues and now had to reexamine entirely.

"Taster." I gestured him in. "Sit. Drink?"

"I'd rather not."

"Of course you wouldn't." I poured two cups anyway, because the ritual mattered. In this room, everything is ritual. The wine was unpoisoned—I had tasted it myself an hour ago, out of habit rather than suspicion—and I drank mine openly, letting him watch my throat work. *See? Nothing in it. This is not that kind of conversation.*

It was, of course, exactly that kind of conversation. Just aimed the other direction.

He sat across from me with his hands loose on his knees, and I studied him the way I study all my problems: thoroughly, and without mercy.

Here is what I saw. A man of perhaps forty, ordinary as bread, the kind of face your eye slides off like rain off slate. Grey going into brown hair, hands scarred in the pattern of a man who has handled sharp things and hot things and things that were trying to kill him professionally. No rings. No crest. No visible marks of any house or country.

And underneath all that—the thing I had spent three sleepless nights confirming—a name written in archives that were supposed to have been ash forty years ago.

*Ravensworth.*

The last royal line of Thornfell, extinct, erased, struck from every map and every ledger by order of King Aldric's grandfather, except that extinction, like most official histories, had been slightly overstated.

"You're wondering why I asked you here," I said.

"You want a second opinion on a dish," he said. "Something exotic. Something you don't trust."

"And if I told you the dish was you?"

Most men blink at that. Some laugh, badly. Ivo did neither. He looked at me with those forgettable grey eyes and said, "Then I'd ask what the seasoning is. Arsenic again? It's been a heavy arsenic season."

That is the trouble with tasters. They cannot be shocked. Shock is a flavor they lost years ago, along with, apparently, every other useful reaction.

I set the archive folio on the table between us and opened it to the page I had marked. "Do you know what this is?"

"A ledger."

"The Taster's Guild founding ledger. Year one. Do you know why the guild was founded?"

"To keep kings alive," he said. "Kings are famously bad at it."

"The guild was founded," I said, "to hunt heirs." I turned the folio toward him. "Read the second entry. Aloud, if you like. I've read it so many times I could recite it backwards, and I'd enjoy hearing someone else's voice stumble over it for once."

He didn't touch the pages. He read without touching, which is a smuggler's habit—you learn to take information without leaving fingerprints—and when he looked up, his face was still doing nothing at all, which was somehow worse than any expression.

"'Let the guild taste for the crown,'" he quoted quietly, "'and let it also taste for the blood. For the poison that killed the Thornfell line ran in their veins, and their veins run still in hidden places. Let every man who swallows for the king be also a man who watches for the king.'" He closed his eyes briefly. "Charming. We really do keep the loveliest founding documents."

"You knew."

"I suspected the guild had a second function. Every institution does. But I didn't know it was *that*." He paused. "When did you find out?"

"When did *you*?" I leaned forward. "Because here is my problem, Ravensworth. Here is the knot I've been chewing for three days. Either you are the last heir of Thornfell, hiding under a false name in the one profession designed to catch you—which would be either the cleverest or the stupidest thing I have ever heard of—or you are an innocent man whose surname happens to match a dead dynasty, in which case the old gods of Thornfell have committed a spectacular clerical error by shouting your family name over the mountains for half the country to hear."

"The gods," he said carefully, "say many things."

"They do. Lately they've been saying them *loudly*. Brannoc healed a dying man at the Stone Shrine in front of nine witnesses. The hazel shrines are blooming out of season. And three weeks ago, at the high pass, something with a voice like a landslide spoke the word *Ravensworth* to a room full of smugglers, two of whom are now in my cells telling me very interesting stories." I picked up my wine and didn't drink it. "So. Which is it? Dead dynasty's ghost, or dead dynasty's son?"

* * *

I want to record, for the sake of accuracy, that I gave him every chance.

That is not how these interviews usually go. Usually there is a script: accusation, denial, evidence, confession, cell. I have run it dozens of times and it works because people are predictable animals who lie badly under pressure. But I did not run the script. I sat in my own sitting room with the fire banked low and the good wine open, and I watched Ivo Ravensworth decide, moment by moment, how much truth he could afford to give me, and I found myself—this is the part I do not enjoy writing down—*hoping*.

Hoping for what? That's the question, isn't it. Hoping he'd deny it cleanly, so I could file my report and close the folder and go back to being a woman who hunts spies instead of kings' ghosts. Or hoping he'd confirm it, so that the strange cold thing that had been growing in my chest since I read that ledger would finally have a shape and a name.

Both hopes were treasonous. I have made a long career of knowing which of my feelings are treasonous. It doesn't stop them; it just means I file them properly.

"I'm going to tell you a story," I said, when the silence had gone on long enough to bruise. "Not an interrogation technique. A story. You'll understand why in a moment, or you won't, and either way I only have to live with myself until morning."

He said nothing. He was very good at saying nothing. I envied it.

"Twenty-two years ago, I was nineteen and newly posted to the border garrison at Cael Ford, which even then was mostly a place where the crown stored its boredom. There was a man there. A farrier. Black-haired, quick-mouthed, sang badly and constantly. His papers said he was born in Vellmark." I turned my cup slowly on its saucer. "His papers lied. I knew it within a month, because he swore in Thornfell dialect when he hammered his thumb, and he kept a sprig of rowan over his door, and once, drunk, he taught me a song about a garden kingdom that the maps forgot. I reported none of it. Do you understand? I was the crown's eyes at nineteen, sharper than most, and I looked directly at a Thornfell exile for two years and wrote down nothing."

"What happened to him?" Ivo asked. Softly. Not the softness of an interrogator drawing a subject out. The other kind.

"He was denounced. Not by me—by a quartermaster who wanted his lodging. They hanged him from the garrison gate for falsified papers. Falsified *papers*, Ravensworth. Not treason, not rebellion. Paperwork." My cup had stopped turning. I made it turn again. "I stood in the crowd and watched, and I said nothing then either, because by then saying something would only have gotten me hanged beside him, and I told myself that was reason enough. I have been telling myself it was reason enough for twenty-two years. His name was Tam. He called me Vess, which no one has called me since, because I made sure of it."

The fire ticked. Somewhere below us, the palace went on with its evening noises, guards changing, doors closing, the great machine digesting its day.

"So when the reports started coming in about answered prayers," I said, "and when I pulled the old ledgers and found the Ravensworth name breathing under forty years of ash, I told myself I was investigating a threat to the crown. And I was. I am. Don't mistake me—if you are the heir, you are the single most dangerous object in this kingdom, more dangerous than any poison, because poisons kill one king at a time and living dynasties kill kingdoms. But I also drove three hours through the pass to sit in this room with you myself instead of sending soldiers, and I need you to understand that both things are true at once. I am hunting you. And I am *Tam*. I have been Tam for twenty-two years, standing in the crowd, saying nothing."

Ivo was looking at me now—not through me, not past me, the way invisible men look at everyone, but *at* me, and I remembered too late that being seen is a vulnerability I have not trained against.

"That's why the deal," he said.

"That's why the deal." I slid a folded paper across the table. "Here are your choices, and I'll be plain, because you strike me as a man who respects plainness even when it's ugly.

"Choice one. You swear before witnesses—neutral witnesses, guild witnesses—that you are no heir of Thornfell. That the name the gods spoke is coincidence or divine mischief, and that you renounce, disclaim, and abjure any claim to any throne, crown, garden, or god outside King Aldric's lawful authority. You sign. I file it. The crown announces the pretender scare was a false alarm raised by smugglers' superstition, executions follow—smugglers', not yours—and life resumes. The gods will presumably have opinions. That will be your problem, not mine."

"And choice two?"

"Choice two is that you refuse, and I arrest you tonight, and you hang from the same gates where they hanged Tam, and the crown writes *pretender* on your corpse and *closed* on my report, and whatever is waking up in those mountains wakes up angrier." I met his eyes. "Those are the official choices. Here is the unofficial one, the one I will deny with my last breath: prove it false, Ivo. Not sign it—*prove* it. Find whatever proof exists that the Ravensworth line ended, truly ended, and bring it to me, and I will burn the pretender file with my own hands. Because I have read the archives, and the archives say the line was poisoned out, root and branch, and yet here you sit with the name and the face and—" I hesitated, then committed treason with my whole chest— "and the look of a man who has swallowed death for twelve years and digested it. If anyone could prove the line died, it should be you. So prove it. Give me a reason to save you that isn't just my own guilty heart."

He was quiet for a long time. Long enough that I counted the fire ticks. Eleven of them.

"You loved him," he said at last. "Tam."

"Yes."

"And you've carried it alone ever since."

"Spymasters carry everything alone. It's in the job description, right after 'betray your friends efficiently.'"

"No," he said, and there was something in his voice I hadn't heard before—not pity, worse than pity, *recognition*—"it isn't. I've read the job description. I've worked alongside it for twelve years. Spymasters delegate their loneliness beautifully. You chose to carry it. Same as I chose mine." He unfolded the paper I'd given him, read it once through—he reads fast, another data point I filed against my will—and set it down unsigned. "If I sign this, and I'm lying, I damn every person who's prayed to Brannoc this winter. If I sign it and I'm telling the truth, I still damn them, because the crown will take my signature as permission to go up into those mountains and put out every shrine like a candle. You know that. You wrote this offer knowing it."

"I wrote it knowing everything," I said. "That's the job."

"Then here's my counteroffer." He stood, unhurried, a man declining a death sentence the way another man declines a second helping. "Give me until the new moon. Nine days. I'll go into the passes, I'll go to the shrines, I'll find your proof one way or the other—either the grave that ends the Ravensworth line or the truth of what my grandparents actually did the night Thornfell fell. And I'll bring it back here and put it in your hands, and you can do what you like with it. Hang me with it or burn it. Fair trade: you get certainty, I get nine days of not being arrested."

"Absolutely not," I said. "You are describing a fugitive expedition. You are the most watched man in this city as of three days ago. You wouldn't reach the city gate."

"I've been smuggling things through this city for twelve years," he said, "including, on two occasions, myself. Your watchers watch for a man trying not to be seen. They don't watch for a man walking openly to buy rope." At the door he paused, hand on the latch, and for a moment the ordinariness fell off him like a dropped cloak, and I saw—clearly, terribly—what the gods must have seen. Something old standing upright in a borrowed face. "Vessa. Lady Corrane. Whichever of you is making this decision right now—thank you for Tam's story. Someone should have told it twenty-two years ago. I'm sorry it was me."

Then he was gone, and I was alone with two untouched cups of wine and a signed-nothing piece of paper, and I did the arithmetic I had been avoiding all evening.

Nine days. New moon. The deepest pass.

Where, according to every source I owned, the Verdant Door was said to stand.

I reached for the bell pull to summon my watchers and give the order to follow him—and stopped, hand in the air, hearing Tam sing badly in the rain at Cael Ford.

*Oh, Vess,* I thought. *Look what you're about to do.*

I pulled the cord anyway. But what I told my watchers, when they came, was this: "The taster goes north on guild business. Let him walk. And if anyone asks who ordered that—"

I smiled the smile that has ended better careers than mine.

"—tell them it was the gods."
