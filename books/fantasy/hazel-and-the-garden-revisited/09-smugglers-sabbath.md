## 9. Smugglers' Sabbath

There is a rule among smugglers older than any of us: you don't ask where a man sleeps, what name he was born with, or who he prays to. The first two questions get people hanged. The third, we all assumed, had no answer worth the asking—because nobody in Thornfell's shadow prayed to anything anymore. The old gods were erased along with everything else. You can't kneel to a country that isn't on the map.

I called the network together anyway.

It took six days and eleven messages, each one written in the trade cipher we use for cargo manifests, which means my summons read, officially, as an unusually large order of salted eels. Marn of the Low Ford read between the lines, as she always does. So did Hesk, who runs the river crossings and has never once told me his real name. So did Petra, who is nineteen and fearless in the way that only the young and the recently orphaned can afford to be. One by one, over three nights, they came down into the cellar beneath the tannery at Cinder Row—a cellar I have used for twelve years and which no tax assessor has ever managed to find, because the door is behind a wall of hanging hides and the hides are behind a reputation for smell so foul that even rats file complaints.

Twenty-three of them came. I had expected fifteen. That alone should have told me something.

I stood on a beer barrel at the far end of the room with a single lantern guttering behind me, and I looked out at twenty-three faces lit from below like a jury of ghouls, and I realized I had no idea how to say what I'd come to say. Ivo Ravensworth, poison-proof, person-proof, excellent at being no one—and for the first time in my career, I had to be someone, out loud, in front of witnesses.

"You're wondering about the eels," I said.

A few laughs. Good. Laughter buys a speaker ten seconds of goodwill, and I needed every second.

"There are no eels," I continued. "What there is, is this: something is waking up in the passes. The old shrines. The old names. And before anyone tells me the old gods are a story grandmothers tell to keep children off the scree slopes—I've *seen* one answer a prayer. Brannoc. He put a dying man back together in the shrine above Greywater Notch while I stood there holding the man's boots. I watched a hole in a lung close like a purse-string."

The cellar went quiet in the particular way cellars go quiet when everyone in them is deciding, simultaneously, whether to believe you.

"Brannoc," said Marn at last. She's sixty if she's a day, built like a dock bollard, with hands that could crack walnuts by accident. She said the name carefully, the way you handle a blade you haven't drawn in years. "You're sure it was Brannoc. Not some hedge-witch with good timing."

"The man's ribs knit themselves, Marn. In front of me. And then the god looked at me—" I stopped. They didn't need the rest. "Looked at me," I finished, "like he knew my grandmother."

Silence again. But it was a different silence now. Heavier. Warmer, somehow, though I couldn't have said why.

And then Hesk—the man with no name, the man who has ferried contraband across the Sorrow for nine years without ever once telling me where he wintered—Hesk took something out from under his shirt.

It was a knot. A small cord knot, green-dyed wool gone grey with age and sweat, tied in a pattern I recognized the way you recognize your own handwriting: three loops and a cross, the covenant-knot, the thing my grandmother used to tie around the handles of her garden shears. I hadn't seen one since I was seven years old. I had assumed I was the last person alive who knew what it meant.

"I pray," Hesk said simply. "Every crossing. Small prayers. Safe water, full nets, my daughter's fever breaking." He turned the knot over in his fingers. "They've been answering. Not big. Not like your miracle at the Notch. But the fever broke in one night when the physicker said three days. The net came up full when every other boat got nothing. I started marking it. Twelve years of small mercies, and I started keeping count two years ago because I thought I was going mad." He looked up at me, and his eyes in the lantern light were wet. "I wasn't mad, was I."

"No," I said, and my voice cracked on it, which embarrassed me deeply. "You weren't mad."

Petra pulled her sleeve back. There, tattooed inside her wrist in faded blue ink, was a rowan sprig—the tree they planted over graves in the old kingdom, the tree that means *return*. "My mother's work," she said. "She did it to me when I was six. Told me never to show anyone. Told me the ink would matter someday." She shrugged, nineteen years old and holding a dead woman's faith like a live coal. "Someday appears to be Tuesday."

One after another, then. Like a dam going.

Marn's family had kept a hearth-blessing, whispered over the fire every solstice, disguised as a cooking rhyme. Two brothers from the quarry roads had a stone they touched for luck that their father had sworn was just a stone, except the stone was warm in winter and cold in summer and had never once been either. The ferryman Doss crossed himself with the wrong hand—not the crown's gesture but the old one, thumb to heart, out to shoulder, down—which he'd learned from his grandmother and performed so automatically for forty years that he'd never noticed he was praying at all until that moment, standing in a tannery cellar, doing it while he talked.

Half the room. Eleven of twenty-three, counting me. Half my network carried Thornfell blood in secret, worn like smuggled goods against the skin, declared to no customs house, hidden even from each other—because the first rule of the trade is you don't ask, and so we had spent years passing contraband back and forth across the same mountains without ever once smuggling the only cargo that mattered: the truth about ourselves.

"We've been praying," Doss said slowly, working it through, "and they've been *answering*, and none of us said a word—"

"Because saying a word gets you a rope," said Marn.

"—and none of us *knew*—"

"Because knowing gets you a rope too," said Petra, "just slower."

"And the gods let us think we were alone," Hesk finished, "because alone was safe."

I climbed down off the barrel. My legs felt strange, like I'd been at sea. "No," I said. "That's not it. I don't think that's it at all." I looked around at them—at the knots and tattoos and lucky stones, at twenty-three lifetimes of careful silence. "I think they answered every prayer they were allowed to answer. Small mercies. A fever. A net. Water under a bridge. Nothing that would draw the crown's eye. They've been keeping us alive the same way we keep contraband moving—one small parcel at a time, never enough in any one place to be worth noticing." I laughed, and it came out rougher than I intended. "Gods above, they've been running us like a smuggling route. We should be flattered. It's the only trade we respect."

That got the laugh I wanted, and under the laugh, something else loosened in the room. Shoulders coming down. Hands finding shoulders. Petra reached over and gripped Hesk's forearm, the old grip, the covenant grip, and Hesk stared at her wrist and then at her face and then gripped back, and I had to turn away and pretend to check the lantern because apparently twelve years of swallowing arsenic does not prepare you for watching two people realize they're not alone.

* *

We talked until the tallow burned low.

Not strategy, at first—we were past strategy, or before it, somewhere in the territory where people simply need to say things out loud that they've carried for decades. Marn told us about the famine year, when she'd prayed to Brannoc for rain over the Low Ford and it had rained for exactly one day, just enough, and she'd told herself it was weather ever since. Doss admitted he still said the old words over drowned men, the ones that send the soul down the river-road home, and that he'd done it for crown soldiers too, because "dead is dead and the road shouldn't care whose flag they rowed under." A young quarryman named Fell—who I'd have sworn was pure lowland stock—went pale when someone mentioned the Verdant Door and said, very quietly, that his mother used to sing him a lullaby about a gate made of hawthorn, and he'd always assumed she'd made it up, and could someone please tell him why the hair on his arms was standing up.

I told them what I could. The god at the shrine. The name spoken over the mountains—Ravensworth—though I kept my voice flat on that part and watched which faces flinched. Vessa Corrane's summons, and the investigation she'd ordered, and the fact that the crown's patience was a candle burning at both ends. The garden above the pass, blooming untended, with a child's carving of my own name in the fence rail. On that one I watched every face in the room do the same arithmetic at the same time, and I decided, since we were apparently done with secrets, to finish the sum for them.

"My grandmother was Hazel Ravensworth," I said. "Her garden is awake. Her gods are awake. And the spymaster of King Aldric has asked me, personally, to find out who the old gods named over the mountains." I spread my hands. "So. You all know me as the man who moves your parcels and tastes the king's dinner. Here's the rest of it: I am quite possibly the most dangerous cargo any of you has ever carried, and I'm asking whether you'll carry me anyway."

Nobody answered right away. Then Marn stood up, joints cracking like green wood, walked over, and cuffed me on the ear hard enough to ring.

"That's for lying to us for twelve years," she said. "By omission. Which is the worst kind." Then she hugged me, which hurt considerably more, and said into my shoulder, "And that's for being ours. We knew, boy. Not your name. Not the bloodline. But we knew you were one of ours. You move through the passes like you grew out of them. Nobody learns those roads—they remember them." She held me at arm's length. "You were never invisible to us. You were family. Family just doesn't ask."

I have been poisoned, shot at, starved in a snowdrift, and once forced to eat a whole plate of the Duke of Marle's oysters as a political statement. None of it undid me like that sentence did.

* *

We worked through the night, and by dawn we had the shape of a plan—or at least the shape of a shape, which in the smuggling trade is often as far as plans get before reality weighs in.

The network would go quiet. No parcels through the high passes for a month; nothing that might lead Vessa's investigators back to us. Instead, the routes would run inward—down, under, along the ways our grandparents had used, the smugglers' roads beneath the smugglers' roads, the ones Fell's lullabies and Doss's funeral rites had kept alive in fragments. If the old kingdom's soul had withdrawn somewhere, then the paths to it were probably old too, and old paths are our native country. Every shrine still standing would be visited—not by me, not yet, but quietly, by people with knots in their pockets and rowan sprigs on their wrists, carrying offerings and questions. What do the gods want? What do they remember? Where is the Door?

"And you?" Hesk asked me, near the end, as the others filed out into the grey pre-dawn one at a time, spaced apart like proper professionals.

"I go back," I said. "To court. To Vessa. To the king's table and the arsenic and the smiling." I flexed my hands, which ached in a new way lately, deep in the bones, as if something in my blood had changed viscosity. "If the crown is going to hunt a Ravensworth heir, the heir needs to be standing next to the hunters, learning what they know before they know it."

"That's a lonely road."

"It's the only road I've ever—" I stopped. Corrected myself. "No. It isn't anymore. That's the point of all this, isn't it. I was never alone. I was only blind." I looked at him. "Twelve years I moved through these mountains thinking I was the last ghost of a dead country, and the whole time half the people I trusted were carrying the same ghost in their pockets. Praying to the same gods. Keeping the same knots." I shook my head. "All those years I thought my great talent was being forgettable. Turns out my real talent was being surrounded."

Hesk smiled and pressed his covenant-knot into my palm and closed my fingers over it. "Then take this," he said. "It's answered four hundred prayers by my count. It can spare a few more."

I walked out of Cinder Row as the sun came up over the annexed territories, over fields that belonged to a crown that had struck their true name from every map, and for the first time in twelve years I did not feel like a ghost walking through someone else's country. I felt like a courier with a full load and a clear road. Behind me, in a tannery cellar, a network of smugglers was already moving—not eels, not brandy, not silk, but prayers and names and the locations of forgotten doors, one small parcel at a time, invisible to the crown, unstoppable as weather.

I was halfway to the city gates when I saw the rider come down the pass road at a gallop, royal colors streaming, and something in my newly changed blood went cold and heavy as lead.

He was riding straight for the tannery district.
