## 10. The King's Last Supper

There is a particular silence that falls over a banquet hall when a hundred courtiers are pretending not to watch one man drink. I have heard it before. It sounds like held breath and silk and the small wet click of ambition swallowing itself.

Tonight the hall was the Amber Gallery of Ravenhold Palace, hung with banners for the Feast of Unification—Aldric's favorite holiday, the one that celebrates the annexation of Thornfell with roast boar and speeches about peace. The king sat beneath the largest banner, which depicted a crowned eagle standing on a serpent's crushed neck. Subtle as a war drum, my employer. Twelve years of tasting his food had taught me that Aldric Vane believes symbolism is something other people should be embarrassed by.

I stood at his right shoulder in my grey taster's coat, unremarkable as furniture, and I watched the cups.

That was the first wrong thing: there were too many of them. A state banquet runs on ceremony like a mill runs on water, and the ceremony says the king drinks from the gold cup, poured by the Master of Cellars, tasted by me, sealed with wax until he lifts it. Tonight someone had added a second cup—a silver one, plain, set slightly behind the gold, the way you'd set a decoy if you wanted a taster to taste the decoy.

I have been poisoned professionally for twelve years. You develop a sense for it, the way a locksmith develops a sense for locks that want picking. Someone in this room was managing me.

* * *

Let me back up to the afternoon, because the evening made more sense once I understood it had started earlier.

Vessa Corrane summoned me to her office three hours before the banquet. Her office smells of ink and cold iron; she keeps a brazier lit even in summer because she says thinking is cold work.

"You'll taste everything twice tonight," she said, without looking up from a letter she was burning. "Once for yourself, once for me."

"Twice the arsenic, twice the fun."

"Ivo." She said my name the way she always does—flatly, carefully, like a woman setting down a glass she suspects is cracked. Since the shrines, since the name *Ravensworth* came whispering out of the mountains, she has been watching me with an expression I can't file. It isn't suspicion exactly. Suspicion I know how to survive. This is closer to grief, and I don't have a procedure for grief.

"Something's moving," she said. "Three days ago a wine shipment from the southern valleys was redirected through the palace cellars. No requisition. No signature. The cellar master swears the order came from the Steward's office; the Steward's office denies it."

"Ghost orders. Charming. What was in the shipment?"

"That's what I want you to find out. Quietly. Before the feast."

So I went to the cellars, where I am known and ignored in equal measure, and I found the crates from the ghost shipment stacked behind the elderflower cordial. The bottles were good wine—too good, Thornfell red, the dark plum-heavy vintage they make above the passes, the kind that costs more than a guardsman's year of wages. And every bottle bore a fresh seal over an older seal, and under the newer wax, if you know how to look—and I know how to look—the older wax carried a stamp I hadn't seen in twelve years.

A raven in a wreath of hawthorn.

The Ravensworth mark. My family's mark, or what remained of it after the maps were burned.

Someone had resealed Thornfell wine with a dead royal house's stamp and shipped it into the king's cellars three days before the Feast of Unification. I stood in the cold dark among the barrels doing arithmetic, and every sum came out to the same answer: *someone wants a Ravensworth pretender to have poisoned the king's cup.*

And here is the thing about being the only Ravensworth left: whoever built this trap did not need me to be guilty. They needed me to exist. The name was already loose in the city—gods speaking over mountains, witnesses babbling, Lady Corrane's agents asking questions in taverns. All the trap required was poison in the cup and a name in the air, and the two would find each other like flint and steel.

I could have gone to Vessa then. I want that recorded somewhere, by someone, in whatever ledger the gods keep: *he considered telling her.* But tell her what? That I recognized the seal because it was stamped into my childhood memory along with lullabies? That I knew the wine because my grandmother served it at harvest? Every word I spoke toward the truth was a step toward the gallows, and I had spent twelve years learning to walk in the opposite direction.

So I did what I do. I became no one, and I waited, and I watched the cups.

* * *

The banquet unfolded with the usual choreography. Aldric ate heartily and talked about unity. The Chancellor gave a speech comparing the annexation to a marriage, which drew laughter from people who remembered it as a funeral. I tasted the boar, the bread, the honeyed pears, the seven wines. Nothing. Clean as snow.

Which told me the poison wasn't coming through the kitchen. It was already in the room.

Near the end of the second course, I saw it happen—or rather, I saw the absence that meant it had happened. The Master of Cellars stepped away from his station to speak with a page, no more than thirty seconds, and when he returned, the arrangement of cups at the king's place had changed. The silver cup had migrated forward. The gold cup—the one I had tasted, the one sealed with my own wax—had shifted half a hand's width back, angled so its seal faced away from the high table.

A sleight of hand performed by a man who never looked at what he was doing. Which meant he didn't know he was doing it. Which meant someone had given him an instruction he didn't recognize as an instruction—a message delivered through a chain of pages, each link innocent, the whole chain poisoned. It was beautiful work. I almost admired it.

Almost. Admiration curdles fast when you realize the trap has two doors, and both of them close on you. If the king drank from the silver cup and died, the investigation would trace the wine to the ghost shipment, the ghost shipment to the hawthorn seal, the seal to the name everyone was already whispering, and the Ravensworth pretender would hang by week's end. If the king drank from the gold cup—the clean one—then whoever ran this game would simply try again, and again, until the story stuck. Traps like this aren't set once. They're set the way a farmer sets fields: in rotation, season after season, until something comes up.

But there was a third possibility, the one that made my hands go cold under my grey sleeves. If *I* tasted the silver cup—if I publicly pronounced it clean, or publicly died on it—then the pretender's guilt was proven by his own tongue. The guild's founding charter, which I had read in the restricted archive with my heart hammering like a fist on a door, was explicit: *the taster's assent is the crown's assurance.* Whatever I blessed, the kingdom believed. Whoever wrote this play understood that better than anyone alive. They weren't just framing a pretender. They were using the office built to hunt my family as the instrument of its final destruction.

The guild founded to kill Ravensworths, killing the last Ravensworth with his own sworn testimony. Somewhere, I thought, some archivist must be weeping with pride.

* * *

The moment came between the toast to the army and the toast to the fallen. Aldric rose, gold cup in hand, and the hall rose with him, and the Master of Cellars presented the silver cup on a cushion for the ceremonial second toast—the *toast of remembrance*, they were calling it this year, remembering the "troubled years of integration." The irony was so thick you could carve it.

Protocol said the taster tastes the ceremonial cup before the king lifts it. Protocol was walking me to the scaffold with a velvet rope on either side.

I crossed the distance in six steps. I remember counting them, because counting is what I do instead of panicking. The silver cup sat on its cushion, catching the light of a hundred candles, and I lifted it, and I did the performance—I swirled, I sniffed, I touched a drop to my lip the way the guild manual prescribes—and underneath the performance I read the cup the way I read everything, with twelve years of poisons pooled in my blood like a library I never wanted.

Hemlock, refined. Not the crude field stuff that kills shepherds' cows—the distilled preparation, clear, nearly tasteless, cut with something sweet to hide the faint edge of it. A heavy dose. Not a sickening dose. A *story* dose: enough to kill a king at a triumphal feast, enough to make a nation furious, enough to hang a pretender from the tallest gallows tree in the capital with the whole city invited.

And beneath the hemlock, faint as a fingerprint, the sweetener carried a note I recognized from the archive—from the guild's oldest manuals, the ones written when the guild still wrote down what it was for. Bitter almond folded under honey. The old sign. The mark the founders used when a poisoning was *guild work*. This wasn't a courtier's plot or a foreign agent's gamble. This was my own profession, my own brothers and sisters in grey, executing the charter's last clause.

I looked up from the cup. Across the hall, Vessa was watching me. She had seen the cup change hands—or sensed it, the way she senses everything—and her face was doing something complicated, a question forming behind her eyes like weather.

I had perhaps four seconds. Here is what four seconds buys you: nothing, usually. But sometimes it buys you the chance to choose which ruin you stand in.

If I announced the poison, the investigation began, and the investigation led where they wanted it to lead, and I would hang with the evidence arranged around me like flowers at a funeral. If I pronounced the cup clean, the king might drink, and I would be the instrument of his death and the proof of the pretender's guilt in a single swallow. There was no path through this room that ended with everyone alive and me anonymous. The age of my anonymity was over; the gods had ended it in the mountains, and tonight the men with the seals and the sweetened hemlock were collecting the debt.

But there was one move left on the board. An ugly one. A taster may claim the cup—it's in the charter, an ancient provision, *the taster's body is the crown's last wall*. If I drank the whole cup, before the king, before the hall, then either I died of poison in front of five hundred witnesses—which proved the cup was poisoned, which unraveled the frame-up at its seams, because a conspirator doesn't poison his own trap—or I survived it, which only one man in the kingdom could do, which told Vessa everything she had been afraid to ask.

Either way, I stopped being invisible. That was the price of the move, and I had been dreading that price since the shrine, since the garden, since the carving of my own name in the wood. But Petra died on a mountain road buying me hours. A smuggler named Oskar healed himself with a prayer I wouldn't dare. Half my network knelt in secret to gods I was only beginning to know. Everyone around me had been paying costs in the open while I hid behind my grey coat, hoarding my invisibility like a miser hoarding coins he never intended to spend.

Twelve years I had trained my body to eat the kingdom's poisons. It seemed fitting that it should eat this one too.

"To the fallen," I said, loud enough for the nearest tables to hear, and I drank the silver cup to its bottom.

* * *

Hemlock, for those fortunate enough never to have met it, begins politely. There is a warmth, a loosening, a sensation like your bones deciding to take the evening off. Then the politeness ends. Your legs forget their argument with the floor. Your mouth goes numb in a spreading ring, and the numbness climbs, patient as frost, toward the muscles that keep your heart honest.

I set the empty cup down on the cushion. I said, "Clean," because the script demanded it, and because a lie spoken clearly is sometimes worth more than a truth shouted. Then I walked back to my place at the king's shoulder—one step, two steps, three, the hall applauding the toast, nobody yet noticing that the taster's walk had become a tightrope—and on the fourth step my left leg resigned its post.

I caught the table. The hall went quiet in that way I described at the beginning, the held-breath silence, except now it was real. Aldric turned. Vessa was already moving, already crossing the floor, and her face had shed every complication and settled on the one thing underneath, the thing she'd been carrying since the shrines.

"The taster is ill," she announced to the hall, in the voice she uses for ordering executions. "The feast concludes. Physicians to the grey chamber."

Grey chamber. Taster's infirmary. She knew the word because she'd read the charter too, and I remember thinking, as the floor tilted and the candlelight smeared sideways, *she knows, she's known, she'll burn the whole archive before she lets them hang me*—and then the frost reached my chest and thought became expensive.

They carried me out past five hundred faces. For twelve years no one had looked at me. Now every eye in the Amber Gallery was on my slack grey face, and I was too busy dying to appreciate the irony, though believe me, later, I appreciated it thoroughly.

* * *

I did not die. I want to be precise about this, because dying was genuinely on the table for most of two days. The physicians bled me, dosed me, argued over me; the frost receded by degrees, the way it came. On the second night, alone in the grey chamber with my heart beating in slow, stubborn thumps, I lay still and took inventory of what my sacrifice had purchased.

Here is the inventory. Item one: the king lived, though he'd have lived anyway—he never touched the silver cup; protocol and Vessa's speed saw to that. Item two: the frame-up proceeded anyway, exactly as designed, because the men who built it had planned for contingencies the way farmers plan for weather. By the second morning, the story in the streets was that the Ravensworth pretender had infiltrated the palace as a taster—*a taster*, the irony had grown teeth by now—and had attempted to poison the Feast of Unification, and had been foiled by divine providence and the vigilance of the crown. The fact that the taster had drunk the poison himself was being spun as panic, as accident, as the desperate gambit of a man whose nerve failed. One version had me collapsing *before* drinking, the poison discovered by my own trembling hand, my guilt confessed by my weakness.

Item three, the only item of value: Vessa knows. She sat beside my bed the second night, in the dark, not speaking for an hour, and when she finally spoke she said, "No taster survives that dose. I've read the manuals. I've read all of them." And then, quieter, in a voice I had never heard her use: "How long have you been able to do that? How long have you been *able*?"

I didn't answer. She didn't press. She rose to leave, and at the door she paused, and she said the sentence that turned my blood colder than the hemlock ever had:

"They've posted the warrant. Your face, Ivo. On every gallows tree from here to the coast. First time in twelve years anyone's bothered to look at you." She pulled her cloak close. "I'm sorry. I'm so sorry. I tried to burn it slower than this."

Then she was gone, and I lay in the dark of the grey chamber, listening to my borrowed heart work, understanding at last that the invisibility I had mourned was never mine to keep—that the whole country had been closing its eyes around me for twelve years, and now, having finally opened them, it had opened them onto a warrant.

Somewhere out there, Brannoc the one-eyed gardener was surely laughing. The Door opens inward, he'd told me. I was beginning to understand what he meant. Everything I had done to stay hidden had been a door held shut from the inside, and tonight, with a cup of sweetened hemlock and a lie spoken clearly, I had felt the hinges begin to move.

By morning, I would need to be gone. The grey chamber had bars on the windows for exactly one kind of guest, and I had just become him.
