## 11. Wanted: One Ghost

I have been looked at exactly four times in my life, and I can name them the way other men name their children.

The first was a midwife, presumably, though I don't remember it and she's certainly dead by now. The second was an old woman in the Saltmarket who caught me lifting bread off her cart when I was nine; she looked at me for three full seconds and then laughed and told me to take the cheese too, since I clearly had the talent for it. The third was a dying man in a shrine who should not have been able to stand, let alone walk, let alone thank me with both his eyes open.

And now there is a fourth.

It is nailed to every gallows tree between here and the coast, printed four hundred times on good rag paper, and it is my face.

* * *

I found the first one outside Millbrook, pinned to the post of the well where every traveler stops, because whoever designed the notice understood people better than I ever have. A crowd had gathered around it—not reading so much as *looking*, the way you look at a fire or a hanging. I joined them, because hiding from your own face is a skill nobody teaches and mine was rusty anyway.

The likeness was good. That was the first insult. Twelve years of being forgettable, of cultivating a face like weather, of practicing the small slouch and the unfocused gaze that makes eyes slide off you—and some court limner had sat down with a description from Lady Vessa Corrane's files and produced *me*. Not a general dark-haired man of medium build. Me, with the scar through my left eyebrow I got falling out of a smuggler's loft at nineteen, with the particular set of my mouth that my grandmother used to say came down the family line along with the stubbornness.

Underneath, in official black-letter:

*WANTED FOR HIGH TREASON AND THE POISONING OF HIS MAJESTY'S CUP. THE MAN KNOWN AS "IVO" IS IN TRUTH IVO RAVENSWORTH, FALSE HEIR OF THORNFELL, LAST OF THE ERASED LINE. REWARD: FIVE HUNDRED CROWNS, AND A PARDON FOR ANY CRIME SAVE MURDER.*

Five hundred crowns. I've moved parcels worth more than that through worse places. But it wasn't the number that made my hands go cold against the well's stone lip. It was the phrase *last of the erased line*, sitting there in government ink, as if the crown were admitting—printing, distributing, *paying for*—the thing it had spent sixty years denying: that Thornfell had a royal line at all, and that someone had gone to great trouble to erase it.

You cannot hunt a ghost without confirming ghosts exist. Somewhere in the Chancellery, some clerk had signed off on that wording without seeing what he'd done. Or maybe someone had seen it perfectly. Maybe that was the point: kill the pretender, and the pretender's existence proves nothing once he's dead.

Beside me, a farmer squinted at the notice, then at the crowd, then at the road.

"They say he drank poison right out of the king's cup," the farmer said. "In front of two hundred witnesses. Saved the old man's life."

"That's what they say," said a drover.

"Then why's he wanted?"

"Because he's a Ravensworth," said a third voice, an old woman with a market basket, and she said the name the way you'd say a prayer you weren't sure was still legal. "That's reason enough. Always has been."

Nobody looked at me. I was a thin, tired man in a drover's coat, and my face—the face on the paper, the face that had finally been *seen*—was apparently still only visible when printed. There's a joke in that. I didn't laugh. I was too busy noticing that the old woman had said the name like it belonged to something, and that half the crowd had nodded, and that the king's men had nailed up five hundred copies of a man and accidentally nailed up five hundred copies of a *country*.

I bought an apple from the well-side stall with my hood up, paid double, and walked out of Millbrook a wanted man whom no one wanted to look at yet. Give it a day. Give it a day and the likeness would migrate from paper into memory, and then every eye on the road would be a knife.

I'd spent twelve years learning how to be no one. It turns out that unlearning it under these conditions is much harder, because the crown had done the one thing I never could: it had made people look.

* * *

Vessa Corrane read the notice forty times before she burned it, and she read it the way she read everything—like an opponent's hand of cards.

She stood in her office above the Spymaster's Court with the shutters closed and the paper held close to the lamp, and she hated it, and she could not have said, if a knife had been at her throat, exactly which part she hated most.

The likeness, perhaps. She had commissioned it herself—or rather, she had authorized the warrant and the Chancellery's limner had done the rest, working from her agents' descriptions, and the result was so painfully *him* that she'd felt something crack behind her sternum when the proofs came back. Twelve years she had known that face. Twelve years of watching it move through her reports like a fish through water—*the taster called Ivo, seen at the Thornfell shrines, seen on the mountain roads, seen speaking with Brannoc's pilgrims*—and she had filed those reports and said nothing, because saying something would have meant explaining why she noticed him at all.

Now the whole kingdom would see the face. Now every gallows tree from here to the sea wore him like a trophy. And the worst of it, the part that made her hold the paper closer to the flame and watch the edges brown, was that the notice was *competent*. The reward was calibrated high enough to tempt but not so high as to draw fools. The pardon-for-any-crime clause would turn half the underworld into informants inside a week. She had written warrants like this herself, dozens of them, and she knew their grammar the way a hangman knows rope.

*Last of the erased line.* She'd argued for cutting that phrase. The Chancellery overruled her—too late, the print run was done—and she had watched them do, in a single sentence, what sixty years of annexation policy forbade: they had put Thornfell's royal house back into the record. Every copy was evidence. Every copy was a small resurrection. She'd tried again to have the run recalled and been told the notices were already on the trees, and the Chancellor had smiled his wet smile and said, *Let the countryside read it, Corrane. Fear is educational.*

Fear was educational. So was grief, though no one at court taught it.

There was one more copy in her office besides the one in her hand—a proof sheet, the limner's first pull, delivered to her desk because the warrant bore her seal. She took it out of the drawer where she'd hidden it and looked at it for a long time.

He looked tired in it. That was the thing the Chancellery hadn't understood, the thing the limner had captured without meaning to: the face wasn't the face of a traitor. It was the face of a man who had spent his whole life making sure no one would ever have cause to study it, and who was about to be studied by everyone.

Vessa thought of a night eleven years ago, a tavern in the annexed quarter, a young exile named Tam who had talked about terraced gardens and a god with one eye until the king's soldiers came, and how she had learned afterward what happened to men who were *seen* in that quarter. She had sworn an oath over Tam's grave that she would never again be the person who pointed. And here was her seal, at the bottom of a death sentence, doing precisely that.

She fed the proof sheet to the lamp. It went up fast, rag paper always does, and she held it until the flame touched her fingers because letting go early felt like a kind of cowardice. Then she opened the shutter and let the ash go out over the city, where it mixed with chimney smoke and became indistinguishable, anonymous, free.

One copy burned. Four hundred nailed to trees. She did the arithmetic and found it obscene, and then she sat down at her desk and began, with steady hands, to write false reports about sightings in the far south—because if she could not stop the hunt, she could at least aim it badly.

* * *

By the third day, the notices had changed the texture of the roads.

I traveled only at night, on smugglers' paths, moving from waystation to waystation through the network that had, a week ago, knelt around me in a cellar and prayed. The network held. That was the miracle I kept waiting to collapse: every safehouse opened, every lookout whistled the old warning, every cache was stocked. Half my smugglers carried Thornfell blood in them somewhere, and now the blood knew it was being hunted, and blood is loyal to itself in ways oaths never manage.

But the roads themselves had turned strange. In the towns, people gathered at the gallows trees and read my face aloud to each other. Children played a game they called *Ravensworth*, one of them running while the rest pointed and shouted. Ballads were already sprouting—I heard a carter singing one, tuneless and new, about the taster who drank death for the king and was thanked with a rope. The ballad had the facts wrong in every particular except the ones that mattered.

"You understand what's happening," Petra's sister Wren said to me. She was sixteen, sharp-faced, and had appointed herself my guard after Petra died, over my objections and everyone else's amusement. She walked beside me now on the high path above the river, hood up, matching my pace. "They made you a story. Stories are harder to kill than men."

"I don't want to be a story."

"No," she said. "You want to be nobody. Well, that ship's burned, Ivo. They looked at you. You can't unlook."

We came over a rise and stopped, both of us, without agreeing to. Below us, at the crossroads by the ferry landing, stood a gallows tree—old oak, the kind they'd hanged smugglers from for generations—and it was papered with my face, a dozen copies layered over each other like scales. And beneath it, in the mud, someone had planted a sprig of hawthorn. It was blooming. Out of season, out of soil, out of all sense—but I had stopped expecting sense from hawthorn some weeks ago.

An old man was standing under the tree. He pulled one of the notices down, folded it with great care, and put it inside his coat, over his heart. Then he spat on the empty nail, looked up at the tree, and said, conversationally, to no one: "Not today."

Wren's hand found my sleeve. "Did you see—"

"I saw."

"He's keeping it. They're *keeping* them." Her voice cracked in the middle, sixteen years old and furious at everything. "You're on every tree in the country and they're taking you home like relics."

I stood on the ridge with the wind coming off the water and understood, finally and completely, the shape of the joke the gods had built my life into. For twelve years I had starved myself of being seen, believing invisibility was safety, and it had been—for me. But a man no one sees can't be followed, can't be loved, can't be missed. I had made myself unmissable in the most literal sense. And now the crown had torn the veil off, and the looking that came pouring in wasn't only hunger for five hundred crowns. Under the trees, in the markets, in the mouths of carters and children and old women with baskets, my face was becoming something else. A flag. A name spoken like a prayer. A country wearing a man's features because it had no borders left to wear.

Visibility was supposed to be my death sentence. It was also, apparently, my coronation, and nobody had asked me which I preferred.

"Not today," the old man had said. I clung to that. Not today. But the army was sealing the passes—I'd had that report at dawn—and King Aldric himself was rumored to be marching north, because a pretender who survives poison deserves a king's personal attention, and kings love nothing so much as a spectacle.

"We keep moving," I said to Wren. "North. Through the deep passes."

"To the Door?"

"To the Door."

She nodded and started down toward the ferry landing, straight past the gallows tree, because Wren feared nothing except being bored. I followed. As we passed under the oak, I reached up—one motion, quick, foolish—and pulled down a single copy of my own face.

I looked at it properly, for the first time. The man in the ink looked back, tired and marked and unmistakably, irreversibly *somebody*.

Then I folded it with great care, the way the old man had, and put it inside my coat, over my heart, where a country goes when it has nowhere else to live.

Behind us, at the crossroads, the hawthorn bloomed. And far to the north, under the mountains, something ancient felt four hundred pieces of paper flutter on four hundred trees, and began—slowly, patiently, the way roots work stone—to pull.
