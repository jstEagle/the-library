## 12. The Road Under the Road

There is a road under the road, and it has no name, because naming things is how the crown finds them.

I learned it the way I learned everything worth knowing: from a woman who smelled of tallow and pipe smoke, in a cellar under a tannery in Lowmarsh, when I was nineteen and still believed that being forgettable was a gift rather than a wound. She drew the route on the floor with her boot heel—through the culvert, down the old mine adit, along the drowned gallery where the water comes to your knees and the ceiling comes to your soul—and she said, *This was a Thornfell pilgrim road once. Before your king's grandfather poured his name over ours like ash over a fire.*

I have run that road perhaps forty times since. Parcels. Letters. Once, a caged falcon that bit me the entire way and which I chose not to resent, because at least the falcon had the honesty of its teeth.

Tonight I ran it for myself.

* * *

They came for me at dusk on the third day after the wanted-notices went up, which I will say for the crown: their paperwork is efficient even when their theology isn't. Six riders to the inn at Harrow's Ford where I'd been foolish enough to sleep indoors like a man with nothing to hide. I was out the back window before their knock finished echoing, and Sef—the innkeeper, another man whose face the world declines to hold—was already saddling my horse and lying to the door with the serene fluency of a professional.

"You went north," he told them loudly, as I went south.

The road under the road began at a culvert two miles out, where Petra was waiting with a lantern hooded to a coin's width of light and an expression of tremendous disapproval.

"You slept *indoors*," she said.

"I was tired."

"You're the most wanted man in three counties and you were *tired*." She shoved the lantern into my hands. "Sef sent word ahead. We're moving you tonight, all the way to the deep galleries, before the hunters get organized. Brannoc's teeth, Ivo, sometimes I think you want to be caught."

I did not tell her that there are worse fates than being caught, and one of them is being followed by people willing to die for you. I did not tell her that every mile of this road now had a price tag attached to it that would be paid by someone who wasn't me. I said, "Lead on, then," because Ivo Ravensworth—or whatever name I was wearing that week—has always been excellent at saying the small true thing instead of the large unbearable one.

Petra was nineteen. I want that recorded somewhere other than my own skull, because by morning there was nowhere else to record it. Nineteen, with a laugh like a cart going over cobbles and a knife she'd named after her grandmother, and she walked point down the pilgrim road like she'd been born in it, which in a sense she had. Her family had carried goods through the Thornfell depths for four generations. Smuggling, in the passes, is less a crime than a memory with cargo.

* * *

The road under the road does not behave like a road. It behaves like a throat.

We went down through the mine adit at Cindershaw, past the rusted winch houses, into the drowned gallery where the water rose to our thighs and cold enough to make thought a luxury. The old pilgrims had cut niches into the rock every hundred paces, and in each niche someone had recently set a candle—recently, because the wax was soft, because the whole mountain was waking up and lighting itself like a house expecting guests.

"Everyone prays down here now," Petra said, her voice bouncing strange off the wet stone. "Since the shrines started answering. Mam says the god of the small doors heard her about the rent."

"The god of the small doors heard her about the *rent*."

"He's a practical god." She glanced back at me, and in the candlelight her face was young and certain and entirely unafraid. "He'll hear you too, you know. Whatever it is you're carrying."

What I was carrying was twelve years of arsenic and a name that had gotten six people's faces onto gallows trees alongside mine. What I was carrying was the knowledge that the Taster's Guild ledger had been written specifically to find me, and that Lady Vessa Corrane had read it, and that she had burned one copy of my death warrant instead of delivering it, and that I could not decide whether that made her my enemy or something far more dangerous.

"I'm not carrying anything," I said.

"Liar," said Petra, cheerfully, and splashed onward.

* * *

We surfaced near dawn in the hollow called the Old Man's Knees—a fold of hillside above the pass where the hawthorn grows in a ring and the wind makes a sound like slow breathing. There were others waiting. That was the part I hadn't expected, though I should have. Half my network had turned out overnight: Dree with her mule train, the Ferrow brothers, old Hask who claimed to be seventy and moved like forty. They'd brought food, rope, and a map drawn on linen showing which passes the royal hunters had sealed and which they merely thought they had.

"They're using dogs now," Hask said. "Not just trackers. Dogs that go quiet near you, then start howling. The handlers think it's witch-sign."

"It probably is," I said. "Everything about me is probably witch-sign at this point."

Dree looked up from the map. "Is it true? What the notices say? That you're—" She stopped herself, but everyone heard the word anyway. *Ravensworth.* It sat in the middle of our circle like a lit coal nobody wanted to touch, except that everybody wanted to touch it.

"I don't know what I am," I said, which was honest, and insufficient, and we all knew both things.

"You're the one the gods spoke over the mountains," said one of the Ferrow brothers quietly. "My gran heard it. Said the wind said it twice."

"And what do you want me to be?"

Nobody answered that directly, which was answer enough. They wanted me to be the thing the notices said, because if the notices were right, then the erasure was wrong, and if the erasure was wrong, then their grandmothers' prayers hadn't been falling on nothing for sixty years. I understood it completely. I also understood that wanting does not care what it costs the wanted.

"We move at first light," I said. "Deep route, all the way to the Verdant Door country. Small party only. Everyone else scatters and runs clean cargo until this blows over."

"It won't blow over," said Dree, not unkindly.

"No," I agreed. "But you can pretend for a while. Pretending is a legitimate trade in these parts."

* * *

The small party was five: me, Petra, Hask, and the Ferrow brothers. We went down again that morning, deeper than the drowned gallery, into the dry galleries where the air tastes of iron and old fires, where the Thornfell miners of the erased kingdom had dug so deep they said you could hear the mountain's heart. I had always taken that for smugglers' poetry. Down there, with the candles burning in their niches and the dark pressing close as wool, I began to suspect it was inventory.

The hunters found us on the second day.

Not by the dogs—by lamplight, far behind us in the galleries, too many lamps moving in formation. Soldiers don't wander mines in formation. Someone had talked, or someone had been broken, or the crown had simply decided to spend men rather than patience. Either way, by the time we heard the boots, the gallery behind us belonged to the army and the gallery ahead was a question mark.

"There's a shaft," Petra said. "Old ventilation rise, two miles on. Comes up in the high pass above the Door country. It's tight, but we fit."

"Tight how?" asked Ferrow.

"Tight like apologize-to-your-ribs tight."

We ran. I will not romanticize it: grown men and one girl stumbling through the dark with soldiers' lamps blooming behind them like a second dawn, breath sawing, boots slipping on rail-slick stone. Hask went down once and I hauled him up and felt, in the grip of his hand on my arm, the entire weight of what I had become—not a man anymore but a reason, a cause wearing my face, and causes are heavy in ways bodies aren't built for.

The soldiers gained. Of course they gained. Soldiers run in relays; fugitives run on terror, and terror has a shorter stride than discipline.

At the shaft, the arithmetic presented itself with terrible clarity. The rise was narrow. One climber at a time, and slowly, and the sound of climbing carries. Behind us, maybe four hundred paces, the lamps swung closer.

"I'll hold the junction," Petra said.

No one had asked her to. No one had discussed it. She said it the way you'd say *I'll get the door*, ordinary as bread, and she was already uncoiling her grandmother's knife and stepping back toward the corner where the galleries met.

"Petra—"

"Somebody holds the junction or nobody climbs." She grinned at me, that cart-over-cobbles grin, and for one obscene moment she looked exactly like every younger sister in every village I'd ever passed through invisible. "Besides. You're the one the wind said twice. I'm just the girl with the knife."

"Petra." My voice did something I didn't authorize. "This isn't—"

"It's exactly what it is." And then, softer, in the old tongue I barely knew and she clearly knew whole: words I didn't understand, except the last one, which needed no translation, because it was my name. Not Ivo. The other one. *Ravensworth,* offered like a blessing, like a key, like a debt.

Then she stepped around the corner, and the dark took her, and I climbed.

* * *

I will tell you what I heard, because I heard all of it, because the shaft carried sound the way a cupped hand carries water, and there was no version of me strong enough to stop my ears.

She didn't fight like a soldier. Soldiers fight to protocols. Petra fought like someone defending a kitchen—short, vicious, inventive, swearing in two languages. I heard steel. I heard a man cry out. I heard her laugh once, that laugh, bright as breaking crockery, and I heard Hask below me whispering prayers to Brannoc at a speed suggesting he'd skipped several centuries of formal devotion and gone straight to bargaining.

Then I heard the shot.

Muskets are loud in mines. The sound came up the shaft and hit me between the shoulders and I kept climbing because stopping was impossible, because climbing was the last instruction she'd given, because grief and obedience had temporarily merged into a single motion of the arms.

The second shot I didn't hear. I've decided I didn't. Whether that's mercy or cowardice depends on the day, and I have not yet had a good day about it.

* * *

We came out of the ventilation rise into evening light and thin mountain air, all four of us, alive, whole, and less. Below us the high pass opened toward the Door country, hawthorn-dark, beautiful, indifferent. Somewhere beneath our feet a junction stood empty and a knife named after a grandmother lay on the stone next to a boy's—no. Next to a young woman's whole unfinished life.

Hask sat down on the rocks and put his face in his hands. The Ferrow brothers stood very straight, the way men stand when standing straight is all the structure left. And I stood at the lip of the shaft looking back the way we'd come, doing the arithmetic I'd been dodging since the culvert at Harrow's Ford.

One. That was the cost so far, of being the heir people choose. One girl of nineteen who had prayed to a practical god about the rent and gotten answered, and who had spent her answer on me.

"This is what it costs," I said aloud, to no one, to the mountain, to whatever was listening. "Do you understand that? This is what it *costs*."

The wind came up the pass and moved through the hawthorn, and it sounded—God forgive me—it sounded like breathing. Like something vast and patient inhaling my grief and exhaling nothing useful at all.

"She chose," said Hask, without lifting his head. "You don't get to steal that from her by making it yours. She chose. That's the whole of it and the mercy of it."

I knew he was right. Knowing it changed nothing. Grief is not a door you walk through; it's a room you learn to furnish.

We buried what we could bring of her above the tree line, under a cairn that the Ferrows built with a mason's care, and I marked it the only way I could think of: I carved her name into the stone with her own knife, which one of the brothers had carried out, God knows how, God knows why, except that of course I know why. You carry the dead's tools. That's half of what inheritance means.

Then I looked east, toward the Verdant Door, sealed and waiting in its deepest pass, and I understood something with the cold clarity the poison had taught me long ago:

Every mile between here and that arch would be paid for the same way unless I stopped paying in other people.

The gods wanted an heir. The crown wanted a corpse. My people—*my* people, and when had I started saying that?—wanted a miracle.

And I wanted, more than any of it, to deserve them. Which meant walking into the Door country not as a ghost dragging a body count behind him, but as a man willing to be seen—even if what they saw, when the light finally reached my face, was a killer's arithmetic and a taster's scars and a name that had cost a girl with a cart-laugh everything she had.

Below us, far down the valley, torches were kindling. Not a patrol this time. A column. The crown had stopped sending hunters and started sending an army, and at its head, I would learn soon enough, rode King Aldric himself, come to end the pretender personally.

The wind said my name twice.

This time, I answered.
