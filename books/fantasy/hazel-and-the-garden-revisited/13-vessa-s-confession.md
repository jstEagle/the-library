## 13. Vessa's Confession

I have arrested forty-one people in my career as spymaster to the crown of Aldric's realm, and I could tell you the story of every one of them—the way a man's voice climbs half an octave when he lies about his brother, the way a woman folds her hands when she's decided to die rather than talk. I keep the stories like coins in a drawer. They are what I am paid in.

Ivo Ravensworth would have been the forty-second.

Instead I stood at the treeline above the shrine of Brannoc in the grey hour before dawn, alone, having dismissed my escort three miles back with a lie so smooth I almost believed it myself, and asked myself the question I had been avoiding for eleven years:

*What exactly do you think you're doing, Vessa?*

The honest answer was: losing.

* * *

Let me tell you how it started, because gods apparently make you tell the truth whether you want to or not, and I'd rather do it on my own schedule, in my own words, like a professional.

Eleven years ago I was not Lady Corrane, spymaster. I was a junior analyst with ink-stained cuffs and a talent for reading other people's letters, and there was a smuggler who ran the Thornfell passes—a boy, really, nineteen maybe twenty, with a face like a door left ajar. You looked at him and your attention slid off. I only caught him because I wasn't looking for him; I was looking for his cargo, and he stepped into my lantern light to move a crate, and for one full second I *saw* him.

He bought me a cup of cider at the tollhouse afterward, which sounds absurd—me, crown intelligence, drinking cider with a smuggler—but that was the kind of man he was even then. He said, "You look tired," and I was, and no one had said it to me in four years of service, and I sat down on the crate he'd been moving and drank terrible cider and told him things I had no business telling anyone.

His name then was Fen. It was also Marek, and Tomlin, and once, memorably, Duchess, because the toll-keeper's wife refused to call him anything else. He wore names like coats. He took them off and put them on and never let one fit close enough to be recognized by.

I should have reported him. I wrote the report. I still remember the weight of the pen. And then I burned it over my candle and watched eleven years of my integrity curl up like a moth's wing, and I told myself it was tradecraft: an unreported asset is more useful than a hanged one.

That is the lie I lived in for a decade. It's a comfortable house, that lie. Good hearth.

* * *

The shrine announced itself the way old shrines do—not with grandeur but with a wrongness in the ordinary. The birds went quiet in a band thirty yards wide, like a seam of silence stitched through the forest. The moss on the boundary stones grew in spirals instead of patches. And the air smelled green. Not spring-green, not grass-green, but the deep cellar-green of a garden that has been tended for two hundred years by hands that loved it.

I had been to this shrine twice before, on routine sweeps. Both times it had been a ruin: fallen lintel, choked well, the god's niche empty except for pigeon filth and a farmer's abandoned boot. Tonight the lintel stood upright. The well water shone black and clean. And in the niche, where the statue of Brannoc had worn down to a suggestion, firelight moved.

He was awake. The old god of small doors and garden walls, keeper of thresholds, the one they'd struck from the liturgy along with everything else Thornfell. Awake, and lit from inside like a lamp behind horn.

And somewhere down in that bowl of quiet, Ivo Ravensworth was praying.

I know because I heard it. Not the words—the words were too low, too worn smooth by use, the way a prayer gets when it's been said ten thousand times in private. But I heard the shape of it, the rhythm, and underneath the rhythm something else: the mountain answering. A sound like a held breath being released after twelve years.

I drew my knife. I want that on the record, whoever's record this ends up being. I came here to arrest him. I had the warrant drafted in my coat, the escort dismissed only because I intended to bring him in quietly, without spectacle, without the mob that had gathered under every gallows tree since his face went up on them. I told myself I was protecting him from the crowd the way you protect evidence—from contamination.

My hand shook on the knife grip. Twelve years of steady hands, and they chose that moment.

* * *

"You're late," said a voice behind me, and I did not scream, which I count among my life's modest triumphs, though my heart attempted to exit through my ribs.

He was sitting on the boundary stone not six feet away, wrapped in a shepherd's cloak, looking thinner than the wanted posters made him. Poison does that. I'd read the banquet reports—I wrote half of them—and I knew what he'd drunk for his king, and I knew what the king's gratitude had amounted to: a warrant.

"You knew I was coming," I said.

"I've known you were coming for eleven years." He tilted his head. "Not tonight specifically. But eventually. You always find me, Vessa. That's the arrangement we never spoke aloud."

"The arrangement," I said, "was that I didn't look."

"And now you're looking." He said it without accusation, which was worse. Accusation I could fight. "Come down, then. If you're going to arrest me, arrest me where the god can watch. He likes witnesses. Says people tell the truth in front of shrines whether they mean to or not."

"That's superstition."

"Is it?" He smiled, and it was the smile from the tollhouse, older now, cracked like glaze. "Then you've got nothing to worry about."

I followed him down. That is the whole confession in miniature, isn't it? I followed him down.

* * *

Brannoc looked like a gardener. That was my first stupid thought, standing in the shrine while torchlight I couldn't locate moved across a face that was mostly weathered stone and partly something else—one eye bright and brown and human, the other a knot of wood grain, closed or absent, I never determined which. He had dirt under his fingernails. Gods should not have dirt under their fingernails. It made him infinitely more frightening than any marble colossus.

"Lady Corrane," the god said, and his voice was the sound a gate makes when it hasn't opened in years and then does. "Spymaster. Taster's guild liaison. Burner of reports."

"I don't know what you're—"

"You burned the report." He said it gently, the way you'd correct a child's arithmetic. "Eleven years ago. Candle, third window, east wing. You told yourself it was tradecraft." The bright eye regarded me with what I can only describe as patience, the terrible patience of growing things. "I was asleep then. But the land remembers what happens on it, even when its god sleeps. Especially then."

I looked at Ivo. He was watching me with an expression I'd spent a decade making sure I'd never see on anyone's face: recognition. Being known. It is the most violent thing that can happen to a person whose entire profession is knowing others.

"You knew," I said to him. "How long?"

"How long have you known?" he returned.

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one either of us has." He sat down on the shrine steps like a man settling in for a long winter evening. "Sit, Vessa. Or run. Those are your options. The knife is theater—we both know you've had a dozen chances to use it and used none of them."

I sat. God help me, I sat, in a waking god's shrine, beside the most wanted man in the kingdom, and the god leaned against his own niche like a farmer leaning on a fence, and waited.

"He forces truth," Ivo said quietly. "It's not cruelty. It's how he tends. A garden can't grow around rot. You have to turn the soil."

"Turn the soil," I repeated. "That's what you call this."

"What would you call it?"

I opened my mouth to say *treason*, and what came out—and I felt it come out, felt it leave me like a tooth leaving the gum, blood and all—was:

"Fen."

The name hung in the shrine air. His face changed. Underneath the heir, underneath the pretender, underneath the ghost on the gallows posters, the boy with the door-ajar face looked out at me for the first time in eleven years.

"You kept it," he said.

"I keep everything. It's the job." My voice was doing something I despised. Fraying. "I kept the report too. Not the one I burned. The second one. The real one. I wrote it a week later, after I'd traced the cargo manifests back four years, after I'd matched the route timings to the old Thornfell passes, after—" I stopped. The god's eye was warm on me and the words were coming up like water into a well shaft, unstoppable, pressurized by eleven years. "After I worked out who you were."

The silence had texture. I sat in it.

"Say it," Brannoc said softly. "Names are doors, lady. Yours has been locked a long time."

"Ivo Ravensworth," I said. "Last heir of Thornfell. I have known since the winter of the year we met. Eleven years. I falsified the guild census entry that gave you taster's immunity—I told them your bloodline markers were common highland stock. I buried three separate inquiries that touched your routes. When Corrane—that is, when I was promoted and given the archives, I pulled the Ravensworth file and replaced it with a forgery, and I have maintained that forgery through two audits." I was shaking now, properly shaking, hands and jaw and the breath in my throat. "I protected you because I loved you, and because I hanged your cousin's memory with my first report, and because every year it became harder to confess and easier to continue, and because—" my voice broke and I hated it and kept going, because the god would not let me stop, "—because you were the only person who ever saw me, and I could not bear to lose the one thing in my life that was true."

There. It was out. Eleven years of careful masonry, and the god had simply turned the soil, and there it all was, roots and worms and all, in the open air.

Ivo was staring at me. Not with horror. That was the unbearable part. With something like grief, and something like relief, and underneath both, terribly, hope.

"You knew," he said again, barely audible. "All this time. While I spent twelve years learning to be no one. While I drank poison for a king who erased my country. While Petra—" His voice caught on her name and I flinched. Petra, dead on the road under the road, covering his escape. I had read that report too. I had filed it. "You knew, and you let me think I was alone."

"You were safer not knowing. Every person who knows is a person who can be broken."

"Don't you dare dress it as protection." He was on his feet now, and the god did not move to calm him, because apparently gods let gardens grow through storms too. "I drank arsenic for Aldric. I nearly died last month for him. And you sat in your tower with my name in a forged file and let me walk into it."

"Yes," I said. Because the god was listening, and lying had become physically impossible, and because it was true. "Yes. I did. And I would tell you it was duty, but you'd know I was lying, and so would he." I nodded at Brannoc. "It was cowardice. It was love. It was both, and they were the same cowardice, and I am done pretending otherwise."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about fracture: it doesn't feel like breaking. It feels like finally exhaling. For eleven years I had held two truths in one body—*I serve the crown* and *I love the enemy*—and the holding had cost me sleep, appetite, the ability to look in mirrors longer than necessary. Now the two truths lay side by side in the shrine dust, irreconcilable, undeniable, and I breathed out for the first time since a candle flame ate a report in the east wing.

"I can't arrest you," I said. It came out flat, factual, the tone I used for casualty figures. "I came to arrest you. I had the warrant. I dismissed my escort with a lie. And I can't do it. So you should know what that means. When I don't report in by noon, Corvane will assume I've turned. He'll seal the passes—he's already sealing the passes—and my name goes on the next set of posters under yours. I'm not asking pity. I'm telling you the tactical situation, because it's the last thing I can give you as spymaster, and after this I'm not spymaster anymore."

"You could still go back," Ivo said. "Tell them where I am. Buy your way clear. He won't hold it against you—the god forced nothing on you outside this shrine."

"He'd know." I looked at Brannoc, who was examining his dirt-blackened fingernails with the studied innocence of a cat near a broken vase. "Wouldn't he. It leaks. Truth like this leaks. I'd spend the rest of my career wondering which word I said in which room found its way back to the king." I laughed, and it came out cracked. "Besides. Forty-one arrests. Do you know what I learned from forty-one arrests? Nobody confesses until the alternative is worse than confession. My alternative just walked into a shrine."

"So what are you saying?" Ivo asked. And there it was again, that hope, that awful, dangerous hope, the thing I had starved for a decade and now couldn't stop feeding.

I stood. My knees complained. Somewhere above us the sky was going from grey to the color of a blade being drawn.

"I'm saying," I said, "that the Verdant Door opens onto a kingdom with no army, no treasury, and a sovereign who can't currently stand up without swaying. And that the king's army seals the passes within days. And that if Thornfell is going to survive its own resurrection, someone who knows exactly how the crown hunts, thinks, and lies needs to be standing on its side of the wall." I held out my hand, palm up, the gesture of a woman offering terms across a table—which was, at least, furniture I understood. "I'm saying I'm defecting, you impossible, invisible, infuriating man. And I'm saying it in front of a god, so it binds."

Brannoc smiled with his one bright eye, and the shrine filled suddenly with the smell of turned earth and blossom, and every candle I could not see lit at once.

Ivo took my hand. His grip was fever-warm and not steady at all.

"Vessa," he said—my actual name, the one I hadn't heard him say in eleven years, "you should know the Door may ask a price. From everyone who crosses."

"Everything worth entering charges admission," I said. "I've read the guild ledgers. Where do we go?"

He looked up past me, toward the mouth of the shrine, toward the mountains, and his face did something complicated—grief and resolve braided together.

"East," he said. "Through the burning shrines. Before the army gets there."

And far off, faint but unmistakable, carried down the pass on the wind like smoke: horns. The king's horns, many of them, closing like a hand.
