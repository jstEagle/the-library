## 14. The Door from the Outside

Three days into the deep passes, I stopped counting the shrines.

Not because there weren't any—there were more than I'd ever noticed in twelve years of running these roads, which said something damning about my powers of observation, or something wonderful about the gods' patience—but because every one of them was lit. Candles. Oil lamps. Little clay dishes of burning fat that some smuggler had carried up a mountain on his back because a god had answered his sister's fever prayer and this was how you said thank you when you were poor.

The old Thornfell religion, as far as I'd ever learned it, ran on exactly two principles: tend what grows, and leave a light where the road forks. Everything else was commentary. The annexation had banned the first principle by paving the terraces and the second by making open flame in the high country a hanging offense. And yet here were the flames, dozens of them, guttering in niches along a route that officially did not exist, tended by people who officially did not exist, for gods who officially had never existed at all.

My country had been erased so thoroughly that its ghost now required three separate official denials to stay dead. There's a word for that kind of bookkeeping. Several words, actually, most of them unprintable.

Brannoc walked with me. Or rather, a one-eyed gardener with mud on his boots and a spade over his shoulder walked with me, humming tunes I half-remembered from a woman's voice twenty years gone, and I chose not to look too closely at the seams. You learn things as a taster. One of them is that the difference between poison and medicine is mostly dosage, and another is that the difference between a man and a god is mostly whether he bothers to explain himself.

"You're quiet," Brannoc said.

"I'm always quiet. It's my best feature."

"Your grandmother talked enough for a regiment."

"So I've been told by everyone who knew her except me." I stepped over a root that hadn't been there last season—or had it? The pass was changing around us, subtly, the way a room changes when someone you love has just left it. Moss where scree should be. Hawthorn seedlings in the cracks of the old Roman-grade paving that the annexation engineers had laid down to prove Thornfell was civilized now. "Everyone keeps telling me about her. Vessa. The ledger clerks. Now you. Nobody tells me anything *useful*, though. Like why she left me a name instead of a warning."

"She left you both," Brannoc said. "You just ate one and spat out the other."

I didn't have an answer for that, so I gave him silence, which is also an answer, and he accepted it with the good humor of someone who has been waiting four hundred years and can wait four hundred more.

* * *

We came to the Verdant Door at dusk on the third day, in a cirque of stone so deep that the sun touched it only at midsummer noon, and even then apologetically.

I had heard of it, the way smugglers hear of everything: as a rumor, a route that didn't work, a gap in the wall of the world where the passes simply stopped and turned back on themselves. Old-timers called it the Green Gap and crossed themselves—which was odd, since crossing yourself was a Lowlands custom, but fear migrates faster than doctrine. They said the Gap was sealed. They said it had been sealed since the Night of Ash. They said no force in the world would open it.

They were right about the seal. They were wrong about everything else.

It wasn't a door in any sense I understood. It was a hawthorn arch grown across the head of the cirque, trunk to trunk, branch woven through branch so densely that the stone behind it showed only in fragments—and the hawthorn was *blooming*. In late autumn. At altitude. In shadow. White blossom in drifts, heavy as snowfall, and under it the smell I had spent my whole life chasing without knowing it: the smell from my grandmother's garden, from the memory interludes that ambushed me at inconvenient moments, green and bitter-sweet and old.

Hawthorn, the herbals say, is the tree of thresholds. It guards the way between. It also, less famously, makes an excellent hedge against livestock, which is presumably why the annexation engineers hadn't cut this one down—they'd probably assumed it was agricultural.

"Ivo Ravensworth," Brannoc said, and his voice had changed. The gardener's drawl was still in it, but underneath was something like bedrock, something that made the cirque's walls carry the sound around and around until my own name seemed to be coming at me from every direction. "Here is the Door your grandparents closed behind them. Here is where Thornfell went when Thornfell stopped being on the maps. Go and put your hand on it."

"What happens if I do?"

"That depends," he said, "entirely on you."

Which is the sort of thing gods say when they want plausible deniability.

* * *

I climbed the last slope alone. The blossom shed petals onto my shoulders as I passed beneath the arch's edge, and each one landed warm, which was wrong, and fragrant, which was worse, because I recognized the fragrance specifically: it was the smell of the ninth dish, the arsenic course, the warmth that spread through my chest when poison met my blood and lost. Twelve years of dinners, distilled into a tree's perfume.

I put my hand flat against the hawthorn's trunk.

Nothing happened.

I want to be precise about this, because precision matters to a man whose entire profession is noticing small differences. Nothing happened—not nothing-as-in-peaceful, but nothing-as-in-*refusal*. The bark was warm under my palm, alive, aware; I could feel something vast turning over on the other side of it, slow as a whale surfacing, the accumulated soul of a whole kingdom sleeping in the dark. And between me and that vastness stood a lock, and the lock looked at me, and the lock found me insufficient.

"Well," I said. "That's embarrassing."

"It isn't," Brannoc said. He had followed me up after all, or had always been there; with him the distinction felt academic. He set his spade against the trunk with the tenderness of a man leaning a tool against a friend's fence. "It's working exactly as designed. Your grandparents built the seal themselves, Ivo. They poured the covenant into it—the land, the laws, the names, the souls of everyone who chose erasure over slaughter—and they keyed it to their blood. To *your* blood. But a key isn't a hand. A key is a hand that knows what it is."

"My hand knows what it is. It's a hand. It tastes things for a living."

"Your hand knows what it's *for*," Brannoc said. "That's different. That's the whole difference." He looked at me then with his single eye, and the eye was brown and ordinary and absolutely bottomless. "Say your name."

"Ivo Ravensworth."

"Your full name."

"That *is* my full name. Two syllables, two words, very economical. I've never needed more."

"You've never *dared* more." He said it without cruelty, which somehow made it worse. "Every heir of Thornfell carries three names, boy. The name their mother gave them. The name their grief gave them. And the name the crown gave them—the title, the burden, whatever they're willing to die holding. The Door opens to all three or none. Your grandparents spoke all three when they sealed it. That's why it holds. That's why it will hold until someone speaks all three again."

I took my hand off the trunk. My palm had left no print in the bark, and somehow that stung worse than a rebuff. Even the tree wouldn't remember I'd touched it.

"And if someone does speak all three?" I asked. "What then? Trumpets? Blossom everywhere? The kingdom pops back out of the box like a jack-in-the-herbaceous-border?"

"The Door opens inward," Brannoc said quietly. "It always opens inward. What comes out is Thornfell—not the territory, the borders are drawn in ink now and ink is a kind of god too, a younger, crueller one—but the *people*. Everyone who went into the dark. Their memories, their gardens, their songs, their stubbornness. All of it, returned to the world, planted in whoever has room for it." He paused. "And the one who turns the key pays the toll."

"There it is," I said. "There's always a toll."

"The poisons in your blood. Twelve years of them, held at bay by borrowed grace. When the Door accepts you—if it accepts you—it will strip the borrowings away to see what's genuinely yours. What's genuinely yours, Ivo, is a body that has drunk more poison than most assassins see in a career. The covenant can take those toxins into itself, transmute them, use them. But the taking is not gentle. Some who have opened Doors like this have died on the threshold. The land grieves them and keeps going, the way land does."

I sat down on the cold stone. It seemed important, suddenly, to be lower than the tree.

"Let me make sure I have the arithmetic straight," I said. "If I claim the inheritance fully—name, grief, crown—I might live, and Thornfell comes back as a people. If I don't claim it, I definitely die anyway, because the immunity's already failing—" I tapped my sternum, where for two weeks now a cold weight had been settling, patient as a debt collector, "—and Thornfell stays erased forever, and Petra died for nothing, and the shrine-burners win, and King Aldric gets to write *Ravensworth, pretender, executed* in a ledger that outlives us all."

"That's a fair summary," Brannoc said. "Though you've left out the part where you refuse out of simple self-preservation, which is the option most sensible men would take, and which no one who loves you would blame you for."

"Who loves me?"

He smiled. One-eyed gardeners should not be allowed to smile like that; it does structural damage to a man's cynicism. "Ask the woman burning your wanted posters. Ask the smugglers lighting candles at the forks in the road. Ask the child who carved your name into a garden row before either of you could walk. You have spent twelve years perfecting the art of being unseen, Ivo Ravensworth, and the tragedy of it—the genuine, honest tragedy—is that you were seen the whole time. You just kept looking away."

* * *

I stayed at the Door that night. Brannoc left—he said something about winter pruning, which I suspect was a joke, since gods prune nothing but certainties—and I built a small fire at the base of the arch, because leaving a light where the road forks seemed suddenly very important, and slept badly in the way of men who have been given a choice and hate both halves of it.

Toward morning I dreamed of my grandmother. Not the real her—I barely had the real her, a handful of afternoons, flour on her hands, a song hummed off-key—but the version the ledger had given me: Hazel Ravensworth, last queen in fact if never in name, standing in a burning garden signing away her own existence with a steady hand. In the dream she turned and looked at me and said, in the voice of every person who has ever told me about her: *You eat poison for a king, little love. Why won't you swallow your own name?*

I woke with frost on my eyelashes and an answer forming, which is the worst time to form answers, before dawn, alone, at the foot of a locked door.

Because the name costs everything, that's why. Because "Ivo" has kept me alive through twelve years of courts and passes and poisoned venison, and "Ravensworth" has gotten Petra killed and Vessa condemned and my face nailed to every gallows tree between here and the sea. Because a name is a handle, and handles are for pulling, and everything that pulls me has wanted to hang me. Because I am very, very good at being no one, and being someone has a body count.

But sitting there in the frost, watching the hawthorn blossoms breathe in the dark, I had to admit the counter-argument was gaining ground. The Door opened inward. Not out. There was no exit here, no escape, no border to slip across in the night with a parcel of contraband and a forgettable face. My grandparents hadn't built a back door out of Thornfell. They'd built a seed vault. And seed vaults are not for the comfort of the gardener; they exist so that something can grow again after the worst has happened, and growing requires planting, and planting requires a hand that stays.

The cold weight in my chest shifted. Two weeks, maybe three, Brannoc had guessed, before the borrowed grace failed entirely and twelve years of arsenic and nightshade and whatever Lady Vessa's predecessors had slipped into royal kitchens came due all at once. I was going to die regardless. The only question on the table—the only actual choice, stripped of all its costume—was whether I died as no one, privately, in a ditch, or as Ivo Ravensworth, publicly, at the threshold of everything my family had saved.

A coward's arithmetic says take the ditch. A taster's arithmetic says something else. A taster knows that the dose makes the poison, and that sometimes the only way through a toxin is straight down its throat, eyes open, trusting the body to remember what it's for.

I stood. I brushed the frost off my knees. I put my hand on the hawthorn again, and this time the bark warmed under my palm like a hand warming another hand, and somewhere on the far side of the wood, the vast slow sleeper turned toward me and waited.

"Not yet," I told it. "There are people coming. There's an army coming, and a woman who burned my poster, and a few hundred smugglers who deserve to watch their kingdom come home. If I'm going to say my name, I'm going to say it in front of witnesses. I've spent twelve years invisible. I'll be damned if I'll be invisible for the five minutes that matter."

The tree said nothing. Trees are famous for it. But a single branch dipped, the way a head dips, and a fall of white petals came down over my shoulders like a benediction or a dare.

Behind me, down the pass, boots on stone. Many boots. Torches threading the dark like sparks up a fuse.

Brannoc reappeared at my elbow, spade and all, looking down the trail with an expression of professional interest. "Speaking of witnesses," he said, "you may want to decide quickly what you're wearing when they arrive. Kings judge these things."

I looked down at my road-filthy coat, my taster's plain black, the clothes of a man designed to be forgotten.

"They can judge," I said. "I'm done dressing for other people's funerals."
