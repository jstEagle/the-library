## 15. Immunity's Price

The first sign was the taste of copper in my morning tea.

I want to be clear about what that means. For twelve years I have drunk things that would drop a warhorse, and the worst they've ever given me is a mild warmth behind the sternum and an afternoon of philosophical melancholy. Arsenic tastes like nothing at all to me now. Aconite is a faint sweetness. The nightshade in the king's wine last spring was, frankly, pleasant—like a memory of blackberries from a summer I probably invented.

Copper, though. Copper is my blood introducing itself to me through the medium of someone else's poison, which is the gods' way of saying: *the account has come due.*

I sat on the cold stone step of Brannoc's shrine with the cup in my hands and watched the steam rise and thought, with the detached professionalism of my trade, *huh.*

"You feel it," said the one-eyed gardener, who had been weeding a patch of nothing in particular since dawn.

"I feel tea."

"You feel your own blood, boy. There's a difference." He snapped a weed off at the root with more violence than the task required. "Sit properly. This will go better if you don't fall over during it."

"During what?"

He looked at me with his one eye—the other a smooth seam of old bark—and did not answer, which from a god is its own kind of answer.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about being a taster: it isn't courage. Courage implies a choice made fresh each time. What I have is closer to arithmetic. Every dose my body takes, it learns. It builds walls against the poison, brick by brick, until the toxin knocks politely and finds no door. The guild calls it *seasoning*. The older tasters call it *paying rent*, though none of them could ever tell me to whom.

I learned this morning to whom.

It started in my fingertips—a prickling, like the moment before a hand goes fully numb, except the moment didn't pass. Then my knees informed me, in writing, that they were resigning. I made it to the shrine's low wall before the ground decided it wanted to be closer to my face, and then the ground won, because the ground always wins eventually, and I lay there in the frost-killed grass looking up at a sky the color of dishwater and thinking about how embarrassing it would be for the last heir of Thornfell to die of lying down.

Brannoc crouched beside me. Up close he smelled like turned earth and woodsmoke and, underneath it, something older—wet stone in a deep cave, maybe, or the inside of a seed.

"Do you know what you were doing," he said, "all those years? When you drank the king's poisons?"

"Staying alive. Staying employed. In roughly that order."

"You were borrowing." He took my wrist—not gently; gods aren't gentle, they're thorough—and pressed two fingers where a pulse should be. "Every time your blood built a wall against a poison, it borrowed the strength to build it. From where, do you think? From whom?"

"I assumed from myself."

"You assumed wrong. You borrowed from the covenant." His grip tightened. "Thornfell's kings were poison-proof too, in the old days. Not because their bodies were clever. Because the land stood surety for them. *Your blood for our blood,* the covenant says. The land absorbs what would kill you, and you keep the garden, and the gates stay open, and everyone lives forever in the way that matters. But the covenant needs a claimant, Ivo Ravensworth. And for twelve years there was no claimant. Only a ghost drinking poison and calling it a living."

"So the walls I built—"

"Were built on credit. And the creditor," he said, with the mild satisfaction of a god delivering bad news, "has called in the debt."

* * *

I spent the next hour vomiting into a drainage ditch, which I mention not for sympathy but because it gave me time to think, and thinking is the one skill I've never let the crown tax.

The arithmetic went like this. Twelve years of seasoning. Hundreds of doses, each one a small loan against a treasury I didn't know existed, held by gods I'd never worshipped, secured against an inheritance I'd spent my whole life pretending I didn't have. Now the inheritance had been claimed—or half-claimed, or gestured at—and the ledger had opened, and every wall my body had ever built was coming down at once, like scaffolding after a coronation.

Which meant every poison I'd ever absorbed was still in me. Sleeping, sealed, patient. And the seals were failing.

By noon I could map them. The arsenic from the venison course, three weeks ago, had set up housekeeping in my liver—I could feel it there, a dull hot coal. The aconite from the winter before hummed in my nerves like a plucked string that wouldn't stop. Old friends. Old enemies. All of them waking up and remembering what they'd come to do.

"You have days," Brannoc said, when I told him. He was watching me with something that on a man I'd have called pity and on a god I'll call bookkeeping. "Four. Five if you're stubborn, and you are, so let's say five."

"And if the Door accepts me?"

"The Door doesn't accept, boy. The Door *trades*. You give it what you are—all of it, name and grief and crown and body—and it gives back what you were meant to be. Your poisons would go into the soil. The soil can eat anything. That's rather the point of soil."

"And if it refuses me?"

"Then you die here, in sight of your grandmother's garden, having come all this way to knock on a door that stays shut." He shrugged, a gesture like a hillside settling. "The gods won't lie to you about the odds. Lying is a human invention. We're very proud of you for it."

* * *

I walked down from the shrine because walking was possible and lying down felt like surrender, and also because Vessa's agents had been shadowing me since the pass, and I wanted them to earn their wages.

There were two of them today—a big one with a crossbow under his oilskin and a small quick one who moved like water finding cracks. I'd known about them for two days. Normally I'd have lost them by now, folded myself into a market crowd and been nobody again by supper. But my legs had opinions, and my vision kept going soft at the edges like a painting left in the rain, and somewhere around the second mile I realized I was doing the thing I'd sworn never to do: leaning on the magic. Letting the covenant carry me. Borrowing again, when borrowing was exactly what was killing me.

So I stopped. Sat on a milestone. Drank from my flask and immediately regretted it, because the water tasted of copper too, which meant it wasn't the tea, it was me, I was the flavor now.

The small agent drifted close enough to speak. She was good—better than good—but she was watching a man walk like a man, and men who walk like men expect to be spoken to.

"You're unwell," she said. Not a question. A report being drafted aloud.

"I'm enjoying the scenery."

"You're Ivo Ravensworth."

The name landed in the empty road between us like a dropped coin. I looked at her—really looked, which I hadn't done to anyone in twelve years, because looking is a transaction and I'd stopped paying—and saw a young woman with a freckled nose and eyes that were doing something complicated. Pity, maybe. Or the particular hunger of someone who has finally caught the thing they were sent to catch and discovered they don't want it.

"That's a dead man's name," I said. "Whoever he was, I hear he's wanted on every gallows tree from here to the coast."

"He is." She hesitated. "Lady Corrane sends a message. She says—" and here her voice did something careful, something rehearsed, "—she says the Door opens both ways, and she knows which side you're standing on, and she says *don't wait past the third day.*"

Then she was gone, quick as water, and I sat on the milestone with my heart hammering out a rhythm that felt less like fear than like a countdown.

Vessa knew. Of course she knew—she'd known long enough to send a message shaped like a warning instead of a warrant. And she'd told me *three days*, which meant she knew about the debt too, or guessed it, which meant somewhere in that beautiful bureaucratic skull of hers lived a piece of knowledge she'd been carrying for years, the way I carried arsenic.

Everyone in this country was carrying something. That was practically the national anthem.

* * *

That night I made it to the smugglers' camp in the hollow below the old terraces, and Petra's sister Hesta took one look at me and started swearing in the old tongue, which I understood perfectly and had never once heard spoken aloud by anyone under sixty.

"You're graying," she said, hauling me upright. "You were dark yesterday. You're gray at the temples *today*."

"It's distinguished."

"It's *dying*, you great idiot, sit down before you fall down." She got me onto a bedroll by a fire and put her hands on either side of my face, and I felt her reach for the old prayers—the small ones, the ones the smugglers had whispered for years over contraband and wounds—and I felt the prayer *fail*, the way a key fails in a lock, sliding and catching and doing nothing.

She pulled back, stricken. "Why won't it—"

"Because I'm full," I said. "Because there's no room left in me for anything but the debt. Because—" and I laughed, which hurt, everything hurt, laughing most of all, "because apparently even miracles check your credit first."

Around the fire, the smugglers had gone quiet. Half of them had Thornfell blood; I'd learned that on the road under the road, learned it in the worst week of my life, when Petra died buying me twenty minutes with a crossbow bolt through her chest. They were looking at me the way people look at a fire that might spread.

Hesta sat back on her heels. She was sixteen and had her sister's chin and none of her patience. "How long?"

"Days. Three, maybe five."

"Then we move tomorrow. First light. The Verdant Door is two days hard walking if we take the deep route, and the deep route is closed—the king's army sealed the passes at dusk. We'd have to go through the burning shrines."

"Burning shrines," I repeated.

"They fired the mountain shrines this afternoon. All of them. Brannoc's among them." Her jaw worked. "They say the king wants the gods' answers stopped before the harvest fair. They say anyone found praying gets the same as the shrines get."

I looked east, toward where the shrine had been, and saw the glow of it above the ridgeline—an orange stain on the underside of the clouds, like a bruise showing through skin. Somewhere in that fire was the wall where I'd vomited this morning, and the step where I'd tasted copper, and the patch of nothing Brannoc had been weeding since before annexation.

"The army's sealing the passes," I said slowly. "Burning the shrines. Hunting a dead man's name. That's not policy, Hesta. That's panic. Somebody told Aldric the Door might actually open."

"Does it matter?"

"It matters," I said, "because panicked men make mistakes, and I intend to be one of them."

* * *

I didn't sleep. Sleep, it turns out, is another thing the body does on credit, and mine had been cut off. Instead I sat by the fire and took inventory, the way I used to inventory parcels: what's in the hold, what's leaking, what's worth saving.

What was in the hold: arsenic, aconite, nightshade, hemlock, four compounds I'd never identified, and the accumulated weight of twelve years of being a wall that poison knocked on politely. What was leaking: everything. My hair was graying at the roots in real time—I watched it happen, strand by strand, silver creeping down like frost across a window. My hands had developed a fine tremor that no amount of taster's discipline could still. When I breathed deep, there was a rattle in the bottom of my chest like gravel in a sack.

What was worth saving: a name. One name, spoken whole, in front of a door that might or might not open, in front of an army that definitely would shoot.

I thought about my grandmother's garden, the terraces blooming untended, the child's carving of my own name hidden in the row of hazels. *Hazel.* She'd named the rows after us, or us after the rows; I'd never known which. I thought about the covenant—*your blood for our blood*—and understood at last that my grandparents hadn't vanished. They'd paid. They'd walked into the Door and given it everything, and the Door had kept a whole nation's soul safe in the dark for sixty years, waiting for someone else to come along and make the same trade.

And I thought about Vessa. *Don't wait past the third day.* Which meant she'd counted. Which meant she knew the terms. Which meant that somewhere in the king's city, a woman who loved a Thornfell exile once was sitting at her desk, deciding whether to burn the kingdom down or let it be born, and I had no way to help her choose, and no time to try.

Toward dawn, Brannoc came. Not as the gardener this time—as smoke, briefly, in the shape of a man, sitting across the fire with his one eye banked like a coal.

"You understand the terms," he said.

"I understand that I'm dying either way. The Door just offers better interest."

"The Door offers *transformation*. Those aren't the same thing, and men who confuse them tend to become very interesting cautionary tales." The smoke shifted. "You should know what acceptance costs beyond the poisons. If it opens, you will never be invisible again. Not for a day. Not for an hour. Every eye in Thornfell will find you, forever, the way roots find water. You have spent your whole life perfecting the art of not being seen. The Door will undo all of it in a single breath."

I looked at my trembling hands. Silver-haired, poison-rattled, thirty-four years old and feeling nine hundred.

"Good," I said.

The smoke regarded me for a long moment. Then, softly, almost warmly, it said: "Then rest while you can, heir of Ravensworth. Tomorrow we walk through fire."

Above the eastern ridge, the glow of the burning shrines had faded to embers, and beneath it—faint, steady, unmistakable—a new light was rising. Not sunrise. Sunrise was hours off.

This light came from underground. From the direction of the deepest pass.

From the Door.

And it was already opening a crack.
