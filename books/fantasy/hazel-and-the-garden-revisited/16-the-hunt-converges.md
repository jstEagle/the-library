## 16. The Hunt Converges

*Vessa*

I have signed a great many death warrants in my career. I used to tell myself it was arithmetic — that a spymaster who flinches at ink is no use to anyone. But I had never before written my own name on one, and I will confess, privately, here, where no one will ever read this: my hand did not shake until after.

The evidence against me arrived the way all damning evidence does — tidily organized, annotated in someone else's handwriting, and delivered by a man who smiled too much. Chancellor's aide. Young. Shiny boots. He laid the folio on my desk with the reverence of a priest presenting relics: my correspondence with shrine-keepers, my payments to smugglers' widows, and worst of all, a copy of Ivo Ravensworth's wanted bill with a scorch mark across the face where I had held it too near a candle. Careless. Twelve years of perfect tradecraft undone by sentiment and bad fire safety.

"The king requires your testimony," the aide said.

"I imagine the king requires my head," I said, "and is willing to accept testimony as an appetizer."

He did not laugh. They never do, the shiny ones. Humor dies young in the palace, smothered in its cradle by ambition.

So I ran. Not because running would save me — I have hunted enough fugitives to know the arithmetic of flight, and mine was poor — but because there was one piece of business left in Thornfell that I could not finish from a cell. His name was Ivo Ravensworth, he had perhaps three days to live, and I had known he was dying since the day I first read his file, twenty years ago, when he was a boy of ten with his family's name struck out beside him in the guild ledger and a note in the margin in my predecessor's hand: *Leave him. A ghost is more useful than a martyr.*

I had obeyed that note for twenty years. I was done obeying dead men.

* * *

*Ivo*

Here is what nobody tells you about being poisoned slowly for twelve years: you get very good at knowing your own body the way a farmer knows weather. The ache behind my eyes meant arsenic. The metallic taste meant something botanical. And on the morning the army sealed the passes, I woke knowing — the way you know rain is coming in your knees — that the borrowed time was called in.

The gods' magic had stripped my protections away two days ago, and now every poison I'd ever swallowed for King Aldric was waking up in my blood like tenants who'd finally read their lease. My hands were yellow at the nails. My gums bled when I thought about it too hard. Brannoc, the one-eyed gardener god, had been frank about the arithmetic: days, not weeks, unless the Verdant Door took me.

And the Door wanted things. Name, grief, crown. A full accounting. I was still deciding whether I intended to pay when Petra's sister came down the mountain path at a dead run with her braid half-unraveled and terror all over her face, which is how news travels faster than horses in Thornfell — by the mouths of people who love you.

"They've sealed the passes," she said. "Soldiers at every gate. And they're burning the shrines."

I looked up at the ridgeline, where smoke stood up straight in the still air, one column, then another, then a third. The old shrines. The ones where smugglers had prayed in secret for years, where Brannoc had healed a dying man, where the gods had woken and started answering. The crown couldn't kill a god, so it was burning the furniture.

"How many men?" I asked.

"Hundreds. Maybe a thousand. And—" She hesitated, and I learned later she'd been deciding whether to tell me, because telling me would hurt me, and she had already lost a brother to keeping me alive. "They have Lady Corrane. In chains. They're saying she's a traitor. They're saying she'll hang with the pretender."

The pretender. That was me. I had a title now. For thirty years I had been no one — the taster, the ghost, the man whose face slid off memory like rain off slate — and now I was a pretender, which meant I existed, which meant somewhere a clerk had entered me into a ledger, and I found, to my considerable disgust, that some small vain part of me was pleased about it even as the rest of me understood what existence cost.

"Where are they holding her?"

"Ivo." She caught my sleeve. "You can barely walk."

She wasn't wrong. I had crossed the courtyard that morning by leaning on walls, and walls don't mind. "Where," I said again, and she told me, because that's what my people do. What my people had always done, apparently, hidden in plain sight, carrying each other's parcels and prayers through the passes while I congratulated myself on being alone. Being blind isn't the same as being solitary. It took me thirty years and a burning mountain to learn the difference.

* * *

*Vessa*

They were moving me to the capital for trial, which was flattering — usually they just dig the hole where they find you — and stupid, because the road to the capital ran within four miles of the Greywater pass, and I had spent twenty years making sure I knew every mile of that road better than the men guarding it.

I escaped at the ford. I won't bore you with the details except to say that a lifetime of reading men pays dividends even in shackles: the sergeant feared his lieutenant, the lieutenant feared the chancellor, and the chancellor, at that moment, feared everything, because the kingdom was coming apart at the seams and everyone with eyes knew whose name was stitched into the tearing edge. Fearful men look away at predictable moments. I looked toward the mountains.

Finding Ivo was easier than escaping, which tells you something about how far the great Ravensworth legend had fallen. I followed the smoke of cookfires, the way you'd follow breadcrumbs if breadcrumbs were made of desperation, and found him in a shepherd's hut with a dozen smugglers around him and death sitting patiently in his chair like an expected guest.

"You look terrible," I said, from the doorway.

"You look worse," he said. "You're supposed to be in chains."

"I was. Chains are easy. It's the paperwork afterward that kills you." I came in, and the smugglers parted for me the way water parts around a stone — not welcoming, but acknowledging. One of them, a girl with her brother's name freshly carved on her heart, spat near my boots. I deserved it. I let it lie there between us like an offering.

"Ivo," I said. "We need to talk. Alone."

"We really don't," he said. "I'm done with secrets. They're heavy, and I'm dying, and I find I've lost my appetite for them." He gestured at the room. "Say it in front of my people."

*My people.* He said it without irony, which from Ivo was more astonishing than any miracle the old gods had managed. So I said it in front of his people.

"I've known what you were for twenty years," I told him. "Since before you were a taster. Since the guild ledger first crossed my desk with your name in the margins. I protected you because I loved a Thornfell exile once, and he died in the annexation riots, and every year I didn't arrest you was a candle I lit for him. And now the king knows I protected you, and there is a warrant with my name on it, and I have burned my life down to stand in this hut and tell you that the passes are sealed, the shrines are burning, and the only way out of Thornfell is the door you're afraid to open."

The silence went on long enough that I heard the wind worrying the thatch.

"You knew," he said finally. "All those years. You watched me taste poison for Aldric and you knew—"

"I knew you were immune. I didn't know why. Not fully. I told myself stories." I laughed, and it came out wrong. "That's the trade, Ivo. We tell ourselves stories so we can keep serving. Yours was that you were no one. Mine was that I was loyal."

"And now?"

"Now I'm a traitor, and it turns out it's the first honest work I've done in decades."

* * *

*Ivo*

There is a particular kind of vertigo in being fully seen by someone you spent years ensuring could never see you. Vessa Corrane stood in that hut with her career burned to ash around her, and she looked at me — not past me, not through me, but *at* me, the way you look at a person — and I felt the strangest grief, because I realized I had built my whole self out of not being looked at, and now that someone was looking, I didn't know what was underneath.

We left that night. There was no choice in it; the army was sweeping the high valleys, burning shrine after shrine, and the smugglers' roads under the roads were the last veins of escape left open. Half my network came with us. Old Tam, who'd carried contraband for forty years and turned out to carry Brannoc's prayer-beads besides. The twins from Lowmarsh. Petra's sister, whose name was Wren, and who walked at my elbow the whole way like a bodyguard half my age and twice my stubbornness.

We moved through the deep ways — the smugglers' tunnels beneath the passes, cut by hands older than either kingdom — and came up into the night through a sheep cleft above the shrine valley, and that was where we saw the fires.

They had saved the great shrine of Brannoc for last. It stood on its knoll above the valley like a grandmother at a family argument, and the soldiers had piled brush against its doors, and the flames were climbing the old stone, and the god's carved face — the one-eyed gardener, the keeper of small doors — was blackening in the heat.

Wren made a sound like something breaking. Old Tam went down on his knees right there in the scree. And I stood and watched twelve years of careful nothing burn, and understood that whatever happened at the Verdant Door, this was the shape of the choice now: the crown had decided that Thornfell must die twice. Once on the maps, once in the ash.

"They think fire works on gods," Vessa said quietly, beside me. Her face was lit orange, and there were tears on it that she made no move to hide. "I wrote reports for years about how the annexation was clean. Bloodless. Erasure without cruelty. I believed it." She shook her head. "It was never clean. It was just quiet. And now it's loud."

Below us, a soldier tore the god's carved head from the doorway and raised it over the fire like a trophy, and the whole valley — every smuggler on that ridge, every hidden believer in every hidden hollow — felt it like a blow. I felt it in my blood, in the pooled antidote of thirty years of poison, in the covenant I hadn't yet claimed. Something ancient flinched.

And then something ancient answered.

The fire went *green*. Not spread — changed, the way a word changes meaning, all at once and entirely. The flames burning the shrine turned the color of new leaves, and the soldiers stumbled back from them, and the smoke rose in shapes that were almost letters, almost the old Thornfell script that had been struck from every map, and the god's severed head, rolling in the embers, opened its single carved eye.

The soldiers fled. I don't blame them. Faith is terrifying when it stops being optional.

"It's not defending itself," Vessa whispered. "Look. It's — remembering. It's showing them."

She was right. The green fire wasn't consuming the shrine; it was *un*-consuming it, peeling back the years, and for a moment the whole valley saw the shrine as it had been — painted, garlanded, crowded with pilgrims, alive — before the image folded closed like a book and the ordinary dark rushed back in, leaving only embers and a hundred soldiers running and a handful of smugglers on a ridge weeping and laughing in the same breath.

"That's what the Door is," I said. The understanding arrived whole, the way poison knowledge always had. "Not an exit. An archive. Everything erased went inside and waited."

"Then open it," Vessa said. She turned to me, and in the ember-light I saw her clearly for the first time — not the spymaster, not the hunter, not the woman who'd signed papers about me for twenty years, but just Vessa, tired and terrified and certain. "Whatever it costs. Whatever it asks. Open it, Ivo. Before they burn everything it remembers."

"It may ask for my life."

"Yes," she said simply. "I know. I've read the same archives you have. I did the arithmetic years ago." Her voice cracked, just once, on the next part. "I've been doing the arithmetic for twenty years, and I am telling you that a country is worth more than a man, and I hate myself for saying it, and I would say it again."

I looked at her a long moment. "Come with me to the Door," I said. "If it takes me, I want the last thing I see to be someone who actually knows my name."

Behind us, down in the dark valley, horns sounded — the army regrouping, the hunt converging on the deepest pass, where a hawthorn arch waited with its roots in a kingdom's stolen soul. We had maybe a day. I had maybe less.

Wren handed me a staff. Old Tam gave me his prayer beads, pressing them into my palm like a debt being repaid. And we went down together, through the burning shrines and the smoking valleys, toward the door that opens inward — a traitor, a ghost, and a country's worth of smugglers, all of us finally, fatally, visible.

The hawthorn was already flowering when we reached it. Out of season. Out of patience.

Out of time.
