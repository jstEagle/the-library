## 17. What Was Erased

I had been poisoned by better gods than this one.

That was my first thought, lying on the cold stone before the Verdant Door while Brannoc's one eye looked at me like a gardener assessing blight. I'd been poisoned by ambitious men, lazy men, a duchess with excellent taste in wine and terrible taste in enemies. Gods were new. But the principle held: something enters your blood, and after that you are not entirely your own.

"You're doing it again," Brannoc said. He was kneeling beside me in his gardener's coat, dirt under his nails, and his voice was patient the way winter is patient. "Arguing with the vision before it starts."

"I'm not arguing. I'm negotiating terms."

"The terms are that you asked to understand." He pressed two fingers against my sternum, and they went *through*, the way roots go through soil—without violence, without asking permission twice. "So understand."

The world folded.

* * *

I stood in a garden the size of a country.

I want to be precise about that, because precision was always my trade—the exact weight of arsenic in a sauce, the exact number of heartbeats between dose and death. So: Thornfell, before. Not the Thornfell of smugglers' songs and burned shrines, but the true thing, and the true thing was *green*. Terraces climbed every mountainside like staircases built for giants who loved vegetables. Orchards grew sideways out of cliffs. Rivers ran through channels carved so carefully that the water seemed less carried than *invited*. And everywhere, everywhere, hawthorn—hedges and arches and lone trees standing in fields like sentries who had decided, long ago, that the watch was worth keeping.

People moved through it all with dirt to their elbows. That was the first thing that undid me. In every history the crown allowed to survive, annexed lands were depicted as either savage or starving, usually both. But these people were neither. They were *tending*. An old woman grafting apple stock with hands steadier than any surgeon's. Children carrying water up switchback paths, singing something in a language I didn't know and understood anyway, the way you understand your own heartbeat.

"They kept the covenant," Brannoc said. He walked beside me now, or rather he was simply present wherever I looked, which I suppose is what gods do instead of walking. "The land fed them. They fed the land back—not just labor. *Attention*. Every garden in Thornfell was also a prayer, and every prayer was also a garden. We answered. That was the whole arrangement. No temples needed. No priests. You planted, we grew. You remembered, we stayed."

"And if someone stopped remembering?"

His single eye turned to me. "You tell me, taster. What happens to a body that stops being tended?"

I said nothing, because I already knew. I'd spent twelve years as an unattended body myself.

* * *

He showed me the covenant's making, because gods are showmen when they want to be.

A circle of people on a mountaintop—I recognized the shape of it, the shrine where Petra died was built on its bones—and among them figures I knew from no portrait and recognized instantly. Ravensworths. My jaw, apparently, has my great-great-grandmother's stubborn set; the woman leading the circle had it too, along with hands stained green to the wrist.

They spoke the words of the covenant aloud, and here is the part no archive recorded, the part Vessa's restricted ledgers never touched: the covenant wasn't a contract about territory. It was a promise about *names*. The land would hold the names of everyone born under the hawthorn. Every child named in Thornfell was written into the soil itself, into root and river and stone, and as long as the land lived, no Thornfell soul could ever be wholly lost—not to death, not to distance, not even to erasure.

"Which," Brannoc said quietly, "is why they had to erase the land."

* * *

The vision changed seasons without warning, the way grief does.

I was standing in the same terraced garden I'd visited above the pass—my grandmother's garden, though I hadn't known it then—but now it was autumn, and there were soldiers on the ridgelines. Not raiders. Annexation armies have a particular look: orderly, well-fed, flying banners that say *this was always ours* over ground they've never seen before. The banners of Aldric's house. The same banner that hung in the banquet hall where I'd drunk poison from a king's cup and been thanked for it.

And in the garden, my grandparents.

I had never seen their faces. I'd been told, in the flat voice foster-house matrons use for damaged goods, that my parents' parents were dead, that my parents were dead, that I should be grateful anyone took me in at all. Now here they were, alive and terrified and *deciding*.

My grandfather was arguing with a man in a grey cloak—one of the old gods, though not Brannoc; this one was younger-seeming, all restless motion, a god of thresholds maybe, of doors. My grandmother stood apart, holding a child. My mother. Three years old, maybe four, with a smudge of jam at the corner of her mouth that made my chest hurt in a way no poison ever had.

"Listen to me," the threshold god was saying, and the wind carried his voice to me across sixty years as if it had been waiting. "The army comes at dawn. You cannot win. You know you cannot win. But there is another way, and it will cost everything you are willing to give and several things you aren't."

"Say it plainly," my grandfather said.

"We take the kingdom's soul into the Door. The gardens, the covenant, the names—all of it, folded inside, safe behind living wood. The land outside goes quiet. Falls dormant. Looks dead." The god's restless motion stilled. "But a door only holds from both sides, and the key is a claimant. Someone of the blood must remain outside. Unnamed. Unmarked. Unremembered. A ghost who carries the claim in his blood until the day the Door can be opened again."

My grandmother spoke then, and her voice was the voice I use, the flat competent voice of someone packing away feeling to deal with later. "And the army? If the land looks dead, what stops them from killing everyone anyway?"

"Nothing," the god said. "Except that dead land feeds no one, and conquest wants tribute. If Thornfell looks emptied—abandoned, failed, cursed—they'll take the valleys and leave the bones. People can survive being overlooked. They cannot survive being important."

Silence. Somewhere down the valley, a dog barked, absurdly ordinary.

"And the child?" my grandmother asked. "The claimant. Who raises a ghost?"

"A family that gives him away. To strangers. Far from here. With no name, no mark, no story—" The god hesitated, and I realized gods can hesitate. "You should know what erasure costs. The land will forget him. The covenant will not be able to find him. He will grow up alone in every way that matters, and he will not know why, and neither will you, most days. That is the price. That is the whole price, and it is enormous."

My grandmother looked down at my mother. My mother looked up at her, and offered her the jam-smudged half of whatever she was eating, because children are generous with exactly the wrong things.

"I'll do it," my grandfather said.

"No," my grandmother said—to him, not the god. "We'll do it. Both of us. Because if you carry it alone you'll break, and if I carry it alone I'll break, and the boy needs the door held steady for sixty years, not ten." She lifted her chin, and there it was again, that jaw, my jaw. "How do we make ourselves forgotten?"

The god told them.

* * *

I don't want to describe it. I'm going to describe it anyway, because Ivo Ravensworth has spent his whole life cataloguing things that hurt, on the theory that a wound precisely measured hurts fractionally less.

They went to the high shrine at night, my grandparents, and they prayed to the gods they had served all their lives, and they asked to be unwritten. And the gods—who had spent centuries gathering every name in Thornfell into the soil like seeds into a granary—took those two names back out. Carefully. The way you'd draw a splinter. My grandfather's name came out of the land first, and he gasped like a man surfacing from deep water. Then my grandmother's. Then, gently, the name of the baby she carried under her heart—my father, unborn, never yet written, never to be written.

"What did it feel like?" I asked Brannoc. I was surprised to hear my own voice; I'd thought I was beyond speech, a ghost watching ghosts.

"Cold," he said. "It feels like cold, and then it feels like nothing, and the nothing spreads. Most people who ask for it stop halfway and beg us to put the name back. Your grandparents didn't stop."

On the mountainside, my grandmother knelt in the frost and recited her own name one last time—*Hazel Ravensworth*—the way you'd recite last rites over yourself. Hazel. The name hit me like a fist under the ribs. My grandmother's name was Hazel, and somewhere in a foster house forty years later, some clerk with no idea he was fulfilling prophecy had handed a nameless orphan a book of fairy tales and told him he'd be called Hazel's boy, *Ivo*, and I had carried my grandmother's ghost around as a nickname my entire life without once knowing it.

She finished the rite. The land let her go.

And then—this is the part I will carry longest—she walked back down to the garden, to my sleeping mother, and she sat by the crib until dawn, memorizing a face that by morning she would not be able to explain why she loved.

* * *

Dawn came. The army came. And the great erasure proceeded exactly as designed, and I watched it from the inside, which is worse than watching from outside, believe me.

The valleys emptied—or appeared to. The gardens went brown in a season, hawthorn hedges closing into thorny tangles no cartographer could push through. The annexation officers wrote their reports: *land exhausted, population scattered, nothing of value.* The crown struck Thornfell from the maps, and here is the mechanism I finally understood, the thing twelve years in restricted archives had circled without landing on: striking the maps wasn't spite. It was the final lock. Because the covenant lived in *names*, the crown had to make sure no name of Thornfell survived anywhere a claimant might find it. Burn the records. Rename the villages. Teach the children of the exiles to sing different songs. A kingdom erased from memory is a kingdom whose door stays shut forever.

Almost forever.

"They nearly managed it," Brannoc said. We were standing in the vision's version of the pass, watching snow bury the last visible terrace walls. "Sixty years is nothing to us, but it is three generations of forgetting, and forgetting compounds like interest. The shrines crumbled. The prayers thinned to whispers, then to habits, then to nothing. We could feel the Door growing heavier every year, harder to open from either side." His eye found me. "And then, twelve years ago, a guild master tested a nervous young applicant with a drop of something bitter, and the drop did nothing, and we felt it."

"Felt what?"

"A Ravensworth name coming back to life." He smiled, and it was not entirely a comforting smile. "Not spoken. Not written. Just—*used*. You signed your guild papers, taster. First time anyone had written that name in sixty years. The land heard it in its sleep."

* * *

The vision loosened its grip after that, scenes coming faster and shallower, a merchant's sample-book of the years between. My grandparents living out their lives in a border town under other names, tending a small garden with obsessive skill, dying within a month of each other and being buried under stones with no inscription—which I now understood was not poverty but discipline. My parents, who never knew what they'd lost, drifting toward Thornfell anyway, pulled by a gravity they couldn't name, dying on the wrong side of a rockslide in a pass their mothers' gods had closed. And underneath it all, the Door, holding. Holding. Holding sixty years of silence the way a dam holds a lake.

Then the visions stopped, and I was on my back on cold stone, and Brannoc's face was above mine, and I was crying, which annoyed me considerably.

"There it is," he said, not unkindly. "Understanding. It looks a lot like grief from the outside. From the inside too, honestly."

"My grandmother gave me away," I said. "Before I was even—my *father* was given away. Unborn. She unwrote her own child to keep him safe."

"She unwrote her whole line to keep a whole people alive. There's a difference, and the difference is everything, and I know it doesn't feel like it right now." He helped me sit up. My body ached the way it ached after the worst poisons, the ones that worked slow. "Ivo. Listen. You've spent twelve years thinking your invisibility was a talent. Something you built. Something you were *good at*."

"It was good. I was excellent at it."

"You were excellent at a wound." He said it without cruelty, which somehow made it worse. "Your grandparents didn't make you a ghost because ghosts are useful. They made you a ghost because a ghost was all that could survive. And every year you polished that invisibility, you were polishing their sacrifice into something it was never meant to be—a life. It was meant to be a *key*, kept hidden in a drawer until the lock needed turning."

I looked past him, at the Verdant Door. The hawthorn arch stood silent in the torchlight, buds tight as fists, and for the first time I saw it accurately: not a wall, not a gate. A held breath. Sixty years of a held breath.

"They're still in there," I said. "The souls. Everyone from before. My grandmother's whole generation. They've been *waiting*—"

"Every soul the covenant ever gathered. Waiting for a claimant to open the Door and let them back into the world." Brannoc rose, brushed off his knees, and became abruptly, terribly businesslike. "Which brings us to the difficult part, and I'll thank you not to interrupt, because the difficult part involves arithmetic."

"I love arithmetic."

"No, you don't. Nobody does." He crouched, eye level with me, close enough that I could see the garden reflected in his one eye—rows and rows of them, going back further than maps. "The Door opens for a full claim. Name, grief, crown—you know this part. But here's what Vessa Corrane's archives don't know, and what your smugglers don't know, and what you need to know *tonight*: a full claim requires the claimant's name to be written into the covenant. Written *fresh*. And the covenant takes names the old way, Ivo. The way it took your grandmother's. Blood, freely given, poured into the soil."

"How much blood?"

"All of it available," Brannoc said. "The Door opens outward for the dead and inward for the living, and the hinge between is a life. Maybe yours. Maybe not—we won't know until the moment comes, and neither will you, and yes, I'm aware that's a monstrous thing to ask a man to walk toward. Your grandmother asked us the same question, sixty years ago, and then she did it anyway."

I sat with that. Outside the shrine, far below, I could hear the army's campfires dotting the passes like a constellation of small stupid fires, and somewhere among them, I had no doubt, Vessa was choosing which order to give at dawn.

"One more thing," I said. "When my grandmother finished the rite. When her name was gone. Did she regret it?"

Brannoc was quiet for a long moment.

"She came back once," he said. "Forty years on. Old, and dying, and nameless, and she stood at the edge of this shrine and she asked us if the boy was alive. We couldn't tell her—the covenant couldn't see you, that was the whole point. So she stood there a while, and then she planted something." He nodded toward the dark mouth of the pass, toward the terraces above it. "An apple tree. On the highest terrace. She said, *if he ever comes home, he'll be hungry.*"

I thought of the garden blooming untended. Of the child's carving with my name. Of the tree I'd eaten from without knowing whose hands had set it.

"She knew I'd come," I said.

"She hoped," Brannoc said. "Kings hope for crowns. Grandmothers hope their boys come home hungry." He stood and offered me his hand, dirt-rough and warm. "Now get up, heir of nothing and everything. Dawn's coming, and the woman who loves you is marching on this shrine with a warrant, and you have perhaps six hours to decide whether you're a ghost or a door."

I took his hand.

Above the pass, faint on the wind, I could almost smell apple blossom.
