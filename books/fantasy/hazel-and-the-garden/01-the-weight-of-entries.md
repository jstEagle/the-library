## 1. The Weight of Entries

The cellar of the Ledgermen's guildhall smells like a library that has been crying. That's not a metaphor I'm proud of, but it's the honest one: old paper, tallow smoke, and beneath it something faintly sweet and rotten, like fruit left too long in a locked drawer. I have spent eleven years breathing that smell, and until this winter I thought it was the smell of safety.

I am Hazel Delacroix, youngest keeper the guild has ever certified, which sounds impressive until you learn there are only four keepers left to be younger than. The Ledger of Impossible Things sits before me on a table scarred by two centuries of ink-knives and candle burns, and my job tonight is simple: balance it.

You cannot balance an ordinary ledger by adding columns. You balance ours by weighing entries against each other — what was given against what was taken, and whether the scales between them still hold. A woman trades her shadow for a winter's warmth; somewhere in the book, a shadow must appear again, or the warmth must be marked as owed. The Ledger does not forgive. It only remembers. And remembering, we believed, was enough.

* * *

The entry came in just after dusk, carried up from the street on the shoulders of a cold snap that had turned the fountain in Copperlane Square into a small, elegant sculpture of despair.

Her name was Vess Aldery. Forty-one years old, a dyer by trade, hands stained the permanent violet of her own vats. She stood at the intake desk with the particular posture of someone about to do arithmetic with their own body: spine straight, jaw set, eyes fixed slightly past the clerk's head, as if looking directly at what she was about to lose might make her flinch.

"I want warmth," she said. "A winter's worth. Real warmth — the kind that gets into the walls."

"And you're offering?"

"My shadow."

The clerk — old Fenwick, whose hearing had gone the way of his patience, which is to say entirely — made her repeat it twice, then wrote it out longhand while I watched over his shoulder, because that is what junior keepers do: watch, verify, initial. Every trade passes through three pairs of eyes or it doesn't pass at all. It's tedious. It's also the entire point. Impossible things are easy to trade and impossible to get back, so the guild's whole architecture exists to slow everyone down.

"Understand the terms," Fenwick said, in the flat voice he used for reciting terms, which never changed no matter how many times people begged him to hurry. "Your shadow will be entered as surrendered, whole and entire, from this hour until the first thaw. You will cast no shadow in sunlight. Mirrors will show you a body without depth. Children may find this alarming. Dogs definitely will."

Vess laughed — a short, startled laugh, the kind that comes when a person realizes they've already decided and are only now reading the contract. "My mother's dog already thinks I'm a ghost," she said. "He'll be thrilled for consistency."

"The warmth will be entered as delivered: coal, oil, and a hearthstone heated in the guild's ovens and set into her chimney by our mason, who owes us nothing and will be paid in full." Fenwick paused, quill lifted. "Balance requires the warmth come from somewhere, Miss Delacroix. Where does the guild get its heat?"

That was my question to answer. I'd been answering it for eleven years, mostly without thinking about it, which should have worried me more than it did.

The guild's warmth comes from the memory-vaults. Not literally — we don't burn memories, whatever the street rumors say. But every impossible thing the guild holds in trust generates a kind of interest, a residue, a surplus of *presence*. A stored shadow leaks darkness. A stored voice leaks sound. A stored summer leaks warmth. We skim the leak. That's the furnace under the cellar floor, the one humming right now beneath my feet as I work: it runs on overflow, on the excess of things too large for their containers. Nothing is taken that wasn't already surrendered. Nothing is burned that wasn't already given up.

That was the doctrine. I could recite it asleep. Some nights I think I did.

Fenwick finished the entry. Vess signed with a violet-stained thumbprint, because she'd sold her name's ink-worth years ago in some smaller trade I didn't ask about, and thumbprints hold better anyway. She took the hearthstone voucher and walked out into the dark without a shadow trailing her, and if you'd seen her on the street that night you'd have thought the lamplight had simply given up on her — light fell around her like water around a stone.

There was a moment, watching her go, where I felt the thing I always felt at the intake desk: quiet pride. Pride isn't fashionable in our line. We're supposed to feel solemn, or mercenary, or nothing at all. But there is a real satisfaction in being the institution that makes a terrible bargain *safe*. Vess would be warm all winter. Her shadow would sit in our vaults, folded like cloth, waiting for spring, and in spring she'd come back and put it on again, and it would fit, because the Ledger remembers exactly how it hung.

We were the reason impossible trades didn't end in ruin. Somebody has to be. I was twenty-six and I genuinely believed we were good at it.

Hold onto that belief for me. I'll need you to remember how it felt.

* * *

Upstairs, in the hall with the long table and the portrait of Founder Corvane — a man painted with such heavy brow that he looks less like a guildmaster than a cliff face considering becoming a man — the senior members were arguing. They had been arguing since October. The subject was the collapse, and the argument had the specific texture of a family refusing to discuss the illness in the room.

I sat in the gallery with the apprentices' records, ostensibly taking minutes, actually listening, because minutes are a fiction we maintain and listening is the job.

Master Quillon spoke first. He's the eldest, keeper of the Seals, a position that mostly means he owns the wax. "The charter is clear," he said, for the fourth time that week, in a voice like a door hinge nobody oiled anymore. "We record trades in impossible things. We do not make them. We do not pursue them. We do not involve ourselves in the affairs of crowns or courts. If the realm forgets us, the realm forgets us. Our duty is to the book."

Master Ilse, keeper of the Vaults, answered the way she answers everything, which is by opening a ledger and pointing at it. "The charter is clear, Quillon, and the charter is dying. Look at the intake numbers. Fifty years ago we recorded nine hundred trades a year. Last year we recorded sixty-one. This year, fourteen, and half of those are repeat customers trading back what they traded before, which is not commerce, it's erosion." She let the page fall open flat. "Nobody comes to us anymore because nobody needs to trade impossible things. Do you know why? Because everything impossible has already been traded. There's nothing left in the market but the dregs."

"There's a third possibility," said Master Corwin, and Corwin always says there's a third possibility, and the third possibility is always worse. He's keeper of Debts, which means he keeps the list of everyone who ever failed to pay us back, and it is a very long list, and I sometimes suspect it has made him the way he is. "The third possibility is that the trades haven't stopped. They're just not coming through *us* anymore. Someone else is keeping a ledger. Someone else is taking the shadows, the years, the names — and not skimming the overflow, but drinking the cup entire."

Quillon called that paranoia. Ilse pointed out that paranoia is a professional hazard when your profession is noticing what's missing. Corwin said nothing further, just looked down the table at me, briefly, the way you look at a junior colleague when you want them to start paying attention without saying so out loud.

I took more minutes. The minutes said: motion tabled, vote deferred, tea cold.

But here is what the minutes did not say, because I did not yet understand it myself: Corwin was right. Something was eating the trades. And it had gotten inside the house.

* * *

It happened during the balancing. Balancing takes hours — you read each entry aloud, softly, to yourself, checking it against its counterpart, and the reading matters because the Ledger is not fully inert paper. Old keepers swore the book listened. I'd always filed that under guild folklore, along with the story about Corvane's ghost pacing the west stair, and the rule that you never whistle in the vaults because the stored voices will whistle back.

Entry by entry. Shadow for warmth. Voice for silence. A fisherman's luck, traded for his brother's recovery, both sides honored, balanced clean. A knight's courage held in trust since 1311, still generating overflow, still warming some orphanage roof we'd forgotten we were obligated to heat — I flagged that one for review, because obligations don't lapse just because the obligation-keepers fall asleep.

And then I reached the section for the year 1188, and the pages were wrong.

Not damaged. Wrong. The parchment was intact, the margins unburned, the binding sound. But where entries should have been — where the index swore entries *were*, cross-referenced in three other places, cited in a dispute settled in 1240, quoted in Corvane's own hand in a letter preserved behind glass upstairs — there was nothing. Not blankness, exactly. Blankness has edges. This was more like fog. Ink that refused to resolve into letters no matter how I angled the lamp. My eyes slid off the page the way water slides off Vess Aldery now.

I checked the index. The index listed forty-one entries for 1188. The pages held none.

I checked the duplicate folio, kept in a separate chest, sealed, rotated annually per protocol. Same fog. Same absence. Two documents, different chests, different centuries of handling, agreeing perfectly on one point: that something had been written there, and that it was no longer readable.

Entries don't vanish. That's the first law, older than the charter, older than the guild itself: *what is entered cannot be unwritten.* We can amend, annotate, seal, redact by mutual consent — but the original remains, even if only as a scar. The Ledger remembers deletions. That's what it's *for*. Memory is the guild's entire inventory, and we do not lose inventory.

Except we were losing it. By weight, if nothing else — I weighed the Ledger on the great brass scale that night, as I do quarterly, and it was lighter. Four ounces. Maybe five. You don't notice four ounces missing from a book the size of a paving stone unless you've spent eleven years learning exactly how heavy it should be, and I have, and it was, and my hands knew before my mind did.

I told myself reasonable things. Damp. A chemical reaction in the old iron-gall ink. Fenwick suggested mice, then immediately retracted it, because Fenwick would rather admit to senility than to mice in the vaults, and honestly the senility was the safer confession.

Then I found the pattern, and the pattern ended the reasonable explanations.

The vanished entries weren't random. They clustered. Years dense with trades of *memory itself* — bargains where the thing exchanged was recollection, remembrance, the past — those years were fogged. Trades of shadows, voices, luck, warmth: intact, legible, balanced. But anywhere the Ledger recorded somebody giving up what they remembered, the record of the giving-up had gone unreadable. As if the book had been permitted to remember every trade except the ones about remembering.

Which is either a flaw in the ink, a trick of the damp, or the signature of something with a very specific appetite.

I sat with that thought until the candle burned down to the collar and the furnace cycled off and the cellar went quiet enough to hear the paper breathe. Around the second hour, Master Corwin came down the stair without announcing himself — he does that, it's a habit from his debt-collecting days and it unnerves everyone, including me, especially now — and stood at the foot of the table and watched me turn pages for a while without saying anything.

"You found it too," he said finally. Not a question.

"I found *something*."

"How far back?"

I told him: the fog starts thin, scattered, a century ago. It thickens through the 1100s. And then there is a gap — a scorched gap, actual burn damage, edges fused like the pages were closed around something hot — running roughly eighty years, and past the burn, the fog resumes, denser than ever, all the way up to within living memory.

Corwin put his hand flat on the table, next to the Ledger but not touching it, which is a courtesy keepers extend to the book. "Eighty years," he said. "Do you know what else happened across those eighty years, Hazel?"

"No."

"Neither do I." He said it plainly, without drama, and that plainness frightened me more than any revelation could have. "Neither does anyone. That's rather the point. There is a century of this guild's history that we cannot read, and the elders argue about intake numbers." He straightened. "Bring it to the table tomorrow. Force the vote. Quillon will call it paranoia. Let him. Paranoia is just pattern-recognition with the volume turned up."

He climbed back up the stair. I stayed.

Because there was one more thing, and I almost didn't look at it, and then I did, because that's the job.

At the edge of the burn — the last legible entry before the scorch swallows everything — there is a name in the margin. Not an entry. An annotation, small, in a hand I recognized before I could explain recognizing it: the slight forward slant, the way the descenders hook like fishing lines. I grew up watching that hand. It's on the flyleaf of the practice-ledger my grandmother kept on the shelf beside her bed, the one she let me copy letters from when I was six, the one I still can't open without smelling her kitchen.

*Delacroix.*

My grandmother's name, sitting at the border of the fire, the last witness before a century went dark.

I want to tell you I felt dread, cleanly, right then, and understood everything. I didn't. What I felt was smaller and stranger: a flicker of pride — *she was there, she was one of us, her hand is in the oldest book* — braided through with a cold thread I couldn't name yet, the sense of standing in front of a door in my own family's house that I had never once noticed was a door.

Pride curdling. Slowly. From the edges inward.

I worked until dawn. When the others came down for the morning balancing, I showed them the fogged pages, and Quillon said damp, and Ilse said check the duplicates, and I had checked the duplicates, and Corwin said nothing at all, just watched me with an expression I couldn't read and didn't try to.

And then, as the bell above the intake desk rang once — someone at the door, someone wanting to trade, the ordinary machinery of our dying guild grinding through another day — a runner came down the stairs with word from the street level, breathless, half-laughing, half-frightened:

"There's a man at the door. Burned. Badly burned. He won't give a name. He says he has something for the Ledger, and he won't leave until a keeper takes it from his hands — he says it can't be set down, it has to be *handed over.*"

Fenwick wanted to send him away. Charter business only, no walk-ins, especially not bleeding ones.

But the man had asked for the Ledger by its full name. *The Ledger of Impossible Things.* Strangers don't know it's called that. Strangers know us, when they know us at all, as the tally-house, the memory-brokers, the shadow-shop on Candle Street.

He knew the true name. And he was holding something he refused to let touch the ground.

I went up to see him.
