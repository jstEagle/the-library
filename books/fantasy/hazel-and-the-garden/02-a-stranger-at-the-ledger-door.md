## 2. A Stranger at the Ledger Door

There is a particular smell to a guildhall at dawn, and it is not incense or old paper or any of the things people imagine when they picture a place where impossible trades are recorded. It is tallow and cold stone and, faintly, the ghost of whatever the night clerk burned in the brazier to stay awake. In my case, three years of night shifts had taught me that the ghost was usually my own eyebrows.

I was in the cellar again, because the Ledger did not sleep and neither, apparently, did I. The missing entries from yesterday were still missing. I had checked four times, then a fifth time with a different lamp, as though the problem might be illumination rather than reality. It was not. The pages simply declined to remember what they had remembered, which is the sort of sentence you hope never to think, let alone write down.

So when the knocking started—slow, arrhythmic, the knock of someone who has forgotten how doors work—I almost welcomed the interruption.

Almost.

* * *

The guildhall's front door is oak older than the city, iron-banded, and carved with our motto: *We Hold What Was Traded.* Beneath it, smaller, added by some long-dead pragmatist: *During Business Hours.*

The man on the step had not observed business hours. He had observed nothing for some time, by the look of him. He was burned—not freshly, not cleanly, but thoroughly, the way a thing is burned when it has been too close to something enormous for too long. His coat was charred leather gone stiff as bark. Half his hair was simply absent, and the half that remained was white, though his face said he could not have been much past fifty. His hands were the worst of it. The skin had healed wrong, pulled tight over the knuckles like wax over a candle's skeleton, and they shook with a fine, constant tremor, as if his body were trying to hold a pen no one had given him.

He fell through the doorway rather than entered it, caught himself on the reception desk, and looked up at me with eyes that were doing two contradictory things at once: focusing on my face with desperate precision, and seeing straight through me into somewhere else entirely.

"You're the keeper," he said. Not a question. A verdict.

"I'm the youngest keeper," I said, because accuracy is a habit in this building, and also because I wanted very much for this to be someone else's problem. "Senior Keeper Vess is upstairs. If you have a trade to record—"

"No trade." His voice cracked like dry kindling. "A debt."

I want to be honest about what happened inside me at that word, because honesty is the one luxury this story can still afford. Every part of me that belonged to the guild leaned forward. Every part of me that belonged to Hazel Delacroix, twenty-three years old and tired, wanted to hand him a cup of tea and point him toward Vess and go back downstairs to argue with vanishing ink.

"Debts are recorded at the front desk," I said. "What was traded, who traded it, and against whom the debt now stands. Those are the three questions."

He gripped the desk hard enough that his ruined hands left marks in the wood—actual marks, which I would later examine and fail to explain—and pressed something into my hands before I could stop him.

It was a map. Folded, small, heavier than it had any right to be. The backing was wax, yellowed and cracked, and set into the wax like scales into a serpent's hide were thin plates of something that caught the lamplight and held it a moment too long. Dragon-scale, I would learn later. At the time I only knew that touching it felt like touching an animal that was pretending to be an object.

"The binding is failing," he whispered. "Follow the map. The garden. Before—" He stopped. His eyes rolled upward, hunting for a word, and came back empty. "Before the last of it. You'll know. Keepers always know."

Then his legs went out from under him, and I learned that a man made of burns weighs considerably more than a man made of ordinary parts, and that catching one teaches you things about your own spine you did not wish to know.

* * *

We got him onto the bench by the hearth—the good bench, the one without the splinter, reserved for clients of distinction, though he hardly qualified and I hardly cared. Old Tam, the porter, helped me carry him and asked no questions, which is either a virtue or a symptom; with Tam it was always difficult to say.

"He needs a physicker," Tam said.

"He needs to not be here," said Vess, arriving with the particular speed of a woman who has heard footsteps in her hall and does not like their rhythm. Senior Keeper Vess was seventy-one, sharp as a ledger-line, and had run the guild's daily affairs since before I was born. She looked at the stranger the way she looked at unbalanced columns: with immediate suspicion and a dawning certainty that correcting him would cost more than it returned.

"Vess," I began, "he says there's a debt—"

"Everyone says there's a debt." She crouched, took the stranger's chin in her fingers, turned his face to the light. Whatever she saw there made her mouth go thinner. "Burned. Lucid in patches. No guild mark, no seal, no letter of introduction. Hazel, we are not a charity. We are not a hospice. We are a *guild*, and the charter is specific about what we take in."

"It's a debt," the stranger said again, to the ceiling, to the fire, to no one. "It's the oldest one. It's been standing so long everyone forgot it was standing." A shudder ran through him. "You're keeping the wrong books."

Vess stood up as though the words had physically touched her. "Tam, fetch water. Hazel, with me. Now."

She marched me into the antechamber and shut the door, and delivered the speech I suspect every senior member of every dying institution delivers eventually, the one about the walls and the foundation and how the roof does not leak if you do not acknowledge the rain.

"That man is outside our charter," she said. "Whatever he carries, whatever he claims, the guild records trades between living parties of sound mind who come to us freely. He came carried. He is not of sound mind. And whatever he's trading isn't his to trade—it sounds like it belongs to the whole realm, and the whole realm is not a client, it's a *catastrophe*. We do not record catastrophes, Hazel. We record what people pay for them."

"And if nobody's recording the catastrophe?" I asked. "Who holds that?"

"The crown holds it. The crown has soldiers." She said it flatly, without irony, which somehow made it worse. "We are six keepers in a building with a leaking roof. Pick your battles by their weight, boy. That one would crush us."

I should note that she was not wrong. This is the trap of Vess: she was very rarely wrong, in the way that a well-kept ledger is rarely wrong, and both can balance perfectly all the way to ruin.

"He gave me this," I said, and showed her the map.

She took it. She held it up to the light. And I watched her face do something I had never seen it do in nine years under her instruction: flicker. Just once, just at the edges, like a lamp in wind. She knew the material. I'd stake my name on it—she recognized dragon-scale the way a banker recognizes a coin that hasn't circulated in a century.

"Where did you find this?" she asked the stranger, later, softer, and that softness frightened me more than her sharpness ever had.

But that came after. First there was the argument, which went the way arguments go in old buildings: nowhere, loudly.

* * *

By midmorning we had assembled what remained of the guild's seniority in the reading room: Vess; Keeper Aldous, who was mostly deaf and mostly asleep and mostly fine with both; and Keeper Ivetta, who was awake in the manner of a drawn crossbow. Six keepers on the rolls, three able to attend, one apprentice. I did the arithmetic on our decline the way I did all arithmetic, compulsively and with mounting dread.

They examined the map around the stranger's supine body, which felt faintly obscene, like appraising jewelry over a coffin.

"Wax and scale," Ivetta said. "Old work. Pre-founding, maybe. Or—" and here she paused, and the pause had weight, "—or from the founding. From *before* the founding. There are entries referenced in the first Ledger that predate the first Ledger. Everyone assumes those were brought over from private books. Nobody asks whose private books."

"Speculation," Vess said.

"Everything is speculation," Ivetta said. "That's why we keep records. To convert speculation into fact at a survivable rate."

The stranger stirred during this. He surfaced from wherever he went—some interior country, burning—and said, clearly enough that even Aldous sat up:

"Ask her about the garden. Ask your grandmother's book about the garden."

The room went quiet in the specific way rooms go quiet when someone has said the exact wrong true thing.

My grandmother, Maren Delacroix, had been Keeper before Vess. Dead eleven years. Her personal ledgers were sealed in the archive, per her own instruction, per guild custom, and I had never once wanted to open them, because wanting to open your grandmother's secrets felt like a betrayal of the woman who taught me to write a fair entry. Now a burned stranger on our bench had reached out of his delirium and put his finger directly on the sealed box, and I could feel the seal warming in my chest like a coal.

"Coincidence," Vess said, but she said it the way you say a word you've suddenly stopped believing.

"He's fevered," Aldous offered, kindly. "Fevered men reach for the nearest names."

"He didn't reach for the nearest names," I said. "He reached for a sealed archive he has never seen, in a building he collapsed into forty minutes ago."

Silence. Then Vess, with the finality of a stamp coming down: "The guild declines the debt. Whatever it is, whoever it binds, it is outside our charter, and we will not be party to it. Hazel, return the map to him when he wakes. Give him coin for a physicker. Close the matter."

And that should have been the end of it. That is the thing I keep returning to, in the nights since. It *should* have been the end. A burned man, refused, paid off, escorted out. The guild survives another season on its shrinking reserves, and I go back to the cellar to fight the vanishing entries alone, and none of what follows ever happens.

Except.

* * *

He woke at dusk, in the interval when the lamps are lit and the hall smells of hot metal, and I was the one sitting with him, because I had volunteered, and because volunteering was easier than admitting I couldn't stay away.

His name, he said, was Corvin. He said it like a man reciting something to keep it from escaping. He drank the broth Tam brought in small, careful sips, holding the bowl in both ruined hands, and between sips he talked, in fragments, the way ice talks when it's breaking.

"There's a garden," he said. "South and east, past where the maps get polite. Walled. Beautiful. Don't let it be beautiful at you—that's how it feeds." His tremor worsened. "I was a keeper once. Different guild. Same work. We recorded what the crown took. Then the crown stopped letting us record, and my partner said we should follow the roots instead of the branches, and I said yes, which is the last decision I remember making clearly."

"Your partner," I said. "Is he—"

"Looking for me. Or reporting on me. With Marlow it was always both." Something moved behind his eyes at the name, grief and suspicion sharing a chair. "Hazel Delacroix." He said my full name, and I had not given it to him. "Your grandmother signed the first page of the debt. I've seen the signature. It's a beautiful signature. They always are, the ones that cost the most."

I felt the floor tilt slightly, the way it does when you stand up too fast, except I was sitting down, and the tilt wasn't in the floor.

"You've seen her ledgers?"

"I've seen the *gap* where her ledgers should be." He closed his eyes. "A hundred years of entries, Hazel. Gone. Not lost—gone. Lost things can be found. Gone things were *made* gone, by someone with access and a reason and a flame." His breathing hitched. "The binding is failing. Do you understand? Everything it ate for a century, it's starting to digest. When it finishes digesting, there won't be a realm to remember itself. There'll just be a garden, and a king who smiles at nothing, forever."

He was lucid for perhaps ten minutes total, in three installments, and each installment was worse than the last, and I wrote down every word afterward in my own notebook, in my own hand, because if the guild wouldn't take the debt, then someone damn well would hold it.

On the third installment, near midnight, he gripped my wrist with those candle-skeleton hands, and his grip was stronger than it had any right to be, and he said:

"They'll refuse it. Your elders. They refused already, didn't they." Not a question. He knew. He'd known when he chose the door, maybe; maybe he'd chosen the door *because* of what he knew about guilds, which is that they refuse exactly the debts that matter. "So it falls to you. The young ones. It always falls to the young ones; that's the oldest debt of all." His laugh was a dry, terrible thing. "Take the map. Take the debt. Write yourself in as the holder. One line. Your hand. That's all it takes to make it real."

"That's forgery," I said. "Unsanctioned entry. It's—"

"It's a choice," he said. "When did you last make one of those? Truly? You balance what you're given. You inherit what you inherit. You check the lamp five times because checking is all anyone ever lets you do." His eyes found mine and held them, and for a moment the fever cleared out of them entirely, and what was underneath was tired and kind and absolutely certain. "Here's a thing that is yours, Hazel. Here's a door only you can walk through. It's terrible. It's probably fatal. But it's *yours*."

Then the fever took him back, and he slept, muttering about roots.

* * *

I sat in the cellar until nearly dawn with the map open in front of me and the Ledger beside it, and I did not touch either one for a long time.

Here is what I knew, laid out like entries awaiting a ruling. The guild was dying; the entries were vanishing; the elders had refused; the stranger had named my grandmother and a garden and a king in the same breath. Here is what I believed, which is a different column entirely: that Corvin was telling the truth, or as close to truth as a burning man can carry; that the vanishing entries and the failing binding were the same event seen from two sides; and that Vess was right about everything except the only thing that mattered.

Because here is the flaw in her arithmetic, the error no audit would ever catch: she calculated what the debt would cost *the guild*. She never once calculated what it would cost *me* to walk away from it. She assumed those were the same number. They are not. They have never been the same number, for anyone, in the entire history of holding things.

Near dawn, I opened the Ledger to the current page. Below the woman's shadow-trade, below the blank spaces where entries used to live, I dipped my pen.

I did not write myself in as the holder. Not yet. I want to claim I was being careful, or principled, or wise. The truth is my hand shook too badly, and not from fear—from something adjacent to fear but warmer, faster, stranger. A thrill. The first pure thrill of my working life. Nine years of balancing other people's trades, and here, finally, was a line only I could sign, a debt only I could choose, and the choosing felt like standing up in a room where I'd been seated since childhood.

Instead, I wrote one line, dated it, and left the holder's space deliberately blank:

*Received into custody, pending ruling: one map, wax and scale, provenance unknown. Condition of bearer: critical. Nature of debt: the binding is failing.*

Then I folded the map into my coat, blew out the lamp, and went upstairs to find Vess, because there was one question I needed answered before I decided anything, and it was not a question the charter covered.

"Vess," I said, when I found her at her desk in the grey light. "The burn scar on the archive wall. The gap where the century of entries should be. Whose name is written at the edge of it?"

She looked up at me, and her face did the flickering thing again, and I knew, before she answered, that I was not going to like it.

"Maren Delacroix," she said quietly. "Your grandmother's name is at the edge of the burn, Hazel. And I have spent eleven years telling myself that means nothing at all."
