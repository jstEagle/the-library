## 6. The Road Through Borrowed Country

The map did not want to be folded.

I learned this on the first morning out of the city, when I tried to tuck it into my coat and it slid out of my hands like a cat being put in a bath, unrolling itself across the road with a smugness that wax and dragon-scale have no business possessing. Marlow watched me wrestle with it for a full minute before offering any help, which told me a great deal about the kind of man he was going to be for the length of this journey.

"It's keyed to direction," he said finally. "It wants to point. You're asking it to sit still."

"Everything in this guild wants something," I said. "Ledgers, maps, elders. It's like traveling with a family of creditors."

"You *are*," Marlow said, and had the decency to look slightly apologetic about it.

We walked north and west, along roads that grew quieter the further we went from the capital. Not empty—quiet. That's a distinction I'd never had cause to make before, and I wish I still didn't. Empty roads are just roads where nobody is. Quiet roads are roads where people are trying very hard not to remember why they came outside.

The first village was called Bracken Hollow, according to a sign whose letters someone had lovingly repainted within living memory and then, apparently, forgotten to care about. The well at its center still worked. The houses were sound. The gardens were weeded—mostly. But when an old woman passed us on the lane carrying a basket of bread, she nodded politely, the way you nod at strangers, and then stopped dead three paces later and turned around.

"I know you," she said. It wasn't a question or a greeting. It was a woman testing a tooth with her tongue to see if it was still there.

"You don't," I said gently. "We're travelers."

"No." She frowned, and behind her eyes something moved like a fish under ice. "No, I know you. Or I knew knowing you. There's a shape of it. Did I sell it?"

Marlow and I looked at each other. He shook his head almost imperceptibly: *don't*.

"What do you mean, sell?" I asked anyway, because I have never once in my life taken good advice.

"The crown buys," she said simply. "Or the crown's men do. Years, mostly. Memories, if you've got good ones. My neighbor sold her wedding day for a new roof. She has the roof. She stands under it some mornings and cries without knowing why." The old woman smiled at me, and it was the politest, emptiest smile I have ever been the object of. "I kept mine, whatever it was. Kept the shape of it, anyway. Like keeping the receipt after the purchase is gone."

She went on toward her house. At the door she paused, looked back at us, and said, "You'll be wanting the miller, if you're collecting. He's got children he can't name."

Then she went inside, and shut the door, and that was Bracken Hollow.

* * *

The miller's name was Aldous, though he introduced himself as "the miller," because that was all he had left of himself that fit. He was perhaps forty, broad as a door, with flour worked so deep into his knuckles it looked like age. He received us in his kitchen because his kitchen was warm, and warmth, I was learning, was the one thing people in these places still trusted.

"My wife says I should stop talking about it," he said. "But she remembers them, so it's easy for her to say."

"Them?" Marlow asked, though I could hear in his voice that he already knew.

"My children." Aldous turned his cup in his hands. "Two of them. I know there were two of them because there's two of everything. Two beds in the back room. Two sets of boots by the fire, one small, one smaller. Two names carved into the beam over the stove, and I don't—" His voice caught, and he steadied it the way you steady a load that's about to shift. "I don't know which names go with which bed. I look at the carvings and it's like reading someone else's family tree. *Wren.* *Piet.* Lovely names. Whose are they? They're mine. They're mine and they're gone and I live in a house full of their ghosts' laundry."

He wasn't crying. That was the worst part. He was past it, into the flat country on the other side of grief, where a man describes the unbearable in the tone of a weather report.

"They took payment," he said. "Three years ago. Bad harvest, bad debts, and a man came through with a crown seal and offered to clear everything for—" he waved a hand at his own head, vaguely, "—for some of this. Said it wouldn't hurt. Said I wouldn't even miss it." He laughed, short and terrible. "He was right. That's the trick of it. You can't miss what you can't remember missing."

I had my ledger out before I knew I'd opened it. Habit, maybe. Or maybe the thing my grandmother's blood apparently does, which is reach for the book when someone is bleeding.

"What are you doing," Marlow said quietly. Not a question. A warning.

"Checking the rates," I said, which was true, and also a lie, and both of us knew it.

Because here is what nobody tells you about being a Ledgerman: the guild doesn't just record trades. We hold reserves. Every impossible thing ever traded passes through our vaults in some form—the memory of it, the weight of it, the *entry*. And entries can be spent. It's the oldest clause in the charter, the one the elders pretend doesn't exist because it makes us not just record-keepers but *banks*, and banks, as everyone knows, only ever take.

There were entries in my ledger from Bracken Hollow. Three years old. Two names, two trades, signed with a crown seal. Wren Delacroix—no. Wren *Miller*. Piet Miller. Sold by their father in a moment of desperation, held in trust by a guild that had done precisely nothing with the trust.

"Aldous," I said. "Your children's memories. The guild holds them. What they looked like. Their voices. The day each of them was born. It's all here, in the reserve."

His head came up slowly, and I saw hope move across his face like weather, and I hated myself for what I had to say next.

"I can't give them back. Not whole. An entry returned by force tears—it takes the surrounding years with it, frays everything near it. If I hand you your daughter's face outright, you might lose your wedding day to pay for it. Your mother's voice. There's no clean withdrawal. There never has been."

"Then what *can* you do?" And there it was—not anger, not yet, just a man standing in a flooded house asking how high the water would get.

I looked down at the ledger. At the entries. At the small, dense script of the reserve column, where the guild had stored two children like coins in a purse.

"There's an old practice," I said. "*Lending*. Against collateral. I could lend you one memory—one small one, something with edges, something that will hold its shape—and take, as surety, a memory of equal weight from you. Something you choose. When the debt above you is settled—if it's ever settled—you trade back and get both."

"And if it's never settled?"

"Then you keep what I gave you," I said, "and I keep what you gave me, and we've both lost something and both gained something, and that's called being alive."

Marlow made a sound that was half cough, half *you absolute fool*, and I ignored him with the ease of long practice.

Aldous thought about it for a long time. The fire ticked. Somewhere upstairs, his wife moved through rooms that remembered more than he did.

"The day Wren was born," he said finally. "That's what I want. Just—the beginning of her. The first minute of her."

"And the collateral?"

He was quiet a while longer. Then: "My father teaching me the mill. The sound of the stones. I can't—I keep reaching for it and it's like a stair that isn't there. Take that. I'd rather have a daughter than a dead man's voice."

So I opened the reserve column, and I did the thing the charter calls *transfer against surety* and what I suspect every Ledgerman who ever did it privately called *pulling teeth*.

It doesn't hurt the way you'd think. No blade, no burn. It hurts the way a tooth comes out: a pressure, a wrongness, a sudden hollow where something rooted used to be, and then the strange relief that is worse than the pain. I gave him his daughter's first minute—her cry, the shock of her, the way (the entry said) he had laughed and wept at once and embarrassed the midwife—and I took the mill-stones, the sound of his father's craft, the low grinding music of a working day.

And here is the part I will not put prettily, because it shouldn't be pretty: it felt *good*. Giving it to him felt like setting a bone, like the ledger was finally doing what it was made for. For one bright moment I understood my grandmother completely. Understood why a person would trade anything to feel that.

Then the hollow closed over, where the mill-stones had been, and I carried a stranger's childhood in my book like a stone in the shoe, and we left Bracken Hollow before dawn.

* * *

It became a pattern, which is how sins always arrive—as routines.

A village where a woman had traded the memory of her sister and couldn't remember why she flinched at lullabies; I lent her back a single afternoon, two girls on a riverbank, and took her memory of her own wedding feast as surety. A man who had sold the taste of his mother's cooking and stood in his kitchen every evening hungry for a flavor he couldn't name; I lent him one dinner, and took the smell of rain on his farm, and he wept over his stew like a man hearing a dead language spoken fluently.

Each time, the same mechanics. Each time, the same pulled-tooth ache settling into me alongside the borrowed weight. By the fourth village I was carrying a miller's childhood, a bride's wedding, a farmer's rains, a fisherman's first boat-launch—memories stacked in my ledger like ballast, none of them mine, all of them pressing.

"You're doing it wrong," Marlow said one night, at a fire neither of us was enjoying.

"Enlighten me."

"You're doing it *at all*." He fed the fire a stick with unnecessary violence. "Every memory you lend, you weaken the reserve. Every transfer frays the entry a little. And every one of those frayed entries is a thread the Verdant can follow straight back to the guild—to *you*. You're leaving a trail of loose stitches across half the country, Hazel, and somewhere ahead of us the thing that eats memories is smelling every single one."

"So we let these people walk around hollow?"

"We let them walk around hollow until we understand what we're walking toward!" He was up now, pacing, and I realized with a small cold shock that Marlow was frightened—not of the Verdant, I thought, but of something adjacent to it. "You think I haven't seen a good man spend himself into ruin one compassionate withdrawal at a time? The guild's whole history is that story. Your grandmother's story. You pull one tooth, then another, and you tell yourself each one is different, and then you look up and you're the thing in the crown."

"That's not—"

"Isn't it?" He crouched, eye-level, firelight in his face. "What did you take from the fisherman, Hazel? Tell me exactly. Without looking."

I opened my mouth. Closed it. Reached for the ledger.

"*Without looking,*" Marlow said softly.

And I couldn't. I could feel the weight of it—four trades, four hollows—but the contents were blurring, running together like wet ink. The mill-stones and the rains and the wedding feast had begun to pool into a single gray sediment at the bottom of me, and I understood, sitting there in the firelight, that this was how it happened. Not with a bargain in a garden. With a kindness on a road. One withdrawal at a time, until the keeper couldn't tell his own entries from the stock.

I didn't stop. Let the record show I didn't stop. But I started writing each trade down twice—once in the ledger, once in a cheap notebook I bought in the fifth village, in my own hand, in plain words, so that some part of me would stay a man describing things rather than a vault storing them.

* * *

The country changed as we went north. Greener—that was the first wrongness. Fields too lush, hedgerows heavy with fruit out of season, orchards bending under blossoms and ripe fruit on the same branch, which no honest tree does. The second wrongness was the birdsong: plentiful, varied, and identical, phrase for phrase, hour for hour, like a music box left running.

The third wrongness was the children. Or rather, the absence of them being missed.

In the town of Vell—though the sign said VELL and then, beneath, in older carving, something that had been chiseled off—we found a market running at full chatter, stalls stocked, coin changing hands, and not one person who could tell us the way to anywhere outside the town. Not one. Ask a direction and they'd smile and offer you apples. Ask about the capital and they'd smile and offer you apples. Ask about their own lives before this valley and you'd get a smile like a drawn curtain and another apple.

"They've been grazed," Marlow said quietly, outside the town that night. He wouldn't sleep under its roofs. "Not harvested—grazed. This is what the Verdant does to the land around its roots. Takes a little from everyone, often enough that no one notices the field getting shorter. These people aren't hollow like the miller. They're *shorn*. And fat and content, which is worse."

"And past this valley?"

Marlow looked at the map, which lay between us pointing, patiently, insistently, northwest, toward a blank space where the cartographer had given up and drawn, in tiny careful letters, a single word: *HERE*.

"Past this valley," he said, "is the garden."

I dreamed that night of my grandmother's handwriting. In the dream she was writing an entry and I was trying to read it upside down, and the ink kept moving, and just before waking I understood that it wasn't moving—it was *growing*, little green shoots rising out of every letter, and my grandmother looked up at me across the desk and said, with great kindness:

"Don't lend what isn't yours, love. Lend what's yours, and it can never be repossessed."

I woke with the notebook under my hand and wrote that down too, though I didn't yet understand it, and outside, far off in the dark, something in the direction of *HERE* hummed like a hive the size of a horizon.

We broke camp before light. Emberlow's caldera lay three days east of the garden, and the map wanted us to fetch fire before we faced roots—which meant crossing the shorn country again, and then country that hadn't been shorn yet.

At the edge of Vell's fields, a child—a real child, the first we'd seen in days—was standing alone at a gate, watching us go. As I passed, she said, in the matter-of-fact voice of the very young:

"You're the one who's been giving things back."

I stopped. "Who told you that?"

"Nobody." She picked at the gate's latch. "The ground talks about you. It says you're pulling teeth." She looked up at me, and her eyes were the exact green of new leaves. "It says to tell you it likes the ones you leave behind. They grow back sweeter."

Behind us, all across the valley, the birds changed their song—all together, all at once, to a tune I knew.

It was the lullaby from the riverbank. The one I'd lent the woman who'd sold her sister.

The Verdant was humming my withdrawals back to me.
