## 7. The Last Dragon

The map stopped being a map somewhere past the Ashfall Reach and became, instead, a dare.

The wax had gone soft in my pocket for three days, and the dragon-scale inlay—the one true piece of scale, dark as a stove door—had begun to warm. Marlow noticed before I did. He always noticed things he wished he hadn't; it was the archivist's curse, and he wore it like a man wears a coat two sizes too small.

"It's warming," he said, for the fourth time that morning. "Hazel. The scale is *warming*."

"I heard you the first three times."

"And you have no comment?"

"I'm saving my comments for something worth commenting on." I squinted up the slope of black glass and cinder. "Like whether we're about to die."

We climbed through a country that had forgotten how to be a country. The Verdant's hunger radiated outward from the capital in rings, the way a stone sends rings through water, and we were deep enough into the third ring now that forgetting had become geography. A village at the mountain's foot had no name anymore—not a lost name, not a burned sign, just an absence where a name should be, a little hollow in everyone's mouth. The innkeeper fed us stew and could not tell us what village we were in, or what her own daughter looked like, though she set a place for the girl at every meal and wept without knowing why.

I had traded away four ledger-memories on the road so far. Small ones, by design: the smell of the guildhall's ink room, a rainy afternoon when I was nine, the name of a boy who'd once beaten me at cards. Each one went out of me like a tooth pulled—there was the socket, there was the ache, and then there was the strange smooth nothing where the thing used to live. I wrote what remained in the ledger's margin: *Gave. Freely. To a woman whose child forgot her face.* The margin was getting crowded. The margins are where keepers hide the things they can't bear to enter properly.

Marlow watched me do it and said nothing, which from Marlow was a sermon.

"You think it's foolish," I said.

"I think," he said, "that you're spending yourself like a man with an inheritance he doesn't believe in."

"Maybe I don't."

"Then you're a fool twice over." He pulled his coat tighter. "Come on. The scale says close."

* * *

The caldera opened beneath us like a mouth deciding.

One moment we were scrambling up a ridge of slag; the next the ground simply wasn't there, and we stood on the rim of a vast bowl a mile across, its floor a lake of cooled basalt cracked into plates, its walls streaked with old fire-scar. The air inside it shimmered faintly, even in the cold, the way air shimmers above a forge that has only recently gone out.

And in the center, coiled around a spire of black glass like a ring around a finger, slept the last dragon.

I want to tell you I felt awe. What I actually felt, first, was the ledger in my satchel going *hot*—not the map this time but the book itself, every page humming against my hip like a struck bell. Dragons, Marlow had told me, were the guild's oldest counterparts. Where we recorded impossible trades, they witnessed them. Every bargain ever struck under the old law had been signed in flame. And flame remembers. That was the whole doctrine, the first sentence of the first ledger anyone ever kept: *What is written may be burned; what is burned is written deeper.*

"She's smaller than I expected," Marlow whispered.

"She's dying," I said, and I don't know how I knew, but I knew it the way you know grief in a stranger's posture—from the way she lay. Not coiled like a treasury, the way dragons are supposed to sleep, each scale guarding a coin. Coiled like someone trying to take up less room.

We went down the inner slope together, boots skidding on glass, and the whole time the heat grew—not furnace-heat, but body-heat, animal and enormous—and the hum in my satchel grew with it until my teeth ached.

Twenty feet from her snout, she opened one eye.

It was the color of the caldera itself: black glass over moving light. It found me, and it stayed on me, and I understood that I was being read. Not looked at. *Read.* Page by page, cover to cover, by something that had been reading for longer than my guild had existed.

"You carry paper," she said.

Her voice came out of the ground as much as the air—a vibration in the basalt, in my boot-soles, in the fillings of my teeth. It was a dry voice. A voice with ash in it.

"I'm Hazel Delacroix," I said. "Keeper of the Ledger of Impossible Things. Such as it is."

"Such as it is," she repeated, and something moved behind the eye that might, in a healthier creature, have been amusement. "Yes. I remember when such as it is was such as it *was*. Come closer, paper-keeper. My eyes are going the way of everything else."

We came closer. Up close she was worse than dying; she was *thinning*. Her scales, which should have thrown back light like hammered bronze, had gone matte in patches, gray as old ash, and where the gray spread deepest I could see through her—not to bone, but to a kind of dim ember-glow, as if she were becoming transparent to the world. Her hoard lay scattered beneath her coils, and I made myself look at it, because Marlow had told me what to expect and I wanted to be brave about it.

It wasn't gold. Or rather, it wasn't only gold. Dragons hoard what matters, and what had mattered to Emberlow—her name was Emberlow, the map knew it even if she might not—was memory. Coins stamped with faces of kings nobody living remembered. A knight's rusted spur. A child's wooden bird. Shelves of it, arranged with a curator's love, all around the caldera floor: the physical anchors of ten thousand years of witnessed things.

Half the shelves were empty. Not robbed. *Emptied.* The dust lay undisturbed where objects had been, and beside some of the empty spaces sat little clay tags in a careful claw-script, labels for things that were no longer there. She had labeled them so she would know what she'd lost. That was the detail that got me. That was the detail I still can't write down without my hand shaking.

"You're forgetting your hoard," I said quietly.

"I am forgetting *everything*, in order," she said. "There is a discipline to it. I began with the recent centuries—they were mostly disappointment—and worked backward. I am now somewhere in the reign of Aldric the Second, whom I remember disliking, though I no longer recall why, or indeed who he was." The eye blinked, slow as a cellar door. "You smell of ledgers and of giving-away. You've been trading yourself down the road. Foolish. Or generous. In my experience the two are indistinguishable at the funeral."

"The Verdant took your memories too?"

"The Verdant takes *everything*, eventually. It is very patient and very thorough and it does not consider itself a thief." Her head shifted, and the whole caldera groaned faintly with the movement. "A thief knows he steals. That thing believes it is a *garden*. It believes it is cultivation. Do you know what it said to me, the last time it reached this far? It said: *you hoard what you could be growing.*" The ember-light behind her scales flared, briefly, angrily. "I have thought about that for sixty years. It is the only argument I have never managed to answer."

* * *

Here is what the last dragon dreamed, while two small creatures picked their way across her floor:

She dreamed in the old order, because the new order had holes in it. She dreamed of her first flight, which was the oldest thing she owned and therefore, by her own discipline, the last thing she would lose. She had resolved this long ago, with the clarity available only to the dying: *the first flight stays until the end. When it goes, I go.*

In the dream she was young and the sky was a country with no borders. She remembered the exact temperature of the thermals above the eastern ranges, the way her mother had flown half a wingbeat behind her—not guiding, never guiding, just *there*, the way a good keeper stands beside a page—and the moment when the ground fell away and she understood, with her whole body, that falling and flying were the same motion seen from different states of courage.

She woke a little, because the paper-keeper was saying something interesting.

"—the binding is failing," Hazel was saying. "The king, the crown, the Verdant—it's all coming loose, and when it comes loose it won't release anything. It'll just consume. Everything it holds. Everyone it's fed on. We need your fire."

"My fire," Emberlow said, "is the only fire that burns roots instead of branches. Yes. I know why you've come. The map told you, and the map is mine—I pressed that scale to my own chest, a hundred years ago, and gave it to a man I thought might matter. He mattered less than I hoped. His partner is standing next to you looking guilty, which suggests the story improved somewhere along the line."

Marlow went the color of tallow. I decided to file that away.

"Here is my difficulty, keeper," she went on. "Fire is memory. Every flame I have ever breathed was made of something I witnessed—you cannot burn what you have not known; that is the first law and the Verdant has never broken it, only *misused* it. And I am running out of witness. I am down to the bedrock of myself. If I give you the fire you need, I must burn memories I swore to keep. If I keep them, the garden wins by default, and everything I ever saw dies inside a corpse." The great eye closed and opened. "So. You are a keeper of trades. Make me a trade."

"What do you want?" I asked, though my stomach already knew, because the ledger in my satchel had gone hot again, hot as the scale, hot as a held breath.

"Not gold. I have gold; gold is what you collect when you've run out of sense." Her head lowered until that black-glass eye was level with me, close enough that I could see my own reflection warped in it, small and upright and terrified. "I want what you have been doing all down that road, paper-keeper, wastefully, generously, foolishly. I want to be *kept*. When my fire fails—and it will fail, soon, mid-wing, I know the arithmetic of my own body—I want what remains of me to live somewhere that isn't ash. You will carry my memories in your ledger. All of them. The flights, the bargains, the names of every king I disliked and the reasons why. You will become my archive, and when I forget a thing, you will tell it back to me, and when I am gone, you will keep telling it. That is my price."

"That's not a trade," I said. "That's a gift with paperwork."

"Yes," said Emberlow, and the ember-light behind her scales rose like a sunrise seen through a keyhole. "I have watched your kind for ten thousand years, little keeper. I know exactly one thing about you for certain: you are at your best when you pretend a gift is a transaction. So. Pretend."

* * *

Marlow pulled me aside, twenty feet down the caldera floor, under the shelf that held the labeled emptiness where a wooden bird used to sit.

"Don't," he said.

"Don't agree to save the world? Bold position. Let me hear the argument."

"Hazel." He gripped my sleeve, and his hands were shaking, and I realized with a small cold shock that Marlow was frightened—for me, maybe, or for himself, or for something he wasn't saying. "Listen to me. I've read the restricted archives. I've read what survived the burning. The Verdant didn't come from nowhere. It started as a *bargain freely made*—a guild promise, a seed accepted, abundance offered in exchange for remembering. That's how it got its teeth. Something agreed to be a garden, and the agreement grew hunger. And now the last dragon wants to pour herself into a ledger carried by a Delacroix, and you're going to say yes, because you're you, and I am asking you to *think*—"

"I am thinking."

"Think harder!"

"Marlow." I looked him in the eye. "Everything we've done since the stranger died in our doorway has been a guess. Guessing hasn't killed us yet. And I'll tell you what I know that you don't." I tapped my own chest, where the pulled-tooth aches of four given memories lived. "Every memory I gave away on that road, I gave *freely*. And do you know what happened? They're gone from me—but they're not *nothing*. The innkeeper looked at her daughter's empty chair and she *wept*, Marlow. Something of me is keeping her company. Freely given things don't vanish. They just change hands." I turned back toward the dragon. "Maybe that's the whole secret. Maybe that's what the Verdant never learned, because it only ever took."

He didn't answer. Behind us, the last dragon waited with the patience of a creature who had already waited out empires.

* * *

I knelt on the basalt and opened the ledger across my knees, and Emberlow lowered her head until her brow nearly touched the pages, and we made the trade.

It was not like the pulled-teeth of the road. Nothing left me. Instead things *arrived*—in a flood, in a tide, in a river with ten thousand years behind it. Her first flight, complete with the temperature of the thermals. Her mother, half a wingbeat behind, forever. The coronation of a king whose name I suddenly knew and disliked, for reasons I could recite. The smell of a forest that burned four thousand years ago. A hatchling's view of the moon. On and on, page after page writing itself in my hand without my hand, my fingers cramping around a pen that moved like a divining rod, and through all of it Emberlow spoke aloud, naming each memory as it landed, the way you'd name items at a border crossing:

"First flight. Mother's shadow on the cloud. The treaty of the Salt Coast, and the perjury that followed. The name of the first keeper, who was kinder than the guild deserves. The taste of lightning—yes, we taste it, write that down, no one ever writes it down—"

And here is the thing I will tell no one else as long as I live: as her memories poured into my ledger, she grew *brighter*. Not restored—she was still thinning, still gray-patched, still transparent at the edges—but lit from within, the way a lamp is brighter for having somewhere to shine. And I felt heavier. Fuller. Like a man carrying a cathedral in a satchel.

When it was done, dusk had fallen in the caldera, and the stars over the rim were sharp as nailheads, and the last dragon looked at me with both eyes open.

"Well, archive," she said. "Ask me my name."

"Emberlow," I said. "Last of the fire-witnesses. And you're going to fly us to the garden."

Something that was almost a laugh moved the ground under us.

"First," she said, rising, wings unfolding like the unrolling of a scorched scroll, "we eat. Flying on an empty stomach is undignified. And second—" the black-glass eye fixed on me, and behind it the ember-light guttered, just once, like a candle in a draft, "—second, archive, you should know that the crown already knows we're coming. I felt it turn toward us an hour ago. It has started setting a table."

The caldera filled with the sound of wings learning their work again.
