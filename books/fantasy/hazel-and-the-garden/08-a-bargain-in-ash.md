## 8. A Bargain in Ash

The caldera smelled of hot stone and old copper, and the dragon lying in the center of it was smaller than I had imagined a last dragon would be.

I don't know what I'd expected. Something vast, surely. Something that filled the sky the way a storm fills a doorway. Emberlow was big—big as a guildhall, her wings folded along her flanks like a moth's wings at rest—but she was thin. The great bones of her showed through hide gone dull and gray, and when she breathed, the sound was less like fire and more like a bellows with a split seam.

She had not moved when we climbed down into the caldera. She had not opened her eyes. But when Marlow's boot scraped against a loose stone, she spoke without lifting her head.

"Ledgermen." Her voice was ash poured over gravel. "I can smell the ink on you from here. You've come about the garden."

Marlow went very still beside me. I'll give him this: he didn't run. He just stood there in the red light of the caldera like a man who has realized the room he's standing in is on fire and is deciding whether to mention it.

"We've come," I said carefully, "because we need your fire."

A sound came out of her then—not quite a laugh, not quite a cough. Sparks drifted from her nostrils and died before they touched the ground. That frightened me more than anything else could have. A dragon's sparks should fall like embers and live. Hers died in the air, small and brief as thoughts forgotten mid-sentence.

"My fire," she said. "Yes. Everyone comes for my fire eventually. The kings wanted it for their wars. The guilds wanted it for their forges. And now a Ledgerman wants it to burn roots." Her eye opened—one eye, amber shot through with veins of black, like honey going bad. It fixed on me with an attention that made my skin feel examined down to the marrow. "And what will you pay?"

"Pay?" Marlow said. "We're asking for—"

"You are asking," Emberlow said, "for the only flame in the world that can unmake what was planted in that garden. Do you know why? Because dragon-fire doesn't burn things, little keeper. It unmakes them. It burns away what a thing *is* until only what it *was* remains. There is no other fire like it, because there is no other dragon. I am the last of a long line of creatures who were themselves bargains—the first dragons traded their hoarding instinct to the first mages in exchange for their flame, and every dragon since has been born owing a debt they spend their whole lives repaying." She shifted, and the ground shuddered faintly under us. "So. What will you pay?"

I looked at Marlow. Marlow looked at me with the expression of a man who has just remembered he left his entire fortune somewhere else.

"I have coin," I said. "Not much. Some ledger-rights. The guild owns certain—"

"I don't want your coin." Her head lifted, finally, and even half-starved as she was, the motion made the whole caldera feel smaller. "Coin is nothing. Coin is what people trade when they have nothing real to trade. I want—" and here her voice did something terrible, something I wasn't prepared for. It wavered. "I am forgetting, Ledgerman. Do you understand what that means, for a creature like me? Dragons do not forget. That is our nature, as much as fire. We are the memory the world keeps of its own wildness. And I am forgetting my hoard. I am forgetting my mother's voice. This morning I tried to remember the name of the mountain where I hatched, and I found a hole where it should be, smooth-sided, like a stone worn by water."

The black veins in her eye pulsed once, slowly.

"The Verdant feeds on memory. Did your archives tell you that? It has been feeding for a century, and it does not care whose memory it takes. King or dragon, it all tastes the same. I have been flying wider and wider circles trying to stay ahead of its hunger, and I am losing. In a year, perhaps two, there will be no last dragon. Only a very large animal that has forgotten how to burn."

* * *

We sat with her while the light in the caldera changed from red to violet. She told us about the hoard—what remained of it. Not gold; dragons' true hoards were never gold, whatever the songs say. Memories. First flights. First fires. The taste of a century's worth of storms. She had been born into a hoard three thousand years deep, and now she kept it the way a miser keeps coins in a leaking purse, counting them nightly, finding fewer each time.

"It's the forgetting that frightens me most," she said, quieter now. "Not death. Death is just the last page. But to die having already lost the book—that is the thing I cannot bear."

And that was when I understood what she wanted, before she said it, and felt my stomach drop through the caldera floor.

"No," I said.

"You haven't heard the price yet."

"I know the price. I'm a Ledgerman. We invented this price."

Her good eye gleamed. "Then say it anyway. Say it so we both know what we're agreeing to."

I took out the Ledger. It sat in my hands heavier than usual, the way it always did lately, as if the entries inside had gained weight since the vanishing began. "You want me to record your memories. All of them. To carry them in the guild's keeping, so that when your fire fails—when *you* fail—there's still a copy of you somewhere in the world."

"Not a copy." She said it sharply, and a spark leapt from her jaw and cracked against the stone. "An archive. There's a difference, and your guild used to know it. A copy is a thing taken. An archive is a thing *kept*. Held in trust. Read back to the world when the original is gone." She lowered her great head until her eye was level with mine, close enough that I could see myself reflected in the black-veined amber, small and pale and sweating. "I will carry you to the garden. I will burn the roots when the time comes, if there is enough of me left to burn. And in exchange, when my fire goes out, you will keep me. Every flight. Every fire. Every name I'm about to lose. You will be my archive, Hazel Delacroix, and you will read me back to the world for as long as you live."

"That's—" Marlow started.

"A bargain freely made," Emberlow finished. "Yes. I know what I'm saying. Do you?"

* * *

Marlow pulled me aside, out of the dragon's hearing—which, given that her hearing could probably catch a whisper across a valley, meant he walked us a long way up the caldera rim and still talked mostly with his hands.

"You see it, don't you," he hissed. "Tell me you see it."

"See what?"

"*This.*" He gestured back down at the enormous gray shape at the caldera's heart. "This is exactly how it started. The Verdant. Don't you remember what the archives said—what *I* told you? A famine. A desperate guild. A bargain freely made, seed and soil, abundance promised in exchange for—something. Nobody wrote down what, because nobody thought they needed to. It was a gift! It was a kindness! And a hundred years later the king is chained in a cellar and half the kingdom can't remember its children's faces." He grabbed my sleeve. "Every catastrophe in our history starts with someone saying *it's a bargain freely made*, Hazel. That phrase should come with a warning label."

"And what's the alternative?" I asked. "We walk away? She dies forgetting, the garden stands, the king stays a puppet, and everyone in the realm keeps trading pieces of themselves to a plant?" I shook him off, gently. "There's no clean option, Marlow. There hasn't been one since my grandmother signed whatever she signed. There's just the least rotten choice available."

He stared at me. Then he laughed—a short, unhappy sound with no humor in it anywhere.

"You sound like her," he said. "Your grandmother. I never met her, but the old archivists talked. *Elsbeth Delacroix always found the least rotten choice, and we always paid for it later.*" He turned away, rubbing his face with both hands. "Fine. Fine. But hear me, and hear me clearly: I will help you make this bargain. And when it goes wrong—and it will go wrong, Hazel, someday, some way, in a form none of us can predict—I will be the one standing next to you saying *I told you*. That's my price. Not coin. Not memory. Just the right to say I told you so, forever, free of charge."

"That's the worst deal I've ever heard."

"I learned from the best." He nodded back down toward the dragon. "Go on, then. Make your bargain. And Hazel—" he caught my arm one more time, and his voice dropped, and for once there was no wryness in it at all, "—when she gives you those memories, watch yourself. Memory carried isn't memory owned. If you start feeling like the dragon, if her flights start feeling like *your* flights—you tell me. Promise me."

I promised him. I even meant it.

That's the thing about promises. They're so easy to mean, right up until the moment they matter.

* * *

Emberlow watched me come down the slope with both eyes open now, and I saw that the second eye had cleared somewhat—or perhaps she was simply spending strength to seem less diminished than she was. Dragons, I suspect, lie about their weakness the way old soldiers lie about their wounds. Bravado as camouflage.

"The archivist disapproves," she said.

"The archivist thinks every bargain is the first bargain of the apocalypse."

"He may be right." She settled her chin onto her foreclaws, which brought her eye level with my chest. "It changes nothing. Will you do it?"

I looked at her for a long moment. At the black veins threading through the amber. At the hollows above her eyes where the flesh had fallen away. At the place on her flank where scales had been shed and not regrown, leaving skin like old parchment.

"Why me?" I asked. "Why not take your memories to the guild itself? The vaults. The deep archive. Let the whole guildhold carry you."

"Because the guild is dying, Keeper Delacroix. I have watched it for a century from the air. Your elders trade in shadows and winters now because no one trusts them with anything larger. Your ledgers erase themselves. By the time my fire fails, there may be no guild at all—only you." Her breath stirred my hair, warm and smelling of cinders. "Besides. There is another reason. One I will confess only because a bargain should begin honestly."

"Which is?"

"When the Verdant takes a memory, it consumes it. When your guild *trades* a memory, it holds it hostage—collateral against a debt. But a memory *given*—" and here her voice dropped to almost nothing, a rumble I felt in my ribs more than heard, "—a memory given freely is a strange thing, little keeper. I have lived three thousand years, and I have seen it happen only twice. Given memory does not sit in the mind the way owned memory does. It roots differently. It cannot be taken the same way. I do not fully understand it. Neither did the mages who studied such things, before the Verdant ate them." Her eye held mine. "If I must lose everything, I would rather lose it to a keeper who understands the difference between a debt and a gift. Even if he only half-understands it. Even if he learns the rest too late."

"That's a lot of faith to put in a stranger."

"You're not a stranger. You're Elsbeth Delacroix's grandson. I knew her." Something moved behind the amber—grief, maybe, or its fossil. "She came to me once, sixty years ago, the night before she bound the Verdant. She asked me to burn the garden down. I refused. I told her the roots ran too deep, that my fire would kill the king along with the plant, that the cost was too high." The black veins pulsed. "She went and did it anyway, her way, with paper and blood instead of fire. And it worked—for sixty years. So perhaps I owe her. Or perhaps I simply owe the truth: that the least rotten choice was mine to make, and I chose wrong, and a kingdom has been paying interest ever since."

I didn't know what to say to that. So I did the only thing a Ledgerman can do when words fail.

I opened the book.

* * *

The recording took the rest of the night.

It was unlike any entry I had ever made. Normally a trade is quick—a memory surrendered, terms written, signatures pressed. This was a slow pouring. Emberlow lay with her head flat on the stone and her eye half-closed, and she gave me her memories one at a time, speaking them aloud in a voice that grew rougher as the hours passed, and as she spoke, I wrote.

Or rather—the writing happened *through* me. My hand moved and I barely guided it. The Ledger drank her in. I felt each memory arrive the way you feel rain arrive on dry ground: a darkening, a weight, a sudden richness. Her first flight—a cliff in a country that no longer exists, wind screaming over her wings, her mother's shadow racing hers along the valley floor. Her first fire—a sneeze, actually, an accident, that incinerated her mother's favorite reading chair, and the shame of it, and the laughter afterward. The names of mountains. The taste of lightning. Three thousand years of hoard, pouring through a boy from a guildhall cellar who had never flown anywhere at all.

Somewhere past midnight, I noticed something that stopped my pen.

I could smell the cliff. The nonexistent cliff, in the country that no longer exists. Salt wind and sun-warmed rock. I could feel the wind screaming over wings I did not have.

"Hazel." Marlow's voice, sharp with alarm. He'd been watching from the rim, and now he was scrambling down the slope. "Hazel, look at me. Whose memory is that?"

"Mine," I said—and then caught myself, horrified. "*Hers.* It's hers. I'm just—it's in the book, I'm only reading it, I'm only—"

But even as I said it, I could see the valley. I could feel my mother's shadow racing mine.

Emberlow opened one eye. "It begins," she said softly. "As I said. Given memory roots differently, little keeper. It is making itself at home in you." And there was something in her voice that might have been apology, or might have been relief, or might have been both wearing the same coat.

Marlow crouched in front of me, gripping my shoulders. "Is it taking you over? Are you still in there?"

"I'm still in here." And I was. That was the strange part. The flight-memory hadn't pushed *me* out; it had simply moved in alongside, the way a guest moves into a house—politely, unpacking its things, hanging its coat by the door. It was mine and not mine, and I could feel the seam between us, thin but present. "I'm fine. I think. Keep talking to me. If I start calling you by dragon-names, worry."

"You have dragon-names?"

"I have *her* dragon-names. Apparently." I flexed my hand. The pen had carved a groove in my finger I didn't remember making. "Keep going," I told Emberlow. "We're doing this. All of it. Tonight."

She gave, and I wrote, and the sky over the caldera went from black to bruise to the pale gray of a morning that neither of us was sure we'd live to see. By dawn, the Ledger was heavier than any book has a right to be—not in the hand, but in some other sense, the way a promise is heavy. Three thousand years of dragon slept between its covers, and part of that dragon was asleep in me too.

Emberlow rose at first light. It took her a long time—her joints crackled like green wood in a fire, and she had to push herself up in stages—but rise she did, and when her wings spread across the caldera, blotting out the sunrise, I forgot, briefly, that she was dying. Whatever else hunger had taken from her, it had not taken the *shape* of her. She was still, unmistakably, a dragon: the oldest argument the world had ever made against its own smallness.

"Climb on," she said. "Behind the shoulder blades, where the scales grow thick. Hold the ridge-bone and nothing else. And Keeper Delacroix—" her head swung around, and her amber eye found mine, and in it I saw the reflection of a boy holding a book full of somebody else's life, "—one more term of the bargain. When we reach the garden, and the burning comes, and you are afraid—and you will be afraid—remember that you are carrying my fire in more ways than one. What I gave you cannot be consumed. Remember that, when the Verdant offers you everything you've ever lost."

"How do you know it will offer?"

"Because it offered me," she said. "Sixty years ago. It knows what every creature wants, and it trades in exactly that." She turned away, spreading her wings to catch the morning wind. "Now hold tight. I have not flown in nine years, and I intend to make this flight one worth remembering."

She launched.

The caldera fell away beneath us like a coin dropped down a well, and the wind screamed over my ears, and somewhere in the Ledger against my chest, a memory of exactly this moment settled into place beside all the others—warm, alive, and already rooting.

Below us, far to the south, past the borrowed villages and the forgetting roads, the garden waited.

And I couldn't shake the thought—riding the wind, clutching a dragon's spine, three thousand years of someone else's life humming in my blood—that Marlow was right.

Every catastrophe begins with a bargain freely made.

I just hoped, this once, the world would let us be the exception.
