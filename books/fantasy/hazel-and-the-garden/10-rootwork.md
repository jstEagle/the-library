## 10. Rootwork

I have been a coward for so long that I had begun to mistake it for a profession.

There is a particular skill to it, the way there is to bookbinding or lock-picking. You learn the angles. You learn which silences to hold and which to fill. You learn that the safest lie is the one you tell while looking directly at the truth—*yes, I'm following Hazel Delacroix, but only to protect him; yes, I sent word to the palace, but only to buy us time.* A coward with good posture can pass for a pragmatist in almost any light.

I should know. I've been passing for thirty years.

Let me start where all confessions should start: with the stranger.

His name was Aldous Vane, though he hadn't used it aloud in years by the time he came stumbling into my archive above the tanneries in Lower Wren. He was already burned by then—not the way you'd think, not fire-burned, but *erased*. Patches of him simply missing. He'd look at his own hands like they belonged to a colleague he'd once shared an office with. He'd start sentences and lose the middle of them and arrive at the end anyway, arriving everywhere slightly wrong.

He came to me because I was the best archivist the guild ever fired, and being fired by the Ledgermen is a distinction of its own kind. It means you read too much. It means you found the scorched gap in the century records and didn't have the sense to stop looking.

Aldous had been to the garden. That's what he told me, in the fragments between the missing pieces. He'd seen what grew there. He'd seen what it was *for*. And he'd made himself a map of wax and dragon-scale—the only material that could hold a record of the Verdant without the Verdant reading it back, without the thing in the crown plucking the route out of his head like a thread from a sleeve.

"I need it retrieved," he said. "Or if not retrieved—" and here he smiled with half a face, "—delivered."

Delivered to whom? I asked. Delivered where?

"To whoever still has a spine," he said. "The guild has none left. I've checked the inventory."

So we became partners, Aldous and I, in the way that two men become partners when one of them is dying and the other one is cheap. He taught me the wax-map's grammar. He taught me about the binding, about the king, about the roots. And then, three weeks before he walked into the guildhall doorway to die, the palace found me.

They always find you. That's the first thing they teach you in the capital, though no one says it aloud. The crown has fingers everywhere, and its fingers are usually men who owe it something. In my case it was simpler than owing. They had my sister's name on a list. Debtors' lists, memory-debt, the kind you can't work off—they'd bought her years against her will during the grain riots, back when the court decided that punishing a district meant thinning its futures. One signature from me and she'd be restored. Whole. Remembering our mother's face again.

You would have signed too. Don't flatter yourself that you wouldn't. Everyone is brave in the hypothetical.

So I reported. Weekly, at first—a dead-drop behind the chandler's on Cordwainer Street, notes written in the ledger-shorthand they don't teach outside the guild anymore. Then Aldous died in the doorway and put the map into the youngest Delacroix's hands, and my reports got more interesting, and the payments got heavier, and my sister got more whole, and I got very good at not thinking about any of it as a single connected thing.

That's the coward's true craft, if you want it named: compartmentalization. You keep the betrayal in one room and the guilt in another and the rationalizations in a third, and you walk through the house of yourself quickly, with the lamps lit just enough to find the next door.

For six weeks I walked Hazel Delacroix through borrowed country and wrote home about every step.

*The boy trades memories to villagers. Small ones. His own. He doesn't seem to understand what he's spending.*

*We've reached the caldera. There's a dragon. This was not in the brief.*

*The dragon is dying. The boy has agreed to carry her memories. Marlow advises caution. Marlow advises nothing. Marlow wants his payment doubled.*

And then we came to the garden, and the house of myself burned down.

* * *

You cannot see the garden's walls until you're nearly upon them, which is the first dishonesty of the place. The road bends around a shoulder of grey hill, and suddenly there they are—white stone gone green at the seams, high as a cathedral's ambitions—and beyond them a glow like late afternoon that has forgotten how to end.

But the walls aren't the garden. I understood that the moment I put my palm flat against the stone.

It was warm. Not sun-warm. *Pulse*-warm. And beneath my hand, deep under the earth, something moved—not the soil shifting, not water, but a rhythm, slow and vast, the way you feel a train coming through the floor of a station before you hear it.

"Hazel," I said, and my voice came out smaller than I intended. "How deep do you think the roots go?"

He looked at me with that expression he has, the young-keeper expression, the one that makes me feel simultaneously ancient and fraudulent. "Why?"

"Because I think we're standing on it."

We walked the wall's perimeter to find a gate, and the further we walked, the worse my certainty grew. Every hundred paces I stopped and pressed my hand to the stone, and every time, the pulse was there—stronger, or stranger, or angled differently, like a heartbeat heard through different rooms of an enormous house. Except a house has edges. This did not. By the time we'd circled halfway around the hill, I had stopped pretending to myself that the pulse was confined to the garden at all.

It was underneath everything. Under the road we'd traveled for six weeks. Under the villages where mothers couldn't remember their children's faces. Under the capital itself—I was sure of it now, sure in the marrow—under the throne room where I'd sat across from a man with kind eyes and a clerk's pen and sold my weekly reports, all of it threaded, all of it rooted, all of it *fed*.

"Hazel." I made myself say it plainly, because plainly was the only currency I had left. "I need to tell you something, and after I tell it, you may decide you'd rather travel with a snake."

* * *

I told him all of it. The dead-drop. The payments. My sister's name on the debtors' list, and the signature I'd given to get her back. Aldous Vane and the map and how I'd sworn to retrieve it for the crown, and how instead I'd let a dying man press it into a boy's hands and followed the boy like a faithful dog, writing home the whole way.

He listened without interrupting, which was somehow worse than shouting. When I finished, he was quiet for a long moment, looking at the white wall and the impossible afternoon-glow beyond it.

"My grandmother," he said finally. "The entries. The burn scar in the archives. Did you know? Before we started—did you know about her?"

"No." And this, at least, was clean. "Aldous never said a name. If he'd known the map would land in a Delacroix's hands, he might have chosen differently. Or he might have chosen exactly this. He was cryptic near the end. Near the middle, too, honestly."

"And your reports. The last one. What did you tell them?"

"That we'd found a dragon." I swallowed. "That we were making for the garden."

Hazel nodded slowly. "Then they'll be waiting for us."

"They were always going to be waiting for us. The question was never whether I'd betray you, Hazel. The question is whether I stop."

"Why would you?" he asked, and there was no cruelty in it, only the genuine curiosity of someone who has watched me haggle over inn prices and knows precisely what I'm worth.

I opened my mouth to give him the answer I'd prepared—the sister, the debt, the arithmetic of one life weighed against a kingdom—and found that the arithmetic wouldn't come. Because I was still touching the wall, you see. Still feeling the pulse. And somewhere in the last hour, without my permission, the compartments in the house of myself had caught fire.

"Because I put my hand on the wall," I said, "and the wall has a heartbeat, and the heartbeat is under the whole country, and I have spent thirty years telling myself I was a small man doing small damage in a large machine. But I'm standing on the machine, Hazel. We all are. It's not large. It's *everywhere*, and it's hungry, and I fed it directions to the only people trying to dig it out."

* * *

Here is what I did next, and I record it precisely because I am, whatever else, an archivist, and archivists believe that exact testimony is the nearest thing to absolution the world offers.

I took out my report-kit—the wax tablets, the shorthand stylus, the little vial of palace-sealed ink that smells of lavender and threat—and I broke them. All of them. Snapped the tablets and scattered the wax and poured the ink into the dirt at the garden's wall, where it sank in fast, faster than ink should sink, as if the ground were thirsty for confessions specifically.

Then I did the second thing, the harder thing. I showed Hazel the drop-point protocol. Every location, every cipher, every sign and counter-sign the palace handlers used with me. Because a betrayal isn't undone by remorse; it's undone by making it unusable. If they came looking for their weekly letter and found silence, fine. But if they came looking and found a double agent feeding them pretty lies—well. I'd been a coward long enough to know that cowards make excellent liars, and liars, pointed at the right target, are nearly useful.

"You could still run," Hazel said. "Take your sister and go somewhere the pulse doesn't reach."

"There is nowhere the pulse doesn't reach. That's rather the point." I looked up at the wall, at the glow seeping over the top like honey off a spoon. "Besides. I've spent my whole life retrieving things for other people. I'd like to try delivering one, for once. To whom it was actually addressed."

He almost smiled. Then his face changed—that keeper's focus coming down over him like a shutter—and he pressed his own palm flat to the stone beside mine.

"It's stronger here," he murmured. "Under the wall. Marlow... these aren't garden roots. Look at the direction they run."

I looked, though looking meant closing my eyes and reading the pulse the way I'd once read ledgers, by pressure and pattern. And there it was, unmistakable now that I let myself see it: the roots didn't spread outward from the garden like a tree's. They *converged* inward from everywhere—from the capital, from the villages, from the whole wound-open country—flowing toward the garden the way blood returns to a heart.

Except hearts pump. This only drank.

"The garden isn't growing into the kingdom," I said slowly. "The kingdom is being pulled into the garden. Everything it takes—every year, every name, every mother's face—it brings it *here*."

"Then my grandmother's memories really are in there." Hazel's voice had gone very quiet. "Still alive. Still growing."

"Along with everyone else's. Along with, I'd wager, a certain dragon's hoard, and possibly a king's entire reign." I straightened up, and my knees cracked, and I was abruptly, enormously tired in the way I imagine soldiers are tired—not of the fighting but of having pretended, for years, that they weren't at war. "Well. I've confessed, destroyed my instruments, and committed treason in ink. I believe the customary next step is to break into paradise."

"The gate's on the north side," Hazel said. "According to the map's deeper layer."

"Of course it is. Nothing about this has ever been simple." I picked up my pack, and found my hands were shaking, and noted—for the record, since the record is all I have—that they were shaking less than they had in thirty years.

We came around the hill's shoulder as the false afternoon dimmed toward a false evening, and there stood the gate: black iron grown through with pale root-fiber, thin as hair, thick as rope, pulsing faintly with that vast buried hunger. And set into the gate, worn smooth by decades of hands that presumably never came back, was a plaque. Old guild-work. Pre-scorch. I knew the letterforms the way you know your mother's handwriting.

It read:

**ALL DEBTS SETTLED WITHIN. ENTER AND BE AT REST.**

Hazel read it twice. Then he looked at me, and I looked at him, and neither of us said what we were both thinking, which was that the Verdant had learned to write in the guild's own voice—and that somewhere inside those walls, blooming on some patient tree, my grandmother's laugh was waiting for him like bait on a hook.

I pushed the gate open first. Cowardice, it turns out, makes an excellent habit even in repentance: I have always been good at going first into rooms where I expect to be punished.

The garden breathed out at us, warm and sweet and full of other people's happiest days.

And far beneath our feet, the roots tightened, like a fist closing gently around everything we loved.
