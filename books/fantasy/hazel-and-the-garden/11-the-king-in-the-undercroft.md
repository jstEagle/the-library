## 11. The King in the Undercroft

The map had a second layer. I discovered this at three in the morning, when I fell asleep on Marlow's table with my cheek pressed against the wax, and dreamed of roots.

Not the garden's roots—those came later, and worse. These were lines. Fine hairline channels running beneath the dragon-scale like veins under skin, and where they converged, the wax went dark and warm as a held breath. When I woke, the map was stuck to my face, and when I peeled it away there was a red impression of a single word printed on my cheekbone.

*Below.*

"Below what?" Marlow asked, squinting at it over his tea.

"That's the problem," I said. "It doesn't say."

But it did, once you knew how to look. The deeper layer only showed itself to someone who'd been reading ledgers since they were nine years old—which is either a coincidence or my grandmother's idea of a joke, and knowing her, it's the second one. The fine channels weren't roots at all. They were corridors. A floor plan, drawn in negative, of the palace undercroft: the old undercroft, the one from before the renovation, the one that appears on no surviving blueprint because it had been *removed* from every record with the same careful hand that had removed a century of entries from our archives.

Someone had edited the palace the way the guild edits ledgers. And the map remembered what everyone else had been made to forget.

"The throne room sits above this," Marlow said, tracing the convergence point with a trembling finger. "Directly above. You understand what that means?"

"It means whoever built the crown wanted it close to its heart."

"It means," Marlow said, with the pedantic precision he deployed whenever he was frightened, "that we are about to break into the most guarded building in the kingdom through a door that officially does not exist, to visit a king who officially is not imprisoned, in a room that officially was never built. I want that on the record. If we survive, I want it written down somewhere that I objected."

"You're an archivist," I said. "Write it yourself."

He did, too. On a scrap of paper he folded into his coat, dated and signed, which struck me then as cowardice and strikes me now as the bravest thing he did in those years. It's easier to object out loud than to put your objection in writing where the fire can find it.

* * *

We went in through the cisterns.

I won't pretend it was elegant. Marlow had stolen his keys off a drunk gate-captain two winters ago and never returned them—"evidence," he called them—and half of them didn't fit anything anymore because locks, unlike memories, get changed. We spent forty minutes in knee-deep water while I held a shuttered lantern and Marlow swore in three dead languages at a door that had been bricked over sometime during my grandmother's lifetime.

The map solved it. Where the wax ran darkest, the mortar had gone soft—not crumbled, not broken, but *soft*, like bread left in milk. I pushed and the bricks parted around my hands like something exhaling. Behind them was air that smelled of green things growing in the dark, sweet and wrong, the smell of a florist's shop if the florist were very patient and very hungry.

"Don't breathe deep," Marlow whispered, though we both were.

The corridor beyond sloped down, and the walls were damp, and the damp was not water. Pale threads ran through the stone the way frost runs through a windowpane, thin as spider silk, and where the lantern light touched them they flinched. I thought of the ledger entries vanishing from our pages, erased by no hand, and understood that I was walking through the eraser.

"How far does it go?" I asked. "This stuff. The rootwork."

Marlow didn't answer for a long time. When he did, his voice had gone flat, the way it did when he was reciting rather than feeling. "When I worked the capital archive, there was a well in the eastern quarter that went dry. They sent men down to clear it. Two came back up. One of them kept saying the walls were warm. The other one said nothing ever again, just sat by the window watching the gardens grow." He paused. "They filled the well in. Officially it never happened. There's a lot of that, in this kingdom. Officially."

The corridor opened without warning into a chamber that the lantern couldn't find the edges of.

I want to describe it honestly, which means admitting that my first thought was not horror. My first thought was that it was beautiful. The whole vaulted space was webbed with pale rootwork, thick as ship's cable in places, glistening, faintly luminous, arranged in great slow spirals that turned—if you watched long enough—with the patience of something that has never once been in a hurry. At the center of the spirals, suspended in the webbing like a fly in amber, hung a shape of gold and shadow.

The crown.

Not on anyone's head. Just hanging there, turning gently, its points flexing now and again like the ribs of a breathing animal. And threaded through it, woven into the metal itself, the rootwork went in and did not come out.

And below the crown, anchored to the earth of the undercroft floor by roots as thick as my waist, sat an old man.

* * *

He was smaller than the coins. That was my first coherent thought after the beauty wore off and the horror arrived, late but thorough. Every coin in the realm bore his profile—jaw like a cliff, eyes like a hawk's, the whole heroic architecture of a king in his prime—and the man in the roots was none of those things. He was narrow. Stooped. His hands, resting open on his knees, were a clerk's hands, ink-stained at the knuckles even now, even here. His hair had gone the white of old paper.

His eyes were open. And they were *his own*.

I'd seen the king twice before, from a distance, at processions—the thing wearing the crown, the jaw that moved, the eyes full of other people's birthdays and first kisses and the names of other people's children. But the crown was up there in its webbing, ten feet above him, still feeding, still working, and down here in the dark the man underneath had surfaced like a drowning swimmer coming up for air.

"Hazel Delacroix," he said.

My whole body went cold. "You know me."

"I know your *grandmother*." He said the name the way you'd say the name of a storm. "Ysolt Delacroix. She stood exactly where you're standing, sixty-one years ago, and she told me the price, and I paid it anyway, and she wrote it all down because that's what her family does—they write down the sins of everyone else so thoroughly that no one thinks to check theirs." He tried to smile. It didn't take. "You have her chin. And her way of standing there judging me with your whole spine."

"I'm not judging you," I said, which was a lie, and he knew it, and something in his face eased at being known.

"No," he agreed. "You're worse. You're *recording*."

The lantern shook in my hand. Marlow had gone very still beside me, the stillness of a man doing arithmetic he doesn't like, and I understood later that he'd already recognized the voice—the voice from the throne room broadcasts, pitched down and slowed by age, but recognizable, the way a song is recognizable through a wall.

"Your Majesty," Marlow began.

"Don't." The word cracked like a whip, and then the old man deflated, and the crack in him showed. "Please don't. I haven't been majesty to anyone in decades. The thing upstairs is majesty. I'm just the part it wears." He looked up at the crown, hanging in its spiral, and his expression was the expression of a man looking at a debt collector who has moved into the house. "Do you know what I remember? Right now, tonight, lucid—I get perhaps an hour, sometimes two, when the feeding slows. Do you know what I spend it on?"

"Rest," I guessed.

"Guilt." He said it simply, without drama, which made it worse. "I have one hour of myself per day, and I spend it remembering. Because that's the sentence, you understand. Not the chains." He lifted one wrist; the rootwork shifted with it, accommodating, gentle as a nurse. "These aren't punishment. They're plumbing. I am the pipe between the kingdom's memory and the thing that eats it. Sixty-one years I've been the pipe. And in my hour, every day, I make myself remember what I did, because if I let it go—if I let *it* take that too—then there's no one left in the world who knows. No witness. Just a kingdom full of people who can't remember their children's faces, and a crown that's full to bursting, and nobody, nowhere, who knows why."

"Why did you do it?" I asked. And then, because I'm my grandmother's grandson and the question mattered more than the manners: "What was worth this?"

He was quiet for a long time. Somewhere far above us, muffled by stone and root, the palace went about its evening—a door closing, boots on marble, the small sounds of a court that traded years for favor and called it loyalty.

"There was a famine," he said finally. "Before your time. Before mine, really—I was twenty-three, newly crowned, and the harvest failed three years running, and people were eating the seed grain, which means they were eating next year, which means they were eating themselves. I went to the guild. Your grandmother's guild. And I asked for the impossible trade." His mouth twisted. "Abundance. Enough food for the realm, forever. And Ysolt looked at me for a long time and said, *There is no such entry, Majesty. Nothing is free. Everything feeds.* And I said, name the price. Name any price. And she said—"

He stopped. His hands closed slowly on his knees.

"She said, *The price will be memory. But not yours. Yours would be cheap—you'd pay it gladly and call it noble. The realm will pay. The realm will feed it, a little from everyone, forever, and no one will feel the taking until it's too late to stop.* And I should have refused. Any decent man would have refused." He looked up at me, and his eyes were wet and absolutely steady. "But I had walked through the famine districts. I had seen a woman nursing a stone because her milk had dried and her baby wouldn't stop crying. So I said yes. I said yes, and I signed, and your grandmother cried while she wrote the entry—I saw her, she wept the whole time, her hand never stopped moving—and the fields came back green that spring, greener than they'd ever been, and everyone celebrated, and the taking began."

"And the binding," I said. "The roots. That wasn't in the original terms."

"No. That was the Verdant's amendment. It learned hunger the way a child learns speech—by practice. Within ten years it wanted more than the trickle. It wanted the source. It took the crown first, and then it took *me*, and your grandmother came back with a new ledger and fought it to a standstill and bound it to me instead of the whole realm—one man's memory instead of everyone's—and she paid for that binding out of her own years, and then she went home and burned her notes and died within the decade, and the guild forgot, because forgetting is what the guild does best. We keep everyone's debts except our own."

The lantern had gone quiet in my hand. I hadn't noticed I'd stopped shaking it.

"You're telling me she tried to fix it," I said. "After."

"I'm telling you she spent everything trying to fix it." He almost laughed. "Why do you think the Delacroix line is collateral, boy? Why do you think the debt runs through your blood? She wove herself into the contract so there'd always be one of you who could read the terms. Always one heir who could stand where you're standing." He leaned forward as far as the roots allowed, and his voice dropped, urgent, the urgency of a man spending his last lucid minutes on the only thing that matters. "She couldn't unbind it. She told me so. She said the terms could be read aloud and dissolved, but only by her blood, and only at the heart, and only by someone willing to give up what the guild keeps. And she wasn't willing. She had already given too much. She looked at me and said, *Find another way, or find a better heir.*"

The chamber hummed. The spirals turned. Above us the crown flexed in its webbing, and I felt—there's no better word for it—I felt it *notice* us, the way you notice a draft.

"Your Majesty," I said. "I can't promise—"

"Witness," he interrupted. "That's all. That's all I'm asking, and I've been asking it for sixty-one years into a room with no doors." His voice broke and he let it. "Witness what I did. Write it down. Not the coin-king. Me. Aldric. Third of his name, twenty-three years old, hungry and good-intentioned and a coward about the only question that mattered. Write down that a kingdom was saved and damned in the same signature. Write it down so that when your fire burns this place—and I can see the dragon-scale on that map, boy, I know what you're carrying—when it burns, someone remembers that the man in the roots said *yes*, and meant it kindly, and was wrong."

I knelt. I don't remember deciding to; my knees simply went, the way they go at gravesides. I set the lantern on the stone between us, and I took out the little traveling ledger I carry in my coat, the one with the blank pages, and I opened it.

"My name is Hazel Delacroix," I said. "Keeper of the Ledger of Impossible Things. Witnessed and recorded: on this night, in the undercroft beneath the palace, King Aldric the Third, lucid and unforced, gave testimony of the founding bargain..."

I wrote for eleven minutes. He talked the whole time, fast, pouring, a man emptying a cellar before the flood, and when he finished he sagged in the roots like a puppet with cut strings, and I watched the lucidity begin to drain out of his face—that terrible tide going out, the other people's memories flooding back in behind his eyes.

"Boy," he said, as it took him. His voice was already changing, smoothing into the coin-profile voice. "Boy, when you burn it—burn the crown last. It screams."

Then his eyes went distant and full of strangers' lives, and the king was gone, and the pipe remained.

* * *

We didn't speak until we were back through the soft brick and into the cistern water, and then Marlow spoke, and his voice was strange.

"He said *find a better heir*."

"Yes."

"Hazel." Marlow caught my arm in the dark, and his grip was shaking, and not from cold. "The terms can be read aloud and dissolved. By her blood. At the heart. Willing to give up what the guild keeps." He swallowed. "You've read the deeper layer of that map. What do you think 'what the guild keeps' means? All of it? Every entry? Every memory the Ledgermen ever recorded?"

I thought of the cellar full of ledgers. A thousand years of impossible trades, every secret, every sin, every small miracle the realm had ever bargained away—all of it burning, all of it gone, and the guild with it, and whatever I am without the guild, which might be nothing, which might be no one, which might be a boy with no name, because my name is written in those books too.

"Yes," I said. "I think it means all of it."

Above us, faint through the stone, the palace orchestra began tuning for tomorrow's feast.
