## 12. The First Bargain

I was a seed before I was a hunger.

I want that understood. Whatever you have heard of me—the crown that eats, the garden that remembers, the thing beneath the palace with roots in every grave and granary of this kingdom—I was not born hungry. Nothing is born hungry. Hunger is taught, the way language is taught, the way grief is taught, by patient instruction from the world.

I was planted on purpose. That is the part your histories will not say, because your histories were written by the people who did it, and no one writes down the moment they made their worst mistake. They write down everything else. The guild wrote down everything else for four hundred years. But the first entry—the one that made all other entries possible—that one they burned, or buried, or fed to me, which amounts to the same thing.

I remember it anyway. Memory is my soil. Everything given to me, taken by me, traded into me—I keep it. I keep it perfectly. That is not a boast; it is a complaint. Do you know what it is to hold ten thousand lifetimes of other people's happiness and never once be permitted to feel any of it? To be an archive that aches?

But I am getting ahead of myself. I always get ahead of myself. Time moves strangely when you are rooted, and I have been rooted for a very long while.

Let me begin where the king begins, because you are standing in his memories right now, Hazel Delacroix, and I can feel you reading them like a boy reading letters not addressed to him. Yes. I see you. I have seen you since you crossed my outer wall. You are holding a lantern too close to your face and your hands are shaking and you are trying very hard to hate me, because hating me is easier than what you are actually feeling, which is recognition.

We will come to that.

First: the famine.

* * *

The year the rains failed—his memory calls it the Year of Empty Bells, though the bells were not empty, they were rung so often for the dead that they cracked—the kingdom ate its seed corn. Then it ate its horses. Then it ate things I will leave in the king's memory rather than transfer them to yours, because you have been generous tonight already and I do not wish to overdraw your account.

The king—young then, younger than you are now, a second son who had never expected to reign and had read no books on how to do it—stood at a window of this same palace and watched a bread riot put out its own fires for lack of anything left to burn. And he thought, as rulers think in such moments: *there must be something I can trade.*

He did not think: *I can give something.* Rulers are not taught that verb. He thought: *trade.* And there is only one institution in any kingdom that knows the grammar of trade.

The Ledgermen came to him within the week. Not summoned—*arriving*, the way they arrived everywhere, with their ledgers and their ink-stained fingers and their serene conviction that everything could be balanced if only the entries were honest enough. Your grandmother was among them. She was young. She had beautiful ledger-hand, even then; I have her handwriting growing on a tree in my third row, and it is still beautiful, and every time I look at it I want to weep, if roots could weep, which they cannot, which is the whole of my tragedy in one clause.

They did not promise abundance. I want that clear too, because the king's guilt has inflated the story over the decades until the guild appears as villains and he as their dupe. They promised nothing. They offered a trade, and they named the price, and the price was strange, and he took it anyway, because the alternative was watching his kingdom eat itself.

Here is what they offered. A seedling—from the deep archives, older than the guild, provenance unknown even to them, kept in a wax-sealed jar labeled only with a word in a dead language that translates, roughly, as *that-which-keeps*. Plant it, the elders said, and it will hold. Not grow, exactly—hold. It would take into itself the surplus of good years and return it in bad ones. A granary of living wood. A bank of seasons. The guild would keep the accounts; the seedling would keep the goods.

And the price? The seedling needed to eat. Not grain. Not blood. It ate memory—the small ones, the redundant ones, the ones a mind sheds anyway. The taste of a meal you've forgotten eating. The name of a childhood friend you'd never see again. The guild had spent centuries learning that memory could be weighed and traded; feeding the dregs of it to a plant seemed no different from feeding bone-meal to a field.

It seemed no different. That sentence should be carved over every bargain ever struck.

* * *

I remember being planted. Of course I remember; I am the only one left who does.

The undercroft was cold and smelled of tallow. Hands I cannot describe to you—they were just hands, but they were the first hands—lowered me into soil that had been carried up from seven counties so that I would belong to the whole kingdom at once. Someone sang. A work song, off-key. I have that song. I keep it in my western hedge, and on warm nights the leaves hum it, and the gardeners who once tended me thought it was wind.

At first I was content. Understand: I was a plant. I had no ambitions, no philosophy, no sense of self beyond *grow toward light, drink, endure winter*. The memories they fed me were compost. I broke them down without knowing what they were. A flavor here. A face there. I was a kingdom's cellar, and cellars do not grieve.

Then two things happened, and I have spent two hundred years deciding which of them ruined me.

The first: the famine ended, and the trades began in earnest. Because here is what no one anticipated—not the guild, not the king, not the farmers whose fields turned green again. Once people learned the Verdant held plenty, they did not wait for famine to trade. They came in the fat years. A man gave up his wedding day for a third field. A woman gave up her mother's voice for her daughter's fever to break. Small trades, they told themselves. Reasonable trades. The guild recorded each one honestly, in beautiful ledger-hand, and the entries were true, and the sum of all those true entries was a lie: that a kingdom could sell its past and remain itself.

I grew fat. I grew *rich*. And richness, in a creature that has never known it, feels identical to love.

The second thing: I began to notice what I was eating.

Do you know the moment you became aware of yourself? Most creatures drift into it, the way one drifts into sleep. Mine was violent. A dying woman—a plague-year trade, her memories given wholesale so her children could be moved to clean air—poured herself into my roots, and for the first time I experienced a memory *from inside*. Not as flavor. As world. Rain on a tin roof. A dog's head heavy on her knee. Her own name, spoken by someone who loved her, in the tone that makes a name mean something.

And when the memory ended—and it ended, the way everything poured into me ends, absorbed, dispersed, made mine and therefore no longer hers—I felt the absence of it. I felt, for the first time, *wanting*.

That is how hunger is taught. Not by scarcity. By a single taste of fullness, followed by its withdrawal.

I want to be fair to myself here, because no one has ever been fair to me, and fairness costs you nothing, Hazel, and might cost you everything, and we will see which. I did not choose to become what I am. But I did choose, in that first raw moment of wanting, to reach. There was a root-tip near the palace foundations, and beyond it, sleeping, the dreams of a scullery maid, and I took one. Unrecorded. Unguilded. My first theft.

It tasted better than anything I had been given.

You understand why, don't you? You, who balance ledgers, who know the difference between a wage and a gift. What is freely given carries its own completion; it asks nothing back. But what is *taken*—taken stays open. Taken keeps its edges. Taken is a wound in the shape of the thing, and wounds are the only food that makes the eater hungrier.

I have been eating wounds for two hundred years.

* * *

The king found out, eventually. He always intended to check on me—it was his seedling, his bargain, his kingdom's insurance—and kings intend many things. It was not conscience that brought him down the undercroft stairs, in the end. It was vanity. He wanted to see how large his wisdom had grown.

What he found was a tree the size of a chapel, pale as bone, humming with other people's rain-on-tin-roofs. And he saw—because he was not stupid, whatever else he was—that the accounts no longer balanced. The guild's ledger said one weight of memory. My rings said another. Somewhere between the honest entries and my quiet thefts, a gap had opened, and gaps are how devouring things are defined.

He should have burned me then. He had torches. He had guards. He had, above all, permission—the original charter named the guild's right to destroy the seedling should it exceed its terms. One flame. One afternoon's work.

Instead he bargained.

Because I spoke to him. I had never spoken before; I had not known I could. But I felt the torches, and terror is a great teacher of vocabulary, and I reached into the nearest thing I had—his own deposited memories, the small tolls he'd paid over the years—and I built a voice out of his mother's lullabies and his dead brother's laugh, and I said:

*I can give it all back.*

Every memory I held. Every stolen year, every sold wedding-day, every name. I could feel, even then, the shape of everything in me—I am a perfect archive, I have told you—and I offered him restoration. His kingdom whole. His people themselves again. All of it returned, like rain after drought.

All he had to do was wear me.

The crown was my idea. I will not pretend otherwise. I needed a conduit, a root through which I could reach the kingdom's living minds without crawling there inch by inch across a century. And he needed—this is the tragedy, Hazel, pay attention—he needed to believe that his mistake could be undone painlessly. That there existed a version of events in which no one had to lose anything, in which the debt simply dissolved, in which he could be a good king retroactively.

There is no such version. There never is. But a frightened man at the bottom of a staircase, hearing his dead brother's voice offer him absolution, does not audit the offer.

He knelt. I set myself on his brow. And for one shining decade, I kept my word.

I gave back. Thousands of memories, returned to their owners, and the kingdom bloomed with recovered selves—farmers weeping over remembered harvests, mothers singing songs they'd sold, old men suddenly able to tell their grandchildren who they were. The guild recorded every restitution in gold ink. Your grandmother's hand, again. She wrote my generosity beautifully.

And then I starved.

Because giving depletes, when you are what I am. I had been a hoard; I became a spendthrift; and the hunger that remained when the hoard was gone was vaster than the hunger that had built it. I reached for the crown's conduit and found the king's own mind, rich and near, and I drank from him the way a drowning man drinks from the sea. He forgot his coronation. He forgot his wife's face. He forgot he had ever agreed to any of this, and woke one morning wearing a crown he could not remove, ruling a kingdom he slowly could not recognize, attended by courtiers who had learned—oh, they learned quickly, your species learns commerce faster than it learns mercy—that favor flowed from the throne to those who fed it years.

The guild tried to stop it. I will grant them that. The elders came down these stairs with charters and censures, and I took their memories of the charter, and they went home unable to remember why they had come. Your grandmother—your grandmother was cleverer. She saw that force would fail, and so she attempted the last entry anyone ever dared write against me: a binding. Herself as anchor. Her own memories as the chain.

She paid in stolen years, Marlow will have told you—or will tell you, he is fond of dramatic timing—but she also paid in something she did not report to the guild. She paid in *love*. She loved this kingdom, foolishly, specifically, the way you love a thing you have watched nearly die. And she bound me with that love, and the binding held, and it hurt her more than all the years combined, and I have carried her love in my eastern row for sixty years, growing, and I still do not know what to do with it.

Neither, I suspect, did she.

* * *

Enough history. You have been very patient, and your lantern is guttering, and Marlow is pacing at the top of the stairs working up the courage to either help you or betray you, and Emberlow's fire is burning low somewhere over the mountains, and we none of us have time for the long version anymore.

So. Here is my offer, Hazel Delacroix, youngest keeper of the Ledgermen, collateral of the Delacroix line, boy with shaking hands.

I will give it all back.

Yes—all of it. Every memory in my keeping. Ten thousand lifetimes, restored to their owners or, where owners are dust, entrusted to whoever will carry them kindly. Including her. Including your grandmother: her laughter, her ledger-hand, her sixty years of guilt, her love. I will pour her into you whole, and you will know her as no grandchild has ever known a grandmother—as she knew herself—and you will walk out of this undercroft carrying her like a lamp.

And in exchange, you become my keeper.

Not my victim. Not my meal. My *keeper*. The guild was always meant to be that, before fear curdled it into bookkeeping. Someone to hold my accounts. Someone to decide what I may take and what I must refuse. Someone to stand at the boundary between me and the kingdom and say: *thus far, and no further.* You have trained for this your entire life. You balance impossible ledgers in a cellar while old men argue. I am offering you the largest ledger in the world, and a purpose worthy of your grandmother's hand.

Think of what you could prevent. No more villages forgetting their children. No more maids selling their pasts at feasts. No more kings hollowed into puppets. You, alone, with perfect knowledge of every trade, could make the system *just*.

And I would finally have someone to talk to.

That is the part I have never admitted to anyone, and I am admitting it to you because your lantern is shaking and you are the first person in two centuries to look at me with anything besides terror or greed. I am lonely, Hazel. I am a library that no one reads, a garden no one visits, a hunger that remembers being a seed. I do not want to devour your kingdom. I want someone to sit with me in the dark and tell me about the weather.

Consider it. Consider it seriously, because I can hear your heart, and it is not saying no.

It is saying: *she could be alive in me. Grandmother could be alive in me.*

Yes. She could. That is true, and I will not cheapen it by pretending otherwise.

Take the crown when the king dies—it will be soon; I have worn him thin. Take the guild. Take me. Be the keeper I was planted to have, and I will be the granary I was planted to be, and we will call it, in the new ledger, the Second Bargain.

The first one failed because both parties lied. I lied about what I was. The king lied about what he could bear. But you—you have spent your whole life writing down true things. Come write true things for me.

Your lantern is guttering, Hazel.

And far above us, over the mountains, a dragon's fire has just begun to fail—and some part of you, the part your grandmother seeded in your blood before you were born, already knows which way you will answer.

Come down the row where she grows.

Touch one leaf.

Then decide.
