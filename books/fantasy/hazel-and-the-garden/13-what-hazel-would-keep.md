## 13. What Hazel Would Keep

I want to tell you that I resisted. That I stood at the garden's gate with my jaw set and my ledger clutched to my chest like a shield, and I said *no* in a voice that would have made my grandmother proud.

I didn't. I said nothing at all. I walked in.

That's the thing about the Verdant's offer—about any offer dressed as mercy—it doesn't need your answer. It only needs your footsteps.

* * *

The garden had no business being beautiful. That was my first thought, and it was a coward's thought, because beauty was exactly its business. Beauty was the hook. Every flower in those rows was somebody's best day, and the Verdant had arranged them with the care of a guildsman balancing a ledger—tall blooms in back, low ones in front, colors sequenced so the eye traveled the whole bed without ever wanting to leave it.

The air hummed. Not with bees—with moments. A wedding somewhere on the left, all gold light and nervous laughter. A mother's voice reading aloud, further down, the words too soft to catch but the cadence unmistakable. Somewhere near the wall, the smell of bread baking in a kitchen that hadn't existed for sixty years.

Marlow hung back at the gate like a man at the edge of deep water. "Hazel." His voice had gone thin. "We came for the undercroft. The map's deeper layer. We didn't come for—" he gestured at everything, helplessly, "—this."

"We came through the garden," I said. "It's on the map."

"It's *on* the map," Marlow agreed. "That's not the same as *in* it."

Emberlow had refused to enter at all. She lay along the outer wall like a ridge of dying coals, her scales dull, one enormous eye tracking me through the gate slats. When I'd looked back at her, she'd said only: *"Count your steps, little keeper. The garden will count them for you if you forget to."*

I should have listened. Keepers are supposed to be good at listening; it's most of the trade. But the air smelled of growing things, of loam and green and rain that hadn't fallen yet, and after weeks of borrowed country—of villages full of people staring at their own children like strangers—the smell of a place where nothing had been forgotten was a kind of drug.

So I walked the rows.

* * *

Here is what I told myself, and I record it now precisely because I'm ashamed of how reasonable it sounded: *I am not accepting. I am surveying. A keeper surveys.*

The first row was strangers' memories. I could feel them the way you feel heat from an oven—not mine, but warm. A boy's first swim in a river gone cold since. An old man's last look at his wife across a breakfast table, both of them knowing. I kept walking. Those weren't mine to want.

The second row was closer to home. Guild memories. I recognized the handwriting before I understood what I was recognizing—a trellis of white flowers grown from a single entry, the one where old Keeper Voss traded his retirement years for the guild's survival during the famine audits. I'd read that entry a hundred times. Here it was *growing*, and standing among the blooms I could feel Voss's relief like sunlight on my face, could taste the terrible peace of a man who has finally paid everything he owes.

I stayed longer than I meant to. That's how it works. You don't fall into the garden; you linger into it.

"Hazel." Marlow's voice, from several rows back. "The sun's moving."

"The sun always moves," I called back, and heard how dreamy my own voice sounded, and didn't care.

* * *

The tree was in the center of the garden, and of course it was. My grandmother's memories grew on it the way fruit grows—low enough to pick, heavy enough to bend the branches, each one wrapped in a skin of pale gold that pulsed faintly, like something breathing.

I knew them before I touched them. Not their contents—I couldn't have named a single one—but their *weight*. Every keeper learns to weigh memory by hand; it's half our craft. And these weighed like her. Like the ledger she'd kept in that sharp slanted hand. Like the letters she'd written my father, which I'd found as a boy in a box marked *DO NOT OPEN UNTIL YOU CAN FORGIVE HER*.

My father never opened the box. He died with it sealed. I'd carried it to the guildhall cellar and put it on a shelf, and I had never once, in eleven years, walked past it without touching it.

I reached up and picked the first fruit.

* * *

Her laugh.

Not a description of her laugh—the laugh itself, whole, arriving in my chest like a bird coming home. It was the laugh from the kitchen on Ledger Street, the one that started as a cough and broke into something wild, the one that made the neighbors complain and made me, aged six, feel like the luckiest child in a city built on debts. She was laughing at something my grandfather had said. I never knew what he'd said. In the memory I was too young to understand the joke, and so the joke stays forever just out of reach, and the laughter stays forever perfect, and I stood under that tree with a piece of fruit going soft in my hand and wept like the six-year-old I had been.

"Hazel!" Marlow again, closer now, alarmed. "Your eyes—"

"They're fine," I said.

"They're *gold*, Hazel."

"Then they're finally matching my personality."

He caught my arm. Up close his face was gray and sweating, and behind the fear in it there was something worse—recognition. "I've seen this," he said. "In the archives. The ones they burned. There were keepers who came to the garden before the binding, before it was the Verdant, when it was still small. They came to visit memories. Just to visit. And every one of them wrote the same thing in the margins of their final entries." His grip tightened. "*Just one more row.*"

"I'm surveying," I said.

"You're *shopping*."

And here is the truth I've had years to sit with: he was right, and I knew he was right, and I picked a second fruit anyway.

* * *

Her hands.

This memory wasn't sweet. It was work—the long gray afternoon light of the guildhall cellar, her pen moving across the Ledger of Impossible Things, and I felt what she felt as she wrote: the particular satisfaction of a true entry. The click of it. *This happened. This is recorded. Therefore it cannot be unwritten.* I had chased that feeling my whole life without knowing I'd inherited it, the way you inherit a nose or a temper. Watching her write, feeling her write, I understood for the first time that my pride in the guild wasn't mine. It was hers, planted in the family line like a seed, watered by thirty years of Delacroix keepers.

Which raised a question I didn't let myself finish asking: if my vocation was planted in me, what else was?

The third fruit I took without deciding to. I remember my hand rising and the stem parting with a sound like a held breath released, and I remember thinking, distantly, *Marlow is saying something, Emberlow is saying something, the map is saying something,* and none of it mattered, because—

* * *

Her guilt.

Oh, she had saved the worst for the center of the tree. Of course she had. Guilt grows deepest.

I was her, in the undercroft, seventy years ago. I was her hands shaking around a pen. I was her looking down at a young king who had come to the guild in secret, desperate, because his kingdom was starving and the guild had offered him abundance at a price no one had read carefully enough. I felt her realize—there in the ink and candle-smell—that the seedling the guild had planted in the palace gardens was already awake, already hungry, already *listening*. I felt her choose anyway. Bind it to the crown, chain the hunger to a throne, save the guild, save the city, damn the king. I felt her tell herself: *someone must be collateral. Let it be me. Let it be my line.*

Let it be Hazel.

I came back to myself kneeling in the dirt with three empty skins of fruit in my fist and gold light leaking out of me like a cracked lantern, and Marlow was on his knees beside me shaking my shoulders, and far away beyond the wall Emberlow was roaring—a sound like a mountain grieving—and none of it reached me properly, because the garden had gone quiet in that particular way rooms go quiet when someone important has entered.

The Verdant spoke. Not in words. In rows. The paths rearranged themselves gently, the way a host turns you toward the dinner table, and every bloom along the new path opened a little wider, and I understood the offer being renewed:

*Stay. Read them all. Every year she lived. Every entry she made. Everything she took from you and everything she hid. You could know her completely, Hazel Delacroix. No other man alive can say that of anyone. And while you read—you'd be safe here. Nothing in the garden is ever lost. Nothing in the garden is ever taken. Isn't that all your guild ever wanted to be?*

And God forgive me, it sounded right. It sounded like the guild's founding charter read aloud in my grandmother's kitchen voice. It sounded like the box marked DO NOT OPEN UNTIL YOU CAN FORGIVE HER, except here the box was open, and the opening cost nothing, and forgiveness grew on trees.

"Just one more row," I heard myself say.

Marlow went white. "Hazel. *Hazel.* Listen to me. I reported to the crown for nine years. I sold people's secrets for less than this garden is offering you, and I am telling you, as the single most qualified expert in this kingdom on the subject of selling out: this is how it feels right before. This exact warmth. This exact certainty. The Verdant doesn't take you kicking. It takes you *grateful*."

"Your warnings ring hollow," I said mildly, and heard, as if from a great distance, how hollow *my* voice had become.

Beyond the wall, Emberlow's roar cut off. In its place came her voice, thin as smoke through the gate slats, and even drowning in gold light, some buried guild-clerk part of me noted that a dragon should never sound tired.

*"Little keeper. Ask the garden one question. Ask it what happens to the memories you're holding for me."*

I frowned. "What happens to—"

The thought snagged. Because I *was* holding memories. Emberlow's hoard-memories, transferred into my ledger in ash and bargain, riding against my ribs in the pages. Her first flight. Her mother's shadow on the caldera rim. Two hundred years of a life that had nowhere else to live.

And the garden's hum changed key. Just slightly. The way a smile changes when it stops being sincere.

*They would be safe here too,* the rows murmured. *Everything is safe here.*

But I had Emberlow's memories in my keeping, and I had spent three weeks learning the difference between a thing that is kept and a thing that is consumed, and the hum of this garden—warm, generous, endlessly patient—was the hum of a mouth.

I looked down at my hands. Three empty fruit-skins. Gold light under my skin, my grandmother's laugh sitting in my chest like a coal, her guilt sitting in my skull like a stone. Mine now? Or the garden's, loaned to me, ripening toward harvest?

I didn't know. That was the horror of it. I had eaten three memories and could no longer tell whose they were.

"One more row," I said again—but slower this time, and my feet, which had been drifting forward, stopped.

Because down the new path the Verdant had laid for me, at the end of the row of my grandmother's tree, I saw what it had saved for last. A single bloom, taller than the rest, still closed. And through its petals, faint as writing under wax, I could see a shape I knew better than my own hands.

A ledger page. Blank. With my name at the top.

*Hazel Delacroix. Entry the first.*

The garden wanted me to pick it. The garden wanted me to see what my own memory looked like in bloom—wanted me to hold myself in my hand the way I'd held her laugh.

And some cold clerk-voice deep in my chest, the voice that balances ledgers at three in the morning, finally spoke loud enough to hear:

*You don't get to audit yourself, boy. Nobody does. That's not a row. That's the whole harvest.*

"Marlow," I said. My voice came out rusted. "Get Emberlow. Get the map. We're going underground."

Behind us, softly, patiently, the closed bloom began to open anyway.
