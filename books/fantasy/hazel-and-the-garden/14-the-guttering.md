## 14. The Guttering

I have flown through storms that could peel the scales off lesser creatures. I have flown with an arrowhead lodged between two ribs for a decade, because removing it seemed like more trouble than the ache. I have flown, once, while actively on fire, which I do not recommend even to dragons.

But I have never flown empty.

That is the only word for it. A dragon is not flesh and fire and wing, whatever the naturalists in their towers like to write. A dragon is memory given weight. Every scale is a year we chose to keep. My left wing remembers the first updraft that ever caught me over my mother's shoulder; my tail remembers the taste of the sea off the southern cliffs; my heart remembers — remembered — a name spoken by someone whose face I can no longer find in myself.

And half of it is gone now. Given away. Poured into a boy's ledger like wine into a cup too small for it, because I asked him to, because I was proud, because I thought I would have time to be careful about it.

Dragons are bad at bargains. We live too long to practice humility and too rarely to practice dying.

* * *

We were three hours out of the caldera when I felt the first gutter.

It is exactly what it sounds like. Imagine your own heartbeat skipping — no, imagine your own *name* skipping, a beat of silence where you should be, and then arriving again slightly wrong, slightly less, like a coin returned worn smooth. That was the gutter. Emberlow, who had been Emberlow for nine hundred years, flickered.

The boy felt it too. Hazel rode in the harness Marlow had rigged from rope and prayer, and I heard him shout something against the wind — "Emberlow? Emberlow!" — his voice pitched in that particular human register that means he has noticed a thing and would prefer very much to un-notice it.

"I'm fine," I said, which is what dragons say instead of the truth, because the truth takes longer and our mouths are built for fire, not confession.

The truth was this: the transfer had taken more than I'd priced. When I made the bargain in the ash of my own roost, I had counted my memories like a miser counts coins, certain I knew which ones I could spare. But memory is not coins. Memory is load-bearing walls. Take out the wrong one and the whole house leans. Somewhere in the giving, I had lost the wall that held up my fire.

A dragon's flame is fed by remembering how to burn. Not the mechanics — any fool lizard can open its throat — but the *why*. The first thing I ever burned was a dead tree on the caldera's rim, and my mother said, *Good. Now the ground can grow something new,* and that sentence, that small green arithmetic, was the wick my whole life has burned along. I reached for it as we flew, reaching the way you reach for a word on the tip of your tongue, and found smoke where the sentence should be.

"Boy," I said. "How far to the capital?"

Hazel twisted in the harness. Below us the borrowed country scrolled past — fields stitched pale at their edges, villages with the gray look of places that had been skimmed, a river ahead catching the late light like a blade laid across the land. The capital sat on that river the way a tick sits on a dog.

"Why?" he called back.

"Because I am going to need somewhere soft to fail."

He understood faster than most humans would have. Credit where due; the Ledgermen breed a certain grim quickness. He didn't waste breath asking if I was sure, or if there was anything he could do, or any of the other courtesies that are lovely when you have years and useless when you have minutes. He just pulled the map from his coat and shouted bearings into the wind, and I turned toward the river with the last of my certainty.

* * *

Here is what failing feels like, in case you are curious, and I know some of you will be, because humans collect other creatures' deaths the way I used to collect crowns: it feels like going out in a room where the lamps are being carried away one by one.

First the far memories go dark — I could no longer tell you, in that hour, the color of the sky the day I hatched. Then the middle ones. I lost my hoard piece by piece, which sounds trivial until you understand that a dragon's hoard is her autobiography told in objects. Each coin was a Tuesday. Each goblet was a grudge. They went out like windows in a house at dusk, and I could feel the rooms behind them going cold.

Then the near ones started, and those I fought for. I gripped the boy's name. Hazel Delacroix, keeper of the ledger, grandson of a woman whose handwriting I had seen in a burning archive a century ago — I held that name the way a drowning man holds a spar, and I want it recorded that I did not let go. Whatever else fails in this account, I kept the boy's name.

What I could not keep was *up*.

My left wing simply forgot itself mid-stroke. There was no pain, no tear, no dramatic snap — worse than that, it hesitated, the way a man hesitates at a door he cannot remember why he opened, and in that hesitation the air withdrew its hand and we dropped.

I have fallen before. Falling is flying's rude cousin and every dragon knows it intimately. But falling while forgetting how to fall — forgetting the tuck, the roll, the flare that turns a plummet into a landing — that is a different animal entirely. I did the arithmetic a hundred feet above the river and did not like the sum. So I did the only thing left in my inventory.

I burned.

Not well. Not the clean white-gold lance of my prime, but a ragged orange cough of everything still lit inside me, aimed down and sideways to break our fall the way a swimmer breaks a current. It cost me — oh, it cost me, I felt whole decades char and flake away like paper — but it slowed us, and then the river did the rest, and the world became white noise and cold and the particular indignity of a nine-hundred-year-old dragon being fished out of a city's bathwater by a twenty-year-old boy standing hip-deep and hauling on a harness strap with both hands.

* * *

I came back to myself in the shallows, half-beached, steam rising off me in sheets. The capital's riverfront had gone quiet in that specific way crowds go quiet — not absent, but watching. Faces at the quay. A fisherman frozen with his net half-thrown. Children peering around their mothers' skirts with the frank appetite children have for catastrophe.

"They're staring," Hazel said. He had my head cradled against his shoulder, which must have looked absurd and probably was, and which I was in no condition to refuse.

"Humans stare," I said. My voice came out cracked, guttering at the edges of each word. "It's what they do instead of scales."

"Not like this." His jaw was tight. "Look at the palace."

I looked. It took effort; looking had begun to cost.

The palace stood above the river on its hill, all pale stone and tall windows, and I had seen it a thousand times across nine centuries of circling this kingdom's affairs. But today its windows were filling. Courtiers coming to them like moths — no, like *tollkeepers*, faces pressed to glass, and beneath my breastbone I felt a sensation I had not felt since I was young enough to be prey to things: the feeling of being *sensed*.

The crown knew. That was the horror of it, arriving cold and clear through my diminishing self. The crown — the thing wearing the king, the root-fed hunger in the undercroft — had felt a dragon's fire gutter over its own city the way a spider feels a fly touch the web's outer ring. And it was interested. I could see the interest. It moved in the windows like oil moving on water.

"It knows I'm dying," I said.

"Can it—"

"Reach me here? No." I gathered what remained of my dignity, which was not much, and arranged it around the fact that I was lying in a river. "But it doesn't need to reach me. It needs to reach *you*. Boy. Listen. Half of me is in that ledger you're carrying. Half of Emberlow, folded into your pages, and I promise you it is not sitting quietly. It's burning through you — I can smell it, smoke and old gold, you've been smelling it too, don't lie to me, you've had that look since the caldera."

Hazel's hand went, involuntarily, to the ledger at his chest. And I saw it then, the thing I had done to him with my bargain: the faint shine in his eyes that was not his own, the way his pupils caught light like a dragon's do. My memories, loose in a human skull, looking for room.

"How long," he said quietly, "before it's all of you?"

"I don't know. Hours. A day, if I'm stubborn, and I am historically very stubborn." I coughed, and the cough came out with a curl of flame that made the nearest fishermen decide they had business elsewhere. "But hear me, and hear me clearly, because I may not be able to say it twice. The clock was days when we left the caldera. It is hours now. Emberlow's fire does not gutter slowly — it goes the way a candle goes when the wax is gone, all at once and then dark. And when I go dark, the last fire in the world that can burn the Verdant's roots goes with me, unless—"

"Unless what?"

"Unless you take the rest." I hated saying it. I want that on the record too, wherever records go when dragons finish with them. "All of me. Into the ledger. Into you. Marlow warned you what that makes you. An archive. A vessel. He's right, and I'm sorry, and I would rather apologize now while I still remember what sorry means."

* * *

He didn't answer right away. He sat down in the shallows next to my ruined head, water to his waist, and stared at the palace, and I watched him do the arithmetic I had already done and arrive at the same ugly total.

Across the river, bells began to ring.

Not alarm bells. Feast bells — I knew their rhythm from centuries of listening to this city celebrate itself, a bright triple pattern that meant the court had been summoned to occasion. Even from the shallows I could hear the change in the streets: doors opening, carriers hurrying, that low human hum of a population invited to something.

Then the criers began, working the quay, and their words carried flat across the water:

*"By grace of the crown! This night the throne opens its bounty! Let all faithful come and be remembered — let all who carry burdens of years lay them down — the feast of memory begins at dusk—"*

Hazel went very still.

"They're calling them in," he said. "Now. Tonight. While the court thinks the dragon is dead in the river."

"The crown felt me fall," I said. "It knows what my falling means. It wants the kingdom's memories poured in before anything can stop it — a last great feeding, a full belly before winter." I lifted my head as high as it would go, which was not high, and made myself look at him straight, because the next part mattered and I would not have it softened by a dying creature's mumbling. "You could run. Take the ledger, take Marlow's keys, take the map's deeper layer, and be three kingdoms away by morning. No one would blame you. I would not blame you, and I am the party most injured by your running, so mine is the opinion that counts."

"You'd forget me," he said. "If I ran. You'd forget you ever asked."

"I already have forgotten most of what I loved, boy. One more name is a small grief." A lie, and he knew it was a lie, and I let him know I knew, and that was the whole conversation, really. Dragons and Ledgermen. We trade in impossible things.

He stood, wrung out his coat, and settled the ledger against his chest with both hands, the way you'd hold a wounded bird or a live coal.

"Then give me the rest," he said. "All of it. Now, before the feast starts, before — whatever happens tonight, you're not leaving pieces of yourself scattered around this city for that crown to pick at. If you're going to go dark, you go dark *whole*, and you go dark *in me*, and I will bring you back if it's the last entry I ever write."

I looked at him for a long moment. Somewhere behind my eyes, another lamp went out — I felt it go, a warm one, something about rain on a mountain I can no longer name — and I understood that the choice was not actually his to make slowly.

"All right," I said. "But we do it properly. Freely given, freely taken. That's the whole trick of it, boy, though neither of us yet knows why. Hold the ledger open. And whatever you feel when it comes through — and you will feel *everything* — do not fight it. Fighting a memory is how it gets torn."

He knelt in the shallows beside me. Around us the city rang and bustled toward its feast, thousands of people walking willingly toward the thing that ate them, and I put my brow against the ledger's open page and began, for the second time in my long life, to pour myself out.

This time I did not count. This time I gave it all — the hoard and the grudges, the Tuesdays and the crowns, my mother's voice and the dead tree on the caldera's rim and the sentence about what burning is *for*. I felt each one leave me like a scale lifting away, and I felt them arrive in him, and I heard his breath change, deepen, go strange — heard, faintly, beneath the boy's heartbeat, the first distant drumbeat of a dragon's fire learning to live in a human chest.

The sun was going down. The feast bells had stopped. In the palace on the hill, the windows were lighting one by one, gold and hungry, and the crown at the center of them had begun, very softly, to sing.

Hours, I thought, as the dark came up to meet me. We have hours.

Hold the name, boy. Hold my name, and yours, and hers.

And then I guttered — not out, not yet, but down to a single blue point, a pilot light, a promise — and waited in the dark of his ledger to see what he would dare.
