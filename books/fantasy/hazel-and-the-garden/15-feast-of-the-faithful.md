## 15. Feast of the Faithful

The invitation said *By Command of His Majesty, a Feast of Thanksgiving*, which in my experience is what a crown says when it's about to eat something.

Marlow had gotten us in the way Marlow got into most places: by looking like he belonged there and complaining about the wine. He'd stolen two sets of livery from a pair of footmen who were, at that moment, locked in a linen closet discovering each other's opinions on the succession. The uniforms fit badly. Mine smelled of someone else's sweat and rosewater, and the collar chafed like a small, persistent accusation.

"Rule one," Marlow murmured as we joined the river of servants flowing toward the great hall. "Don't look at the throne."

"Why not?"

"Because everyone who looks at the throne starts wanting to be looked at by the throne. It's how they fill the queue."

"What queue?"

He didn't answer. He didn't need to. We came through the service arch into the hall, and I saw it.

* * *

I have stood in the guildhall cellar among ledgers that weigh more than I do. I have watched a woman sign away her shadow with a steady hand and walk out into the sun lighter than she came in. I thought I understood appetite. I was wrong.

The feast itself was almost incidental — roasted things, gilded things, towers of fruit that no one touched. What drew the eye was the dais, and what drew the soul was the crown.

From this distance it looked like gold, the way a hook looks like a curve. But I had seen it up close once, in the capital, during the procession, and I knew what moved beneath the filigree: a slow flexing, like the palate of some vast mouth testing its next swallow. The king sat beneath it very still, the way a man sits when he has learned that movement costs him. His eyes were open. They were full of weather from other people's lives — I could see it from thirty feet, seasons that had never happened to him drifting behind his pupils like clouds crossing someone else's country.

And before the dais, in a line that wound back between the banquet tables and out through the western doors, stood the courtiers.

They queued. That was the word for it, the ugly domestic word. They queued to trade their years to the crown.

An old duke went first, or rather a man dressed as an old duke; I recognized the coat from portraits forty years younger than its wearer. He climbed the three steps, knelt, and bowed his head beneath the crown's reach. I saw his lips move — the old formula, the one our own ledger used in its cruelest entries: *freely given, fully surrendered, no recourse*. The crown dipped. Just a fraction. Just enough.

The man who stood up afterward stood straighter. His coat fit better. His hands were smooth. And his face — his face was the worst part, because it wasn't his face anymore. It was a face from the middle of his life, borrowed back, and behind it his eyes had gone shallow as a puddle in August. He descended the steps smiling, and the courtiers around him applauded softly, the way you applaud someone who has won a raffle.

"He bought back thirty years," Marlow said beside me. "Cost him everything after them. He'll live another decade and remember none of it. No children's names. No wars. No wife." He paused. "He had a wife. She left when he made the first trade. He doesn't know that now. Doesn't know there's a hole where she was. That's the mercy they sell down here — the hole doesn't ache, because the ache was part of what got eaten."

"And the crown gets—"

"Thirty years of a duke. His harvests, his grudges, his Latin. Everything the Verdant can grow." Marlow's jaw worked. "This is what the entries were feeding. Every vanished line in every ledger in the guildhall. They weren't erased, Hazel. They were *harvested*. Routed here."

I thought of the woman in chapter one of my own tenure — not chapter, entry — the woman who'd traded her shadow for a winter's warmth, and how her signature had faded from the page overnight like breath off glass. Somewhere in that queue's long history, her warmth had become a flower in a walled garden, and her shadow had become root.

"Keep your face stupid," Marlow said. "You're a footman. Footmen are furniture with legs."

"I've been called worse by better."

"No, you haven't. You've been called 'the youngest keeper' by people who wanted something from you. Different thing entirely." He handed me a tray of wine glasses as a prop. "Hold this. Look bored. And whatever happens, don't put it down."

* * *

What happened was the maid.

I want to say I sought her out, that I was already hunting for the undercroft when she crossed my path, but the truth is less flattering and more important: I was standing by a pillar holding a tray, feeling properly furious and properly useless, when the universe decided to stop being abstract at me.

She couldn't have been more than nineteen. Servants' grey, sleeves rolled, the raw-knuckled hands of someone who scrubs other people's floors so her own mother can cough in a warm room. She wasn't in the courtiers' queue — she'd been sent to refill glasses along it, and she moved through the ranks of the beautiful and the traded like a minnow through reeds, head down, quick.

Then she stopped. At the end of the queue, where it bent past a candelabrum, she stopped and looked at the dais, and I saw the exact moment the crown noticed her.

I felt it too, from across the hall — a pressure behind the eyes, a sweetness in the throat, like the smell of bread when you haven't eaten in two days. The Verdant's attention, reaching out through gold and bone. *Come here,* it said, without words, without sound. *You look delicious.*

She set down her pitcher. That was the terrible part, the part I keep returning to: she set it down carefully, first, because she was a good servant even in the act of unmaking herself, and then she walked to the end of the queue and stood in it.

"No," I said, under my breath, to no one, to the whole gilded room. "No, no, no."

"Hazel." Marlow's hand landed on my shoulder like a sandbag. "Hazel. Whatever you're calculating, don't."

"She's a child."

"She's a transaction. Like all of us. Move and we lose the keys, the map-layer, everything, and she still climbs those steps, because nobody in this hall is going to stop her, least of all two footmen with a wine tray."

The queue advanced. One. Two. Three trades ahead of her, each one a small quiet amputation, each trade applauded, each emptied-eyed graduate of the crown's mercy stepping down lighter and less. I counted her place the way you count the waves between you and drowning.

Four ahead. Three.

I did the arithmetic I always do, the ledgerman's reflex: what does it cost, what does it buy, who signs, who suffers. And the arithmetic came back obscene. A mother's life — I learned later that was the price on the table, the crown's brokers had gotten efficient, they posted rates near the kitchens like a butcher posts cuts — a mother's consumption, cured, in exchange for a daughter's entire past. Nineteen years. Every birthday. Her name's whole history in her mouth. Gone into a plant.

Two ahead.

"You're doing the face," Marlow said quietly. "The Delacroix face. Your grandmother had it, in the one portrait that survived the burn. It's the face of someone about to write something down."

"One ahead."

"Hazel—"

She reached the steps.

I watched her climb them, and I will be honest with you, whoever finds this account, because honesty is the only currency I have left that isn't someone else's: I almost looked away. There is a mercy in looking away and I nearly took it. But I am a keeper of the Ledger of Impossible Things, and the first law of the ledger is *witness*. If no one watches the trade, the trade owns you.

So I watched.

She knelt. She said the formula — she'd clearly been coached, someone had whispered it to her by the kitchen door, *freely given, fully surrendered, no recourse* — but she stumbled on *freely*, because nothing about it was free, and the crown didn't care, the crown has never cared, the crown takes the word and files the difference as profit.

It dipped toward her like a heron striking water.

And she gave it everything. I felt it go. The whole hall felt it go — a hush like the intake of breath before a sneeze, candles guttering in a wind that wasn't wind, and somewhere far below us, deep under the palace, in the dark where the roots run, something swallowed and was pleased.

When she stood, she was still nineteen. That was the obscenity of it. She hadn't traded her years forward like the duke; she'd traded her years *back*, all of them, and what remained was a girl-shaped vessel with no one home. She turned from the dais smiling — smiling, because joy was one of the last things to leave and it left last, waving from the shore — and she walked down the steps and past me, close enough to touch, and her eyes passed over mine with nothing in them at all.

No recognition. No curiosity. Nothing.

Somewhere in this palace a woman would wake tomorrow cured of a wasting sickness, and her daughter would bring her breakfast, and the daughter would not know why she was crying.

I set down the tray. Marlow had told me not to. I set it down anyway, on a table, precisely, because I am still a guildsman and I do not break crockery out of anger; I break contracts.

"Keys," I said.

* * *

Marlow's stolen keys were cold in my palm — six of them, iron and brass, heavy as sins, lifted from the under-steward's ring while the man was busy applauding the duke's rejuvenation. Marlow had mapped the servants' corridors the way I map ledger margins: obsessively, in pencil, with corrections.

"Third landing, past the linen vault," he recited as we slipped out through the service arch, into corridors where the music arrived thin and guilty. "Door with the blackened hinges. That's the old well stair. Below that—" he hesitated.

"Below that is the undercroft."

"Below that is the undercroft, and below *that* is the garden's root-cellar, and if the map's deeper layer is honest, below *that* is the king." He caught my arm at the landing. "One more thing, and then I'll shut up forever, or at least until it's inconvenient. The feast runs till dawn. The crown will be busy digesting — tonight of all nights, with a full hall of fresh trades in it, the binding will be at its loosest. It's the only window we get. But Hazel — the Verdant knows what we are. It tasted you in the garden. When we go down there, we aren't sneaking past it. We're walking into its mouth and hoping it likes to talk."

"It offered me a job once."

"Then smile," Marlow said, "like a candidate."

The third landing. The linen vault, breathing lavender. The door with the blackened hinges — the first key turned with a grinding reluctance, as if the lock itself had been told to forget how to open.

Beyond it, the well stair went down into air that smelled of green things growing where no light had ever reached. Rich air. Sweet air. Air like the inside of a flower, if flowers ate.

I thought of the maid's empty eyes. I put her in the front of my mind, where the ledger puts the entry you cannot reconcile, and I started down.

Behind and above us, faintly, the feast played on — violins, laughter, applause, the small soft sounds of a court selling itself one satisfied smile at a time. Ahead of us, the dark breathed.

And somewhere in the breathing dark, something that wore a crown and grew a kingdom stirred, and turned its attention downward, and waited for us with what I can only describe, in the language of my trade, as *interest accrued*.
