## 16. Unbinding

The undercroft smelled of loam and old promises.

That's the thing about cellars beneath palaces—everyone assumes they'll smell of stone and dust and the ghosts of bad decisions made three centuries ago. This one did smell of all that. But underneath it, thick as a second skin, was the green wet breath of growing things. Roots. The Verdant's roots had been drinking this air for a hundred years, and the air had learned to taste like them.

I stood at the top of the stairs with Marlow's stolen keys still warm in my fist and my grandmother's ledger open against my chest, and I thought: *this is the stupidest thing I have ever done, and I once traded a man's Tuesday for a boat.*

"Hazel." Marlow's voice behind me, low and fraying at the edges. "The feast won't last forever. The moment the crown notices the dragon's down—"

"Then we'd best be quick." I started down the steps.

The undercroft opened up around me like the inside of a throat. Vaulted ceilings gone soft with moss. Pillars wrapped in pale root-flesh so thick they looked less like architecture now than like ribs. And in the center, where a throne room's heart should be, the Verdant's trunk rose out of the floor in a slow spiral of bark and bloom, and chained to it—waist-deep in root, arms bound with living wood, crown fused to his skull like something grown rather than worn—was the king.

He was awake. That was the worst part. In the procession he'd been a puppet with someone else's memories behind his eyes, but here, close to his own chains, some of him had come home. His eyes found me across the dark and they were *clear*, clear as winter water, and tired in a way I recognized from every mirror I'd avoided for the last month.

"You're a Delacroix," he said. His voice was rust and honey. "I can smell the ledger on you."

"That's either very flattering or very rude."

"Both." Something moved at the corner of his mouth that might have been a smile, if smiles could survive a hundred years underground. "Your grandmother stood where you're standing. She didn't come down the stairs. She came up through them."

I looked down. The flagstones beneath my feet were veined with fine white rootlets, delicate as handwriting. Of course she had. Grandmother Odile had never used a door in her life if a window would do.

"I've brought her entries," I said. "The original contract. All of it."

The king's gaze went to the book against my chest, and what crossed his face then wasn't hope. It was fear—the specific, exhausted fear of a man who has been told the cure before and watched it fail. "It will fight you."

"I'd be insulted if it didn't."

"It doesn't fight fair, boy. It fights with *you*." He pulled against his bonds, just enough to make the roots groan. "Read anyway. Read until your voice gives out. It's more than anyone's done in a century."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about unbinding an impossible contract: you don't cut it. You don't burn it, break it, or argue with it. A trade, once entered in a Ledgerman's hand, is only undone one way—you read the terms aloud, in full, in the presence of both parties, and you let the words do what words do when they're honest. They dissolve. Truth is a solvent. My grandmother taught me that when I was nine years old, using a sugar cube and a cup of water, and I'd spent twenty years thinking it was a lesson about accounting.

The Verdant knew I was reading before I spoke. I felt it the way you feel weather change—a pressure shift, a green attention turning toward me from every root in the room. The flowers along the trunk's spiral shivered open, all at once, like eyes.

I put my grandmother's ledger on a fallen block of masonry, steadied my hands, and began.

"'Entry the first. In the year of the famine, the guild of Ledgermen, being unable to feed the realm, does offer the following trade to the crown of Aldermere: abundance without end, in exchange for—'"

The first memory hit me before I finished the sentence.

Not the Verdant's memory. Mine. It came up through the soles of my boots like cold water through cloth, and suddenly I was eight years old again, standing in the guildhall doorway, watching my mother walk away down the street with a stranger's coat over her arm, and I was *knowing*, with the full horrible clarity of childhood, that she wasn't coming back—

"—in exchange for the crown's memories of its founding," I said, and my voice cracked but held, because that was the trick, wasn't it, the whole ancient trick of the Ledgermen: you keep speaking while the world tries to swallow you. "'Terms: the trade shall bind crown and garden together, root to throne, until—'"

Another one. My father's hands going slack around mine in the fever-house. The particular weight of a body that has stopped being a person. I relived it in the space between two syllables, complete and total, the smell of tallow and boiled herbs, and somewhere far above me Marlow shouted my name and I kept reading because stopping was dying, stopping was *joining*, and I had not walked through borrowed country and bargained with a dying dragon to become mulch in a basement.

"'—until such time as the terms are read aloud by blood of the binding hand—'"

The Verdant changed tactics. It stopped showing me grief and showed me *want*. I was back in the garden—that impossible paradise above us—and my grandmother's tree was blooming, and a petal peeled itself loose and drifted into my palm and became her laugh, her actual laugh, the one I'd been too young to remember properly, filling my head warm as brandy, and the Verdant poured itself into the wanting like water into a dry bed: *stay, little keeper, stay and read forever, we'll give you everything back, everything, you need only stop—*

"No," I said, and heard how small it sounded, and said it again louder. "No. 'Until such time as the terms are read aloud by blood of the binding hand, in the hearing of the bound party, freely and without payment.'"

My voice was shredding. Every word cost me a memory now—I could feel them going, small ones first, the name of my first landlady, the taste of a specific apple from a specific orchard, the tune my father hummed while shaving—and the horror of it was that I couldn't tell which losses were the Verdant's theft and which were simply the price of standing in one place too long and being human. The roots around the king's chest were tightening. The flowers were screaming. Not metaphorically—I could hear them, a high green frequency like a saw drawn across the inside of my skull, and threaded through it, almost gentle, almost kind:

*You are alone down here, Hazel Delacroix. You have always been alone. Put down the book.*

And then a second voice joined mine.

It was cracked and rusty and it missed half its notes, a voice that hadn't been used for anything but ceremonies and lies in a hundred years, but it was deep and it was human and it came from the throne of roots at the center of the room, and it was reciting.

"'Entry the second,'" said the king. "'Being the crown's acknowledgment of terms received...'"

The Verdant *shrieked*. Every flower in the undercroft snapped shut. The roots convulsed, and the king gasped—wood creaking against wood—but he kept going, word after word, his eyes fixed on mine across the dark, and I understood: the contract required the *bound party's* hearing. Both parties. My grandmother had written it that way a century ago, careful as a woman defusing a bomb, waiting for a descendant stubborn enough to climb down here and finish the sentence.

She'd known it would take two voices. She'd just hoped it wouldn't take a hundred years.

"'Entry the third,'" the king rasped, and I picked up the next line, and we fell into a rhythm, call and answer, two broken voices trading the terms of a terrible bargain back and forth across the cellar while the garden screamed and stole from us. He gave up memories too—I watched them go, watched his face flicker through coronations and betrayals and a young man's terror on the day he first wore the crown—and each time one left him he set his jaw and reached for the next line like a drowning man reaching for rope.

Somewhere in the middle of Entry Seven, I realized I was crying, and that it didn't matter, and that my voice was still working.

We kept reading.

* * *

Entry Eleven broke him for a moment. It was the clause about the feeding schedule—the cold arithmetic of how often the garden would eat, and how much, and from whom. The king's voice failed on the phrase "the realm consents," and he hung there in his chains with tears cutting tracks through a century of grime, saying, "I signed it, I signed it, they were starving, there was no—" and the roots surged toward his mouth like they meant to stuff the words back in.

"The realm consents," I said for him, loud, taking the line myself. "Signed and sealed. Read on, Your Majesty. You don't get to stop. Neither of us gets to stop."

He laughed—actually laughed, wet and awful—and found the next line.

That was the endurance, in the end. Not courage. Courage implies a choice made once, cleanly, at the start. This was something meaner and more ordinary: the refusal to stop, renewed every single sentence, by two men who had nothing left except the sentence in front of them. My legs had gone numb. My ledger-hand was bleeding where I gripped the pages. I had lost, by my rough count, most of a decade of small memories, and the Verdant was digging deeper now, going for load-bearing walls—the face of the first person I ever loved, the reason I became a Ledgerman at all, the—

"*Entry the fourteenth,*" the king thundered, and the roots flinched, and I caught the thread again.

"'—and upon the full and honest recitation of all terms, the binding shall be deemed dissolved, and neither party shall hold claim upon the other, in perpetuity, world without end, amen.'"

One entry left. Entry Fifteen. The seal clause. My grandmother's signature, and beside it, in a different ink, a king's.

The Verdant threw everything it had left at us. Not memories this time—it tore the memories *away*, ripped the floor out of my past so completely that for one vertiginous second I didn't know my own name, didn't know why I was kneeling in a cellar holding a book, and the silence where myself used to be was so vast I nearly stepped into it. Nearly accepted it. Nothing hurts in the void. Nothing grieves there.

Then the king's hand—freed to the elbow now, the roots retreating from it like a tide going out—closed around my wrist.

"Delacroix," he said. "Hazel Delacroix. Keeper of the Ledger. You are losing yourself; I am finding myself; between us we might manage one whole person. *Read.*"

I read.

"'Entry the fifteenth. Final term. Let it be known that this bargain was made in desperation and sealed in good faith, and let it be known further that desperation is not a foundation, and good faith cannot hold what hunger will not release. To whichever of my blood comes after: I am sorry. I could not save the realm and the guild both. I chose, and the choosing was wrong, and wrong choices must be unmade aloud. —Odile Delacroix, Keeper, who loved too many things and paid for all of them.'"

The last word left my mouth and the undercroft went silent.

Not quiet—*silent*, the way a held breath is silent. Every root in the room trembled on the edge of something. The king and I stared at each other across ten feet of dark, both of us raw as skinned knees, waiting to learn whether a hundred years of chains could really be dissolved by two voices and a dead woman's honesty.

Then the roots around the king's waist groaned, split, and began to withdraw—not fast, not gracefully, but with the terrible reluctance of a debt collector accepting, at last, that the paper in front of him is real. Bark sloughed from his arms. The crown cracked along one seam with a sound like ice on a spring river.

He sagged forward, free to the waist, breathing like a man surfacing.

"It's loosening," he whispered. "After a century, it's actually—" His eyes snapped up to mine, and the joy in them curdled into something else. "Boy. The heart. The binding dissolves, but the *garden* doesn't. The contract was the cage, not the beast. When the cage goes—"

Above us, faint through a hundred feet of palace stone, a bell began to ring. Then another. Then all of them.

Marlow appeared at the top of the stairs, gray-faced, keys jangling. "The feast is over. The court's coming down. And Hazel—" He swallowed. "The dragon's fire. We never got it. The roots are still alive, and in about four minutes, so is every guard in the palace."

The king got one leg free of the collapsing roots and planted his bare foot on the flagstones of his own prison, standing—for the first time in a hundred years—like a man rather than a harvest.

"Then we'd best be quick," he said, in my own tone, and reached for the crown.
