## 17. The Price of Fire

There is a particular indignity in dying on your belly, and I would know, because I have catalogued every one of them since the river.

Dragons are not built for crawling. We are built for the long grammar of the sky—climb, bank, hold, fall—and our bodies assume that grammar even when the ground insists on prose. So I dragged myself out of the capital's mud and up the road toward the garden with my wings folded wrong and my tail carving a furrow behind me like a sentence someone had given up on halfway through. Every joint had forgotten its purpose. That is what memory-hunger does at the end: it does not take your memories all at once, like a thief. It takes them one verb at a time.

I still remembered *fly*. I remembered it the way you remember a country you were exiled from as an egg—vividly, uselessly, with no road back.

I did not remember my mother's name. I had checked that morning, the way you check a pocket for a coin you're fairly sure is gone. Nothing. There had been a name once; there had been a whole litany of names, hers first among them, and now the space where she lived was smooth and warm and empty, like a stone that has been held too long.

What I remembered instead was Hazel Delacroix's ledger, which is a strange thing to carry in your skull when your own skull is being emptied. Half of me lived in those pages now. My first hoard—the river-smoothed glass, the copper kettle I loved more than any gem because it sang when rain hit it. The smell of the caldera in spring. The taste of goat, which I mention only because it was the last thing I thought about before sleep for four hundred years and it seemed important that someone should know. All of it written down in a boy's careful hand, transferred by bargain, carried in a book that smelled of tallow and dust.

A dragon's archive walking around on two legs. If I had been well, I'd have laughed at the shape of it.

I was not well. I crawled.

* * *

The garden's wall announced itself before I saw it, the way a fever announces itself before the sweat. Warmth came off it in waves—not sun-warmth, not hearth-warmth, but the warmth of a thing that has eaten so much happiness it can no longer contain it, the way a fat man's chair creaks. The air hummed. Not bees. Something underneath bees, something patient, something that had learned over centuries that if you hum sweetly enough, things walk into you on their own.

I crested the last rise at dusk and saw it: a walled paradise glowing faintly green against the darkening fields, flowers visible even from here, blooming in colors that do not occur where honesty occurs. And beneath it—I felt this rather than saw it, the way you feel a heartbeat through a floor—roots. Vast ones. Pale threads running out under the whole kingdom, drinking from everything that had ever been loved and could not hold onto it.

My fire stirred at the sight of it. That surprised me. I had assumed the fire was gone; it had guttered mid-flight, after all, had dropped me out of the sky like a stone with regrets. But deep in the furnace of me, under the ash, something coals-and-alive turned over at the smell of the Verdant's roots and said, in the old tongue of my kind, the word that means *that*.

*That* is what we burn. Dragons were made, whatever the songs say, not for gold but for this—for the things that grow too large by eating what they should not. Fire is not destruction. Fire is the world remembering itself sharply enough to refuse.

So I crawled closer, and I gathered what remained, and I opened my jaws at the wall, and nothing happened.

Nothing. A curl of smoke, gray and embarrassed, and then not even that.

I tried again. I reached for the furnace the way I had reached for it ten thousand times, the way you reach for your own hand in the dark, and found—rooms. Empty rooms. The fire was there, I could feel its heat like a body feels a fever, but the knowledge of it was gone. How to open the second throat. How to let the flame meet the air in the old way. How to aim. These had been memories, and memories are food, and I had been feeding myself to my own survival for weeks without noticing which meals mattered.

You cannot burn what you cannot remember how to burn.

I lay there at the edge of the humming garden, a creature composed almost entirely of heat and no instruction, and I will confess what I felt, because dragons are honest or we are nothing: I felt foolish. Four hundred years of hoarding, and the one treasure I never thought to guard was the knowing itself. I had guarded the kettle. I had guarded the glass. I had let the *how* walk out of me unattended, because it had seemed so much a part of me that it could not possibly be taken.

The Verdant hummed. Somewhere beyond the wall, a flower opened with a sound like a small sigh, and I knew—it knew, the way parasites know—that I was dying at its doorstep, and it was content to wait. It had waited centuries. It could wait out one more guttering flame.

Except.

Except that I heard footsteps on the road behind me, and I knew the gait before I knew the name. A boy who walked like his ledger weighed more than he did.

* * *

Hazel knelt in the dirt beside my head, and Marlow stood a few paces off, holding a lantern he didn't need, because the garden glowed enough to read by and both of us knew it.

"You shouldn't be here," Hazel said. His voice cracked down the middle. "Emberlow. You shouldn't have crawled."

"Where else," I said. Talking cost me. Each word was a coin spent from a purse I could feel thinning. "Would I go. This is. The errand."

"The fire's gone. We saw it fail. You fell out of the *sky*, Emberlow—"

"I fell." I let a little smoke out with the words, mostly for effect. Dying dragons are permitted theater. "Out of the sky. Yes. But the fire isn't gone. It's sleeping in rooms I can't find. Do you understand? I have the heat. I don't have the *how*. The Verdant didn't take it. I spent it. Buying miles. Buying time. Buying—" I stopped, because the next item on the list was *buying your ledger-space, boy*, and I did not want him carrying that weight on top of everything else.

He carried it anyway. He always did. That was the whole trouble with Hazel Delacroix; give him a debt and he'd wear it like a coat.

"There has to be a way," he said. "Marlow, the archives—there must be something about dragon-fire, some entry—"

"There isn't," Marlow said quietly. He had the look of a man who had already searched himself hollow and found the same room empty. "Fire isn't recorded. It isn't traded. It was never in any ledger. That's the point of it."

"That's the point of it," I agreed, and closed my eyes, and considered the end from the inside, which is a thing I recommend to no one.

Here is what I saw, in the dark behind my eyelids: the caldera. Always the caldera. My roost, my hoard, my long centuries of keeping watch over a kingdom that never once looked up. And threaded through the vision, pale as roots, the understanding that most of it was already gone—that I was looking at the last few rooms of a house whose other wings had burned down slowly, quietly, while I slept, and that soon even the caldera would be smoke, and then the smoke would be nothing, and the world would contain no dragon anywhere in it, only a boy's book full of a dead creature's inventory.

An archive is not a life. But it is not nothing either. I had made my bargain with my eyes open.

And yet.

And yet there was one thing left that was not inventory. One memory I had kept apart from all the trading, the way you keep one candle unlit in case the dark gets truly total. I had told myself it was for the end. I had not known until this moment, lying in the dirt with a boy's hand on my jaw, what kind of end I had meant.

"Hazel."

"I'm here."

"Your ledger. Take it out."

His breath caught. "Emberlow, no—you gave me half of you already, you can't just keep—"

"Boy." I opened my eyes. The garden light made them ache, but I looked at him anyway, because looking at him was the last use I had for eyes. "I am going to tell you something about fire, and then I am going to give you something, and you are going to stop arguing with dying creatures, because it is rude and also inefficient."

He took out the ledger. His hands shook. Good. Reverence and fear live in the same tremor, and I have always preferred my archivists reverent.

"Fire needs a living hand," I said. "That is the oldest law of it, older than ledgers, older than your guild, older than the Verdant. Flame that is merely released is just heat. Flame that is *aimed*—flame that knows what it is for—that requires a will behind it. A rememberer. Someone who holds the knowing and the wanting in the same place at the same time." I paused to gather breath, and the gathering hurt. "I have the wanting. I have lost the knowing. You—" I tapped the ledger with one claw, gently, the way you tap a bell. "You have my knowing. Every page. The furnace-rooms. The second throat. The angle of the jaw. You have carried it for weeks, boy. You have been reading yourself to sleep with a dragon's memories. Which means there is exactly one living hand in this kingdom that can aim my fire."

He stared at me. Behind him, Marlow went very still, in the way of a man watching a trap close around someone else and being unable to say whether it is cruel or kind.

"No," Hazel said. "No, that's—if I breathe your fire, I'm not—I'm a *keeper*, Emberlow, I write things down, I don't—"

"You think writing things down is not breathing?" I said. "What do you think fire is? It is the world's oldest ledger. Everything it touches is entered, and nothing it touches is ever the same. Your grandmother knew this. She wrote her bargains in ink because she was afraid of the other kind of writing. You are not afraid. I have watched you be afraid of many things, boy, but never of the page."

The garden hummed. A root shifted somewhere beneath us—I felt it through my belly, a slow muscular movement, the Verdant turning in its sleep or pretending to. We did not have long. Whatever window the crash had opened, the crown and the garden would be closing it.

"Now," I said. "The gift."

"Don't," he whispered. "Please. You've given enough. Keep one thing. Everyone deserves to keep one thing."

"Everyone deserves," I said, "to be *aimed*."

And I reached for the candle. The one unlit memory, the one I had saved through all the hungering years, through the long self-cannibalism of my decline, through the river and the mud and the crawling: my first flight.

I will describe it once, here, so that it exists twice—in his pages and in this telling—and then I will let it go, because that is what gifts require.

I was young. Younger than Hazel, though dragons count differently, and the counting hardly matters. The caldera wind came up the cliff-face in the hour before dawn, and my mother stood at the edge with her shadow stretched long behind her, and she said—she said something, I have lost the words now, but I remember their shape, which was the shape of every mother's words at every cliff-edge in the history of flying things: *the wind will hold you, the falling is only the beginning of the rising, jump.* And I jumped. And the wind held. And for eleven seconds—or a year, the two are the same at that altitude—I was not a creature with a name or a hoard or a duty. I was simply a thing the sky had decided to permit.

That is the memory. That is the whole of it. Eleven seconds of permission.

I did not trade it to him. Understand this, whoever reads these pages after: I did not sell it, did not bargain it, did not enter it as a debt owed. I leaned my great tired head forward until my brow touched the ledger in his hands, and I gave it. Freely. The way my mother gave me the wind—not as payment, not as purchase, but because some things rot if you keep them and fly if you let them go.

"Take it," I said. "Write it as a gift. First entry of whatever comes after. And then, boy—when you stand at the garden's heart, and the roots come for you, and the Verdant offers you everything it has ever stolen—remember the wind. Remember that it held you before you had done anything to earn it. That is how you aim a fire. Not with anger. With permission."

Hazel was crying. I record this without cruelty; it seemed structurally necessary, the way weather is necessary. He pressed the ledger to his chest and the new memory settled into him—I watched it happen, watched his eyes change the way eyes change when a room in them is suddenly furnished—and he breathed out, and the breath came out warm.

Warmer than a boy's breath should be.

"Oh," he said softly. "Oh, I can feel it. The furnace-rooms. Emberlow, I can feel where they *are*—"

"Then we are not finished," I said, and hauled myself another body-length up the slope on legs that had forgotten everything except the going, "you and I. You will be the hand. I will be the coal. And between us we are going to teach a garden what happens when it eats the wrong keeper's family."

Behind us, far off in the capital, bells began to ring—a feast-bell sound, greedy and bright, the crown calling its faithful to dinner. The Verdant hummed louder, as if answering.

Hazel helped me up the last stretch of the rise, one small hand braced against scales that had gone dull as old coins, and together—we must have looked absurd, a dying dragon and a boy with a book—we looked down at the garden that had eaten a kingdom's memory, and I felt, in the empty rooms of myself, the fire turn over in its sleep and begin, very slowly, to wake.

It was not my fire anymore. That was the price, and I paid it gladly.

It was his.
