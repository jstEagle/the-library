## 18. Crownfall

I have carried a great many things in my life. Ledgers, mostly, which weigh nothing and everything at once. A dead man's map of wax and dragon-scale. My grandmother's guilt, in the form of sixty years of entries I never wrote but inherited like a debt collector inherits the furniture.

But I had never carried fire before.

Emberlow's last gift sat in my chest like a swallowed coal—not burning me, exactly, but *waiting* to burn, the way a held breath waits to become a word. She had pressed it into me with her dying snout against my sternum, that final coherent memory of hers: her first flight, the sky opening under her wings like a book no one had read yet. *Take it,* she'd said, or thought, or simply was, because dragons don't distinguish between meaning something and being it. *Freely given. Now go make it useful.*

Marlow walked beside me through the garden's burning rows, and for once he didn't talk. That was how I knew he was frightened. Marlow talked when he was comfortable, talked when he was nervous, talked over corpses and kings alike. Silence from Marlow was a scream from anyone else.

The garden did not want us to reach its heart.

I don't mean guards sprang up, or thorns barred the way. The Verdant was subtler than that, and crueler. It made the walking pleasant. Every step forward, the air grew warmer, sweeter, thick with the smell of rain on hot stone and bread in an oven and—this was the worst of it—my grandmother's kitchen, which I had not smelled in twenty years and would have sworn on any ledger you liked that I had forgotten entirely.

"There," Marlow said hoarsely. "Don't breathe it in too deep. It's not air. It's bait."

"I know what it is."

"Do you? Because you're smiling."

I wasn't. Except that apparently I was. I dragged my hand down my face and kept walking.

The paths between the flowerbeds were paved with memory. I could feel them under my boots—not physically, but the way you feel a word on the tip of your tongue. Each flagstone was somebody's afternoon. Somebody's wedding. Somebody's last conversation with a parent who died too soon, preserved here in bloom while the person who lived it wandered home hollow, wondering why they couldn't cry at the funeral.

And threaded beneath all of it, pale as veins under skin, the roots went down and out—under the garden wall, under the capital, under the whole kingdom, drinking.

"Emberlow said fire needs a living hand to aim it," Marlow said. "What does that mean, practically? Because if the answer is 'you must hold the flame and think loving thoughts,' I want to renegotiate my role here."

"It means I can't just throw it. It has to go where I mean it to go, all the way through me." I flexed my fingers. They felt very far away. "It means whatever's in me when I let it out comes out with it."

Marlow stopped walking. "Hazel. What's in you?"

I looked at him. He had soot on his face and betrayal in his recent past and, God help him, loyalty arriving late but arriving. And I realized I didn't entirely know the answer to his question. The dragon-fire was there, yes. But so was Emberlow's first flight. So were the fragments of my grandmother I'd gathered walking these rows—the laugh, the ledger-hand, the terrible careful guilt. So were every memory I'd traded away on the road, pulled tooth by tooth from my own head to give forgetful villages back their children's names.

"That," I said, "is an excellent question, and I'll answer it right after I commit arson."

*

The heart of the garden was not what I expected, though by then I should have stopped expecting anything.

I expected a tree. Something vast and black and obviously evil, a villain you could point at. Instead there was a clearing, soft-grassed, ringed by white flowers that hummed faintly with other people's happiest moments, and in the center of the clearing, rising out of the earth like a root that had decided to become architecture, stood the Verdant itself.

It looked like a young tree. That was the horror of it. Slim-trunked, silver-barked, leaves like green coins trembling in a wind I couldn't feel. It was beautiful the way ledgers are beautiful—all that order, all those columns of taken things balanced perfectly—and it was small, and it was old, older than the guild, older than the binding, and it was *hungry* the way only a thing that started as a bargain can be hungry.

It knew I was there before I spoke. Of course it did. I was carrying half a dragon's archive and a Delacroix's blood; I rang like a struck bell.

*Little keeper.* Its voice didn't come through my ears. It came up through the soles of my boots, through the roots, through everything its roots touched, which was everything. *You brought my fire to me. How thoughtful.*

"It's not yours."

*Everything given within my walls is mine. That is the first law of gardens.* The leaves shivered, pleased with themselves. *You are full to bursting, Hazel Delacroix. I can taste her in you. Your grandmother. I held her for sixty years and she was the sweetest thing ever planted here. You've been gathering her petals like a beggar. Come. Sit. I will give you all of her—every year, every hour, every entry in her hand. You need only open your ledger and let me write.*

And here is the thing I will confess now, standing in the ash afterward, because a wry narrator who lies about this part isn't worth the paper: I wanted to. My hands were already moving toward the bark. Somewhere behind me Marlow shouted my name and it sounded like it came from another country.

Then I felt it—the coal in my chest, Emberlow's freely given flight—and the Verdant reached for it.

And *couldn't take it.*

I felt the attempt the way you feel a hand close on empty air. The Verdant's hunger wrapped around that memory—that bright, wind-roared, sky-opened moment of a young dragon's first flight—and found nothing to grip. No debt. No taking. No trade. It had been *given*, freely, and it turned out—I nearly laughed, standing in the devil's own garden—that memory given freely cannot be consumed. It can only be carried, or passed on. The Verdant had spent centuries eating a kingdom's past and had never once been fed by choice, and so it had never learned that some things don't go down.

*What are you,* it said, and for the first time its voice sounded less like a god and more like a starving animal.

"A keeper," I said. "That's the joke, isn't it? You were made by keepers. Bound by keepers. Fed by keepers. And none of us—" I put my hand flat against my own sternum, over the fire, over the flight, over my grandmother's laugh sitting alongside it like heirlooms on a shelf instead of wounds in a wall— "none of us ever figured out the difference between a debt and a gift until tonight."

*I offered you everything.*

"You offered me a trade. There's a difference. I'm told it matters."

I knelt at the base of the young tree, in the soft grass that was woven from stolen afternoons, and I let the fire rise.

It didn't hurt. That surprised me most. I had braced for agony, for the coal to tear its way out of me, but Emberlow's gift moved through me the way her memory of flying must have moved through her—like opening wings. The heat poured down my arm, into my palm, into the pale root-flesh at the tree's base, and where it touched, the roots did not scream.

They *sighed.*

Fire ran outward from my hand along the vein-roads beneath the kingdom, and everywhere it went, memories came loose—not destroyed, I understood suddenly, *released*. Sixty years of my grandmother pouring back into the world to find whatever was left of her to return to. The maid's mother's life. The villages' children's names. The king's own history, flooding home to a man chained in an undercroft who had begged only for a witness. The Verdant's hoard emptied itself in a great silent exhalation, and the tree began, petal by petal, leaf by leaf, to come apart into light.

And in the last of it, as the silver trunk whitened and the green coins fell like spent change, the Verdant spoke one more time. Not hungry now. Not bargaining. Almost—almost—curious.

*Nobody ever gave me anything.*

I should have run. I should have finished it and walked away. Instead I knelt in the burning grass, held out my hand, and gave it the only thing I had left that was truly mine to give: the memory of this moment. The sighing roots. The falling light. The look on a monster's face when it learns it can be fed without feeding.

*Here,* I said. *Freely given. Try it.*

The Verdant took the memory the way a drowning man takes air.

And let go.

The crown—I felt it crack from a mile away, felt it through the ground, a sound like a bell breaking, like a jaw unclenching after forty years. The garden's wall folded down into warm dust. The humming stopped, and in the silence underneath it I heard, faint and far off, the city waking up to itself, people in the streets stopping mid-step and saying each other's names out loud just to hear them fit again.

Marlow found me sitting in the ash, soot-black, sobbing, laughing, both at once, neither winning.

"Well," he said, sitting down heavily beside me in the ruins of paradise. "The elders are going to have questions about the charter."

"The charter's burned, Marlow. Everything's burned."

He looked around at the grey, soft, strangely peaceful ruin—at the ash that already smelled, underneath everything, like spring soil. "No," he said slowly. "It smells like a beginning. Which is worse, honestly. Beginnings always mean paperwork."

I laughed until my ribs hurt, and then, over his shoulder, I saw him coming across the ash—a thin, stooped figure in a crownless circlet, an old man walking carefully, like someone testing ice, carrying in his eyes the whole returned weight of a reign he finally remembered.

King Aldous reached us, sank to his knees in the ash beside me, and said, in a voice rusted from decades of disuse:

"Keeper. I remember everything now. God help me—I remember *everything*."
