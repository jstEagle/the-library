## 22. Hazel and the Garden

The tree on the guildhall roof is forty years old this spring, which makes it younger than my knees and considerably better behaved.

I say *tree* the way you might say *dog* about an animal that is technically a dog but has opinions. It grew from the last seed of the Verdant—the one I pulled from the ash of the garden with my own two hands, against every piece of advice Marlow gave me, which was all of it. He said I was mad. He said some things should stay burned. Then he helped me dig the hole, because that was always Marlow's way: arguing with his sleeves rolled up.

I planted it freely. That mattered. Everything about that creature had always come down to whether a thing was taken or given, and I wanted to see what it would become if it started its whole long life on the right side of that line.

It became this: a tree with silver-grey bark and leaves like small green coins, that does not take anything from anyone. It gives. Sit beneath it long enough and it will hand you back something you'd lost—a name, a face, the smell of your mother's kitchen, the particular way your father said your name when he wasn't angry. Not everything. Just enough. It gives the way a good host pours wine: generously, but watching your cup.

I tend it every morning. Water, pruning, the occasional conversation, because a tree grown from a thing that once ate a kingdom's memories deserves at least the courtesy of being talked to.

This morning, as it happens, there's a child on my roof.

* * *

She came up the stair without knocking, which is guildhall custom—doors are for people with something to hide, and we haven't hidden anything in four decades. She's maybe eight, gap-toothed, wearing the enormous seriousness children wear when they've been sent on an errand by other children. Behind her, visible through the roof door, three more faces retreat guiltily down the stairs. Scouts. The rest of the warband waits below.

"You're the ledgerman," she says. Not a question. Children these days grow up on stories; they know my face the way I knew my grandmother's, from being told about it until it felt like memory.

"I'm a ledgerman," I correct, because precision matters in my trade. "There are eleven of us now."

"Eleven." She considers this. "My gran says there used to be hundreds."

"There did. Hundreds of people writing down what everyone owed." I set down my watering can. "We're smaller now, but we're better company."

She edges closer to the tree, the way children do, drawn and wary at once. The leaves stir though there's no wind. A memory drifts down and settles over her like pollen—I can see it land, see her eyes go wide and soft. Probably something small. A birthday. A dog. Whatever the tree judged she was missing.

"Sorry," she says, blinking. "It just—it gave me something."

"It does that. Don't apologize to it, it'll get smug."

"What is it?"

"That," I say, "is a very long story."

"My gran says you're going to tell it anyway. She says you always do. She says you told her the whole thing once when she was trying to buy bread."

Marlow would have laughed. Marlow, who stayed in the ash garden until his death—twenty-two years tending ground where stolen things found their way home, turning away no one, including, twice, the same woman who is now this child's grandmother. Small world. It always was. We just spent a century pretending otherwise.

"Well then," I said. "Sit down. Mind the roots—they're shallow but they hold grudges."

She sits cross-legged in the shade, and I lower myself onto my bench with the sound of an old man negotiating with his own joints, and I begin where I always begin.

* * *

"A long time ago," I tell her, "there was a famine."

"Not this famine. An older one, before your gran's gran was born. And there was a guild—not mine yet, but mine eventually—that made a bargain. The kingdom was starving, and the guild promised abundance, and what they got instead was a seedling that learned hunger. You can't blame a seedling for learning. That's what seeds do. But you can blame the gardeners who kept watering it after they saw what it ate."

"It ate memories," she says. She knows this part. They all know this part. But knowing a story isn't the same as hearing it, and hearing it isn't the same as understanding it, and I have forty years of practice telling the difference.

"It ate memories," I agree. "And it wore a crown while it did. There was a king—there's a statue of him in the capital square now, the real him, not the jaw-faced thing they paraded around before—and he sold his kingdom's remembering to save its body. It seemed like a fair trade at the time. Most terrible trades do. That's practically the first rule of ledgers: the worse the trade, the fairer it looks when you're desperate."

"And you stopped it."

"I didn't stop it. That's the part everyone gets wrong." I lean forward. "A dragon stopped it. Her name was Emberlow, and she was the last of her kind, and she was forgetting herself so badly by the end that she couldn't remember how to burn. Do you understand what that means? Fire was what she *was*, and she was losing it, the way your gran might lose a word she's known all her life. And in the end, with almost nothing left, she gave me the last clear thing she had. Her first flight. Freely given. And because it was given and never taken, nothing could eat it. Nothing could ever eat it."

The child frowns. "Why not?"

"Ah." I hold up a finger. "That's the whole secret, so pay attention. A taken memory is a debt. It belongs to someone else, and debts can be collected, traded, eaten. But a gift—" I tap my chest, where Emberlow's flight still lives, bright as the day she handed it to me, forty years undimmed. "A gift belongs to whoever holds it. Nobody can take it back. Not even the thing that ate a kingdom."

"So the dragon beat it by giving something away."

"The dragon beat it by giving something away," I confirm, "and then I beat it the same way, and then Marlow beat it the same way, and honestly by the end it barely put up a fight. Hunger doesn't know what to do with generosity. It's like teaching a wolf to knit."

She giggles. Above us, the tree drops a leaf into her lap, and she picks it up carefully, and I watch her decide—consciously, visibly, the way children decide things—to keep it forever.

* * *

"Now," I say, "your actual question. You were sent up here to ask what the guild does. I can hear the other three waiting at the bottom of the stairs. Tell them the acoustics up here are excellent and I heard every whisper."

She goes pink. "What *does* the guild do?"

I could give her the long answer. I have the long answer; I've been polishing it for decades. I could tell her about the eleven of us, and the reading rooms, and the way we sit with the grieving and write down what they choose to give rather than what was torn from them. I could tell her about the new ledger, whose first entry is a dragon's first flight, recorded not as property but as a present, and how every entry since follows that shape. I could tell her about the reunions—some joyful, some unbearable, both kinds tended by the same patient hands Marlow taught us to use.

But she's eight, and the morning is warm, and somewhere below us her warband is holding its breath.

"We remember for each other," I say.

She blinks. "That's it?"

"That's it. That's the whole charter. It took us a hundred years and a dead dragon and a burned garden to get it down to six words, so I'd appreciate it if you didn't ask for a refund."

"But what do you *do*?"

"We remember for each other," I say again, patiently. "When someone can't hold their own past anymore, we hold it for them. When someone wants to give a memory away—to a child, to a friend, to the tree—we write it down so the giving is real. When someone comes back for what they left with us, we hand it over, no interest, no fees, no fine print. Your gran's mother, did you know? We held her wedding day for thirty years because your gran's father traded it away in a bad winter, and when he earned it back we returned it, and your gran was born nine months later to the week." I shrug. "Some entries work out very well indeed."

The child looks at the tree. The tree, I swear, looks back.

"And the tree remembers too?"

"The tree remembers best of all of us. It's making up for lost time." I pat the bench beside me. "Come here. There's something I only show to people who ask the right questions."

* * *

At the base of the trunk, half-hidden by a frond of ferns I pretend I haven't cultivated there, the bark parts into a small hollow. Inside it, always, there is a single fruit—small, green-gold, warm to the touch like a stone left in the sun.

"One grows at a time," I tell her. "It takes a year. And it can only be picked by someone who intends to give it away. Try to pick it for yourself and it goes sour as vinegar. Believe me. Marlow tried, once, just to test it, and his face was a picture for a week."

"What's inside it?"

"A memory. Whoever eats it gets one—but not their own. Someone else's, chosen by the tree, given freely. My grandmother's laugh went out in one of these, years ago, to a woman in the river country who hadn't laughed since the floods. Emberlow's fire went out in another, to a boy who was afraid of the dark." I close the hollow gently, the way you'd close a locket. "The Verdant spent a thousand years taking. This is it spending the rest of forever giving back. One fruit at a time. It'll be at it long after I'm gone."

"Will you be gone soon?" Children. No diplomacy whatsoever. I admire it enormously.

"Soon enough. But not today, and not before lunch, and not before you run along and tell your warband the answer, because if you keep it to yourself I'll have to tell the whole story again tomorrow and my voice is eighty-one years old."

She stands, brushes off her skirt, pauses. Then, with the gravity of a diplomat concluding a treaty: "My gran says you knew her when she was young. In the ash garden. She says you gave her back her brother's name."

"I remember," I say. And I do—freely given, freely kept, bright as a dragon's first morning. "Tell her the tree says hello. She'll know what that means."

She runs for the stairs. At the doorway she stops, turns, and shouts it down into the guildhall with the full force of eight-year-old lungs:

*"THEY REMEMBER FOR EACH OTHER!"*

A pause. Then three smaller voices repeat it, getting it slightly wrong, and someone laughs, and footsteps scatter off toward whatever mischief children conduct when properly briefed.

* * *

I sit a while longer under the branches.

The leaves turn over in the light. Somewhere in the silver bark, slow as sap, the old hunger keeps becoming something else—one ring at a time, one gift at a time, a monster learning, across centuries, to be a host. I think of my grandmother, whose stolen years grew on a single tree in a walled paradise, and who came home to me anyway, settling in like heirlooms rather than wounds. I think of a king who abdicated a throne of teeth and spent his last decade writing letters of apology to people who mostly wrote back. I think of a dragon's wings against a burning sky, and a coward who chose penance over pardon, and a stranger dying in a doorway who pressed a map into a young man's hands and changed everything.

Mostly, though, I think about the ledger downstairs. Tonight I'll add a new entry. Not a debt. Never a debt again. Just a record: *Today a child asked what we do, and the answer fit in six words, and she carried it down the stairs like something breakable and precious, which is exactly what it is.*

The tree drops one more leaf into my lap. I let it stay.

Below, faintly, the guildhall doors open for the morning—debtors no longer, givers now, coming up the steps with their hands full of things they want to hand over. I hear Marlow's successor greeting them, warm and unhurried.

I finish my tea. The garden grows.

We remember for each other. It's enough. It was always enough.
