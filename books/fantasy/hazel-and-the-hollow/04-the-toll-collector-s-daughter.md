## 4. The Toll Collector's Daughter

Hazel had learned to count in salt.

Not the way other children counted—on fingers, on beads, on the knots their mothers tied in apron strings—but in the white crust that crept up her ankles as she walked, in the crystals that glittered on her sleeves each morning when she woke beneath whatever hedge or culvert had sheltered her through the night. The salt flats had been her whole world once: the shimmering pan where her father collected his tolls from the caravans crossing toward the mountains, the low stone tollhouse with its crooked chimney, the smell of brine and bread and her grandmother's dried lavender hung above the door.

Now the flats were a wound, and Hazel counted the days since it had been made in salt-crusted footsteps. Forty-one. Forty-two. She had stopped being sure somewhere around thirty, because some mornings the sun rose in the wrong place and she had to trust her feet instead of her eyes.

Her grandmother had called it *unwriting*. She had said the word with her mouth full of blood, propped against the doorframe of the tollhouse while the world behind them went strange and soft, like a painting left out in the rain.

"They're not burning us, little wren," Gran had said, pressing the iron key of the tollhouse into Hazel's palm and folding her small fingers over it. "Burning leaves ash. Ash is something. This is worse. They are taking us out of the sentence of the world."

Hazel hadn't understood then. She understood now, walking through country that did not know her, that had never known her, that would never admit to knowing her. When she asked for water at farmhouses, doors closed before she finished speaking—not rudely, but with the blank confusion of people who could not quite say why they were closing them. When she begged bread at markets, coins passed through her cupped hands as though her palms were made of morning fog. Once, a shepherd's dog had looked straight at her and then looked *through* her, whining at nothing, and Hazel had cried for an hour behind a stone wall, because if even dogs forgot Vessany, what hope was there in anything else?

But her grandmother had given her two things before the end, and Hazel carried both like coals banked in her chest.

The first was the key. Cold iron, older than any lock she'd ever seen, worked into the shape of a door seen edge-on—a door that was only a line, a threshold collapsed into itself. Gran had worn it on a cord around her neck until the day she died, and when she pressed it into Hazel's hand, the metal was warm from her skin, and Hazel had thought: *this is the warmest thing in the world, this is the last warm thing.*

The second was the instruction.

"Find the keeper of the gate," Gran had said, and her voice had been going thin and papery for three days, the unwriting eating inward from the edges of her the way frost eats a window. "The Hollow Gate. A man keeps it who has made himself small enough to hide inside his own ledgers. He will not want to see you. He will be afraid. Be afraid *with* him, little wren, but do not go away."

"How will I know him?"

"You'll know him by his ink," Gran had said, and smiled with cracked lips. "A man who spends thirty years writing down other people's names smells like nothing else in the world. Ink and old promises." Her hand had found Hazel's hair, clumsy with weakness. "And when you speak to him, speak the old tongue. Speak it loud. It is the only thing they could not take from us, because it lives in the body—in the tongue, in the throat—and you cannot unwrite a throat without killing it first."

Gran died at dusk on the third day, and the unwriting took her gently after that, the way tide takes a sandcastle. By full dark there was no body. There was no grave. There was only a girl sitting alone in a doorway that opened onto a house that was no longer there, holding an iron key, saying words in a language that predated every kingdom on the map, and beginning, very quietly, to count her steps away from home.

Forty-two days later, she stood before the Hollow Gate.

* * *

It did not look like much.

That was Hazel's first thought, and she felt almost insulted by it. After forty-two days of hunger and invisibility and sleeping with the key clutched so hard its shape printed itself into her palm, she had imagined—she didn't know what. Towers. Fire. Something worthy of the word *gate*, which in Gran's mouth had always sounded enormous, load-bearing, like the word *sky*.

Instead there was a door set into a hillside, framed in black wood gone silver with age, and above it a lintel carved with symbols that made Hazel's breath catch—the same looping script as the oldest pages of Gran's book, the one book the unwriting had somehow missed, hidden in a floor cavity beneath the hearthstone. And beside the door, a bell-pull of braided leather, worn smooth by centuries of hands.

Beyond the door, light. Lamplight, gold and steady, and the faint scratch-scratch of a nib on paper.

Someone was in there. Someone was *writing*.

Hazel's legs, which had carried her over mountains and across rivers that forgot her halfway through the ford, abruptly refused to hold her. She sat down hard on the doorstep, in the mud, in the drizzle, and put her face in her hands, and for a long moment she simply breathed.

She was filthy. She knew it without looking—salt-stiff clothes, hair matted into ropes, boots held together with wire and prayer. Forty-two days of being unseen had made her careless of being seen; there had been no one to see. But now there was a door, and lamplight, and a keeper, and suddenly she wanted desperately to be clean, to be presentable, to be the kind of child a grown man would look at and believe.

*He will not want to see you,* Gran had said. *He will be afraid.*

"All right," Hazel whispered to the door, in the common tongue, because it seemed polite to warn it. "I'm coming in anyway."

She pulled the bell.

* * *

The man who opened the door was exactly what Gran had promised and nothing like what Hazel had pictured.

He was thin and stooped and gray, with ink stains worked so deep into his fingers they looked less like stains than like shadow, as though his hands had been dipped past the knuckle in permanent dusk. His eyes behind his spectacles were the pale, startled color of a creature that has lived underground a long time. He smelled—before he said a single word, before he even finished opening the door—of ink and lamp oil and paper dust and something underneath all of it, something older and stranger, like the air in a room where promises have been kept for a very long time.

*Ink and old promises.* Gran had never lied to her. Even dying, even unwritten, she had not lied.

But he flinched. That was the thing Hazel noticed second, and it frightened her more than anything else in forty-two days. He flinched when he saw her—not at her dirt, not at her size, but at something else, something about her that she couldn't see—and his eyes went wide behind the spectacles, and his mouth opened and closed twice before any sound came out.

Then she spoke the old tongue, and everything changed.

"*Esse varo telenn ath?*" she said. *Are you the keeper of the door?*

She watched the words land on him like stones dropped into still water. Watched him go pale, then gray, then some third color that faces aren't supposed to be. Watched his hand rise to the doorframe as though the ground had tilted.

He answered her. In the old tongue. Badly—his accent was terrible, all wrong rhythms, a scholar's tongue and not a mother's—but he answered, and the sound of it went through Hazel like sunlight through cold water. Words from home. Words from Gran's knee, from the tollhouse, from the salt flats, spoken by a living mouth in a world that had forgotten every one of them.

She should have wept. Part of her wanted to, hugely, helplessly. But Gran had also taught her this: *never show a stranger your whole heart in the first minute, little wren. Strangers spend hearts like small coin.* So instead of crying, Hazel lifted her chin, and pushed past him into the warmth, and stood dripping on his clean floor, and waited to see what he would do.

What he did was stare at her as though she were a ghost, or a debt come due, or both.

* * *

He gave her tea. That counted for something. Gran had a whole philosophy about tea—it was the smallest possible kindness between strangers, the coin of the realm of decency—and this gray, stooped, trembling man had put a chipped blue cup in her hands before he asked her a single question, and his hands had shaken doing it.

They sat across a table buried under ledgers, and Hazel drank tea too hot and too sweet, and studied him the way she'd studied strangers all along the road: for danger, for softness, for the tells that separated people who would help from people who would turn you out.

He was afraid. That was plain as weather. But here was the thing Hazel had learned in forty-two days of being nobody: fear comes in two kinds. There is the fear of the man who shuts the door, and there is the fear of the man who opens it anyway with shaking hands. She had met plenty of the first kind. They always had good reasons. The reasons never got her fed.

This one had opened the door. Had given tea. Had answered in the old tongue, badly, which meant he had *learned* it—from books, from dead pages—which meant somewhere in this hillside full of ledgers were pages written in her grandmother's language, and the thought made Hazel's chest ache so sharply she had to set down the cup.

He kept glancing at her hands. At the key, specifically, hanging on its cord outside her shirt where she'd taken to wearing it openly, a dare and a beacon both. Every time his eyes touched it, he flinched again, small and involuntary, like a man touching a stove he already knew was hot.

*You know what this is,* Hazel thought. *You know exactly what this is, and it scares you, and you haven't thrown me out yet.*

That was something. That was maybe everything.

But she didn't trust him. She wanted to be clear with herself about that, sitting in his lamplight drinking his tea. Trust was a bridge, and bridges took time to build, and this man was a guild-man—Gran had warned her about the guild too, in the last lucid hour, her grip sudden and fierce on Hazel's wrist: *"The guild wrote us out, little wren. Remember that. Whatever face it shows you, remember."* So this ink-smelling keeper belonged, however humbly, however sadly, to the family of the people who had erased her grandmother mid-word, mid-breath, mid-love.

And yet.

And yet he had answered in the old tongue. And yet he smelled like promises kept. And yet when she had shivered—he had risen without a word and fetched a blanket that smelled of cedar and long storage, and wrapped it around her shoulders with a carefulness that suggested he had not touched another person in years and was relearning how, the way one relearns a language.

"I'm not leaving," Hazel told him, in the common tongue, so there could be no mistake. Her voice came out smaller than she wanted, rusted from disuse. "Whatever you're afraid of. I walked forty-two days. I slept in ditches. I watched my grandmother—" Her throat closed. She swallowed it down. Grief could wait; grief had gotten very good at waiting, these six weeks. "I have nowhere else. There *is* nowhere else. My whole country is a hole in the map and you know it, I saw you know it, the moment I said the words you went white as salt."

The keeper looked at her for a long moment. Then he did something strange: he reached across the table, past the ledgers, and turned one of them toward her—an old one, bound in green leather gone brittle, its pages soft as cloth—and opened it to a place marked with a ribbon.

There was writing there. Old writing. *Her* writing, the old tongue, in a hand so fine it looked embroidered.

"This," he said quietly, "is older than the guild. Older than me. Older than everyone I work for." His finger hovered over the page without touching it. "And until tonight, I believed I was the only person alive who could read it."

Hazel looked at the page, and the page looked back, and something in her chest that had been clenched since the salt flats loosened by one degree. Not trust. Not yet. But the beginning of the thing that comes before trust, which is recognition.

"My grandmother could read it better," she said. "She taught me on her knee. Before—" She stopped. Started again, stubborn, because she had come too far to be turned away by her own grief. "Before your guild erased her."

The flinch again. Deeper this time. He didn't deny it. That, too, counted for something—or against him, she wasn't sure which, and wouldn't be for a long time.

"What's your name?" he asked.

And Hazel laughed—a short, cracked, astonished sound, because of all the questions, all the dangers, all the things a keeper of impossible ledgers might ask a mud-streaked child who had just accused his masters of erasure, he asked the one question nobody had been able to answer in forty-two days.

"That's the problem," she said. "Ask anyone else. Ask the farmers whose doors close. Ask the dogs. Ask the map." She leaned forward, and the key swung free of her collar and caught the lamplight, and she watched his eyes follow it the way a drowning man watches a rope. "Nobody can. We don't have names anymore. We have holes shaped like names. You people took them."

"Not my name," the keeper said softly, and then, stranger and quieter, almost to himself: "*My* name..."

Hazel didn't understand that. She tucked it away with all the other things she didn't understand, in the growing pack she carried behind her ribs alongside the key and the grief and the forty-two days.

Outside, the rain thickened against the hillside, and somewhere far off, too far to be real, too near to ignore, something howled—not a wolf, wolves had a music to it, and this was flat and procedural, like a list being read aloud.

The keeper's head came up. For the first time since she'd arrived, he looked less afraid of her than of whatever was listening beyond his door.

"Drink your tea," he said, and began, quickly, quietly, closing the ledgers one by one. "And then I need you to tell me everything, little wren—"

Hazel went rigid. "*What did you call me?*"

"—from the beginning," he finished, and blinked at her expression. "Your grandmother's phrase. From the old records. I assumed—I read it in a margin, decades ago, a note in that hand—" He gestured at the green ledger, at the embroidered-fine script. "*Little wren.* I thought it was a pet name someone lost."

Hazel stared at him. Somewhere under her breastbone, under the key and the grief and the stubbornness, something turned over—small, unwilling, hopeful as a sprout in salt.

"It wasn't lost," she said. "It was mine. She called me that every day of my life."

The keeper removed his spectacles and polished them very slowly on his sleeve, and when he put them back on, his eyes were wet, and Hazel decided—for tonight, for tonight only—that she would stay.

"And tomorrow," she added, because she was her grandmother's granddaughter, and hope was a thing you paid for in vigilance, "you're going to tell me what just howled."
