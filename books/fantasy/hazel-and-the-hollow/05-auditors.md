## 5. Auditors

The rumor arrived three days before the Auditors did, in the way rumors always arrived at the Hollow Gate: folded inside an ordinary delivery, disguised as something dull.

Wendell found it on a Tuesday morning, tucked between a consignment note for six vials of bottled homesickness and an invoice for a decade of borrowed sleep owed by a merchant in Lowmarsh. The slip of paper was gray, unmarked, and smelled faintly of cold iron—the guild's preferred stationery for messages that were never meant to exist. He read it twice, standing very still among his shelves of ledgers, while the morning light came through the high window in its usual dusty columns and the kettle ticked itself toward boiling on the little stove he was not technically permitted to own.

*Unwritten child sighted at the borderlands. Inspectors dispatched. Prepare the Gate for review.*

He read it a third time, as though the words might rearrange themselves into something kinder. They did not.

Behind him, in the back room where she had slept for two nights now on a pallet of folded sacking, Hazel was dreaming. He could hear her breathing—quick, shallow, the breathing of a child who had learned that sleep was a country with poor borders, easily crossed by things that meant her harm. She talked sometimes in that old tongue, the language that predated kingdoms, syllables falling out of her mouth like river stones worn smooth by centuries no one else remembered. Last night she had said a word over and over—*ammarath, ammarath*—and when he'd checked it against the oldest ledger, the one written before the guild existed, he had found it. *Ammarath.* Grandmother. Or perhaps simply: *the one who kept me.*

Wendell set the gray slip down on his desk and pressed both palms flat against the wood, as if he could hold the desk—and himself, and the whole careful architecture of his invisible life—steady by force.

They were coming. Of course they were coming. A child who spoke a dead nation's tongue, walking out of salt flats where a village used to be, asking after doors—there was no version of the guild's attention that ended well. The Auditors would come with their gray coats and their gray eyes and their instruments for measuring truth the way a butcher measures meat, and they would find Hazel, and then—

And then what? He did not know. That was the worst of it. Thirty years of keeping the Hollow Gate's books had taught him every punishment the guild could levy against a keeper who erred: fines, demotion, the slow humiliation of being reassigned to some sub-basement archive where the ledgers were all blank. But he had never once seen what they did with an unwritten thing. Because unwritten things were not supposed to exist. The guild's whole trade ran on the principle that everything could be recorded—grief, years, names, debts—and what could be recorded could be owned. An unwritten child was not merchandise. She was a contradiction. And Wendell had learned, in thirty years of balancing impossible accounts, that contradictions were the one thing institutions could not tolerate.

The kettle began to whistle. He silenced it with a hand that only trembled a little.

"Hazel," he said quietly, and then, because she woke like a small animal, ready to run, "it's morning. There's bread."

* * *

She ate standing up, the way she did everything—poised on the balls of her feet, back never quite to a wall, eyes tracking his hands whenever they moved. She was perhaps nine years old, small for it, with salt-cracked lips and hair the color of wet slate, and she watched him now across the desk with an expression he was beginning to recognize as her default setting: suspicion braided tight around hope, like wire around a living branch.

"They're coming," he told her. There was no point softening it; she had fled an entire erased country. Softness would insult her. "Auditors. Guild inspectors. Three days, maybe less."

Hazel chewed, swallowed, and said something in the old tongue. Then, catching his look, she translated in her careful, accented Common: "What will they do?"

"I don't know." He hated saying it. He hated more how much she seemed to expect it—how unsurprised she was by the admission, as though adults admitting ignorance were simply weather in her world. "But I know this: they cannot take what they cannot find. And they cannot find—" he hesitated, because the idea had been forming in him since dawn, half-formed and terrible, "—what is already recorded."

Her eyes narrowed. "I don't understand."

"Neither do I, entirely." He turned to the shelf behind him and drew down a ledger he almost never touched: Volume One of the Gate's current cycle, the intake registry, the book where everything that passed through the Hollow Door was entered, weighed, and bound to its entry forever. "The guild believes the ledger is the world. That's not poetry—it's doctrine. What is written here *is*, in the way the guild understands existence. And what is written can be... amended. By someone with the authority to amend it." He looked at his own hands. Ink-stained, precise, thirty years obedient. "I have that authority. It's the only authority I have."

"You want to write me into a book."

"I want to write you into a book so thoroughly that you stop being a rumor and become a line item. Rumors get investigated. Line items get filed." He opened the ledger to a fresh page. The paper was heavy, cream-colored, faintly warm to the touch the way all guild paper was warm, as though the books themselves had a pulse. "There's a category for it. There's always a category. 'Specimens, inert.' 'Effects, miscellaneous.' Things too trivial to inspect but too present to ignore. If you are an effect—if you are a miscellaneous object stored in the keeper's back room, duly noted, duly valued—then the Auditors will glance at their copy of my registry, see your entry, and move on. Auditors trust paper the way priests trust scripture. It's their whole weakness."

"And if they don't trust it?"

Wendell was quiet a moment. Outside, wind moved through the hollow behind the gate, making the old stone moan its one long note. "Then we're past cleverness," he said. "But I think they'll trust it. I've kept these books for thirty years without a single discrepancy. My records have weight. That weight has to go somewhere, and I intend for it to go under you."

Hazel studied him for a long moment, and he saw her doing the arithmetic children do—weighing whether this ink-smelling man with his careful voice was a door or a trap. He did not blame her for the slowness of it. He only wished, obscurely, that he could tell her which he was. He wasn't entirely sure himself.

"What does it cost?" she asked finally. "Nothing costs nothing. Grandmother said that. Grandmother said especially nothing costs nothing."

He should have lied to her. He recognized that later, turning the moment over like a stone with something glinting underneath. He should have said *nothing, it costs nothing,* and let her walk into the trick unknowing, the way the guild walked everyone into its tricks. Instead he heard himself say, honestly, because thirty years of honest bookkeeping apparently rotted a man's capacity for the alternative:

"It costs me. The entry has to be anchored to something of equal substance—that's the law of the ledger, the first law. A record must balance. To make you real on paper, something else has to become less real. And the only currency I hold in sufficient quantity..." He touched the edge of the page. "...is myself."

* * *

The Auditors arrived a day early, because of course they did.

Wendell heard the gate mechanism before he saw them—the deep mechanical sigh of the Hollow Door cycling through its states, though no delivery was scheduled. He had spent the previous night writing Hazel into existence, and the memory of it sat in his chest like a swallowed coal.

It had been simple, mechanically. That was the horror of it. He had entered her as *one (1) item, personal effects, provenance uncertain, value nominal: child's coat, wool, salt-damaged; contents miscellaneous.* He had written her into the registry as baggage—as a coat with things inside it—and the ledger had accepted the entry with a soft sound, almost a sigh, the sound of paper agreeing. But the balancing clause had lit up beneath his pen like a hairline crack filling with red ink, demanding equivalence, demanding payment, and there had been only one account under his authority with anything in it.

His name. Not the letters of it—he still had those, though he hadn't spoken them aloud since he was twenty-two—but the recorded weight of them. Thirty years of service, thirty years of perfect entries, all the accumulated substance of Wendell Brightwater as the guild understood him. The ledger had asked for three years of it. It had presented the figure the way a toll bridge presents its price: without malice, without mercy, as simple arithmetic.

He had signed. His pen had barely shaken. And somewhere in the guild's master volumes, three years of Wendell Brightwater had quietly ceased to have ever happened—which meant, according to doctrine, that they had never happened at all. He did not yet know which three. He suspected he would find out the way one finds out about a missing tooth: gradually, and always by surprise, the tongue going to a gap that shouldn't be there.

Now he stood in the great receiving hall with his hands clasped behind his back and his face arranged into the mild, forgettable expression he had polished over three decades, and watched three figures in gray come through the door.

The lead Auditor was tall and dry as a winter stalk, with spectacles that caught the lamplight and turned his eyes into two small eclipses. The other two flanked him in silence, carrying between them a case of instruments—brass calipers, tuning forks, a scale whose pans hung empty and hungry. Wendell knew the type. He had served tea to their kind twice before in thirty years, routine inspections, pleasant enough in the way that a knife is pleasant right up until it isn't.

"Keeper." The lead Auditor's voice was papery. He did not offer a name. Auditors never did; a name was a handle, and handles could be grasped. "You've been notified of the purpose of our visit."

"Rumors of an irregularity at the borderlands," Wendell said. "I was distressed to hear it. The Gate keeps clean books, sir. It always has."

"Clean books." The Auditor smiled without warmth, the way a lock smiles. "Then this will be brief. We'll see the registry. All of it. And we'll walk the premises."

"Of course." Wendell bowed slightly and led them toward his office, each step measured, each breath rationed. Behind his sternum his heart performed its work with the steady desperation of a man bailing water. *You have done nothing wrong,* he told himself, and then corrected it, because lying to himself was a skill he had never developed: *You have done one wrong thing. You have hidden it inside a thousand right ones. Now be a wall.*

The inspection was everything he had feared and nothing he had expected. The Auditors did not rummage. They *read*. The lead Auditor stood at Wendell's desk and turned the pages of the intake registry with fingers that moved like a blind man's, reading by touch, by weight, by some sense finer than sight—and Wendell stood aside and watched him approach the entry, page by page, the way one watches weather approach across a flat field.

*One (1) item, personal effects, provenance uncertain, value nominal: child's coat, wool, salt-damaged; contents miscellaneous.*

The Auditor stopped on the page. His finger rested below the line of ink. The eclipses of his eyes lifted and fixed on Wendell.

"This entry," he said. "Filed two nights ago."

"Yes, sir. A consignment left at the outer post. No courier claimed it."

"No courier." The finger tapped once, softly, on the word *contents*. "The entry feels... recent."

"The entry is accurate, sir. You're welcome to verify the item against it."

And here was the moment Wendell had built the whole night around, the hinge on which everything swung: the Auditor's gaze sliding past him, toward the back room, toward the shelf where—under a dust cloth, beside jars of preserved moth-wing and a crate of unlabeled keys—sat a small shape wrapped in burlap and twine, exactly the size and weight of a folded child's coat. Wendell had written the description so precisely, anchored it so firmly to physical fact, that the ledger had *helped*: the bundle weighed what the entry said it weighed, smelled faintly of salt and wool, held its shape like cloth. The record and the world, agreeing perfectly. Hazel herself was at that moment sitting inside the oldest ledger in the building—the founding volume, the one written before the guild existed—because Wendell had discovered, in a fever of desperate scholarship at three in the morning, that the ancient book's pages were the one place in the Hollow Gate where the guild's instruments went quiet. Old magic underwrote the new. The founding ledger did not report to anyone.

The lead Auditor crossed the room. Lifted the dust cloth. Unwrapped one corner of the burlap and peered at the bundled coat within—real, actual, salt-stiffened, purchased that morning from a rag cart at ruinous expense—and consulted a slim gray notebook of his own.

"Consignment logged at the outer post," he murmured. "No claimant. Value nominal." He rewrapped the corner with a delicacy that was somehow worse than roughness. "In order."

Wendell breathed. It took everything he had to make the breath silent.

They walked the premises for another hour. They measured the Hollow Door with their brass calipers and struck it with their tuning forks and listened to its note with closed eyes. They checked the seals on the grief-jars and counted the vials of borrowed years against their entries and found, everywhere, the same immaculate correspondence that thirty years of Wendell Brightwater had guaranteed. And through all of it Wendell stood at his post, answered every question in his mild forgettable voice, and felt something happening inside him that he had no ledger-category for.

It was terror, at first. Terror had carried him through the night of writing, through the signing away of three years, through the long gray morning. But somewhere in the second hour—somewhere around the moment the lead Auditor held Hazel's entry up to the light and found it seamless—terror changed its chemistry. It densified. It settled downward through his chest and hardened into something with edges and weight.

Resolve.

Because he understood, standing in that lamplit hall while men in gray verified his lies and found them true, that he had crossed a line from which the guild did not issue maps back. For thirty years he had been honest inside a dishonest institution, and had told himself that this was integrity—the clean hands, the balanced books, the small dignities of the scrupulous clerk. But honesty in service of the guild's ledger was just obedience with good posture. This—this forged entry, this stolen coat, these three vanished years, this child sleeping inside a dead country's book—this was the first thing he had ever done that was actually *his*.

The Auditors packed their instruments. At the door, the lead one paused.

"The rumor concerns a child," he said, not turning around. "Unwritten. Speaking a dead tongue."

"I've seen no child, sir. Only the consignment."

"Mm." The eclipse-eyes reflected the Hollow Door, dark and patient. "The guild erases things, Keeper. It's necessary work. But erasure leaves... textures. Ripples. A country the size of Vessany leaves ripples for decades." He adjusted his spectacles. "We will find the source of the ripple. We always do. It's only arithmetic."

"Of course, sir," said Wendell Brightwater, whose arithmetic had just cost him three years and would gladly cost him thirty more. "Safe travels."

The door sighed shut behind them. Wendell stood alone in the hall until the echo of the mechanism faded, and then he walked—not quickly, because quickness was a habit of the guilty—to the back room, to the founding ledger, and opened it.

Hazel climbed out of the pages like a swimmer coming up through green water, blinking, disoriented, smelling of vellum and deep places. She looked at him. He looked at her. Neither of them spoke for a moment, and in that moment Wendell understood that whatever he had been before tonight—a keeper, a clerk, a ghost in his own life—was over. The guild had sent three gray shadows through his door and he had lied to them with a straight spine, and there was no version of the future in which they did not learn of it eventually. The ripples, as the Auditor said, always spread.

"We can't stay here," he said.

Hazel wiped vellum-dust from her cheek and regarded him with those old, old eyes. "Where do we go?"

"I know a man," Wendell said, and was surprised to hear something almost like hope in his own voice, rusty as an unused key. "A smuggler. He owes money to everyone in the borderlands except, by some miracle, me." He was already reaching for his coat, already calculating what could be carried and what had to burn. "God help us both, I think we need Brannoc Vale."

From deep within the founding ledger, faint as breath, a page turned itself.
