## 7. Nine Names for One Door

The founding ledger smelled like a cellar that had once been a garden.

Hazel noticed this on the second night in the Hollow Gate, when Wendell finally let her touch the book. It was wrapped in oilcloth inside a locked drawer inside a locked cabinet inside a room behind the counting floor, and it took him the better part of an hour to get it open, muttering apologies the whole time as though the locks were guests he'd kept waiting. When the wrappings came away, the smell rose up out of the pages—damp and green and faintly sweet, like mint gone over in the dark.

"That's Vessany," she said.

Wendell looked at her over his spectacles. "The ledger?"

"The paper." She pressed her palm flat against the first page, careful of the ink. "They made it from reeds off the Sennet marshes. Grandmother said you could smell summer in it even in winter." Her throat tightened around the word *grandmother*, but she had gotten good at that—at tightening and letting go, tightening and letting go, like working a knot loose a little at a time instead of all at once. "It's really ours."

"It's really yours," Wendell agreed quietly, and turned the page.

Brannoc sprawled in the corner with his boots up on a stool nobody had invited him to put them on, eating plums out of a paper sack and pretending not to be interested, which was how Hazel had learned he was interested in nearly everything. He'd been a courier once, he'd said, back before his disgrace, which meant he could read manifests in six languages and cargo seals in four more. But this book stopped him cold. He'd looked at the opening page for a long moment, turned it sideways, turned it back, and then set it down with the carefulness of a man setting down something that might be sleeping.

"Older than anything I ever carried," he'd said. "Older than anything anybody carried. This isn't guild script. This is what they were writing *before* there was a guild."

"There was always going to be a guild," Wendell murmured. "Somebody just hadn't invented the word yet."

Now, by lamplight, the three of them bent over the founding ledger while the counting floor above them ticked and settled like a ship at anchor. Hazel sat closest to the pages because she was the only one who could read them—not all of them, not easily, the letters swam sometimes when she was tired—but enough. The old tongue lived in her mouth the way water lives in a well. She hadn't known until this week that it didn't live in everyone's.

"Here." She traced a line of script that curled down the margin like a vine. "This part repeats. It's a warning. My grandmother used to say it when we crossed the toll bridge—*nine fastenings, nine forgettings.* I thought it was about the bridge."

Wendell went very still. Brannoc stopped chewing.

"Read it again," Wendell said. "Slowly."

"*Nine fastenings, nine forgettings,*" Hazel recited. "*What was shut with nine names can only open the same way. The door does not want a key. The door wants to be remembered.*"

The lamplight flickered. Somewhere overhead, a ledger-page turned on its own in a draft, soft as a sigh.

"Well," said Brannoc, after a silence. "That's ominous as a funeral bell, that is."

***

They worked through the night, and the ledger gave up its secret grudgingly, the way old people tell stories—with digressions, with warnings, with long looks at the fire.

The door out of Vessany was real. That much Wendell confirmed from the guild's own shipping records, cross-referenced against marginalia so old the ink had gone brown as dried blood. A gate, built in the last days of the country, keyed against the erasure itself—the great unwriting that the guild had filed and sealed and razored out of history. Nine locks, because nine was the number of completeness in the old reckoning, and each lock answered to one name: a true name, a stolen name, taken from the people of Vessany when the country was erased and stored somewhere in the guild's deep holdings like coins in a miser's mattress.

"You can't pick these locks," Wendell said, tapping the diagram that spread across two pages—a door drawn in careful architectural lines, its nine wards arranged like petals. "You can't force them, bribe them, or trick them. They're not mechanisms, exactly. They're more like... wounds. Each one closes over a name. To open one, you have to say the name back to it."

"So we say the names," Brannoc said. "Where are they written?"

Hazel already knew, from the way Wendell's face did something small and terrible, before he said it.

"They're not written anywhere anymore. That was the whole point of an erasure. The names weren't destroyed—" he paused, choosing words the way he chose everything, precisely and with visible cost, "—they were *unwritten*. Pulled out of every record, every memory, every mouth. The guild keeps them, but not as words. As absences. You can't read an absence."

"But I know some of them," Hazel said.

Both men looked at her.

She hadn't meant to say it. The words had come up on their own, pushed by something stubborn in her chest. She thought of her grandmother's hands, knotted like tree roots, pressing Hazel's own hands flat on the salt-flat stones the last morning, before the gray came over the village like weather. *Say them back to me. Again. Again. Until your mouth knows them without your head.*

"I know how they sound," she amended. "Grandmother drilled me. Nine names, over and over, the whole way across the flats. I can say every one." She took a breath. "But I don't—I don't remember what they *are*. Who they belonged to. What they mean. They're just sounds. Beautiful sounds, but empty ones, like..." She searched. "Like coins from a country that doesn't exist."

"They don't exist," Brannoc said gently. "That's rather the trouble."

"No." Wendell leaned forward, and his eyes had the light in them that Hazel was beginning to recognize—the light of a man doing arithmetic he hated. "No, that might be exactly right. Listen. If the names were unwritten everywhere else, but this child carries their *sounds*—the pronunciation, the shape of them in a living mouth—then she's not carrying empty coins. She's carrying the molds the coins were struck from." He looked at Hazel, and something in his expression made her sit up straighter. "The locks won't care if you remember what the names mean. Locks don't need meaning. They need *fitting*."

"So I can open them."

"You can pronounce them. Whether that opens them—" He spread his hands. "There's one way to find out, and it's nine locks away."

Brannoc snorted. "I've smuggled live wyverns with better odds."

***

The trouble—and Hazel learned this over the following days, in the counting room, with chalk and slate and Wendell's endless patient questions—was that knowing the sounds and being able to produce them on command were two different countries between which no road ran.

The first name came easy. It always did; it was the oldest one in her mouth, the one grandmother had taught first, and when Wendell asked her to say it she said it without thinking, and the lamp flames all leaned toward her at once, every wick in the room bending like grass in wind, and Brannoc fell off his stool.

"Do that again," he said from the floor.

"Don't," said Wendell, whose voice had gone thin. "Not yet. Not until we understand what it costs."

The second name stuck in her throat like a fishbone. She knew its shape—she could feel it sitting behind her teeth—but whenever she reached for it, it slid away, the way a dream slides when you try to carry it into the morning. She tried a hundred times. She tried whispering it, singing it, saying it backward, saying it with her eyes shut, saying it into Wendell's cupped ear like a secret. Nothing. The sound lived in her somewhere, she could feel it, a weight under her breastbone shaped exactly like a word, but it would not come up.

On the third day she threw the slate across the counting room.

It hit the wall and cracked clean in half, and the clatter rang through the whole Gate, and Hazel stood there shaking with her fists balled, hating herself, hating the ledger, hating the gray morning on the flats when her grandmother's voice had started fraying at the edges and she'd promised, *promised*, to keep the names safe.

Brannoc picked up the slate halves. He didn't say anything clever, which from Brannoc was practically a eulogy. He set them on the table, and then he sat down across from her, and he said, "My hometown had a song. Can't remember a note of it. Know it had one, though. Know my mother sang it. That's not nothing, little miss. Knowing there's a hole is how you start mapping it."

"It's not a hole," Hazel said, and her voice cracked in the middle. "It's nine holes. And everyone's waiting on me to fill them, and I can't even—"

"Hazel." Wendell crouched down to her level, which he did rarely and awkwardly, like a man folding himself into a space he wasn't built for. "May I tell you something about ledgers?"

She sniffed. Nodded.

"When an entry goes missing from a book—an honest mistake, a torn page, spilled ink—we don't guess at what it said. We look at what's *around* it. The entries before and after. The sums that don't balance quite right. The absence has edges, and the edges tell you the shape of what was lost." He tapped her forehead, very lightly, with one ink-stained finger. "You are not an empty book, Hazel. You are a book with nine entries razored out. But you grew up *inside* those entries. Every song, every story, every name-day custom your grandmother beat into your stubborn skull—that's the writing around the hole. We stop trying to pull the names out of the dark. We read toward them instead."

Hazel wiped her nose on her sleeve. "How?"

"Tell me about the names," Wendell said. "Don't say them. Describe them. Which one tastes like smoke? Which one would your grandmother say when she was angry? Which one did she never say at all?"

And that—*that*—was the door.

***

It took six days. Hazel kept count by scratching marks into the edge of the counting table until Wendell caught her and, instead of scolding her, gave her a proper tally-book with her initials embossed on the cover, H.V., and told her that any expedition worth its salt kept records. She wrote everything down: the names' shapes, their weights, their tempers. Because they had tempers. That was the thing nobody had warned her about—that names could have personalities, the way weather did.

The smoke-tasting one was third, and it belonged, she decided, to the smiths' quarter, because grandmother had always said it the way you'd strike iron. The one she'd never been allowed to say aloud was seventh, and it felt cold and formal, like a judge's chair, and when she finally coaxed it out of hiding by reading aloud a list of Vessany's old high offices from the founding ledger, it came up her throat so suddenly that the candles guttered and Brannoc swore in three languages.

One by one, they surfaced. Not remembered—*retrieved*, hauled up out of her like buckets from a deep well, each one dripping with things she almost-knew. By the sixth evening she stood at the table with her tally-book open, and spoke all nine in sequence, softly, carefully, the way you'd walk across ice, and the lamplight held steady, and the air tasted faintly of reeds and summer, and Wendell wrote the pronunciations down in a cipher of his own devising, and Brannoc—who had gone quiet somewhere around the fifth name and stayed quiet—finally cleared his throat.

"I know the fourth one," he said.

Hazel blinked at him. "You can't. It's Vessany's. You said your town was—"

"Three valleys over from where Vessany's border used to be. Close enough that half our wedding songs were stolen from theirs, or the other way round; nobody remembers which, because—" he laughed, short and bitter, "—because somebody tidied the maps." He tapped the fourth line in her book. "That's not just a name. That's the name of the ferry at Callowmere. My grandfather worked that ferry. I used to think *Callowmere* was just a place. It's not. It's what the name became when they pulled the true one out of it—a shell, a placeholder, a coat hung on a hook where a man used to be." His jaw worked. "The guild didn't erase a country, little miss. They erased a thousand of these, all wearing country costumes."

Nobody said anything for a while. The lamps burned. Above them, the counting floor ticked and settled.

Then Wendell closed the founding ledger with the gentleness of a man closing a coffin, and said, "We have nine names. We have one child who can speak them. What we don't have is passage to the door itself, or any notion of where the guild keeps the original thefts—and make no mistake, if the locks are wounds, someone will be waiting at them." He looked at Hazel, and there was something new in his face when he looked at her now, something that had replaced the polite wariness of that first muddy night. Respect, maybe. Or fear. With Wendell it was hard to tell the difference. "You've done the impossible part. Now comes the expensive part."

"What's the expensive part?" Hazel asked.

"Buying our way through the Underways," Brannoc said, standing and stretching until his spine crackled. "Which means the Market of Unclaimed Things, which means haggling with people who sell grief by the bottle, which means—" he scooped the last plum out of the sack and pointed it at her like a scepter, "—you stay close, say nothing, and touch nothing. Especially nothing."

Hazel hugged her tally-book to her chest and nodded solemnly.

But as they climbed the stairs toward the cold night air, she lingered a moment at the top step, looking back down at the counting room, at the cracked slate still lying by the wall, at the founding ledger asleep in its wrappings. Nine names. She had carried them across the salt flats without knowing what they were, the way you carry a stone in your shoe without knowing it's the key to a door.

*The door wants to be remembered.*

Somewhere far below the world, she thought, a drowned tollhouse was holding its breath, waiting for her voice.

And somewhere closer—much closer—down in the lamplit streets of the city, a black market was opening its stalls for the night, selling the belongings of everyone who had ever been unwritten.

Hazel pulled her coat tight and followed the men into the dark.
