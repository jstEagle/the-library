## 8. The Market of Unclaimed Things

The Market of Unclaimed Things did not appear on any map, which was fitting, because everything it sold had been taken off maps too.

Wendell had heard of it the way one hears of most forbidden places: in fragments, over decades, from couriers who spoke too freely after their third drink. *Down past the Tannery Stair. Through the door with no handle on the inside. Bring coin, bring silence, and don't ask where anything came from.* He had filed these fragments away for thirty years the way he filed everything—carefully, alphabetically, and without ever intending to use them.

Now Brannoc Vale was leading them down a stair that smelled of tallow and old salt, and Wendell's carefully alphabetized life was coming apart at the seams.

"Rules," Brannoc said over his shoulder. He had insisted on walking first, his broad frame filling the narrow passage, his lantern swinging in a rhythm that seemed almost ceremonial. "Rule one: don't haggle unless you mean to buy. Rule two: don't touch anything with your bare hands. Rule three—" he paused at the bottom of the stair, before a door that was, as promised, handleless, "—don't look for anyone you know."

"Why not?" Hazel asked.

Brannoc's jaw worked. "Because sometimes they're there."

He pressed his palm flat against the wood and said something low in a language Wendell didn't recognize—though that was becoming a familiar sensation lately, an ache like reaching for a stair that wasn't there. The door swung inward on darkness and the smell of dust, candlewax, and something sweeter underneath: dried flowers, perhaps, or the ghost of dried flowers.

Hazel slipped her hand into Wendell's.

He looked down at her in surprise. She was staring straight ahead, mud still faintly crusted at her hairline despite three days of relative safety, her chin set in the stubborn angle he had come to know as her default position. But her fingers were cold, and they held on tight.

"I don't like doors," she said quietly. "Doors are how they took everything."

Wendell did not trust himself to answer. He squeezed her hand instead, and together they followed Brannoc through.

* * *

The market opened beneath them like a held breath.

It was built in the bones of some vast cellar—or perhaps a cistern, or a collapsed cathedral; the architecture had given up declaring itself centuries ago. Vaulted ceilings lost themselves in shadow overhead, hung with hundreds of lanterns of mismatched glass, each burning a slightly different color, so that the light below came down patchwork and uncertain, like memory itself. Stalls lined the walls and crowded the center aisle, and every stall was piled with things.

Not treasure. That was the first thing Wendell understood, standing there with Hazel's hand in his. Not jewels, not weapons, not the glittering contraband of ordinary black markets. These were *belongings*. A child's wooden horse with one ear chewed smooth. A wedding dress folded with terrible care. A wall clock, stopped at some hour that had mattered to someone once. A shelf of shoes, all left-footed, arranged by size.

"Where are the right shoes?" Hazel whispered.

"There aren't any," said a voice beside them.

The speaker was a small woman wrapped in gray shawls, her face so lined it looked plowed rather than wrinkled. She wore a merchant's badge of tarnished copper on a cord around her neck. "Right shoes get claimed," she said. "People keep the rights. It's the lefts that end up here—the ones nobody thinks about until they're gone." She smiled without warmth. "You're new. I can always tell. New ones walk through looking at the *things*. Old ones walk through looking at the *gaps*."

"We need passage through the Underways," Brannoc said flatly. "South passage, past the Borderlands. I'm told the Ferryman's toll is negotiable in goods."

"Everything here is negotiable in goods." The woman's eyes moved over the three of them with the unhurried appraisal of someone pricing livestock—or souls. "Coin's no good down here. Coin belongs to kingdoms, and we don't trade in what kingdoms own." Her gaze snagged on Hazel and stayed there, and something flickered across her face—not recognition exactly, but its cousin. "Well now," she said softly. "Aren't you a long way from anywhere."

"You know me?" Hazel asked.

"No," the woman said. "But you've got the look. Unwritten folk have a look. Like a page that's been erased but not cleaned properly—you can see the ghost of the letters if the light's right." She turned and beckoned. "Come. If it's the Underways you want, you want Maren's stall. And on the way—" she spread her hands at the market entire, "—look all you like. Looking's free. It's the only thing that is."

* * *

They walked the aisle slowly, because there was no other way to walk it.

Wendell had spent thirty years among impossible things. He had inventoried bottled grief by the crate, logged borrowed years with their interest schedules, recorded the transfer of names not yet earned with the same detached precision he brought to grain futures. Nothing in the Hollow Gate's ledgers had ever moved him. Numbers were numbers. Impossibility, properly catalogued, became merely inventory.

This was not inventory.

Here, impossibility had been *lived in*. Here was a music box that played a lullaby in a language whose name had been scraped off the lid. Here was a set of house keys on a ring worn bright with handling, labeled in a script that made Hazel stop dead and breathe hard through her nose. Here was an entire stall of portraits—faces painted in oils, in charcoal, in faded daguerreotype—and every single one had been defaced, not maliciously but *administratively*, a neat rectangle of blankness where each face should be, as though some clerk somewhere had decided that faces were clerical errors.

Hazel stood before the portraits for a long time.

"That's how they do it," she said finally, her voice very small. "Grandmother told me. They don't burn the pictures. Burning leaves ash, and ash can be read. They just... take the middle out. The part that says *this is someone*." She touched the empty rectangle gently, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal. "The rest stays. The shoulders. The trees behind them. Just not the person."

"Hazel." Wendell put a hand on her shoulder.

"It's fine." She wiped her nose with the back of her wrist, briskly, the way children do when they have decided crying is inefficient. "I knew what this place would be. Grandmother described it. She said, *if you ever find the market under the world, girl, don't buy anything back. Everything there wants to be bought back, and wanting is how they catch you.*"

The shawled woman, walking ahead, snorted. "Your grandmother sounds like she sold here once."

"She did," Hazel said. "Before the flats."

The woman stopped walking. Turned. Looked at Hazel again, longer this time, and whatever she saw made her expression shift from commerce to something older and wearier. "Vessany stock," she murmured. "By the deep roads. Thirty years I've worked this market and I've never once—" She caught herself, glanced at Wendell and Brannoc, and visibly reassembled her merchant's mask. "Maren's stall is two rows down. Mind the puddles. Some of them aren't water."

"What are they?" Brannoc asked.

"Tears that got separated from their owners," the woman said, and walked on, leaving Wendell unable to determine whether she was joking.

* * *

Maren's stall was less a stall than a fortress of trunks, rope, and iron fittings, presided over by a man built like a doorframe who introduced himself only as Maren and shook hands with Brannoc like they were old enemies pretending otherwise.

"Vale." Maren's voice was gravel poured slow. "I thought you were dead."

"I thought you were dead."

"One of us was misinformed." Maren spat expertly into a puddle that, Wendell noted, did not ripple. "What do you want?"

"Underways passage. South fork. Four travelers' toll, paid in goods, discreet as the grave."

"Three travelers," Wendell corrected, then caught himself. "That is—I—"

"He counts us," Hazel explained solemnly to Maren. "He counts everything. He's a keeper. He can't help it."

Maren's eyes went to Wendell with sudden professional interest. "A keeper? Guild-bred?"

"Formerly adjacent," Wendell said, with the smoothness of long practice. "I keep accounts. I don't make policy."

"Nobody keeps accounts without making policy," Maren said. "Accounts are just policy wearing spectacles." But he shrugged and began hauling a trunk forward. "Passage I can arrange. The Ferryman takes payment in unclaimed goods—something with weight to it. Something that remembers being owned. You'll find such a thing here or you won't, but I'll tell you now, the Underways don't take money, don't take blood, and don't take promises. They take *proof*. Proof that you came from somewhere. Because the Underways are where geography goes to forget itself, and the only way through a place that forgets is to carry something that remembers."

He waved a hand at the market entire. "So. Shop."

* * *

Wendell meant to shop efficiently. He meant to move through the aisles with ledger-keeper's discipline, identify a suitable item of sufficient provenance-weight, negotiate, pay, and leave. He had done harder errands. He had once balanced the guild's grief reserves during a plague year without spilling a drop.

Instead, forty minutes later, he found himself standing frozen before a coat.

It hung on a peg at the edge of a stall near the market's far wall, apart from the main crush, as though even the other objects gave it room. It was a man's traveling coat, dark wool gone soft with age, cut in a style thirty years out of fashion. Moths had found the cuffs. The lining was patched at both elbows with careful, uneven stitches—the work of someone mending love more than skill.

And down the front, where buttons should have been, were six buttons of pale green glass, each one swirled through with a thread of white like smoke caught mid-sentence.

Wendell knew those buttons.

He knew them the way you know your own heartbeat, which is to say: not as information but as fact, as ground, as the thing underneath everything else that you never think about because thinking would require imagining its absence. His mother had kept a jar of them on the mantel of a house in a town whose name he had not spoken since he was nine years old. She'd bought them off a peddler, she said, glass blown by a craftsman who put a little of the sky in every one. When his father's coat lost a button, she sewed on one of hers. When Wendell tore his sleeve climbing the mill fence, another. There had been eleven in the jar. He used to count them on winter evenings while she read aloud, and she'd tease him for it—*counting again, my little clerk*—and he'd say someone had to keep track, and she'd laugh and say, *then let it be you.*

He had been nine when the men came with documents. He remembered the documents better than the men: heavy paper, wax seals, language dense as river stone. He remembered being told the family was being *relocated for administrative reasons*, and he remembered his mother sewing the last four buttons off her own good coat onto his child's jacket in the dark of the night before, saying nothing, her needle flashing, her breath careful and controlled the way breath gets when someone is holding a scream in a closed room.

He remembered arriving at the Hollow Gate orphan intake with a jacket full of green glass buttons.

He remembered the intake clerk removing them. *"No personal effects,"* the ledger entry had read, in a hand Wendell would later learn to imitate perfectly. *"Subject processed clean."*

He had never seen the buttons again. He had assumed, in the way of children and then adults, that they had been discarded—that they had gone wherever unclaimed things go, which is nowhere, which is the trash, which is oblivion.

They had gone here. They had been here all along, six of them, sewn onto a stranger's coat—or not a stranger's, maybe, maybe the coat belonged to someone else from home, someone else processed clean, someone else who had walked out of the same erasure wearing his mother's sky—and they had waited thirty years in the dark under the world for him to walk past.

"Wendell." Hazel's voice, very close. "Wendell, you're shaking."

He could not speak. His mouth would not assemble words. Thirty years of ledgers, thirty years of perfect invisible order, and all of it was coming apart in front of a moth-eaten coat, because the coat was proof, and the proof was simple, and the simple thing was this:

*He had not been born invisible. He had been made invisible. By the guild. By the same machine, running the same process, that had unwritten Hazel's country and her grandmother and her village on the salt flats. He was not the keeper of the gate. He was one of the things the gate had swallowed.*

"They took mine too," he managed at last. His voice sounded like someone else's. "My buttons. When they brought me in. I was nine. I thought—" He stopped. Started again. "I thought I was staff. All these years, I thought I was staff. I thought I'd been *hired*."

Hazel took his hand. Her grip was small and fierce.

"You weren't hired," she said. "You were kept. There's a difference. Kept things belong to somebody." She looked up at him with eyes far too old for her face. "But kept things can be *let go*. That's what Grandmother said. Anything kept can be let go."

Behind them, Brannoc had gone very quiet, and when Wendell turned, the smuggler was staring at the coat with an expression Wendell had never seen on him—not the easy grin, not the debt-dodging charm, but something raw and young, the face of a boy from a town that no longer existed.

"Those buttons," Brannoc said hoarsely. "Green glass. Sky-swirl. My gran had a jar of them. Whole jar. She said they came from—" He stopped. Swallowed. "She said they came from over the border. From Vessany. Glassblowers there put weather in their work, she said. Wind in the blue ones. Smoke in the white."

The market's patchwork light fell over the three of them, and Wendell felt the vertigo open beneath his feet like a trapdoor: his mother's buttons, Brannoc's grandmother's jar, Hazel's erased nation—all threads of the same weave, all leading back through the same door, to a country the guild had murdered and then hired its orphans to sweep up after.

His whole life, laid out in a line he had never been allowed to see.

"The coat," he said to the stall's keeper, and his voice was steady now, steady the way ice is steady over deep water. "How much?"

The keeper—a gaunt man with ink-stained fingers, ink-stained the way Wendell's were, and that detail landed like a knife between the ribs—shrugged. "For the coat? Nothing. Coats are cheap. It's the buttons that cost." He leaned forward. "Buttons like that, with provenance like that? Those'll weigh plenty on the Ferryman's scale. But understand what you're buying, keeper. Once you claim a thing here, it's yours—truly yours, in the way the guild never lets anything be truly yours. And once it's yours, you carry it. Through the Underways and past them. Whatever it remembers, you remember. That's the toll. That's always been the toll."

Wendell reached out and, breaking rule two, touched the nearest button with his bare hand.

It was cool. It was glass. It was a piece of sky his mother had kept in a jar on a mantel in a town that had a name, and the name was trying to surface in him like a drowned man, and he let it, he opened every ledger-door inside himself and let it rise—

—and got as far as the shape of it, the sound of its first syllable, the way it sat in the mouth—

—when the lanterns above the market guttered, all of them at once, every color dying to a uniform sickly yellow, and the crowd noise of the aisles dropped into a silence with edges on it.

At the far entrance—the doorless doorway they had come through—a figure in gray stood silhouetted against the stair-light. Tall. Still. Wearing the featureless brass badge of the Guild of Impossible Things.

An Auditor. Down here. Where Auditors had no jurisdiction and had never needed any, because no one erased ever came this deep.

Except, Wendell realized, his hand still resting on his mother's button, that the guild had known about this market all along. Had probably built it. A place where the evidence of erasures gathered—and could be watched.

The Auditor stepped forward, and the light steadied, and somewhere in the crowd a woman began, very quietly, to sing—in a language that predates kingdoms, in a language Hazel knew, in a language that was rising everywhere now like water finding its level.

Hazel's fingers tightened around Wendell's.

"They're here for us," she said. "Aren't they."

Wendell lifted the coat from its peg, shrugged it on over his shoulders—it fit, of course it fit, it had always been going to fit—and turned to face the gray figure advancing up the aisle.

"No," he said. "They're here for *me*."
