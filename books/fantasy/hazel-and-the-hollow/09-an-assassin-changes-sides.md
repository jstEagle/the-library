## 9. An Assassin Changes Sides

Hazel smelled the knife before she saw it.

That was the strange thing she would remember afterward—not the blade itself, not the woman holding it, but the smell: cold iron and something underneath it like winter pears, sweet and wrong, the scent of a poison that had been applied so many times to so many edges that it had soaked into the steel's memory. She was sitting cross-legged on the floor of Brannoc Vale's safehouse—a room above a shuttered dye-works that smelled of mordant and old salt—copying letters from the founding ledger onto a scrap of paper because Wendell said her hand remembered what his could only guess at.

The letters came out of her fingers without her asking them to. That was how Grandmother had described it once, in the last weeks, when her voice had gone thin as onion skin: *The tongue isn't something you know, little wren. It's something that knows you.* Hazel wrote the ninth name of Vessany over and over, trying to make it stick in her head instead of just her hand, and the candle guttered, and the smell of cold iron and winter pears slid under the door.

She didn't scream. Screaming, Grandmother had taught her, was for people who had someone to come.

Instead she did what she had done on the salt flats when the sky went white and the village went quiet and the road home simply stopped existing: she kept writing. Her pen didn't even stutter. The letter *vess* curled its tail; the letter *ann* stood straight as a fence post. If she was going to die, she was going to die mid-word, and whatever god kept count of such things would have to mark it down as an unfinished sentence, and unfinished sentences were the one kind of debt even the guild couldn't collect.

The door opened without a sound. Doors that opened with sound were doors that wanted you to know.

The woman who came through it was ordinary in the way a well-made lock is ordinary—everything about her designed to be forgotten the moment you looked away. Grey traveling cloak, grey hood, hands bare and unremarkable except that they were very still, the way water is still right before it goes over a fall. Her eyes found Hazel first, then Wendell, asleep in the corner chair with the founding ledger open on his chest and his spectacles sliding down his nose, then Brannoc, snoring on the cot with one arm flung over his face like a man surrendering to a dream he'd already lost.

"Three of you," the woman said. Her voice was quiet and level, a voice built for saying last things. "The guild sent me for the child."

Hazel put down her pen. Very carefully. The way you set down something you might need again.

"I'm not going," she said.

"Nobody ever is." The woman crossed the room without disturbing the dust. Hazel watched her feet—soft boots, no heel, the kind of footwear made for people whose profession required them to arrive unannounced—and noticed that she stepped around the scattered papers rather than through them. Around the papers. Not over them. A person who respected paper, even now, even here. "You're the one who writes it," the woman said, and there was something new in her voice, something that hadn't been there when she'd announced herself to the sleeping room. "The old tongue. You're the one who writes it properly."

"Who wants to know?"

"The guild wants to know. I don't." The woman crouched, slow and deliberate, the way you'd approach a bird or a trap, and looked at the scrap of paper in front of Hazel. At the name written nine times in a child's careful, stubborn hand. "May I?"

Hazel should have said no. Every story Grandmother had ever told about the guild said no, every warning stitched into the lining of her coat said no. But the woman's eyes were doing something eyes weren't supposed to do—they were wet at the edges, bright in a way that had nothing to do with killing, and she was looking at those nine repetitions of a stolen name the way Hazel looked at the door of a house she couldn't go back to.

Hazel slid the paper across the floorboards.

The woman picked it up in both hands, the way you'd hold a wounded animal or a holy book, and read it.

Hazel watched the color leave her face. It went in order, like a tide going out—the cheeks first, then the lips, then the space around the eyes, until the woman looked carved from tallow, from candle-stuff, from something meant to be burned. Her breath made a small sound, a sound Hazel had heard once before, from a man on the salt flats who had just realized the road home wasn't there anymore.

"This is my name," the woman said.

Wendell woke the way he always woke—all at once, silently, thirty years of habit pulling him out of sleep like a fish on a line. He didn't move. He lay in the chair with his lashes lowered and took inventory: candle low, Brannoc breathing, Hazel upright and unharmed, and a stranger kneeling three feet from her holding a piece of paper like it was the last page of the world.

He knew who she was. Of course he knew. There wasn't a keeper of any gate in the guild's service who didn't know Serevan by reputation alone—the knife the Auditors lent out when a problem needed to stop being a problem. He had filed her travel requisitions twice. He had balanced her expense accounts. Somewhere in the Hollow Gate's ledgers there was a column with her purchases in it: whetstones, boot wax, pear-blossom essence for the blades, and never once, in eleven years of entries, a single item that suggested she existed when she wasn't working.

"Serevan," Wendell said, and sat up, and pushed his spectacles into place, and did the bravest arithmetic of his life: if she had wanted them dead, the candle would already be out. "You're a long way from your last invoice."

"You're Brightwater." She didn't look at him. She was still looking at the paper. "The keeper. They told me the child had found a keeper who'd gone soft." Her thumb moved over the ink, feather-light, following the curve of the letter *vess*. "They told me she was dangerous. Unwritten. A contamination risk." A sound came out of her that might have been a laugh if laughter hadn't been cut out of her somewhere along the way, cleanly, like a bad tooth. "They sent me to retrieve a contamination. And she's sitting on the floor writing my name."

"That's not your name," Wendell said carefully. "Your name is—" He stopped. Because he had checked, of course he had checked, he was a keeper, checking was the whole of his religion. Serevan's file was thin even by assassin standards. No birthplace. No family record. An intake date and a training date and a first commission, and between the intake date and everything after it, a gap in the ledger exactly the width of a razored page.

"My name," Serevan said, "is the ninth name on your list of stolen ones. Or the fourth. I can't tell which repetition is which, because she writes it the same every time, and it's the same every time because it's *true*." She finally raised her head, and Hazel saw that the wetness in her eyes had spilled over and she either didn't know it or didn't care. "I have spent eleven years being a thing the guild points. I don't remember my mother. I don't remember whether I had a mother. I don't remember anything before an intake date, and I told myself that was normal, that everyone arrived somewhere, that memory was just the first thing you traded." Her voice stayed level. That was the terrible part, Hazel thought. The voice never moved at all. Only the face fell apart behind it. "And this child writes my name in a language that predates kingdoms, and it's in a ledger of names the guild stole when it erased a country, which means—"

"Which means they didn't recruit you," Wendell said quietly. "They made you. Out of the part of Vessany they cut away."

The silence that followed had weight. It pressed down on the candle flame and made it stand very straight.

Brannoc chose that moment to wake up, because Brannoc's instincts for danger had always operated on a delay, arriving precisely after the danger had become something more interesting than dangerous. He sat up on the cot, took in the scene—the knife-woman, the pale child, the trembling paper—and said, with the weary air of a man reading a bill he'd been expecting:

"Oh, we're doing this, are we. Right. Someone put the kettle on."

They sat in a circle on the floor because the safehouse had one table and Brannoc was using it to store cheese. Hazel kept the founding ledger open between them, weighted at the corners with a spool of thread, a horseshoe, and half a wheel of cheddar, and Serevan sat as far from all of them as the room allowed, with her back to the wall and her knives—there were seven, Hazel counted, seven, like a horrible kind of jewelry—laid flat on the boards beside her where everyone could see them.

"Why show us?" Wendell asked. "You could have taken her while we slept. You could be halfway to the Hall of Auditors by now with a bonus in your pocket."

"I read the paper first." Serevan said it simply, as though it explained everything, and maybe it did. "Eleven years, the rule has been: assess before you act. Assess the exits, assess the weapons, assess the target. Nobody ever told me to assess the *handwriting*." She looked at Hazel, and Hazel looked back, and something passed between them that Hazel didn't have a word for yet but suspected she would keep for the rest of her life. "You write it the way it's supposed to be written. The tails curl left. Do you know that nobody alive curls them left? I've seen fragments. Archive scraps. Forgers' attempts. They all get it wrong, and I always thought—that's just decay. That's what happens to a language when the country dies." Her jaw tightened. "It doesn't decay. It gets murdered. And the murderer keeps the body in a vault and sells tickets."

"The nine names," Wendell said. "You've seen them before. In the Archive."

"I've seen *a* list. Nine entries, sealed, red-flagged, above my clearance—which is to say above nearly everyone's clearance. I was sent to look at it once, years ago, to confirm the seals were intact. I stood in front of it for four minutes and felt—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "I felt homesick. Standing in a vault under the guildhall, looking at a sealed box, I felt homesick so hard my teeth hurt. I reported the seals intact and asked to be assigned elsewhere. Farther away. I thought the vault was leaking something. Some residue." Her laugh came again, that broken-toothed sound. "It wasn't leaking. It was calling. My own name was in the box and it was calling me and I walked away and asked for more work."

Hazel hugged her knees. "What happens if you say it? Your name. At the lock."

Serevan looked at her for a long time. Outside, the dye-works creaked in the wind, and somewhere below a cat knocked over something breakable and fled the scene.

"Wendell," Serevan said. "Explain it to her. You're the one who reads ledgers."

"It costs," Wendell said. His voice had gone gentle, the voice he used for the sad columns, the accounts of grief sold by the bottle. "Every name spoken gives back a piece of Vessany—but it takes a piece of the speaker. A memory of home. That's the toll design. The guild built it so that opening the door would hurt more than leaving it shut. So that anyone who tried would pay for their country with their country." He hesitated. "For you, I think—it would be worse than a memory. If your whole self was built out of the erasure, then speaking your true name might unmake everything they trained into you. Everything they *made* you."

"Good," Serevan said.

Everyone looked at her.

"They made me a knife," she said. "They took a child out of a country that no longer exists and ground her down into a thing with an intake date. And I have killed—I don't even know the number, because I stopped keeping it, because keeping it was the last part of me that acted like a person." She picked up the nearest knife, turned it over once, and set it down again with its edge facing away from all of them—an act so small and so deliberate that Hazel felt her throat close. "If speaking my name burns the knife out of me, then let it burn. I'd rather be nobody from nowhere than exactly what they built."

Brannoc, who had been uncharacteristically silent, chewing on a rind of cheese and watching her with narrowed eyes, finally spoke. "You understand we can't trust you. No offense meant. Enormous amounts of offense meant, actually, but professionally delivered."

"None taken. You shouldn't trust me. Trusting me would be stupid." Serevan gathered her knives, one by one, and slid them into the sheaths hidden in her cloak. "But consider the alternative. The guild will send another after me. Then another. Each one better briefed than the last, because I'll be listed as compromised, and compromised assets generate thorough follow-ups. You need someone who knows how they hunt." She stood, and pulled her hood back, and for the first time Hazel saw her whole face—ordinary, tired, young in a way that startled Hazel, young in a way that said *they started grinding early*. "Besides. You're missing six names and a route through the Underways, and I've escorted cargo through the Underways for eleven years. I know which tunnels the guild watches and which ones it forgot." A pause. "Which ones it erased."

"And why?" Wendell asked, and it wasn't hostile, it was the question of a keeper verifying an entry. "Why change sides? People don't. Not people like you."

Serevan looked down at the scrap of paper still in her hand—nine repetitions of a name, in a child's left-curled hand—and closed her fingers around it.

"Because my last obedience was to come here and take her," she said. "And I'm still holding the knife from it. It hasn't been cleaned. It hasn't been put away. As far as the guild knows, I'm on task." Something moved through her face then, fast, like weather—grief and fury and a kind of terrified freedom, all in the space of a breath. "So let's find out what I am when I'm not obedient. Let's find out what's left of the girl from the country that got erased." She tucked the paper into her cloak, over her heart, which Hazel was fairly sure was not standard procedure for carrying intelligence. "The guild expects my report within two days. When it doesn't come, they'll send the Auditors down the Underways after us. We should be underground before they finish wondering."

Brannoc groaned and stood, joints cracking like green firewood. "Underways. Wonderful. I died in the Underways once, you know. Briefly. Long story involving a toll troll and a barrel of pickled eels." He pointed a cheese-rind at Serevan, gravely. "You carry your own torch."

"Noted."

Hazel sat very still amid the sudden motion of packing, the sweep of the candle shadow, the grown-up machinery of departure grinding into gear around her. And she thought about Grandmother, dying on the salt flats with the white sky coming down, pressing the last words into her hands: *Find the keeper of the gate, little wren. The door remembers our name even if the world doesn't.*

Grandmother hadn't mentioned the assassin. But then, Grandmother had always said the road would provide what the road required, and Hazel was beginning to suspect the road had a sense of humor.

She picked up her pen. On a fresh scrap of paper, in letters that curled left, she wrote the eight remaining names—one for each lock still shut between them and home—and folded the paper into Brannoc's map case, next to the route south.

Then she looked up at Serevan, who was watching her with an expression that on anyone else would have been called hope.

"When we get to the second lock," Hazel said, "it's yours. I'll hold your hand if you want. Grandmother held mine, when we crossed the flats. It helps. A little."

Serevan didn't answer. But her hand, resting on the hilt of the knife she'd sworn off using, trembled—just once, just slightly, like a door deciding whether to open.

And far to the north, in a hall of perfect records, a report failed to arrive, and a bell that hadn't rung in eleven years began, softly, to toll.
