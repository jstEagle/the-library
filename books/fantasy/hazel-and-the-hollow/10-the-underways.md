## 10. The Underways

The entrance to the Underways was a well.

That was the first thing Wendell disliked about it. Thirty years of keeping the Hollow Gate had taught him that doors announced themselves—lintels, hinges, thresholds, the honest grammar of passage. A well was a door pretending to be an absence, and he had spent his whole life among things that pretended to be nothing at all. He did not trust it.

"It's called the Throat," Brannoc said, spitting over the lip with the casual irreverence of a man who had spat into worse places. "Because everything that goes down it gets swallowed."

"Charming."

"I didn't name it. The smugglers before me did, and they were mostly dead, so their judgment stands." Brannoc shouldered his pack—a monstrous thing of waxed canvas and iron rings that clinked like a miser's conscience—and swung himself onto the rope ladder without waiting for agreement. That was Brannoc's way. He moved through the world as though the world had already agreed to him and was only catching up on paperwork.

Hazel went next, quick as a squirrel down a drainpipe, her bare feet finding rungs Wendell could barely see in the grey morning light. She paused halfway, hanging over the dark, and looked up at him with an expression he was beginning to know too well: the look of a child who has already decided something is true and is merely waiting for the adult to catch up.

"The stones are warm," she said. "Down there. I can feel them from here."

Wendell put his palm flat against the well's rim. Old granite, lichened, cold as any stone in the borderlands ought to be. But beneath the cold, faint as a heartbeat under a blanket, something else. Not warmth exactly. *Attention.*

"Geography forgets itself down here," Brannoc called up from the gloom. "That's what makes it useful. And what makes it hungry. Keep one hand on the wall at all times, keep your name in your mouth, and whatever asks you questions—lie."

"Lie?" Wendell lowered himself onto the ladder. His knees objected. They had been objecting for two days now, ever since they'd left the market road, and he suspected they would continue objecting until one or both of them were done entirely.

"The Underways don't remember where anything is," Brannoc said. "So they ask. Corridors lean toward you and want to know if you're going north. Stairwells ask how many steps you've counted. You lie, and they believe you, because they have no way of knowing better. It's the only place in the world where lying is a survival skill instead of a sin." A pause, and then, with the ghost of his old courier's grin: "You'll be fine, ledger-keeper. Lying's practically your native tongue."

Wendell chose not to dignify that. He descended into the dark, hand on the wall, name in his mouth, and felt the Underways close over him like water.

* * *

The dark was not empty.

That was the second thing—the thing no amount of Brannoc's briefing had prepared him for. Wendell had expected tunnels: damp stone, dripping ceilings, the honest squalor of the underground. Instead the passages around them breathed. Walls curved where walls had no reason to curve, swelling like throats mid-swallow. Doorways opened off the main corridor into rooms that were clearly, impossibly, elsewhere—one gave onto a sunlit orchard seen from above, as though the door hung in the sky over someone's harvest; another showed a frozen lake at dusk, its ice lit from below by something patient and green. Neither room acknowledged them. Neither room existed. The Underways wore other places the way a beggar wears a stolen coat—badly, and with no idea whose arms had filled the sleeves.

"There," Hazel whispered, an hour or a year into the descent. She had stopped at a break in the left-hand wall, where rubble had fallen away from something older than the rubble.

Wendell saw it and felt the floor tilt under his certainty.

An archway. Not the crude mouths the Underways made for themselves but a *built* arch, carved from pale limestone, its keystone worked into the shape of a sheaf of wheat bound with a ribbon. He knew that wheat. He had spent three nights bent over Hazel's drawings while she traced Vessany's borders from memory, and in the margins of those maps, in her careful child's hand, she had drawn the same sheaf again and again—the mark pressed into every tollhouse, every bridge-stone, every boundary marker of a country that no longer was.

But this arch was wrong. The proportions were wrong. The wheat was right, the ribbon was right, the limestone was right—but the arch itself stood four feet too low, its springing points pinched inward, as if someone had taken Vessany's architecture and tried to fit it through a keyhole. A disguise worn by something that had never bothered to learn the body underneath.

"It's wearing us," Hazel said quietly. She reached out and touched the keystone, and her voice went strange and small. "It's wearing my country like a coat it found."

Brannoc had gone still in a way Wendell had learned to read as fear. The big man stood with his lantern raised, and the light caught his face, and for once there was no grin anywhere on it.

"Tollhouse," he said. "Or what the Underways thinks a tollhouse looks like. I came through here eleven years ago, moving cargo for a man in Marrowgate, and it was the same then. Ruins down here dressed in shapes they don't own." He hesitated. "I always figured it was mimicry. Caves copying what travelers expect to see, so the travelers stay calm." He looked at Hazel. "Now I'm thinking maybe it's not copying. Maybe it's *remembering*. Badly. The way you remember a face when you've been told to forget it."

Wendell ran his fingers along the arch's inner edge and found, half-erased by time and by something less natural than time, a line of script. Not the guild's trade-tongue. Not any kingdom's alphabet. The letters moved under his fingertips like fish under ice—he could feel their shapes trying to resolve and failing, each one sliding into illegibility just as his eye arrived at it.

Unwritten writing. Text that had been erased so thoroughly that even the stone had forgotten how to hold it.

He did not tell Hazel. She had already guessed; he could see it in the set of her shoulders, the way she touched the keystone the way you touch a sleeping animal you're afraid to wake. But some griefs should be allowed to arrive in their own time, and Wendell had spent thirty years learning the mercy of silence.

They walked on. Behind them, he was almost certain, the tollhouse arch straightened itself slowly, remembering its proper proportions now that no one was watching—as if ashamed of its disguise, or relieved to set it down.

* * *

They camped in a chamber the size of a cathedral, where the ceiling was lost in darkness and the floor was carpeted in a moss that glowed faintly blue-green, casting their shadows upward so that they seemed to walk on the underside of the sky. Hazel declared it the finest inn she had ever stayed in and fell asleep mid-sentence, curled against Brannoc's pack like a cat against a warm stove. She had been doing that more often. Wendell told himself it was exhaustion. He knew it was trust, and the knowledge sat in his chest like a coal.

Brannoc took first watch, which meant Brannoc took all the watches, because he never woke anyone else. Wendell lay awake anyway, listening to the Underways breathe.

Around midnight—or what he decided was midnight, since the Underways kept no hours and offered no opinion on the matter—Brannoc spoke without turning his head.

"They're following us."

Wendell sat up. "Auditors?"

"Three of them. Maybe four. Entered the Throat about six hours behind us." Brannoc turned a small tin cup in his hands, watching the moss-light swim in it. "I heard them at the second junction. Auditors walk a particular way. Even step, no echo. Like they're apologizing to the ground for touching it." He glanced back at Wendell, and his eyes were flat and serious in a way that made him look ten years older and twenty years harder. "The guild knows exactly where we are, Wendell. Has known since the market, I'd wager. They're not chasing. They're *escorting*. Herding us somewhere they've chosen."

"Then we change our path."

"You can't change your path down here. That's rather the point of the Underways. There's one road and it goes where it goes, and geography forgets itself, but *we* don't get to forget our way—we'd just wander until the corridors got bored of asking us questions and started eating the answers." Brannoc drank, grimaced at whatever the cup contained, and drank again. "No. We keep walking. We walk faster. And when they close the distance—" He shrugged, a big slow shrug that carried more weight than it appeared to. "We deal with it."

"Deal with it how?"

Brannoc smiled, and it was the smile of a man standing in front of a debt collector with empty pockets and a full heart. "I've been thinking about bridges."

* * *

The bridge came the next day, and it was dying already.

It spanned a chasm that the Underways had no business containing—a gulf so wide that the lantern light failed before it found the far side, filled with a wind that blew upward, carrying smells that belonged to the surface: cut hay, woodsmoke, rain on hot stone. Somewhere far below, water roared, or something that had agreed to be water for the chasm's benefit.

The bridge was old even by Underways standards, and it had been built by someone who understood weight and then abandoned by everyone who understood maintenance. Its planks were grey as bone. Its ropes had gone the color of old teeth. As they stepped onto it, the whole structure shivered and sighed, and Wendell felt, through his boots, the particular tremor of a thing deciding whether to go on existing.

"Quickly, lightly, and don't look down," Brannoc said—which was, Wendell reflected, the traditional advice of men who intended to do none of those things themselves.

They were a third of the way across when the corridor behind them lit with white light.

Lantern-glass. Auditor-lanterns, the kind that burned with a flame fed on recorded oaths, throwing light that fell on people the way a stamp falls on paper—flat, official, final. Wendell turned and saw them: three figures in grey, walking in perfect unison across the ruined tollhouse arch, their faces smooth and courteous and utterly empty, the way faces become when they have been filed under a category instead of a name.

"Hazel." He kept his voice level, because children take their weather from adults. "Walk. Don't run. Runners fall."

She walked. Brannoc walked, unhurried, massive, taking the bridge's shudders into his stride like a man crossing a ship's deck in weather he'd personally offended. Wendell walked between them, heart hammering, mind running through ledgers—*what clauses govern pursuit, what jurisdiction applies underground, what can I file, what can I stall*—and hating himself a little for reaching for paperwork while a child's life crossed a dying bridge.

The Auditors reached the near end of the bridge and stopped.

For a moment Wendell let himself hope. Perhaps the guild's contract-law did not extend across chasms. Perhaps even Auditors feared heights.

Then the lead Auditor raised one hand, palm outward, and spoke a single word in the guild's legal cant—the word for *foreclosure*—and the bridge began to die on purpose.

Wendell felt it happen through his feet: not collapse but *reclamation*, plank by plank, the Underways calling in a loan it had extended centuries ago. The planks behind the Auditors didn't fall—they *unmade*, dissolving into dust that drifted upward on the impossible wind, and the dissolution began to travel along the bridge toward them like fire along a fuse.

"Off the bridge!" Brannoc roared. "Both of you! *Now!*"

"We won't make it—" Wendell began.

"I wasn't talking to me." Brannoc dropped his pack. He dropped it the way other men drop everything they own, which, Wendell realized with a lurch, was exactly what it was. Then he turned to face the coming ruin, planted his feet on the last solid span, and put his shoulder under the bridge's main cable as though it were a drunk friend and he the only sober man in the city.

"What are you *doing*?"

"Holding it." Brannoc's voice was strained already, and the cable hadn't fully come taut. "Somebody owes this bridge a debt and I'm paying it. Go, Wendell. Take her and *go*."

"I will not leave you to—"

"*GO!*"

And Wendell went, because there are moments when obedience is the only love you have left to give. He caught Hazel's hand and ran, the bridge screaming behind them, planks vanishing a pace behind his heels, the upward wind tearing at his coat, Hazel's grip crushing his fingers white. Ahead, the far side rushed closer—dark stone, solid stone, a ledge with an honest lintel of raw rock—and behind them Brannoc held the center span with his whole furious body, roaring something that might have been a song, might have been a curse, might have been the names of everyone he owed and everyone who owed him, shouted into the chasm like a man settling accounts.

They came off the bridge onto solid rock and spun.

The last thirty feet of span were gone. Between them and Brannoc yawned forty feet of empty air, and Brannoc stood on the surviving island of bridge with the cable groaning across his shoulders, holding together a structure that had already ended everywhere except under his feet.

"Brannoc!" Hazel screamed it, and the chasm took her voice and gave it back broken.

The big man looked up. Even at that distance, even in that light, Wendell saw him grin.

"Ledger-keeper!" he bellowed. "Mark my account current!"

Then the Underways finished its arithmetic. The last of the bridge dissolved upward into dust and wind, and Brannoc Vale went down into the roaring dark with his arms spread wide, like a man embracing the whole falling world—

—and the chasm swallowed him, and the wind stopped, and the silence afterward was the loudest thing Wendell had ever heard.

Hazel was crying, great silent gulping sobs, pounding her small fists against the stone. Wendell knelt and held her, and stared across the gap at the place where the bridge had been, and felt something in his chest tear loose and refuse, absolutely, to be filed under *losses, acceptable*.

Because the chasm had not finished. Far below, past the reach of lantern light, the roaring had changed—and Wendell, who had spent thirty years listening to documents, heard what the sound was doing. It was not the sound of a body striking water.

It was the sound of water being *moved aside*. Repeatedly. Rhythmically. Stubbornly.

As if something down there were swimming. Or climbing. Or simply refusing, with the entire unreasonable mass of its will, to accept the verdict of a collapsing bridge.

Hazel lifted her tear-streaked face. She had heard it too. Her grandmother's language had a word for what Brannoc was—Wendell had heard her use it once, shyly, translating it as *the friend who argues with rivers*—and she said it now into the dark, once, like a summons.

Far below, something answered.

And behind them, deeper in the Underways, in the direction they had been fleeing, the white light of Auditor lanterns bloomed again—not three lights now, but seven, closing fast, and the lead voice rang off the stone with terrible courtesy:

*"By order of the Guild of the Hollow Gate: the unwritten child, the renegade keeper, and the courier Vale are summoned to appear. Resistance will be entered into the record."*

Wendell rose, pulled Hazel behind him, and faced the light with nothing in his hands but a ledger-man's fury and a dead friend's example.

"Then enter *this* into the record," he said quietly, and reached for the countersuit he had been drafting in his head since the market of unclaimed things.
