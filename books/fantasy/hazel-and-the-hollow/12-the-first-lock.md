## 12. The First Lock

The tollhouse had been drowning for a long time before anyone thought to mourn it.

Hazel saw it first from the ridge, where the road gave out and the marsh began—a dark shape rising out of the reeds like the shoulder of something sleeping. It had been a grand building once; she could tell by the way it slumped. Grand things slump differently than humble ones. The roof had folded inward at one corner like a hand relaxing, and pale water stood in the windows, and on the lintel above the door there were letters worn to ghosts that Brannoc read aloud in a voice gone careful and quiet.

*"Vessany collects her tolls with open hands,"* he said. *"That's what it says. That's what it used to say."*

"Used to," Serevan repeated, and said nothing else.

They came down through the reeds single file, because the ground would not hold two abreast. Hazel went last, as she always did now—not because they made her, but because someone had to keep watch behind, and Serevan's eyes were for the front, and Brannoc's were for his feet. Behind them the borderlands lay flat and gray under a sky the color of old tin, and somewhere far back along the way they had come, the guild was still looking for them. She could feel it the way you feel weather coming: a pressure behind the ears, a taste of iron.

But here, at the edge of the drowned place, the feeling changed. Here the air tasted of salt and stone and something older underneath—something that smelled, faintly and impossibly, of bread baking. Her grandmother's kitchen had smelled like that. The thought arrived whole and uninvited, and Hazel stopped walking and let it sit in her chest until it stopped hurting so much.

"You all right, little ledger?" Brannoc called back. He'd taken to calling her that after the night she'd recited the nine names in their proper order without looking at the page. She hadn't decided yet whether she liked it.

"I'm all right."

It was even mostly true.

* * *

Inside, the tollhouse was not entirely drowned. The water had come in through the broken eastern wall and filled the lower rooms to knee-depth, but the great hall at the center stood dry, its floor raised on old stone pilings, its ceiling lost in shadow. Someone had kept the water out of this room long after everyone stopped paying tolls. Hazel didn't know how she knew that, but she knew it the way she knew the names—the knowing lived in her body somewhere below thinking, down where her grandmother's voice had been planted before it could be pulled up like everything else.

Wendell lit the lamps they'd brought, three of them, and set them in a triangle around the far wall. And there it was.

The door.

It was smaller than Hazel had imagined, which felt like a joke the world was playing. All this way—all the salt flats and the hiding and the Underways and Brannoc's bridge and Serevan's blade still wet with her last obedience—and the door home was barely taller than Wendell, set into the wall like a servant's entrance, oak gone black with age, bound in iron that had not rusted. Not one fleck of orange on it anywhere. The water had risen around it for thirty years and the iron had refused, absolutely, to corrode.

"It's waiting," Hazel said.

"Everything about this place is waiting," Wendell murmured. He had his ledger out already, of course—he carried it the way other men carried weapons—and he was writing by lamplight, quick small strokes. "Nine locks. We discussed this. The founding ledger says each is keyed to a name the guild took when—" He stopped. He did that sometimes, stopped just short of the word *erasure*, as if saying it gently might make it smaller. It didn't. Nothing made it smaller.

"The names are mine to speak," Hazel said. "We've been over it too."

"We have." Wendell looked up at her then, and his face did the thing it did when he was trying very hard not to be afraid on her behalf. He was bad at it. His eyes always told the truth first. "Hazel, we know the locks take something. The ledger is clear on that. *Each name spoken returns to the door what was stolen with it.* We assumed that meant the name itself. A word. Sound leaving your mouth."

"But?"

"But I reread the passage last night. Four times." He closed the ledger. That was how she knew it was bad; Wendell never closed the ledger while he was working. "There's a marginal note in the founder's hand. Very old ink. It says: *The door does not want words. The door wants what the words were made of.*"

The lamps flickered. Outside, wind moved through the reeds with a sound like a crowd whispering.

"What were the words made of?" Hazel asked, though some part of her, the part that had crossed the salt flats alone at seven years old with her grandmother's last instruction folded into her pocket, already knew.

"Memories," Serevan said from the doorway, where she'd posted herself with her back to the room, watching the water. She always answered questions like this flatly, quickly, as if speed could sand the edges off them. "You pay with remembering. Each name you spend, you lose a piece of Vessany. You won't get to choose which piece. Or perhaps you will, and choosing is worse."

"That can't be right," Brannoc said. "That's—that's a rotten bargain. That's the guild's kind of arithmetic."

"It's exactly the guild's kind of arithmetic," Wendell said quietly. "Which is why I believe it. They built the lock. They built it to hurt."

Hazel walked toward the door.

She didn't decide to. Her feet simply went, across the dry stones, past the triangle of lamplight, until she stood close enough to see herself reflected, small and wavering, in the black iron. Up close the door wasn't plain at all. There were lines cut into it, fine as spiderwork, and when she tilted her head the lines resolved into script—the old tongue, her tongue, the language no living kingdom remembered except one mud-streaked child and whatever was left sleeping inside this wood.

Nine locks, and she could only find eight. She counted twice. The ninth was nowhere, or everywhere, or hidden the way her grandmother had hidden sweets in her sleeve: plainly, once you knew to look.

"Hazel." Wendell's voice behind her, gentle. "You don't have to be the one. We could study it longer. Weeks, maybe. There might be another way to—"

"There isn't." She turned around. "You know there isn't. The Auditors found the Underways. They'll find this place. Every day we wait is a day they get closer, and every day I wait is a day I forget more anyway." She said the last part before she understood it, and then understood it, and her stomach went cold. Because it was true. Vessany was already leaking out of her. She couldn't remember the color of the salt flats' sky at noon anymore—she knew it had been blue, but the blue itself was gone, like a page torn out with the next page stuck to it. The country was being erased twice: once by the guild, and once by distance.

"If I don't spend my memories on the door," she said, "I'll lose them for nothing."

Nobody argued. That was the worst part, how nobody argued. Brannoc sat down heavily on an overturned toll-cart and put his face in his hands. Serevan didn't move at all, which for Serevan was a kind of weeping.

"All right," Wendell said at last. "All right. Then we do it properly." And he came forward and knelt beside her at the door, and opened the founding ledger across his knees, and became, for a few minutes, exactly what he had been for thirty years: the keeper, keeping. "Say it when you're ready. Not before. And Hazel—if it takes too much, if you start to lose something you can't afford—stop. Stop mid-word if you have to. Promise me."

"Doors don't work that way."

"Promise me anyway."

"I promise," she said, because he needed it, and because promises are sometimes doors too—they open things in the people who hear them.

She turned back to the iron. She laid both palms flat against it. The metal was cold, then less cold, then—strangely—warm, warm like a hand that has been holding yours a long time, and beneath her palms she felt a trembling, very fast and very small, like a bird's heart.

She chose the first name. The oldest one. *Aurelian Vess,* who had been a shepherd before she was anything else, whose name the country itself had been built around, whose flocks had grazed these hills when the hills were green instead of drowned. Her grandmother had taught her the name in pieces, singing it, the way you teach a child anything you're afraid they'll need someday.

Hazel took a breath that tasted of bread baking.

"Aurelian Vess," she said.

The lock answered.

It did not click or turn. It *answered*—the script on the door flared white-gold, every line of it, and the warmth under her palms surged up her arms and into her chest, and the world went sideways and bright, and for one impossible moment Hazel was standing in a green field with sheep all around her and a woman laughing nearby, a woman with—

With—

The memory went out of her like water through fingers.

She felt it go. That was the cruelty of it, the precise and perfect guild-cruelty of it: she felt the exact shape of the thing as it left. A woman laughing near a flock of sheep. Her grandmother, younger than Hazel had ever known her, young and laughing in a green field, and the field was Vessany before the drowning, before the erasure, and the laugh was warm and low and it rose at the end the way her grandmother's laugh always rose, and Hazel reached for the face above the laugh and the face was

gone.

Not blurred. Not faded. *Gone*, the way a tooth is gone from the gum—you can find the absence with your tongue forever. She knew she had known the face. She knew the knowing had existed. But the thing itself had been paid out, spent, spent *on the door*, and the door drank it the way the marsh drank the road, without comment, without gratitude, and the first lock unfolded with a sound like a held breath released after thirty years.

Hazel came back to herself kneeling on the stones. She didn't remember falling. Wendell had her shoulders; Brannoc was saying her name over and over in a voice cracked down the middle; Serevan had finally turned around.

"One," Wendell said hoarsely. "One lock. Look—one lock, Hazel. It's open."

She looked. In the black iron, high up where the ninth lock should have been and wasn't, a mechanism had revealed itself—wheels within wheels, turning slowly, turning *gratefully*, like a joint unbending after years of being wrong. One-ninth of the door was awake.

"What did it take?" Brannoc asked softly. "What did it take from you, little ledger?"

And here was the thing Hazel discovered then, kneeling in a drowned tollhouse with her company gathered around her like a wall against the dark: she could answer him honestly, and the honest answer was unbearable, and she was going to say it anyway, because her grandmother had not taught her the names so that she could flinch from their price.

"My grandmother's face," she said. "From before. When she was young. There was a field, and sheep, and she was laughing, and I had her face, and now—" Her voice did something she hated, wobbled and split, and she pushed through it. "Now I know she laughed. I know the laugh went up at the end. I know she was beautiful, because I remember knowing that. But the face itself is paid. It's in the door now. Maybe—" and this came to her as she said it, arriving like light through water, "maybe that means it's *in* Vessany. Maybe the door is keeping it safe for me. Maybe when the country comes back, everything I spend comes back with it."

"Maybe," Wendell said, and his voice was wrecked, and he didn't pretend otherwise. He took off his coat and put it around her shoulders, and it smelled of ink and old promises, exactly as it had the first night, and she let it.

"Eight more," Serevan said. She was staring at the turning wheels with an expression Hazel had never seen on her—something raw and unfinished, a sentence with the ending torn off. "Eight more names. Eight more prices." Her hand drifted, without her seeming to notice, to her own collarbone, where a name lived that she had not yet spoken aloud to any of them. "Child. When you spend the rest—spend them slowly. Make the door wait for every coin. Make it *ask*."

"Why?" Hazel asked.

"Because," Serevan said, "things that ask are things that can be bargained with. And things that take without asking—" her jaw tightened— "those are the things I used to work for."

Outside, the light through the broken eastern wall had gone from gray to the deep bruised purple of evening. Somewhere out in the reeds, a bird called three notes and got no answer. Wendell was already writing again, recording the price in his ledger in his small precise hand, and when Hazel leaned over to look she saw he had not written *one lock opened*. He had written:

*Aurelian Vess — paid in full. Price: one memory, Hazel's own. Held in trust by the door. To be returned.*

"To be returned," she read aloud.

"It's not a legal claim," Wendell said. "I can't enforce it. There's no court that would hear it." He looked up at her, and his eyes told the truth first, the way they always did, and the truth in them was fierce. "But I've spent thirty years learning what the guild's ledgers can do. Tonight I started a different kind of book. And in my book, little heir, debts run both directions. The door owes you now. And I intend to collect."

Hazel pulled his coat tighter around her shoulders and looked at the door—eight locks still sleeping, eight names still waiting in her mouth like coins under her tongue, eight prices she could not yet imagine—and she found, underneath the grief where her grandmother's young laughing face used to be, a hard bright seed of something that was not grief at all.

"Then let's rest," she said. "Tomorrow we pay again."

And far out in the marsh, where the reeds whispered their crowd-whisper, something else whispered back—and this time, all four of them heard it.
