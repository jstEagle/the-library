## 14. Serevan's Name

The drowned tollhouse stood at the edge of a lake that had no business being there.

Hazel had learned, in the weeks since the Underways, that geography in the borderlands was less a fact than a rumor. Rivers wandered. Hills migrated. And sometimes, without warning, a lake appeared where the maps insisted there was only dry stone—dark water lying flat and patient as a held breath, as if it had been waiting all this time for someone to come and ask it a question.

The tollhouse leaned out over the water on three legs of black timber, its fourth corner sunk into the lake itself. Windows like closed eyes. A roof furred with moss. Once, Hazel knew from the founding ledger's careful script, it had collected tolls for the road into Vessany: one coin, one kindness, or one true name, depending on the traveler. Now it collected nothing but rainwater and silence, and the second lock waited inside it like a heart waiting to be found.

They came down to it at dusk, when the water turned the color of old pewter and the first moths rose out of the reeds. Brannoc went first, because Brannoc always went first when there was a chance of falling—he said it was so no one else had to learn what the bottom looked like, though Hazel suspected he simply liked being useful and would die before admitting it. Wendell followed with the ledger wrapped in oilcloth against his chest, carrying it the way other men carried infants. Serevan came last, silent as spilled ink, her eyes moving over the reeds and the ridgeline and the darkening sky in that constant counting way of hers.

Hazel walked between them, and she was afraid, but she had gotten good at walking anyway.

That was something the road taught you, she thought. Not courage exactly. Courage was a story-word, big and shining, the kind of thing grandmothers put in songs. What the road actually taught you was smaller and stranger: how to keep your feet moving while your stomach argued, how to be afraid in one part of yourself and practical in another, how to cry quietly enough not to wake the people who needed their sleep more than they needed your grief. She had cried for her grandmother every night since the first lock. She had done it silently, into her sleeve, in the hour before dawn, and then she had gotten up and helped break camp, because the names would not speak themselves and grief does not open doors.

She missed her grandmother's face most of all. That was the terrible trick of it—the memory of loving her was still there, warm and whole, but whenever Hazel reached for the face itself, her mind slid off it like rain off glass. She remembered small hands teaching hers to fold the morning-blessing. She remembered the exact tuneless hum of the evening song. But the face above those hands was a soft bright blur, a candle seen through wet paper, and every time she tried to look at it she paid the price again, a little ache where the memory used to sit, like a tooth pulled clean but still complaining.

*Worth it,* she told herself, as she told herself every day. *One name spoken. One lock opened. Worth it.*

The tollhouse door was unlocked. It swung inward at Brannoc's touch with a groan of swollen wood, exhaling air that smelled of lake-slime and, beneath it, faintly, impossibly, of beeswax—as if someone had polished the pews within living memory and the smell had simply refused to leave.

Inside: a single long room, half its floor gone to black water. Wooden benches in rows, rotted soft. And on the far wall, above a counter built for a collector who had been dead thirty years, the second lock.

It looked, at first glance, like nothing at all—a disc of pale wood set flush into the wall, uncarved, unmarked. But when Hazel stepped closer the grain moved. Slowly, lazily, like worms turning in fruit, the whorls of the wood rearranged themselves, and Hazel understood with a lurch that she was looking at writing—not letters, but the *idea* of letters, the raw material of language waiting to be told what to become.

"It knows we're here," she whispered.

"Everything here knows we're here," Serevan said. Her voice was flat, professional, but her hand had gone to her knife, which was a habit Hazel had catalogued early: Serevan touched the knife when she was frightened, the way other people touched charms. "Question is whether knowing and *caring* are the same thing."

"They're the same thing," Hazel said. "Grandmother said the locks aren't guards. They're *listeners*. You don't fight a listener. You just have to have something true to say."

Brannoc had wedged himself by the door, arms crossed, wearing the expression he wore whenever magic got too comfortable. "And if what you say turns out wrong? If the lock doesn't like your answer?"

"Then it stays shut," Hazel said, "and we try again tomorrow, and the day after, until we run out of tomorrows." She shrugged, because it was easier than saying *and then the Auditors find us and everything ends.* "Locks are patient. We have to be too."

She set down her pack and took out the founding ledger, the one with the razored pages, the one whose surviving fragments she had spent weeks learning to read. The nine names were written there in the founder's cramped, greedy hand—but written as thefts were written, in inventory-script, each name reduced to a line item, an asset, a weight of value extracted. Reading them made Hazel's skin crawl. It was like watching someone weigh a baby on a fish scale.

Seven names remained. Seven memories left to spend, and only four of them who could pay.

"Second name," Wendell said softly. He had come to stand beside her, and he read the entry upside down with the ease of a man who had spent thirty years reading ledgers in bad light. His finger stopped on the line, and then his finger *flinched*, a tiny motion, quickly stilled. "Ah," he said.

Hazel looked up. "What?"

Wendell didn't answer. He was looking past her, across the ruined room, at Serevan.

Serevan had gone very still. Stillness was her native country, but this was different—this was the stillness of a deer that has heard the bowstring, not the stillness of a hunter choosing her moment. Her eyes were fixed on the ledger in Hazel's hands, on the line Wendell's finger had touched, and her lips were moving slightly, shaping syllables without sound, the way a person reads something they cannot believe.

"Serevan," Wendell said carefully. "The second name. Read it."

"I've read it."

"Read it *aloud.*"

For a moment Hazel thought she wouldn't. Then Serevan crossed the room in four strides, took the ledger from Hazel's hands—gently, which frightened Hazel more than any violence would have—and read the line in a voice like a blade being drawn slowly:

"*Asset recovered: one name, female, infant age, extracted Vessany province of Sel Maro, year of erasure. Designation upon extraction: Sereth va Anhari.*"

The words fell into the room and lay there.

Hazel watched Serevan's face do something she had never seen it do. It *broke formation*. All the trained flatness, the assassin's weather-smooth calm—it cracked along lines Hazel hadn't known were there, and behind the cracks was a face much younger, much more lost, a girl's face looking out through a killer's eyes.

"That's not possible," Serevan said. Her voice stayed level. Her hands did not. "I was born in Karvess. I have papers. I have a *history*. The guild raised me from—" She stopped. Started again. "I remember my mother's kitchen. I remember—"

"Do you?" Wendell asked, very gently. "Or do you remember being *told* about your mother's kitchen?"

The silence stretched so long that the lake outside seemed to lean in to listen.

"The guild doesn't recruit orphans," Wendell said, still gentle, still terrible. "I've kept their books for thirty years; I know where the knives come from. They *make* them. And the cheapest material for making a knife is a child with no name to anchor her anywhere else." He nodded at the ledger. "They didn't steal a name from some Vessany family, Serevan. They stole a name and *built you around the hole it left.* You're not holding someone else's inheritance. You're holding your own."

Hazel's chest hurt. She thought of her grandmother saying, once, in the old tongue: *A name is the first home you ever have. Everything else you can rebuild.* And the guild had burned this woman's first home down before she was old enough to walk, and then handed her a knife and called the ashes a childhood.

"You don't have to say it," Hazel blurted. "You don't. We'll find another way, we can—"

"There is no other way." Serevan's voice had gone quiet, which was worse than loud. "There are seven names and four of us and Auditors close enough that Brannoc can smell them. There is no other way. There is only whether I say it or whether I'm a coward about it."

"It won't just open a lock," Wendell said. He said it like a man pulling a splinter, hating the necessity. "You understand that. Speaking your stolen name at a listener-lock costs the speaker a memory of Vessany. But this isn't a memory for you, is it. This is—" He searched for the word and didn't find a kind one. "This is the load-bearing wall. Your training. Your guild-loyalty. Whatever the guild wove you out of instead of a childhood. Say the name, and the lock takes its payment, and I don't know what's left after."

"Neither do I," Serevan said. And then, and this was the thing Hazel would carry for the rest of her life, Serevan almost smiled. "Good. I've never once been curious about anything. Curiosity gets knives killed. Maybe whatever's left will get to be curious."

Brannoc pushed off the doorframe. "Sereth," he said—the stolen name, trying it in his mouth like a coin he wasn't sure was gold. "Va Anhari. That's a Sel Maro name. My grandmother was a Sel Maro woman. Before—" He waved a hand that meant *before the erasure, before the unwriting, before the hole in the map.* "We used to sing a naming-song for babies from that province. Didn't know why. Just knew the tune." His voice roughened. "Girl, if you're going to do this, do it while someone here remembers how to sing you *into* something, not just out."

Serevan looked at him for a long moment. Then she began, methodically, to take off her weapons.

The knife first. She laid it on the counter beneath the lock, and the sound of steel on rotten wood was very loud. Then the second knife at her back. The garrote coiled at her wrist. The thin needles in her sleeve-cuffs, the flat poison vials, the lockpick case sewn into her belt. She stripped herself of her trade piece by piece, laying them out in neat rows like offerings, like evidence, like the contents of a life being inventoried, and Hazel understood suddenly that this was a ritual, that Serevan was doing in front of witnesses what the guild had never allowed anyone to do: *putting the knife down.*

When she was done she stood in a plain gray shirt with empty hands, and she looked smaller. She looked, Hazel thought, her actual size.

"How does it work?" Serevan asked Hazel. "The speaking."

"You hold the name in your mouth like water," Hazel said. Her grandmother's voice was in her own now; she could feel it. "Don't spit it. Don't swallow it. Let the lock drink."

Serevan nodded. She stepped up to the pale wooden disc. Up close, the grain had stopped moving; the whorls had arranged themselves into a listening shape, a cupped-hand shape, a *waiting* shape.

"Sing her in, courier," Serevan said, without turning around. "In case there's an empty space afterward. Fill it with something."

So Brannoc sang. His voice was a gravel-road voice, cracked and unlovely, but he sang the Sel Maro naming-song the way you sing things you didn't know you'd kept: slowly, uncertainly at first, then gathering, the old vowels rising out of him like fish rising to feed, and Hazel joined in on the third line because her grandmother had sung it too, everyone from Vessany had sung it too, it was the song you sang a child so the world would know what to call her—

And Serevan spoke her name.

"*Sereth va Anhari.*"

The old tongue came off her tongue strangely, clumsily, a language she'd been robbed of before she could keep it—but the lock didn't care about accent. The pale wood *drank*. The whorls swirled, tightened, flared with a light like sun through honey, and the payment came due.

Hazel saw it happen and had no words for it, then or ever. It was not dramatic. There was no scream, no flash. Serevan simply *changed*, the way a landscape changes when a cloud passes: everything in the same place, all the light different. The assassin's coiled readiness drained out of her shoulders. The counting eyes went wide and unguarded. Twenty years of training, of loyalty woven into the place where love should have gone, of obedience worn smooth as river stone—all of it lifted away like fog burning off, and what stood there blinking in the sudden bare daylight of herself was a woman of perhaps thirty who had never killed anyone, standing in a drowned tollhouse, holding absolutely nothing.

She swayed. Brannoc caught her—one arm, quick as a man catching a sack of grain, except that his face was wrecked with tenderness—and eased her down onto a bench that somehow held.

Behind her, with a sound like a great sigh, like a door in a house where someone has finally come home, the second lock opened. Pale wood split along a seam no one had seen, folding back into the wall, revealing a hollow lined in lead, and in the hollow, a key of green-gold metal, warm to the touch, shaped like a reed.

Nobody moved toward it.

Serevan sat on the bench and wept.

Not loudly. Not beautifully. She wept the way rain falls on a roof in the night—steadily, helplessly, without any plan—and when Hazel knelt in front of her and took her empty hands, Serevan looked down at her with eyes that were still swimming, still new, and said, in a voice with no craft in it at all:

"Why am I crying? I don't—nothing hurts. Nothing happened. Why am I *crying?*"

"Because something ended," Hazel said, and her own tears came then, hot and unashamed, "and endings are heavy even when they're good ones. You gave yourself back. That's the heaviest thing a person can carry, giving yourself back."

"I don't remember giving anything."

"I know," Hazel said. "I'll remember it for both of us. That's what we do now. That's the whole company—we remember for each other."

Outside, the lake had begun to glow. Faintly, from below, green-gold light was rising through the black water, the drowned quarter of the tollhouse waking, and far out in the middle of the lake something surfaced and sank again—something long and pale, like a finger beckoning, pointing down toward the deep where the third lock slept under thirty years of forgetting.

Brannoc's song had run out. In the quiet, Wendell picked up the reed-shaped key, wrapped it beside the first, and said what they were all thinking:

"The next one's underwater."

Serevan—Sereth, Hazel corrected herself, though the name still fit her like borrowed clothes—wiped her face with the heel of her hand and looked out at the glowing water with brand-new eyes.

"Then somebody," she said, "had better teach me to swim."

* * *

They made camp that night in the tollhouse loft, because none of them could bear to leave the lock behind unwatched, and because the lake-light rising through the broken floorboards was the closest thing to a hearth any of them had.

Hazel couldn't sleep. She sat by the gap in the floor with the founding ledger open on her knees, reading the remaining seven names by green-gold light, mouthing them silently, learning them the way you learn the path to a well in the dark. Five locks left. Five payments. She counted what the company had left to give and found the arithmetic ugly: her own memories, dwindling; Wendell's thirty years, already mortgaged; Brannoc, who owed everyone except, miraculously, her; and Sereth, who had given the largest thing of all and didn't know it.

A floorboard creaked. Sereth lowered herself onto the boards beside her, moving carefully, like a woman learning her own body's habits from scratch. For a while neither of them spoke. The lake breathed below them.

"In the guild," Sereth said finally, "there was a rule. Never sleep in a room with a window facing water. Reflections show you things." She paused. "I don't know why I'm telling you that. I don't know why I'm telling you *anything.*"

"Because I asked you to remember for both of us," Hazel said. "And remembering starts with talking."

Sereth considered this with the seriousness of someone encountering the concept for the first time. "Then tell me something I should remember. About me. About today."

So Hazel told her. She told her about the knives laid out in rows on the counter, about the naming-song, about the way the lock drank and the fog lifted, about a woman putting down twenty years and standing up lighter. She told it slowly, thoroughly, leaving nothing out, and Sereth listened with her whole body, hoarding the story the way a starving person hoards bread.

When she finished, Sereth was quiet a long time. Then: "The woman you're describing sounds brave."

"She was."

"I don't feel brave. I feel like a house with the furniture gone."

"Furniture isn't the house," Hazel said, and thought of her grandmother, and had to look at the water for a while.

Below them, the lake pulsed its slow green-gold pulse, and far out at its center, the pale thing surfaced again—closer now, Hazel was almost sure, closer than before. She watched it sink, and felt the founding ledger grow warm against her knees, and read by its light the third name on the founder's list.

She read it twice.

Then she looked up at the sleeping shapes of her strange, battered, chosen family, and out at the beckoning water, and understood that the lake was not waiting anymore.

The lake was calling.
