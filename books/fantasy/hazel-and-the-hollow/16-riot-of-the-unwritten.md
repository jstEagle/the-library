## 16. Riot of the Unwritten

Hazel had always thought that escaping meant running. That was what Gran's stories taught: when the salt riders came, run; when the unwriting wind rose off the flats, run; when a door closed behind you, run before it finished closing. Running was the first thing she'd learned and the last thing she still trusted.

But the Archive did not let you run. The Archive let you *fall*.

They came up out of the dream together, the four of them, hands knotted into a chain—Wendell's grip cold and ink-stained, Serevan's iron, Brannoc's huge and shaking—and the moment Hazel's boots found stone instead of starlight, she understood that Wendell had done something in there. Something he wasn't saying yet. His face was pale as ledger paper, and his left hand kept opening and closing like it was trying to hold something that had already been taken from it.

"Move," Serevan said. "Now. When the Archive wakes fully, it will want its dreams back."

"It can't have them," Hazel said.

"No," Serevan agreed, and there was something new in her voice, something that hadn't been there before the second lock, before her name. "It can't."

Behind them, down the spiral of the vault, the dreaming began to rise.

Hazel felt it first in her teeth—a hum, low and vast, like the sound a country makes when it turns over in its sleep. Then light: not lamplight, not guild-light, but the soft gold of remembered afternoons, spilling up the stairwell in ribbons. And with the light came voices. Hundreds of them. Thousands. They were murmuring at first, half-formed, the way people talk in dreams—but they were getting louder, and clearer, and they were all speaking the old tongue.

*Vessany,* they said. *Vessany, Vessany.* A name being remembered by ten thousand mouths at once.

"The doors," Wendell breathed. He was staring at the great bronze gates at the top of the stair, the ones stamped with the guild's seal—a circle with a line struck through it, the mark of things unwritten. "Hazel, the doors are sealed from the outside. We came in through the under-croft, we can't—"

"We don't need out," Hazel said.

She didn't know where the words came from. That was a lie, actually—she knew exactly where they came from. They came from the same place the nine names had come from, the place Gran's voice lived now, the place that had been hollowed out of her memory lock by lock until what remained was not emptiness but a kind of room, a clean bright space where knowing could stand. She walked to the bronze doors and put both palms flat against them, and she spoke—not a stolen name this time, nothing the guild had ever owned, but her own. The one Gran had given her at the tollhouse window with the salt wind coming through.

"Hazel Vessany-who-was. Daughter of the Toll Collector. Last heir of the unwritten country." Her voice cracked on *unwritten*, and she let it crack. "Open for the people you swallowed. Let them walk."

The seal split like an eggshell.

* * *

What came out of the Living Archive was not a mob. Hazel would spend the rest of her life correcting anyone who called it that. It was a procession. It was a homecoming with no home to come to, so it made one out of itself as it went.

They poured up the under-croft stairs and into the streets of Corvane's guild quarter, and they came out *dressed*—in the way dreams dress, which is to say perfectly. Farmers in coats the color of wet slate. Fishwives with nets looped over their shoulders, nets that shimmered because they had been dreamed rather than woven. A whole choir of bell-ringers, robed in Vessany blue, who fell into step without speaking and somehow already knew the song. Children. That was the part that undid Hazel, the part she had to look away from and then make herself look back at: children, dozens of them, erased before they'd ever drawn breath outside their mothers, walking now with the solemn wonder of kids at a festival, holding the hands of grandmothers they'd never met except in the dark of the vault, where everyone met everyone eventually.

An old man stopped in the middle of Lamplighter's Row and looked down at the cobblestones, and Hazel watched him remember them. Watched his face go through confusion and grief and then a kind of terrible delight, because these stones had been laid by Vessani masons two hundred years before the guild existed, before the erasure, back when this street had another name—*Sorrow's End*, Gran would have said, *because the road from the coast ended there and everything past it was joy*. The old man knelt and put his palm on the cobblestones like a man greeting a horse.

"They built on top of us," he said, in the old tongue, and Hazel translated without meaning to, the words coming out of her in Common for the crowd gathering at the corners. "They built their city on our bones and called it theirs. But bones remember."

"Bones remember!" someone echoed, and then it became a chant, and the chant became the night.

Brannoc caught up to her, breathing hard, his courier's satchel bouncing against his hip. "Lass. Lass, listen—the Auditors garrison is four streets over. When they hear this—"

"Let them hear it," Hazel said.

"That's my girl," Brannoc said, and then, more quietly, in the accent of his own vanished hometown, "that's *all* of our girls," and he waded into the crowd like a man walking into the sea.

* * *

The lanterns started an hour past midnight.

No one gave the order. That was the thing about the unwritten, Hazel learned: they had spent thirty years being nobody's subjects, and so no one among them waited for permission anymore. A fishwife climbed a guild lamppost and hung her own lantern from the arm of it, a little glass thing with a candle stub inside, and painted on the glass in tar was a single word in the old tongue: *REMEMBER*. Then a bell-ringer did the next one. Then a farmer. Then a child too small to reach the post, who handed her lantern up to a stranger with the gravity of a queen surrendering a crown.

By the second hour, every lamp in the guild quarter burned with a word on it. *Remember. Vessany. We Were Here. We Are Here.* The streets turned gold and the gold turned upward, gilding the faces of the guild clerks who had crept out of their dormitories in their nightshirts and stood blinking in doorways, watching ten thousand impossible people fill their tidy streets.

One clerk, young, freckled, clutching a ledger to his chest like a shield, asked Hazel—who had ended up, without quite deciding to, at the front of everything—"Is this a riot?"

Hazel looked at him. She thought about the salt flats. She thought about her village, which was not ruins now but simply *nothing*, a hole in the world shaped like everything she'd loved. She thought about Gran's last instruction, delivered with cracked lips in a language only one living child could speak: *find the keeper of the gate, and make them give it back.*

"No," she said. "A riot is when people break things. We're putting things back."

Then she took the freckled clerk's ledger out of his arms, opened it to the first page, and read aloud what the guild had recorded there. It was a list. Bottled grief, three vials, sourced from the Vessani displacement. Borrowed years, eleven, sourced likewise. Names, forty-one, purchased at erasure rates. She read it in both tongues, loud enough for the street, and the street went very quiet, and then the quiet broke into something that was not a cheer. It was deeper than a cheer. It was the sound a country makes when it stands up.

They marched on the Hall of Records at dawn.

* * *

Serevan fought twice that night, and both times Hazel watched, and both times she understood that the woman was different now—not softer, never softer, but *herself*, fighting for something instead of against it. The first time was at the mouth of Cooper's Alley, where four Auditors in grey tried to form a record-line, quills out, chanting the containment clauses, trying to write the crowd back into compliance. Serevan moved through them like weather. She didn't kill them. Hazel noticed that. She disarmed them, broke their quills one by one—snapped each nib with a small precise cruelty that made the Auditors flinch worse than any blade—and left them sitting in the alley holding their broken instruments like men holding broken faith.

The second time was worse. Guild enforcers came with fire this time, real fire, trying to burn the lanterns off the posts, and a bell-ringer—an old woman, dreamed back into the world for exactly one night—stepped between the flame and her lamp and would not move. Serevan got there first. What passed between her and the enforcer captain lasted three seconds and involved no words Hazel could hear, and afterward the enforcers withdrew, and Serevan stood in the lantern light with her blade drawn and her face wet, and when Hazel touched her elbow she said, "I used to be the reason nights like this ended badly. Let me be the reason this one doesn't."

"You are," Hazel said.

"I don't know why I'm crying."

"Because your name fits you now," Hazel said. "It takes some getting used to."

* * *

Near morning, at the steps of the Hall of Records, Wendell found her.

He looked terrible. He looked, Hazel thought, like a man who had argued with a ghost and lost the argument and won something better instead, and when she asked him what happened down in the dream he shook his head and said, "Later. Maybe much later. Ask me if I refused, and I'll tell you yes, and leave the rest for a warmer day." But he took her hand, and his grip was steadier than his face, and together they climbed the steps while the crowd parted, and the doors of the Hall of Records—oak and iron, built to withstand sieges—stood open, because somewhere inside, the clerks had voted. Or fled. Hazel suspected both.

Wendell turned to face the crowd from the top step, and Hazel saw him search for words the way he searched ledgers: carefully, thoroughly, dreading what he'd find. In the end he didn't find words. He found the old man who had knelt to the cobblestones, and the old man came and stood beside him, and raised his voice in the old tongue:

"We are the unwritten. We were erased by contract, by quill, by men who never dirtied their hands. We do not ask the guild for justice." A pause, and the whole street leaned in. "We *file* for it. Tonight. In their own hall. With their own books. Let them read what they wrote about us, and let them choke on it."

The roar that went up shook ash from the gutters. Lanterns swung. Somewhere a Vessani bell rang—one of the dreamed ones, a bell that had hung thirty years in a vault and remembered every note—and the sound rolled across the rooftops of Corvane like a tide coming in, and in the windows of the tall houses, ordinary citizens lit candles and set them in their windows, one by one, then dozens, then hundreds, until the whole city wore the gold.

Hazel stood on the steps with Wendell's hand around hers and Brannoc laughing like a landslide on her left and Serevan silent and shining on her right, and she felt the furious joy move through her like blood, like spring flood, like the exact opposite of erasure. *This,* she thought. *This is what justice looks like when it stops waiting to be granted and starts insisting on itself.*

And then, above the square, the sky answered.

It came as a shape first—a rectangle of deeper darkness against the pre-dawn grey, high over the Hall of Records, unfolding like a page turning. Then light spilled from it, cold and white and orderly, guild-light, archive-light, and descending through it came figures in grey robes, a dozen of them, floating on platforms of solidified clause, and the foremost carried a staff crowned with the guild's seal, and when he spoke, his voice arrived everywhere at once, in every ear, without traveling through the air.

"CITIZENS OF CORVANE. THE LIVING ARCHIVE HAS BEEN BREACHED. ITS CONTENTS ARE GUILD PROPERTY AND WILL BE RETURNED. THOSE HARBORING ERASED PERSONS ARE IN BREACH OF CLAUSE ONE, SUBSECTION ZERO—" he paused, and Hazel felt Wendell's hand tighten around hers, "—WHICH STATES THAT NO UNWRITTEN THING MAY CLAIM EXISTENCE BEFORE THIS COUNCIL. INCLUDING," the voice said, and Hazel knew, she *knew* what was coming, "THE KEEPER OF THE HOLLOW GATE. WENDELL BRIGHTWATER IS HEREBY SUMMONED. HIS NAME IS GUILD PROPERTY. HIS BODY FOLLOWS HIS NAME. COME DOWN FROM THOSE STEPS, KEEPER, OR BE COLLECTED."

The crowd went still. The lanterns burned. And Wendell Brightwater, invisible for thirty years, stepped forward to the edge of the platform, looked up at the descending council, and smiled the smallest smile Hazel had ever seen.

"Gentlemen," he said, "you should have read my countersuit more carefully. Clause One protects *your* records. But I filed mine elsewhere." He lifted his hand, and in it was a single sheet of paper, glowing faintly the way documents glow in dreams. "You'll find I no longer belong to you. None of us do. And as for collection—" he glanced down at Hazel, at Brannoc, at Serevan, at the ten thousand risen dead of Vessany filling the square below, "—I'm afraid you're outnumbered by everything you threw away."

The council descended anyway.
