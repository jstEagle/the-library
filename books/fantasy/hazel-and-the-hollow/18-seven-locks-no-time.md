## 18. Seven Locks, No Time

Hazel had learned to count in the old tongue before she could count in any other, and so she counted the locks as they came, because counting was the only prayer she knew that still worked.

Three locks behind them. Six ahead.

The drowned tollhouse's corridor sloped downward into stone that sweated cold water, and the door waited at the bottom of it like a held breath. Behind them—above them, back through the flooded stair and the broken grate and the long dark of the Underways—the Auditors were coming. She could not hear them yet. That was worse. Serevan said you never heard an Auditor until it was too late; they moved the way a debt moves through a family, silent until it takes everything.

"Third lock," Wendell said, and his voice had gone thin as ledger paper. "Hazel—the third lock."

She knelt before it. The lock was iron gone green with age, shaped like a coiled fish, its keyhole a small open mouth. Around its rim, letters crawled in the old tongue, and when she read them aloud they tasted like her grandmother's kitchen: salt bread and woodsmoke and something bitter underneath.

*Vessany, who was called the Breadbasket of the Sea.*

The name rose in her the way a bubble rises through deep water. She could feel it—whole, round, shining—and she could feel what it was tethered to. A memory. The guild had stolen nine names and locked each one behind the price of remembering, and the price was always paid from the same purse.

"Which one will it take?" she asked. Her voice echoed strangely off the wet stone, coming back smaller than she'd sent it.

"I don't know," Wendell said. "I'm sorry. I don't know."

Brannoc crouched beside her, big hands hanging useless at his knees. He'd been holding bridges and breaking doors for two days without sleep, and there was dust in his beard and blood on his knuckles, and he looked at her now the way people look at a fire they can't warm themselves by. "You don't have to do this one, little bird. We could find another way."

"There is no other way." She said it gently, because he needed gentleness, and then she put her hand flat against the cold coil of the fish and spoke the name.

"*Vessany.*"

The lock drank it. She felt the drinking happen—a pulling sensation behind her eyes, like a hook set gently in a fish's mouth—and then something went out of her, quiet as a lamp blown out.

She sat back on her heels and searched herself the way you search your pockets after a thief has passed. What was gone?

For a long moment she couldn't tell. That was the cruelest part. You couldn't miss what you couldn't remember missing.

Then Brannoc touched her shoulder and said, "It's done, little bird," and she heard his voice and thought: *I don't know what his village sounded like.* She had known. Two days ago, around the fire, when he'd confessed about the grain manifests and the decree, he had told her the name of the town he came from—a town with a bell tower that leaned, he'd said, like a tired old man—and she had *known* that town, had known its sound, because every town in Vessany had a sound and she had carried all of them out of the salt flats in her chest like a basket of eggs.

Now one egg was gone. One town was just a word again: *a bell tower that leaned.*

"It took a town," she whispered.

Wendell made a sound low in his throat, and when she looked up at him his face was doing something complicated—grief and fury braided together, the way rope is braided so it won't snap under load. "Which one?"

"I don't—" She stopped. Tried again. "One with a leaning bell tower. I think. I only know it's gone because I know how many I used to have."

Serevan stood at the mouth of the corridor with her knives drawn, watching the dark behind them. She did not turn around. "Then we move faster," she said. "Grief keeps. Auditors don't."

* * *

The fourth lock was a ring of nine small bells fused into a wheel, and it cost Hazel the smell of rain on Vessany slate.

The fifth was a knot of woven hair, and it cost her the game she and the other tollhouse children played with knucklebones and lies, the one where the loser had to carry everyone's secrets home. She remembered that there had been a game. She remembered losing it, once, gloriously. She could no longer remember what the secrets were or whose they'd been, and when she tried to reach for the shape of the game itself, her mind slid off it like rain off oilcloth.

By the sixth lock she was crying without deciding to, the way you bleed without deciding to, and Brannoc carried her on his back between the locks because her legs had begun to shake, and she kept her face pressed into his collar and counted what remained. Grandmother's voice. Grandmother's hands kneading dough. The road out, the salt flats burning white under a sun that had no business being kind. The instruction: *find the keeper of the gate, tell him the door remembers him even if the world doesn't.*

Those she guarded with her teeth. Whatever the locks asked next, they would have to fight her for those.

"Hazel." Wendell's hand on her ankle, steadying her as Brannoc set her down before the seventh lock. His face was gray. Not metaphorically gray—actually gray, the color of old paper, and she realized with a lurch that the locks had been taking something from him too, or that the corridor itself was, or that thirty years of careful invisibility were finally catching up to a man running toward a door instead of away from everything else. "This one's different."

She looked. The seventh lock was not iron or bone or bell. It was a sheet of vellum, stretched across the doorway like a drum skin, and written on it in ink that still gleamed wet after thirty years was a single line:

*A memory freely given, freely lost, of the road home.*

"Not hers," Wendell read aloud, and his voice cracked down the middle like dropped crockery. "It wants—it wants someone who remembers a road home. Someone who's been walking away from one for a long time."

"No," Brannoc said.

"Brannoc—"

"You've given enough. You gave three years of your own life to hide her in a ledger entry. You gave—" He stopped, jaw working. Somewhere far above them, faint as a thought, came a sound none of them wanted to hear: the soft, precise click of Auditor boots finding the flooded stair.

"There's no time to argue about who owes what," Wendell said. "There's only time to pay." And before anyone could stop him—not Brannoc, not Serevan, not Hazel with her small fierce hands—he pressed his palm to the vellum and said, clearly, in the accent of a man reading his own eulogy:

"The road home. I remember the road home."

The vellum drank.

Hazel watched it happen and would spend the rest of her life trying to describe it and failing. It wasn't that Wendell changed. It was that something *left* him, and the leaving showed. He swayed. Brannoc caught him under the arms. And when Wendell opened his eyes again they were clear and calm and terribly empty in one particular corner, the way a house is empty in the room where the piano used to be.

"What did it take?" Hazel demanded. She grabbed his sleeve. "What did it take from you? Tell me so I can keep it! Tell me and I'll write it down and it won't be gone, it'll be *somewhere*—"

He smiled at her. It was a real smile, which somehow made it worse.

"I don't know," he said. "That's rather the point, isn't it?" He flexed his fingers, testing himself like a man testing a numb limb. "Let me see. The guild. I know I was raised by the guild. I know the dormitory smelled of tallow and the master of acolytes had a whistle he blew for supper. I know these things the way you know things from books." His brow furrowed, delicately, like a hand smoothing a page. "But I can't—I can't feel it anymore. There was a morning. I remember that there was a morning. My first morning there, when I was very small, and someone—" His voice stopped working. He tried again. "Someone walked me down the corridor. Held my hand. I don't know who. I don't know their face. I know only that it happened, and that it mattered, and that it's gone."

"That's not fair," Hazel said furiously, and her tears came hot and fast. "That's not FAIR, it was supposed to be MY memories, it's MY country—"

"And it is your country," Wendell said, kneeling so his eyes were level with hers, "and that is exactly why it isn't only yours. A country is a thing many people carry. I have been carrying my piece for thirty years without knowing it. Consider this me finally putting it down."

Boots in the water above. Closer now. Serevan turned fully toward the sound, and Hazel saw her roll her shoulders the way she did before violence, unhurried, almost tender, like a woman greeting an old acquaintance she intended to disappoint.

"Seventh lock's open," Serevan said. "Two more. Move."

* * *

They ran.

The corridor narrowed and the water rose to their ankles, then their shins, and the eighth lock appeared out of the gloom like a drowning thing surfacing: a great wheel of black wood studded with teeth, turning slowly against nothing, grinding out a sound like a millstone full of grief.

Hazel didn't wait to be asked. She climbed onto Brannoc's shoulders and put both hands on the wheel and spoke the eighth name into its teeth.

"*Vessany, who kept the lanterns lit along the coast roads.*"

The wheel shuddered. Drank.

And this time she felt the taking so sharply she screamed.

It wasn't a town. It wasn't a smell or a game. It was the *lanterns themselves*—every lantern she had ever seen in her life, snuffed at once inside her memory. The festival lights of the harvest. The little flame her grandmother let her carry to the henhouse on dark mornings, cupped in a jar. The beacon on the cliff that guided the fishing boats home, which she had watched from her grandmother's window on nights too beautiful to sleep, thinking: *that light means someone is coming home to someone.*

All of it, dark. All of it, gone. She knew the words for those lights and nothing else. She was left holding the vocabulary of warmth with no warmth in it.

Brannoc lowered her to the ground and she couldn't stand, so she didn't; she knelt in the rising water and shook, and Brannoc knelt with her, and this time he didn't say *you don't have to* because there was nothing left to say. Instead he took her hand and pressed it flat against his own chest, over his heart, so she could feel it beating, steady and stubborn and stupidly brave, and he said, "Still here, little bird. Still here. One more."

"One more," she repeated, because repeating things made them true.

And then the water upstream went quiet.

Not calm—quiet. The specific silence of something drinking the sound of its own approach. Serevan went rigid. Wendell raised his head, and Hazel saw him do the arithmetic on his face and arrive at a number he hated.

"How far?" Serevan breathed.

"Close enough," Wendell said. "Ninth lock. Now. Everyone else goes ahead—Brannoc, take her—no. No, she has to speak it. But we hold the corridor while she does."

They ran the last stretch, and the ninth lock rose before them, and Hazel's heart broke a little more just looking at it, because it was not iron or vellum or a wheel of teeth.

It was a door within the door. A small one, child-height, painted the faded blue of a certain window she could no longer quite picture, and set into it was a keyhole shaped like a hand waiting to be held.

Above it, in the old tongue: *The last name is the first name. Speak the one who kept you.*

Her grandmother's name.

Hazel knew it. Of course she knew it—she had been saying it since before she could say anything else, *Maren, Maren, Maren*, a name like a hearthfire, a name like safe. She opened her mouth to give it, to pay the last price, already bracing for whatever the lock would tear out of her—

And the blue door spoke first.

Not in words. In a feeling, vast and patient, pouring through the keyhole like light through a crack: *not yet, little one. This lock does not want the dead. It wants the living. It wants the name of the one who kept the gate.*

Hazel blinked. Looked at Wendell.

Wendell had gone absolutely still, the way a man goes still when the floor opens onto a truth he's been standing on his whole life without knowing. His lips moved. No sound came out. Then, barely audible, half to himself, in the tone of a bookkeeper discovering an error thirty years deep in the ledgers:

"The keeper of the gate. The ninth name is the keeper of the gate. It's always been the keeper of the gate." He looked up at her, and his eyes were wide and wet and terrified. "Hazel. Your grandmother. What did she call me? When she sent you—what name did she give you for me? Not *keeper*. Not *Brightwater*. What did she—"

And Hazel, standing in the rising water with the Auditors' silence closing behind them and her grandmother's last instruction unspooling in her memory like thread from a spindle, understood.

The door didn't want a sacrifice.

The door wanted a *name*—a true one, a kept one, one that had been hidden for thirty years inside a child's cargo of memories, entrusted to her on a burning road by a dying woman who had smiled and said: *tell him the gate remembers him, and tell him the gate knows what he gave up to guard it.*

"Your name," Hazel whispered. "Wendell. Your *real* name. She gave it to me. She's been keeping it for you—all this time, she kept it safe, she sent it with me—"

Behind them, at the mouth of the corridor, the silence ended. Boots struck water. Lantern-light bloomed, cold and white and exact, and Serevan drew both knives with a sound like winter starting.

"Hazel," Wendell said, and his voice was shaking, and it was the first time in thirty years that the invisible keeper of the Hollow Gate had ever sounded like a man who wanted to be found. "Say it. Whatever it costs—say it."
