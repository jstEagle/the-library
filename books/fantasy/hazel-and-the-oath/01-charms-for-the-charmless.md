## 1. Charms for the Charmless

The rain never came to Vellamark. That was the first thing a stranger noticed, if strangers ever came: the sky over the city was a pale, unbroken porcelain, and had been for twelve years, and the people below it had learned to call that absence *peace*. They said it in the markets, in the bathhouses, in the low voices of mothers putting children to bed — *no storms here, no storms ever again* — as though weather were a wolf they had starved at the door.

Sebastian Okafor knew better. He knew what had been traded away to buy that porcelain sky, because he had been one of the things traded.

He rose before dawn, as he always did, in the narrow room above his shop on Tallow Lane, and he lay still for a moment with his palm flat against the floorboards, listening. Not with his ears. With the thing under his skin that he had spent twelve years teaching to be quiet.

It hummed.

It always hummed. Fainter some days than others — a dry autumn day like this one kept it drowsy, sullen, half-asleep under his ribs like a banked fire — but it never stopped entirely. The storm-gift did not die when you starved it. It only learned patience, which was worse. His people had called the gift *the answering*, because that was what it felt like: the sky asked, and you answered. Thunder rolled across the high plains of his childhood and something in his blood rolled back, a greeting between kin. Now the sky over Vellamark asked nothing, sealed behind the Wardens' covenant-stones, and the answer in him had nowhere to go. It circled. It waited. Some mornings, before he was fully awake, he caught himself humming back to it, low and tuneless, an old habit from a country that no longer existed on any map the city would acknowledge.

He got up. He dressed in the clothes of a small man: a coat gone soft at the elbows, boots resoled twice, a shirt the color of dust. He looked at himself in the spotted mirror and practiced, briefly, the face.

The face was the hardest work he did, harder than any working of power he had ever attempted in the years when he still worked power. It was not a disguise exactly — his skin was dark enough, his accent worn smooth enough, that he passed in Vellamark's crowded quarters without comment — but a *posture*. Smallness was a performance, and like all performances it demanded rehearsal. He softened his eyes. He rounded his shoulders just slightly, a man accustomed to being overlooked. He arranged his mouth into the mild, apologetic expression of someone who sold trinkets and apologized for the price.

*Charm-seller,* the license on his wall read, renewed each year with a fee that took him two months to earn. *Okafor, S. Minor charms. No thaumaturgy.* The last words were redundant — there was no thaumaturgy in Vellamark, not legally, not anywhere the Wardens' quieting stones could reach — but the license carried them anyway, the way a scar carries the memory of the wound.

He went down to open the shop.

* * *

The morning market on Copperwheel Square smelled of woodsmoke and frying onions and the particular mineral tang of the fountain water, which came up from the deep aquifers and tasted faintly of iron and old stone. Stalls were unfolding along the square's edges like slow flowers: fishmongers laying out their silvered catch, a spice-woman stacking saffron in paper twists, the rope-seller uncoiling his wares with the tenderness other men reserved for children. Above it all, on the square's four corners, stood the Warden lanterns — tall black posts crowned with glass globes that held no flame, only a slow grey shimmer, like fog trapped in milk.

Everyone in Vellamark knew what the lanterns were for, and everyone agreed, publicly, not to know. They were *wardens against misfortune*, the criers said. They were *the city's blessing*. Children were taught to touch their foreheads politely when passing, a little bow of gratitude to the men who had saved them from the storm-years. Sebastian touched his forehead too, every time, with the same mild smile. It was the most elaborate lie of his day, and he performed it perhaps forty times before noon.

His stall sat in its usual place, third from the fountain, wedged between the spice-woman and a man who sold secondhand buttons by the cupful. He laid out his inventory with the care of a priest arranging relics, which was, in its way, exactly what he was doing — though his relics were hollow.

Charms for the charmless. That was what he privately called them, and the name was more honest than anything else about his trade. A knotted red cord *for safe travel*, blessed by nothing but his own steady hands. A tin medallion stamped with a sunburst *for good health*, purchased wholesale from a foundry in the Low Quarter. Dried lavender in muslin bags *for restful sleep* — and this one, at least, half worked, because lavender genuinely did help sleep, and Sebastian took a certain bitter comfort in being honest by accident. Little cards in his careful merchant's hand explained what each item was *for*. None of them did anything. None of them could. In Vellamark, nothing did.

And yet they sold. That was the strange grace note of his exile, the joke the world had played on him: the charmless bought charms. They bought them the way they bowed to the lanterns — not because they believed, but because believing had been outlawed and longing hadn't. A woman clutched a knotted cord through a difficult labor and swore afterward it had helped. A sailor wore his tin sunburst through three shipwrecks and credited the fourth escape to it too. People needed to feel that something was watching over them, even a lie, even a piece of tin. Especially a piece of tin. Tin didn't ask anything of you. Tin didn't demand oaths or tithes or the surrender of your daughters to the Gate. Tin just hung there, warm from your neck, and let you pretend the sky was empty because you preferred it that way.

Sebastian understood the pretending better than anyone alive. He made his living from it. He lived inside it.

"Morning, Okafor." The button-man, Hesk, nodding over the gap between their stalls. "Quiet one today."

"They're all quiet ones," Sebastian said, with the small rueful laugh the role required. "That's why I can afford onions."

Hesk laughed too, the same laugh they exchanged every morning, and turned to his cups of bone and horn buttons, and Sebastian settled onto his stool and became, officially, a small man in a small trade in a city where nothing ever happened.

* * *

Midmorning brought the patrols.

They came through the square the way they always did, at the change of the watch: six Wardens in their slate-grey coats, walking not in formation but in the loose, deliberate sprawl of men who wanted you to know they weren't trying. Their leader was a woman named Brakke whom Sebastian had catalogued years ago as *dangerous but lazy* — dangerous because she was observant, lazy because observation cost effort she preferred to spend elsewhere. She liked the market. She liked the fishmonger's free smoked eel and the way vendors straightened when she passed. She was, in her way, performing too: performing authority, performing vigilance, performing the peace.

Her eyes moved across the stalls. Sebastian watched her look at him — really watched, the way he watched everything now, with the whole of his attention gathered into a single quiet point behind his mild face — and saw her gaze slide off him like rain off waxed canvas. *Charm-seller. Minor charms. Nothing.* Twelve years of practice had made him transparent, and transparency, he had learned, was its own kind of armor. The storm-caller who had once stood at the Hollow Gate with lightning answering his heartbeat could not have been found in this city by torture. But the man who sold lavender and apologized for prices? Anyone could find him. Everyone did. Nobody looked.

Brakke paused at the spice stall, two down from his. "Maren. Anything I should hear about?"

The question was ritual; so was the answer. "Nothing, Warden. Quiet quarter."

"No lights after curfew? No strangers asking after the old roads?"

"No, Warden."

Sebastian kept his hands moving among his cords and medallions, sorting, straightening, a man absorbed in honest triviality. But the word *strangers* moved through him like a fish through still water, and the hum under his ribs stirred once, briefly, the way a sleeping dog stirs at a name.

No strangers came to Vellamark. That was the other thing about the city, the thing the guidebooks — if such a city had guidebooks, which it forbade — would have said. The Wardens controlled every road in, every gate, every river landing. Travel required papers, and papers required reasons, and the reasons acceptable to the Wardens numbered approximately none. Pilgrims had stopped coming decades ago, when the shrines were decommissioned and their stones quietly carted away to places no map admitted to. Traders came, but traders stayed on the sanctioned routes and left before dark. Nobody came to Vellamark looking for the old roads, because the old roads led to the old places, and the old places had been erased so thoroughly that even Sebastian sometimes had to close his eyes to remember the shape of them.

So when Brakke asked after strangers on the old roads, she wasn't asking. She was performing. And Maren answered the performance, and the patrol moved on trailing the smell of leather and lamp-oil, and the square exhaled and resumed its commerce, and Sebastian sorted his worthless charms and told himself the cold spot between his shoulder blades was only the wind off the fountain.

Except there was no wind. There was never any wind in Vellamark worth the name. The Wardens' stones had seen to that.

* * *

The afternoon passed the way his afternoons passed: in small transactions, small talk, the accumulated sediment of a deliberately small life.

A boy bought a luck-knot for his sister's wedding, paying in copper counted out one coin at a time, and Sebastian shorted the price without appearing to and told the boy the knot wanted tying in silence, which gave the child a sense of ceremony and cost Sebastian nothing. An old woman returned the lavender bag she'd bought a month ago, insisting it had lost its virtue, and Sebastian replaced it gravely, explaining that lavender tired, like all of us, and needed changing with the seasons, and the old woman went away satisfied and slightly less lonely, which was arguably the true product he sold. A clerk from the tithe-house haggled over a tin sunburst for his mother and won two coppers off the price and left feeling victorious, and Sebastian let him feel victorious, because a man who feels victorious comes back.

Between customers he mended. This was his real craft now, the one skill the exile had actually improved: he could mend anything. Cords, chains, clock-hands, kettles, the broken clasp of a widow's locket, the sprung tooth of a comb. His hands had once shaped storms; now they shaped small repairs, and there were hours — he was ashamed of the hours, and could not stop having them — when the mending satisfied him more than the storms ever had. Storms took. Mending gave back. There was a purity in it that his old life, for all its thunder, had never possessed.

But underneath the satisfaction, always, the loneliness. That was the thing he could never mend, and he had tried. Loneliness in Vellamark was not the sharp kind, the kind that howls. It was the smooth kind, worn like river stone, fitted so perfectly to the shape of his days that he sometimes mistook it for contentment. He knew everyone in the square and was known by everyone, and known *fully* by no one, because the self he presented was a coat he'd sewn so well he sometimes couldn't find the seams. Hesk liked him. Maren trusted him. The boy with the wedding knot thought him wise. And every one of them would have crossed the street if they knew what slept under his skin, what he had been, what he had done, what he had failed to do on the night the covenant broke and the sky over Vellamark closed like a book.

Twelve years. Long enough that the performance had eaten the performer, mostly. Long enough that some mornings the face in the mirror required almost no rehearsal at all, and that frightened him more than any patrol — the suspicion that smallness was not his mask anymore but his actual shape, that the storm-caller had finally starved down to a charm-seller, that if the sky ever did ask again, there would be nothing left in him to answer with.

He was thinking this — mending a kettle-spout, the square going amber and long-shadowed toward closing, the lanterns on their corners beginning their evening shimmer — when the light changed.

Not the daylight. Something else. Something in the quality of the air, a pressure shift so slight that no one else in the square noticed, that no instrument in Vellamark could have registered, but that Sebastian's body knew the way a deaf man knows footsteps through a floor.

The hum under his ribs turned over in its sleep.

He set down the kettle. He did not know why. His hands had acted ahead of him, the way they had once acted ahead of him in weather, reading signals his mind hadn't parsed yet, and he sat very still on his stool with his palms flat on the stall's counter and listened to the square with everything he had.

Onions frying. The rope-seller's last customers. A cart on cobbles. The fountain's iron-tasting water falling and falling. All ordinary. All wrong, somehow, in aggregate — the ordinary sounds arranged around a silence, the way a crowd arranges itself around a man carrying something heavy.

Then he saw her.

She came out of the lane between the chandler's and the tithe-house, the lane with no name that everyone used and no one acknowledged, and she came fast, faster than the square's rhythm allowed, a woman in a travelling cloak the color of wet slate, and she was holding her side, and she was leaving a pattern on the cobbles behind her that Sebastian recognized with a sick drop of his stomach, because he had seen men bleed out on the ash-fields below the Gate and the pattern was the same: too much, too fast, already past stopping.

No one else seemed to see her. Or rather — they saw, and looked away, the way the city looked away from everything it preferred not to know. A woman bleeding in the market was a Warden matter, and Warden matters were contagious, and the crowd flowed around her like water around a stone.

She came straight for his stall.

Later — in the long years after, in the telling and retelling that would follow — Sebastian would try to reconstruct those few seconds, and would always fail to make them add up. She should have gone to ground somewhere hidden. She should have sought a surgeon, a temple, a ditch to die in alone. Instead she crossed Copperwheel Square at a stumbling run, past forty indifferent witnesses and four humming lanterns, directly to the third stall from the fountain, to the man selling knots and lavender and lies, and her eyes found his across the distance with a certainty that made the hair rise on his arms, because he knew those eyes.

He knew them. He had stood beside them at a threshold between worlds, twelve years and one lifetime ago, and he had watched the owner of them walk away from him into the ruin of everything.

*Mara.*

The name rose in him like floodwater and he had no time to say it, because she reached the stall, and her hand shot out and gripped his wrist with a strength that had no business remaining in a dying body, and she pulled herself close, and up close he could see it wasn't Mara after all — younger, sharper, the same grey eyes in a different face, a face he didn't know and yet somehow recognized, the way you recognize a song transposed into another key —

— and she pressed something folded and stiff into his hands, and her mouth opened, and what came out of it was not a plea for help.

It was an oath. Old words. Old tongue. Words that had not been spoken aloud inside Vellamark's walls since the night the sky closed — binding-words, debt-words, words with teeth in them, words that took hold of whatever was under his skin and *squeezed*.

The hum beneath his ribs woke all the way up.

Around the square, on their four corners, the Warden lanterns flickered — all four at once, a stutter in the trapped grey light, the first irregularity in their shining in twelve years — and somewhere across the city, Sebastian knew with absolute certainty, heads were turning toward the soundless alarm.

The woman's grip tightened on his wrist. Her blood dripped through his fingers, warm as rain.

"Hear me," she whispered, in the old tongue, in the voice of a realm he had betrayed, "and be *bound*."
