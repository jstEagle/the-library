## 2. The Dying Stranger

The bells of Vellamark rang the ninth hour, and Sebastian Okafor began the small theater of closing his stall.

It was a performance he had refined over twelve years: the unhurried folding of cloth over trays of charms that were not charms, the careful stacking of wooden boxes that held nothing but air and cedar smell, the last lingering arrangement of the shelf so that tomorrow's customers would see abundance. He moved slowly on purpose. A man who packed up too quickly looked like a man with somewhere to be. A man with somewhere to be was a man the Wardens might wonder about.

He had nowhere to be. That was the whole architecture of his life.

"Closing, charm-seller?" called Old Maret from the pickle stall across the lane, her voice carrying the particular satisfaction of a woman who had never once hurried for anyone.

"Closing," he agreed, and smiled the smile he kept for neighbors—warm at the edges, empty in the middle.

The market of Vellamark emptied the way water leaves a tide pool: reluctantly, in rivulets, leaving things behind. A dropped glove. A bruised apple rolling into the gutter. The smell of fried onions and tallow candles and the mineral tang of the canal. Overhead the sky did what Vellamark skies always did—hung there, pale and featureless as unglazed pottery, twelve years without a single cloud worth the name. Twelve years without rain. The city had made its peace with dust; it swept it every morning and complained about it every evening, and no one remembered anymore that the sky had once been capable of weeping.

Sebastian remembered. Sebastian remembered everything, which was the cruelest part of the bargain he'd made with his own survival.

He was blowing out the stub of candle in his lantern when the woman came around the corner of the tanner's awning.

He saw her before he understood what he was seeing—a shape moving wrong against the evening crowd of stragglers, listing like a boat taking water. Then she stepped into the light of his stall's last lamp, and he saw the blood.

It was everywhere. Soaked through her traveling cloak, black in the lamplight, still wet enough to shine. Her left hand pressed against her side as though she were holding herself together by main strength, and perhaps she was. Her face was gray as ash and set with a ferocity that had nothing to do with dying and everything to do with arriving.

She looked directly at him. Not at the stall. At him.

"No," Sebastian said quietly, to himself, to the universe. "No, no—"

She reached the counter and her knees went. She caught herself on the wood hard enough to rock it, and the tray of harmless tin charms jumped and rattled like teeth. Around them, the last market stragglers paused, stared, and—this was Vellamark, this was what twelve years of Warden rule taught—looked away. A man pulled his child closer. A woman drew her shawl over her face. Nobody screamed. Screaming brought Wardens.

"Help me behind your counter," the woman said. Her voice was low and cracked but utterly level, the voice of someone who had decided how she would spend her final minutes and would not be argued with. "Now. Or I die in the street and you hang beside me."

Every instinct Sebastian had honed for twelve years said *walk away*. Every older instinct, the one buried under the exile and the false name and the smallness, said something else entirely. He came around the counter, took her weight across his shoulders—she weighed less than she should have, the weight of someone who had been bleeding a long time—and hauled her into the narrow space behind the stall, between the shelving and the canvas back wall, where the lamplight didn't reach.

He laid her down on the folded sacking he slept on some nights when the shop above let the cold in. Her blood soaked into it immediately, warm through the burlap, and he knelt there with his hands hovering uselessly because he knew wounds and he knew this one was past mending. Something had opened her side in a long cruel line. No blade he knew left edges like that.

"Wardens?" he asked. It was the only question that mattered.

"Two streets back. Losing them cost me." She laughed, or tried to; it came out as a wet rattle. "They'll come here. They always follow the blood."

"Then I should carry you out the back and we both—"

"*No.*" Her hand shot up and seized his wrist with a strength that startled him, that had no business being in a dying body. Her eyes were dark and fever-bright and they held his the way a hook holds a fish. "Listen to me. We don't have time for your fear. I know what you are, Sebastian Okafor."

The name landed in his chest like a stone dropped down a well. He hadn't heard it spoken aloud in twelve years. In Vellamark he was Seb, the charm-seller. In Vellamark he was nobody, carefully, expensively nobody.

"You're mistaken," he said. "My name is—"

"I don't have time for your lie either." She fumbled at her cloak with her free hand, and he helped her despite himself, drawing out a bundle wrapped in oilcloth. She unwrapped it with shaking fingers: a folded square of heavy paper, stained dark at one corner, and beneath it a small object that made his breath stop in his throat—a signet ring bearing the crest of the Gate-wardens, the old covenant order, the order that had not existed since the night everything broke. The ring was worn nearly smooth. It had been worn for decades.

"My mother's," she said, following his eyes. "And hers before her. We kept the eastern watch. My name is Ysolde Verren. Remember it, because my daughter won't know to."

"Ysolde—" The name snagged on something deep in his memory, an old grief wearing an old face. He pushed it away. There was no time, and grief was a luxury of people with futures.

She pressed the folded map into his hands and closed his fingers over it with both of hers, and her hands were ice now, the fire spending itself fast.

"The Hollow Gate is failing," she said. "Not broken—*failing*. There's a difference. Broken, it would have killed us all by now. Failing, it leaks. And what leaks through is patient, and hungry, and the Wardens' seals are feeding it instead of stopping it, do you understand? They think they're holding the door shut. They're nailing it open."

"I haven't been near the Gate in twelve years. I can't—"

"You can. You're the only one alive who has walked the whole road and lived." Her grip tightened. "I would have gone myself. I've spent twenty years going myself. But they found me tonight, and I'm done, and the oath needs a bearer who isn't bleeding out in a charm-seller's stall."

"I don't take oaths," Sebastian said. "I especially don't take oaths from strangers who are about to make me a corpse."

"Not a stranger." Something moved through her face then, briefly—the ghost of an expression he almost recognized, a softness at odds with the iron of her voice. "You knew me once, storm-caller. Long ago. You were kinder then. But there's no time, and maybe it's better you remember after I'm gone."

Before he could speak—before he could ask the question rising in him like floodwater—she began to speak words that were not Vellamark words.

They were old. They were the tongue of the covenant, the binding-speech that gate-wardens used, that storm-callers answered, and hearing it after twelve years of silence was like hearing his own heartbeat after thinking himself dead. The syllables fell into the air and *stayed* there. The lamp flame leaned toward her. The tin charms on the tray trembled, all together, though no hand touched them.

Under his skin, in the place where his storm-gift coiled, leashed and starved and pretending to be dead, something *answered*.

"No," Sebastian whispered. "Don't. Whatever you're doing, stop—"

"Hear me, keeper of the western road," Ysolde Verren said, and her voice doubled itself, one woman's voice and something vaster speaking underneath it, the way wind speaks through a canyon. "By the word that binds the Gate, by the blood that sealed it, by the storm that remembers: I lay this charge upon you. Carry the map. Find the threshold. Keep the oath until one comes who can speak it whole. Refuse, and the bond will find you wherever you run, whatever name you wear. This is not a request, Sebastian Okafor. This is a debt, and debts of the Gate are paid in full."

The last word rang like struck bronze. The lamp flared white. Every hair on Sebastian's arms stood up, and under his skin the caged storm turned over in its sleep and *recognized* the words, recognized the authority, recognized the ancient machinery of obligation that he had fled across half a world to escape.

An oath spoken in the binding-tongue could not be refused. That was the first law of the covenant, older than the Wardens, older than the ban. He had watched men try. He had watched what remained afterward.

"You bind me to a dead woman's errand," he said hoarsely, "and I don't even know what the errand *is*."

"You will. The map knows the way for those who walked it." Her grip on his wrist loosened at last—not releasing him, simply running out of strength. Blood pooled beneath her, soaking through the sacking, spreading toward the leg of the counter. "One more thing. One thing only, and then I'm finished."

"Speak."

"My daughter." For the first time her voice wavered, and the iron in it bent, and underneath was just a mother, dying badly, far from home. "Hazel. She's twenty-two. She lives on Tanner's Row, the blue door, third house past the well. She doesn't know. God forgive me, she doesn't know any of it—I kept it from her to keep her safe, and it bought her nothing but ignorance." Her breath hitched. "When the Wardens finish asking their questions, they'll go to her. She carries the other half. She carries the mark and doesn't know what it is. Find her before they do. Tell her—" A cough took her, terrible and long, and when it passed she was quieter, smaller. "Tell her I'm sorry. Tell her the mark is not a curse. Tell her it's a *door*."

Then her eyes went past him, to the middle distance, to something he couldn't see, and her hand went slack, and Ysolde Verren died in a charm-seller's stall in the magic-free city of Vellamark with the smell of tin and cedar and blood, and the lamp flame stood perfectly straight, and outside, the dust hung in the windless air.

Sebastian knelt there for a long moment, the folded map heavy in his fist, listening to the silence where her breathing had been.

*You knew me once, storm-caller.*

He unfolded the map a hand's width. Lamplight caught ink lines, a route drawn north through country he knew too well, and at its end a symbol he had hoped never to see again: two interlocking circles, one open, one closed. The mark of the Hollow Gate.

He folded it shut as if it had burned him.

* * *

He worked quickly after that, because panic makes either fools or efficient men, and Sebastian had spent twelve years learning to be efficient.

He wrapped Ysolde's body in the sacking and the spare canvas from the stall's frame. He banked the lamp low. Through the slats of the counter he watched the lane, and it was while watching that he saw them: three figures in slate-gray coats moving up the far side of the market with the unhurried menace of men who owned the ground they walked on. Warden patrol. And not a routine one—they moved in the pattern he'd seen only twice before in twelve years, spread wide, checking doorways, one of them crouching at intervals along the gutter.

Following blood.

Sebastian's heart hammered against his ribs. Under his skin the storm-gift stirred, offering itself—*let me, I can call fog, I can call wind, I can blind them*—and he crushed it down the way he had every day for twelve years, because using it was death, because one flash of true power in Vellamark would bring the entire Warden garrison down on this quarter inside a bell's turn.

Instead he did mundane things. He doused the lamp completely. He dragged Ysolde's body deeper into shadow behind the shelving—he could not move her far, not tonight, not with patrols in the lanes—and threw a bolt of sale-cloth over the stain of blood, weighting the corners with charm-boxes. His hands shook and he let them shake, because fighting the shaking cost more than the shaking did.

The knock came eleven minutes later. Three blows, spaced evenly, the knock of men who expected doors to open.

Sebastian opened it a hand's width, blinking, arranging his face into the mild confusion of a tired tradesman. "Closed. Anything wrong?"

A Warden officer stood in the lane, young, sharp-faced, rainless-dust on his boots. Behind him, the other two had drifted toward the mouth of the alley behind the stall, and Sebastian's stomach dropped.

"Market closes at ninth bell," the officer said. "You're late putting out your shutter."

"Lady died at my counter not ten minutes past. Took a moment to collect myself." He said it flatly, bored, and watched the young man's eyes flicker—because it was true, and truth was the best armor he owned.

"Died? Of what?"

"Of being cut open, from the look of her. I gave her water. She wouldn't take it. Bled out on my floor like a slaughtered pig, and now if you'll forgive me I'd like to be sick in private." He let his voice roughen. Let his hands show their trembling. Fear read as guilt to stupid men, but exhaustion read as innocence, and he had learned the difference.

The officer studied him for a long moment. "Name?"

"Seb. Charm-seller, fifteen years on this pitch. Ask Old Maret, ask anyone."

"We'll ask everyone." The officer's gaze slid past him into the dimness of the stall, lingered on the stacked boxes, the covered counter. "We're looking for a woman. Gray cloak. Wounded."

"Then you're behind her. She came from the east, moving fast, heading for the canal stairs. If she's still upright, you might catch her at the toll bridge." He pointed, steady, certain, and hated himself for how easily the lie came, and loved the skill of it anyway, because it was keeping him alive.

The officer hesitated. Somewhere behind the stall, under the canvas, in the dark, a dead woman lay wrapped in sale-cloth, and a folded map pressed against Sebastian's ribs where he'd tucked it inside his shirt, and it felt warm, unnaturally warm, like a living thing against his skin.

"If she comes back," the officer said, "you'll send for us."

"If she comes back, I'll send for a priest. She's past your help."

They moved off toward the canal, boots scraping the dusty cobbles. Sebastian shut the door, put his back against it, and slid down until he was sitting on the floor of his own stall among the harmless charms, breathing like a man who had surfaced from deep water.

Through the wall, faintly, he heard the officer's voice giving orders in the next lane. Not dispersing. Reorganizing.

*They'll go to her,* Ysolde had said. *Tanner's Row, the blue door. Find her before they do.*

Sebastian sat in the dark with a dead woman's blood drying somewhere behind him and a warm map against his chest and an oath coiled around his spine like a second skeleton, and he thought about the blue door on Tanner's Row, and about a girl of twenty-two who carried a mark she didn't understand, and about how in the morning—or sooner, because Wardens did not sleep on unanswered questions—there would be a knock on that blue door too.

He could stay here. He could fold the map into the coals and burn it, and let the oath do what oaths did to refusers, and spend whatever was left of his small quiet life paying that price instead. He had survived twelve years by wanting nothing, owing nothing, touching nothing that mattered.

Ysolde's voice came back to him in the dark: *This is not a request.*

Outside, high over the rooflines of Vellamark, the empty sky went on being empty. But far to the north, past the salt flats and the ash passes, something old was leaking through a broken door, patient and hungry, and it had waited twelve years already, and it knew how to wait.

Sebastian got up, took down his travel cloak from its peg, and blew out the last candle.

First, the blue door. Before the Wardens knocked on it.
