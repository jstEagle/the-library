## 4. What the Map Shows

The map did not sleep.

Sebastian had laid it out on his worktable at midnight, weighted at the corners with four brass charms he sold to tourists as *tokens of safe travel*—worthless tin and glass, every one—and it had lain there under the single lamp like a thing breathing. Not moving. Maps didn't move. But the paper had a quality of attention, the way a sleeping animal has attention even with its eyes shut, and by the third hour of studying it Sebastian understood that this was no cartographer's work. It was a covenant document wearing the clothes of a map.

He had not touched it since the woman died in his stall.

That had been two days ago. Two days of opening the shop on schedule, selling his little lies—*a charm for restful sleep, madam, a charm for honest children, sir*—and smiling at customers while the folded paper sat in the drawer beneath his till like a coal that hadn't finished burning. Two nights of lying awake in the room above the shop listening for boots on the stairs. The Wardens had come once already, a pair of them, gray-cloaked and polite, asking whether he'd seen a woman pass through the market on the evening of the ninth. He had said no. He had smiled his smallness at them, the smile he'd practiced for twelve years, the one that made him look like exactly what he appeared to be: a tired man who sold trinkets because it was easier than real work.

They had believed him. They always believed him. That was the whole architecture of his life in Vellamark—built on the city's conviction that nothing worth noticing could live behind such an unremarkable face.

But the map was not unremarkable, and neither, apparently, had the dying woman been.

Now, past midnight, with the lamp turned low and the shutters drawn, he finally unfolded it all the way.

It opened wider than its folds should have allowed. The paper was old—not fragile-old but *kept*-old, oiled against damp, rubbed soft at the creases from years of being taken out and studied and folded away again. Someone had carried this for a long time. Someone had needed it often.

And Sebastian knew the hand that had drawn it.

He knew it the way you know your own signature in a stranger's ledger—with a lurch, with denial, with the sick certainty that follows. The linework was fine and exact, the coastlines rendered in short confident strokes, the mountains hatched in the old style, the style they taught at the Gate before anyone thought to teach anything else. And there, in the lower margin, small enough to be missed: a storm-sign. Not decoration. A warding mark, the kind gate-wardens used to seal a document against unfriendly reading.

His own teacher's mark. Master Ellery Voss's mark, or someone trained in the same line—the same double-stroke lightning bent into a circle, the sign of the threshold-keepers.

Sebastian sat down slowly on his stool, because his legs had opinions about standing.

*Only three people alive ever drew that sign,* he thought, and then corrected himself, because grief and time had thinned the number. *Two. Perhaps one.*

He traced the route without meaning to. His finger moved along the inked road north out of Vellamark, through the chalk hills where the road forked at a place marked only with a small square—the waystation at Greyford, though the map didn't name it; the map named almost nothing, as if names were dangerous. Past the salt marshes. Along a coastline that hooked east like a question mark. Then inland, up into heights that the map rendered not in mountains but in a long descending spiral of marks, each one smaller than the last, tightening toward a center point that was drawn not as a place but as a *door*—two upright strokes and a lintel, the oldest symbol in either realm's script.

The Hollow Gate.

Twelve years since he had walked that road. Twelve years since he had stood in that doorway and watched everything he was burn down to ash and exile. He had buried the route so thoroughly inside himself that some mornings he could almost believe he'd never known another life than this one—never stood in wind that spoke, never called thunder down out of a clear sky to save a drowning boat, never been *Sebastian Okafor, storm-caller of the eastern threshold*. In Vellamark he was Seb the charm-seller, and the hum under his skin was just nerves, just a heart beating too loud, just nothing.

The map refused to let it be nothing.

Because here was the thing—the thing that made his hands go cold on the table's edge. The route on the map was correct. Not approximately correct, not the way a scholar might reconstruct it from old records. It was *exact*, down to details that existed nowhere in any archive, because they existed nowhere except in the memory of people who had walked it. There was the spring that ran bitter above the salt marshes—you had to fill water below it, not above, or you'd spend a day retching. There was the stretch of coast where the path dipped below the high-tide line and you crossed at slack water or not at all. There was the cairn field in the ash country where the stones were stacked by pilgrims and you added one or you passed badly.

No one alive knew those things. No one alive *could* know them, because the Wardens had burned the records and scattered the keepers, and the road itself had been warded shut—

Except the map showed the wards too. Little knots of ink at each crossing-point, each one annotated in a cramped, hurried hand with a word or two in the old tongue. *Broken.* *Broken.* *Faded, will hold against the ignorant.* *Broken by fire, 11th year.*

Eleventh year. One year after his exile. Someone had been walking this road, year after year, testing the seals, noting their decay, keeping the way alive in secret the way a hearth-keeper keeps embers under ash.

Someone had been preparing for the Gate to be needed again.

Sebastian pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes until he saw constellations. The lamp hissed. Somewhere out in the sleeping city a dog barked twice and gave up, and the silence afterward was enormous.

He thought about the woman. The stranger who had bled out across his counter with her fingers locked around his wrist, who had spoken words in the old tongue that he had felt sink into his bones like cold iron—the oath, the binding, the debt he had not chosen and could not refuse. She had known what she was doing. Dying, she had still known: she had picked the one man in Vellamark who could read this map, walk this road, stand at that door. She had spent her last strength buying his obedience because she couldn't buy his willingness.

*You owe me the road,* she had said. Blood on her teeth. *Take it to the Gate. Take my half with you.*

He hadn't understood then. He understood now, looking at the second object she'd left him: the smaller fold of paper tucked inside the map's outermost crease, which he had avoided opening for two days because he had known, somehow, that it would make everything worse.

He opened it.

Inside was a single sheet, covered edge to edge in the old tongue, in the same hand as the map. Half a text. It began mid-sentence—*and the speaker shall bind the ways with a word of both—* and ended mid-oath, the final lines simply absent, torn away. But Sebastian had spoken the covenant once, long ago, standing shoulder to shoulder with his partner at the threshold while the storm-realm leaned close to listen. He recognized the cadence. This was the oath itself. Half of it.

Half an oath. A beginning without an ending. A door with one hinge.

And beneath the text, in the common script this time, in letters gone shaky—as if written by someone who already knew she was dying—a single line:

*My daughter carries the other half. She doesn't know what it is. Don't tell her until you must.*

Sebastian read it four times. On the fourth reading, the last clause finally detonated.

*Don't tell her until you must.*

Which meant the woman had known Hazel would come. Had counted on it. Had bound a stranger to a road and a dead woman's errand and left her daughter to follow the debt like a bloodhound—and left Sebastian holding the leash and the lie at the same time.

"You clever, cruel woman," he said aloud, to the empty shop, and his voice cracked in the middle of it.

* * *

She came at midmorning, when the market was at its loudest and the light through the shop's front window lay in warm bars across the floor. He heard her before he saw her—the particular stride of someone raised walking walls and rooftops, quick and flat-footed—and when he looked up from the tray of charms he was pretending to sort, she was already in the doorway, filling it.

Hazel. Though he hadn't known her name yet; he would learn it within the hour, spoken like a thrown stone. What he saw first was the resemblance, and it went through him like a hook through the gill. The same dark eyes, but hers unclouded, furious, alive. The same jaw set at the same stubborn angle. Younger than he'd expected—twenty, twenty-one—and dressed in practical traveling clothes that had seen recent miles, with a satchel slung across her body and her weight forward on her feet like a held breath.

She looked around the shop—at the hanging charms, the jars of dyed salt, the shelves of harmless glittering nothing—and her lip curled with something between contempt and despair.

"You're the charm-seller." Not a question. "The one she came to."

"I'm a charm-seller," Sebastian said carefully. "I don't know who—"

"She came here the night she died." Hazel crossed the floor in five strides and put both hands flat on his counter, leaning in, and up close he could see she hadn't slept, that grief had hollowed her cheeks and ringed her eyes and that she was running on something harder than rest. "The Wardens won't tell me anything. They took her body and gave me back a receipt. A *receipt*, like she was goods lost in transit. So I've spent two days asking questions the Wardens don't like, and every answer ends up here. At this stall. With you."

"Many people come to this stall."

"Don't." Her voice dropped, low and shaking. "Don't do the small-man voice. I've watched you all morning from across the square. You sell sleep-charms to insomniacs and luck-charms to gamblers and every single customer walks away a little poorer and a little happier, and you smile like none of it costs you anything. My mother walked into this shop bleeding and walked out dead, and whatever she came here for—" her hands curled against the counter wood, "—it's still here. I can feel it."

Sebastian held her gaze and did the arithmetic he'd been dreading since midnight. She didn't know. The mother's note was explicit: *she doesn't know what it is.* Which meant the oath-mark—if she carried it, and the note implied she did—was just a birthmark to her, a curiosity, a family oddity. Which meant he could still, theoretically, end this. Give her nothing. Let the map stay folded. Let the debt rot in a drawer until it faded, if debts like that faded, which he suspected they didn't.

Which meant he could spend the rest of his life wondering what had starved on the far side of a door he'd refused to open.

"Hazel," he said, and she flinched at her own name, and he realized the mother must have written it somewhere he'd find it, or perhaps he'd simply guessed right, mothers' daughters being legible to men who had loved—no. He shut that door firmly. "Your mother. What was her name?"

"Maren." Said the way you say a prayer you're angry at. "Maren Vale."

*Maren.* The name landed in him like a stone dropped down a well, falling and falling without reaching bottom. He kept his face still. Twelve years of practice at keeping his face still.

"And what did she do," Sebastian asked, "that put blood on her?"

Hazel's composure flickered—for a moment he saw the girl underneath, the one who had lost a parent and been handed paperwork instead of answers. "She traveled," Hazel said. "Every year, same time, always alone, always north. She told me it was trade. Salt, cloth, whatever. I found her ledgers after—" Her breath caught. "There's no trade in her ledgers. There are notes. Wards, decay-rates, distances. Notes on places that aren't supposed to exist anymore."

The cairn field. The bitter spring. Yearly, alone, north. Keeping the embers.

Sebastian looked at this furious, grieving young woman who was her mother's daughter in every visible way, and he made the mistake of glancing—just a flicker—at the drawer beneath the till.

She caught it. Of course she caught it. She'd been raised by Maren Vale, and Maren Vale had missed nothing in the years Sebastian had known her, and the apple, evidently, had not rolled far.

"What's in the drawer?"

"Nothing that concerns you."

"It's the only thing in this entire city that concerns me." She came around the counter before he could move, and her hand closed on the drawer pull, and he could have stopped her—he was stronger, faster, trained in ways no charm-seller should be—but stopping her meant touching her, meant revealing speed and certainty no charm-seller possessed, and so he froze, and she pulled, and the drawer slid open onto the folded map and the folded oath and two days of his cowardice.

She stared at them. Her hand hovered over the map without touching, the way you hover over a body.

"That's her handwriting," Hazel whispered.

"Hazel—"

"She drew this." All the fury had gone out of her voice, replaced by something worse. Wonder, and under the wonder, terror. "This is her hand. I'd know it anywhere. She—" Hazel lifted the map with both hands, reverent, and began to unfold it, and Sebastian watched the route reveal itself to her inch by inch, the road north, the spiral, the door, and he watched her arrive at the center symbol and go very pale and very still.

"What is it?" she asked. "What's at the end of this?"

*Don't tell her until you must.*

"The truth is," Sebastian said slowly, "I don't know yet. I need more time with it. Time I can't have with the Wardens circling this district and you standing in my shop asking questions they'll want answered." He gentled his voice into the shape of kindness, which was also the shape of dismissal. "Go home, Hazel. Grieve your mother. Let me study this, and if there's something you need to know, I'll send for you. I swear it."

It was a good performance. It was, he thought bitterly, maybe the best performance of his twelve-year career, because most of it was even true.

Hazel folded the map along its worn creases, precisely, the way her mother must have done a thousand times, and set it on the counter between them. Then she looked up at him, and everything raw in her face had been packed down into something dense and immovable, bedrock under topsoil.

"My mother bled out on a stranger's counter rather than go home to me," she said. "Do you understand? She was dying, and she chose not to come home. Which means whatever she was doing mattered more than goodbye. More than me." Her voice didn't break, and he could see what it cost her that it didn't. "So no. I'm not going home to wait for a letter. Whatever road she drew, whatever's at the end of it—I'm walking it. If you have any decency in that small-man act of yours, you'll tell me what you know. And if you don't—" she shouldered her satchel, chin up, eyes bright and terrible, "—then I'll follow you, because I've been watching you all morning, charm-seller, and nobody smiles like that unless they're hiding something enormous."

The shop was very quiet. Out in the market, a vendor called the price of early apples, and the ordinary world went on being ordinary, indifferent to thresholds and oaths and the specific catastrophe of being known.

Sebastian looked at the map. At the girl. At the ghost standing between them, drawn in fine confident lines, walking a road she'd kept alive for eleven years so that someone—this someone—could finish what she couldn't.

*Only I have ever walked it,* he thought. *And now I never stop walking it.*

"There's a place," he heard himself say, "three days north. Where the road forks at Greyford. If we're going to survive the first week, you'll need to learn six warding signs before we reach it, and you'll learn them tonight, and you'll complain about every one."

Hazel blinked. "Is that a yes?"

"That's a *don't tell her until you must*," Sebastian said grimly, folding the map and putting it inside his coat, against his chest, where the hum under his skin rose to meet it like a tide answering the moon. "And I suspect I'm going to hate how soon *must* arrives."

He blew out the lamp, though it was morning, and turned the sign on his door to *closed* for the first time in twelve years.

Through the window, at the far end of the square, a gray cloak paused, noted the closed sign, and moved on—but slowly. Taking note.

Sebastian reached for his travel pack, and began, silently, to count the hours they had left.
