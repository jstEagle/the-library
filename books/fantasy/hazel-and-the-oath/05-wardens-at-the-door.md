## 5. Wardens at the Door

I had been in Sebastian's shop for three days when the Wardens came.

Three days of dust and lamplight, of sitting on a stool behind his counter while he pretended to inventory charms that were only glass and wire and painted wood. Three days of my mother's folded map lying between us like a body neither of us wanted to touch. He had not yet agreed to take me north. He had not yet agreed to anything. He only kept looking at me the way a man looks at a door he has nailed shut and can still hear knocking.

"You should eat," he said that morning, sliding a heel of bread across the counter without meeting my eyes.

"I'm not hungry."

"You're grieving. Grief eats you whether you feed it or not." He said it flatly, like a man quoting a rule of trade. "Better to be the one holding the bread."

I took it mostly to make him stop talking. The shop was small and close, smelling of beeswax and cold iron and something faintly metallic underneath—ozone, I would learn to call it later, though that morning I thought it was only the lamp. Shelves climbed every wall, crowded with little hanging things: knots of colored thread, river stones wrapped in copper, wooden birds with their wings burned smooth. Charms for the charmless, he called them. Nothing here did anything. That was the point. In Vellamark, magic was a crime, and so the whole city had learned to buy the shape of it instead—the gesture, the token, the pretty lie—and call itself safe.

Outside the window, the market was waking. Fish carts. A woman arguing about the price of onions. Two Warden constables in grey walking their morning circuit with the unhurried confidence of men who had never once been told no.

Sebastian watched them through the gap in the shutters, and I watched him watch them, and I understood something then that I couldn't have put into words: this man was afraid all the time. Not the sharp fear of a moment—something older, worn smooth by years, the way a stone in a stream goes round from constant turning. He had built an entire life out of being unremarkable. And I had walked into it carrying a dead woman's oath, and I could feel his careful smallness cracking under the weight of me.

"The map," I said. "You've looked at it again."

"I've looked at it."

"And?"

"And nothing." He turned back to his shelf of useless birds. "It's ink on skin-parchment. Routes and marks. It proves nothing except that your mother liked drawing maps."

"Liar," I said quietly.

His hands went still on the shelf.

"My mother didn't draw maps," I said. "She drew floor plans when we needed a new table, and she drew them badly. She once labeled our own kitchen *north*. Whatever she made that map for, she made it because someone needed to find a place they'd never been, and she needed them to find it exactly." I stood. My legs had gone stiff from three days of stools. "You know where it leads. You've known since the night she died in your doorway. So say it, or give it back, and I'll find someone who'll say it."

For a long moment he said nothing at all. Then, very softly, almost to himself: "You sound like her."

"Good," I said. "Someone should."

He opened his mouth to answer—

And that was when the knock came. Not a customer's knock. Three blows, spaced evenly, struck with the flat of a gauntlet. Iron on oak. The sound went through the shop like a crack through ice.

Sebastian's face changed completely. Not into fear—into blankness, which is worse. Every line of feeling left it at once, the way water leaves sand. "Back room," he breathed. "Now. Take the map. Don't run. Walk."

The knock came again.

I took the map from its hiding place beneath the counter—wrapped now in oilcloth, tied with cord—and walked, heart hammering, through the curtain into the back room where he slept on a narrow cot among crates of unsold stock. There was a window high on the wall, too narrow for a person, opening onto a lightless slot between buildings. I heard the front door open. I heard boots.

"Warden inspection," said a voice out front, official and bored. "License and registry, charm-seller."

"Of course." Sebastian's voice had become something else entirely—mild, slow, faintly apologetic, the voice of a man who had never done anything interesting in his life. It was a performance so complete it frightened me. "Everything's in order. It's always in order. You gentlemen keep us all honest."

"Mm." Boots crossed the floorboards. "We've had a report."

"A report? On my shop?" A small laugh, self-deprecating. "The most exciting thing here is moths."

"A woman died outside this establishment four nights ago. Bled out in the street. Witnesses say she came from your direction. Say she was carrying something."

My hands tightened on the oilcloth until the cord bit into my palm.

"Vellamark has a great many dying people," Sebastian said. "It's why I sell health charms. Glass ones. They work as well as anything."

A pause. Then a different voice—younger, sharper: "Open the counter drawer."

"By all means."

I heard the scrape of the drawer. I heard the younger Warden moving along the shelves, touching things, and I realized with a lurch that I could hear him *thinking*—not his thoughts, but his method, the way he worked down a row of objects like a man searching a field for buried metal. He was looking for something real. Something that hummed.

And there was one thing in this shop that hummed. I had felt it the first night, sleeping in the back room—a low, patient vibration in the air, like a held breath. I had assumed it was the building settling. Now I understood it came off Sebastian himself, off his skin, off whatever he carried inside him that he spent every day pretending wasn't there. If the young Warden got close enough to the curtain—

"Mind the merchandise," Sebastian said pleasantly, and I heard him move—deliberately, loudly, dragging his feet, jingling the door chimes, positioning himself somewhere between the Warden and the back room. Making noise. Being large. Being *in the way*.

"What's through that curtain?"

"Storage. Crates. Rat droppings, if we're honest."

"We'll be the judge of that."

Boots. Two steps toward the curtain. My whole body went cold and stupid; I pressed myself against the cot and thought, absurdly, of my mother telling me to stand up straight. The map seemed to burn through the oilcloth. Somewhere beyond the walls, the city went on selling fish and arguing about onions, indifferent, safe, asleep.

Then the front door banged open hard enough to rattle the shelves.

"Constable!" A new voice, breathless, aggrieved, carrying the particular authority of a man who is late and wants everyone to suffer for it. "There's a situation at the tannery gate. Unregistered goods, a whole cartload, and the dock-master swears there's storm-glass in the shipment. *Storm-glass.* You want to stand here counting wooden birds while that sits unclaimed?"

Silence. Then the bored voice, reluctantly: "Storm-glass?"

"Or so he says. Could be nothing. Could be a promotion."

More silence. Then boots retreating, the younger one protesting—"We haven't finished the search"—and the older one answering, "Finish it tomorrow. Registry says Okafor's been clean twelve years; he'll keep till morning." The door. The street. Gone.

I stayed pressed against the cot, shaking, until the curtain moved and Sebastian came through it, and behind him came a stranger.

* * *

The stranger was perhaps fifty, broad as a door, with a smuggler's weathered face and a grin that arrived a half-second before the rest of his expressions, as if even his face liked to get ahead of itself. He wore a dock-worker's coat over better clothes than a dock worker should own, and he smelled of tar and salt and, faintly, of some sweet spirit drunk in a hurry.

"Well," he said, looking around the cramped back room with delight. "Twelve years, and this is where you live. Seb, I've seen nicer tombs."

"It has good light," Sebastian said dryly.

"It has a slot." The stranger peered up at the window. "That's not a window, that's an apology." Then his eyes found me, and the grin settled into something more careful. "And this is her. The daughter."

"You know who I am?" My voice came out rougher than I intended.

"He knows everything," Sebastian said. "It's his one skill. Corvin Asch, Hazel. Corvin, this is Maren's daughter, and before you say another charming thing—yes, it's true, yes, she has the map, and no, you may not see it."

Corvin raised both hands. "Wouldn't dream of it." A beat. "Might steal it, but wouldn't dream of looking at it."

"Corvin." Sebastian's voice had an edge I hadn't heard in it before. "How did you know they were coming?"

"I didn't. I knew they *might*." Corvin dropped onto a crate, making it groan. "Word came down yesterday from the garrison house. Questions about a death in Lantern Row, questions attached to your address. I've got a cousin who sweeps floors there—don't ask what for, he doesn't know either—and when your name came out of a Warden's mouth after twelve years of silence, I figured either you'd finally done something worth hanging for, or somebody was finally doing something to you. Either way, it seemed like a bad morning to sleep in." He spread his hands. "So I invented a cart of storm-glass. Which, incidentally, I now have to go invent for real, or the dock-master will have questions of his own."

"Why?" I asked.

Both men looked at me.

"Why help us? Why risk it?" I held his gaze, because my mother had taught me that questions asked while looking away sound like apologies. "You don't know me. You owe me nothing."

Corvin studied me for a moment, and the grin faded entirely, and what was underneath was older and wearier and, I thought, kinder. "Your mother," he said, "once sat with me for two days in a fever-house on the salt coast when every other soul in that town would've let a smuggler rot. Didn't ask what I carried. Didn't ask who I'd stolen it from. Just sat there and kept me alive and called me *brother* when the fever made me forget my own name." He shrugged, as if that explained everything, and maybe it did. "Some debts don't come due for twenty years. When they do, you pay them glad."

He stood, joints cracking. "Here's what I know. The Wardens will be back—tomorrow, maybe tonight, with a writ this time, and a writ means they turn the place inside out and take everything including you, charm-seller, because a man who lies that smoothly is a man hiding something, and they're right, aren't they." He said it without accusation, simply as fact, and I watched Sebastian absorb it without flinching. "So you have hours, not days. And you cannot leave through any gate, because your name went into the watch-book this morning and it will not come out."

"There are ways out of Vellamark that aren't gates," Sebastian said slowly.

"There are ways out of Vellamark that aren't gates," Corvin agreed, grinning again. "And I know all of them, because I dug half of them."

* * *

They left me packing while they argued logistics, which meant they left me alone with the map, which I think was deliberate on Sebastian's part and accidental on Corvin's, or possibly the reverse. I unwrapped it just enough to look at the top edge, where my mother's handwriting marched in her cramped, slanting script—the same hand that had mislabeled our kitchen *north*, now tracing a route across country I'd never seen, toward a mark at the far margin shaped like a broken circle.

*Hollow Gate,* the label read. Beneath it, smaller, almost hidden in the fold: *If I do not come home, he must.*

*He.* I had spent four nights assuming *he* meant Sebastian, because who else could it mean? But sitting there in the lamplight with the paper trembling in my fingers, I let myself consider, for the first time, that I had no idea. That my mother had entire rooms of her life I had never entered. That the woman who checked my window latch every night and hated thunder and made terrible maps had also bled out in a stranger's doorway pressing an oath onto a man who flinched at lightning.

The curtain moved. Sebastian stood there with a satchel already packed, and something in his face had shifted—not softer, exactly. More decided. Like a man who has stopped arguing with a current.

"We go tonight," he said. "Through the undercity. Corvin's route. It comes up outside the walls, past the leper cisterns—it's ugly, and it's wet, and you'll hate it."

"I won't hate it."

"You will. Everyone hates it. That's how it stays secret." He held out a second satchel. "Food, flint, a knife. Do you know how to use a knife?"

"No."

"Then it's for cutting rope and cheese, and if anyone comes near you, you run and let me handle it. Understood?"

"Is that what you did?" I asked. "Twelve years ago. Ran and let someone else handle it?"

The question landed harder than I'd aimed. He went very still, and for a moment I saw the fear come back into his face—but changed, deepened, turned inward. "No," he said quietly. "Twelve years ago I handled it, and it cost everything, and the people who survived cost me more than the ones who didn't." He shouldered his pack. "Ask me again on the road. I might answer by then. Tonight we walk."

* * *

The undercity began behind a false wall in a chandler's cellar, and it was every ugly wet thing he had promised.

We went down by lantern-light into tunnels that had been sewers once, and cellars before that, and something older before *that*—the walls in the deepest stretches were fitted with stones no mason in Vellamark could cut anymore, marked with signs worn nearly to nothing. Sebastian paused at one, ran his thumb across it, and said nothing, but his hand lingered, and I filed that away with all the other small evidences accumulating against the story of who he claimed to be.

Corvin led. He moved through the dark like a man walking his own hallway, calling hazards before we reached them—"step left, rotten plank, don't touch the green stuff, it's not moss and it bites"—and filling the silences with talk, endless talk, about docks and cousins and the price of illegal brandy, and I gradually understood that the talk was a kindness. The dark down there pressed close enough to taste. His voice was a rope we held onto.

Somewhere in the third hour, wading a channel ankle-deep in cold runoff, I heard it.

Boots. Above us—no, ahead of us, echoing down through some connecting shaft, several pairs, moving with purpose. Corvin froze mid-sentence. Sebastian's hand found my shoulder and pulled me flat against the tunnel wall, into a recess behind a fallen beam, and the lantern was hooded, and the dark came down on us like a lid.

The boots grew louder. Light flickered in a shaft ahead—Warden lanterns, white and harsh, none of the warm amber of ours. Voices, distorted by stone: "...says they went to ground somewhere in the old channels... sweep the north junction first..."

"They can't know," Corvin breathed, so close to my ear it was barely sound. "Nobody knows this stretch. Nobody living."

But the boots kept coming, and they were not wandering. They were *methodical*. Junction by junction, shaft by shaft, closing.

In the blackness beside me, Sebastian made a sound I had never heard from him—a short exhalation, almost a word, in a language I didn't know. And the air changed. I felt it against my skin like the moment before summer lightning: pressure, charge, the fine hairs on my arms lifting. The ozone smell from the shop, ten times stronger, wilder.

"Don't," Corvin whispered sharply. "Seb. *Don't.* They carry detection-irons. One spark of yours and they'll have us pinned to the wall."

The pressure faltered. Held. Faded, grudgingly, like a tide refused. And in the dark I heard Sebastian breathing carefully, the way a man breathes when he is holding something enormous shut behind a door, and I thought: *that's what the humming is. That's what he is. And he can't even use it to save us.*

The boots reached our junction. Stopped. Lantern light slid along the tunnel mouth, six paces away, throwing our shadows backward into the recess where we crouched. A Warden's voice, close enough to hear the wet click of his throat as he swallowed: "Smell that? Ozone. Fresh."

My heart stopped. Beside me, I felt Sebastian begin to move—to rise, to step out, to give himself up for us, I understood that with total clarity—and I did the only thing I could think of. I grabbed his wrist. And the map, wrapped against my chest, was between us, and through the oilcloth I felt something pass from my hand into his—or his into mine, I couldn't have said which—a pulse, a recognition, like two instruments finding the same note.

The Warden sniffed again. "Rat-musk and bilge," he muttered. "This whole sewer stinks of worse than ozone. Come on—north junction."

The light withdrew. The boots receded. For a long time none of us moved, and the only sound was three people breathing in the dark, and the distant conversation of water.

When Corvin finally hooded the lantern open again, he looked at Sebastian, then at me, then at the space between us where my hand still gripped the wrist that had almost betrayed us. His grin came back, but it was different now. Smaller. More careful.

"Right," he said softly. "North junction it is. And after that, friends, we're out of my tunnels and into my boat, and I'm going to need the pair of you to tell me exactly what it is I'm smuggling—because I've run contraband of every kind out of this city, and I have never once been paid in whatever *that* was."

Sebastian rose, wrung the sewer water from his coat hem, and looked at me with an expression I was beginning to recognize: the look of a man whose nailed-shut door was splintering.

"Get us to the salt road," he said, "and I'll tell you everything."

Above us, faint through forty feet of stone, thunder rolled—for the first time in twelve years, thunder over Vellamark—and all three of us looked up at the dark as if it had spoken.
