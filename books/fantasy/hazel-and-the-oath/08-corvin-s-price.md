## 8. Corvin's Price

The inn at Millbrook had a name once, painted on a hanging sign that had long since surrendered to the weather. Now it was just *the inn*, the way the village was just *the village*, and the road north was just *the road*—as if naming things too precisely might invite attention, and attention, in these borderlands, was a kind of debt.

Sebastian sat with his back to the wall, as he always did, watching the door through the steam rising off his cup. The tea was terrible—oversteeped, bitter with some local herb meant to stretch the leaves—but it was hot, and heat was a small luxury he had learned not to refuse. Twelve years of exile taught you to hoard small luxuries the way misers hoarded coin.

Hazel sat across from him, hood down for once, her mother's map spread between them under the pretense of studying it. She'd gotten better at pretending. Three days on the road had burned the market-girl softness out of her posture; she sat now like someone who expected the door to open badly at any moment.

It was a reasonable expectation. It opened anyway.

Corvin came in the way he always came into rooms: like smoke finding a draft. He was a narrow man, grey-stubbled, with hands that looked too delicate for his trade until you noticed the burn scars laced across the knuckles—rope burns, actually, from thirty years of hauling cargo that preferred not to be hauled. He wore the smile of a man who had never once told the whole truth and had grown fond of fractions.

"Storm-caller." He dropped into the chair beside Hazel without asking, which was Corvin's way of announcing that whatever he was about to say would be worse than the discourtesy.

"I'm a charm-seller," Sebastian said quietly.

"You're a charm-seller the way I'm a spice merchant." Corvin signaled the serving girl with two fingers. "Ale. The one you water least." He waited until she'd gone, until the room's noise reasserted itself—a pair of drovers arguing over freight rates, a fiddler tuning in the corner with more optimism than skill—before leaning forward, elbows on the table, voice dropping to the register he used for prices and confessions.

"We have a problem."

"We've had several," Sebastian said. "You'll need to be specific."

"The Wardens found my boat."

The words landed on the table between them like a dead bird. Hazel's hand went still on the map. Outside, the wind moved through the barley fields with a sound like slow breathing, and somewhere a dog began barking at nothing, or at something.

"When?" Sebastian asked.

"Two nights ago. While we were sleeping in the hollow past the ford." Corvin accepted his ale from the girl, drank half of it in one pull, and set it down with exaggerated care, the way men do when they're buying time to think. "They didn't take anything. Didn't torch it. Left a note pinned to the mast with a knife."

"What kind of note?"

"The kind written in gold." Corvin turned his ale mug slowly, watching the ring of moisture it left. "A contract. My name, my mark, their seal. Payment for services rendered and services to come."

"And what services," Hazel said, very evenly, "are those?"

Corvin didn't look at her. That was answer enough, but he gave the rest of it anyway, because Corvin had always been honest in the way of men who believed honesty cost less than being caught lying. "Tracking. They want to know where you sleep, which roads you take, how many you are. And when you reach wherever it is you're going—" his eyes flicked to the map, deliberately, letting them both see that he knew exactly what the map was, "—they want me to send word ahead."

The fiddler in the corner found his key and launched into something jaunty. It felt obscene. Sebastian became aware of his own heartbeat, steady and slow, and beneath it the other thing—the storm-gift coiled in his chest like a second heart, humming its low note of pressure and light. He had spent twelve years learning to keep that hum beneath hearing. Right now it wanted to rise. Right now it would have been so easy to let a little static crawl across the table, to let the lamps gutter, to watch Corvin's smugness curdle into fear.

He didn't. That was the whole discipline of his life, distilled: he didn't.

"How much?" Sebastian asked.

Corvin blinked. "What?"

"The gold. How much are they paying you to sell us?"

"Sebastian—" Hazel started.

"No. Let him say it. He came here to say it; let him say it properly." Sebastian kept his voice level, almost gentle. "How much?"

"Four hundred crowns now. Six hundred on delivery." Corvin met his eyes, and there was something underneath the mercenary flatness—something tired, something old. "That's three years of runs. That's a new hull, new sails, crew paid through winter. That's me not ending up like every other smuggler who got old."

"You could have just taken it," Hazel said. "You could have taken the contract and never told him. Why are you telling us?"

And there it was—the question Sebastian had been circling since Corvin walked through the door. Because a man who had already decided to betray them would not have come in person. A man who had decided would simply have sent word ahead and collected his gold while they slept. Corvin was here because some part of him was still negotiating—with them, yes, but mostly with himself.

"Why am I telling you," Corvin repeated. He laughed, short and without humor. "Because twelve years ago a man pulled me out of the Vellamark canal with a hook and chain around my ankle, and that man used magic to do it, in a city where magic gets you the same as drowning, only slower. And then that man stood in front of three Warden patrols and lied to all of them with a straight face while I bled in his cellar for a week." He drained the rest of the ale. "I keep waiting for that debt to feel paid. It doesn't. It just keeps accruing interest."

"So don't pay it," Sebastian said. "Walk away. Take your boat south, forget our faces."

"They'd find me by spring. They found my *boat*, Sebastian. They know my routes better than I do—which means someone talked, which means someone I trusted is already theirs, which means there is nowhere I can run that their gold doesn't reach first." Corvin's hands flattened on the table. For the first time since entering, he looked his age, and older. "I'm not telling you this because I have a choice. I'm telling you because I don't, and because some stupid, stubborn piece of me wants you to hate me for what I have to do, rather than wonder later why I never gave you warning."

The fire popped in the hearth. The fiddler sawed on. Hazel had gone pale, but her jaw was set in a way Sebastian recognized—he'd seen it on another face once, years ago, across a windswept threshold, in the last hour before everything broke.

"Then let's talk terms," Sebastian said.

Corvin frowned. "Terms?"

"You said six hundred on delivery. Delivery of what, exactly? Our location? Our bodies? A sealed Gate and proof of death?"

"Location first. The rest depends on what Ilsette wants when she arrives."

The name went through Sebastian like cold water through cloth. He had known, of course—had known since the raid in the undercity, since the precision of it, since the way they'd seemed to know not just where he was but *who* he was, what he'd been. Only one person alive had both the knowledge and the motive. Hearing the name spoken aloud was different. Hearing it made it a fact instead of a fear.

"She's coming herself," he said.

"She's coming herself," Corvin confirmed. "With a full retinue. Whatever you two are walking toward, she wants to be standing on it when you arrive."

* * *

They moved to Corvin's rented room above the stable, because Corvin didn't discuss business in rooms with ears in the walls, and because he poured a spirit from his own flask—harsh, peaty, tasting of smoke and salt—that made the conversation feel slightly more survivable.

Hazel sat on the windowsill with the map in her lap, guarding it without seeming to. Sebastian stood by the shuttered window, looking out through the slats at the darkening street, organizing himself. This was the part he had been dreading since the ford, since before the ford, if he were honest—since the moment he'd understood that Corvin's loyalty was a bridge with a toll on it, and that eventually he'd have to pay.

He had so little left to pay with. That was the joke of it. An exiled storm-caller's assets: one broken gift, one guilty conscience, one map, and a memory full of things the Wardens had spent twelve years trying to make sure no one remembered.

But there was one thing. There had always been one thing.

"The stones," Sebastian said.

Corvin stopped mid-pour. "What about them?"

"When the Wardens broke the covenant, they didn't destroy the anchor-stones. You can't destroy them—they're not objects, not really, they're *knots*, tied places where the two realms hold each other. So they took them. Eleven stones from eleven gate-sites, crated up and carted away in the first year, before anyone outside the Wardens' inner circle understood what was happening." Sebastian turned from the window. "Everyone assumes they were sunk in the harbor, or melted down, or locked in the Citadel vaults. Everyone's wrong."

"You know where they are." Corvin's voice had changed. The merchant was gone; something hungrier and warier had taken its place.

"I know where they were put. Whether they're still there is another question."

"Sebastian." Hazel had risen from the windowsill. "You never said—you never told me you knew this."

"You never asked what I'd trade to keep us alive." He said it gently, but he saw it land, saw her understand that this was what the road was going to be from here on: a series of payments, each one a piece of himself, until either the Gate or the grave took the rest. "Sit down, Corvin."

Corvin sat. He hadn't touched his drink since Sebastian spoke. That, more than anything, told Sebastian how deep the hook had gone. Every smuggler on the coast grew up on stories of the covenant-stones—not as holy relics, though they were that too, but as *leverage*. A stone could anchor a route no Warden could close. A stone could make a harbor immune to interdiction. A stone in the wrong hands could unmake the ban itself, and half the underworld had spent a generation hunting rumors of them and dying for nothing.

"Twelve years ago," Sebastian said, "when they exiled me, they marched me out through the Saltmarsh Road. Not the main road—the old survey road, the one that runs behind the tidal flats. I asked why. The escort captain told me to shut up. But I'd walked that road before, in another life, and I knew what lay along it: the old salt-works. The ones that closed when the weather turned bad, forty years back. Deep galleries cut into the rock below the waterline. Dry as bone inside, if you know the drainage channels—and the Wardens' engineers redug those channels themselves, the year after my exile. I've seen the supply ledgers since. Quarried stone, 'ceremonial,' eleven crates, inbound to a site with no name and no garrison and no reason to exist."

"The salt-works," Corvin breathed. "Under everyone's feet. Under the *tide*."

"Where no pilgrim would look, no smuggler would dig, and no flood would ever be allowed to rise high enough to matter. They keep the flats drained with a pump-house staffed by six men who think they're maintaining an irrigation system." Sebastian let a breath out slowly. "Ilsette knows I know. It's one of the reasons I was exiled instead of killed—my testimony at the tribunal included what I'd seen of the removals, and killing a witness who's already published his knowledge is wasteful. She's spent twelve years assuming that knowing and reaching are different things. That no one would be mad enough to dig beneath the sea for them."

"Are you mad enough?" Hazel asked quietly.

"For the stones? Yes. For anything else—" He shook his head. "Here is my price, Corvin. You take the Wardens' contract. All of it. You report what they've hired you to report: our numbers, our road, our pace. You tell them we're heading for the Ash Pass, because we are, and because a lie wrapped around a truth is the only kind that holds weight. And when Ilsette comes—and she will come, she'll come *fast*, because she cannot risk us reaching the Gate before her—you make sure her retinue takes the low road along the river. The flooded road. The slow one."

"And you take the high road." Corvin was already doing the arithmetic; Sebastian could see it moving behind his eyes, the timings, the tides, the margins. "Buying yourself two days. Maybe three."

"Three days is enough to reach the salt-works, lift what we can carry, and be gone before her outriders realize the high road is empty ahead of them."

"And after? When she learns the charm-seller played her? When she comes for the man who told her secrets to a smuggler?" Corvin's mouth twisted. "She doesn't forgive, Sebastian. I've seen what she does to people who cost her."

"Then I'll owe you a second favor," Sebastian said. "Consider it a subscription."

"It's not enough." Corvin said it flatly, without apology. "Gold I can spend. Favors rot. I want the location written down. Sealed. Held by a third party, released to me if I die inconveniently."

"Done."

"And I want it tonight, before I leave."

"Done," Sebastian said again, and watched the last of Corvin's hesitation go out of him—not because the man was satisfied, but because a bargain fully struck leaves no room for doubt, and doubt was the only thing that had been keeping Corvin human through all of this. Sebastian understood that. He had done his own share of trading pieces of himself to survive, and he knew the strange mercy of a deal: it tells you exactly what you are worth, and spares you the guessing.

Hazel followed Corvin to the door while Sebastian inked the location onto a scrap of oilcloth, writing in the old survey cipher that only three living people could read, and hoping he was still one of them. At the threshold, Corvin paused.

"The daughter," he said, too low for Hazel to hear from the stairs—or so he thought. "The Wardens' description of her doesn't match any record in Vellamark. No birth registration, no guild papers, nothing. Ilsette's hunters flagged it. They think she's foreign." He glanced back, and his eyes were old and sorry. "They're wrong, aren't they. About what she is."

Sebastian said nothing.

"That's what I thought." Corvin tucked the oilcloth away. "For what it's worth—I hope you get there. Both of you. And I hope, when she catches you, that you've got something left to bargain with besides maps." He stepped out into the blue dusk, and his footsteps faded down the stair, unhurried, a man going to collect his blood money with a clear conscience and a sealed letter in his coat.

Hazel came back into the room slowly. She didn't sit. She stood over the table where the map lay, and the lamp caught the oath-mark on her wrist—the twin spiral, half storm, half root—and for a long moment she said nothing at all.

"You gave him the salt-works," she finally said. "The one thing the Wardens can't afford for us to have. You handed it to a man on their payroll."

"I handed it to a man who owes me more than they can pay him. There's a difference."

"Is there?" Her voice cracked, not with anger—with fear, the specific fear of someone watching their last asset walk out the door in a stranger's pocket. "Because in three days, either we're digging stones out of the tide-flats, or Ilsette is dragging us back to Vellamark in chains, and either way it's because you *trusted* him."

"Yes," Sebastian said. "That's what trust is. It's a wager you place knowing you might lose everything." He folded the map, slid it inside his coat, against his chest, where the storm hummed its low restless note. "I lost everything once already, Hazel. Everything except the part of me that still chooses to believe people can be better than their debts. I'm not giving that up too. It's the last thing I own."

Outside, far off toward the coast, thunder rolled—dry thunder, sound without rain, the sky clearing its throat over a land that had forgotten what weather was. Hazel turned toward the window, and Sebastian saw her hand rise unconsciously to the mark on her wrist, fingers tracing the spiral the way a tongue finds a chipped tooth.

"Did you hear that?" she whispered.

He had. And it wasn't the direction thunder should have come from, and it wasn't the season for storms, and it rolled twice more before fading—close, and closer, like something vast turning over in its sleep and beginning, at long last, to dream of them.

"Get your pack," Sebastian said. "We leave within the hour. Before that sound finds a reason to get louder."
