## 9. Storm Without Thunder

The road north had taught me things no book in Vellamark ever could: how to sleep sitting up against a fence post, how to tell a Warden's horse from a farmer's by the way it carried its head, how to read Sebastian's silences the way other people read weather.

That last one was the most useful, and the most unsettling.

For three days he had been watching me. Not the way men sometimes watched me—quick, apologetic glances that slid away—but with a long, measuring attention, like a man counting coins and not liking the total. He thought I didn't notice. He was wrong about many things, my mother's old friend, but I had learned to notice things too. My mother had raised me on noticing. *Watch the hands, Hazel. The mouth lies; the hands only ever tell you what they're about to do.*

So when we came down out of the pine scrub into the shallow valley called Miller's Rest—a scatter of roofs around a dead mill, a stream running thin and silver through it—and Sebastian stopped so abruptly I nearly walked into his back, I watched his hands.

They were very still. Too still. The stillness of a man holding something down.

"What is it?" I whispered.

"Smoke," he said. "Wrong smoke." He nodded toward the village. Woodsmoke from hearths rises straight and lazy in still air. This rose in quick gray ropes from behind the mill, and beneath its smell was something sharper—oiled leather, cold iron, the particular char of a fire built to burn hot and fast rather than warm and slow.

"Wardens?"

"Maybe. Maybe just travelers being careless." His voice was flat, which meant it wasn't careless at all. "We go around. There's a ford two miles east."

"We need food, Sebastian. We've been eating pine nuts and whatever Corvin left us." I said it lightly, but my stomach cramped as I spoke, three days of hunger making itself heard. "And the map says there's a well here. A *marked* well. You told me yourself the old wells are safe ground—that they can't be warded because they predate the Wardens' whole order."

"I know what I told you." He rubbed the bridge of his nose, a gesture I'd come to recognize as the shape of an argument he was losing with himself. "I also told you that safe ground draws people like blood draws crows. If there's a hunter on our trail—and there has been since the pass—we're walking into his palm."

"The pass was two days ago. If someone were following, we'd have seen them."

"That," Sebastian said quietly, "is exactly what worries me."

I should have listened. I want that written somewhere, in whatever ledger keeps accounts of fools: *she should have listened.* But grief makes its own arguments, and hunger makes them louder, and underneath both ran something I couldn't name yet—a pull toward that marked well, faint and constant, like a thread tied behind my navel drawing taut the closer we came. I had felt it for days without admitting it. It felt the way my mother's hand had felt on my hair when I was small. It felt like being *known*.

"We look," I said. "We don't enter. We circle the edge, fill the waterskins at the stream, and decide. That's fair."

Sebastian looked at me for a long moment. Something moved behind his eyes—not anger. Fear, maybe, or its cousin, resignation.

"Fair," he echoed. "Your mother used to negotiate like that. She always made surrender sound like diplomacy." He sighed and unshouldered his pack. "Fine. Stream first. Stay low, stay quiet, and if I say *run*, you run and you do not look back and you do not wait for me. Swear it."

"You know I won't—"

"*Swear it*, Hazel." And there it was again, that strange intensity, that weight he kept putting on words as though words were stones he was building something with. "Please."

I swore it. I even meant it, in the way one means things at nineteen, which is completely and conditionally.

* * *

The stream ran cold enough to ache. We knelt in the reeds below the mill and filled the skins, and for a few minutes the world was only water-sound and the small industry of drinking, and I let myself believe we might simply pass through this valley like ghosts and be gone before anyone knew we'd breathed its air.

Then the dogs started.

Not barking—Warden dogs didn't bark, Sebastian had told me once; barking was noise, and noise was information given away. They *sang*, a low rising keening from the tree line north of the mill, and Sebastian went rigid beside me like a wire pulled taut.

"Hunter," he breathed. "That's a tracker's cry. They've got our scent off something—" His head snapped up, and I saw him do the arithmetic of the wind, the trees, the distance. "Corvin's cloak. The scrap I gave the boy at the crossroads to throw them off. It worked too well—they know it's ours, and now they know we know."

"How far?"

"Close. Coming fast. Two riders, maybe three, and the dogs will run ahead." He was already moving, hauling me up by the wrist, and we crashed upstream through the reeds, water to our knees, thorns raking my sleeves. Behind us the singing swelled, and under it now—hooves.

"They'll cut us off at the ford!" I shouted.

"No—they'll cut us off at the *well*. They want us cornered on open ground." His grip on my wrist was bruising. "Which means the well is the last place we should—"

But my feet had already decided.

I don't know how else to say it. The thread behind my navel pulled, hard, and my body turned toward the mill before my mind had finished the sentence, and I was running—not away from the hunters but *toward* the thing they wanted us to avoid, dragging Sebastian after me because he wouldn't let go of my wrist and I was stronger than I looked, stronger than I had any right to be.

"Hazel—*Hazel*—"

The mill loomed up, its wheel frozen mid-turn, furred with moss. Beyond it, half-hidden in a stand of alders, stood the well: older than the mill, older than anything, a ring of black stone carved all over with signs I suddenly recognized—the same warding marks Sebastian had been teaching me on the road, but deeper, surer, cut by hands that had known what they were doing instead of merely remembering. And above the well, in the air itself, something shimmered. Not visible, exactly. More like the way you can feel a note held just below hearing.

The thread in my chest sang toward it.

"Hazel, stop—" Sebastian's voice cracked into something raw. "Whatever you think you feel, it isn't—"

Hooves burst from the tree line.

Three riders, gray-cloaked, and ahead of them on foot two lean shapes with hackles high—Warden hounds, big as wolves, moving with the terrible economy of animals bred for one purpose. The lead rider wore no hood. She was young, maybe thirty, with a face like a closed door and a long hooked blade across her saddlebow, and when she saw us her expression didn't change at all, and somehow that was worse than hatred.

"Hold," she said, and the word dropped into the valley like a stone into water.

Sebastian stepped in front of me. Slowly, deliberately, he raised his empty hands. "Just travelers," he called. "Bound for—"

"I know what you are, Okafor." Her voice was pleasant, almost gentle, which frightened me more than shouting would have. "Exile. Charm-seller. Thief of Warden property." Her eyes found me over his shoulder, and there was something in them—curiosity, cold and clinical. "And the daughter. Maren's girl. The mother bled out in a market stall rather than talk to us, I'm told. Let's see if the child has better sense."

My mother's name in that mouth. Something went through me then that I had no word for—not rage exactly, though rage was in it, and not fear, though fear was there too, but some third thing made of both, a storm-front building where my heart should have been.

"You knew her," I said. My voice came out wrong. Doubled, almost. Like two people speaking at once, slightly out of step.

The huntress tilted her head. "Everyone who mattered knew Maren Voss. Gate-warden. Traitor. Dead." She swung down from her horse, unhurried, and the hounds flanked her like shadows given teeth. "Here is what happens next. You give me the map. You give me the stones' location—I know he knows it; they always know—and you kneel, both of you, and you're taken alive, which is more than your mother managed. Refuse, and—" she gestured vaguely at the mill, the alders, the beautiful indifferent morning, "—well. The valley's remote. No one hears anything out here."

Sebastian's hands were still raised, but I felt him trembling—not with fear. With calculation. He was measuring distances, odds, the hook-blade, the hounds, the two mounted men spreading wide to flank us. I had seen him do this arithmetic before, in the shop, when Wardens came asking questions, and I had seen him always arrive at the same answer: *smallness. Surrender. Survive.*

But this time he glanced back at me. Just once. Just a flicker.

And I understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that he was not going to surrender. That whatever he saw in my face had changed his sums.

"Hazel," he said softly. "Get behind the well."

"No," I said.

"*Hazel.*"

"She killed my mother." I didn't know if it was true. I didn't care. "Or her kind did. Same thing."

The huntress smiled thinly. "There's spirit. The mother had spirit too, right up until—"

She never finished it.

Later, trying to reconstruct those seconds, I could only manage fragments. The hounds lunging. Sebastian shouting something—my name, or a warning, or both. The hook-blade coming off the saddle in a single fluid motion. And then—

Then the sky *answered* me.

There was no thunder. That's the part I cannot make anyone understand, the part that wakes me even now with my heart slamming: there was no thunder at all. Lightning without sound, light without heat, a white silence that opened inside my chest and poured outward through my hands. I hadn't spoken. I hadn't done *anything*. I had only reached—not with my arms, with something deeper than arms—toward the shimmering above the well, toward that held note below hearing, and it had *opened* to me like a door opening to the right key, and what came through me was not mine and was entirely mine, was every storm that had ever broken over the sea, was my mother's voice saying *watch the hands, Hazel* in a language I had never learned and somehow knew.

The bolt took the hook-blade out of the air. It didn't strike the huntress—it struck the *blade*, and the metal screamed and glowed and tore itself apart in her grip, and she staggered back with smoke curling off her gauntlets, and the hounds—brave, terrible, loyal hounds—flattened themselves to the earth and began, very softly, to whimper.

The silence afterward was enormous.

I stood with my hands still raised, and they were glowing. Faintly, along the old scar-lines on my palms—scars I'd had since childhood, since a fever when I was six that the doctors never explained—thin lines of pale blue light, like veins of lightning trapped under the skin. And the air around me tasted of rain. Rain that hadn't fallen in twelve years. Rain I had never smelled but recognized instantly, the way you recognize a parent's footsteps.

"Oh," I said. Very quietly. To no one. To myself. "Oh, no."

Because I knew. In that moment I knew what Sebastian had been circling for days, what his measuring glances had been weighing, what the doubled voice in my throat meant. Magic was outlawed. Magic was hunted. Magic was the thing that had gotten my mother killed in a market stall with a map pressed into a stranger's hands. And it lived in me—not borrowed, not trained, not smuggled. *Born.*

The huntress stared at me. For the first time, the door of her face cracked open, and what showed through was awe, and awe curdled instantly into something hungrier.

"So it's true," she breathed. "The old blood. Ilsette said the line survived, but I thought—" She caught herself, and the door shut again, and the hunger stayed. "Take them," she said to her riders. "*Carefully.* The girl especially. Unmarked skin. The Master Warden will want her breathing."

Everything happened quickly after that, and everything happened slowly. Both at once—that's what terror does to time. Sebastian moved. I had never seen him move like that; the shambling charm-seller of the market stall vanished and something else stood in his place, something that had once guarded a gate between worlds. He wrenched the iron band off the old well—one motion, the warding signs flaring—and hurled it spinning into the lead rider's chest, and the man went down. He grabbed my arm. The horses screamed and shied as the air around us thickened, pressure dropping, the dead millwheel groaning as if remembering how to turn.

"Run," Sebastian snarled, and this time I ran.

We fled downstream, through alder and reed and bramble, the huntress's voice cracking behind us—*"the dogs, loose the dogs, don't lose that girl"*—and the hounds came, but slowly, reluctantly, whining, as though the ground itself where I had stood was suddenly disagreeable to them. Once a rider drew level through the trees and leveled a crossbow, and I threw out my hand without thinking, and the bolt veered—*veered*, bent mid-flight like a bird changing its mind—and buried itself in a trunk a full yard from Sebastian's head.

I hadn't aimed it. I hadn't done anything. That was the horror of it. It was as if the storm used my body the way a player uses a glove, and I was only beginning to guess at the size of the hand inside.

We ran until the valley walls closed in and the pursuit fell behind, and then we ran because stopping was unthinkable, and then at last the pines swallowed us and Sebastian dragged us both down into a hollow beneath a granite shelf, and we lay gasping in the dark green dimness while somewhere far off a dog sang once, twice, and gave up.

* * *

For a long time neither of us spoke. My hands had stopped glowing, but I could still feel it under the skin, coiled and patient, like a river under ice. I kept my fists clenched. I didn't want it touching anything. I didn't entirely want it touching *me*.

When Sebastian finally spoke, his voice was hoarse.

"Show me your hands."

"No."

"Hazel."

"I said *no*." I was shaking. I heard my own voice climb and hated it. "Don't—don't look at me like that, don't *study* me like one of your trinkets, I didn't ask for this, I don't know what it *is*—"

"It's yours."

"Then I don't want it!" The words tore out of me, and with them, horribly, a flicker—that blue light licking up between my knuckles—and I stuffed my fists against my chest like a child hiding contraband. "Do you understand? Everything magic touches dies. My mother died for it. Your whole life burned down for it. The Wardens burn villages over *less* than what I just did back there, and I did it without even *trying*, I didn't choose it, it chose *me*—" My breath hitched. "What am I, Sebastian? What did my mother *make* me?"

He was quiet for a moment. When he answered, he didn't reach for me, didn't touch me—he had learned that much about my fear already—but he leaned close, and his eyes in the dimness were the color of storm-water, and for once there was no measuring in them at all. Only a grief so old it had worn smooth, like a stone carried in a pocket for years.

"Twelve years ago," he said, "I stood at a gate between two worlds, and I watched it break, and I have spent every day since telling myself I understood why it broke." He exhaled slowly. "I was wrong. I was looking at the crack and calling it the cause." He nodded, very gently, at my clenched fists—at the thin blue seam of light showing between my fingers. "You weren't made, Hazel. You were *meant*. Half of one realm, half of the other, born under a covenant that was already dying. Do you know how rare that is? Do you know how *impossible*?" His voice dropped. "There hasn't been blood like yours since the Gate was whole."

The hollow was very quiet. Somewhere above, wind moved through the pines with a sound like the sea.

"And the huntress," I whispered. "She said a name. Ilsette. She said the Master Warden would want me breathing. Sebastian—if they know what I am, if they've *always* known—then my mother wasn't hiding me from the ban." My stomach turned cold. "She was hiding me from *them*. From whatever they want a bridge between realms for."

Sebastian went very still. I watched the same realization move through him like weather—watched it reach his eyes and darken them.

"Yes," he said finally. "Yes, I think you're right. And I think I know what they want." He looked north, toward the mountains we had not yet crossed, toward the Hollow Gate waiting somewhere beyond them like an unhealed wound. "And I think we have less time than the map says we do."

Above us, through the pine canopy, the afternoon sky had begun to gather clouds. Gray, heavy, impossible clouds—the first in twelve years—moving with slow purpose against the wind.

Toward the Gate. Toward me.

As if the storm, too, had finally found the thing it had been searching for.
