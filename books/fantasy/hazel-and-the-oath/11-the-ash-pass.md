## 11. The Ash Pass

The mountain announced itself long before we saw it.

We smelled it first—a faint sulfur breath on the wind, like a struck match held under the world's tongue—and then, as we climbed out of the last green valley, we began to hear it: a low, patient grinding beneath the earth, the sound of something enormous turning over in its sleep. Sebastian stopped at the tree line and looked up at the pass for a long time without speaking.

"What is it?" I asked.

"Nothing," he said, which was how he always said *everything*.

The trees ended not gradually but all at once, as if drawn by a line no one had dared cross. Beyond it the world turned grey and then black—fields of volcanic ash compacted into ridges hard as iron, slopes of scree that hissed and slid under our boots, and above it all the pass itself, a notch in the jagged spine of the range where the sky seemed thinner, paler, worn through like old cloth. Somewhere beyond that notch, my mother had told me once, when I was small enough to believe her stories were only stories, there was a door.

I had spent twenty-three years thinking she meant it the way mothers mean such things. *Beyond the mountains there is a door.* The way you might say *beyond the sea there are dragons* to a child who needs the world to be larger than her bedroom. But my mother had died in a market in Vellamark with a stranger's blood on her hands and an oath pressed into mine, and now every story she had ever told me was being re-read in a new light, each one revealing teeth I hadn't noticed before.

We made camp that first night just below the tree line, in the lee of a fallen pine silvered with age. Sebastian built the fire small—he always built it small, a habit I'd come to understand was less about warmth than about not being seen—and sat staring north with his hands open on his knees, palms up, the way he did when he thought I wasn't watching. As if waiting for something to land in them. As if listening for a voice that had stopped answering years ago.

"How many times have you crossed this?" I asked.

He didn't pretend not to understand. "Four times going, three coming back."

"And after?"

"After," he said quietly, "the road closed." He fed a twig to the fire. "Your mother was the last person to walk it before me."

That night I dreamed of ash falling like snow, soft and endless, covering everything I loved.

* * *

The second day, we found the first bones.

I almost stepped on them before I saw them—a hand, or what remained of one, reaching up out of the ash as if the mountain had swallowed the rest of its owner mid-crawl. The fingers were fused together by heat, blackened into a single grasping shape. Sebastian crouched beside it and did not touch it.

"Pilgrim," he said.

"You can tell that from a hand?"

"The wrist." He nodded at a leather band still circling the bone, cracked and bleached but legible: a stitched sigil in faded blue thread. "Storm-knot. Pilgrims wore them so that if they died on the road, the storm would know them. Would carry word home." He paused. "It was a kindness. Mostly it was a hope."

I looked at the hand a long moment. Then, because it seemed suddenly unbearable to leave it reaching for nothing, I knelt and pushed ash over it until it was covered—not buried, exactly. Tucked in.

Sebastian watched me do it and said nothing, but something in his face shifted, the way ice shifts on a pond in early spring.

We found more as the day went on. A ribcage half-dissolved into the slope, ferns of white mineral blooming from between the bones. A boot, empty, standing upright in the scree as if its owner had simply stepped out of the world. A Warden's helm—the high-crested kind, the kind I'd seen glinting in Vellamark's streets my whole life—dented inward at the temple, its lacquer bubbled and peeled, resting in a hollow like a bowl someone had set down and forgotten.

"They came this way too," I said. "Wardens."

"Wardens especially, near the end." Sebastian walked with his eyes down, reading the ground the way other men read books. "After the Gate broke, after the ban, they wanted it sealed properly. Buried. There were those among them who believed if they could destroy the threshold entirely, the storm-realm would forget the way in." His mouth twisted. "The mountain disagreed."

The wind picked up as we climbed, carrying grit that stung my cheeks and tasted of metal. By afternoon the ash lay in drifts against the rocks like dirty snow, and our footprints behind us looked like the tracks of two small animals crossing an enormous wound. Twice the ground shuddered under us—that same deep grinding, closer now—and twice Sebastian stopped, tilted his head as though listening, and then resumed walking without explanation.

At dusk we reached a shelf of rock broad enough to camp on, sheltered by an overhang furred with pale lichen. While Sebastian saw to the fire, I walked to the edge of the shelf and looked back the way we had come.

The world below was already dark. Above, the notch of the pass caught the last red light and held it, glowing faintly, like a coal in a grate. And along the ridge line to the east, half-buried in the ash, I could see shapes. Dozens of them. Stones stacked in cairns, some collapsed, some still standing. Markers.

Graves, or memorials, or both.

I counted thirty before the light failed, and knew I hadn't counted them all.

* * *

On the third day we entered the field of the dead.

There was no gentler way to think of it, though I tried. The pass narrowed there, funneling travelers between walls of black glass where the ancient eruption had melted the stone and left it slick and strange, humming faintly when the wind passed over it. And in that narrow place, the remains thickened until walking became a kind of wading.

Pilgrims first—I knew them by their storm-knots, some still tied, most rotted to threads. They lay in twos and threes, clustered as people cluster in cold weather, huddled together against a death that had come for them anyway. Farther on, exiles: I recognized these because Sebastian did, stopping before each small group with an expression I couldn't read, murmuring names under his breath like a man paying debts. *Maren of the Low Fells. The brothers Ashk. Old Tam, who owed me a coat.* People who had tried, in the years after the breaking, to reach the Gate and mend what was broken. People the mountain had kept.

And then, thickest of all, Wardens.

They had come in formation; you could still see it in the way they'd fallen, ranks preserved even in ruin, shields fused to arms, swords rusted into the stone where they'd been driven point-first as last stands or last prayers. One entire rank lay face-down in the ash, unmarked by any wound I could find, as if the air itself had refused them. Their armor was different from the modern kind—older, heavier, worked with the same storm-sigils the pilgrims had worn, which no Warden in Vellamark would dare display now.

"They used to guard the road," Sebastian said. He stood very still amid the dead, his voice low, as if afraid of waking them. "Before the ban. Before Ilsette's generation. Warden used to mean *keeper*. It meant you stood between the realms and kept the door honest." He looked at the faceless rank in the ash. "Whatever they were sent here to do, in the end—they died at their post. Whatever else you believe about them, Hazel, give them that."

I gave them that. I also gave them my dread, because the question would not leave me: *if armed ranks of keepers could not cross this pass alive, what exactly were we walking toward?*

Near the far end of the field, where the walls of black glass drew close enough that I could touch both at once, we found the shrine.

It was nothing—a niche carved into the glassy wall, barely deep enough for two cupped hands, holding a stub of candle wax, a bird's skull, and a strip of cloth embroidered with a sigil I knew better than my own name. My mother's gate-mark. The same mark that burned, faint and warm, on the inside of my left wrist, the mark I'd carried since childhood believing it was a birthmark, a blemish, a flaw.

Someone had left it here recently. The cloth was not yet faded.

"She came through," I said. My voice sounded strange in the narrow place, flattened by the glass walls. "Recently. Within the year."

"Yes."

"Alone?"

Sebastian was quiet for a moment. "Gate-wardens traveled alone. It was part of the office. What you carry, you don't share until you must." He glanced at my wrist, where the mark sat under my sleeve like a coal banked in ash. "She must have known she was near the end. They leave offerings when they can't finish the walk. Asking the mountain to remember them. Asking whoever comes after to finish it."

I touched the cloth. It crumbled slightly under my fingers, soft with age and weather, and I felt something crack in my chest that I suspected would not heal cleanly. All those years of her absences—the yearly weeks she'd claimed were trade journeys south, the salt-smell on her cloak when she returned, the way she sometimes stood at our window at night looking north as if expecting someone. She had been walking toward this. Year after year. Carrying a duty she could never explain to a daughter who was, apparently, the reason for it.

"Why didn't she tell me?" I asked. It came out smaller than I intended. "All those years. She let me think she was selling ribbon."

Sebastian looked at me then, really looked, and I saw that the question hurt him too—or its answer did. "Because telling you would have made you part of it," he said. "And everything that is part of the covenant becomes a target for it. Your mother spent her whole life keeping you outside the story." His jaw tightened. "She failed. Or the story grew faster than she could hold it back. Either way—" he gestured at the field of the dead around us, the pilgrims and exiles and keepers sleeping in the ash, "—this is why she didn't tell you. This is what she was protecting you from knowing existed."

I stood in the silence of the pass, surrounded by everyone who had ever tried to do what I was doing, and understood, finally, the true weight of the folded map in my pack. It wasn't directions. It was a relay baton, passed hand to hand down a line of runners nearly all of whom had fallen.

My mother had run it alone for twenty years. Now it was mine.

* * *

We climbed out of the field of the dead as the sun went down behind the western peaks, and the temperature fell with it, fast and vicious. We made camp in a hollow beneath a leaning slab of basalt, and Sebastian built the fire small again, and I wrapped myself in both blankets and watched the stars come out hard and clear in the thin air.

The grinding under the earth was louder now. Not continuous—it came in slow swells, like breathing, like something vast drawing breath and letting it go. Between swells the silence was so complete I could hear my own pulse.

"Hazel." Sebastian's voice, quiet across the fire. "Tomorrow we cross the saddle. After that, the Gate is half a day's walk. I want you to know what you'll see before you see it."

"Tell me."

He fed the fire a stick, considering. "You've seen the drawings in the map margins. Forget them. They're flatter than the truth." He spoke slowly, choosing words like a man crossing scree. "Imagine a doorway standing alone in a valley. No house around it, no wall. Just a frame of black stone taller than three men, older than anything, with air on both sides that doesn't match. On this side, our air. Cold, thin, ordinary." He paused. "On the other side—light that comes from nowhere. Wind that has never touched ground. A sky the color of the inside of a thundercloud, lit from within. That's the storm-realm. That's what your mother tended."

"And now?"

His hands stilled. "Now the frame is broken. Cracked through, top to bottom, the night twelve years ago. And what leaks through isn't the storm anymore. It's the storm's *wound*. Unshaped power. Weather without a mind behind it. It's been bleeding into our world for twelve years, and everything wrong with the seasons, everything dying in the lowlands, everything the Wardens blame on magic—" he shook his head, "—is the Gate, failing. Slower than a heartbeat. Faster than we can afford."

I pulled my sleeves down over my hands. Under the wool, my mark was warm. Warmer than it had ever been, I realized. Warm the way a hand grows warm when it's held.

"Sebastian." I waited until he met my eyes across the small fire. "The others. The pilgrims, the exiles, the Wardens in their ranks. Why did they die? Was it the mountain? The cold? Or—"

"Or the Gate?" He looked into the flames. "Some of each. The mountain kills the careless. But near the threshold, the leaking power gets into everything—stone, air, blood. It pulls at whatever in you answers it. If nothing in you answers, you mostly just sicken. If something does—" his voice dropped, "—it calls. And people follow the call off the path, into the glass fields, into the fumaroles. Smiling, some of them. The old records called it *going home the wrong way*."

The fire popped. High above us, wind moved through the notch of the pass with a sound almost like voices.

"I'm frightened," I said. It cost me something to say it plainly, but I had learned on this road that Sebastian traded only in plain things, and lying to him was like lying to a scale—he weighed it instantly and gave you back the truth of yourself. "Not of dying. Of answering. Of wanting to go through and not being able to stop."

For a long moment he said nothing. Then he reached across the fire and laid his hand over mine, over the sleeve, over the hidden mark, and his palm was hot—hotter than skin should be, hot the way stones stay hot hours after the sun leaves them.

"When the call comes," he said, "you look at me. Not at the Gate. At me. I have walked this road four times and the call knows my name better than it knows yours, and I am still here. That is the whole of my teaching, Hazel, and it took me twelve years and everything I loved to learn it: the oath is not spoken at the threshold. It is spoken at every step before it. Every step you choose instead of the easy pull. That's what your mother practiced her whole life, alone, in the dark, so that when the real moment came she'd be ready." His grip tightened, gentle and fierce at once. "You are not walking into this blind. You are walking in carrying twenty years of her practice. Feel the difference."

I did feel it—or I wanted to, desperately—and I held onto his hand until the fire burned low, and slept without dreaming of ash for the first time since the tree line.

* * *

We crossed the saddle at dawn.

The wind at the top was unlike any wind I had known—it came from both directions at once, warm from the south and cold from the north, tangling around us like two hands arguing over the same thing. Below us, on the far side, the land fell away into a high valley ringed with peaks, and even at that distance I could see that the valley was wrong. The grass grew in spirals. The shadows fell toward the sun. And at its center, catching the first light like a blade catching flame, stood a vertical line of darkness that could only be the Gate.

My mark flared so hot I gasped and pressed my wrist against my ribs.

Beside me, Sebastian had gone rigid. He was staring down into the valley, and his face—his careful, guarded, performed-smallness face—had come apart entirely. What was underneath was grief and hunger and terror braided into one expression, the face of a man looking at the grave and the home and the crime scene that were all the same place.

"It's worse than I remembered," he whispered. "The cracks. It's spreading."

And then the wind changed.

It came up the southern face all at once, a single warm gust smelling of rain and ozone and something green and living that had no business existing at this altitude—and in it, threaded through it, brief as a struck bell, I heard a voice.

Not words. Not quite. More like the memory of a voice, the shape a voice leaves in a room after the speaker has gone. It moved past my ear, through my hair, against the burning mark on my wrist, and every hair on my body rose, and somewhere far below us in the wounded valley, the dark line of the Gate flickered—once, twice—like a lamp guttering, or like something waving.

Sebastian grabbed my arm. "Did you—"

"Yes," I said.

We stood on the saddle with the two winds pulling at us, and the voice-memory dissolved, and the Gate below steadied into darkness again. But my heart was hammering with something that was not only fear, and when I looked at Sebastian I saw the same terrible recognition dawning in his face that was rising in mine:

The storm-realm knew I was coming.

And it was not waiting patiently.
