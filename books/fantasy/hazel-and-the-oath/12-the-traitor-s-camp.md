## 12. The Traitor's Camp

The Ash Pass narrowed as they climbed, and the world turned the color of old bone.

Sebastian felt it before he saw it—the way a man feels weather in an old wound. The air had changed. Not colder, exactly; the pass had been cold since dawn, its black slopes still crusted with last winter's ash. But there was a wrongness to it now, a flatness, like a room where someone had just stopped speaking. His skin, which had hummed faintly with storm all his life, went quiet in a way that was not peace.

He stopped on the trail and put out one hand, palm up, the way he'd done ten thousand times in another life to read the pressure of coming rain.

Nothing came.

"Hazel." He kept his voice low, though the wind had died too, and sound carried strangely here, close and muffled, as if the mountain were listening through cotton. "Stop walking."

She stopped ahead of him, her mother's cloak pulled tight, her breath steaming. Twelve days on the road had worn her thin—there were shadows under her eyes and a new sharpness to her jaw that hadn't been there when she'd stormed into his shop demanding answers—but she'd also grown steadier, surer of her feet on broken ground. She looked back at him with the question already forming.

"The magic's gone," she said. It wasn't a question after all. She'd learned to feel it too, these last weeks, or perhaps she'd always felt it and only now had words for it. "All of it."

"Not gone." Sebastian turned slowly, scanning the ridgelines. "Held. There's a difference. Somewhere near, someone is holding it."

He thought of the warded cell they'd escaped from in Vellamark's undercity, the way his gift had gone mute behind those walls, smothered like a coal under ash. This was larger. Vaster. A cell the size of a valley.

And then he saw the camp.

It sat in the bowl of the pass below them, where the trail bent around a shoulder of black rock: white tents in precise rows, cook-fires burning without smoke, horses picketed in perfect lines. Warden blue on every banner, but not the garrison-blue of the city patrols—this was deeper, darker, the blue of deep water, of officialdom. At the center of the camp rose a pavilion taller than the rest, and above it flew no banner at all, only a single gray pennant stitched with a circle bisected by a line.

The mark of the Master Wardens.

Sebastian's mouth went dry. "That's not a patrol. That's not even a hunt. That's an *expedition*."

"Here?" Hazel crouched beside him among the rocks. "There's nothing out here. There's nothing for fifty miles except—"

She stopped. Her eyes went to the pass ahead, to the high notch in the ridge where the trail crested and dropped beyond, toward the place the map ended. Toward the Hollow Gate.

"Except the Gate," she finished quietly.

"They know." Sebastian's stomach had turned to stone. "They've always known. We've been running north thinking we were escaping them, and they've simply been letting us walk to them." He thought of Corvin, dead two nights past with three crossbow bolts in his back, buying them their escape with his life, and grief flared hot and useless in his chest. *Don't let it be for nothing. Don't let this be where it ends.*

"We go around," Hazel said. "There has to be another way up."

"There isn't. I told you—I've walked this pass. Once. When it was still called something other than Ash." He traced the ridgeline with his eyes: sheer scree to the east, the volcanic spine to the west, walls of glassy rock that would shed a climber like water. The pass was the only throat, and the throat was full of Wardens. "We could wait for dark."

"And then what? Walk through four hundred men?"

"Three hundred, maybe. They're stretched thin." He heard how hollow it sounded and hated it.

Hazel said nothing. She was looking at the camp with an expression he recognized because he'd seen it on her mother's face, years ago, across a fire at the foot of this very pass: the look of someone doing arithmetic with things that cannot be counted. *One gate-warden against a wall of steel.* Maera had done that arithmetic once too, and lost.

"We should split up," Hazel said at last. "If they take one of us—"

"No."

"You don't even know what I was going to say."

"I know exactly what you were going to say, because your mother said it to me in almost this exact spot, and I refused then too." The words were out before he could stop them, and he watched them land—watched Hazel go still, watched the question rise in her like water climbing a wall. He'd told her pieces on the road. Fragments. Never this place, never this close to the truth of it.

Before she could speak, the rocks around them moved.

Not rocks. Men. They came up out of the scree on both sides with the silent, unhurried competence of people who had been waiting a long time and were very good at waiting—gray-cloaked, faces masked to the eyes, crossbows leveled and steady. Six of them. Eight. Sebastian hadn't heard so much as a boot-scrape, and he understood suddenly why the air felt held: they'd warded the whole slope, laid quiet over it like a sheet over furniture, and the two of them had walked into the parlor.

A voice came from behind them, unhurried, almost gentle.

"Don't run. The wardens on this slope have orders to shoot anything that moves downhill faster than a walk, and I'd hate for that to happen before we've had our talk."

Sebastian knew the voice.

Twelve years was a long time. Voices thickened, slowed, roughened. But he knew it the way you know a scar—by its shape under everything else—and his whole body went cold in a way that had nothing to do with the altitude. He turned.

She stood ten paces up the trail, where the rock gave her the height, hands folded loosely in front of her: a broad-shouldered woman in Master Warden's gray, silver at the temples, a face he remembered as handsome and now saw as carved—cheekbones like quarry stone, eyes pale and patient as a fisherman watching a line. No weapon drawn. She didn't need one. Eight crossbows made weapons redundant.

"Ilsette," Sebastian said.

"Sebastian Okafor." Something moved through her face—not quite a smile, not quite regret. "You've aged well, for a dead man. We buried you symbolically, you know. Closed casket. Very moving. Ilsette wept beautifully."

"You testified against me."

"I did." She said it plainly, without flinching, and somehow that was worse than denial. "Every word true, as it happens. That was the obscenity of it, if you recall. You *did* break covenant terms. You *did* unseal a breach without sanction. You *did* spend storm-gift on the unanointed. The tribunal didn't need me to lie. They needed me to make truth sound like treason." She tilted her head. "I've often wondered if you ever forgave me. I decided somewhere around year five that you hadn't, and stopped wondering."

"Forgave—" Sebastian's voice cracked and he hauled it down. Behind him he heard Hazel shift, heard the soft click of a crossbow being re-leveled, and forced himself to stillness. "You stood in front of the conclave. You'd shared my watch at the Gate for six years. You ate at my fire. And you stood there and helped them cut me out of my own name."

"Yes." Ilsette's pale eyes didn't waver. "And I'd do it again, which is why I won't insult you by apologizing. But you've misunderstood something fundamental, Sebastian, all these years, and I'm tired of it, and we have time before the transfer wagons arrive. So let me show you what you actually served, and what you actually broke."

She turned and gestured, and two of the masked wardens came forward—not to seize them, merely to flank, herding them gently up the trail toward the overlook. Sebastian walked because the crossbows left him no philosophy worth defending, and Hazel walked pressed close to his side, her hand finding his sleeve, and he felt her trembling and felt his own rage rising beneath the fear like floodwater under ice.

From the overlook, he could see into the heart of the camp.

What he'd taken for supply wagons stood in a ring at the center, and they were not wagons. They were cages—great iron-latticed frames on iron wheels, each one veiled in canvas, and from each cage came a light. Not torchlight. A glow that seeped through canvas seams like dawn through shutters, blue-white and restless, flickering in rhythms that Sebastian's body knew the way a sailor knows swell. His breath caught.

"Covenant-stones," he whispered.

"Seven of them," Ilsette said beside him. "Recovered over eleven years. There were twelve." She said it the way a merchant reports inventory. "Each one is a binding-anchor of the old covenant—a piece of the agreement itself, given form so that flesh could carry it. When the Gate broke, the stones scattered. The Order gathered them. For safekeeping, the histories say." Her mouth curved, without warmth. "The histories are ours. We wrote them."

Below, one of the cages shuddered. The light inside flared, dimmed, flared again, and Sebastian felt it in his teeth, in the old scar-tissue of his gift—a tugging, a grieving, like hearing a child cry through a wall.

"What are you doing to them?" Hazel's voice, shaking. She had her mother's blood and apparently her mother's sight, because her hand had risen halfway toward the cages as if pulled. "They're *hurting*. Can't you hear them?"

Ilsette looked at Hazel then, really looked, and Sebastian saw the fisherman's patience sharpen into something else—assessment, appetite. "Interesting," she murmured. "Yes. They hurt. Stones feel, in their fashion. The Order's founders discovered that a covenant-stone, properly caged and properly starved, can be made to yield up what it holds. Power. Raw and undiluted. Enough to ward a city the size of Vellamark so thoroughly that no unlicensed working can breathe inside it. Enough to keep four hundred men invisible on a mountainside. Enough"—she spread her hands, encompassing tents, wards, the held-flat silence of the air—"for all of this."

Sebastian stared at her. The rage had gone very quiet now, the way a river goes quiet just before it goes over the falls. "The ban," he said slowly. "The ban on magic. Twelve years. Executions. Families torn apart. Children taken for *singing in the rain*—"

"The ban is real," Ilsette said. "I enforce it. I believe in it, in its way—an ungoverned gift is a wildfire, and I have seen wildfires. But understand what it actually is, Sebastian. It is not protection. It is *monopoly*. The Order did not outlaw magic to save the city from it. The Order outlawed magic so that it would be the only thing in the world that had any. Every charm-seller's trinket burned, every hedge-witch drowned, every storm-caller exiled—and all the while, in vaults under the Citadel, the stones drink and the Wardens grow fat on what they yield. Vellamark doesn't live in a world without magic. Vellamark lives in a world with exactly one wizard, and the wizard is an institution."

The words landed in Sebastian like stones dropped down a well. He thought of his stall in the market, the little brass bells and luck-knots, the careful smallness of his performed life. He thought of the patrols, the questions, the neighbors who vanished. He had spent twelve years believing he'd been exiled from a frightened, virtuous city—that the ban was fear, and fear could be forgiven. Fear was human. Fear he could have made peace with, someday, in some version of himself that healed.

This was not fear. This was theft, dressed as virtue, enforced by fire.

"You knew," he said. His voice came out low and terrible and calm. "At the tribunal. You stood there and helped them exile me, and you knew the whole time that the covenant I broke was already gutted—that the Order had its hands in the stones, that the ban was a lie. You let them cast me into the dark for protecting the very thing they were robbing."

"I let them do what kept the Order stable," Ilsette said. "Yes."

"And Maera." The name came out of him like a blade leaving a wound. "Maera Voss. Your fellow gate-warden. Your friend. When she started asking where the stones went—when she found out what the vaults were for—you're the one who reported her, aren't you? You're why she ran. Why she hid for twelve years in a charmless city with a daughter she couldn't tell the truth to. Why she came back to the Gate alone, bleeding, to die in a stranger's stall."

Something crossed Ilsette's face then—the first crack in the quarry-stone, quick and quickly sealed. "Maera was warned," she said. "Repeatedly. She chose her path."

"She chose honesty."

"She chose *collapse*, Sebastian. Look at the Gate." Ilsette's composure returned, harder than before, and she swept an arm toward the high notch in the ridge, toward what lay beyond it. "You've seen it. You of all people have seen what the breach does when it runs unchecked—realms bleeding into each other, storms without shape, land that remembers being ocean. Left alone, it doesn't heal. It widens. In twenty years it takes the coast. In fifty, the lowlands. The Order's answer is to seal it permanently, with the stones, with enough bound power to close that door forever and throw away the key. One realm, one law, one quiet world. No more breaches. No more gate-wardens. No more oaths."

"And both realms starve," Sebastian said. "You know that. The covenant wasn't a wall, Ilsette—it was a *door*. Things passed through it that both sides needed. Weather. Seed-time. Memory. Dreams, if you want the old words. Seal it and the storm-realm forgets us and we forget it, and everything the old rites kept alive dies quietly in the dark, and Vellamark gets its clean gray forever." He stepped forward, crossbows be damned, and Ilsette's guards tensed but she raised two fingers and held them. "You don't want to seal the Gate. You want to own the only door between worlds, and a door that's locked is easier to own than a door that opens."

For a moment—just a moment—he thought he'd reached her. Something moved behind the pale eyes, some sediment of the woman who had once sat across his fire and argued theology until dawn, who had believed in the covenant once, fiercely, before ambition taught her that belief was a currency to be spent.

Then it sealed over, and she was the Master Warden again.

"It doesn't matter what I want," she said. "It hasn't for a long time. The Order wants the Gate sealed, the stones transferred to the Citadel, and the last two people alive who could reopen it either dead or in irons." She looked at Hazel. "Though I confess the girl complicates things. Maera's daughter. Half a bloodline I can't account for, carrying half an oath I can't read, standing on my mountain with storm-light in her eyes." She smiled thinly. "You've been teaching her signs, Sebastian. I can smell the warding-marks on her like cheap perfume. Do you know what she is? Have you told her?"

"I know what I am," Hazel said, and her voice rang clear across the overlook, and Sebastian heard the storm in it, faint and gathering, answering her pulse. "My mother's daughter. That's enough."

"For now," Ilsette said softly. "Bind them. Gently. The girl especially—unmarked flesh brings a better price at questioning than bruised."

The guards closed in. Sebastian fought—not because fighting would help, but because his body refused otherwise, twelve years of buried fury finally finding its target—and took a rifle-butt across the temple for it, and the world went to sparks and ringing gray. Through the haze he heard Hazel shouting his name, heard the snap of bindings, felt hands hauling him upright.

Ilsette appeared in front of him, close enough that he could see the fine lines at her eyes, the woman inside the monument.

"One kindness, since you asked none of me," she said quietly. "When we reach the Gate tomorrow, and the sealing begins, I'll have them hold your head up so you can watch. You should see it, Sebastian. You should see what your treason bought."

They dragged him down toward the cages, toward the grieving blue light, and the last thing he saw before they hooded him was Hazel's face over a guard's shoulder—furious, tearless, and utterly unbroken—and the last thing he heard was the stones in their cages, singing mourning into the held, stolen silence of the mountain.

* * *

The cell they put him in was iron and canvas, and it moved.

He understood, as the hours passed and the wagon swayed, that they were traveling—down off the overlook, along the pass floor, toward the Gate. Toward tomorrow. His head throbbed where the rifle-butt had caught him, his wrists were bound behind him with warded cord that drank at his skin like a leech, and through the canvas seams he could see the glow of the nearest covenant-stone, close enough now that its light lay on his knees like fallen sky.

It was humming. Very softly. And though every rational part of him knew it was impossible—caged, starved, warded, miles of held air between them—the humming found the shape of a rhythm he knew. An old cadence. Watch-cadence, the gate-wardens' walking song, the one Maera used to beat out with two knuckles on the Gate's stone lip when the night watches ran long.

*Four beats, rest. Four beats, rest.*

Sebastian closed his eyes and breathed, and let himself hope, for the first time since the crossbows rose out of the scree, that hope was not a cruelty.

The stones remembered. The stones were reaching. And somewhere in the column behind him, in another wagon, he would have bet his worthless life that Hazel was listening too.

Tomorrow they would reach the Gate. Tomorrow Ilsette would begin the sealing, with seven stolen anchors and an army's worth of hoarded power, and unless something impossible happened, both realms would go dark forever while he watched from the front row, bound and powerless and *invited*.

But tonight, in a rolling cage lit by stolen light, an imprisoned stone was singing his partner's song.

Sebastian pressed his forehead against the cold iron lattice, matched his breathing to the four-beat rhythm, and began, silently, to plan.
