## 15. Half an Oath

The stones came home in a canvas sack that smelled of ash and old blood.

Hazel carried it herself. She would not let Sebastian touch it, would not let Corvin's ghost be handed off to anyone else's arms, even though her shoulders burned and the strap cut a groove into her collarbone that she could feel with every step up the last slope of the Ash Pass. Seven stones, each the size of a fist, each humming faintly against her hip like trapped bees. She had counted them four times since the breaking-out. Four times they had all been there. She counted them again anyway.

Sebastian walked ahead of her, and he was different now—she could see it even if he never spoke of it. The storm-gift he had spent in the escape had not come back to him. When he raised his hand to shield his eyes from the pass's sulfur haze, there was no shimmer under his skin, no low thrum answering him from the clouds. He moved like a man walking through a house where every piece of furniture had been removed overnight. Familiar rooms, empty of everything that made them home.

He didn't complain. He didn't mourn out loud. That was somehow worse.

The wind on the pass carried grit and the smell of hot stone, and it moaned through the ribcages of the dead pilgrims they'd passed two days ago—the ones whose remains littered the trail like fallen leaves, some bleached white as bone, some still wearing the rusted honor-chains of Wardens who had died reaching for power they were never meant to hold. Hazel had made herself look at each one. Her mother had died for this road too, somewhere, sometime, in a year Hazel couldn't name because no one had ever told her. She felt their company like a weight of witnesses.

And above them all, at the top of the world, the Hollow Gate waited.

She heard it before she saw it. A sound like a bell struck underwater, deep and wrong, repeating at intervals that grew shorter the higher they climbed. *Doom. Doom. Doom.* Not thunder. Thunder had a voice. This was a heartbeat with an arrhythmia, a covenant counting down the hours of its own life.

Then the trail bent around a shoulder of black rock, and there it was.

Two pillars, each taller than Vellamark's bell tower, standing on a shelf of stone above a chasm that dropped away into cloud. They had been carved once—she could see the ghosts of the carving, spirals and storm-sigils worn smooth by centuries of weather—but now they were cracked from crown to base, and between them hung what remained of the Gate itself: a curtain of light, or something like light, torn down the middle like a sheet ripped in half by a furious hand. Through the tear she could see nothing and everything—a shimmering absence that hurt to look at directly, the way the sun hurts, the way grief hurts.

The air around it moved. It breathed. In, out, in, out, each breath pulling faint warmth from the human side and pushing back cold that smelled of ozone and rain-that-hadn't-fallen-in-twelve-years.

"We're here," Sebastian said quietly, and his voice cracked on the second word, and Hazel understood that for him this was not a place. This was the wound he had carried out of these mountains twelve years ago and had never once let close enough to bleed.

They camped that night on the shelf below the pillars, in the lee of a fallen lintel carved with words neither of them could fully read. Sebastian built no fire—"the light draws eyes," he said, though Hazel suspected it was more than that; fire was a human thing, and this place belonged to something older—and they ate cold travel-bread and harder cheese while the Gate's broken heartbeat slowed through the hours of dark. *Doom. ... Doom. ... Doom.* Slower. Sicker. Like a heart deciding, hour by hour, whether it wanted to keep beating at all.

Hazel did not sleep. She sat with the sack of stones in her lap and laid them out one by one on the flat stone beside her, arranging them in the pattern Sebastian had described: three along the eastern pillar's root, three along the western, and the seventh—the keystone, the one Corvin had died buying them time to reach—at the center, where the two halves of the torn light almost touched.

Each stone was different. One was veined with silver that moved when she wasn't looking. One was warm as a held hand. One was so cold it burned her fingers through her gloves. And the keystone—Corvin's stone, the one he'd called "the ugly one" with his last crooked smile—was plain gray granite, unremarkable, except that when Hazel pressed her palm flat against it she felt something answer. Not a sound. A pressure. Like pressing her ear to the chest of someone sleeping and feeling, beneath the quiet, the enormous machinery of a life going on without her.

*You're real,* she thought at it. *Whatever you are. You're real.*

The stone said nothing, but the pressure steadied, and stayed.

* * *

Dawn came gray and windless, which Sebastian said was itself an omen. "Storm-realm weather," he murmured, watching the colorless sky. "It leans close when the Gate is dying. It wants to see."

They prepared the rite through the morning, and Sebastian taught it to her the way he seemed to teach everything: twice fast, once slow, then in silence, until the knowledge lived in her hands and not just her head.

"Place the stones," he said. "Root them. Each one goes into the earth at the pillar's foot—you'll have to dig; the ground is volcanic glass under the ash, break it with the pry-bar. Then stand between the pillars, facing the tear. Speak the opening verse. I'll speak the answering verse. The stones will carry our voices down into the covenant-roots, and if the roots still live—if anything down there still listens—they'll answer."

"And then?"

"And then," he said, and paused, and Hazel watched him weigh how much truth to give her, "then we find out whether twelve years of silence killed it or only wounded it."

He showed her the opening verse. It was written in the old tongue, the storm-tongue, the language her mother must have spoken every day of her life at this post and had never once taught her daughter—not a word, not a lullaby, not a name. Hazel had thought about that a great deal on the road north. She had decided, somewhere past the salt village, that her mother had kept the language from her the way you keep a knife from a child: not because the child was unworthy of it, but because the world the knife belonged to had been declared contraband, and love sometimes looks like amputation.

Now she stood at the foot of the eastern pillar with the verse written on a strip of oilcloth in Sebastian's careful hand, and she sounded it out syllable by syllable like a child learning letters, and the syllables came out of her mouth wrong. Flat. Toneless. Words with the music boiled out of them. She knew they were wrong. She could hear them being wrong. But she had no way to hear what right sounded like, because the only person who could have shown her was eleven years dead, and the man who might have taught her had spent twelve years pretending the language didn't exist so that the Wardens wouldn't feed him to it.

"You're doing fine," Sebastian said, and it was a kind lie and they both knew it.

"I'm doing badly," Hazel said. "Teach me faster."

"There is no faster." He rubbed the back of his neck, where an old scar rode like a rope-burn. "There's only true or false, and right now everything you say is false, and the Gate will know it, and—"

"And what? What happens if the Gate knows it?"

He looked at her for a long moment, and then he told her, because at the top of the world there was nowhere left to hide the truth: "Then it shudders. And every shudder widens the tear. We get one attempt, maybe two, before the whole structure lets go. If it lets go with us standing inside its threshold..." He exhaled. "I've seen a Gate fail, Hazel. Once. From the outside. It doesn't explode. It *inhales*. Everything within reach gets pulled through into a realm that has no floor and no sky and no mercy, and then the breach seals itself over the hole it made, neat as scar tissue, and no one who went through ever comes back, because there's no longer any door to come back through."

The wind picked up, tasting of iron. Below them the chasm breathed its slow cold breath.

"So we don't fail," Hazel said.

"That's the plan," Sebastian said dryly. "Plans are wonderful things. I've had several."

* * *

They began at noon, when the light fell straight down into the chasm and the tear in the Gate caught it and threw it back doubled, so that for a few minutes the ruined pillars looked almost whole, almost crowned, almost what they had been when her mother had stood between them and called herself their warden.

Hazel placed the last stone. Dug its bed with the pry-bar, set it, packed the ash around it, pressed both palms down on top until she felt it root—felt it *drink*, there was no other word, felt the ground take it the way ground takes rain after drought. Six stones rooted. The keystone waited in her arms, warm now, warmer than it had any right to be, warm like something alive that had been waiting a long time for someone to pick it up.

She carried it between the pillars.

Standing in the threshold changed her breathing. She hadn't expected that. The air was thicker there, charged, and every inhalation felt like drinking lightning very slowly, and her heartbeat picked up a second pulse slightly out of time with the first, and she realized with a lurch that the second pulse was coming from *outside her body*—from the stones, from the roots, from whatever vast slow thing slept beneath the mountain and was now, for the first time in twelve years, aware of her.

It knew she was there. She felt it know.

"Hold the keystone against your sternum," Sebastian said from just behind her, his voice tight. "Both hands. Don't let go, no matter what you feel. Begin the verse."

She began.

The first line came out of her mouth and died in the air like smoke. She knew it died because she could feel the Gate flinch—a tiny contraction of the torn light, a wince. The second line she got half-right, one phrase landing with a ghost of resonance before the rest crumbled, and the flinch became a tremor, and loose ash skittered off the pillars and rained down around them.

Keep going. Her mother's voice, imagined, or remembered, or invented—she couldn't tell anymore, and had stopped needing to. Keep going. You started it. Finish it.

Third line. Fourth. Her voice shook. The old tongue fought her—it was a language built for lungs larger than hers, for throats that had grown up inside it, and every syllable she mispronounced was not a small error but a broken promise, a word offered to the covenant in a shape it could not recognize. She felt each failure land. She felt the thing beneath the mountain receive her words, test them, find them counterfeit, and withdraw a little further each time, like a dog that keeps approaching a stranger who keeps raising a hand.

By the seventh line the Gate was trembling steadily. By the ninth, the tear had visibly widened—a handspan, maybe more, the torn edges of light curling back on themselves like paper burning. Loose stone cracked off the western pillar and plunged into the chasm and the sound of it falling went on far too long.

Sebastian stepped forward. "Stop," he said. "Hazel. Stop."

But she was in the ninth line, and stopping mid-oath was worse than failing at the end—that much he had taught her, that much was true in every tradition she'd ever heard of—so she didn't stop. She drove through the tenth line with her voice cracking and her hands white-knuckled on the keystone, and she reached the closing phrase, the one that meant *let the door remember itself*, and she said it, and she said it *wrong*, tone-flat, human-flat, a foreigner's accent in a house that had never invited foreigners—

—and the Gate answered.

Not with acceptance. With rejection. A single massive convulsion, the whole threshold contracting like a throat refusing poison, and the shockwave of it knocked Sebastian off his feet and sent Hazel staggering back against the eastern pillar with the keystone grinding against her breastbone, and for three full seconds the tear in the light gaped wide enough to walk through, and through it she saw—

She saw a country. No sky, no sun, but light everywhere, sourceless, and shapes moving in it, vast and slow and sorrowful, and one of the shapes turned toward her, and it had no face she could hold onto but it *looked* at her, and what she felt from it was not anger.

It was hunger. Enormous, patient, starving hunger. Twelve years of a door closed, twelve years of a covenant severed, twelve years of a realm cut off from the half of itself that lived in human blood and human weather—and it looked at her standing in the wreckage of the doorway with a counterfeit oath in her mouth, and it *ached*.

Then the convulsion finished, and the tear snapped narrower than it had been before—smaller, tighter, the edges fused a little, scarring—and the cold breath from the chasm stopped entirely, and the silence that followed was the worst sound of the entire journey, because it was the silence of something giving up.

"It's worse," Sebastian said from the ground, hoarse. He hauled himself upright against the fallen lintel and looked at the Gate with the expression of a physician reading a chart he already knew the end of. "The tear's contracted. Scarred. That's—it's trying to heal itself shut. If it seals completely, that's it. Both realms starve. Slowly, quietly, permanently, and no one in Vellamark will even know why the rain never comes."

Hazel slid down the pillar until she was sitting in the ash. The keystone lay in her lap, cool again, spent. Her hands were shaking. Her voice was gone—literally gone, scraped down to a raw whisper—and what was left of it kept replaying the moment of rejection, the flinch, the withdrawal, the ache on the other side of the tear that she had caused by coming to a door she couldn't open.

"I failed," she whispered. "I stood in my mother's doorway and I failed."

"No." Sebastian crossed the shelf and knelt in front of her, and took her shaking hands in his steady ones, and for once—for the first time since the market stall, since the dying stranger, since the beginning of all of it—he did not correct her, or comfort her, or manage her. He simply told the truth. "You didn't fail, Hazel. You learned. There's a difference, and the difference is this: a failure means the door was closed to you. What happened here means the door was closed to *that voice*. To the half-trained, half-heard, borrowed voice of a woman who was never given her own language." His grip tightened. "The covenant didn't reject *you*. It rejected the translation."

"How do you know that?" It came out as accusation and plea at once.

"Because of what you saw through the tear." He turned her wrist over, gently, and pointed to the oath-mark she had carried all her life without understanding—the mark her mother had left on her skin, the twin of the one the dying stranger had borne. "That mark isn't decoration. It's a signature. Your mother wrote you into the covenant before you could walk. The storm-realm has known your name for twenty-three years. It wasn't looking at a stranger up there, Hazel. It was looking at family, standing behind a locked door, speaking in a voice it couldn't verify." He let go of her wrists and straightened. "Family behind a locked door. Think about what that feels like. Think about what it does."

Hazel thought about it. She thought about her mother keeping the language from her, keeping the post from her, keeping the whole hidden half of her inheritance sealed in silence for two decades—and she thought about the shape turning toward her through the tear, faceless and starving, and she understood, finally, with her whole body, that she had been looking at a mirror.

Both of them locked out. Both of them aching. Both of them unable to prove who they were to the only door that mattered.

"The voice," she whispered. "I need the voice. My own voice. The one it can verify."

"Yes."

"And I don't have it. Because no one taught it to me. Because teaching it was illegal, and loving me apparently looked like letting me go." Her whisper broke. "So how, Sebastian? How does a woman learn her own name's language in one afternoon, at a door that's sealing itself shut as we sit here talking about it?"

Sebastian looked up at the scarred tear in the light, and then at the keystone in her lap, and then at the western horizon, where the sky had begun to gather itself into a colorless, sourceless, storm-realm gray—and something in his face shifted, some calculation finishing, some terrible arithmetic completing itself.

"There's one way," he said quietly. "One way I know of. It requires a speaker of both bloods—someone the covenant can verify from either side. Someone whose voice carries in both tongues without translation." He paused, and the pause was heavy, and Hazel felt the weight of what was coming before he said it. "The rite demands it. The old texts are absolute. One blood alone cannot hold the door. It was designed that way on purpose, so that no single realm could ever own the crossing."

"But I'm—"

"You're what the texts call *both-blooded*, yes. Storm in your veins from your mother, human in your bones from your father—or the other way around; the texts don't care which parent gave which. Either way, you qualify. You're the first person to stand in that threshold in twelve years who qualifies." He ran a hand through his gray-streaked hair, and when he spoke again his voice had dropped into something careful and grim. "Which means the rite *can* work. Today. With what we have. But only if the voice it hears is yours—whole, verified, unmistakable. And the only way to make your voice unmistakable to a dying covenant in the next few hours..."

He stopped. He actually stopped, the way men stop when the next sentence costs more than they've agreed to pay.

"...is if the other half of the old rite stands with you. The answering verse. The storm-side anchor. Someone to hold the door's near edge while you claim the far one." His jaw worked. "Twelve years ago, that was your mother's role and mine. She spoke the opening. I spoke the answer. Two voices, two bloods, one door." He looked at his own empty hands—empty now, truly empty, the storm-gift spent in a smuggler's warehouse covering her escape. "I can't anchor you anymore, Hazel. Whatever's left of me, the Gate won't hear it. I'm a human voice proposing a human verse to a storm-door, and it will reject me exactly the way it rejected you. Worse. Faster."

"Then who—"

And then the sky answered before he could.

Over the western rim of the pass, the colorless gray thickened, gathered, descended—a wall of storm-realm weather rolling toward the shelf with a deliberation that had nothing natural in it, moving against the wind, descending when storms should rise. And inside that gray, Hazel began to hear it: not thunder, not yet, but voices. Many voices, layered, distant, speaking in the old tongue with a fluency that made her halting verses sound like what they were—the stammerings of a foundling at a family door.

Something was coming through the failing Gate. Or toward it. Something that had waited twelve years on the far side of the scar, and had seen her face through the tear, and had not forgotten.

Sebastian heard it too. He went still, then went quiet, then went pale, and the last thing he said before the first of the voices resolved close enough to have a shape was also the simplest, and it landed on Hazel like a stone dropped into still water:

"They're coming to the door," he said. "And we have about an hour to decide whether that's salvation, or the end of everything Ilsette hasn't managed to destroy yet."
