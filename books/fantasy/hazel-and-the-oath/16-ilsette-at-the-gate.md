## 16. Ilsette at the Gate

The hour gave them forty minutes.

Sebastian knew it the way he knew weather—not by reasoning but by the pressure behind his eyes, the taste of iron on his back teeth, the way the storm-gift in his chest turned like a compass needle toward the pass they had climbed out of. Something was coming up the Ash Pass with a purpose that did not need to hurry. Purpose never did.

"Hazel." He kept his voice level, because she was still staring at the Gate and her face held the particular blankness of someone listening to a sound no one else could hear. "Hazel. Look at me."

She looked. Her eyes were wrong—storm-grey where they should have been brown, lit from somewhere behind the iris—and for one vertiginous moment he saw her mother in them so completely that his knees nearly went. Maerin had worn that same look the night the covenant broke, standing in this same scar of black glass and ash, hearing the same far-off voices.

"We have to move," he said. "Not away. *Up.* The overlook above the western arch—if we're going to meet what's coming, we meet it from ground we choose."

"The voices—"

"Are the storm-realm pressing against a door that barely holds. And whatever else is coming is coming through the pass, on foot, which means Wardens, which means Ilsette." He was already gathering their gear: the stolen covenant-stones wrapped in Hazel's cloak, the waterskins, the coil of rope he'd taken from Corvin's pack because Corvin would not need it anymore. His hands did not shake. He noticed that, distantly, the way you notice your own heartbeat in a quiet room. Twelve years ago his hands had shaken for a week after the Gate broke. Grief had made him clumsy then. Now it only made him precise.

Hazel shouldered her half of the stones without argument. That was the thing about her that undid him most—not her power, not her blood, but her willingness. She had been a charm-seller's customer three weeks ago. A girl who bought luck-knots and didn't believe in them. And now she climbed a volcanic pass carrying half a broken covenant because her mother had died pointing the way.

They climbed.

* * *

The overlook was a shelf of basalt above the western arch of the Hollow Gate, close enough to feel the heat of the deep fissures breathing through the rock, high enough to see the whole wound of it laid out below. The Gate had been an arch once—two pillars of veined white stone and a span between them that had hummed like a struck bell, day and night, for as long as either realm had records. Now the right pillar stood. The left had sheared off at shoulder height, its stump fused into slag, and the space between them was not empty but *thin*—a shimmer like heat-haze over a road in summer, except the haze ran vertical, and through it, sometimes, when the wind came from the north, you could see colors that did not belong to any sky.

It was worse than Sebastian remembered. Worse than his nightmares, which at least had the decency to blur. He could see the fraying now, the edges of the thin place unraveling thread by thread, and beyond it—not a landscape, exactly, but a *pressure*, a vastness leaning against the tear the way floodwater leans against a door.

And under all of it, faint as a pulse felt through a wrist, the hum. Still there. Still singing. Twelve years of neglect and it had not stopped singing.

"It's beautiful," Hazel said quietly beside him. It wasn't a question or a wonder. It was grief wearing wonder's clothes.

"It was," Sebastian said. "It is. Both things are true."

He set the stones down between them, seven lumps of grey-white rock that looked like nothing—quarry leavings, ballast, trash. Each one weighed as much as a sleeping child. Each one held a fragment of the binding-song, and together, if the rite worked, if the speaker was right, they would give the Gate back its voice.

If the speaker was right. He glanced at Hazel and looked away again quickly, because hope was a weight too, and he couldn't carry both it and the stones.

Then the needle behind his eyes swung hard, and he forgot hope entirely.

"They're here," he said.

They came out of the ash-fog on the eastern approach in a column of thirty, maybe forty—Wardens in their slate-grey field coats, lanterns hooded, boots crunching the volcanic glass in unison like a single animal walking. At their center moved a palanquin of sorts, a litter carried by six, hung with grey silk, and Sebastian's stomach dropped through the mountain before the litter even stopped, because he knew that silk. He had helped choose that silk, twelve years ago, from a merchant's stall in Vellamark, laughing, because Ilsette had said a woman in her position ought to suffer one indulgence and it might as well be beautiful.

Master Warden Ilsette Vane stepped down onto the ash of the Ash Pass like a woman stepping into her own garden.

She had not aged the way he had. That was the first obscenity of it. His hair had gone iron at the temples; hers was still black, still cut sharp as a verdict. She wore the Master's chain now—heavy links of pale metal that he did not recognize, that he suspected very much were not silver—and she walked ahead of her soldiers without waiting for them, straight across the glass, straight to the foot of the standing pillar, and stopped.

And looked up. Not at the Gate. At the overlook.

"I know you're there, Sebastian," she called, and her voice carried the way it always had, clean and cold and reaching, a voice built for courtrooms and betrayals. "You always did pick the dramatic ground. Come down. I won't waste arrows on you. Arrows are for people who matter less."

Beside him, Hazel had gone rigid. "That's her," she breathed. "That's the one who—"

"Yes." He rose. His legs felt fifty years old. "Stay here with the stones. If I raise my left hand, run east. Don't argue. Don't wait. *Run.*"

"Sebastian—"

"*Please.*" It came out rawer than he intended, and she heard it, and something passed over her face—the same stubbornness he'd been fighting since Vellamark—but she nodded, once, and crouched by the stones with her hand flat on them, and let him go.

He climbed down the switchback with his heart hammering a rhythm he hadn't felt since the last time he'd stood in front of this woman. Twelve years. Twelve years of rehearsing this conversation in the dark, in cheap rooms, in the space between customers at his stall—everything he would say to her, the accusations he would sharpen, the justice he would finally demand. And now, with her actual face in front of him, every rehearsed word evaporated, and what remained was just a tired man in a stolen coat walking across hot glass toward the person who had ended his life.

"You look terrible," Ilsette said, when he was ten paces away. She hadn't moved. Her guards had fanned out in a crescent behind her, crossbows leveled, and she hadn't even glanced at them. All her attention was on him, and it was the worst thing he'd felt in years, being seen by those eyes again. "Exile agrees with no one. You've got ash in your hair."

"You look exactly the same," he said. "Which tells me everything about where the Wardens' money goes."

"Some of it." She smiled, and it was the old smile, the one that had made young Sebastian Okafor trust her with his life and his oaths and the safety of the Gate itself. "I missed you, Seb. Isn't that pathetic? Twelve years, and I still walk past charm stalls and check for your terrible handwriting on the price signs."

"Don't." His voice cracked on it. He hated that. "Don't do that. Not here. Not on this ground."

"Where else?" She spread her hands, and the Master's chain caught the light of the fissures and threw it back red. "This is where we met, in a manner of speaking. This is where you broke us. So yes—I'll be sentimental here, if it's all the same to you."

"I didn't break anything." The words came out of him with twelve years of pressure behind them, and he let them, because he had promised himself that whatever happened today, he would not swallow them again. "The breach was already open, Ilsette. The night it happened—you know it was. You were *there*. The left pillar was failing, the storm-side was panicking, and there was a child in the crossing-chamber who was going to die when the seal collapsed. I chose the child. I would choose the child again. Every time, in every world, I choose the child."

"And the Gate fell because of it." No anger in her voice. That was the horror of it—there had never been anger. Only arithmetic. "One child against the door between worlds. You did the math wrong, Seb. You always did the math with your heart instead of your head."

"The math was never yours to do!"

"No," she agreed, mildly. "It was the council's. And the council decided, after, that doors between worlds are how cities burn. So we closed them. All of them. We banned the gift, we burned the registers, we put out the word that magic was plague, and Vellamark has had twelve years of peace. Twelve years of no storms, no breaches, no children dying in crossing-chambers." She tilted her head. "Has it been so unbearable? Selling charms?"

Something in Sebastian went very still and very cold, the way air goes before lightning.

"You don't know," he said slowly. "You actually don't know. You sealed the crossings, you banned the gift, and you never once asked what the Gate *was for*."

"I know what it was for. Passage. Trade. The old kingdoms' sentimentality—"

"It was a *lung*." His voice rose, and the ash around their feet stirred, and several crossbows shifted, and he did not care. "The two realms breathe each other, Ilsette. That's what the covenant is—that's what it was always for, before passage, before trade, before anyone thought to make it political. The storm-realm isn't a country. It's the other half of the sky. The rain, the growing-season, the charge in the soil—all of it cycles *through* the Gate. Twelve years you've had it shut, and you've been living on the last breath in the room. Why do you think the rains stopped? Why do you think the wells drop a hand-span every year? Why do you think the crops come in thinner, the winters bite harder, the whole city feels like it's holding its breath?" He took a step toward her, and this time the crossbows did not matter at all. "You didn't ban a plague. You strangled a lung and called the silence peace."

For the first time, something flickered behind Ilsette's eyes. Doubt, maybe. Or the memory of doubt, long buried. "The city is fine," she said.

"The city is *dying*, slowly enough that no one notices until it's done. And it isn't only ours." He gestured up at the shimmering tear, at the vastness pressing through it. "You think the storm-realm doesn't starve too? They lost their bridge to the world that anchors them. Everything they are leaks out through that hole a little more every year. Sealing it permanently—your plan, I'm guessing, since you brought forty soldiers and a litter fit for a coronation—doesn't save anything. It kills both realms. Slowly, politely, with excellent paperwork. But dead is dead."

Ilsette was quiet for a long moment. The wind moved the grey silk of her litter. Somewhere above, Sebastian knew, Hazel was listening to every word, and the stones sat in their cloak like seven sleeping hearts.

"You're lying," Ilsette said at last, but softly, and he heard the crack in it. "Or you're wrong. Or—" She stopped herself. Closed her eyes. When she opened them, the doubt was gone, walled over, and what looked out was the arithmetic again, cold and complete. "Or it's true, and it doesn't matter. Do you want to know why I really came, Seb? Not to seal it. Sealing is the second task."

She turned and gestured, and two soldiers came forward bearing a case of black lacquer, and opened it, and inside, on velvet, lay a stone that made Sebastian's vision whiten at the edges. White-veined. Gate-stone. But not a fragment—a *keystone*, the size of a skull, humming faintly with a song he recognized the way you recognize your mother's voice.

"There are eleven of these," Ilsette said. "We've spent twelve years collecting them—from ruins, from dead wardens' caches, from the pockets of exiles who thought they'd hidden them well." Her eyes flicked up, briefly, toward the overlook, and Sebastian's blood froze. "The Gate can be rebuilt, Seb. Not restored—rebuilt. Under new terms. With the keystones in Warden hands, the door opens only when we say, passes only whom we allow, breathes only as much as we permit. Magic returns to Vellamark—not free, not wild, not plague. *Managed.* Rationed like grain, guarded like gold. The storms come back because we sell them back to the sky at a price." She smiled again, and this time it reached her eyes, and it was terrible. "You keep talking about lungs and breathing. I'm offering the city a heart it can hold in its fist."

"That's not a heart," Sebastian whispered. "That's a cage with a pump in it. And the storm-realm will never agree—"

"The storm-realm," Ilsette said, "is leaking out through a hole in the world and has been begging at our door for twelve years. Beggars don't negotiate. They accept terms." She lifted the keystone from its velvet, and the hum deepened, and the tear in the Gate shuddered visibly, threads of light whipping loose along its edges. "I gave you a chance just now, whether you noticed or not. I offered you the sentimental version—come back, stand beside me, help me build it. Your exile ends. Your name is restored. You'd be good at this, Seb. You always understood the machinery better than anyone."

The offer hung in the ash-thick air. Behind him, forty crossbows. Above him, a girl of both realms and seven fragments of a broken song. Before him, the woman who had taught him half of everything he knew and burned the other half to keep her seat.

Twelve years ago, he realized, he would have said yes. Not from cowardice—from love. That was the poison of it. She had always known exactly where to aim.

"Ilsette," he said, and his voice was steady now, steadier than it had been in twelve years, because the answer had been growing in him all this time like a callus over a wound. "When I saved Hazel—when I broke the terms and pulled that child out of the crossing-chamber—you testified that I did it for ambition. For power. You stood in front of the council and told them the storm-caller Sebastian Okafor wanted the Gate for himself." He shook his head slowly. "I've spent twelve years wondering how you could say it. How you could look me in the eye and lie. And standing here now, watching you hold that keystone like a crown—it wasn't a lie to you, was it? You testified what you would have done. You put your own heart in my chest and convicted me for it."

Her face did not change. But her hand, on the keystone, tightened until the knuckles went white, and that was answer enough.

"So no," he said. "I decline. I decline the pardon, the partnership, the managed heart, all of it. You want to rebuild the Gate as a lockbox. But the Gate was never ours to own, Ilsette. It was ours to *keep*—both realms', together, that was the whole covenant, the whole point, the entire meaning of the word *oath*—and the moment one side grabs the key, it stops being a gate and starts being a wound. You're not offering Vellamark a heart. You're offering it a tumor, and calling the growth prosperity."

Silence. The fissures breathed. Far above, the tear in the Gate flickered, and through it, for half a heartbeat, Sebastian saw—or dreamed he saw—a shape like a figure standing on the far side, tall and robed in moving light, watching them with the patience of weather.

Ilsette followed his gaze upward. And when she spoke again, all the warmth was gone, and what remained was the Master Warden entire, the arithmetic given flesh.

"Take him," she said. "Gently, if he allows it. And send four men up to the overlook. There's a girl up there with a bag of stones that belong to the Office now."

The crossbows came up. Sebastian closed his eyes—

—and the world *cracked*.

Not a sound. A pressure. The air over the Ash Pass compressed like the instant before a strike, and every hair on Sebastian's arms stood, and the storm-gift in his chest—his last ember of it, the dregs he'd been hoarding for the rite—turned over and *answered*, involuntary, joyful, a dog hearing its name.

Above the overlook, the sky, which had been ash-grey and dead for twelve years, split open with a single silent flash of blue-white light. No thunder followed. Just the light, and the smell of ozone rolling down the pass like a tide, and every soldier on the glass stumbling back a step, and Ilsette's head snapping up, her composure cracking for the first time in twelve years into something very like fear.

Because that lightning had not come from any cloud. There were no clouds. It had come from *between*—from the girl standing at the edge of the overlook with her hand on the covenant-stones and her eyes full of storm, answering her blood's oldest call.

"Oh," Sebastian said softly, to himself, to the sky, to the woman who had once been his friend. "Oh, Ilsette. You should have taken my deal."

He raised his left hand.

And Hazel, at the edge of the overlook, wrapped in light that was not entirely hers, turned toward the shimmering tear in the Gate—and stepped toward it—as the storm-realm, on the far side of twelve broken years, began at last to speak.
