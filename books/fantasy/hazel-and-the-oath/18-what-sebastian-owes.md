## 18. What Sebastian Owes

The Gate was dying, and it sounded like a choir forgetting its own song.

Sebastian stood at the threshold's edge with the wind tearing at his coat and the stolen covenant-stones humming in their circle around him, and he listened to the sound that had haunted twelve years of dreams: the Hollow Gate unraveling. It was not thunder. Thunder would have been merciful. This was the space between notes, the silence where harmony used to live—a vast slow groan of two worlds pulling apart at the seam, and every second it widened, something on both sides starved.

Behind him, Hazel knelt among the stones with her mother's map spread across her knees, her lips moving through the oath in the old tongue. Her voice kept catching. Not from fear—from *incompleteness*, the way a river catches where the bed has cracked. She had half the words. Half the blood. The Gate wanted more than either.

*It wants both,* Sebastian thought. *And it will take them out of her if she isn't given time to find them.*

He looked down at his own hands.

They were shaking. They had been shaking since Vellamark, though he'd hidden it well—hidden it the way he'd hidden everything for twelve years, behind charm-seller's smiles and trinkets that did nothing and a life so small no one bothered to look twice at it. But here, at the seam of the world, there was nothing left to hide behind. The storm under his skin rose toward the surface like a drowning man toward light, and he could feel how little of it remained. A guttering lamp. A coin purse nearly empty.

Enough. He made himself believe it. Enough for one thing done properly.

"Hazel." His voice came out rougher than he intended. "Stop."

She looked up. Wind whipped her dark hair across her face—the same dark hair her mother had pushed out of her eyes a thousand times, he'd wager, in a life Sebastian had only ever seen the edges of. Her eyes were storm-grey. They had always been storm-grey. He had told himself for years that it meant nothing.

"I can't get the cadence," she said. "The words are right but they won't—they won't *take*. The Gate keeps slipping away from me."

"Because you're fighting it alone." He crouched beside her, knees cracking, and took the map gently from her hands. Ilsette's hunters were coming; he could feel them the way he once felt weather, a pressure in the old scar tissue of his gift. Minutes, perhaps. No more. "Listen to me. The oath isn't a recitation. Your mother tried to tell me that once and I didn't listen, and I've regretted it every day since. It's a *conversation*. You speak, and the storm answers, and you hold the door open between your voice and its answer. That holding—that's the warden's work. That's what I'm for."

"You said your gift is almost gone."

"It is." He smiled, and found, to his own surprise, that the smile didn't hurt. "Which is why I'm going to spend all of it at once. On you. On this."

Understanding dawned in her face, and she recoiled from it as if from a blow. "No. Sebastian—no. You'll burn out. You told me what that means. You'll never—"

"Call storms again? Charm rain out of a clear sky? Feel the lightning walking the ridge of the world?" He laughed softly. "Hazel, I haven't been able to do any of those things properly in twelve years. What I have left is a candle-end. And a candle-end spent lighting a hearth is not wasted. It's finally *home*."

* * *

He had owed a debt since the night the Gate first broke, though he had spent twelve years pretending the debt ran the other way—that the covenant had failed *him*, that exile was a wound dealt by others. Standing here now, with the wind screaming through the hollow arch and Hazel's grey eyes fixed on him, he could no longer pretend.

Here was the truth he carried: the night the breach opened, there had been time to seal it or to save the child screaming on the human side of the threshold. Not both. He had chosen the child. He had chosen Hazel, three years old and crying for a mother she couldn't see, and the Gate had torn wider while he carried her to safety, and the realms had begun their long starvation, and his people had put the blame on his shoulders because someone's shoulders had to bear it.

He had never regretted choosing her. He had only ever regretted believing, afterward, that the choice made him worthless. That was the exile he had built for himself, brick by brick, in a charmless city, selling nothing-charms to nothing-people. The Wardens' sentence had merely given it walls.

Now the debt came due, and he found he was glad.

"Hold out your hands," he said.

Hazel hesitated—and then, because she was her mother's daughter, she did not argue further. She set her palms in his. Her hands were cold; the Gate was drinking the warmth out of everything near it. Under her skin he felt the other blood answering, faint and wild, a second pulse out of rhythm with her heart.

"When I let go," he said, "you'll have everything I have left. It will feel like standing in a river. Don't fight the current. Speak the oath into it. Let it carry the words where they need to go."

"And you?"

"I'll be holding the door."

* * *

He reached inward for the last of his gift the way an old man reaches for a name on the tip of his tongue—and found it waiting, small and bright and *his*, the final ember of the storm that had lived in him since birth. Twelve years of rationing it. Twelve years of sips instead of drinks, of hiding its hum beneath his skin when Warden patrols passed his stall, of being a storm-caller who dared not call so much as a breeze.

He stopped rationing.

He opened himself the way he had not opened since he was young and arrogant and whole, and the ember roared up through him—not large, never large again, but *all of it*, every scrap, every hoarded fragment—and he poured it through his hands into Hazel like a man pouring out the last water of a dry country into another traveler's cup.

The sensation was indescribable and then, abruptly, describable: it was *lighter*. With every thread of power that left him, something heavy went with it. The weight of Vellamark's gray skies. The weight of the stall, the trinkets, the performed smallness. The weight of the night he'd run from the Gate with a child in his arms and shame in his mouth. All of it draining out through his palms, and underneath the draining, astonishingly, *relief*—the relief of a man setting down a load he had forgotten he was carrying because he had carried it so long it had grown into his spine.

Hazel gasped. Light moved under her skin like fish under ice. The stones in their circle flared, and the Gate's dying groan changed pitch—deepened, steadied, became something closer to a held breath.

"The door is open," Sebastian said, and his voice sounded far away to him, thin as paper. "Hold it. Hold it and *speak*, Hazel. Speak like the storm is listening—because it is. It has always been listening. It was listening the day you were born."

She rose. The wind bent around her, not against her. And she began to speak.

* * *

Sebastian braced himself against the arch of the Hollow Gate and held.

There was no magic left in him to do it with. That was the strange, clean truth of it—he had spent everything, down to the dregs, and what remained was only a man: sixty-one years old, bad knee, hands scarred from a lifetime of weather-work, leaning his whole mortal weight against a threshold between worlds. Flesh holding a door. Nothing more.

But the Gate recognized him anyway. Old stone remembered old hands. As Hazel's voice climbed through the oath's first movements—her mother's half, spoken now with a mother's fire in it—the threshold steadied under Sebastian's grip, and he felt the two realms lean toward each other through him like exhausted travelers leaning on the same wall.

He thought of his mother, who had taught him the first warding sign with flour still on her fingers. He thought of the salt village, of ordinary people leaving bowls of rainwater on their windowsills for a sky that never answered. He thought of Corvin, dead on the ash road with a smuggler's grin and a debt repaid, and he thought, *I'll see you on the other side of this, old friend, or I won't see anything at all, and either way we're square.*

The pain came then, as he knew it would—not the burning of power spent but the ache of its absence, a phantom-limb grief spreading through him where the storm had lived. He welcomed it. Grief was the tax on love, and he had loved the storm his whole life, even in the years he'd been forbidden to touch it. Better this ache than the gray numbness of Vellamark. Better to be emptied by giving than hollowed by hiding.

Through the widening doorway he glimpsed it—the storm-realm, his homeland, unchanged and starving: the lightning-fields gone dim, the thunder-herds moving listlessly across plains the color of old pewter. His people stood at their side of the threshold, drawn by the sound of Hazel's voice, and one of them—an elder with white braids and a face he knew—raised a hand. Not in greeting. In *recognition*. In acknowledgment that whatever else he had broken, he was here now, at the end, doing the work.

Sebastian's eyes burned. The wind took the tears before they fell, which he decided was fitting. Storm-caller to the last: the sky kept his secrets.

"Hazel," he called, and his voice cracked down the middle. "Whatever happens—whatever you become after this—you tell them. Both sides. Tell them the ban was a lie and the hunger was a lie and the only real thing was the door. Tell them a charm-seller held it open."

Her voice rose to meet his, and in it he heard two tongues braiding together, and the Gate began—

—to sing.

* * *

The last of his strength went out of him like a tide going out, and Sebastian Okafor slid down the ancient stone of the Hollow Gate to his knees, and then onto his side, and the world tilted and softened and grew very quiet.

Above him, impossibly, the arch blazed with steady light. Through half-open eyes he watched Hazel stand at its center with her arms raised and her hair streaming upward, and he watched the storm-realm pour itself gently toward her like a sea finding its shore, and he understood, with the calm clarity of a man at the end of his accounts, that the ledger was balanced. Everything taken, everything owed, everything given. Paid in full. Not with return—for he could feel it now, the great silence where his gift had been, permanent as a missing tooth—but with something better than return.

Freedom. Real freedom. Not the small counterfeit he'd sold himself in Vellamark, but the kind that comes when there is nothing left to hide and nothing left to lose and the last thing you did with your life was the truest thing you ever did with it.

Rain, he thought hazily. Let there be rain. Let it fall on Vellamark until the gutters run and the Wardens' gray banners droop and every windowsill bowl in the salt villages overflows. Let it fall on my little stall and wash the dust off the nothing-charms, and let some child pick one up and feel, just for a moment, that the world is larger than the walls around it.

His eyes were closing. The singing was very loud now, and very beautiful, and somewhere inside it he could swear he heard a woman's voice—her mother's voice—saying words he couldn't quite catch, words that sounded almost like *thank you*, or maybe like *enough*, or maybe simply like his name, spoken the way no one had spoken it in twelve years:

*"Sebastian."*

The Gate blazed. The storm leaned close. And the last thing Sebastian Okafor saw before the dark took him kindly was Hazel turning toward him through the light, reaching out her hand, her mouth shaping words he was too far gone to hear—

—and whether they were a farewell or a summons, he would not know until he woke.
