## 20. Aftermath of Rain

He woke to a sound he had not heard in twelve years.

It came soft at first, half-dreamed—a whisper against glass, a thousand small fingers tapping. Sebastian lay still on the narrow cot in the smuggler's loft above the tannery row, his body a map of bruises and burns, and listened to the impossible thing happening outside the window.

Rain.

Not the grey drizzle that Vellamark's weather-casters had once been paid to manufacture before even that pretense was abandoned. Not the acid spit of the ash winds off the pass. Real rain, falling from real clouds, in long silver ropes that sheeted down the window and pooled in the broken gutter and sang on every tin roof in the district like a city full of struck bells.

He sat up too fast and the world tilted. His hands—his hands were just hands. He stared at them in the pre-dawn gloom, turning them over, palms and knuckles and the old burn scar across the left one from the night the Gate first broke. They felt heavy. Hollow. Like gloves someone else had worn for forty years and left behind.

The storm-gift was gone.

He had known it would be. He had poured it out deliberately, cup by cup, into the failing threshold while Hazel's voice climbed toward the words that would hold two worlds together—had felt it leave him the way heat leaves a cooling forge, the way blood leaves a wound, unstoppable and final and, strangely, painless. What remained was this: an ache in the marrow where the lightning used to live, and a silence inside him so vast he could hear his own heartbeat stumble around in it, looking for walls.

Sebastian Okafor, who had called his first thunder at six years old. Who had walked the storm-realm's high roads as a young man with lightning braided through his hair. Who had spent twelve years hiding that inheritance under charm-seller's rags in a city that outlawed the very sky.

Empty. For the first time in his life, empty.

He should have wept. He found, instead, that he was smiling.

* * *

By the time he'd dressed—the same ruined coat, one sleeve scorched to the elbow, smelling of ozone and ash and Corvin's cheap lamp-oil—the rain had thickened into something joyous. It hammered the streets. It filled the cisterns. It ran in bright rivers down the channels built for it generations ago by engineers who had never believed the water would actually come, and it overflowed them, and no one minded.

Sebastian stepped out into it.

The cold hit him like a blessing. It soaked through his shirt in seconds and plastered his hair to his skull and he stood there in the middle of Tanners' Row with his face turned up, mouth open, letting it fall on his teeth and tongue. Around him the city was waking wrong—or waking right, depending on how you counted. Doors opening. People spilling out into the downpour without umbrellas because no one in Vellamark owned an umbrella; why would they? A woman across the lane stood in her doorway with a washing basin held out into the rain, laughing, actually laughing, at the sound it made filling up. Two children ran the length of the street barefoot, shrieking, slapping the puddles, and their mothers did not call them back inside.

No one was afraid of it. That was what undid him. Twelve years of Warden edicts, twelve years of *magic is forbidden, the storm is forbidden, wonder is forbidden*, and here were the people of Vellamark standing in the rain like it was the most natural thing in the world—which, he thought, his throat tightening, it was. It always had been. The ban had never lived in them. It had only ever lived above them, in the sealed grey ceiling the Wardens maintained over the city, and that ceiling was cracking now. He could feel it—not with power, he had none, but with the ordinary human sense of pressure changing, of weather arriving, of a long-held breath finally being let go.

Somewhere north, beyond the Ash Pass, Hazel was standing in the Gate she had reforged, learning its rhythms. And the weather was her handwriting now, falling on the whole world.

He went to find bread.

It seemed important, suddenly, to do something small and mortal. His coins were few—he'd fled the shop with nothing but the map and the clothes he wore—but the baker on Cordwainer Street opened early on account of the rain, and there was a line already, people talking over each other in the queue.

"—saw it myself, the clouds coming in off the sea like a herd of sheep—"

"—the Wardens put out a notice saying it was a natural weather event, a *natural event*, after twelve years of telling us the sky was closed—"

"—my grandmother used to sing a song about the rain, I thought she'd made it up—"

Sebastian bought a loaf and ate half of it standing in the doorway, watching the street run silver. An old man beside him nodded at the sky and said, conversationally, "About time," as if the rain were a tardy relative, and Sebastian laughed—a rusty sound, startled out of him—and the old man looked pleased with himself.

Then he saw them: four Wardens walking down the center of Cordwainer Street, unhooded, unhurried, and the crowd's chatter thinned the way it always did, the old fear flexing its muscles even in the rain.

But these Wardens weren't patrolling. Their grey cloaks were soaked through. Their faces were grim in a different way than usual, and between them, wrists bound, walked a tall woman with iron-grey hair plastered flat to her skull, her chin high, her eyes fixed on some horizon only she could see.

Ilsette.

Sebastian's hand closed around the remaining bread until it crumbled.

She passed within ten feet of him. She did not see him—why would she look for a powerless charm-seller in a doorway, dripping, ordinary? But he looked at her. He took his time about it. He studied the deep lines around her mouth and the fury banked behind her eyes and the way her shoulders stayed squared even bound, and he waited for the rage to rise in him, the rage that had carried him through the pass and the camp and the cage.

It didn't come. What came instead was something quieter and more terrible: pity. She had hoarded the storm's source behind warded vaults for twelve years, believing scarcity was safety, believing that if no one could touch magic then no one could be hurt by it—and now the sky itself had testified against her, and there was no vault big enough to hold the rain.

Her escort halted at the corner. One of the younger Wardens said something Sebastian couldn't catch, and Ilsette answered, loud enough to carry:

"The ban stands until the Council revokes it. Weather is not law."

But even as she said it, a gust came down Cordwainer Street and tore the notice-poster from the wall behind her—the one decreeing, in official block letters, *MAGICAL PHENOMENA ARE FORBIDDEN WITHIN CITY LIMITS*—and sent it tumbling end over end through the flooded street, past the children, past the laughing woman with her basin, gone.

Nobody moved to pick it up. Nobody ever would.

They took her away south, toward the Council hall, and the crowd closed behind them like water behind a boat, and someone began, hesitantly, to talk about the rain again.

* * *

He spent the morning walking.

He told himself it was aimless, but his feet knew better. They took him down Merchant's Way to the little stall with its striped awning, half-collapsed under the weight of rain, the charms still hanging in their neat rows—copper bells, knotted cords, painted stones, all of it harmless, all of it lies he had told gently for twelve years. *This one for safe travel. This one for a calm heart.* He had sold smallness by the handful because smallness was safe, because a man who sold trinkets could not be a threat, could not be hunted, could not be remembered.

He stood under the ruined awning and touched the bells one by one. They swung and rang softly in the wet wind, and for the first time since he'd hung them, they sounded honest. Not charms. Just bells. Just beautiful things making beautiful noise, needing no covenant, no gift, no hidden blood.

A girl of maybe nine stopped at the front of the stall, dripping, breathless from some errand, and pointed at a blue-painted stone strung on a leather cord.

"How much?"

"Two coppers." Then, because he was tired of lying even in small ways: "It doesn't do anything. It's just pretty."

The girl considered this with great seriousness. "Good," she said. "My gran says the ones that do things are trouble." She slapped her coppers on the counter, took the stone, and ran back out into the rain, holding it up so the water could reach it.

Sebastian watched her go and felt something in his chest come loose—not painfully, the way a knot slips when you stop pulling at it.

He spent an hour salvaging what the rain hadn't ruined, which wasn't much, and didn't mind. Then he folded the awning, stacked the unsold stock in the dry corner of the shuttered stall, and left the key under the loose brick where he'd always left it, though he suspected he would never need it again. Some other seller could have the pitch. Some other smallness. He was done performing his own diminishment.

The city, meanwhile, was busy becoming something else.

By afternoon the rumors had shape. The eastern districts reported the old fountains running—fountains that had been dry monuments for a generation, now gushing brown-then-clear water while crowds cheered like it was a festival. The river gate had been opened for the first time in years to keep the lower streets from flooding, and the smell of moving water had come back to the canal quarter, washing out the stale mineral stink of stagnation. Someone had climbed the Warden tower on Bell Hill and hung a wind-chime from the beacon arm, and the sound carried across half the city, bright and irregular and utterly unlawful, and no one climbed up to take it down.

And everywhere, in doorways and queues and market lines, the old words were surfacing like fish rising. Grandmothers singing songs about the rain. Children asking why the sky had been shut. Men who remembered their fathers speaking of the storm-realm the way you speak of a country you emigrated from—with grief, and pride, and the careful vagueness of the officially silenced.

The ban could not hold. Sebastian heard it in every conversation: not rebellion, exactly, but the simple arithmetic of a lie that had become unsustainable. You could forbid magic. You could not forbid weather. And weather, it turned out, had been magic all along—or magic had always been weather, or the distinction had never mattered except to the people who needed it to matter in order to keep their vaults locked and their power intact.

Twelve years, he thought, standing on the bridge over the reopened canal. Twelve years of grey. And it ends not with armies or edicts but with water finding the lowest ground, the way water does.

He thought of Ilsette in her cell tonight, wherever they were keeping her, listening to the rain on the roof. He found he could not hate her. That frightened him a little—hate had been load-bearing, these last weeks, part of the frame that held him upright. Without it there was just the ache, and the silence where the storm had been, and the strange lightness of a man who owed nothing to anyone except, perhaps, a dead woman whose oath he had kept, and a living one who had fulfilled it better than either of them had dared hope.

* * *

Toward evening the rain gentled to a steady whisper, and Sebastian climbed the tannery stairs to the loft and found he was not alone.

Corvin's sister, Maren—a broad, capable woman with her brother's sharp eyes and none of his mercenary edges—was building up the fire. She'd taken over the loft's lease, she told him, now that Corvin wouldn't be needing it. She said it plainly, the way you say things that must be said plainly or not at all, and Sebastian bowed his head and let the moment be what it was.

"He talked about you," Maren said, poking the fire. "All those years. 'I owe the storm-man a favor,' he'd say. 'One day the storm-man will call it in, and I'll pay it glad.'" She looked up. "He paid it glad."

"I know," Sebastian said. "I was there."

"Was it worth it? What he bought?"

Sebastian thought of the warded cells bursting open. Of the covenant-stones passing hand to hand down a chain of prisoners and smugglers, out of the Wardens' vaults, north toward the Gate. Of Hazel's face when the last stone had cleared the pass. Of Corvin himself, grinning through the smoke, waving them on with both arms like a man hailing a ship into harbor.

"Yes," he said. "It was worth everything. It wasn't worth *him*, but it was worth everything."

Maren nodded slowly, satisfied, or as satisfied as anyone could be. She set a bowl of stew in front of him—actual meat in it, a luxury the reopening river trade had already made possible—and left him alone with the fire and the rain.

He ate. He slept a little. He woke in the dark and lay listening to the roof, and the silence inside him was still there, vast and clean, and he lay in the middle of it like a man lying in a felled forest clearing, seeing sky for the first time.

Powerless. He turned the word over. All his life it had meant shame, exile, the thing the Wardens threatened, the thing he'd pretended to be. Now it simply meant true. He was, at last, exactly what he appeared to be: a man of no particular gift, in a city of no particular magic, on a night of perfectly ordinary rain.

It was the freest he had ever felt, and he did not yet know what to do with freedom. That, he supposed, was tomorrow's problem.

Near dawn, footsteps on the stairs—light, quick, familiar in a way that stopped his heart and restarted it. He was on his feet before the knock came, and when he pulled the door open, rain-smelling and travel-worn and unmistakably alive, standing in the grey first light with her mother's oath-mark visible at her collar—

"Hazel," he said. "You're supposed to be at the Gate."

"I was," she said, and her smile was tired and blazing at once. "That's why I'm here. Sebastian—the Gate wants to show me something. And it says it can't do it without you."
