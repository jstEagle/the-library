## 1. The Shop of Small Charms

The trouble with selling luck is that nobody believes in it, and the ones who do believe are the ones you least want believing.

I opened the shop at half past seven, as I had every morning for twenty years, and swept the step, as I had every morning for twenty years, and pretended — to myself, mostly — that this was a life rather than a vigil. The sign above the door reads HARTWELL & SONS, which is a lie twice over: there were never any sons, and there has been no Hartwell but me since my father died owing more than money. But the sign is old paint on old wood, and old things make people comfortable. Comfortable people buy luck-knots.

I sell three kinds. The red ones for travelers, the blue for brides, and the plain undyed ones for people who can't afford color but want to feel they've bought something anyway. Each knot takes me eleven minutes to tie if I'm honest about it, and about four seconds if I'm not. The honest ones sell better. There's a lesson in that somewhere, but I've had twenty years to find it and haven't.

"Morning, Hartwell." Mrs. Pellwick from the chandlery, first customer through the door as always, because being first makes her feel the day owes her something. "Two blue knots. My niece marries Sunday."

"May she be happy," I said, "and may her mother-in-law live far away."

That got the laugh it always gets. Mrs. Pellwick has bought a blue knot before every wedding in her family for six years and tells me the same joke lands fresh each time. That's the other thing about luck: it's mostly repetition with good manners.

What she doesn't know — what none of them know, the whole street of them, the whole gray respectable city of Corvane with its Warden banners and its Salt War memorials and its annual festival commemorating our deliverance from sorcery — is that every knot I tie honestly carries a thread of the real thing. Not much. A whisper. Enough that a bride's marriage bends gently toward kindness, enough that a traveler's road curves away from ditches. A thimbleful of magic spread across a city's worth of knots, so thin the Wardens' instruments have never once tasted it. My father taught me the trick. Dilution, he called it. *The sea isn't salt because it tries, Bram. It's salt because it's patient.*

My father said a great many things, most of them from the cellar, most of them wrong in ways that took me years to understand.

* * *

The cellar door sits behind the shelf of dried lavender, and it locks with an iron key I wear on a cord around my neck, under my shirt, against my skin. Twenty years, and the key has worn a groove in my sternum I could probably hang a picture on.

I went down at noon, as I always do, carrying the day's first honesty: a candle, a jar of lamp oil, and the small brass instrument called a stillness-gauge that I keep calibrated to lie on my behalf.

The gate fills the back wall of the cellar like a wound fills a bandage. It's older than the house, older than the street, older probably than the city, though the city would dispute that, cities being vain. Grey stone, twice my height, carved over every inch with script in no alphabet the Wardens ever managed to catalogue, and down its center a seam where the two halves meet — closed, sealed, waxed over by my own hand with a compound of beeswax, ash, and salt, renewed every season whether it needs it or not.

It doesn't need it. It hasn't needed anything from me in twenty years. That's what frightens me. Gates that need tending are gates that are merely shut. This one is *sealed*, and the seal holds the way a held breath holds — perfectly, silently, and with the constant sense that eventually something must exhale.

I checked the wax. I checked the seams. I ran my palm along the cold stone and felt, as I always feel, nothing — and then, beneath the nothing, the way you feel beneath silence the fact that someone might be listening, a faint pressure, like the memory of a voice deciding not to speak.

"All quiet," I told the gauge, and adjusted it to say so.

The ledger was where it always was, in the iron box behind the loose brick. I didn't open it. I never open it anymore. Opening it does nothing except remind me of arithmetic I can't change. But I touched the box, the way you touch a scar to confirm it still aches, and put the brick back, and climbed the stairs, and locked the door, and became again the harmless man who sells knots.

* * *

The Wardens came on the last Thursday of autumn, which is when they always come, punctual as frost.

There were two of them this year: a young one who did the looking and an old one who did the yawning. The young one had the flat gray uniform and the flatter gray expression of a man who has inspected forty charm shops and found forty shops full of nothing, and resents each one for wasting his morning. The old one I knew. Sergeant Vosse had inspected my father's shop too, back when inspections were less ritual and more terror, and he had signed the paper that let me keep the premises after the trials. I've never decided whether that was mercy or laziness. With Vosse it was usually both.

"Hartwell." He didn't look up from his clipboard. "Annual certification. Any unlicensed workings on the premises?"

"None," I said, in the voice I keep for this, mild and slightly bored, the voice of a man with nothing better to hide than bad bookkeeping.

"Any contraband salts, sigils, or speaking-stones?"

"Contraband salts," I repeated, "in a shop that sells string."

He almost smiled. Twenty years ago that joke would have gotten me a cell. Now it got me a grunt, which is progress of a kind, though I sometimes think progress toward what.

The young one walked the floor while Vosse asked his questions. He ran his thumb along the shelf edges where the residue of twenty years of diluted charms lives in the wood grain — harmless, tasteless, invisible to everything but a very good instrument held by a very suspicious man — and paused at the lavender shelf.

My heart did not speed up. I'd trained it not to, long ago. But I watched him the way you watch weather.

"What's behind here?"

"Lavender," I said. "Behind the lavender is a wall. Behind the wall is the neighbor's cellar, which floods. You're welcome to check. Mind the smell."

He looked at me a moment longer than comfort allows, then moved on. Vosse stamped the certificate without reading it, the way he has for fifteen years, and paused at the door on his way out.

"You look tired, Hartwell."

"I'm always tired, Sergeant. It's November."

"Mm." He settled his hat. "Your father used to say that too. Right up until the year he wasn't lying about it." And he left, taking his terrible little gift for unease with him.

I stood in the empty shop among my honest knots and thought about how good I'd gotten at this. Twenty years of small lies, laid one atop another like courses of brick, until the wall between my life and the truth was load-bearing. Take out one lie and the whole structure comes down on whoever's standing underneath. So far, only me.

* * *

That evening I tied knots by lamplight and listened to the frost tick against the windows. First frost came early this year — the glass had gone ferned white by dusk, and the street outside had that hushed quality frost brings, the city holding its breath the way the cellar gate holds its seal.

The loneliness, when it came, came the way it always did: not as a blow but as a settling, like snow finding its level. I had made my peace with it years ago, or something adjacent to peace — an armistice, at least. No wife. No children. Friends kept at the exact distance where they'd never ask to see the cellar. My father's debt paid down in installments of invisibility, one careful decade at a time. Some men inherit houses. I inherited a door, and the door's whole demand was that I remain a man worth forgetting.

I was tying my last knot of the night — a plain one, undyed, for no one in particular — when I heard the knock.

Not at the shop door. At the *cellar* door. The one behind the lavender, the one that opens onto nothing but stone and seal and twenty years of silence.

Three knocks. Slow. Spaced like words in a sentence I hadn't heard spoken aloud since I was nine years old, kneeling on these same steps while my father pressed his finger to his lips.

The old tongue. Knocked, not spoken — but knocked in its rhythm, in its grammar, in the cadence my father had drilled into me before the wars made knowing it a crime.

I sat very still. The knot fell apart in my hands.

Then came a second knock, softer, uncertain — a child's knock, the kind that asks permission rather than demands entry — and beneath it, muffled through stone and wax and twenty years of my careful life, a voice. Thin. Young. Speaking words in a language older than kingdoms, older than the law that banned it, older than the guilt I'd built my whole existence on top of.

I understood enough to be afraid.

And underneath the fear, rising through it like water through chalk, something else entirely — something that sounded, God help me, like an answer arriving to a question I'd stopped letting myself ask.

I picked up the iron key from around my neck and went to see who had come knocking on the one door in Corvane that should never, ever open.
