## 2. A Knock in the Old Tongue

The first frost came early that year, and it came to my door before it came to anything else.

I know because I was awake for it. I am always awake for the frost, the way a man who has buried something in his yard is always awake for the sound of shovels. Frost means winter, and winter means the Wardens' inspection is only months off, which means the cellar gate needs its annual dressing of lies: fresh dust on the hinges, cobwebs coaxed into artful disrepair, the salt-scars on the stone painted over with ordinary damp-stain. Twenty years I have done this. I could do it drunk, blindfolded, and mid-argument with myself, and some years I have.

So when the knock came—three soft raps, spaced like a heartbeat slowing down—I assumed it was trouble wearing a small shape.

It was past midnight. The shop was dark except for the lamp I keep burning over the workbench, because a charm-seller whose lamp goes out is a charm-seller whose luck has run dry, and I sell enough luck that superstition seems wise. Through the window I saw nothing but my own reflection and, behind it, the street going white. The frost had arrived while I wasn't looking, laying itself across the cobbles like a creditor across a ledger.

The knock came again. Three raps. Then, unmistakably, a third time—and this time I heard what was wrong with it. Not the rhythm. The *words*. Between the raps there was a voice, high and thin, saying something in a language with no edges to it, all flowing vowels and consonants that rolled like water over stones.

I stood very still in my own shop, among my harmless little knots and bells and bottled breaths, and felt twenty years of careful invisibility begin to crack like river ice.

Because I knew that language. Not well—I was never allowed to learn it well—but enough. Enough to know that no child in this city should be speaking it. Enough to know it predates the kingdoms, the Wardens, the Salt Wars, every law ever written on paper that could burn. It is the language the mountain speaks, they used to say, back when people still said things like that openly. It is the language the court beneath answers in.

And it was standing barefoot on my frosted doorstep.

I opened the door because the alternative was listening to it knock until the neighbors woke, and a neighbor waking is a Warden's breakfast. That's what I told myself, anyway. The truth is closer to this: twenty years of silence makes a man so starved for any voice at all that he'll open his door to the end of the world if the end of the world has good manners.

She was small. Seven, maybe eight years old, though children from under the mountain—if she was from under the mountain—age strangely, or so the old stories go, and the old stories were the first casualty of the wars. Barefoot on ice, and her feet weren't even blue. She wore a rough grey shift that might have been woven from fog and discipline. Her hair was dark and uncombed and full of road-dust, and her eyes were the pale grey of the sky just before snow, and she looked up at me with an expression I recognized immediately because I wear it myself most days: the expression of someone who has been sent somewhere they did not choose to go.

Then she spoke again, and the words went through me like a splinter finding old wood.

I caught perhaps one phrase in ten. *Under-the-hill. Owed. Winter-closing.* And one word she said twice, pressing her small fist against her own collarbone as she said it, the way you'd point to a wound: a word that sounded like water going backward, like a debt being called in.

"Slowly," I said, in plain city speech, and then, hating myself, in the old tongue, my accent mangled and my grammar twenty years rusted: "Slowly. Again."

Her face changed. Something loosened in it—the way a lock loosens, not the way a fist does. She said the whole thing over, slower, and I understood more this time, and what I understood made me want to close the door, bar it, brick it up, move to the coast, change my name, and take up fishing.

She was looking for the keeper of the gate.

*You are the keeper,* she said—or rather the old tongue said, through her mouth, in a voice too old for her teeth. *The last payment walks. Bring what is owed before the winter closes.*

I have been called many things by many people. Charlatan, mostly, behind my back; a decent sort, occasionally, to my face; once, memorably, "a menace to civic order" by a Warden with a flair for the dramatic. But never before had anyone addressed me as a function. A role. A hinge on which something enormous swung.

"I think you've got the wrong shop," I said, in city speech, which was cowardice dressed as caution.

She didn't understand the words but she understood the tone, the way dogs and creditors do. Her chin came up. She pressed her fist to her collarbone again and repeated that backward-water word, insistent now, and then—because I didn't answer fast enough, or maybe because she decided I needed convincing—she pulled the neck of her shift aside.

I wish I could tell you I looked away. I looked. Any father would have looked, though I didn't yet know why that sentence would come to me, or why it would arrive with such terrible certainty, the way a name arrives years after you've forgotten the face that owned it.

On her collarbone, just above where a heart beats in a chest too small for everything it was about to carry, was a brand. Old work, healed long ago, silver-pale against her skin. Shaped like an eye.

Shaped like a closed eye.

I sat down. There was a stool behind me—I'm told I sat on it; I don't remember choosing to—and the cold came in around my ankles and I let it, because the alternative was thinking clearly, and thinking clearly had just become unbearable.

A closed eye, branded on the collarbone. In the old law—the law beneath, the law that never stopped existing just because we passed laws on top of it—a closed eye means collateral. It means: *this soul is held.* It means the bearer doesn't entirely belong to themselves. Somewhere, in a vault I have spent two decades pretending not to know about, there is a lantern with her name on it, and someone is keeping it lit, the way you keep a lamp burning over a workbench. The way you keep a gate sealed under a charm shop.

My father's handwriting flashed through my memory—that careful, cramped script I'd found once, as a boy, in a book he burned before I could read the whole page. He'd been reading it aloud, softly, in the old tongue, and I'd asked him what it said, and he'd looked at me with an expression I now understood was fear, and he'd said: *Nothing that concerns you yet.*

*Yet.* Fathers are careless with that word. They scatter it like seed and then die before the harvest.

"How did you find me?" I asked. City speech again. My hands were shaking, so I folded them, so she wouldn't see. Children shouldn't see grown men shake. It teaches them the wrong lessons about grown men.

She cocked her head. Then she reached into her shift and produced—of all things—a luck-knot. One of mine. Cheap twine, three strands, the pattern I've sold by the hundred to people who wanted safe journeys and mild weather and their mother-in-laws to visit less often. Except this one was mine in a truer sense: I'd tied it myself, badly, years ago, and kept it in a drawer because the knot slipped and I meant to redo it and never did.

Except it wasn't slipped anymore. It was perfect. Every strand seated, every loop true, tied the way knots are tied under the mountain, where they take their craft seriously and their souls seriously and apparently their deliveries seriously.

Someone below had untied my carelessness and retied it as a message. The message was: *we know your work. We know your hands. We know where you live.*

"They sent you," I said. "Through the gate." My voice came out strange. "Through *my* gate."

She nodded once, gravely, the way a magistrate nods, and I thought: oh, they've taught her manners the way other children get taught prayers.

*

Here is what I did next, and I record it without pride: I nearly turned her away.

I stood in my doorway with the frost biting my feet and the child shivering—not from cold, I noticed; from something else, some inner weather—and I calculated. I calculated the way I've calculated for twenty years: what will the Wardens see, what will the neighbors hear, what is the smallest possible version of this problem. And the smallest possible version involved closing the door. Sending her back down the mountain, or to the poorhouse, or anywhere that wasn't my cellar. Letting the debt be collected by whoever collects debts, far from me, where I wouldn't have to watch.

I want to be clear that I understood exactly what that meant. A closed eye means collateral, and collateral gets claimed. This year, apparently, the claim had walked up my street on bare feet that didn't feel the cold, and knocked three times, politely, and spoken in the language of the thing that owns her.

I stood there doing arithmetic with a child's life, and the sum kept coming out the same, and I hated the sum, and I reached for the door handle anyway—

—and she sneezed.

It was such a small, human, ridiculous sound. All that ancient language, all that mountain gravity, all those magistrates' manners, and then a sneeze, tiny and explosive, that knocked her back half a step and left her blinking at me with wet eyes and an expression of profound personal offense, as though her own body had betrayed her in front of company.

And something in my chest, something I had bricked up and salted and painted over with damp-stain along with everything else, cracked straight down the middle.

"Oh, for—" I said, uselessly, and stepped aside, and held the door open.

She looked at the open doorway. She looked at me. And then, with the gravity of a magistrate and the muddy feet of a child, she crossed my threshold.

*

I fed her bread and honey because it was what I had, and she ate the way children eat when they've learned not to trust that food will always come: fast, guarded, watching me between bites. I put wool socks on her feet, which she examined with the suspicion of an art critic. I set her in the chair by the workbench lamp, and she curled up in it like a cat deciding whether the lap is trustworthy.

"You can't stay," I told her. "You understand? Whatever they sent you for—you can't be here when the sun comes up."

She looked at me with those pre-snow eyes and answered in the old tongue, one short phrase, and I understood it perfectly, because some sentences need no grammar:

*I am already here.*

I busied myself with the kettle. I did not look at the cellar door. The cellar door, I noted, was shut, which it always is, and locked, which it always is, and yet I felt it the way you feel a tooth you're trying not to touch. Somewhere below my floorboards, behind twenty years of my own careful lying, the gate she'd come through was cooling, settling, forgetting itself—and she had crossed it as easily as crossing a street.

Which meant the gate opened for her. Which meant the gate *knew* her.

Which meant, whatever else was true, whatever the ledger said and whoever had signed it, the mountain considered this child mine to keep or mine to give. And I had just given her socks.

She finished the bread. She licked honey from her thumb with great dignity. Then she slid down from the chair, padded across my floor in her borrowed socks, and stopped in front of the cellar door, and laid one small palm flat against the wood.

And in the old tongue—softly, the way you speak to something sleeping—she said a word I hadn't heard since my father's mouth, the night he burned the book.

Behind the door, in the dark, something answered.

It was a very small sound. A settling, almost. A sigh of stone. But I heard it, and she heard it, and we stood there together in the lamplight, the keeper and the payment, listening to my twenty years of silence acknowledge that it had never really been silent at all.

She turned and looked up at me, and pointed at the door, and said—in city speech, her first words in it, accented and careful and absolutely clear:

"What is owed?"

And God help me, I didn't know. But I was suddenly, desperately certain that somewhere in this house my father had left the answer, and that I had spent twenty years walking past it.
