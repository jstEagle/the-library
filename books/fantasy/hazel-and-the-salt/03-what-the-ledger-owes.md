## 3. What the Ledger Owes

The man with the tired face does not know I am writing this. He thinks my pictures are the scribbling of a simple child, and I let him think it, because a simple child gets fed and a clever one gets questions.

I will write it the way I see it. Words are still strange to me — they come out of the air here flat and small, like pebbles dropped down a well that never reaches water. My mouth has been shut since before I can remember, which is not the same as never having had anything to say. Down under the mountain, everyone spoke the old tongue until they didn't. Up here, people speak in little clipped sounds, *yes* and *no* and *that'll be two coppers*, and they seem to believe these sounds are the whole of language. It is like watching birds argue about the shape of the sky.

So: drawings. The man — Bram, Bram, it took me three days to stop hearing it as *Brrr-am*, like cold — gave me charcoal and paper on the second morning, and I have filled eleven sheets already. He looks at them the way he looks at his jars of luck-knots: kindly, and without understanding anything at all.

* * *

The first picture was of the mountain. I drew it from below, the way you see it when you are being carried toward it, which is how you always see it. A triangle with teeth. Under its roots I drew the door, and under the door I drew the stairs, and under the stairs I drew the long room where the pale ones sit, and under them — because there is always an under — I drew the lanterns.

I did not draw what the lanterns are made of. Some things you keep for yourself even on paper.

Bram looked at the mountain-picture for a long time. His hands went still on the counter, which for him is like other men shouting. Then he said, "Where did you see this?" in his flat pebble-words, and I pointed at myself, at my chest, and then at the drawing, and then I drew a line from my chest to the smallest lantern and back again.

He did not ask me anything else that day. He went into the cellar and I heard him moving things around down there for a very long time, things that sounded heavy and old, and once he said a word in the old tongue — just one, *no*, or possibly *not yet*, they sound alike — and after that he came up and made tea and smiled at me with only the bottom half of his face.

I have decided I like him anyway. This is inconvenient. Liking people makes the ledger harder to read.

* * *

Here is what I know about ledgers, and I know more than most children, because I was raised by one.

Not a book. A *ledger*. The pale ones keep everything in it — every bargain, every winter's payment, every name sold and bought back and sold again. When they speak your debt aloud, you feel it in your teeth. I have felt mine since before I had teeth to feel it with. The brand on my collarbone is not a mark the way a scar is a mark. It is a *receipt*. Somewhere beneath the mountain there is a page with my name on it, written in a hand older than kingdoms, and on that page it says what I am worth and who holds me and when the account comes due.

This winter. The account comes due this winter. I could have told Bram that in the old tongue on the first night, if he had known enough to hear it. He knows maybe forty words, and half of them are numbers, which tells you something about what his family used the tongue for.

Instead I drew it.

I drew a page. On the page I drew columns of marks, and beside each column a small snowflake, because the payments come when the frost does. Most of the snowflakes I crossed out — paid, paid, paid, going back and back, further than my memory goes, further than his face goes when he tries to remember his father. At the bottom of the page I drew one snowflake left uncrossed. And next to it, a stick figure with hair like mine and a closed eye drawn on her chest.

Me. One payment remaining. Her.

Bram sat with that drawing for so long the candle burned down a finger's width. He kept touching the uncrossed snowflake, very lightly, as though it might go off. Then he said, "My father," and stopped, and started again, "my father paid *what*?" — and I shrugged, because I don't know. Children aren't shown the sums. We're only shown the door we're carried through.

But I knew where the book was. That is the thing about being collateral: you spend your whole life pressed up against the paperwork.

* * *

It was under the floor of the cellar, behind the sealed gate he pretends isn't there.

I want to be fair to Bram here, because fairness is a thing the pale ones never taught me and I'm teaching myself out of spite. He did not want to open the cellar. I could tell by the way he stood at the top of the stairs for the length of two whole teacups, holding the lamp, breathing like a man at the edge of cold water. Twenty years, Odile Vane would later say — twenty years he'd kept that door shut and himself shut with it. But I walked past him and down the stairs, because I am eight years old and determined, and because the brand on my collarbone pulls a little, like a fishhook of light, whenever I get near things that belong to the court. The floor behind the gate pulled hardest of all.

He followed me. Of course he followed me. That is the whole of Bram Hartwell in one sentence: he follows children into cellars he swore never to enter again.

The boards came up easy. Underneath, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff as bark, a book. Not a grand book — a shopkeeper's book, the kind with ruled lines and a stub of pencil tied to the spine, except the pencil had never been used, because everything in it was written in the old tongue, in ink that still shone wet after all these years. Wet ink dries. Debt ink doesn't. I put my finger near it and the letters leaned toward me like plants toward a window.

Bram read it the way a man reads a letter from a dead relative: slowly, twice, with his lips moving. And I watched his face do something I had only ever seen faces do in the long room under the mountain, on the pale ones' clients, at the moment the sum is read aloud.

His father's handwriting. His father's bargain. Page after page of winters, each one crossed off, each crossing-out a payment made — and I watched Bram understand, line by line, that the shop above us, the luck-knots, the harmless little charms, the whole careful invisible life — none of it was an inheritance. It was an *installment plan*. His father hadn't built anything. His father had been *paying*. Every year a little something owed to the thing under the mountain, and every year a little something handed over, and the gate in the cellar wasn't sealed to keep magic in.

It was sealed to keep the *creditor* out.

"Twenty years," Bram said, in the flat city words, and then in the old tongue, badly, "*twenty years I kept it*" — and the ink on the page seemed to ripple, amused. Ledgers have no sympathy. I know. I was filed in one.

Then he turned the last page, and there it all was, the way I'd drawn it: the final snowflake, uncrossed. One payment remaining. And beside it, in his father's hand, a description — age, sex, hair color, the closed-eye brand — written years before I was born, as though ordering a coat.

He looked at me. I looked at him. And here is the part I will not draw, because some pages you keep blank on purpose: I waited to see if he would look at me the way the pale ones look at property. Everyone does, eventually. Everyone looks at you and sees a number wearing your face.

He didn't. He looked at me like I was a door he'd kept shut for twenty years and had just realized he'd been keeping shut the wrong way.

"We're going to need help," he said. "And there's only one person alive who knows the court's law well enough to break it."

I didn't know who he meant. But I drew her anyway that night, from the shape of his anger: a woman made of straight lines, with salt on her hem and fire where her kindness used to be. He looked at the drawing for a long time and then laughed — one dry note, like a knot snapping.

"Yes," he said. "That's her exactly."

* * *

Tomorrow we go to find her. I have packed my charcoal. You can burn a name off a roll, the pale ones say; you cannot burn it off a page of mine.
