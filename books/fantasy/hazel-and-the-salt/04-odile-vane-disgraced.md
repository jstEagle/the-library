## 4. Odile Vane, Disgraced

The salt-boilers' quarter smells like a wound that refuses to heal, and I have come to love it the way one loves an old scar: not because it is beautiful, but because it is honest about how it got there.

My name was struck from the Warden rolls eleven years ago. I know this because I wrote the order myself—well, I countersigned it; some junior clerk with clean hands and a clean conscience did the actual striking—and because every year on the anniversary I pour myself two fingers of plum brandy and toast the woman who used to be me. She was efficient. She was thorough. She burned forty-one names from the rolls in her career, and one of them belonged to a woman named Maren Hartwell, and if you had asked Odile Vane, Prosecutor of the Third Bench, whether she felt anything about it, she would have said: *the law does not require my feelings.*

The law did not require my feelings when they took my bench, either. That at least was symmetrical.

Now I live above a boiling-house on Brine Street, where the workers render sea-salt into the coarse gray blocks the city permits itself, and I earn my keep doing small illegal things with great discretion. A charm to keep a kettle from scaling. A word to settle a fevered child. Nothing anyone could hang me for, which is to say nothing worth doing, which is to say exactly enough.

I was mending a cracked boiling-pan—the iron kind, wide as a cartwheel, split along its seam like a bad marriage—when the bell over the stair door rang.

I knew who it was before I looked up. Twenty years of hating someone teaches you their footsteps.

* * *

Bram Hartwell stood in the doorway of the boiling-house looking like a man who has rehearsed a speech all the way across town and dropped it on the stairs. He was thinner than I remembered, grayer, though we were of an age and I had no room to talk. Behind him, half-hidden by his coat, stood a child.

A child. Barefoot. In early frost.

"Well," I said, and set down my mending-tongs with deliberate slowness. "If it isn't the keeper of the most boring charm shop in Vellmark. Come to buy salt, Hartwell? You're standing in the only place in the city where you can't pretend you don't know what I am."

"Odile." His voice did the thing it always did—went careful and low, as if I were a tripwire. "I need your help."

"No."

"You haven't heard—"

"I heard the shape of it from the doorway. The answer's still no." I turned back to the pan. It was easier than turning my back on him entirely, which is what I actually wanted to do, and harder than it sounds, because my hands wanted to shake and I have never once let them. "Eleven years, Bram. Eleven years you've walked past this street with your collar up. What changed?"

He didn't answer right away, and that silence told me more than any speech would have. I glanced back despite myself.

The child was looking at the boiling-pans. Not the way children look at fire—with hunger or fear—but the way I used to look at court documents. Reading them. Her eyes moved along the row of pans and settled on the broken one, the one leaking its thin thread of brine onto the floor, and her brow furrowed like something in it offended her sense of order.

"Hazel," Bram said softly. "Don't touch—"

She touched. Or rather, she didn't touch. She opened her mouth and said a word.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is the last professional habit I have left. It was not a word in any language licensed by the Wardens' Concordance. It was not a cantrip, not a hedge-charm, none of the little kitchen-magic we prosecute children for stealing off their grandmothers. It was older. It came out of her the way water comes out of a spring—not performed, not even chosen—and the sound of it went through the boiling-house and through me and rearranged something I had spent eleven years keeping carefully bent out of true.

The pan closed. The seam knitted like flesh. The brine stopped mid-drip, gathered itself, and slid back inside.

Then the child looked up at me, and there was a brand on her collarbone, just visible above her collar. Shaped like a closed eye.

I have prosecuted soul-collateral cases. I know what that mark means. I put that mark's legal definition into the record myself, in the case of *In re the Ferrow Estate*, and I remember thinking at the time how tidy it all was—a soul held in escrow against a debt, perfectly lawful under the Concordance, no feelings required.

The child blinked at me and said, in accented but unmistakable market-Vellish: "Pan is better now. Is that good?"

* * *

We went upstairs. I told myself it was to get them out of sight of the boiler crews, who gossip worse than magistrates. I told myself a lot of things over the next hour.

Bram talked. I let him, mostly, because listening while saying nothing is a prosecutor's oldest weapon and I am nothing if not consistent. He told me about the gate beneath his shop—I had suspected for twenty years and never once had cause to act on it, which should tell you everything about how the law works when the law is run by people who prefer not to look. He told me about his father's ledger, the bargain struck generations ago, payments due each winter. He told me about the brand, and the court beneath the mountain, and the long winter coming early this year, earlier than any living memory, and how when the passes sealed, Hazel would belong to that pale, patient, immaculately legal court for a generation.

"And you want me," I said, "to help you steal a soul."

"I want you to help me petition for her release. Lawfully. First."

"And when they refuse? Because they will refuse, Bram. I've read their precedents. Their law is older than ours and tighter than ours and it has never once been wrong, which is the most frightening thing about it. They will refuse, and then you'll do the theft anyway, and you'll want me along because I'm the only person alive who knows both their procedures and our weaknesses." I poured the plum brandy—one glass, then, after a moment, a second, because apparently I'd become sentimental in my exile. "You want a disgraced prosecutor. You couldn't find a live one, so you found me."

"I found the best one," he said quietly. "They said so, at your trial. Even the men who stripped your bench. They said the only person who ever beat the mountain court's paperwork was Vane."

That landed somewhere I hadn't armored. I drank instead of answering.

Here is what I did not say: I remembered Maren Hartwell. Of course I did. Forty-one names, and hers was the one that kept me up at night, because hers was the one where the evidence was thin and I thickened it. A phrase here, a witness there, a signature on a writ I knew to be flawed. She was a hedge-witch at worst and a herbalist at best, and I built her into a Salt War remnant because the rolls needed filling that season and my superiors liked a full ledger the way the mountain court likes a paid debt.

I burned her name. And now here was her son—or so I guessed, from the jaw, from the stubbornness—in my rooms, asking me to save a branded child, and the universe had arranged it all with the neat cruelty of a well-drafted contract.

"Why me?" I asked anyway, because I wanted to hear him say it. Cruelty is also a habit. "Why not run? Take her north past the Wardens' reach, disappear, raise her wild. People do it."

"Because the mark follows," he said. "Because the debt follows. Because running means she grows up hunted, and hunted children grow into desperate adults, and I've read enough ledgers to know how those stories end." He looked at Hazel, who had fallen asleep sitting upright in my one good chair, chin on chest, hands folded like a tiny magistrate. "And because you once told the tribunal that a law which cannot be appealed isn't justice, it's arithmetic. I was in the gallery. I never forgot it."

"I was younger then."

"You were right then."

* * *

I refused him twice more that night, formally, with reasons attached, because old habits die hard and I have always preferred my refusals documented. Then I sat by the window until dawn, watching frost climb the glass, and thought about arithmetic.

At first light, Hazel woke, padded to my worktable without asking, and picked up the shard of mirror I use for scrying. She held it a moment, frowned, and spoke one soft syllable of that ancient tongue—and the mirror, which had shown me nothing but my own tired face for eleven years, filled instead with an image of a mountain pass, white and silent, and a wind moving down it like something being shut.

She looked at me and said, matter-of-fact: "Door is closing soon. We should go."

I looked at Bram. He looked at me. Somewhere below us, the boiling-house fires roared, rendering the sea into something the city could legally swallow.

"Get your coat, Hartwell," I said, reaching for mine. "And understand this: I'm not doing it for you. I owe a debt of my own, and I intend to pay it to the right account." I nodded at the sleeping-waking child, at the closed eye on her collarbone. "And if we meet anything on that road wearing a Warden's badge, you'll both stay behind me, because I have spent my whole life learning how to burn names, and I would very much like to spend what's left of it learning how to unburn one."

Hazel smiled then—small, sudden, certain, like frost forming—and said a word I didn't know, in a language older than kingdoms.

Somehow, impossibly, I understood it anyway.

It meant *yes*.
