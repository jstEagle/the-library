## 6. The Court Beneath the Mountain

I had prosecuted eleven cases before the Court Beneath the Mountain, back when I still believed the law was a wall that kept the wolves out. I knew the descent by heart: the salt caves below the Vane holdings, the way the walls went from grey to white to a white so pure it hurt, the way your own breath began to taste of verdicts.

What I had forgotten was how much it smelled like my mother's kitchen.

That was the cruelty of it. Salt everywhere, salt in the air, salt crusting the lantern-brackets and glittering in the seams of the stone like frost that had made up its mind to stay forever. It should have been home. Instead it was the antechamber to the place where I had once stood in my Warden's black and argued, with perfect diction, that a woman named Maren Hartwell had trafficked in contraband enchantment and deserved the burning of her name from every roll in the city.

Bram walked ahead of me with Hazel on his shoulders, and neither of them spoke. The girl had gone quiet the way children go quiet when they understand more than the adults around them have admitted aloud. She had her small hands laced in his hair like she'd been doing it all her life, which, I supposed, in a manner of speaking, she had.

"You've been quiet since the second cave," I said.

"I'm rehearsing," Bram said.

"Rehearsing what? You've read the petition four times."

"Reading isn't the same as saying." He ducked under a low arch of crystal-salt that would have taken my eye out if he hadn't warned me. "My father wrote this petition. Did you know that? Found it folded inside the ledger. *Petition for deferral, winter of the boy's majority.* He never filed it. Twenty years sat on his desk and he never filed it."

"And you think you'll do better because you actually mean it?"

"I think," Bram said, "that the court doesn't care whether we mean it. That's rather the point of law, isn't it? You taught me that."

I let that one go. There was no answer to it that wasn't either a lie or an apology, and I had run out of both somewhere around the third cave.

Hazel tugged his hair twice—the signal we'd worked out—and pointed down. Below us, through a seam in the salt, light was coming up. Not firelight. Not lamplight. Something older and flatter, the color of a sky seen through paper.

"The court," I said unnecessarily.

"They'll already know we're here," Bram said. "They always know. That's the first thing they teach Wardens about this place, and the last thing anyone believes."

* * *

The chamber opened like the inside of a bell.

Pillars of salt rose into a dark so high our lanterns couldn't find its ceiling, and between them stood the magistrates: pale, tall figures robed in grey-white, their faces smooth as unmarked wax. No eyes that I could see. No mouths either, and yet when they spoke, the words arrived inside your skull fully formed, the way a headache arrives—suddenly, and with the certainty that it had been there all along.

**THE PETITIONER IS RECOGNIZED,** said the nearest one, and the voice was not unkind, which was somehow worse.

Bram set Hazel down. She took three steps forward before he caught her sleeve, gently, and drew her back against his leg. She didn't fight him. She just looked up at the magistrates the way you look at weather.

"Honored court," Bram said, and his voice only shook a little. "I am Bram Hartwell, son of Aldous Hartwell, keeper of the gate at Corren Street. I come under the old forms to petition for the release of the debt held against—" his breath caught, steadied— "against my daughter. Hazel Hartwell. Collateral of the bargain struck by Aldous Hartwell in the year of the Salt Wars, payment due this winter, final installment of the original instrument."

The silence afterward had texture. It pressed against the skin.

**THE INSTRUMENT IS KNOWN TO THE COURT.**

"Then you know the terms were coerced," Bram said. "My father signed under threat. The city was burning his shop around his ears. He had nothing left to pledge but—"

**COERCION IS NOT A DEFENSE,** the magistrate said, with the mildness of someone correcting arithmetic. **A BARGAIN STRUCK UNDER DURESS IS NONETHESS A BARGAIN. THE COURT DOES NOT WEIGH THE CIRCUMSTANCES OF SIGNATURE. ONLY THE SIGNATURE.**

"That's monstrous."

**IT IS LAW.**

And there it was. The distinction I had spent twenty years failing to teach my Warden cadets, the one that got me exiled when I finally said it out loud in a courtroom: *law* and *good* are not the same word, and the space between them is where most of the world's suffering is conducted, politely, on schedule, with receipts.

I stepped forward. Old habit straightened my spine; I hated myself for it.

"Honored court. Odile Vane, formerly Senior Prosecutor of the Warden's Bench. I appear as advocate for the petitioner." My voice came out in the old courtroom register, flat and precise. "I move that the instrument be examined for defect of form. The signature of Aldous Hartwell was witnessed by one witness only. The old forms require two."

They had taught me that in my first year. Two witnesses, always two, because a bargain witnessed twice cannot be denied. I had watched prosecutors win cases on that technicality for a decade. It was a thin reed, and I knew it, but a drowning woman grasps reeds.

The magistrates did not confer. They did not need to.

**THE SECOND WITNESS WAS THE GATE ITSELF,** said the central figure. **THE GATE STANDS. THE GATE REMEMBERS. TWO WITNESSES ARE SATISFIED.**

Bram made a sound low in his throat, half laugh, half wound. "Of course. Of course it does."

**THE DEBT IS VALID,** the court said, all of them together now, the words arriving like a tide. **WITNESSED AND BINDING. PAYMENT FALLS DUE AT FIRST FROST OF THE LONG WINTER. THE COLLATERAL SHALL BE SURRENDERED.**

Hazel stepped out from behind Bram's leg.

She walked to the center of the chamber floor, bare feet silent on the salt, and turned to face the magistrates with her chin up. Then she raised one hand and drew, in the air, in the old tongue's flowing script, a single word. I couldn't read it. But I felt it land—a pressure behind my eyes, a taste of iron.

The nearest magistrate inclined its smooth head, almost courteously.

**THE COLLATERAL ACKNOWLEDGES THE DEBT,** it said. **THIS IS NOTED WITH APPROVAL.**

"No," Bram said. His voice cracked clean down the middle. "No, she doesn't—she's a child, she doesn't know what she's—"

**SHE KNOWS EXACTLY WHAT SHE SIGNS,** the court said. **THAT IS WHY SHE WAS CHOSEN.**

*Chosen.* The word went through the chamber like cold water through cloth. I saw Bram go still, absolutely still, the stillness of a man hearing something he will spend the rest of his life trying to unhear.

"What do you mean, chosen?" I asked. "The instrument names collateral by bloodline. It's mechanical. Firstborn of the line, due when due."

**THE LINE WAS CHOSEN,** the court said. **THE BARGAIN WAS SHAPED TO PRODUCE HER. A SOUL THAT SPEAKS THE OLD TONGUE NATIVELY HAS NOT BEEN HELD BY THIS COURT IN NINE HUNDRED YEARS. YOUR FATHER DID NOT MERELY PLEDGE HIS GRANDCHILD, ADVOCATE. HE PLEDGED A TREASURE, AND WAS PAID ACCORDINGLY.**

I looked at Bram. Bram looked at the floor. And I understood, with the particular nausea of a prosecutor who has just lost a case, that we had walked in here believing we were appealing a tragedy, and had instead confirmed an investment.

**THE COURT FINDS THE PETITION DENIED,** the magistrates said. **THE DEBT STANDS. LET THE PETITIONER AND THE ADVOCATE WITHDRAW. THE COLLATERAL MAY REMAIN UNTIL FIRST FROST, IF SHE CHOOSES. SHE WILL BE TREATED WITH ALL COURTESY DUE AN ASSET.**

"All courtesy due an asset," Bram repeated. His voice had gone very quiet, which in Bram meant the opposite of calm. "You keep using that word. Asset. She has a name."

**EVERYTHING HERE HAS A NAME,** the court said. **NAMES ARE HOW WE KNOW WHAT WE OWN.**

Hazel turned then and walked back to us, unhurried, and took Bram's hand. She looked up at him and signed, quick and small, the signs we'd taught her on the road: *Not yet. Not done.*

"Not done," Bram echoed hoarsely. "Right. Right."

We withdrew. The magistrates did not watch us go—they had no eyes to watch with—but I felt their attention on my back the whole way to the cave mouth, patient as salt.

* * *

We made camp in a side gallery where the salt glowed faintly, enough to see each other's faces without a lantern. Bram sat with his back to the wall and the ledger open across his knees, though he wasn't reading it. He was staring at the word *chosen* the way you stare at a stain you can't scrub out.

"They planned it," he said finally. "Before I was born. Before Hazel existed. My father didn't stumble into a bad bargain—he shopped for one. And they paid him extra for—" He stopped. Started again. "For her. For what she is. As if she were a vintage."

"Yes," I said. There was no gentler version of yes.

"And the law holds. Every form observed, every witness satisfied. If I brought the whole Warden's Bench down here, they'd review the file and rule the same way, wouldn't they?"

"In a heartbeat," I said. "I would have ruled the same way, twenty years ago. That's what I can't make you understand, Bram—I wasn't cruel on that bench. I was *correct*. And correct is the most dangerous thing a person can be, because it feels exactly like good from the inside."

Hazel was drawing in the salt dust with one finger. We both leaned over to look. She had drawn the vault—not the chamber, the vault beneath it, the one I remembered from my prosecution days, where the court stored what it held. Rows of hanging lights, drawn carefully, dozens of them. And among them, one small light circled twice, dimmer than the rest, with a little closed-eye brand beside it.

Then she drew a door. Open. With herself standing in it, holding it shut—or perhaps holding it open; with Hazel it was never entirely clear, and I suspected that ambiguity was the whole point.

"She knows where her soul is," I said quietly.

"She knows everything down here," Bram said. "She's known since we crossed the threshold. Haven't you, love?"

Hazel nodded. Then she tapped the circled light, and looked at me, and tapped it again, harder, until I understood what she was asking.

*How long,* she was asking me. *How long does it last, once they take it out.*

I had prosecuted cases touching on the vault. I knew the answer, and I wished to God I didn't.

"It dims," I said. "Slowly. The ones they hold longest go dark. They call it *ripening*, because even their cruelties get pleasant names." I met her eyes, those grave, old-tongue eyes. "Yours has been down there since the brand was struck. It's already fading. I saw it in the drawings you showed Bram."

Bram closed the ledger. When he spoke, his voice was steady in the way that ice is steady right before it takes your weight.

"First frost is six days away," he said. "The passes seal after that, and whatever happens in this mountain stays sealed for a generation. So we have six days to find a way through a system that is perfectly legal, perfectly fair, and perfectly determined to take her." He looked up at me, and there was something in his face I recognized from the trial records I'd once read of his mother—something that had nothing left to lose and had finally noticed the fact. "Odile. You know their procedures better than any living soul above ground. Tell me every rule they have. Every loophole, every form, every dusty clause. Because if the law won't give her back—"

He didn't finish it. He didn't have to.

I looked at Hazel, who was watching us both with the patience of someone who had already decided what she was willing to lose, and I heard myself say the sentence I'd sworn on my exile I would never say again:

"Then we don't ask the law. We work around it."

Somewhere far above us, muffled by a hundred fathoms of salt and stone, the wind changed key—and I knew, with a prosecutor's certainty, that the long winter had begun arriving early.
