## 7. Theft of a Soul

There is a particular kind of quiet that comes before a crime. I'd imagined it would feel like dread. It doesn't. It feels like the moment after you've signed a lease on a shop you can't afford—terrifying and oddly liberating, because whatever happens next, you did it on purpose.

The court beneath the mountain kept its own hours. Time down there was a formality, like a doily—something laid over the real workings to make them presentable. The magistrates did not sleep, but they *recessed*, and recess was the only word in their ancient law that had ever been written by anyone who understood mercy, even accidentally.

That was our hour. Odile's hour, technically, since she'd spent three days memorizing the recess protocols the way other people memorize scripture, and had pronounced them, with something like disgust, "simpler than a salt tax."

"You're certain the writ will hold?" I whispered.

We crouched in a gallery of white salt columns, above the great floor of the court where the magistrates sat in their tiers like frost forming on a window. Their voices, when they spoke, were the sound of ice settling on a lake—patient, structural, indifferent to what was underneath.

"Certainty is for Wardens," Odile said. She was smoothing the forged writ against her knee one last time, checking the seals she'd carved from boiled salt and candle ash. Twenty years ago she had prosecuted my mother with these same hands. Tonight they were committing forgery against the only institution she'd ever served. She caught me looking and her mouth went thin. "Don't. Whatever you're about to say, don't."

"I wasn't going to say anything."

"You were going to say it with your face. Your face has been saying it for three days." She tucked the writ into her coat. "It's a good forgery. I taught the forgers the court caught, before I became one of them. There's a joke in there somewhere. I'll find it after we're hanged."

Above us, somewhere in the dark of the passes, the wind had begun to change. I could feel it through the salt—a low pressure, a gathering, like the mountain drawing a breath it intended to hold for a generation. The long winter was coming early. We had hours. Maybe fewer.

* * *

The vault lay below the court floor, down a stair that had not been built so much as grown, each step a ridge of crystal that rang faintly underfoot. Odile's distraction was simple and, in its way, beautiful: a writ of *audit*, demanding the court produce its full registry of holdings for inspection under a statute so old that refusing it would be a greater scandal than obeying. The magistrates could not ignore it. Their law was a cage they had built around themselves across ten thousand winters, and they were proud of every bar.

I heard the summons echo down through the salt—three pale figures rising from their tiers, the registry being unrolled, a voice like a closing door reciting holdings—and I went the other way, down.

The vault door was not locked.

That frightened me more than any lock would have. A lock implies the possibility of thieves. This door was simply *heavy*, and it opened on a room that made me stop breathing for a moment I'll never get back.

Souls hung from the ceiling like lanterns.

That's the only honest way to say it. Hundreds of them, thousands, each one a small warm light in a cage of woven salt-crystal, suspended on chains that went up into darkness. They were all colors and none—some burned steady, some guttered, some had gone so dim they were only a memory of light, a lantern with the wick long cold. The room smelled of nothing at all, which was somehow worse than any smell. A room full of souls should smell like something. Rain. Bread. My mother's shop, before.

I found Hazel's by counting.

Odile had gotten the registry number from the audit writ—forgery has its uses—and it led me to the far wall, to a lantern hanging lower than the others, at the height a child could reach if she stood on her toes.

It was dimming.

Not guttering—dimming, the way a coal dims when the fire is being starved on purpose. I could see the shape of it through the crystal: something small and quick and stubborn, with edges like laughter. Even dimmed, even caged, I knew it. I had known it since the night she knocked on my door in a language older than kingdoms.

I put my hands around the cage and pulled, and the salt did not break, and I did not expect it to. You cannot break a court's holdings. You can only *steal* them, and theft, I was about to learn, is a craft like any other.

Odile had taught me the grammar on the frozen road: how the old tongue builds a sentence the way a mason builds an arch, each word leaning on the next, the meaning held up by tension rather than nails. She'd said the language was designed for oaths, that its grammar assumed a speaker who would be held to every word. Hazel had taught us the music—the way you bent a vowel to make the mountain listen, the way a sentence could be a question and a prayer at once.

I spoke the words Odile had given me for this exact purpose, my breath fogging in the cold of the vault, and the salt cage did not break but *unremembered* itself. That's the closest I can come to it. The crystal forgot it was a cage, forgot it had ever held anything, and came apart in my hands like frost off a sleeve.

The lantern-light settled into my palms, warm and light as a bird.

It was so small. That's what undid me, standing there in a vault full of stolen people with a child's soul cupped in my hands like a coal I was afraid to breathe on. It was so small, and they had taken it as *payment*, as a line in a ledger, as the balance owing on a bargain my father's father's father had struck, and they had done it all *correctly*, witnessed and binding and lawful, and there was no word in any language for how wrong a lawful thing can be.

The alarm, when it came, was not a bell.

It was the mountain itself. Every crystal in the vault rang at once—a single note, vast and clear, the note of the long winter arriving at the passes and finding them still open. Above me, through leagues of salt and stone, I heard the court's recess end early. I heard the registry being rolled up. I heard, very faintly, a voice like a closing door say a word I had learned to dread in three days of listening: *breach*.

I ran.

* * *

Odile was waiting at the stair, and she was not waiting calmly. She had the forged writ in one hand, already burning—she'd lit it off a salt-lamp, destroying the evidence and, I suspect, enjoying it—and her other hand was out for me.

"Tell me you have it."

I opened my hands. The small light lay there, dim but stubborn, and Odile looked at it for a long moment with an expression I had no name for. Twenty years ago she had burned my mother's name from the rolls. Now she looked at a child's soul like it was something she'd spent her whole life failing to protect.

"Run," she said.

We ran. Up through the grown stair, across the empty court floor where the magistrates' tiers stood vacant and frost-pale, toward the passage that led to the salt caves and the surface. Behind us the single vast note went on and on, and the walls began to weep—brine seeping from the crystal, the mountain sealing itself, the long winter closing its fist.

The passes would shut within the hour. If they shut with us inside, we would be entombed in the court's own cellar, and the court's law, I suspected, had a great deal to say about thieves caught on the premises.

We reached the first cave chamber and stopped, because the way out was closing.

Not ice—salt. A wall of it, growing down from the ceiling like a stalactite with somewhere to be, slow and inexorable and utterly indifferent to us. Odile swore in three languages and reached for her salts, and I stood there with a soul in my hands doing the arithmetic of a dead man: one passage, one wall, two of us, no time.

Then, from the dark of the tunnel behind us, a voice.

It was small. It was clear. It was a child's voice speaking the old tongue, and the mountain stopped to listen.

Hazel stood at the mouth of the passage we had come by—Hazel, who was supposed to be waiting above with the mules, who had plainly decided that waiting above was a thing that happened to other people—and she was looking at the closing wall the way you look at a door that has been shut in your face one time too many.

She said something in the old tongue. I didn't understand all of it. I understood *witness*, and I understood *mine*, and I understood, with a chill that had nothing to do with the cold, that she was not asking the mountain for anything.

She was informing it.

The salt wall paused.

And in the silence that followed, from far below, from the court that had just discovered its vault breached, came the sound of many feet, and Odile grabbed my arm and said the four worst words in any language:

"They're coming. *Both* ways."
