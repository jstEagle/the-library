## 8. The Long Winter Falls

I had never said so many words at once in my whole life.

That sounds like a small thing. It is not a small thing. Imagine holding your breath for seven years and then being told the air was yours all along — that you could have been breathing the entire time, that everyone around you simply preferred it when you didn't. That is what speaking felt like. The old tongue came out of me like water finding a crack in a dam, and once it started, I could not have stopped it if I'd wanted to.

I did not want to.

The salt wall hung above us, half-closed, glittering like the inside of a shell. Behind us, down the long throat of the cave, the court's feet were coming — many feet, patient feet, the kind of feet that belong to people who have never once had to run because the law always runs for them. Above us, through a hundred fathoms of stone and frost, I could feel the Wardens' boots on the mountain road, and their lanterns, and the thin whine of their salt-compasses turning toward us like needles toward a wound.

Odile had my arm. Bram had my other hand. Between them they were having a silent argument with their eyebrows, which would have been funny on any other day of my life.

"Both ways," Odile said again, as if repeating it might make it less true.

"It doesn't matter," I said.

They both looked at me. I don't think either of them had heard me say a full sentence before. Bram's face did something complicated, the way bread does when it rises — slow, from underneath, all at once.

"Hazel," he said carefully, "there are magistrates below us who own your soul, and Wardens above us who want to arrest Odile and probably burn this mountain down out of principle, and the pass closes in—"

"I know." The words felt strange in my mouth, round and heavy, like river stones. "It doesn't matter. Listen."

And then I did something I had never done in front of them. I let go of both their hands, stepped forward into the cold, and spoke to the mountain.

Not *at* it. Not a spell, the way Odile thought of spells — she kept making little gestures with her fingers, as if the old tongue needed hands to hold it up. Not a prayer, the way Bram flinched whenever he heard it, like the words might cost him something. I just talked. The way you talk to someone standing next to you in the dark.

*Old one,* I said. *You are cold and you are closing, and everyone down here is shouting, and nobody has asked you anything in a very long time.*

The salt wall shivered. Frost crawled a little further down its face, and stopped.

*I am going to tell you a story,* I said, *and then I am going to ask you to be a witness, which is different from asking you for a favor. Witnesses don't take sides. They only remember.*

Behind me, Odile whispered, "What is she doing?"

"She's talking to it," Bram said. His voice had gone strange and soft. "She's actually — it's listening."

Of course it was listening. Nobody had ever listened to *it.*

*

Here is what I know about the old tongue, now that I can say it out loud: it isn't magic. Everyone up top thinks it's magic, and everyone down below thinks it's law, and they are both wrong, and their being wrong is the reason everything is broken.

The old tongue is just true speech. It's the language things speak when no one is translating them into power. Stone speaks it. Ice speaks it. Salt speaks it, endlessly, the way lonely people talk. When I say a word in the old tongue, I'm not commanding anything. I'm agreeing with it. And things, it turns out, have been waiting a very long time for somebody to agree with them instead of using them.

My mother taught me three words before the court took her voice away. I learned the rest from the mountain itself, in dreams, before I ever walked down here. It used to talk to me at night through the floor of whatever cellar or stable or borrowed bed I slept in. *Small one,* it would say. *Small warm one. Tell them we remember too.*

So I stood in the freezing dark with my soul hanging dim in a vault somewhere behind me, hunted from above and below, and I told the mountain a story.

I told it about the Salt Wars, which it remembered better than any book, because it had been the battlefield. I told it about the men who carved the gate under Bram's shop, and how they hadn't sealed the magic *away*, whatever the histories say — they'd sealed it *in trust*, the way you seal a letter, meaning to open it again when the world got kinder. I told it about Bram's father and his ledger and his bargains, and about the court beneath, which had learned to speak law the way other people learn to counterfeit money. I told it about a brand shaped like a closed eye, and a little girl who couldn't speak for seven years because the court had taken her words as interest.

And then I said: *Witness this. All of it. Remember it truly, because tonight everyone down here is going to claim the law is on their side, and I want the mountain itself to remember what the law was before it became a thing people hide inside.*

The silence afterward was enormous.

Then the mountain answered — not in rumble, not in avalanche, but in the old tongue, plain as a grandmother:

*I witness.*

Frost bloomed across the ceiling in patterns like writing. The salt wall, which had been grinding closed all this time, went still. And every lantern-soul in the vault behind us — I could feel it even through the stone — flickered, just once, like a room full of people turning their heads.

"What did you do?" Odile breathed. She wasn't reaching for a weapon. For the first time since I'd known her, Odile Vane looked like a woman standing in a church she didn't believe in.

"I asked it to remember," I said. "It's very good at remembering. It's the only thing it's ever been allowed to do."

*

We ran anyway. Being witnessed doesn't stop feet.

Down and sideways through the salt galleries, Odile leading by some old prosecutor's instinct for where the court's corridors would bend, Bram carrying me over the bad ice because he is stronger than he looks and more frightened than he'll admit. The magistrates' footsteps echoed everywhere and nowhere; sound does strange things in salt. Once, rounding a corner, we saw two of them — pale, tall, robed in grey that moved like fog — and Odile shoved us into a side gallery and held up a forged writ like a torch, and for one horrible moment I thought paper was going to save us.

It didn't. But the mountain did.

The gallery floor groaned, and a seam of ice slid between us and the magistrates, smooth and sudden as a drawn knife. Not an attack. A *boundary*. The mountain marking a line and saying, in its slow stone way: *this far, and no further, until the witness has spoken.*

The magistrates stopped. One of them tilted its head, and its voice came out dry as ledger-paper.

"The child invokes witness."

"I invoke witness," I agreed, stepping out from behind Odile before she could grab me. My heart was going like a bird in a box, but my voice came out steady, and I remember thinking: *so this is what it's like, having a voice. This is what everyone was keeping from me.* "Under the First Rule. Any party may call the mountain to hear a grievance before payment falls due."

"That rule has not been invoked in four hundred years," said the second magistrate.

"Then it's overdue," I said, and Odile made a noise that might have been a laugh strangled at birth.

Bram put his hand on my shoulder. He was shaking. "Hazel," he murmured, "what grievance?"

I looked up at him. I had been saving this. Since the night I knocked on his door with frozen feet, I had been saving it, the way you save the last sweet thing in your pocket.

"The debt," I said, loud enough for the magistrates, loud enough for the vault full of dimming souls, loud enough for the mountain. "You say it's valid. You say it's witnessed. Fine. Then witness *this*: the debt was struck against a man who is dead, for goods never delivered, against a family that never signed, and the collateral is a child who was six years old when you branded her. Your law says debts must be paid. My mountain says debts must be *just*. And I call the mountain to judge which of those comes first."

The silence went on so long I could hear the winter falling outside — the real one, the long one, pressing down on the passes like a lid.

Then the first magistrate said, in its dry paper voice, the sentence that changed everything:

"The mountain has heard. The mountain will answer. But not to you, child."

Its head turned — slowly, slowly — past me, past Odile, past the ice wall it had raised itself.

"To the debtor's son."

Bram's hand tightened on my shoulder.

"The court summons Bram Hartwell," the magistrate said, "to answer for his father's bargain. Before the mountain. Before the winter seals. Come."

And the ice wall opened like a door.

*

Later — much later, when I had time to be afraid properly — I understood what the mountain had done. It hadn't taken my side. Witnesses don't take sides. It had simply refused, for the first time in four hundred years, to let the court decide alone in the dark. It dragged everything into the light where things could be seen: the debt, the debtor's son, the child, the truth.

But standing there, in that moment, all I knew was that every eye in the deep places had turned to Bram, and Bram had gone very quiet in the way he goes quiet when he's decided something, and I had lived long enough with quiet men to know that their silences are the loudest things about them.

He knelt down so we were eye to eye. Up close, I could see he was terrified. Up close, I could see he was smiling anyway.

"Whatever happens in there," he said, "you keep talking. Promise me. No more seven years of quiet. Not ever again."

"I promise," I said, and the old tongue wrapped around the words and made them true, because that's what promises do in that language — they stick.

He kissed the top of my head, the way fathers do, the way mine apparently always meant to.

Then he stood, straightened his coat like a man walking into weather, and followed the magistrates down into the dark, and the ice began to close behind him, and the last thing I heard was Odile swearing softly in three languages, and the mountain saying, gentle as snowfall:

*Now, small one. Now we see what the son remembers.*
