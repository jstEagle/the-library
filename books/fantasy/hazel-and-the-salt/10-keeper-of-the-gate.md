## 10. Keeper of the Gate

The first thing you should know about living with a man who has no soul is that it is quieter than you'd think.

I had imagined — in the caves, in the dark, while the mountain sealed itself around us like a fist — that a soulless Bram would be an empty Bram. A husk. Something you'd find standing in a corner, breathing out of habit. But souls are not lamps that go out all at once. They are more like hearth-fires, and when you take the fire away, the room stays warm for a long time from the stones alone.

Bram Hartwell's stones were very warm indeed.

He still rose at dawn. He still ground his chicory too fine and complained about it. He still counted the till twice at closing, once for money and once, I think, for the pleasure of counting. What was gone was something underneath all of that — the hum, the low singing note that used to run through him when he looked at me and thought I wasn't watching. Odile said she could feel it across the shop, like a struck bell that had stopped mid-ring. I couldn't feel it at all anymore, and that was the worst part: not what he'd lost, but that he no longer knew he'd lost it, and so he never grieved, and so someone had to grieve for him, and that job fell to me and to Odile, who was constitutionally unsuited for it and did it anyway.

"I've boiled worse men back to life," she told me the first week, over the salt-pans, not looking at me. "Your father is the only one I've ever tried to boil back a *soul*, and there's no recipe for it. Believe me. I checked the forbidden ones."

"Did you check the *really* forbidden ones?" I asked, because by then I could ask things like that, my voice arriving in full sentences the way spring arrives — all at once and then everywhere.

"Hazel." She stirred the pan. "The really forbidden ones are just the regular ones with better marketing."

That was Odile. Twenty years disgraced, one act of treason against her own former court later, and still she talked like a woman reading a warrant aloud. She had moved into the room above the charm shop without anyone offering it, the way weather moves into a valley. She fixed the roof. She re-hung the door that had hung crooked since before I was born. She took down the Warden's inspection notice that had been nailed above the lintel for two decades and burned it in the stove, and then, after a moment, fished it out again, because "evidence is evidence, even when it's ash," which I have decided is the most Odile sentence ever spoken.

And Bram sold luck-knots.

That was the part that undid me, some mornings. He would stand behind his counter with his gentle hands and his gentle voice and sell small charms to people who needed them — a knot for safe travel, a knot for a easy birth, a knot against bad dreams — and every knot he tied was empty. Had always been empty, actually; that was the joke of the whole city, that magic was contraband and everyone bought the counterfeit anyway because hope doesn't check its provenance. But now Bram sold them knowing, and he sold them kindly, and he wished each customer well in a voice with nothing ringing under it, and they left warmer than they came, and I would sit on the stairs and watch and think: *the fire is gone but the stones are warm, the stones are warm, the stones are warm.*

* * *

The gate stayed shut.

Of course it did. That was the whole architecture of my father's sacrifice — the debt paid, the ledger closed, the court beneath the mountain satisfied until such time as ancient law found another loophole, which the magistrates assured us, in their voices like frost forming, would be "not within your lifetime, keeper." They had said it to Bram. They had said it to me. They had said it, I suspect, to the vault itself, because those pale magistrates loved completeness the way other people love children.

But here is what nobody tells you about a sealed gate: shutting a thing is not the same as finishing with it.

I went down to the cellar on the third morning home, while Odile argued with the chimney and Bram opened his shutters to the frost-bright street. The stairs were exactly as I remembered them from the night I arrived barefoot — steep, cold, smelling of dust and old salt and something older than both. And the gate stood where it had always stood, iron banded, rune-sealed, shut by law and by vow and by twenty years of a tired man's vigilance.

Except the vow was spent. The law had been paid off. And the runes —

The runes were listening.

I don't know how else to say it. I put my hand flat against the iron and the old tongue rose in me unbidden, the way a song rises, the way grief rises, and the gate didn't open and it didn't close and it didn't do anything at all except *attend*. Like a dog hearing its name. Like a child waiting to be told whether it was wanted.

"You're mine now," I told it. "Did you know that? Nobody wrote it down. There's no ledger entry. That's rather the point."

The gate said nothing, because gates don't. But under my palm the iron was warm, and I am telling you honestly, as the new keeper, that warmth was not coming from my hand.

* * *

Winter settled over the city the way it does — gradually, then completely. The long winter, the sealing winter, the one that shuts the mountain passes for a generation. We were inside it now, all of us, sealed in with our secrets and our salt-boilers and our shops of small charms, and the Wardens came around in the second month the way they always did, annual inspection, boots on the stairs.

Odile answered the door. This was strategic; she was still technically a Warden herself, stripped of rank but not of paperwork, and she produced documents with the serene menace of a woman who had once burned names from rolls and knew precisely how much paper could hurt you. The young inspector — he couldn't have been older than I was — peered past her into the shop, saw a charm-seller tying knots, saw a girl doing sums in the window, saw nothing else, and left.

"They'll be back," Odile said, latching the door. "They always come back. Trained that way. I trained them that way."

"Good," I said.

She looked at me. "Good?"

"Somebody should keep watch on the watchers." I smiled at her, and she made the face she made when I reminded her too much of Bram-before, or possibly of herself-before, and went upstairs to fix something that wasn't broken.

Because here is my confession, written where only a keeper would think to look: I do not want the gate forgotten. I do not want it sleeping under twenty more years of dust and lies. My father kept it shut by hiding it. His father kept it shut by owing it. I intend to keep it shut the way you keep anything you love — *attending to it.* Daily. With my hand on the iron and the old tongue in my mouth, asking it how it fares, and listening, truly listening, to the answer.

Some days the answer is silence, and silence from a gate is peace.

Some days the answer is warmth.

And some days — this is the thing I have told no one, not Odile with her forbidden recipes, not Bram with his warm stones and his empty bell — some days, deep in the iron, faint as a pulse through a wall, something knocks.

Once. Twice.

In the old tongue, the rhythm says: *still here. Still here.*

It might be echo. It might be the mountain settling. It might be the court beneath, checking its seals, murmuring through a hundred feet of salt the way the dead murmur through floorboards. I have gone down every evening for a season with my palm against the bands, and I have never once answered, because a keeper's first duty is to know what stays shut.

But I have started leaving a lamp lit on the cellar stairs.

Not for the gate. Gates don't need light.

For whatever, someday, comes knocking on the *other* side — barefoot, branded, speaking a language older than kingdoms — so that when I open the door, and I will open it, because choosing what opens is half my office too — it won't arrive the way I did, in the cold and the dark, owing everything.

It will arrive to lamplight. To warmth. To a girl on the stairs who speaks its tongue.

Last night the knock came three times instead of two.

New rhythm. New word.

In the old tongue, three knocks say: *soon.*

I blew out the lamp, went up to bed, and lay awake smiling in the dark like a fool — because I am the keeper of the gate, and I chose this, and I choose it still, and I would like to see, just once, the faces of whatever waits below when it learns that this time, the door is not locked against it.

This time, the door is simply *waiting.*
