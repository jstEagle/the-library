## 1. The Date That Was Written

There are three rules to selling charms in a city that has outlawed magic, and I follow all of them: never let them see your hands shake, never let them see your hands glow, and never, under any circumstance, let them see you.

The first two are easy. The third takes practice.

By day I am the woman in the narrow shop on Tallow Lane with the hand-lettered sign — *Sable's Curiosities: Keepsakes, Trinkets, Nothing Enchanted, We Promise* — and the slightly bored expression of someone who has never once felt the hum of a ley line under her fingernails. My charms are, officially, lucky rabbit bones and polished river stones and knotted red thread. Unofficially, the river stones hold warmth in winter and the knots hold grief together, and the rabbit bones — well. Corvane's Wardens can arrest a person for witchcraft, but they have never yet figured out how to arrest a souvenir.

By night I am something else. By night I am a cartographer.

Not of Corvane, whose streets I could draw drunk and blindfolded and probably have. I map the places that shouldn't exist. The drowned city that surfaces in the harbor fog every ninth year. The road outside the north gate that is longer going home than going out. The country I found once, at the bottom of a dream, with borders drawn in a hand that looked unsettlingly like my own.

I keep those maps in a locked case under my bed, because a woman can survive selling charms in Corvane, but a woman caught drawing doors into places that don't exist will burn.

I want to be clear about that. I have spent seven years being nobody, and I have gotten very good at it. Nobody is safe. Nobody is forgettable. Nobody's face is on a Warden's list. I have made an art of being overlooked, the way other people make an art of embroidery, and I was, until tonight, quite proud of my needlework.

*

The trouble began, as trouble usually does in my shop, with a customer who talked too much.

He came in an hour before closing, a thin man with rain in his collar and a scholar's stoop, and he wanted — of all things — a charm against forgetting. "Not for me," he said, when I raised an eyebrow. "For my mother. She's losing the edges of things. Names first, then faces. I thought — something to hold the borders of her."

*Something to hold the borders of her.* I have heard a thousand requests in seven years and that one went through me like a needle through silk. I sold him a river stone and told him to keep it in her left pocket, because the left side is nearer the heart and hearts remember what heads forget. I did not charge him much. Some nights I still don't know why I bother pretending I'm a fraud.

He paid, he thanked me, he left. And he left something else behind: a book, bound in cracked green leather, that had been sitting on his satchel and slid off when he shrugged it back on. I found it after closing, under the counter, and I would have put it in the lost-and-found box with the single gloves and the brass button if the title hadn't stopped my hand.

*The Seeded Years: A True Account of Things to Come.*

Prophecy. In Corvane, possessing prophecy is a fine on the first offense and a pyre on the second, and the Wardens do not ask which is which. I should have burned it in my stove and swept the ashes out with the morning.

Instead I sat down with it under my lamp, because I am, despite everything, a cartographer, and a book that claims to map the future is just a map with worse manners.

It was old. Older than old — the colophon said it had been set down a hundred years and a day ago, in a hand that looped and slanted like rain falling sideways. The early pages were the usual prophetic mush. Kingdoms rising, kingdoms falling, a comet with opinions. I skimmed. I snorted. I turned a page and the world went quiet.

There was a date.

Not a kingdom. Not a comet. A date, written in that sideways rain of ink, a hundred years before it happened: *the seventeenth of Embermonth, in the year the tallow lamps burn blue.*

I looked at my lamp. My lamp, which has burned the same tallow oil for seven years, was burning blue. It does that sometimes. It did that sometimes. I had never thought to wonder why, and now my hands were not shaking, because rule one, but they wanted to.

I read on.

*On that day, in the city of chained rivers, the shopkeeper of Tallow Lane will find the book. She will know her name in it, and knowing, she will be known.*

I did not know my name in it. That was the one mercy. I turned the page with the particular care one reserves for things that might bite, and there, beneath a drawing of a door — a door drawn in a hand that looped and slanted like rain falling sideways, a hand I had seen before, at the bottom of a dream, drawing the borders of an impossible country —

There, beneath the door, was my name.

Not Sable. Sable is a working name, a hiding name, a name I chose the way you choose a coat: warm, plain, forgettable. The name in the book was the name I had not used since I was six years old, the name that came before Corvane, before the shop, before the careful art of being nobody. The name my mother used to call across a garden I have never been able to find on any map, in a country that does not exist.

*Elowen,* the page said, in the margin, in smaller ink, like a footnote. *She was born in Elowen. She will return to Elowen. The door remembers her hands.*

I sat in my shop on Tallow Lane and I did not move for a long time. The lamp burned blue. The rain started against the windows, and it fell sideways, and I want you to understand that I noticed that, and I want you to understand that I told myself it was wind.

Here is the thing about being seen, after seven years of not being seen: it does not feel like relief. It feels like a hand closing around the back of your neck. Every rule I had, every careful habit, every morning I had spent arranging my face into forgettability — all of it was a wall, and the wall had a name written on it now, in century-old ink, in my mother's language, and walls with names on them get found.

I got up. I did the sensible thing. I put the book in the stove.

The book declined to burn. I want that on the record. I have burned things in that stove for seven years — receipts, regrets, one memorable hat — and nothing has ever declined before. The green leather sat in the flames and did not char, and the flames turned blue to match the lamp, and the door drawn on the open page seemed, in the firelight, to be ajar.

The clock over the counter began to strike the hour. I had not known it was so late. I had not known the day was so nearly over. The prophecy had said an hour — I was almost certain it had said an hour, though I could not now find the line, though the page showed only the door and the name and the margin note in a language I suddenly realized I could still read, though I had not read it since I was six —

*The door remembers her hands.*

My hands were glowing. Rule two. Seven years of rule two, broken in a single evening, my fingers lit from inside like paper over a candle, and the glow was not staying in my fingers, it was going *into* the page, into the drawn door, and the drawn door was opening, and the shop was —

The shop was dissolving.

Not burning. Not flooding. Dissolving, the way sugar dissolves, the way a name dissolves when you stop saying it. The counter went first, softening into grey, and then the shelves, and then the blue lamp, and the last thing to go was the window, and through it — through the place where the window had been — I saw Corvane itself coming apart, the chained rivers running loose and bright, the rooftops lifting like a flock of startled birds, the whole city folding up along creases I had never mapped, though God knows I had tried, and somewhere in the middle of it I heard my own voice saying my own true name out loud for the first time in twenty years, not because I chose to but because something was pulling it out of me like a splinter, like a thread, like a border being redrawn —

And then there was grass under my hands.

Real grass, wet with real rain, and above me a sky the color of a bruise healing, and the smell of woodsmoke and bread and something flowering that I had no name for and, at the same time, every name for. I pushed myself up. My fingers were still faintly glowing. In the grass beside me lay the green book, closed, unburnt, patient.

And ahead of me, down a hill I did not recognize and recognized completely, a city was burning — no. Not burning. *Flickering.* Whole streets going transparent and returning, like a lamp about to fail, like a country holding its breath, and figures were running up the hill toward me through the rain that fell sideways, and the one in front, a woman with flour on her sleeves and terror on her face, was shouting a word as she ran.

She was shouting my name.

My true name. The one from the book. The one from before.

I had spent seven years learning how to disappear. It took the world one evening to remember me anyway.
