## 2. A Country With No Edges

The woman with flour on her sleeves reached me first, and I want it on the record that I did not run. Running is for people who believe escape is possible, and I had just watched a city dissolve like sugar in tea. There was nowhere to *go*. Corvane was behind me and also, apparently, nowhere at all.

"Sable." The woman caught my hands. Her grip was warm, desperate, and dusted with flour that smelled of cardamom — which was impossible, because cardamom was outlawed in Corvane along with everything else that made life worth living. "You came. You actually — the book said tonight, and I told them, I told *Marek*, but he said prophecies rot like anything else left a hundred years—"

"I think you have me confused with someone else," I said, because seven years of habit dies hard, and also because I am a coward with excellent posture.

"You're Sable Vane."

"That's a work name."

"It's your name." She said it the way you'd say *the sun rose* or *water is wet* — not an accusation, just geography. "It's been your name since before any of us were born. It's in the ledger."

"There's a ledger?"

"There's a ledger," said a voice behind her, dry as old vellum. Its owner was a broad man with a soldier's bearing and a baker's wife's hand still wrapped around his wrist, as if he might bolt. He looked at me the way surveyors look at fault lines. "Marek Ostrow. Formerly captain of the Third Wall Guard. Currently the man who has to tell you where you are, which I promise you will hate."

"Try me," I said. "I've had a strange evening."

"Elowen," he said.

I laughed. It came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "Elowen fell eighty years ago. My grandmother used to say—" I stopped. My grandmother had said a great many things about Elowen, all of them in a whisper, all of them after checking the door was locked. *The country that drew itself out of the world, little wren. The country that refused to be found.*

"Your grandmother," Marek said quietly, "was born three streets from here."

* * *

They took me down into the city while it flickered.

I want to describe this properly, because no one will believe me otherwise. Elowen did not exist the way other places exist. Streets appeared under our feet only when we believed in them hard enough — or rather, when enough people remembered them at once. A boulevard would shimmer into being, cobbles and gas lamps and window boxes full of flowers that had been extinct for decades, hold for a dozen heartbeats, then thin to gauze. The survivors walked it like sailors walk a deck in a storm: leaning into the fades, never stopping, never looking down.

"Sigils," the flour-woman explained. Her name was Tamsin, and she talked the way ovens radiate heat, constantly and comfortingly. "Every building, every street, every well — the founders inked sigils into the borders when they sealed the country. The seal keeps us *in*, keeps Corvane's edicts from reaching us, keeps the country off every map ever drawn since. But sigils fade. Ink fades. Memory fades." She gestured at a street going translucent ahead of us, and a boy of maybe ten ran forward and stood on its corner, arms spread, *saying* something — and the street solidified around him like a bone knitting. "So we remember out loud. Every day. All of us. Or we stop existing."

"What happens if you stop existing?"

Tamsin smiled at me, and it was the worst smile I have ever seen, because it was kind. "You're standing in what happens."

* * *

They fed me, because of course they did; Tamsin was a baker and grief has never once beaten bread. We sat in a kitchen that kept having to be re-remembered — the table would vanish and Marek would thump it back into being with his palm, muttering dimensions under his breath like a prayer — and they laid it all out for me with the terrible patience of people who have rehearsed this conversation for generations.

A prophecy, written a century ago by a cartographer named Vane. My surname. A Sigil-bearer who would come from outside the seal, carrying the gift of mapping, who would redraw Elowen's borders and open the door.

"The book you found," Marek said. "It didn't find you by accident. The seal chose to show itself. It's been waiting for a Vane."

"I sell charms," I said. "Illegal ones, tiny ones, luck-knotted thread and fever-cooling beads. I am not — I draw maps of places that don't exist for people who can't afford to leave Corvane. That's not a gift, that's a *hustle*."

"Show us your hands," said a new voice.

An old woman sat in the corner I could have sworn was empty. She had ink-stained fingers and eyes like someone who had read too much and slept too little for a very long century. "I'm Wren. I keep the archive. Show us your hands, girl."

I showed them my hands, mostly to make the point that there was nothing to see.

There was something to see.

Across my right palm, faint as breath on glass, a line had drawn itself — a clean cartographic stroke, curving up from my wrist toward my fingers. And as I watched, horrified, my hand twitched against the table, finger moving like a pen, tracing a small shape in spilled flour:

A door.

Just a door. Two posts, a lintel, a handle.

The kitchen wall behind me — the one that had faded twice since I sat down — went solid as stone. Solider than stone. It acquired a grain, a history, a hairline crack from some winter eighty years gone. Everyone stared at it. Then everyone stared at me.

"That," I said carefully, "is a coincidence."

"You drew a door," Wren said, "and a wall remembered how to be a wall."

"I draw doors all the time. It's a standard cartographic symbol. It means *here is an opening*. It doesn't *do* anything."

"Child," Wren said, with infinite gentleness, "nothing in this country does anything anymore except through belief. You just gave a wall yours."

* * *

They gave me a room in a house that only half-believed in itself. Through the window I could see the edge of the city — the actual edge, the place where Elowen simply stopped, dissolving into a grey nothing that wasn't fog or dark or distance, just *absence*, patient as tide.

I sat up most of the night telling myself the obvious truths. That prophecy books are forgeries. That a shared delusion among two hundred starving survivors was more likely than magic. That my hand had cramped. That I had hit my head. That somewhere in Corvane my shop was sitting unlocked and Inspector Dree was going through my stock of illegal charms and I needed to get *home*.

Except home was a city that had dissolved around me like sugar in tea.

And except — I lay there admitting it to the ceiling, which kept politely existing whenever I glanced at it — except that when the boy who'd held the street corner together had finished, he'd looked up at my window and waved. Not worshipful. Just glad. Like I was weather he'd been waiting for.

And except that when Tamsin pressed a roll into my hands at dinner, warm, cardamom-scented, made from a recipe that existed nowhere else in the world because Elowen existed nowhere else in the world — I had eaten it, and something in my chest had unclenched for the first time in seven years. Something that had been holding very still, the way you hold still when you're hiding.

I knew these streets. That was the thing I hadn't said aloud. When Marek led us down the Hill Road, my feet had known the turn before my eyes did. When the rain fell sideways, I'd thought, *of course it falls sideways here, it always falls sideways in the low quarter,* and I had never in my life been to the low quarter.

My grandmother's whispers. My own hands. A ledger with my name in it, written before I was born.

In the morning, Wren found me in the kitchen, trying very hard not to do what I was doing, which was drawing. On the table, in spilled flour, my hand had spent the night working without my permission: streets, lanes, a fountain I'd never seen but recognized, and at the center of it all, drawn over and over until the flour was black with graphite-thin lines—

Doors. Dozens of them. Every one facing outward.

Wren looked at the map for a long time. Then she looked at me, and her ancient eyes were wet.

"Girl," she said, "the archive has been asking for you. There's something there about your family you need to see before you draw another line."

My hand, traitor that it was, was already reaching for a pencil.
