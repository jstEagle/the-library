## 3. The Cartographer's Debt

The archive of Elowen had been a cathedral once, or something like one — the architecture had that hushed, self-important grandeur that cathedrals and tax offices share. Now its roof was mostly sky, and the sky was the wrong color, a bruised lavender that never quite committed to night. Rain fell upward in slow, reluctant threads. I'd been here three days and I still hadn't stopped flinching at it.

"You'll get used to it," said Merrin, handing me a lantern that burned with a flame the color of weak tea. "Or you won't. Old Tamsin never did. She spent forty years complaining about the weather and then she faded on a Tuesday."

"Cheerful," I said.

"I'm a baker, not a eulogist." She shrugged, flour still ghosting her forearms even though — as far as I could tell — there was no wheat left in Elowen to mill. The bread she baked appeared from stubbornness alone. It tasted like memory of bread, which is to say: almost right, and heartbreaking if you thought about it too long.

I'd come to the archive because I couldn't sleep, which in Elowen meant something different than it did in Corvane. Here, sleep was when the country frayed. You'd close your eyes and wake to find a street shorter than it had been, a neighbor's face slightly less certain, a door that opened onto nothing but white. The survivors called it *the thinning*. They said it like people talk about weather. Bad year for the thinning. Terrible pity about the Hendersons' house. Nobody said *we are all going to evaporate*, but everybody thought it, the way you think about a debt coming due.

So I worked. Cartographers are good at working through grief; we do it professionally. Every map is an elegy for somewhere else.

The archive's catalog had survived better than its contents. Drawers upon drawers of index cards, each written in a hand so precise it looked printed. I pulled drawer after drawer — *BORDERS, HISTORICAL; BORDERS, MYTHICAL; BORDERS, DISPUTED* (a fat drawer, that one) — until I found *BORDERS, FINAL*.

It was nearly empty. Three cards. The first two were technical: sigil schematics, anchor points, notes in a shorthand I half-recognized and couldn't read. The third card made my hands go cold in a way the lavender sky never managed.

*Eriksen, R. — Sealing Party, Lead Cartographer. See also: Vane.*

My surname. My actual, hidden, carefully buried surname — not Sable Vane, the charm-seller with the forgettable face, but the name my mother used only indoors, whispered like a password.

I stood very still. Upward rain drifted past the broken roof. Somewhere below, Merrin's ovens breathed their impossible warmth into the stone.

"See also," I muttered, "Vane," and went looking.

* * *

What I found took me until what passed for dawn, and by the end of it I was sitting on the floor with paper spread around me like a fallen nest, and I was not reading anymore. I was remembering.

There was a census page. *Household of Eriksen, R.* Two adults. One child, female, age four. And beside the child's entry, in the margin, someone had written: *draws doors on everything. Walls. Floors. Her father's letters. Do not give her ink unsupervised.*

I knew that handwriting. Not from the archive. From the inside of my own skull, where it lived under seven years of Corvane cobblestones: *Do not give her ink unsupervised.* My mother's voice. My mother's joke, told at dinners I suddenly remembered — low tables, candlelight the color of honey, a woman laughing while a small girl drew spirals on the tablecloth and the spirals *warmed under her fingers*.

There was more. A letter, unsent, folded into a ledger of the sealing party's final session. My mother's hand again, tighter now, the joke gone out of it:

*The border will hold only if sealed from within. We knew this when we agreed. The country survives; we do not. R. has drawn the sigil true — she is better at this than I ever was, and she is six years old, God forgive us both, she doesn't understand what she's drawing. When the seal takes, the child will be put outside. She will remember nothing. That is the mercy of it. If you are reading this, then either the seal has failed — in which case, find her, she is the only one who can redraw it — or it has held, in which case burn this letter and let her live her life unhaunted.*

Nobody burned it. A century passed, and nobody burned it, and here I sat, haunted precisely as specified.

Here is what I understood, sitting on the cold floor of a country that had eaten my childhood and spat me over the border: my parents hadn't just helped seal Elowen. They had chosen it. Chosen the country over themselves, and chosen forgetting over grief for me, and the seal had held for a hundred years on the strength of their signature — and mine, a child's spirals, warmed under small fingers, folded into the foundation of a nation like a love letter buried in a wall.

And the seal was failing. I could feel it now that I knew what to feel for — a wrongness in the air, the way the lavender sky flickered at its edges like a lamp running dry. Every day the thinning took another street. Every night another face went uncertain. The country my parents had died to save was dying anyway, slowly, politely, on a schedule no one had voted for.

And the prophecy — I'd finally read the whole thing, in the archive's copy, the same book that had swallowed me out of Corvane — was specific in the way prophecies always are when they want something from you. *A bearer shall return who carries the mark of the first drawing. She shall redraw the borders with her own hand, and the door shall open, and Elowen shall be free.* It didn't mention the cost in the opening lines. The cost was at the bottom, in smaller script, the way costs always are:

*For the redrawing requires the bearer entire. What she draws, she becomes. The map and the maker shall be one, and the maker shall remain.*

Remain. Inside. Forever. A woman turned into a border, a person stretched thin along a boundary line, holding a country together with her own spine until the end of time. The prophecy had waited a hundred years to eat me, and it had arranged for me to be raised, seasoned, and delivered like a course in a meal my parents had paid for with their lives.

I laughed. It came out wrong, cracked down the middle. Grief and betrayal don't blend; they sit side by side inside you like oil and water, taking turns rising to the top.

"Sable?"

Merrin stood in the archive doorway, lantern raised, flour-dusted, worried. Behind her, half-hidden, was one of the exile children — Wren, the small fierce one who had appointed herself my shadow since I arrived. They were both looking at me the way people look at a doctor who has just read the test results.

"Well?" Merrin said. "Did you find the original sigil?"

I looked down at the papers around me. My mother's letter. My own four-year-old marginalia, preserved in a census record like a fossil. The prophecy with its fine-print appetite.

I found that I could not say *yes, and it wants my life*. Because here was the other thing I understood, the thing sitting underneath the grief like a keel: these people — the bakers and soldiers and children born in exile inside their own vanished home — they had done nothing wrong. They had inherited a sacrifice made by my family and been trapped inside it for a century, fading street by street, baking bread out of sheer stubbornness, waiting for a door that my parents' letter promised would come.

They deserved the truth. But if I gave them this truth — *the price of your freedom is the last Eriksen* — they would refuse it. I already knew them well enough to know that. Merrin would sooner let the whole country thin away than spend a stranger. And then everyone would fade politely, on schedule, and the debt my parents had paid would buy nothing at all.

"No," I heard myself say. "Not yet. But I found something better."

Wren crept forward and picked up the census page, frowning at the margin note. "Who's the kid who draws on everything?"

I looked at my hands. Ink-stained. Always ink-stained, even in Corvane, where I'd scrubbed them nightly and the stains came back by morning like a signature refusing to be forged over.

"A friend of the family," I said.

Because I couldn't say it yet. Because saying it — *that child was me; this country remembers me; I am the last line of a family that built its own tomb* — would make it real, and I needed one more night to figure out how to cheat a prophecy.

But as Wren set the page down, the lantern light caught the old ink, and the marginal spirals — my spirals, drawn a hundred years ago by a child who didn't know what she was drawing — began, very softly, to glow.

And beneath them, in fresh ink that hadn't existed a moment before, new words surfaced on the census page, in handwriting I recognized as my own:

*Find another way. There has to be another way.*
