## 1. The Night the Stars Went Out

The moon-wool smelled of cold and secrets.

Xanthe Vane crouched in the shadow of a granite overhang, three days deep into the Thornfell passes with forty pounds of contraband wrapped against her spine, and let herself breathe. The wool came off the high meadows above Vell's Hollow, sheared from sheep that grazed only under starlight — sheep whose fleeces drank the night sky the way other flocks drank grass. Woven into cloth, it glimmered faintly, held warmth like an embrace, and fetched prices in the southern cities that could keep a smuggler fed for a year. The Wardens called it restricted. The Wardens called a great many things restricted, and Xanthe had built her whole life in the gaps between their restrictions.

She knew these mountains the way she knew her own hands. Not by maps — maps were for people who trusted paper more than stone — but by the small voices that lived here. The marmots who whistled warnings from the scree slopes. The choughs who rode the thermals and gossiped about which ledges held old eagle nests. The pika who had spent all summer cursing her in squeaks as she crossed their hayfields, because pikas held grudges the way priests held scripture.

Tonight the mountain was quiet.

Not sleeping-quiet. She knew sleeping-quiet; it had a texture to it, a soft breathing quality, the hush of small hearts beating slow beneath fur and feather. This was different. This was the silence of animals holding still, the way a warren goes silent when the hawk's shadow crosses the grass.

Xanthe eased her pack straps and listened with more than her ears.

Her gift was a secret she kept even from herself on most days — folded up small and hidden in the same deep pocket where she kept the memory of her mother's voice. Beast-speech. The old blood, the wild gift, the thing the Wardens burned women for two generations back and still wrote treatises about. She had never asked for it. It had simply always been there, a second language murmuring underneath the first: the irritation of a mule, the simple joy of a dog, the long slow thoughts of goats, which were mostly about food and hillsides. She did not command beasts, whatever the stories said. She could only listen, and answer, and be answered. It was less a power than a conversation the rest of the world had forgotten how to have.

And right now, every creature within the reach of her listening was saying the same thing.

*Down,* said the marmots, in the flat tone of absolute conviction. *Down down down.*

*Wrong,* said the choughs, wheeling somewhere overhead in the dark. *The sky is wrong.*

*Hide,* said everything else, in a hundred small voices, overlapping like rain.

Xanthe's mouth went dry. She had heard fear before — had heard it from a rabbit flushed by a fox, from a bear annoyed out of its winter sleep. This was not that. This was fear with no shape to it, no hawk-shadow, no wolf-scent. Fear of something the animals couldn't name, which meant it was something older than naming.

She should turn back. Three days to Vell's Hollow, five to the low country, and the moon-wool would keep. Every instinct honed by ten years of smuggling screamed at her to retrace her steps, to get below the treeline, to be small and gone.

Instead she looked up.

That was her mistake, or her fate — she never decided which. She tilted her head back to check the weather, the way any sensible traveler would, and saw that the stars were going out.

Not clouding over. There was no cloud tonight; she'd have smelled the snow coming, felt it in the ache of her old knee scar. The stars were going out one by one, or so it seemed at first, and then in handfuls, and then in whole constellations, like candles snuffed by a passing hand. The Shepherd's Bow, which she'd steered by since she was nine years old and running her first cargo of untaxed salt — gone. The Weeping Sisters, trailing their faint silver veil across the northern sky — gone. The dark came down not like night but like a lid, pressing, total, and where the Milky Way had spilled its river of light there was now nothing at all, a blackness so complete it looked less like sky than like a wound.

Xanthe stood very still in the throat of the pass and felt dread and wonder collide in her chest like two rams butting heads.

It was beautiful. That was the terrible part. A sky without stars was a canvas, a held breath, a door left open onto something vast. Some buried girlhood part of her wanted to stand in it forever, arms spread, drinking in the enormity.

The rest of her remembered what her mother had said, once, in the low urgent voice she used for the truest things: *The stars aren't lights, little fox. They're eyes. And the wards of Thornfell were raised under them for a reason.*

The border wards.

Xanthe had grown up half in Thornfell and half outside it, and everyone knew the wards the way everyone knew the weather. Old magic, older than the Wardens' order, older than the realm itself if you believed the ballads. A lattice of protection woven along the mountain crests, humming quietly century after century, keeping out the things from beyond the peaks — the nameless things, the hungry things, the things the old songs only ever called *the otherside*. Nobody paid the wards much mind anymore, the way nobody pays attention to a heart beating. They were simply there. They had always been there.

They were there now, too — she could feel them the way you feel a distant bell through the soles of your feet — and they were screaming.

There was no other word for it. The lattice that spanned the crest of the pass above her had begun to sing, high and thin and fraying, a note like a harp string stretched past bearing. And then, one after another, in a cascade that ran eastward along the mountains faster than any eye could follow —

They snapped.

Xanthe felt each one break. That was the horror of her gift, in moments like this: she could not *not* hear it. Each ward-line sang as it died, a descending wail, and the sound of it went through her like a fishhook dragged through silk. One. Three. Seven. Dozens. The great ward-stone at Greytooth Pass, which she'd leaned against a hundred times while sharing dried plums with the pikas, gave a cry like a dying swan and went silent. The lattice that had stood for a thousand years came apart in the space of a hundred heartbeats, cut strings singing out into the dark, and when the last of them broke, the silence that followed was worse than the breaking.

Because in that silence, from far below and behind her — south, toward the heart of the realm, toward the Spire city gleaming in its valley like a blade laid on velvet — Xanthe heard something else.

A heartbeat. Enormous. Slow. Struggling.

It came up through the bones of the mountain rather than the air, and it was the loneliest sound she had ever heard: a vast slow pulse, each beat weaker than the last, like a fire sinking into its own ash. Something below the earth. Something caged. Something that had been burning, once, so brightly that entire wards had been strung from its light like lanterns from a single flame —

— and was now guttering.

*Oh,* Xanthe thought, with a clarity that felt like falling. *Oh, that's what fed them. That's what they've been feeding on.*

The stars hadn't gone out. She understood that suddenly, the way you understand a thing you'll spend years unpacking. The stars had been *hidden* — veiled, smothered, drawn behind some curtain — because something in the deep places of the world had stopped shining, and the sky above Thornfell had been reflecting its light all along, the way snow reflects a fire. Take away the fire, and the reflection dies with it.

Every star in the sky, and it came down to one dying light beneath the Spire.

Xanthe became aware, distantly, that she was shaking. Her hands, her jaw, the fine tremor in her knees. Forty pounds of moon-wool pressed against her spine like a rebuke — *you are a smuggler, Xanthe Vane, you move goods, you do not move toward catastrophe* — and she told it to shut up, and it didn't listen, because wool never does.

Then the fox cried out.

It came from downslope, maybe fifty yards, a sound she knew in her marrow: the thin, cracked scream of an animal in pain and past dignity. Any other night she might have passed it by — hard years teach hard lessons, and a smuggler who stops for every wounded creature ends up a graveyard of good intentions. But not tonight. Tonight the whole world was screaming in frequencies only she could hear, and this one voice cut through the rest like a knife through fog.

She found it in the scree below the trail, tumbled against a boulder. A red fox, vixen by the slight build, one foreleg bent wrong beneath her, flank dark with blood that was blacker than the starless night. A rockfall, maybe — the mountain had shuddered when the wards broke, she realized now, a tremor she'd taken for imagination. Or something worse. Something that had come through the gap.

The vixen's eyes found Xanthe in the darkness, and they were bright with a feverish intelligence, and her mouth opened, and she spoke.

"*Help.*"

One word. Ragged, wet, shaped by a throat never meant for human language. To anyone else it would have been a yelp, a cry, nothing. To Xanthe it landed like a fist.

She dropped to her knees in the scree before she'd decided to move. "I'm here." Her own voice sounded strange, rusty — she hadn't spoken beast-tongue aloud to anything but mules in months. "I'm here, sister, I've got you."

"*Help,*" the fox said again, and then, horribly, "*it's dark inside me. The fire is going out of everything. Can't you feel it? The fire is going out.*"

"I feel it." Xanthe shrugged her pack off one-handed, working by touch, pulling the roll of linen she carried for wrapping cargo — moon-wool, actually, a scrap of seconds-grade she'd allowed herself, because even smugglers got cold. "What happened? What hurt you?"

But the vixen wasn't listening anymore. Her head had turned, nose working, ears flat, and what poured out of her next came fast and broken, a flood of scent-images and terror given words:

"*Big feet. Many big feet, coming up the pass, smelling of iron and smoke. They're looking. They're looking for the one who hears us. They're calling her witch-beast, speaker-of-beasts, they have her face on paper, they will pay silver for her — run, run, sister-who-listens, RUN —*"

Xanthe's blood turned to ice water.

Wardens. On the pass. Tonight, within hours of the wards falling — which meant they'd known, or been sent, or both, and either way the hunt had already begun, and the shape of it had her name on it. *Beast-speaker.* The old accusation. The one that ended in fires.

Later — much later, in the years of story that grew out of this night — people would ask Xanthe why she did what she did next. Why she knelt in the open scree under a starless sky, with Warden lanterns already bobbing somewhere down the pass, and set the fox's leg with splints from kindling wood and strips of her own shirt, and bound the wound with contraband moon-wool that glowed faintly against the dark fur like a captured piece of the vanished sky. Why she fed the vixen water from her flask drop by drop, and spoke to her in the old tongue until the animal's breathing steadied, and finally lifted her — forty pounds of wool be damned — and tucked her inside her coat against her heartbeat.

Why she ran *toward* the Spire instead of away from it.

The honest answer was that she didn't decide. The honest answer was that a dying fox had told her the fire was going out of the world, and Xanthe Vane had spent her whole life listening to creatures smaller and wiser than herself, and she was not going to start ignoring them on the night the stars went dark.

"Where does it hurt worst?" she'd asked the fox, near the end, when the leg was splinted and the bleeding slowed.

The vixen had pressed her hot forehead against Xanthe's wrist and answered with an image instead of words: a tower, impossibly tall, piercing the clouds. Beneath its roots, a cavern. In the cavern, a coil of scales gone dull as old bronze, and a heart that beat like a funeral drum.

And around that heart, chains. And beside those chains, a figure in white-and-gold, lifting something glowing from the dragon's opened chest, smiling the smile of a person doing a kindness.

Xanthe had never seen the Spire. She'd never seen a dragon. No one living had — the last one had vanished beneath the Warden citadel a hundred years ago, according to the songs, and the songs disagreed about whether it had died or slept or never existed at all.

But the fox had seen it. Seen it the way animals see things — in the ground's memory, in the dreams that rise up through roots. And the fox was showing it to Xanthe now, pressing it into her mind like a brand, along with one final word, spoken clearly enough to chill the marrow:

"*Hurry.*"

Far down the pass, a Warden horn sounded — one long note, then another, hunting-call cadence. Lantern-light flickered between the pines like will-o'-wisps with badges.

Xanthe Vane pulled her hood up, shifted the fox gently against her chest, felt the small ragged heartbeat stuttering there beneath her own, and started north.

Toward the Spire. Toward the caged fire. Toward whatever was waiting in the dark where the stars used to be.

Behind her, the mountain held its breath.

Ahead, in the black vault of the sky where the Shepherd's Bow had hung all her life, something moved — vast and slow and winged, or perhaps only a trick of her terrified eyes, a suggestion of scale against the void.

The fox whimpered once, softly, and pressed closer.

"*Hurry,*" she said again.

Xanthe ran.
