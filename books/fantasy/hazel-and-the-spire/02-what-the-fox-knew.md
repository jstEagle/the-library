## 2. What the Fox Knew

The waystation had been a shrine once. Xanthe could tell by the shape of it — the round hearth-stone at the center where an altar fire would have burned, the niches in the walls still holding the ghosts of offerings, dried wax pooled like old tears on the shelves. Someone had walled up the eastern arch and put a roof of slate over what remained, and now travelers called it a waystation because that was easier than calling it what it was: a place where people used to pray to things they no longer believed in.

She barred the door with a length of mountain ash she found leaning in the corner — someone's abandoned walking staff, or someone's abandoned hope — and only then let herself sink down against the wall and shake.

Her hands were still trembling. She held them up to the light of her single candle and watched them shudder like leaves, and thought, *this is what fear looks like when it has nowhere left to go.*

Outside, the night was wrong. She had known it was wrong since the stars went out, but here, in this stone room with its dead hearth, the wrongness pressed against the walls like floodwater. No owl called. No nightjar churred from the scree slopes. Even the wind seemed to move without sound, as though the whole world were holding its breath behind a closed door.

And in the corner, beneath the tattered remains of an old traveling cloak, something breathed.

* * *

She had carried the fox for two miles.

It had come to her on the pass, out of the sudden starless dark, limping and trailing blood, and it had done the one thing no wild creature should ever do: it had walked straight to her. Not away from her. To her. It had looked up into her face with eyes gone glassy with pain, and it had said — in a voice like dry leaves, in words that existed nowhere outside the secret room of her skull —

*Help me, speaker. Please. Before I forget how.*

So she had knelt in the middle of the trail with Warden hounds baying somewhere below and bound the fox's wounds with strips of her own shirt, and wrapped it in the oilcloth from her pack, and run. Moon-wool smuggler, beast-speaker, fugitive-in-the-making — she had run through the Thornfell passes under a sky with no stars in it, carrying a dying fox against her chest like a stolen child.

Now the fox lay where she had set it, near the warmth of the candle, breathing in shallow, rattling pulls. It was a red vixen, young, its coat darkened with blood along the flank where something — a snare, a hound, a Warden's blade — had opened it to the bone. Xanthe had done what field-craft she knew: cleaned it, closed it, packed it with the yarrow salve Marisol-of-the-market sold back in Lowmarsh for coppers. But the wound wasn't the worst of it. The worst of it was the way the fox's ears kept twitching toward nothing, tracking sounds that weren't there, and the way its nose worked at air that smelled of nothing, as if the world itself had been pulled out from under its senses.

*They took the lights,* the fox had whispered on the trail, between gasps. *They took all the little fires in the sky.*

Xanthe fed the candle another inch of wick and crouched beside the animal. "Can you drink?" she asked aloud, then caught herself and thought it instead, shaping the words carefully the way her grandmother had taught her — not pushing, just offering, the way you'd offer a hand to a strange dog. *Can you drink?*

The fox's ear swiveled toward her. Its eyes opened.

*You're still there,* it said, wonderingly. *Everything else went quiet, but you're still there.*

"I'm still here." Her voice came out rougher than she meant it to. She dipped a rag in her water skin and held it to the vixen's muzzle. The fox drank in tiny, desperate laps. "What happened tonight? On the mountain. The animals — they all went silent at once, every one of them, like—"

*Like a door closing,* the fox finished. It tried to lift its head; Xanthe eased it back down with two fingers behind its ears, and the vixen allowed it, which told her more about how bad off it was than any wound. *We felt it go. All of us. Every living thing on this side of the mountains felt it go.* A shiver ran through the small body. *The wards.*

"The border wards." Everyone knew the wards. They'd stood longer than the kingdom, longer than the roads — woven into the passes of Thornfell by hands no history book named, humming so constantly and so low that nobody heard them anymore, the way you stop hearing your own heartbeat. They kept the old things out. Or in. Depending on who you asked, and how much ale they'd had.

*They didn't fall,* the fox said. *That's the thing you have to understand, speaker. Falling is fast. This was slow. Like watching a great beast bleed out from a wound too deep to close. We felt it start years ago, maybe. Getting thinner every winter. And tonight it finally tore, and everything the wards were made of came pouring out of the world, and the sky—"*

The fox stopped. Its breath hitched.

"—the sky went dark," Xanthe finished quietly. "Because the wards were made of starlight."

*Of what the starlight became,* the fox corrected, and there was something in its tone — an oldness, a gravity that did not belong in so small an animal — that made the hair rise on Xanthe's arms. *You carry moon-wool. You know wool holds what sheep see. You know some things drink light and keep it. Your grandmothers knew. The ones who built the wards knew best of all.*

Xanthe sat back on her heels. Her heart was doing something complicated and painful in her chest. "Tell me," she said. "All of it. Whatever you know."

The fox was quiet a long moment. Outside, the wind moved through the pass with that same terrible soundlessness. When the vixen spoke again, its voice had dropped to almost nothing, the way creatures speak when they speak of things larger than themselves.

*Beneath the Spire,* it said, *there is a fire that has burned for a thousand years. Older than the wards. Older than the kingdom. The first men found it sleeping under the mountain and they were wise enough, then, to kneel. They built their Spire above it like a crown. They fed it. Tended it. Called it holy.*

"And the wards?"

*Woven from its fire,* the fox said simply. *Every thread of them. Dragon-fire spun thin and stretched across the passes, a hundred miles of burning light strung between the peaks. That is why the borders held when nothing else in the world could hold them. Not walls. Not armies. One captive flame, giving and giving and giving, for a hundred years.*

Xanthe's mouth had gone dry. "A dragon," she whispered. "There's a dragon under the Spire."

*Was,* said the fox. *Is. I don't know the word anymore. Tonight, when the wards tore, we all felt it — the caged fire going dark. Feeling it fade. Feeling itself die, after a century of being drained to keep strangers safe. Every animal from here to the sea felt that, speaker. Do you understand? The oldest thing alive is dying alone under a city of men, and the men above it lit candles and told each other everything was fine.*

The candle guttered. Neither of them moved.

Xanthe thought of the stories her grandmother told, back when Xanthe was small enough to sit under the loom while the shuttle clacked — stories of the Dragonfast, of the war that ended when the last dragon was taken alive, of the peace bought at a price nobody spoke of aloud. She had thought they were stories. Grandmothers' embroidery, bright threads over bare cloth.

But she had heard the wards hum. She had slept in their shadow her whole life. And tonight she had felt them snap like cut harp strings, one after another after another, a sound she would hear until she died.

"Why are you telling me?" she asked. "Why come to me? There are healers in the valley towns. There are—"

*Because you're the only one left who can hear,* the fox said, and there was no accusation in it, only exhaustion. *Do you know how few of you there are now? Once, every village had a speaker. Now the Wardens test children at seven winters and take the ones who hear, and the families learn to say their daughter died of fever. You hid well. Your grandmother hid you well. But hiding is over now, speaker. The Wardens know what broke the wards. They've always known. And they will burn every hedge and hollow in Thornfell looking for someone to blame — and someone who can talk to beasts is exactly the kind of someone they need.*

As if summoned by the words — and later, Xanthe would wonder if the fox had somehow known, had heard them coming on frequencies of fear that human ears couldn't touch — hooves sounded on the trail below the waystation.

Many hooves. Moving fast.

The fox's head came up off the ground, ears rigid. *Riders,* it said. *Six. Maybe eight. Iron and horse-sweat and that oiled-leather smell they think hides them. Wardens.*

Xanthe was already moving. She pinched out the candle — darkness swallowed the room, leaving only the grey ghost-light of a starless sky through the slitted window — and gathered her pack with hands that had stopped shaking, because terror, past a certain point, curdles into something colder and more useful. She scooped the fox up against her chest, ignoring the hot spike of pain the vixen couldn't quite bite back.

"Quiet now," she breathed. *Quiet now,* she thought, wrapping the fox in the thought like a blanket.

*Where will you go?* the fox asked. Below, the hoofbeats slowed. Voices — clipped, disciplined, carrying in the dead air. Lantern-light bloomed against the shuttered window, throwing bars of gold across the floor.

"Away from the road. Down the goat track south, over the—"

*No,* said the fox.

Xanthe froze halfway to the back wall, where a loose stone marked the gap into the root cellar. "No?"

*South is what they'll expect. South is where everyone runs.* The vixen lifted its muzzle, and even wounded, even half-blind with pain, its dark eyes caught the lantern-glow and held it, and there was something ancient looking out of that small face, something that had watched a hundred winters die. *You heard what I told you. The caged fire is going dark. In days, maybe hours, it goes out forever — and everything your grandmothers wove, every gift like yours, every green and growing magic in this world, gutters with it. Or—*

"Or what?"

*Or someone carries it out.* The fox's breath was failing; Xanthe could feel the small heart stuttering against her own ribs, two hearts beating out of rhythm, out of time. *The High Warden has been drinking from the flame for years, speaker. Sipping. Hoarding. That's why the wards thinned — he was skimming the cup before it reached the saucer. He means to be the only fire left in the world, and everyone else the wax. If the flame dies in his vaults, it dies owned. But if it were taken — if someone carried it free, up the mountain, back to the crater where dragons once slept—*

Hooves crunched to a halt outside. A fist hammered the barred door. "Waystation! Open in the name of the Wardens of Thornfell! Travelers within, make yourselves known!"

Xanthe's blood turned to ice water. She looked at the door. She looked at the loose stone and the black gap of the cellar beyond it, the road south, the valley towns, the safe direction, the sensible direction, the direction every instinct she owned screamed at her to take.

*They'll search the cellar,* the fox murmured. *They always do. But the roof — there's a gap under the old eaves, I smelled pigeon on the wind. Small. Too small for a man with a pack. But you're small, speaker. Smugglers are small.*

"You want me to climb onto a roof with Wardens at the door."

*I want you to live long enough to choose,* the fox said. *That's all any of us ever wanted.*

The hammering came again, harder. "Open the door or we break it!"

Xanthe Vane had spent her whole life learning the geography of escape — every bolt-hole, every false wall, every gap in every fence between Lowmarsh and the sea. She had never once, in twenty-six years, pointed those skills at anything bigger than a crate of untaxed moon-wool.

She looked down at the fox in her arms. She thought of a fire older than kingdoms, dying alone in the dark under a city of stone. She thought of her grandmother's hands on the loom, and the story she'd never finished, and the question Xanthe had been too young to ask:

*What happened to the last dragon, child? What do you think pays for your peace?*

The door splintered at its hinges.

Xanthe went up — up the shelving, up onto the joists, silent as smoke, the fox pressed to her chest and its whisper threading through her mind like a guide-rope: *left, left, the gap, mind the nail — yes — there.* Behind her, the door burst inward and boots thundered across the floor she had vacated, and a voice she would learn to hate called out into the dark:

"She's here. The tracker says there's warm blood, fresh. Find the beast-speaker!"

Beast-speaker. So they already knew. So it had already begun.

Crouched in the freezing dark under the eaves, with the whole starless sky spread out above her like a lid on the world, Xanthe felt her fear finish its long slow turn — from the thing that makes you run away into the thing that tells you exactly where to run.

South was safety. South was small.

North, forty leagues distant, the Spire stood black against the dead horizon, and beneath it a fire was going out.

"Hold on, old girl," she whispered to the fox, to herself, to whatever was left listening in a world gone silent. "We're going to the Spire."

Below her, through a crack in the slates, she saw the lanterns swing, and heard a Warden sergeant say, in a voice flat with routine cruelty:

"Sweep the valley. If she hears animals, she'll run for the wilds. Burn the hedgerows if you have to."

And Xanthe, already moving north along the rooftop ridge, smiled in the dark — a small, sharp, terrified smile — because they were looking everywhere except the one place she was going.

* * *

Dawn found her three miles into the high country, sheltering in a hollow of granite with the fox warm against her throat, watching the sun rise into a sky that still held no stars from the night before — as if the dark had left a stain.

*They'll follow the valleys,* the vixen murmured, drowsing. *For a day, maybe two. Then the trackers will read the ground right and come uphill. You'll need help, speaker. Friends. Smugglers travel alone, but no one crosses Thornfell alone now.*

"I know," Xanthe said, and thought of Greytooth Pass ahead, and the scattered folk who worked it — a stonemason too big for anyone's comfort, a witch who sold charms too honest for the towns, a child quick-fingered enough to steal the whiskers off a cat. Strangers. Maybe allies. Maybe nothing.

Above the eastern peaks, the sun cleared the ridge, and its light ran down the mountainside and touched the far-off shape of the Spire, forty leagues north, a needle of black stone piercing the pale morning.

Somewhere under that needle, a fire was dying.

"Not today," Xanthe whispered to it, across all those miles, not knowing yet whether she was making a promise or a prayer. "Hold on. I'm coming."
