## 3. The Captain Without a Badge

The rain had not stopped for three days when Corin Ashe finally surrendered to it.

He sat in the back room of the Drowned Lantern, a tavern at the wrong end of a river town whose name he had never bothered to learn, because towns like this one existed only as waypoints — places where the road bent and men like him were permitted to bend with it. His cloak hung from a peg by the fire, dripping onto flagstones that had seen a century of worse. His boots had given up pretending to be waterproof sometime around the second day. The tavern-keeper's wife had taken pity on him twice already, sliding him bowls of barley stew he hadn't ordered and couldn't quite afford, and each time she'd done it without a word, the way people feed stray dogs.

He was trying very hard not to be a stray dog.

The badge lay in his breast pocket, wrapped in oilcloth. Not discarded — he wasn't a man who discarded things — but folded away, the way you fold away a letter that has stopped meaning what it used to mean. A Warden's badge of the Third Circle, silver chased with mountain ash leaves, the clasp cracked where a crossbow bolt had once struck it and been turned aside by nothing more than luck and leather. He had carried it eleven years. It had made him someone. Now it made him no one, and he could not decide which truth hurt more.

Disgrace, in the Wardens, came in flavors. There was the flavor earned by cowardice, which was rare, because cowards rarely lasted long enough to be disgraced. There was the flavor earned by cruelty, which was commoner than the Order admitted and punished less often than it pretended. And then there was the flavor Corin wore, which had no name in any regulation he'd ever read: the flavor of being right at the wrong moment, in front of the wrong people, with the wrong man's blood on your report.

Two years ago, at the garrison of Coldmere, he had written the truth about a raid that had gone wrong — a raid ordered by a superior, executed badly, ending with three smugglers dead who should have been taken alive, and one of them a boy of perhaps fifteen. The report had been honest. The report had been damning. The report had named names, and one of those names had since risen high enough that honesty had become an inconvenience rather than a virtue.

Corin Ashe, captain, hero of the Greytooth action, savior of the Harrow Bridge convoy, was quietly relieved of command, stripped of active duty, and posted to nowhere, to do nothing, until such time as he learned to be less himself.

He had spent two years learning nothing of the kind.

* * *

The summons came at dusk, delivered by a courier too young to grow proper stubble, riding a horse too fine for the errand. That combination alone told Corin something was wrong. Nobody wasted good horseflesh on a disgraced captain.

The boy stood in the doorway of the Drowned Lantern's common room, rain streaming off his oilskin hood, and looked around with the particular expression of someone who has been told exactly whom to find and is now discovering that finding a man and respecting him are different tasks entirely.

"Captain Ashe?"

"Was." Corin didn't look up from the map he was sketching on the tabletop in spilled ale — the old habit of hands that needed work. "Sit down before you drip on something."

"I've orders." The boy produced a leather dispatch case, sealed with grey wax and the sigil of the High Warden's own office — not a regional seal, not a garrison stamp. The top of the whole apparatus. "From Sarel Voss herself."

That got Corin's attention. He dried his hands on his shirt, took the case, and broke the seal with his thumbnail while the boy hovered, uncertain whether to be dismissed or to witness history.

The letter inside was brief, formal, and written in a hand so controlled it looked typeset. *By order of the High Warden of Thornfell, all loyal officers are commanded to seek and apprehend the individual known to be a beast-speaker, suspected of complicity in the collapse of the border wards on the night of the vanished stars. The individual travels alone, carries contraband, and is considered dangerous. Reward commensurate with service. Report findings through established channels only.*

Corin read it twice. Then a third time, slower, because the third reading was where the trap showed itself.

*A beast-speaker.* The words sat strangely on the page, like a term borrowed from a language the writer didn't speak. Beast-speakers weren't criminals. They weren't even, strictly speaking, illegal — the old law had never known what to do with them, so it had simply looked away, the way the law looks away from most things it doesn't understand. They were hedge-folk, herders, women who talked to crows and men who could calm a spooked ox with a word. Half the mountain villages had one and pretended they didn't. The Wardens themselves had occasionally used them — informally, off the books, when a tracker went missing or livestock scattered before a storm. Corin had met three in his years of service. Two had helped him. One had saved his life.

And now the High Warden's office wanted one hunted like a wolf with rabies, blamed for the fall of the wards, with a reward attached and channels specified — *established channels only*, which meant no letters home, no garrison gossip, no witnesses who might ask inconvenient questions about what, precisely, a lone woman with a pack-mule had supposedly done to bring down wards that had stood for a hundred years.

Corin set the letter down. The boy was still standing there, dripping, waiting for acknowledgment.

"You rode from the Spire," Corin said.

"Yes, sir."

"In this weather. On that horse." He glanced out the window at the animal, a dappled grey worth more than the tavern. "Who gave you the mount?"

"The quartermaster at Thornfell Gate, sir. Captain Hessler's requisition."

Hessler. Corin turned the name over. Aldric Hessler, who had been a lieutenant under him at Coldmere, who had wept at the funeral of the boy their bad raid had killed, and who had then, with the fluid pragmatism of the ambitious, attached himself to the rising star of the High Warden's inner circle. Hessler was sending fast horses and sealed orders after a beast-speaker. Which meant Hessler knew things. Which meant the hunt was real, and large, and moving fast.

"Where did you pick up my trail?" Corin asked.

"The garrison at Marlow Ford, sir. They said you'd come through heading north." The boy hesitated. "They also said—" He caught himself, visibly deciding whether obedience or curiosity would get him in worse trouble. Curiosity lost. "They said I should tell you the High Warden expects you to accept, sir. Formally. In writing. And that refusal would be... noted."

There it was. Not an offer. A hook.

Sarel Voss had not summoned a disgraced captain out of respect for his record. She had summoned him because a disgraced captain was a man with one door left open, and men with one door open walk through it however it's held. Accept, and he was hers — her hound, her deniable instrument, hunting a woman through the mountains with no oversight and no witnesses. Refuse, and the note in his file grew a new line, and the door closed, and there was nothing left in the world for Corin Ashe but rain and barley stew.

He looked at the boy for a long moment. "Get upstairs. Ask Marta for a room and tell her it's on my account. Dry off. We ride at first light."

"Yes, sir." Relief flooded the young face — relief at being given orders, any orders, by anyone who sounded like he knew what he was doing. He was halfway to the stairs before Corin added:

"And leave the horse in the stable. If she founders, I'll answer for it."

The boy blinked, nodded, and vanished upward. Corin sat alone with the letter, the rain, and the badge wrapped in oilcloth against his chest, and understood with perfect clarity that he had just been conscripted into something, though he could not yet see its shape.

* * *

He walked out into the wet dark because sitting still had become unbearable.

The river town at night was a smear of lamplight and mud, boats knocking against pilings, the smell of fish and woodsmoke braided together. Northward, past the last roofs, the mountains stood up against the sky like black teeth — and above them, where the stars should have been, nothing. The same nothing every night now, a week since the vanishing. Corin had grown up under these skies. He knew the constellations the way other men knew the faces of their mothers: the Antlered Stag, the Weaver's Loom, the long bright river of the Starfall Road that the old songs said was the path the dragons flew between worlds. All of it gone. The sky above Thornfell was a lid, smooth and lightless, and everyone in the town below went about their business beneath it as if a missing sky were a thing a person could simply adjust to.

Nobody adjusted. They just stopped looking up. That was worse.

He found himself at the small shrine at the river's edge — not a temple, nothing so grand; just a stone alcove where travelers left offerings to whatever listened. A candle stub guttering behind glass. A child's knitted mitten, left by someone who believed the dead could feel cold. Corin stood there longer than he meant to, rain running off his jaw, and let himself remember.

Greytooth Pass, four years ago. Winter. He'd been tracking a smuggling ring through the high snowfields with two rangers, both of them dead by the second day, taken by an avalanche that had come down without warning — without *natural* warning, though he hadn't known then what unnatural warning sounded like. Corin had survived by falling into a crevasse the avalanche skipped over, and had lain at the bottom of it with a shattered leg, freezing, bleeding, shouting himself hoarse into ice that swallowed sound.

It was the third night when she found him. He'd heard her before he saw her — voices, plural, murmuring in the dark above him, and he'd thought at first he was hallucinating, because one of the voices was a woman's and the others were... not. A raven's croak shaping itself around human syllables. A fox's yip carrying words. He'd lain there listening to animals talk in the dark and had thought, quite calmly, *so this is how it ends, mad and frozen in a hole.*

Then a rope came down, and a woman climbed down it into the crevasse with a lantern and a splint kit, and she looked at him with eyes the color of wet slate and said, "You're louder than the ravens said you'd be."

Xanthe Vane. He hadn't known her name then. He'd known her only as the stranger who stayed with him two days in the ice while her animals — her ravens, her foxes, a half-wild mountain cat that slept against his ruined leg and kept it warm — brought her firewood and food from her camp above. She'd set the leg with a skill that outmatched any field medic he'd served with. She'd fed him broth melted from snow. And on the second night, feverish, drifting, he'd heard her talking to the cat about him — not *to* him, *about* him, low and unguarded, the way you talk about someone who can't hear you — saying, *"He's hurting, but he's kind. You can tell. Even the trapped ones, if they're kind, they don't fight the ones helping them."*

Kind. No one in the Wardens had called him that in years. Kindness wasn't a professional attribute. It wasn't in the regulations. But she'd seen it in him at the bottom of a hole in the world, when he had nothing else left to be, and she had pulled him out of the dark because of it.

Three months later, recovered, he'd tried to find her again. Asked at the passes, at the waystations, put out quiet inquiries through people who moved in the mountains' gray economy. Nothing. She was smoke. She was a rumor with a face. He'd told himself she was probably better off unfound — a woman like that, known to the Wardens, was a woman in danger — and he'd let it go, and told himself that too was a kindness.

Now her description sat in a sealed letter from the High Warden of Thornfell, and the reward on her head had a number, and the charge was treason against wards he suddenly understood he'd never truly comprehended.

Because here was the thing that had been gnawing at him since the first briefing, the thing the official account could not digest: the wards had not failed. Wards did not fail. The border wards of Thornfell were the oldest working magic in the realm, maintained by a standing office of wardwrights, inspected seasonally, fed — fed, the briefings said, by an arrangement dating to the Dragon Accords, some source of power the documents described only as *the traditional provision*. Every officer learned the phrase and no officer ever asked what it meant. Asking questions about the provision was the fastest way Corin knew of to end a career early.

The wards had not failed. They had been *cut*. Cut like harp strings, the survivors said, all along the border at once, in a single night, while the stars went out overhead. Sabotage on that scale required access, coordination, knowledge — not a lone woman with a mule and a gift for talking to foxes. Unless the lone woman was the scapegoat. Unless the story had been written before the crime.

Corin stood at the shrine in the rain and felt the two halves of himself grind against each other like millstones.

One half was the Warden. Eleven years of oaths, of structure, of belonging to something larger than his own appetites. The Order had taken a rough-edged boy from a mining family and made him a captain, had given his life a spine. Duty wasn't an abstraction to him; it was load-bearing. Remove it and he wasn't sure what remained standing.

The other half was the man in the crevasse, who owed a debt no ledger recorded, to a woman who had asked nothing in return, and who was now being hunted by the institution that had made him — hunted on charges that smelled, from the first reading, like rot dressed in seals and wax.

Duty said: accept the commission, find her, bring her in, trust the process, believe that the Order's justice, however slow, bends toward truth.

Gratitude said: she pulled you out of a hole in the world, and now the world is digging one for her.

And underneath both, quieter than either, a third voice — the voice that had gotten him disgraced in the first place, the voice that had insisted on writing the true report about Coldmere — said: *you know what the Order does to inconvenient truths. You have seen it. You have bled for it.*

* * *

First light came grey and starless, as every light came now.

Corin stood in the stable yard in full kit — the old kit, brushed and oiled, the sword with the notched guard he'd never replaced because the notch had a story, the cracked badge finally unwrapped and pinned to his cloak where it belonged, or where it had once belonged, or where wearing it might still mean something if he could decide what. The courier-boy stood beside his fine dappled horse, looking nervous and scrubbed and faintly heroic, the way young men look when they haven't yet learned which orders deserve their heroes.

"Sir?" the boy said. "The reply. The garrison at Marlow Ford needs your formal acceptance for the dispatch north."

Corin took out paper and pencil — pencil, not ink; ink was for decisions that deserved permanence — and wrote six lines. He accepted the commission. He would pursue the suspect known as Xanthe Vane, beast-speaker, through the northern passes. He would report through established channels. He signed it *C. Ashe, Capt. (ret. active service)*, a contradiction in terms that he chose deliberately, a small insolence hidden in plain sight, and sealed it without letting himself think too hard about what he'd just done.

Because here was the arithmetic he'd performed in the dark hours, alone, staring at the starless ceiling: if he refused, Voss would simply send someone else. Hessler, probably — Hessler, who owed his rise to the same machinery that had buried Corin, who would hunt Xanthe Vane with the clean enthusiasm of a man who had never once doubted his masters. Hessler would find her. Hessler would put a bolt in her or a chain, and file her under traitor, and the truth of the fallen wards would go into the ground with her.

If he accepted, then for a while — weeks, maybe, if the mountains were kind — the hunt would belong to a man who did not want it to succeed too quickly. A man who knew the passes better than any officer in the Order. A man who could slow the net, misdirect it, widen the search into country where a clever woman and her animals could run forever. A man who told himself, walking the knife-edge of it, that he was merely buying time — time in which the truth might surface, time in which he might learn what was actually beneath the Spire, time in which duty and gratitude might, by some miracle, stop pointing in opposite directions.

It was self-deception and he knew it. It was also the only move on the board that didn't end with her blood on his hands or his honor in the mud. So he took it, and pinned on the badge, and told the boy to ride hard for Marlow Ford.

They rode north through the morning, up out of the riverlands into the foothills, the road climbing through pine and shadow. By midday the rain had turned to sleet. By late afternoon they reached the garrison post at Marlow Ford, a squat stone keep squatting squatter than ever under the lightless sky, and Corin handed over his sealed acceptance to a sergeant who saluted with the careful blankness of a man following orders he didn't examine.

Inside, warm and low-ceilinged, the garrison commander — a grizzled veteran named Osk, who had served under Corin once, briefly, before politics separated them — poured him tea without being asked and leaned close.

"You're taking the beast-speaker commission," Osk said. Not a question.

"I am."

Osk studied him for a long moment over the rim of his cup. Then, quietly, in the tone men use for things they'll deny having said: "Description came down yesterday. Updated. Dark hair, slate-grey eyes, thirty-odd years, travels with animals that act like they're under orders. Last seen heading toward Greytooth Pass." He paused. "You know Greytooth."

"I was pulled out of a crevasse there once," Corin said. "By a woman matching that description."

Osk set down his cup. The silence between them filled with everything neither man could safely say aloud — and then Osk said the smallest possible version of it, the version that could pass for coincidence if anyone asked:

"Aye. Funny thing, the mountains. Full of holes a body falls into. And sometimes," he added, gathering the dispatch papers, straightening, becoming a garrison commander again, "sometimes the holes are the only place left to hide."

Corin Ashe stood in the lamplight of a garrison under a starless sky, wearing a badge that no longer protected anyone, carrying orders he intended to betray in spirit if never in letter, and understood that he had just crossed a line he could not see the far side of.

North, beyond the pass, somewhere in the white silence, a woman was running toward the Spire instead of away from it — toward the thing that had cut the wards, toward the caged fire the fox had spoken of, toward whatever truth the High Warden was willing to kill to bury.

And Corin Ashe, captain without a command, was going to follow her.
