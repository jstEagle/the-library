## 4. Smoke on the Pass

Greytooth Pass had a name like a warning, and for once the land was honest about itself.

Xanthe came down from the high snowfields with her hood up and the fox bundled against her chest inside her coat, a knot of warmth and misery that had not stopped trembling since the waystation. The pass itself was a throat of grey stone between two shoulders of mountain, narrow enough that a cart's axle would scrape both walls at once, and the wind funneled through it with a sound like someone breathing through gritted teeth. No stars overhead. There were no stars anywhere now — just that low, wrong ceiling of cloudless dark, as if the sky had been shuttered from the other side.

*They took them,* the fox had said. *The ones who feed on fire. They took the small lights first.*

She tried not to think about it. She thought about nothing else.

Her plan was simple, which was to say it was barely a plan at all: move north through Greytooth before word of her could outrun her, keep off the Warden roads, buy passage or steal it in the lowland towns beyond, and reach the Spire before whatever was dying beneath it finished dying. She had three days of hardtack, half a skin of wine she was saving for the fox's pain, a knife, and a talent she had spent thirty years pretending was a party trick.

It would have to be enough. It had always been enough before.

The trouble with Greytooth was that everyone knew it was the only way through. Including, apparently, the Wardens.

She smelled the smoke before she saw anything else — not woodsmoke, but lamp-oil and cold iron and that particular scorched-wool smell of Warden cloaks drying too close to a fire. Her feet stopped without her permission, the way they did when her body knew something her mind hadn't caught up to. The fox went rigid against her ribs.

*Six,* it breathed. *No. Seven. They are sitting quiet. Waiting for the rabbit.*

"Rabbit," Xanthe whispered. "That's flattering."

*It is accurate.*

She eased back along the trail until the wind's teeth covered the sound of her retreat, then climbed. There was a goat path above the pass proper — she'd used it twice in her smuggling years, once carrying moon-wool and once carrying a man who'd paid her very well not to ask what he'd done. It was narrow, ice-glazed, and suicidal in the dark, but the dark was total now, and total dark cut both ways. If she couldn't see the Wardens, they couldn't see her.

She was forty feet up the cliffside, pressed into a seam of rock with her boots wedged on ledges the width of her palm, when the trap sprang anyway.

Not for her. For whoever came behind her.

Lamplight bloomed in the throat of the pass below — three lanterns unshuttered at once, and voices, Warden voices, flat with procedure. *"Halt, in the name of the Vigil."* And then another voice, younger, cracking, and the sound of running feet, and Xanthe looked down into the pass and saw a child.

A small figure in an oversized coat bolted straight down the center of the pass, arms full of — she squinted — bread? A whole stolen loaf, still trailing its cloth. The child ran with the practiced economy of someone who had been chased many times and had opinions about how to do it well: jink left, jink right, use the shadows. But the shadows were all lit now, and there were Wardens at both ends of the throat, and Xanthe watched the noose close with a sick, familiar clenching in her stomach.

*"Stop, or we stop you,"* one of them called, and there was a note in it she recognized — the boredom of men who had been standing in a frozen pass for hours waiting for smugglers, and had been given a pickpocket instead, and intended to make the arrest worth their time.

The child skidded, turned, found both ends of the pass blocked, and did the thing that broke Xanthe's heart in a completely predictable place: stood up straight, lifted her chin, and glared at seven armed adults as if *they* were the ones who should be ashamed.

"Don't touch me," the child said. Her voice carried up the rock face, thin and furious. "I know my rights. I'm a citizen of Thornfell."

"You're a thief," said the nearest Warden, and reached for her.

Xanthe didn't decide. That was the truth of it, later, when she told the story and hated how noble it sounded. She didn't weigh risks or count swords. Her body simply moved, the same way it moved when a dying fox begged, and she was whistling — a long, falling whistle, the kind that carries over mountains, the kind every goatherd in the high country knows.

And the mountain answered.

Not goats. Something bigger. From the shadowed shelf above the far end of the pass came a grinding, groaning sound, like a hillside deciding to stand up, and then a shape detached itself from the darkness that made the Wardens' lanterns look suddenly small. It was man-shaped the way a barn is house-shaped. Eight feet of grey-green hide and knotted muscle, with a brow like a quarry overhang and eyes that caught the lamplight amber and calm. It wore a stonemason's leather apron, tool-holes empty, and it stepped down into the pass with the unhurried weight of a landslide that had somewhere to be.

"That'll be quite enough of that," it said, in a voice like gravel poured slow.

Everything happened at once after that, and Xanthe experienced most of it from her ledge, clinging to the rock with her heart slamming, watching chaos bloom below like ink in water.

The Wardens were trained, she'd give them that. Three of them turned on the troll with pikes leveled, calling the formal challenge — *"Beast-blooded, yield to the Vigil"* — which was, in Xanthe's professional opinion, the single stupidest sentence you could say to a troll. The big one didn't even break stride. He caught the first pike shaft in one hand, folded it like wet bread, and swatted the second Warden into the pass wall with the flat of his palm, gently, the way you'd move furniture you didn't own.

Then the air filled with light.

It came from the cliff opposite — a burst of green-gold sparks that hung in the air like a swarm of angry fireflies, and a woman's voice chanting something quick and old and slightly out of breath. The sparks dove. Where they touched, Wardens' cloaks began to smolder, and two of them broke and ran, which was the smartest tactical decision anyone in uniform made that night.

The child, meanwhile, had not wasted her gift. While every eye was on the troll and the witch, she'd gone low and fast, and Xanthe saw her dart between the legs of the last standing Warden — saw her hand flick — and then the man's sword belt simply wasn't on him anymore, and he was holding air, and the child was already twenty feet away, laughing, a bright and shocking sound in all that dark.

Which left one Warden. The sergeant, by his knots. He'd kept his head, backed against the wall, crossbow up, tracking — not the troll, not the witch, not the child.

Tracking the ledge where Xanthe crouched. Because he'd seen her whistle. Because someone had briefed them, and the briefing had said *beast-speaker*, and beast-speakers whistle.

"Bolt-hole's done," the fox breathed. "*Move.*"

She moved. The crossbow snapped and the bolt shrieked off the rock where her head had been, and then she was scrambling down the goat path with no technique whatsoever, trusting her hands more than her judgment, and the last six feet she simply fell, hit the frozen scree of the pass floor, rolled, and came up with her knife out facing the sergeant's leveled blade.

He was older than her. Tired around the eyes. He looked less like a hunter than a man doing arithmetic he didn't like.

"Xanthe Vane," he said. "By order of the High Warden's commission, I—"

Whatever he ordered her to do, she never learned, because the troll's hand closed over his entire sword arm from shoulder to wrist, and the witch's green sparks settled on his crossbow and ate the string, and the child appeared at his elbow and relieved him of his boot knife, his purse, and — with visible delight — his badge.

"Careful with that," the troll rumbled.

"It's mine now," the child said. "Finder's law."

The sergeant stood there, disarmed four times over by four people who had never met before tonight, and Xanthe watched something complicated move across his face. Not fear. Recognition, maybe — the look of a man realizing the story he'd been sent to enforce had holes in it the size of a vanished sky.

"The woman," he said quietly, to Xanthe. "They say you killed the wards."

"I was forty miles away selling wool," she said. "Ask your stars where I was. Oh — wait."

His jaw tightened. He looked up, briefly, at the starless dark, and Xanthe saw the arithmetic finish behind his eyes, and she understood that this man would not report the troll or the witch or the child if he could possibly avoid the paperwork. Whether that made him decent or merely tired, she couldn't say.

"Greytooth's closed," he said instead. "Wardens at both ends till the thaw. You want north, you go over the Shoulder, and you go hungry." He retrieved his ruined dignity piece by piece. "I never saw any of you."

"You definitely didn't," the child agreed, pocketing his badge.

He walked away down the pass, and the dark took him, and Xanthe let out a breath she felt she'd been holding since the night the sky went out.

* * *

They regrouped — there was no other word for it — in the lee of the troll's camp, a hollow under an overhang where a fire burned in a pit lined with flat stones so its light showed from nowhere. The troll, whose name was Brack, produced tea from a dented copper kettle the size of a cauldron. The witch, Marisol, sat as close to the flames as she could get and shivered anyway, wrapped in three shawls that had seen better decades. The child — Pip, she announced, "just Pip, don't make it weird" — divided the stolen loaf into five pieces with the solemnity of a priest, keeping the largest for herself on grounds she described as "finder's tax."

The fifth piece she set down in front of the fox.

Xanthe had unwrapped the animal as soon as they'd stopped moving, and now it lay in her lap, wound dressed with Marisol's moss-poultice, breathing shallow but steady. It raised its head at the offered bread.

*I like her,* it said.

"She likes you," Xanthe translated, and then heard herself, and closed her eyes. *Well. That's done, then.*

Because they were all staring at her. Brack with mild, patient curiosity, like a man watching weather. Marisol with the sharp hunger of a scholar finding a text she'd only read about. And Pip with wide, uncomplicated wonder, bread forgotten.

"You talked to it," Pip said. "Just now. Its ears went funny and you answered."

"Yes," Xanthe said. There didn't seem to be much point denying it. These three had just committed assault on Warden officers on her behalf; the least she owed them was honesty. "I hear beasts. Have since I was smaller than you. It's not a grand thing. Mostly it's dogs complaining about cats."

"What's it complaining about?" Pip demanded, leaning at the fox.

*Tell her I said she has crumbs in her hair,* the fox said, *and that her pockets smell of other people's silver, which is rude.*

Xanthe hesitated. Then, because the child had earned it: "It says your pockets smell of other people's silver."

Pip's grin split her whole face. "It's not wrong."

"And you," Xanthe said, turning to the others, because if she was doing this, she'd do it properly. "A troll stonemason who happens to be camped in exactly the pass where Wardens set an ambush, and a hedge-witch who can throw battle-lights, and a child who robs sergeants for sport. Nobody's that lucky. Who are you really?"

Brack sipped his tea. His amber eyes reflected the fire, and behind the firelight there was something old and careful.

"I'm a stonemason," he said. "Truly. I built the bridge at Millbrook, and the aqueduct at Corren's Ford, and the ward-stones at—" He stopped. Set down the cup. "At Thornfell. The border wards. My family has cut and laid the ward-stones of this march for nine generations. We cut them, and the Wardens fed them, and for nine generations it worked." He looked at his enormous hands, gentle around the little cup. "Three nights ago, the stones went silent. I felt it through my boots, fifty miles away, the way you feel a building settle wrong. So I came to see why. And I found soldiers hiding in my pass, hunting a woman, and I found myself asking: who feeds the wards now? And nobody would answer me. So I answered it myself, badly, by hitting people."

"He hit them *so gently*," Pip said admiringly.

"They were doing their duty badly," Brack said. "It seemed fair."

Marisol laughed — a dry, cracked sound, like pages turning. When she spoke, her accent was lowland, soft-edged, worn thin. "I'm easier. I was a hedge-witch in the Fenmarch villages. Birthings and blessings, wart-charms and weather-sense. Small magic. Cheap magic." She held up her hands; they trembled, and not from cold alone. "Except that magic isn't small anymore, is it? Everything's been getting harder since the stars went. My charms cost double the strength. My scrying's gone blind. Whatever the beasts drink from, girl, we all drink from it. And it's draining." She fixed Xanthe with eyes red-rimmed and fierce. "So when I heard the Wardens were hunting a beast-speaker — blaming *her* for the wards, as if one woman unmade nine hundred miles of border — I thought: either she's guilty, or they're lying. And I've met the Wardens' new commissions. They lie the way rivers run downhill."

"So you followed me," Xanthe said slowly.

"I followed the smoke," Marisol said. "You're easy to track. You leave kindness everywhere you go — fed animals, paid debts, healed livestock. Every village from here to the Spire has a story about a wool-trader who talks to foxes." Her mouth twisted. "You should be harder to find, dear. Kindness is a trail sign."

Xanthe looked down at the fox, breathing in her lap. At the child already asleep against Brack's enormous side, mid-chew, crumbs in her hair, a stolen Warden badge clutched in her fist like a talisman. At the witch feeding the fire stick by stick, muttering charms over it to make it burn warmer than wood should. At the mason watching the northern dark with the expression of a man reading cracks in a wall he loved.

She had spent her whole life traveling alone because alone was safe. Alone meant no one to betray, no one to bury, no one whose death you'd have to carry. Alone meant the gift stayed a secret and the secret stayed cheap.

Alone also meant that in the pass tonight, she'd have died on a sergeant's blade with the truth still in her coat, and no one would ever have known there was a truth at all.

"I'm going to the Spire," she said.

Brack and Marisol both looked at her.

"There's something under it. Something caged. The fox calls it the caged fire — the thing the wards drank from, the thing everything drinks from. And it's dying." Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "The Wardens didn't lose the wards. Someone *emptied* them. Someone's harvesting whatever fed them, and they're willing to hunt a smuggler across a mountain range to keep it quiet. I mean to find out what, and I mean to stop it, and I have a knife and half a skin of wine, so as plans go—"

"It's terrible," Marisol said.

"It's a suicide," Brack said, at the same moment.

"—it's terrible and it's a suicide," Xanthe agreed. "But it's going north either way. I wanted you to know what you saved in that pass. In case you want it back."

Silence, except the fire and the wind and the child's snoring. The fox opened one eye and regarded Xanthe with something that was almost pity.

*Ask them,* it said. *You great coward. Ask them to come.*

She didn't get the chance.

"We're coming," Brack said simply, as if announcing rain. "Someone must carry you over the Shoulder when you fall. Also, I wish to see what is under the Spire. I laid stones over it once. I would know what I built on."

Marisol snorted and pulled her shawls tighter. "I'm coming because my magic is dying and I want to look the thief in the eye before it takes the rest." She jabbed a stick at Xanthe. "But understand me, beast-speaker. I'm not your soldier. Hedge-witches don't take orders and we don't die for causes. I'm coming because the road's safer shared, and because—" she faltered, just for a breath, and Xanthe saw thirty years of solitary craft and solitary nights behind it, "—because the tea's better with company. That's all. Don't make it a speech."

"Wouldn't dream of it," Xanthe said.

From Brack's side, without opening her eyes, Pip mumbled: "I heard Spire. I'm in. Spire's got rich people." A pause. "Also you lot would die without me."

*She's not wrong,* the fox observed. *About the dying. Possibly not about the rest.*

Xanthe leaned back against warm stone, under a sky with no stars, surrounded by strangers who had bled for her before learning her name, and felt something in her chest that she refused, firmly, to call hope. It was too early. Hope got people killed on mountains.

But when she slept, she dreamed of fire — a vast, caged, guttering fire, deep beneath white roots and black stone — and in the dream the fire turned toward her, the way a lost animal turns toward a whistle.

And far to the south, though she couldn't have known it, a man without a badge was climbing into Greytooth Pass, following her trail of kindnesses, one day behind and closing.
