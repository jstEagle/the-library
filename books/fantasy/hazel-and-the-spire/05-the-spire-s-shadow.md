## 5. The Spire's Shadow

The road to the Spire city was a river of the desperate.

Xanthe had walked it for three days among them, and every step had taught her something the songs never sang. Refugees from the border villages moved in slow, straggling columns — farmers with their hands still smelling of turned earth they would never plant again, grandmothers carrying grandchildren on backs bent like winter branches, a boy of perhaps ten leading a blind mule that carried nothing but a birdcage wrapped in sacking. They had fled the night the stars went out, when the wards fell and the old stories crawled back over the mountains like smoke given legs. Now they came to the Spire because there was nowhere else, and the Spire did not want them.

She saw it before she saw the walls: the great pale needle of it rising out of the morning haze, so tall its crown dissolved into a sky that still felt wrong to her — too empty, too blue, scrubbed clean of anything but sun. A hundred years of lantern-light and legend had made the Spire the axis of the world, the place where order lived. Xanthe had smuggled past it a dozen times and always felt the same small awe, the way you feel standing under a cathedral roof. But she had come then as a ghost slipping along the edges. She came now with a dying fox in a basket on her shoulder and a question burning through her chest like swallowed embers, and awe curdled fast into something colder.

The gates were choked. Wardens in their grey-and-silver stood in ranks at the archway, sorting the flood of humanity into streams: those with papers, those with coin, those with neither. And above the gate, painted fresh that very week by the look of the ladders still leaning against the stone, hung a banner long enough to shroud a barn.

*THE STARS FELL BECAUSE OF THE UNFAITHFUL.*

Beneath it, in smaller letters, in a hand meant to look official and stern: *Report beast-speakers. Report strangers. Report your neighbors. The Warden protects the faithful.*

Xanthe stopped in the middle of the road and stared at it until her eyes watered, and what she felt then was not fear. It was a kind of grief with teeth.

"They're saying it was us," muttered an old woman beside her, a shepherd by her crook and her cracked knuckles, one eye clouded white. "The beast-talkers. The hedge-folk. Anyone who ever listened to anything that wasn't a priest or a Warden." She snorted, bitter as ash. "I've kept sheep sixty years. Sheep tell you things, if you've ears. That's not wickedness. That's just listening."

"Have you reported anyone?" Xanthe asked quietly.

"Aye," the old woman said, and shame crossed her face like weather. "My own sister's girl. She talks to her hens. Always has, since she was small. I told the gate-wardens where she lives." Her voice dropped to almost nothing. "They took her Tuesday. I keep telling myself it's for her protection."

Xanthe looked at the woman, and at the banner, and at the boy with the birdcage, and understood that she was looking at the machinery of a lie being built in real time — mortared brick by brick, neighbor upon neighbor, until the whole wall of it stood tall enough that no one could see over it anymore. The stars had not fallen because of the unfaithful. The stars had gone dark because something beneath the Spire was drinking their light, some engine of the Wardens' own making, and rather than confess it they had chosen to feed the innocent to the fire instead.

Her hand found the woven lid of the fox's basket without thinking, the way you touch a wound.

"You should move along," said a Warden sergeant pleasantly, tapping her shoulder with two fingers. "Gate queue's this way. You'll want papers."

"I have papers," Xanthe said, which was true; she had excellent forged ones, bought years ago from a forger in Saltmarsh who charged extra for calm handwriting. "Merchant. Wool and dyes."

"Wool," the sergeant said, narrowing his eyes. "There's been moon-wool moving through these parts. Contraband since the ward-fall. You'll open your packs."

Moon-wool was her trade — fleece from high-pasture sheep that grazed where starlight used to fall, wool that held light the way other cloth held dye. It had been legal once, half-legal always, and now it was contraband because everything faintly magical was contraband, because magic itself was becoming contraband, because the Wardens needed someone to blame and the blameless were easier to arrest than the guilty. She opened her pack as ordered. The sergeant rummaged through spare tunics and dried plums and a coil of good rope, missing entirely the false bottom where a dozen skeins of shimmering grey rode flat against the frame, and missing even more completely the basket whose occupant growled softly at him, low and feverish, until he assumed it was a dog and waved her through.

Inside the walls, the city had changed its skin.

Xanthe knew Thornfell City — everyone called it Spirefall now, though the signs hadn't caught up — from years of slipping through it at odd hours. It had been a loud place, a trading place, a place of fish-stalls and dyers' rows and taverns where carters argued about freight rates. Now it moved in silence and queues. Curfew bells rang at dusk and dawn both, and between them the streets ran quiet as a held breath. Squads of Wardens walked the avenues in threes, not hurrying, not speaking, their presence itself the message. Chalk marks bloomed on doorposts like a rash: a slash for searched, a circle for cleared, a black triangle for *reported*. Whole lanes wore triangles down both sides, and behind those doors, Xanthe realized with a sick lurch, families had been taken — or had fled, or had simply stopped answering, and no one on the street asked after them because asking was itself a kind of reportable offense.

And everywhere, everywhere, the posters.

*THE CAGED FIRE GOES DARK — SABOTAGE BY BEAST-SPEAKERS CONFIRMED.*

She read that one four times, standing in a lane off the Dyers' Row, her back against a wall that smelled of old urine and older stone. *Sabotage confirmed.* Someone had confirmed it. Someone had signed their name to a lie and hung it in every square. And beneath the headline, in careful print: *"The heart-flame of the last dragon has been stolen by agents of the wild faiths. Without it, the ward-lights fail and the stars cannot return. Any who harbor such agents harbor the enemies of all light."*

It was almost beautiful, in its way. Almost elegant. The truth, inverted and gilded: the flame had indeed been stolen — but not by beast-speakers. Stolen *from* the dragon, by the High Warden herself, a century of it siphoned away drop by drop while Ysolde lay chained beneath their feet, and now the theft was complete and the flame sat locked in a vault three streets from where Xanthe stood reading, and the Wardens had written the crime onto the victims so cleanly that mothers wept in the squares cursing the very people trying to save them.

Xanthe pressed her palm flat against the poster and held it there a long moment, as if she could warm the lie out of the paper.

"That won't help," said a voice near her elbow — a dyer's apprentice, maybe sixteen, red-stained fingers, watching her with wary interest. "They paste new ones up every night. My master says the ink's cheaper than bread now."

"Does your master believe it?" Xanthe asked.

The apprentice shrugged, noncommittal, and glanced over his shoulder. "He believes whatever keeps his shop unmarked. Everyone does. That's how it works, isn't it? Not that people believe. Just that they stop *not*-believing, out loud, where anyone can hear."

That was the sentence that lodged in Xanthe like a splinter and worked deeper all day. Not that they believed. Just that they stopped not-believing out loud. She had seen fear before; smuggling taught you fear the way sailing teaches salt. But this was different. This was a whole city learning to hold its tongue in unison, a thousand throats swallowing the same words at the same hour, and the silence that resulted was louder than any bell.

By evening she had found her way to the tanner's yard off Tanner's Row where Brack had told her a cousin of his kept rooms — a broad, slow-smiling woman named Odile who cured leather and asked no questions, which made her worth more than gold. Odile fed her stew and let her sleep in the drying loft among hanging hides that smelled of oak-bark and smoke, and the fox slept too, curled against Xanthe's ribs, feverish but alive, and Xanthe lay awake under the eaves listening to the curfew bell toll nine times across rooftops she could not walk until morning.

Somewhere below her, beneath a hundred feet of foundation stone and older rock than that, a dragon was dying in the dark. She could feel it now — not hear, not see, nothing so simple. Feel. A wrongness in the deep places, a pressure behind her breastbone like weather trapped underground, the sense of something vast breathing shallowly, waiting to be allowed to stop. Every beast-speaker learned early that the earth itself spoke, if slowly, if in root-time. And the earth beneath the Spire was speaking pain.

*They took my fire,* the ground murmured, in the wordless way ground speaks, more tremor than language. *They took my fire and they are calling me thief.*

She lay there and let that sink into her, and by the time sleep finally took her she had stopped thinking of the ward-fall as a disaster. Disasters happen. This was a *decision*. Somewhere above her sleeping body, in halls lit by lanterns fed on stolen fire, men and women in silver-grey had chosen this — chosen the lie, chosen the scapegoats, chosen the war that would follow — and were choosing it again every morning, freshly, deliberately, with banners and chalk and curfews and cells. One fallen ward she might have forgiven as accident. This was architecture.

* * *

Corin Ashe arrived in the city the same evening, and he arrived as a ghost.

No badge. No escort. No name spoken aloud in any gatehouse ledger. He came through the south postern dressed as a drover, mud on his boots and a drover's hat pulled low, and the young Warden at the postern looked at his papers twice and waved him through without once suspecting that the man walking past him had commanded half the southern watch until eight months ago. That was the point. Corin Ashe had spent his career teaching younger Wardens to read faces, to weigh words, to notice the man who didn't fidget quite right — and now he used every trick of that craft to become unreadable himself. He walked like a tired man with sore feet, because his feet were sore. He answered questions like a man with nothing to hide, because he had practiced having nothing to hide.

His commission sat folded against his heart: hunt the beast-speaker Xanthe Vane, wanted for the ward-fall, reward posted in every garrison from here to the coast. He had accepted it knowing exactly what it was — knowing that Voss intended him to be the blade, and that a blade that hesitates gets melted down for a quieter one. He had told himself he would control the hunt, steer it, slow it, lose her if he must. He had told himself a great many things on the ride north, and each of them had sounded reasonable at the time, the way all treason sounds reasonable one lie at a time.

Now he stood in the Warden barracks' outer corridor — he still had access; disgraced captains retain keys longer than anyone finds comfortable — and he watched the operation around him with his old officer's eye, and what he saw frightened him more than any ambush in Greytooth Pass ever had.

They weren't hunting her. Not really.

Oh, there were patrols out. There were search grids drawn on maps in the operations room, neat blue circles radiating from the Dyers' Row, lists of names, informants' reports stacked in folders thick as Bibles. But Corin had run hunts for fifteen years, and he knew the difference between a hunt and a theater, and this was theater with a body count. The searches circled the same three districts again and again, sweeping up hedge-witches and hen-talking farm girls and one unfortunate tinker who owned a myrtle wood flute that some informant swore "whistled animal-song." The reports that actually mattered — the ones describing a woman with a scarred left forearm and a fox — sat in a folder marked with a red tab that only three people were permitted to open, and those three people reported directly to the office of the High Warden.

Sarel Voss was running two hunts, Corin realized. The public one, loud and stupid and sweeping, designed to fill cells and headlines. And the private one, quiet and precise, designed to find Xanthe Vane before anyone else did — because Voss did not want the beast-speaker arrested. Arrest required charges, trials, witnesses, all the slow machinery that lets truth leak. Voss wanted her *found*, quietly, by people who answered only to her, and what happened after that would happen in a room with no windows.

Corin had grown up inside the Wardens. He had believed in them the way you believe in a house you were raised in — structurally, unquestioningly, the way you trust floorboards. He had known the Order had rotted spots; every institution does. But he had thought the rot was local. A bad commander here, a bribed quartermaster there, the usual damp getting in. What he was looking at now was not rot. It was a load-bearing wall of lies, and the whole building stood on it, and the building was the realm.

He sat that night in his rented room above a chandler's shop — he dared not sleep in barracks, where his face was still known and his disgrace still discussed — and he wrote a letter he did not send. To whom, he wasn't sure. To the Order he had been. To the captain he had failed to be. The candle burned down while he wrote, and what he wrote was this:

*I keep asking myself when it happened. When did we become this? Was it gradual, so gradual none of us noticed, the way a river cuts a canyon without once raising its voice? Or was there a day — a specific day — when the Order looked at itself and chose the lie, and I was there, and I saluted, and I said nothing?*

*I remember the oath. Protect the helpless. Speak truth even when it costs. I recited it at nineteen with my mother watching from the third row, proud as a peacock. I can still feel the weight of those words in my mouth. And now I am hunting a woman who saved my life, on behalf of a lie I can prove is a lie, and the only reason I haven't burned my commission is that a blade inside the machine can sometimes jam the gears.*

*Sometimes. Not always. Tonight I don't know which.*

He sealed it unsent and put it in his kit, next to the whetstone, and slept badly, dreaming of harp strings cut.

* * *

Their roads converged on the second morning, though neither knew it yet.

For Xanthe, the day began with the fox. The fever had broken in the night — she felt it in the animal's purring, uneven warmth, the way its breathing eased from ragged to rhythmic — and by midmorning the creature was well enough to be cross about the basket, scratching at the wicker and demanding, in its clipped and gravelly speech, to know why it was being imprisoned in a box when clearly the world outside contained mice.

"The world outside contains Wardens," Xanthe told it, feeding it scraps of Odile's smoked fish through the lattice. "You nearly died. You'll stay in the basket until I say otherwise."

"You said that yesterday," the fox observed. "And the day before. Foxes are not furniture."

"No," Xanthe agreed, "but foxes are evidence. If anyone catches you talking, they catch me talking, and then we both hang. So: basket."

The fox submitted to this logic with bad grace, and Xanthe carried it with her anyway — she always carried it, basket and all, because leaving it anywhere felt like abandoning a witness, and the fox was the only other soul in the world who had heard the caged fire going dark and lived to describe it. Its account had grown more detailed as it healed, more precise, the way wounded memories do. It had been near the mountain wards when they fell. It had heard the harp-string snap of the ward-lines failing. And it had heard, underneath that, something the posters never mentioned and the criers never cried: a sound from below, from beneath, from *down*, a vast slow groaning like a mountain exhaling its last breath.

"It wasn't sabotage," the fox had told her, curled against her chest in the waystation, days ago now. "Sabotage is quick. This was slow. This was a century of slow, ending all at once. The fire under the Spire went thin. Thin as winter honey. And when it went thin, the lines went slack, and when the lines went slack, the dark came through, and the dark ate the stars, and everyone above ground blamed whoever was nearest and smallest."

Nearest and smallest. That was the phrase Xanthe carried through the city that second day like a stone in her pocket, rubbing smooth with handling. Nearest and smallest. The refugees at the gates. The hen-talking nieces. The flute-playing tinkers. The whole apparatus of the cover-up was a machine for finding the nearest and smallest and grinding them fine, because the truly guilty were neither near nor small — they were at the top, in the bright rooms, wearing silver.

That afternoon, following a tip Odile had passed along through the tanners' whisper-network (tanners hear everything; blood and hides pass through many hands), Xanthe went to look at the vault.

Not to enter it. To stand near it. There was a difference, and the difference was survival.

The Warden vaults occupied the ground floor of the Assize Hall, a squat granite block in the administrative quarter, guarded more heavily than the granaries, more heavily than the armory, more heavily — she noted with a bitter twist — than the refugee gates had been. Four sentries at the door, rotating hourly. Iron shutters. A ward-line of its own humming faintly in the lintel stone, tuned, she could feel through her gift, to repel exactly the kind of creature she was. Standing across the street, pretending to study a farrier's signboard, Xanthe let her senses reach toward that hum the way she'd once reached toward birdsong, gently, listening without touching.

And the vault answered.

Not in words. In heat. A single thread of warmth threading out through the stone toward her, faint as breath on a winter window, and threaded through the warmth a voice — not the fox's gravel, not the ground's slow murmur, but something older, vaster, compressed almost past recognition, like a shout heard through a mountain's worth of stone. Like a heart beating in a locked room.

*Here,* it said. *I am here. They keep me here, in the dark, in the cold, and they call it keeping safe.*

Xanthe's eyes stung. She blinked and looked hard at the farrier's signboard, at the painted horse, at nothing.

The heart-flame was in there. Three streets from Odile's tannery, behind iron and humming stone and four men with halberds, the stolen heart of the last dragon waited in its vial, dimming. Dimming — that was the thing she couldn't shake, the detail that turned outrage into urgency. The fox had said the fire went thin. The vault itself had whispered it: *cold, they keep me cold, they say it keeps me safe.* Cold was killing it. Whatever Voss intended for the flame — weapon, relic, engine of holy war — she was storing it the way misers store gold, in the dark, locked away, and fire does not consent to be stored. Fire spends. Fire gutters. Every day the flame sat in that vault it burned a little lower, and when it guttered out entirely, it would take the last of Ysolde's life with it, and the age of magic would end not with a war but with a whimper in a strongbox.

Unless someone stole it first.

Xanthe stood at the farrier's corner until the shadows lengthened, mapping without paper: the sentries' rotation, the delivery entrance on the north side where supply carts came at dawn, the drainage grate in the alley behind, the window of the records office above where a clerk worked late with a lamp. Smuggling was mostly this — standing still, watching, letting a building tell you its habits the way a fox tells you its boundaries. By the time the lamplighter came down the street with his long pole, she had the vault's daily rhythm in her head as surely as a tune.

She also had company she didn't notice.

* * *

Corin noticed her.

He had been working the Dyers' Row district since dawn, walking it in his drover's disguise, showing a worn sketch of a woman's face to shopkeepers and receiving the shrugs that shopkeepers give. The sketch was poor — Voss's artists worked from informants' descriptions, and informants described fear more accurately than faces — but Corin knew the real face. He had seen it across a burning riverbank four years ago, when a beast-speaker smuggler had dragged a drowning Warden captain out of the Greywater and vanished before he'd finished coughing. He had never told anyone. Some debts you keep private, because the moment you speak them they become currency, and he refused to spend her.

So he hunted her with his eyes instead of the sketch, and on the second afternoon his eyes found what they were looking for.

A woman at the farrier's corner, studying a signboard too intently, too long. A woman whose weight shifted from foot to foot the way watchers shift, whose gaze flicked — sentries, rotation, alley, grate, window — in the pattern Corin himself had taught a hundred recruits to recognize. *Target assessment*, the training manuals called it. He had written part of that manual. He was watching a woman case a Warden vault using techniques he had personally codified, and the realization landed in his chest like a dropped anvil: somewhere in the Order's own doctrine, in his own doctrine, was the exact shape of the thing now walking toward the heart-flame.

He did not approach. Approaching was amateur work. He marked her, memorized her coat, her stride, the basket on her shoulder with its suspicious growl, and drifted after her at the distance the manuals prescribed — close enough to keep, far enough to seem chance. For six streets he trailed her through the evening crowds, and for six streets he fought the strangest battle of his career, which was the battle between what he knew he should do and what he knew he would do.

Should: signal the inner circle. Three names, three dead-drop locations, one encoded phrase. Within the hour, Voss's private hunters would converge, and Xanthe Vane would vanish into a room with no windows, and the hunt would be over, and his duty would be discharged, and the Order would call him restored.

Would: he already knew. He had known since the waystation. He would follow her. He would watch. He would wait. Because everything he had seen in two days inside the machine — the theater-hunts, the red-tabbed folder, the banners blaming the nearest and smallest — had convinced him of one thing beyond argument: whatever happened to Xanthe Vane inside a Warden interrogation room, it would not be justice, and he could not be the instrument of it. Not for the woman who pulled him from the river. Not for anyone. Not anymore.

But he could not warn her either. Not yet. Warning her meant revealing himself, and revealing himself meant losing the only position from which he might still protect her — the hidden one, the inside one, the man walking three steps behind who knows the hunt better than the hunters. So he did the hardest thing in surveillance, harder than any arrest: he stayed silent, and followed, and hated himself with a thoroughness that surprised him by how familiar it felt.

At the tanner's yard he lost her — deliberately let her go, marking the door, marking the street, marking the smell of oak-bark and smoke that clung to the place. Then he walked the long way back to his rented room, and climbed the stairs, and sat on the bed with his boots still on, in the dark, doing arithmetic he despised.

Every hour he delayed reporting, Voss's private hunters ranged wider. Every hour he delayed, the chance grew that one of them would find her first — some younger officer, ambitious, unburdened by rivers or gratitude, who would not hesitate at the door of a tanner's loft. His silence was not neutral. His silence was a wager, and the stake was her life, and he was placing it on his own ability to keep winning a race he was also running.

And if he warned her — if he broke cover, knocked on Odile's door, told her *they're coming, run* — then he became what the Order already said he was. A traitor. Formally, irreversibly, with witnesses. And a traitor loses access, and access was the only weapon he had left.

Jamming the gears from inside, or pulling the alarm from outside. He could not do both. He had to choose, and the choosing felt less like strategy than like standing at a fork in a burning road, knowing both paths led through fire, and picking one not because it was safe but because of who he would refuse to become on either.

He did not choose that night. He lay awake until the curfew bell, listening to the city hold its breath, and somewhere in the small hours he made himself a promise that felt smaller than a decision and heavier than one: *not yet*. Not tonight. Tomorrow he would walk the Dyers' Row again, and watch the tanner's door, and wait one more day for clarity that might never come — and if it didn't come, he would act anyway, because a man who waits forever for clarity dies waiting, and a woman in a drying loft deserved better than his endless deliberation.

* * *

Xanthe dreamed of the dragon that night.

It was not a dream she chose. Beast-speakers learn that some dreams arrive addressed to them, delivered by routes no courier admits to, and this one bore all the hallmarks: too vivid, too structured, too *hers*. She stood in a cavern of black stone veined with dull ember-light, and before her, coiled in chains that were older than the Spire above, lay a dragon the color of cooled ash — vast beyond reason, wings folded like grave-clothes, one eye half-open and the color of tarnished copper.

Ysolde. Though in the dream she did not introduce herself. She simply looked at Xanthe, and the look went through her like a spear of warm rain.

"You came," the dragon said, and her voice was the sound a mountain makes settling, deep and slow and grieving. "After a hundred years. After a hundred years of them telling the world I was a story. You came."

"I came," Xanthe said, in the dream, in the voice dreams lend you, which is braver than your waking voice. "I found the vault. I know where they keep it. Your fire — it's three streets from where I sleep, in a bottle, going out."

"Going out," Ysolde agreed, and something in her vast frame shuddered, a landslide in miniature. "Cold keeps nothing. Cold only slows the spending. They think they are saving me for later. Later is a country where fire does not live." Her eye brightened, dimmed, brightened. "Little speaker. Listen to me. I have been dying for a hundred years, politely, in the dark, so that their lamps would burn and their order would stand. I have earned the right to ask you one question, and I will ask it only once."

Xanthe leaned closer. The chains clinked, soft as wind chimes in a tomb.

"When you take my fire back — and you will, little one, I see it in you, you have the look of the ones who cannot leave a locked thing locked — when you take it back, what will you do with it? Will you give it to me? All of it? And if you give it to me, all of it, what do you think I will do?"

The question hung in the cavern air, and Xanthe felt the true weight of it settle onto her shoulders for the first time. Not *can* you free her. Not *dare* you steal the flame. But: a dragon who has been caged for a century, betrayed, drained, blamed, forgotten — given back her whole heart, her whole rage, her whole ancient strength — what will she do with it? What would anyone do?

"I don't know," Xanthe said honestly, and the honesty seemed to please the dragon more than any brave answer would have.

"Good," Ysolde rumbled. "Distrust yourself a little. It will keep you careful. Now wake, little speaker. Wake, and count your friends, because you will need more than courage where you are going, and courage runs out faster than love."

She woke in the drying loft with the fox's tail across her cheek and the curfew bell fading, and lay still a long time, staring at the rafters, doing exactly what the dragon told her. Counting friends. Brack, Pip, Marisol — a stonemason, a pickpocket, a hedge-witch, met under fire at Greytooth Pass and trusted since out of necessity sharpened into choice. Odile below, unknowing or half-knowing, feeding her stew. A fox in a basket. And somewhere out in the dark city, a disgraced Warden captain walking three steps behind her shadow, and she did not know it, and he did not know that she did not know it, and the whole delicate arrangement balanced on the edge of a knife that neither of them could see.

The count came to seven, if she counted generously. Seven souls against an empire's worth of silver-grey. It should have felt hopeless. Instead, lying there in the oak-bark dark, Xanthe felt something she had not felt since the stars went out: a fierce, stubborn, unreasonable hope, the kind that doesn't argue with the odds because it isn't listening to them.

Tomorrow she would go back to the vault. Tomorrow she would watch the dawn delivery carts, and count the seconds of the sentry change, and begin — quietly, carefully, in the smuggler's way, one measured piece at a time — to plan the impossible thing.

She did not know that across the city, in a room above a chandler's shop, Corin Ashe had reached the end of his own deliberations. She did not know that he had decided. She did not know that the decision he had reached was to find her first — not to arrest her, not to warn her, not yet either of those, but to *speak* to her, man to woman, river to riverbank, debtor to creditor, before Voss's private hunters closed the distance he had spent two days refusing to close.

Two people, three streets apart, each planning to approach the other first. Each certain the other was the danger. Each carrying, unbeknownst, the key to the other's survival.

The dawn carts would roll at first light. The sentries would change at the seventh bell. And somewhere between the seventh bell and the striking of the dawn delivery, in a tanner's yard that smelled of oak-bark and smoke, two roads that had been converging for four years — since a riverbank, since a drowning, since a debt kept private — were about to meet at last.

Whether they met as hunter and prey, or as something else entirely, depended entirely on who knocked first.
