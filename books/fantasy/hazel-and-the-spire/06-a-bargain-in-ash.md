## 6. A Bargain in Ash

The granary had burned three nights before Corin ever set foot in it, and the city had already begun to forget.

That was the thing about Ashfall Quarter — the Spire's oldest district, its poorest, the place where the realm's failures were swept when no one wanted to look at them anymore. A granary feeding half the quarter's children went up in flames, and by the second morning the Wardens' criers were calling it an accident, a lamp left burning, a tragedy of carelessness. By the third morning, the ash was being shoveled into carts and the story was being shoveled in after it.

Corin Ashe did not believe in careless lamps. He had spent eleven years learning what fire looked like when it was set on purpose, and this one had started from the inside, low, in a room where no lamp hung. He also did not believe in coincidence — not in a week when the stars had gone out, the border wards had fallen like cut harp strings, and every Warden in Thornfell had been handed a name to hunt.

*Beast-speaker. Female. Travels alone. Considered dangerous.*

He turned the notice over in his mind for the hundredth time as he moved through the granary's gutted shell, boots crunching on char that still held the ghost of heat. The description fit a woman he had known four years ago on a riverbank in the rain. The description would have fit half the smugglers in the passes. But the timing of her appearance in the Spire city — two days after the ward-fall, slipping through gates that should have been sealed — that fit only one person he had ever met.

And she was here. He could feel it the way you feel weather coming: a pressure behind the eyes, a wrongness in the air. The rats had gone quiet in the walls. Even the pigeons roosting in the scorched rafters sat unnaturally still, heads cocked toward the far end of the granary as if listening to something no human ear could catch.

Beast-speaker.

*Considered dangerous.*

He thought about the riverbank. He thought about hands hauling him out of black water, about a voice — rough with cold, furious with him for being so careless — saying *don't you dare die on me, I didn't wade in here for a corpse.* He had never learned her name until the notice went up. *Xanthe Vane.* Four years of carrying a debt he couldn't repay and couldn't forget, and now the debt had a name and a price on it, and the price was his to collect.

Captain Sarel Voss had given him the commission personally. That should have been his first warning. Captains did not hand hunting commissions to disgraced officers; they handed them to men they wanted tested, or men they wanted ruined, or men whose ruin they wanted witnessed. Corin had been all three since Greywater Bridge, since the order he'd refused, since the word *insubordinate* had been nailed to his name like a sign on a condemned house.

"Find her," Voss had said, smiling that small, patient smile of hers, "and bring me her head's worth of answers. You have a talent for finding things, Captain Ashe. It would be a shame to waste it."

So here he was, finding things. Finding them in a burned granary at the hour before dawn, because a tanner's boy had sworn he'd seen a woman slip in through the loading door at moonset, hooded, moving like someone who knew how not to be seen. And because the rats had led him here — which was absurd, which was superstition, except that the rats had been leading him all night, scurrying ahead along the walls whenever he paused, always in the same direction, always toward the granary, as if something in the dark had decided to deliver him.

The loading door stood open at the far end, a rectangle of grey pre-dawn light. And against that light, framed in it, stood a woman with a bundle pressed to her chest.

She had heard him. Of course she had — she'd probably heard him three streets back. She didn't run. She stood very still, and even across forty feet of ash and fallen beams, Corin could see her weighing him, the way you weigh a wolf at the treeline: *can I outrun it, can I outfight it, or do we come to terms.*

"Warden," she said. Her voice was lower than he remembered, roughened by roads. "You're a long way from your barracks."

"You know why I'm here."

"I know what the notices say." She shifted the bundle in her arms — carefully, the way you shift something fragile, something alive. "I'm surprised they sent their worst soldier to catch me."

Their eyes met across the ruin. Four years fell away between them like a dropped cloak. He saw her recognize him — truly recognize him, past the badge and the duty and the years — and he saw the exact moment she understood who he was: the man from the water. Her chin lifted a fraction. Defiance, or maybe just bracing.

"Corin Ashe," she said. "You got out of the river after all."

"You pulled me out of it."

"Then we're even for the rescue, and I can stop owing you." A ghost of the old sharp humor. "Though I notice you're the one holding the sword."

He hadn't drawn it. He looked down and realized his hand was already on the hilt, thumb breaking the guard loose, his body doing the work his conscience hadn't authorized. He made himself let go. Made his voice level.

"The fox," he said. "In the bundle. That's why the animals have been acting strange all night. That's why they led me here."

Something flickered across her face — surprise, quickly mastered. "You noticed the rats."

"I noticed everything in this city has been holding its breath since the stars went out." He took one step forward, gravel grinding under his boot. "Xanthe Vane. The border wards fell the same night you crossed into Thornfell territory. Every Warden from here to the coast is looking for you. The High Warden herself signed your warrant." Another step. "Give me one reason — one — to believe the wards weren't yours."

"They weren't mine." No flinch, no darting eyes. She said it plainly, the way people say true things. "But I know why they fell. And I know why the granaries are burning, and why the refugees are being blamed, and why the criers are lying." She lifted the bundle slightly. "You want your reason? Come and look. Then decide whether you're taking me in."

"It's a trick."

"Probably," she agreed. "Everything's a trick until you look at it. That's what the Wardens taught me, watching them burn my brother's ferry license over a bribe he wouldn't pay." She knelt, slow and deliberate, and set the bundle down on a beam that had escaped the worst of the fire. "I'm kneeling, Warden. My hands are empty. Whatever you think I am, I'm not fast enough to beat a drawn blade from my knees. So either I'm stupid, or I'm telling the truth, and you get to choose which."

Every instinct he had trained into himself over eleven years said *arrest her now*. Kneeling suspects were the easy ones. Kneeling suspects with warrants signed by the High Warden were career-making ones. One binding, one escort, one report, and Corin Ashe walked back into the Wardens' good graces with the name of the traitor who felled the border wards. Everything he had lost came home. Everything he had been stripped of — the rank, the oath, the right to be called anything but *the captain without a badge* — restored in a single morning's work.

He crossed the last ten feet and knelt across from her.

Later, he would try to explain to himself why. He would tell himself it was tactics — control the scene, secure the evidence, keep her where he could see her. He would tell himself it was caution. What it actually was, was the memory of rain and black water and a stranger's hands, and the simple animal fact that when she said *look*, some part of him older than his oaths still obeyed her.

She unwrapped the bundle.

It was a fox. Red coat gone dull and patchy, ribs standing out like barrel staves, one foreleg wrapped in stained linen. It lay in the crook of her arm with the terrible limpness of an animal that had stopped fighting, and its eyes — amber, fever-bright — found Corin's face and *held* it.

"This is Rook," Xanthe said quietly. "He was hurt the night the wards fell. Caught in a snare on the pass road, near the first ward-stone. He's been dying slowly ever since, and I've been keeping him alive with willow-bark and stubbornness." Her fingers moved through the fur behind the ears, gentle. "Ask him what happened."

"He's a fox."

"He's a fox who talks. That's rather the point of me." She met his eyes. "You've heard the stories. Beast-speakers. Half of them are lies and the other half are exaggerations, but the gift is real, and it runs in families, and it chose me the way weather chooses a valley. I don't summon beasts. I don't command them. I *hear* them. And for the last six days, everything with ears has been screaming."

The fox made a sound. Not a bark, not a whine — something between, a cracked, effortful noise, and though Corin knew — *knew* — that foxes did not speak, the sound resolved in his mind into words the way notes resolve into a tune:

*"The caged fire is going dark. The caged fire is going dark. She promised. She promised."*

He recoiled. His shoulder hit a fallen rafter and ash puffed up around him, and he was on his feet with his heart slamming before he remembered he was supposed to be the one in control here.

"That's not—" He stopped. Started again. "Animals don't talk."

"No," Xanthe said softly. "They don't. Which means whatever is happening beneath the Spire is big enough that even a dying fox can shout it across the gap between us." She rose too, unhurried, cradling the fox against her chest again. "You want to know why the wards fell? I'll tell you, and then you can arrest me with a clear conscience, or you can help me, or you can walk away and pretend you never knelt in this ash. Those are your choices, Warden. There isn't a fourth one, no matter how much you wish there were."

"Talk," he said, because his throat was too tight for anything longer.

"The border wards aren't stone," she said. "Or they're not only stone. Ask yourself what fed them. Ask yourself why the old ward-stones are warm to the touch, why they hum in summer, why the animals avoid them. The Wardens' own archives call it *the Vigil Flame* — some holy mystery they dress up in ceremony once a year. But there's nothing holy about it. It's dragon-fire. Real fire, from a real dragon, and it's been bleeding into those wards for a hundred years." Her voice dropped. "There's a dragon under the Spire, Corin. The last one. Chained in the undercrofts since before either of us was born, and the Wardens have been drinking her life through a straw to keep their borders pretty. And now someone has stolen half her heart-flame — siphoned it off into a vial, into a vault — and she's dying, and when she dies, every scrap of magic in this realm dies with her. The wards. The hedge-craft. The speaking-gift. All of it, guttering out like a lamp in wind."

The silence afterward was enormous. Somewhere outside, the seventh bell began to ring, and the sound seemed to come from very far away, from another country, from a life Corin used to have.

"That's—" He laughed, and hated the sound of it. "That's madness. That's a smuggler's tale. The Vigil Flame is—"

"Is what? Do you know? Have you ever seen it?" She stepped closer, and the fox's fever-eyes watched him over her arm. "You're a good soldier, Corin Ashe. I could see that on the riverbank, before I knew your name. Good soldiers trust the chain of command because the alternative is unbearable. But you refused an order once. At Greywater Bridge. They broke you for it." She let that sit between them. "So you already know the chain can be wrong. You've *felt* it be wrong. The only question left is how wrong, and I'm telling you: all the way down. To the roots. To the vaults. To the High Warden's own signature on the theft."

"You can't know that."

"I know the flame was taken. Rook knows it — the whole mountain knows it, the grief of it has been rolling downhill like a rockslide for six days. And I know who benefits." Her eyes were steady and dark and terribly certain. "Wards fall. Stars vanish. Refugees blamed. Curfews. Burned granaries in the poor quarters, called accidents. A beast-speaker hunted across three provinces — *me*, the perfect monster, the reason for every new lock and every new law." She shook her head slowly. "Someone is building a fire, Corin. And they mean to feed it with fear."

He should arrest her. The words arranged themselves obediently in his mind — *you are under warrant, you will come with me* — the way drill-ground words always arranged themselves, clean and hollow. Eleven years of service stood behind them. The oath stood behind them. His father's grave stood behind them, and his father had been a Warden before him, and his grandmother before that, a whole line of Ashe shoulders squared under the same grey cloak.

But his father had also told him, once, sitting on a fence rail above the summer fields: *"The uniform doesn't make you right, boy. It just makes you responsible for finding out if you are."*

The fox made its cracked sound again. *"She promised. She promised."*

"What did you promise?" Corin asked, and heard his own voice come out strange.

Xanthe looked down at the fox. When she answered, all the iron had gone out of her voice, and what remained was raw.

"That I'd take the flame back to her. The stolen half. That I'd free her or die trying." She swallowed. "She's been down there a hundred years, Corin. Alone. In the dark. Used. And everyone who ever knew she existed is either dead or lying about it. If I don't go back for her, no one ever will."

"And if you free her? Dragons burned the Dragonfast crater into the map, Vane. The old war—"

"—is exactly what someone wants," she cut in, fierce now. "Don't you see? Free her angry, and she burns cities, and the realm unites against her, and whoever holds the stolen flame holds the only weapon that can kill her. Let her die, and magic dies, and the same someone holds the only fire left in the world. Either way, they win. Either way, there's a war." Her jaw tightened. "Unless somebody steals the match first."

Outside, the seventh bell finished ringing. Dawn was coming up grey over the broken roof, and Corin became aware, distantly, that his patrol would be waiting at the Tanner's Row checkpoint in less than an hour, and that his absence would be noted, and that every minute he stood in this ash was another degree of treason.

He thought about Voss's smile. Small, patient, certain. *Bring me her head's worth of answers.*

He thought about the burned granary, started from the inside, low, in a room where no lamp hung. About the criers calling it an accident. About whose grain had burned, and whose had not.

He thought about black water, and hands, and *don't you dare die on me.*

"If I let you walk out of here," he said slowly, "and you're wrong — if any part of this is a lie —"

"Then you'll hunt me again," Xanthe said. "And next time I won't kneel."

A long moment. The fox breathed its thin, rattling breath. Dust drifted down through the grey light like snow that had forgotten how to be cold.

Corin Ashe reached to his belt, and Xanthe tensed — he saw her weight shift, saw her calculate the distance to the loading door — and then his hand kept moving, past the sword, to the Warden whistle hanging beside it. The long whistle, the one that carried three miles and summoned every patrol in the district. He unhooked it. He weighed it in his palm for one breath, two.

Then he hurled it deep into the collapsed stairwell at the back of the granary, where it struck stone with a bright, final clatter and rolled away into the dark.

"Treason," he said. His voice didn't shake, which surprised him. "That's what that was. In case you were wondering."

Xanthe stared at him. "Why?"

Because the chain can be wrong. Because I've felt it. Because you pulled me out of a river and I've been drowning ever since in slower water. Because a dying fox just shamed me more thoroughly than any court-martial ever managed.

"Because I need to know if it's true," is what he said. "All of it. The dragon. The vault. The High Warden. If I'm going to burn my life down, Vane, I'm going to do it in daylight, over something real." He straightened, and felt — strangely, terrifyingly — lighter than he had in four years. "So you're going to show me. Beneath the Spire. Tonight. And if there's no dragon down there, I drag you to Voss myself and testify against you with a clear conscience."

"And if there is?"

He looked at the fox, dying in her arms, six days of grief rolling down off the mountain like a rockslide. He looked at the ash of a burned granary and thought about fires that start from the inside.

"Then God help us both," Corin Ashe said, "because I'm going to help you steal fire from the most powerful woman in the realm."

Xanthe smiled — the first real one, sharp and bright as a drawn blade. "Welcome to the smuggling business, Captain."

Far below the granary, down through the roots of the city, something ancient stirred in its chains and felt, for the first time in a century, the faint warmth of hope — and the rats, all across Ashfall Quarter, began to sing.
