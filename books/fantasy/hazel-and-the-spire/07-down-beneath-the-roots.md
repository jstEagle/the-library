## 7. Down Beneath the Roots

The rats came at moonset.

Xanthe felt them before she saw them — a tide of small warm minds pressing against the edges of her gift like fingers against a drumskin. She sat up in the hayloft of the tannery where the family had hidden for two days, and there they were: dozens of brown bodies flowing over the rafters, silent as spilled ink, and every one of them looking at *her*.

"They want you to follow," she said softly.

Pip's head popped up from the straw beside her, hair full of chaff. "Rats don't want things. Rats want cheese."

"These want me to come with them. Down." Xanthe's mouth had gone dry. "Under the Spire."

Below them, through the gaps in the floorboards, Marisol was grinding something bitter-smelling in a mortar, and Brack's low voice rumbled through a conversation with Corin that neither of them seemed to be enjoying. The words drifted up in fragments — *vaults*, *ward-signatures*, *impossible*, *suicide*, said with feeling by both men in turn.

Xanthe swung down the ladder and landed in the tallow-light of the workroom. Four faces turned to her. She held up one hand, palm out, the way you'd calm a spooked horse, though what she wanted to calm was herself.

"The rats know a way in," she said. "Under the roots. Under everything. They've been living down there since before the Spire had a name, and they say—" she paused, because saying it aloud would make it real, "—they say the fire is still burning. Barely. A candle in a hurricane. And it's calling for help."

Corin set down the map he'd been scowling at. In the two days since the granary — since he'd stood in the smoke with his sword half-drawn and let her walk away — something in him had changed posture entirely, the way a tree changes after lightning. He looked less like a Warden now and more like a man deciding, moment by moment, who he was going to be.

"Rats," he said. "You're proposing we trust our lives to rats."

"I'm proposing we trust my gift," Xanthe said. "The rats are just the ones who know the road."

"And what does the road cost?"

She thought of the fox, its ribs like a broken harp, its mind flickering with images of a vast dark warmth going cold beneath tons of stone. *The caged fire going dark,* it had told her. *And when it goes, all the small voices go with it.*

"Everything, probably," she said. "But we're already spending it. We just haven't picked what to buy."

Brack laughed — a sound like a quarry settling. He filled most of the doorway even sitting down, his grey-green hands resting on knees the size of millstones. "I like her," he told Corin. "You should keep her."

"That's the general plan," Corin muttered, and then went red as if the words had escaped without parole.

Marisol smiled into her mortar and said nothing, which from Marisol was a whole speech.

* * *

They went down at the dead hour, when even the curfew patrols had given up on the night and gone to their bunks.

The entrance the rats chose was not an entrance at all. It was a sinkhole behind the tanners' row, half-swallowed by blackberry bramble, breathing cold air that smelled of wet stone and old iron. Brack went first, lowering himself into the dark with a rope around his waist and a lantern hooked to his belt, and his voice floated up sounding very far away: "Steps. Old ones. Older than old."

One by one they descended — Pip wriggling down like an eel, Marisol with her satchel of charms clinking, Corin last, pausing at the lip to look back at the sky. The stars were still gone. Nine nights now. The heavens above Thornfell were a smooth black lid, and everyone in the city pretended not to notice, the way people pretend not to notice a wound in a friend's arm.

The stairs spiraled down farther than any cellar had a right to go. The walls changed as they descended — dressed granite, then rough-hewn blocks, then stone so ancient it had rounded like river rock, carved here and there with shapes Xanthe didn't recognize until she did: wings. Coiled tails. Long necks arched against some forgotten ceiling. Dragons, but dragons rendered lovingly, the way you carve what you love.

"Nobody built this to keep a dragon in," she whispered, trailing her fingers over a wing-tip worn smooth by centuries. "This was built *by* them. Or for them. Before it was a prison."

"Everything's a prison if you change the locks," Marisol said quietly.

The rats met them at the bottom.

They poured out of cracks in the wall in their dozens and then their hundreds, and Xanthe knelt and opened her gift wide and let them pour into it too. It was like plunging her hands into a river of small desperate hearts. Hunger, mostly — hunger and cunning and a fierce territorial love for these deep places — but underneath all of it, threaded through the colony like a vein of ore, was *reverence*. The rats of the undercrofts had been tending something for a hundred years. Bringing it scraps of warmth. Sleeping near it in winter.

*Down,* they told her, in the wordless way of beasts. *Down, down, past the standing water, past the iron doors, past the sleeping two-legs. But gently, gently, walker-of-voices. She sleeps thin now. She startles.*

"She says to be gentle," Xanthe translated. Her throat was tight. "There's a creature down there the whole colony has been... keeping company with."

Corin's hand had found his sword hilt without him seeming to notice. "Wardens patrol the undercrofts. I know the rotation — I helped write it, three years ago." His jaw worked. "There's a garrison post at the third landing. Twelve men. We can't fight twelve."

"We won't," Xanthe said. "The rats will show us the way around. There's always a way around, if you're small enough or patient enough." She glanced at Pip. "Or both."

Pip grinned, all teeth, and produced from somewhere inside their coat a greasepot and a coil of rag. "Been waiting all week for someone to say that."

* * *

The way around was a chimney of stone barely wider than Brack's shoulders, and Brack only made it because stone, given time and kindness, will sometimes consent to move. Xanthe watched him press his palms flat against the walls and murmur to them in his own tongue, low and courteous, the way you'd ask an elder to shift in a crowded room — and the walls *did* shift, groaning apart by inches, enough for the big stonemason to wedge through while the rest of them flattened themselves against the opposite wall and tried not to breathe on the garrison post's back door.

Twelve Wardens slept beyond it. Xanthe could feel them — not their minds, she couldn't read humans, but their presence, the dull animal hum of sleeping men. One of them snored. Somewhere a kettle ticked as it cooled.

Corin stared at the door for a long moment. "I trained four of those men," he said. "Hale. Ostrick. The twins. Good soldiers. They think they're guarding the realm." He turned away first. "Come on."

Past the garrison, the character of the deeps changed. The rat-road ran along a channel of standing water, black and mirror-still, and above it the ceilings rose into vaulted darks where their lantern light couldn't find the top. Roots had broken through somewhere far above — great pale roots hanging down like the ribs of a buried god, dripping condensation, and the rats ran along them fearless as tightrope walkers.

And it got *warm*.

Not the stale warmth of pipes or furnaces. This was a living warmth, pulsing faintly, rhythmic, and Xanthe stopped walking before her mind caught up with why she'd stopped, because her body knew before she did. It was a heartbeat. Faint as a memory of a memory, slow as a season turning, but vast — the largest heartbeat she had ever felt, so large she wasn't hearing it so much as standing inside it.

"Oh," Marisol breathed. Her charm-bag had begun to glow, every charm at once, a soft gold shimmer through the canvas. "Oh, mercy. Do you feel—"

"Yes," Xanthe said.

Pip, for once, said nothing at all. The child had gone very still, eyes huge, one hand pressed flat against their own chest as if checking that their small heart hadn't been shamed into silence.

The rats led them up a final slope of rubble to a wall — no, not a wall. A door. A hundred feet of it at least, iron-banded oak gone black with age, and across it someone had laid wards in silver and salt, layer upon layer upon layer, decades of them, like scar tissue over a wound. Some had been renewed recently. Some were older than Corin's grandfather. All of them were fraying, because everything fed on dragon-fire in this country, and the fire was dying.

A single keyhole sat at the center, big enough for a fist. Beside it, bolted to the door, a brass plaque, green with age:

*YSOLDE THE DAWNBRINGER. BY DECREE OF THE FIRST COUNCIL, HELD IN PERPETUITY.*

"In perpetuity," Corin read aloud, and his voice cracked in the middle of the second word.

Xanthe put her hand on the door.

The wards flared — weakly, a sickly silver shimmer — and she felt them taste her, searching for authorization, for rank, for the cold clean signature of Warden blood. Finding none, they began, sluggishly, to sting.

"Let me," Marisol said, and knelt, and began to unpick them the way she'd once shown Xanthe one mends a net: not fighting the weave, but finding the knots that wanted, secretly, to be free. "These aren't hatred-wards," she murmured, working. "Whoever laid the first ones loved her. You can tell. They're built to *hold*, not to hurt. Someone just kept re-laying them for a hundred years without ever asking why."

The last knot slipped. The great door sighed inward, and warmth rolled out over them like a tide.

* * *

The cavern beyond was large enough to hold a cathedral, a hill, a piece of the sky that had fallen and never been picked up. Their lanterns showed them walls furred with moss that glowed faintly green, fed by channels of water that trickled down from roots far overhead. And filling the cavern the way a mountain fills a valley—

The dragon.

Xanthe had imagined it a hundred times since the fox spoke: the caged fire, the vast dark warmth. Nothing had prepared her. Ysolde lay coiled in the center of the cavern like a range of hills that had decided to sleep, and she was *ruined*. Her scales, which must once have burned copper and gold — Dawnbringer, the plaque had said, dawn-colored — were grey as ash, dulled, cracked along the flank where the ribs showed through. Her wings lay folded tight against her body, and the membranes were translucent, starved, veined like autumn leaves. Her head rested on her foreclaws, and her eyes were closed, and with each of those enormous, glacial breaths the whole cavern warmed and cooled, warmed and cooled, the tide Xanthe had felt from a mile of stone away.

Around the dragon's chest, where the heart would be, someone had bolted a harness of iron and glass. From it ran a pipe — a pipe as thick as a man's torso — up into the rock, toward the Spire above. And through the glass of the harness's central chamber Xanthe could see it: a knot of light, golden-white, beating in time with nothing, flickering like a lamp about to fail.

Half of it was missing. The chamber was built to hold a sun and held instead a dying coal, and the empty space around it was the loneliest thing Xanthe had ever seen.

Beside her, Corin made a sound she had never heard from him — small, wounded, involuntary. "That's what they're feeding the wards with," he whispered. "That's what I swore my life to. A tap into a chained heart."

The dragon's eye opened.

It was the size of a cartwheel and the color of deep water, and it moved with a slowness that was not laziness but economy — a being conserving what little remained. The great pupil contracted, found the lanterns, found the small soft creatures standing at the edge of her dark, and Xanthe felt the attention of it settle on her like snowfall.

Then, into the silence of all their minds at once — not words, not quite, but meaning arriving whole, the way weather arrives —

***Little voice.***

Xanthe's knees nearly went. She gripped Brack's arm; the stonemason was trembling too, this mountain of a man, tears running freely down into his beard and apparently unconcerned about them. Pip had backed against Marisol and Marisol had her arms around the child and her lips moving in some hedge-charm that was probably just a prayer wearing work clothes.

"You speak," Xanthe managed. "You — I thought—"

***I speak to stone and root and rat. To anything that listens slowly.*** The eye blinked, an age passing between lid and lash. ***You listen slowly, little voice. The rats have spoken well of you for nine nights. They do not speak well of anyone.***

"They told me you were dying."

A silence. In it, Xanthe felt the truth of it roll off the dragon like heat off a forge — grief, exhaustion, and beneath them a stubborn banked ember of *dignity* that refused, absolutely refused, to become self-pity.

***Dying is a strong word. I prefer *dimming*. Though I grant the distinction matters less each year.*** The great head shifted; scales ground against stone like glaciers calving. ***A century ago they came with chains and scripture and told me it was for the good of the realm. That my fire, spread thin through their wards, would protect ten thousand hearths. And I — fool, old fool — I believed that protection could be given rather than taken. So I opened my own heart to the tap. Half my flame runs in your border-wards tonight, child. Or it did, until your stars went out.***

"The High Warden," Corin said hoarsely. "Sarel Voss. She has the other half."

Something moved through the cavern then — not wind, not sound, but pressure, the dragon's attention swinging to Corin with the weight of a falling tower. ***A Warden. In my dark. Wearing no badge.*** The pupil narrowed to a blade. ***Tell me why I should not close my hand, little captain, and solve one problem for the price of a breath.***

"Because he unchained himself first," Xanthe said, stepping forward, into the line of that gaze, her heart hammering but her voice steady — steadier than it had any right to be. "Because he chose us over them before we ever asked. And because—" she swallowed, "—because I have something to say to you, and I'd rather he heard it too."

***Say it.***

Xanthe walked forward until she stood beneath the dragon's jaw, close enough to feel breath like a warm sea wash over her, close enough to see the fine cracking of the scales, the grey creeping in from the edges like frost claiming a window. Close enough to see, deep in the great dark eye, a reflection of herself: small, mortal, lit by lantern-light and by something else.

"They stole half your heart," she said. "And they'll take the rest when you're too weak to stop them, and they'll wear it as a weapon, and they'll call it peace. I've seen the woman who'll do it. I've seen how she smiles." Her voice shook now, and she let it. "I can't promise you vengeance. If I brought your flame back whole, you'd burn this realm to its roots, and maybe you'd be right to, and maybe the war your death was supposed to prevent would begin with me as its midwife. I can't promise you that. What I promise is this: *you will not die in a cage.* However this ends — however it ends — these people and I will open this door, and break this harness, and you will stand under the open sky again, or none of us will live to see whether we failed."

The silence afterward lasted a long time. Water dripped. The half-heart in its glass chamber guttered and steadied, guttered and steadied.

Then Ysolde laughed.

It was a terrible, wonderful sound — like rockslides in love, like thunder remembering joy — and it shook dust from the glowing moss and sent the rats pouring delightedly along the roots overhead.

***A hundred years,*** the dragon said, ***and the first soul to visit me brings neither bread nor scripture nor pity, but a promise she may not be able to keep.*** The great eye gentled. ***You are either the best thing that has happened to me in a century, little voice, or the worst. I find I no longer much care which.***

Her foreclaw uncurled — slowly, joints crackling like green wood in fire — and extended toward them. Resting in the cup of it, where the talons curled inward, was something that made Marisol gasp and Corin go rigid.

A scale. Shed, old, dull — but humming faintly, and shaped, Xanthe realized as she lifted it, exactly like a key.

***The wardens' locks answer to iron and rank,*** Ysolde said. ***My locks answer to this. When you go to take back what they stole from me — and you will, I see it in you the way I once saw weather coming over mountains — take my scale. The vaults below the Spire were built on my bones' foundations. Stone remembers whose heart warms it.***

Xanthe took the scale. It was warm. Of course it was warm. Everything in this place was warm; everything here was borrowed from the creature they were leaving behind.

Above them, muffled through a hundred feet of rock, a bell began to ring — the Spire's alarm bell, urgent and wrong, ringing at an hour when no honest bell rang. Corin's head snapped up. "They know something. Patrol change, or someone talked, or—" He looked at the others, at the dragon, at Xanthe, and finished differently than he'd begun: "We have to move. Now."

***Go,*** Ysolde said, and her eye was already closing, her breath already slowing, the tide of her ebbing back toward its long dim vigil. ***And little voice — when you hold my fire in your hands, and it whispers to you, and it will — remember that it whispers lies to whoever carries it. It whispered them to me once. That is how the first war started. Not with hatred. With a lie that felt like love.***

They ran. Behind them the great door sighed shut, and the wards re-knotted themselves, and the heartbeat faded mile by mile as they climbed back toward the cold.

But Xanthe carried the scale against her sternum, and it stayed warm the whole way up, and in the dark of the rat-chimney, as the alarm bell tolled above, she understood with perfect clarity what came next.

Somewhere in the Spire above them, behind Warden steel and Voss's smiling face, the other half of a dragon's heart sat in a vault, waiting to be stolen.

Pip, panting beside her, grinned into the darkness. "So," the child said. "When do we do the fun part?"
