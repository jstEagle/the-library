## 8. Theft of Fire

Marisol had never liked vaults.

She had grown up in a village where the only locked thing was the church poor-box, and even that had a key hanging on a nail beside it because everyone knew whose hands were honest and whose were not. Locks, her grandmother used to say, were for people who had forgotten how to trust their neighbors. Marisol had spent forty years proving her wrong in small ways — picking the latch on the miller's grain shed during the famine winter, slipping through the hedge-witch's herb cupboard when no one was looking — but she had never stopped believing that a lock was an argument, and that somewhere behind every one of them was something somebody was afraid to lose.

The Warden vault beneath the Spire was not afraid of losing anything so small as grain or silver.

She stood now in a service corridor two levels below the street, pressed into an alcove behind a rack of ceremonial halberds that hadn't been polished since the last war, and she listened. That was most of what hedge-craft was, if she was honest: listening. Listening to walls. Listening to the hum of iron wards laid into stone like veins of cold lightning. The vault door ahead of them sang a note she could feel in her back teeth, a low, grinding drone that never quite resolved, the sound of a hundred years of oaths compressed into metal. It was the ugliest music she had ever heard, and she had once heard a dying bear sing.

Xanthe crouched beside her, fox-wrapped in a sling against her chest like a babe, its bandaged flank rising and falling with shallow breaths. Corin stood at her other shoulder in borrowed livery, his captain's bearing doing more work than any disguise could, because men who had been captains all their lives carried themselves a certain way and no tailor could counterfeit it. Brack filled the corridor behind them like a landslide waiting for permission. And Pip —

Pip was grinning.

That was the thing about the child that Marisol could never decide whether to love or fear. Eleven years old, quick fingers, quicker mouth, and a smile that opened like a trapdoor over a cellar full of knives. Pip had picked three locks on the way down without breaking stride, had talked a bored sentry out of his post with a story about a rat infestation in the records office that was so detailed Marisol half believed it herself, and now stood bouncing on the balls of small boots in front of the ugliest door in the realm as though it were a birthday present.

"Third tumbler's got a false gate," Pip whispered, fingers dancing inside the mechanism with a pair of bent awls. "Feel that? Clicks twice but doesn't catch. Old trick. Whoever built this thought they were clever."

"They *were* clever," Corin murmured. "They built it under a mountain."

"Clever's not careful." Click. Click. The third tumbler seated true. "There. Heart of the last dragon, coming right up."

Marisol put her hand flat against the stone and listened one last time. The drone of the wards had changed pitch since they'd entered the corridor — thinner, she thought, or maybe just tired. Everything in this place felt tired. The torches burned low and blue-tinged, fed by oil that smelled of ash. Even the rats had gone quiet; Xanthe's little scouts had reported the underlevels nearly empty, the vermin driven off or dead, as though the building itself were slowly exhaling everything alive within it.

Except, of course, for the thing they had come to steal.

"You're sure about the route out?" she asked Xanthe, low.

"The rats say the coal chute on the north face is unwatched since the fire," Xanthe said. "It's a long climb up, but Brack can boost us and the grate's rusted through at the elbow." She scratched the fox's ears absently. "They also say there's a new patrol pattern below. Doubled. Someone's nervous."

"Someone's always nervous," Corin said. "Nervous is the natural state of a garrison built on a lie."

Marisol looked at him then — really looked, the way she looked at herbs before she cut them. He had changed in the weeks since Greytooth Pass, or perhaps he had simply stopped hiding what he was. The badge was gone from his coat, melted down or buried, she didn't know which. But the man remained, and the man was afraid tonight. She could smell fear on him the way she could smell rain coming, sharp and green underneath the lamp oil and leather. Not fear of the vault. Fear of what taking the flame would make him.

Good, she thought. A man who isn't afraid of what he steals deserves to be caught.

"Pip," she said. "Last chance to be sensible."

Pip looked up at her with those enormous dark eyes, and for one moment the grin fell away and Marisol saw the child underneath — thin wrists, a bruise fading yellow along the jaw from some old life none of them asked about, the particular watchfulness of someone who had learned young that doors open for boldness and stay shut forever for caution.

"Sensible got me a cell in Copperlane," Pip said. "Bold gets me a dragon. Open it."

* * *

The vault was smaller than Marisol expected, and colder.

Not cold the way cellars are cold, with a damp breath of earth. This was a manufactured cold, a warded cold, the air scoured of warmth the way a wound is scoured of blood. Frost feathered the corners of the ceiling in high summer. Their own breath hung around them like ghosts refusing to disperse. In the center of the room, on a plinth of black basalt, under a bell jar of warded glass thick as a fist, sat the vial.

It was smaller than a thumb. The glass was old, hand-blown, slightly crooked, the kind of vessel a village glassworker might have made for a tincture of willow bark. Inside it burned a curl of light the color of a forge seen through a keyhole — gold at the heart, shading to ember-red at the edges, moving. Not flickering. Moving, the way a living thing moves, slow and deliberate, turning over on itself like a sleeper who dreams.

Marisol had tended a thousand fires. She had coaxed hearths back from gray ash, breathed life into banked coals with nothing but her own lungs and a stubbornness her neighbors called witchcraft, which was fair, because it was. She knew fire the way a midwife knows birth: intimately, respectfully, with a clear-eyed understanding of what it costs and what it takes.

This was not fire. This was the memory of fire. The first fire. The fire before fire, from which all other fires were descended, and it looked at her — she would swear this until her dying day, she would swear it on her grandmother's grave — it *looked* at her through the glass, and it was hungry, and it was lonely, and it was so old that loneliness had become its whole architecture.

"Oh," she said softly. "Oh, you poor thing."

"Don't touch the jar," Corin said sharply. "Warded glass. It'll take skin."

"I wasn't going to touch it." She hadn't been. But her hands had wanted to, the way hands want to cup water, the way hands want to warm themselves. That was the danger of the thing, she understood suddenly. It didn't need to tempt you. It only needed to be cold where you were warm, and lonely where you were loved, and every hand in the world would reach for it on its own.

Brack ducked under the lintel and filled the doorway behind them, blocking out the corridor light. His voice came out gentle as ever, a landslide trying to be a lullaby. "Small jar," he rumbled. "Big trouble."

"All the biggest troubles come in small jars," Marisol said. "Ask any apothecary."

Xanthe unwound the fox sling and set the animal gently on the frost-rimed floor. The fox stood on three legs, its fourth bound tight, and limped to the plinth, and sat, and looked up at the vial with an expression Marisol had no name for except *recognition*. Then it spoke — a dry whisper of a voice, like leaves dragged over slate.

"That's her. That's the piece they cut out of her. It remembers being whole."

Marisol felt the words go through her like a needle through cloth. Around her, she saw the others feel it too: Xanthe's jaw tightening, Corin's hand flexing on his baton, Brack's great shoulders drawing in as though bracing against weather. Only Pip leaned closer, nose almost to the glass, eyes wide as coins.

"It's so little," Pip whispered. "How can something so little be worth a whole war?"

"Because it's not little," Marisol said. "It's just far away. Everything old looks small from a distance." She drew the padded satchel from her shoulder — quilted wool, lined with ash-silk, the kind of wrapping you'd use for a newborn or a bomb, and tonight it was both. "Corin. The jar lifts straight up?"

"Counterweighted. Lift and turn a quarter-turn sunwise, then set it in the cradle. Don't tilt it." He paused. "And don't look at it too long. I've seen what longing does to disciplined people."

Too late, Marisol thought. But she kept it behind her teeth.

The jar came away with a sound like a held breath released. For one heartbeat the vault was utterly silent — no ward-drone, no frost-crackle, nothing — and in that silence Marisol heard, faint and far, a sound she would carry to her grave: a cry from somewhere above them, deep in the bones of the Spire, long and low and cracked with a century of grief. The dragon, feeling her heart move.

Then the alarm began.

* * *

Later, Marisol would try to reconstruct exactly how it went wrong, and she never fully could. That was the nature of disasters; they assembled themselves out of a dozen small rights, each one reasonable, each one defensible, and only the sum of them was ruin.

The route out was good. The rats had not lied. The coal chute existed, the grate was rusted, the patrol had moved north. What none of them had counted on — what no amount of listening to walls could have told them — was that the vault itself would call for help. The wards didn't merely sound an alarm. They *screamed*, down channels cut through the mountain's living rock, and every Warden in the Spire heard it in their teeth the way Marisol had heard the vault's drone, and came running with the mindless urgency of white blood cells toward a wound.

They made the first corridor. They made the second. Brack took a portcullis off its hinges with a sound like the world's largest knuckle cracking, holding it overhead while four bodies scrambled beneath, and Marisol saw the tendons stand out in his neck like bridge cables and thought, absurdly, *I will owe this man a lifetime of soup.*

They made the stairwell. And in the stairwell, at the landing where the coal-chute passage branched east, the Wardens were waiting.

Six of them. Then nine. Then more, pouring up from below, lanterns swinging, the blue-white glare of warden-lights washing the stone the color of drowned flesh. Corin stepped forward with his empty hands raised and his old authority rolling off him like heat off a forge — *"Stand down, I'm still your superior, this is a Warden operation"* — and it bought them ten seconds, fifteen, the soldiers hesitating because the voice was Ashe's voice and the posture was Ashe's posture and orders wore him like a second skin.

Ten seconds is a long time. Fifteen is longer. But it is not enough.

It was Pip who saw the gap — the maintenance hatch, child-high, half-hidden behind a stack of salt barrels, leading to the drainage galleries. Of course it was Pip. Small bodies fit through small doors; that was the whole grim arithmetic of the child's survival. Pip slipped toward it between one lantern swing and the next, quick as a dropped coin, and Marisol saw the plan assemble itself in the kid's head the way plans did: *I go through, I open the far side, they follow, we're out.*

And then a Warden sergeant — young, frightened, his light shaking — shouted "*There! The small one!*"

Everything after that happened very fast and very slowly at once, the way the worst moments do.

Pip ran. A bolt from a crossbow meant for the child's back buried itself in a barrel instead, salt geysering white. Two Wardens closed from the left. Corin roared and threw himself into them like a battering ram wearing a man's body. Xanthe screamed — Marisol would remember that scream, wordless, animal, torn out of her — and the fox screamed with her, and somewhere in the chaos of bodies and lights and salt-smoke, small hands closed around the hatch wheel and hauled it open and a small body wriggled through into the dark.

Alone.

The hatch clanged shut behind Pip. On the far side, alone, in the drainage galleries, with the Wardens between the child and everyone who loved them.

"No," Marisol heard Xanthe say. "No, no, *no*—"

"Go," Corin snarled, bleeding from the eyebrow, holding two soldiers off with fury and footwork. "GO. The flame can't be taken back. Get it out. *Get it out.*"

* * *

Here is what Marisol knew about power, knowledge bought the way all real knowledge is bought:

Hedge-magic has no schools. No towers, no oaths, no hierarchy of masters. It has grandmothers, and grandmothers teach one lesson above all others, and the lesson is this: *you are a candle, not a bonfire, and a candle that burns at both ends lights one room and dies in it.*

Her whole craft was economy. A pinch of feverfew, not a handful. One word spoken backward, not seven. Warmth drawn from her own body to coax a seedling in late frost — a spoonful of herself, measured, replaceable with sleep and broth. Forty years of spoonsfuls. She had never once taken the ladle to the well.

Standing in the drainage gallery with the vial burning against her heart and Pip alone on the wrong side of a locked hatch and Wardens flooding the corridors like water finding every low place, Marisol took the ladle to the well.

She did not decide it, exactly. Decision implies alternatives considered calmly. It was more like a dam failing — forty years of thrift giving way all at once to the simple arithmetic that a child was behind her and a fire was against her chest and both of them were worth everything she had and more.

She turned to face the gallery they'd come through, planted her feet the way her grandmother had taught her — *rooted like elder, flexible like willow* — and opened herself.

Magic, when it comes, does not feel like power. That is the lie every tower-taught mage believes and every hedge-witch knows better. It feels like *drainage*. Like blood leaving the face. Like standing in a river that flows out of you instead of past you. She pulled the heat out of her own marrow and pushed it ahead of her down the corridor as a wall of force — not flame, she hadn't the reserves for flame, but pressure, raw and howling, a wind with weight behind it, the deep exhalation of the earth itself given her voice for one terrible moment.

The Wardens in the gallery went down like wheat. Lanterns guttered. Stone dust fell from the ceiling in gray curtains. Somewhere a pipe burst and water sheeted across the floor, flash-freezing where her drained cold met it, and the pursuit slid to a halt in a tangle of limbs and ice and shouting.

Marisol stood in the middle of it, swaying, and felt what she had spent.

It was not a spoonful. It was not a ladleful. It was years. She felt them go — felt the strength of her forties drain out through her feet, felt the reserves she'd hoarded against old age pour away like sand through a split sack. Her hair, she would find later, had gone white at the temples in the space of a single spell, two clean wings of frost above her ears. Her hands shook. Her knees told her, in no uncertain terms, that they were seventy years old and done negotiating.

And beneath the exhaustion, quiet and unmistakable, lay the other cost, the one nobody warns you about: the silence. Her inner senses — the ones that tasted weather coming and felt the green pulse of growing things — had gone dim. Not dark. Dim. Like a room where someone has closed the shutters halfway. She had bought their escape with a portion of her sight, and the portion did not feel like it was coming back.

"Worth it," she said aloud, to no one, to the dark, to her grandmother's ghost. "You hear me, old woman? Worth it."

Brack caught her before her knees finished their argument. He lifted her like she weighed nothing, which was a lie her body appreciated anyway, and ran.

* * *

They came out through the coal chute into night air that tasted like mercy, all five of them minus one, and the cold hit Marisol's drained body like a slap and she welcomed it because feeling anything at all meant she was still alive.

Xanthe collapsed against the mountainside with the satchel clutched to her chest and the fox pressed to her throat, and she was crying without any sound at all, tears freezing on her cheeks, saying Pip's name over and over like a prayer that had lost its god. Corin stood at the mouth of the chute with his sword drawn at last — actually drawn, actually bare, the traitor's steel — facing back down the dark they'd climbed out of, daring anyone to follow.

Nobody followed. The mountain had swallowed them and the mountain, for now, was on their side.

"We go back," Xanthe said. Her voice cracked on the second word. "Tonight. Now. We go back for—"

"And do what?" Corin didn't turn around. His voice was terrible and steady. "Walk into a garrison of two hundred with one vial and a bad leg? They'll take the flame AND the child, and then it's all been for nothing — Pip included."

"Then what do we DO?"

Marisol, shivering in Brack's arms, watched Corin turn. Watched him look at Xanthe — at the woman he'd let walk out of a burned granary, the woman whose treason had become his own — and saw something resolve in his face, some final internal battle ending not in victory but in surrender to a higher authority than the Wardens' law.

"We get help," he said. "People I knew. People who owe me. People who've spent years moving things through this country that the Wardens didn't want moved." He sheathed the sword with a click that sounded, in the frozen dark, like a door closing on his entire former life. "Smugglers. If anyone can reach a Warden cell and take a child out of it, it's the people I used to hunt."

Xanthe stared at him. "You'd go back to them? After—" She gestured at everything: the badge, the treason, the burned bridges.

"After," Corin agreed quietly. "After is the only direction I have left."

Marisol closed her eyes. Against her chest, wrapped in ash-silk and quilted wool, the stolen heart of the last dragon pulsed — slow, patient, terribly awake — and through the silk she felt it listening. Felt it attend to Corin's voice, to Xanthe's grief, to the shape of the promise being forged on a frozen mountainside: *we will come back for the small one.*

The flame warmed, very slightly, against her skin.

And far to the south, down in the dark of the Spire, in a cell that smelled of lye and cold iron, a Warden with a charming smile knelt to speak kindly to a captured child — and asked, in a voice like honey poured over a blade, the first question of many.
