## 9. Interrogation

The cell smelled of lye and cold iron, and Pip decided — with the particular stubbornness that had kept a small orphan alive through six winters on the streets of the Spire city — that she hated it.

Not feared it. *Hated* it. Fear was for people who had something to lose. Pip had lost everything twice over: once when the fever took her mother, and once more tonight, when rough hands in grey cloaks had pulled her out of the vault corridor while Marisol's fire bloomed behind them like a terrible sunrise. Everything else was just waiting to be lost. So there was nothing left to be afraid of, and what remained was hate, and hate was warm. Hate kept you fed.

She sat on the stone bench — no bed, only a bench worn smooth by other small backs, which told her things about this room she tried not to think about — and she counted. Counting helped. Counting was how you survived a market crowd, how you tracked a mark's purse-strings, how you knew when the watchman's rounds would pass and when you could slip from shadow to shadow. She counted the cracks in the ceiling (eleven), the drips from the pipe in the corner (one every nine heartbeats, slower than her own), the hours since they'd taken her coat with all its hidden pockets (she'd stopped counting that one, because it made her throat tight).

They'd taken her boots too. That felt deliberate. Small cruelty, or maybe just procedure. Either way her feet were cold, and cold feet made it hard to feel brave, so she tucked them up under her and wrapped her arms around her knees and pretended she was somewhere else. The roof of the Tanner's Guildhall, say, where the tiles held the sun's warmth long after dark. Or the bakery on Cordwainer Street at dawn, when the ovens breathed heat through the floor grates and you could stand on them like standing on the back of a sleeping animal.

*You're not brave,* she told herself sternly. *You're just small and stubborn, and those aren't the same thing.*

But then she thought of Xanthe's face when they'd run — that fierce, stricken look, half-turned toward the noise of the capture, Corin's arm barring her like a gate — and she thought of Marisol screaming her name in a voice that cracked down the middle, and she thought: *maybe they are the same thing.*

The lock turned.

Pip had heard a lot of locks in her life. Cheap ones that rattled, good ones that whispered, the great iron monstrosity on the debtors' door at the counting-house that groaned like a dying man. This one did none of those things. It turned with a sound like a held breath being released, smooth and soft and wrong, and the door swung open without a creak, because of course it didn't creak. Nothing about this place was allowed to be imperfect.

The woman who came in was not what Pip expected.

She'd imagined a Warden interrogator as a big scarred man with a beard and a truncheon, someone whose face you could spit at and feel good about. Instead, a tall lady in silver-grey swept into the cell like she was entering a drawing room, followed by two guards who carried a small table and a chair, set them down with absurd gentleness, and withdrew. The lady wore her dark hair coiled at the nape of her neck, and her uniform had no rank-braid at all, which somehow made Pip's stomach drop further than any amount of braid could have. You only got to dress plain when everyone already knew who you were.

"I do apologize for the accommodations," the woman said. Her voice was warm. That was the horrible part. It was a voice like fresh bread and hearth-smoke, like somebody's mother grown elegant, and it filled the little cell the way water fills a cup. "We weren't expecting a guest of your... stature. Most of our visitors are larger and considerably less polite."

She sat down on the chair. She didn't loom. She arranged herself at Pip's eye level, hands folded, and smiled, and her smile was the kind that reached all the way to her eyes, which was the second most horrible thing about her, because Pip knew — she *knew*, the way street kids know rain coming in their bones — that it was false from root to crown. But knowing didn't help. Some lies are built so well you can't find the seam.

"You're cold," the woman observed. "That was thoughtless of us. One moment."

She snapped her fingers — softly, almost apologetically — and one of the guards reappeared with a folded grey blanket, which she shook out and draped across Pip's shoulders herself. Her hands were cool and light and careful. They smelled of some clean herb, rosemary maybe, and underneath it something else, something faintly scorched, like a candle snuffed too close to the wick.

"There." She settled back. "I'm Sarel Voss. High Warden of Thornfell, though titles are such tedious things between friends. And you are?"

Pip said nothing. She pulled the blanket tighter and looked at a point on the wall just past Voss's left ear, which was a trick an older pickpocket named Wick had taught her: *never look 'em in the eye, never look at their hands, look at nothing and they've got nothing.*

"Shy," Voss said, nodding as if this were charming. "That's all right. I'm told you're called Pip. A good name. Practical. A name for someone who gets into small places and comes out again." She tilted her head. "Though I notice you haven't come out of this one."

*Yet,* Pip thought, and kept her face still.

Voss studied her for a long moment, and Pip had the uncomfortable sensation of being read like a ledger — weighed, assessed, entered into columns. Then the High Warden sighed, and the sigh was so genuinely weary-sounding that Pip almost believed it.

"They didn't leave you, you know," Voss said gently. "Your friends. I want you to understand that. People will tell you stories — that your kind abandons its own, that the people who ran from that vault cared nothing for you. But I saw the reports. The witch burned nearly everything she had to cover your escape. The big one — Brack, is it? — he broke a Warden's arm getting to the door when he heard you'd been taken. They love you, child. That's precisely why I'm confident they'll come back."

Something cold slid down Pip's spine, colder than the cell, colder than the missing boots. Because it was true, all of it, every word, and that was the trap of it. Voss wasn't lying about the love. She was using it, the way you'd use a rope thrown to a drowning person — except the rope was tied to an anchor.

"So here is what I propose," Voss continued, folding her hands again. "No chains. No threats. I'm not going to hurt you, Pip. I have never hurt a child in my life, and I never will. What I'm going to do is be honest with you, because I believe children deserve honesty far more than adults ever give them credit for. And in exchange, all I ask is that you listen. Can you listen? I think you can. I think you're a very clever listener."

Pip shrugged. It cost her something to move at all, but a shrug was safe. A shrug promised nothing.

"Good girl." Voss leaned forward slightly, and her voice dropped, confiding, intimate, the voice of a woman sharing a secret over a kitchen table. "Let me tell you what actually happened three nights ago, when the stars went out. Not the story we've been telling the city — you're too smart for the story, I can see it in your face. The truth."

And she told it.

She told it beautifully. That was the thing Pip couldn't stop noticing even as her chest went tight: Voss told the truth the way con artists told lies, with rhythm and detail and perfectly placed pauses. The wards of Thornfell had been failing for years, she said. Decaying. Fed by an old covenant that no one living fully understood, sustained by a creature beneath the Spire that the founders had bound out of desperation, and now that creature was dying, and when it died, everything would go with it — the wards, the borders, the protections that kept the wild things out and the realm whole.

"The beast-speakers knew," Voss said softly. "A network of them, generations deep, hiding in plain sight among us. Smugglers, hedge-witches, tinkers, thieves." Her eyes flicked, very briefly, to Pip's bare feet. "People who move through cracks. They let the wards fall, Pip. They wanted them to fall. And now they've stolen the last of the creature's fire — the one thing that could have been used to rebuild the protections properly, under proper stewardship — and they're carrying it north to give it back to the monster, so that it can rise and burn the way it burned a century ago."

She let that sit. The drip in the corner counted out nine beats. Eleven.

"That's why I need your help," Voss said. "Not information — I know where they're going. I need you to help me *save them*. Because here is the tragedy, child: your friends don't know what they're carrying. They think they're freeing a prisoner. They're delivering a weapon into the jaws of a war. When the dragon rises, the old blood will answer — every beast-speaker, every hedge-witch, every creature of the old pacts — and the realm will tear itself apart the way it did before the Wardens existed. Thousands will die. Your friends will die first, used and discarded by the very people who sent them." She shook her head, sorrowful. "Unless someone reaches them in time. Unless someone they trust tells them the truth. And who do they trust, Pip? Who do they love?"

Her eyes were kind. Her eyes were terribly, perfectly kind.

"A small one," Voss said. "Who they came back for once already, and will come back for again."

* * *

They brought food after she left. Good food — bread still warm, butter, a bowl of stew with actual meat in it, the kind of meal Pip had eaten maybe ten times in her life and remembered every single one. They brought a cushion for the bench. They brought her boots back, warmed by a fire.

That was worse than the cold had been. Cruelty you could fight. Kindness with hooks in it got under your skin like a splinter you couldn't reach.

Pip ate the stew anyway. She wasn't stupid; refusing food was a fool's game, and besides, hating Voss didn't mean hating bread. She ate methodically, and while she ate, she did what she always did: she counted, and she thought.

*She lied,* Pip told herself. *About the beast-speakers letting the wards fall.* Hadn't she? Pip hadn't been there when the stars went out — she'd been asleep under the tanner's awning like any sensible person — but she'd been with Xanthe and the others for weeks now, riding cold passes and sharing thin soup, and she had watched Xanthe talk to foxes and grieve for a mountain and carry the weight of the ward-fall like stones sewn into her coat. Xanthe didn't let anything fall. Xanthe *fixed* things. That was the whole shape of her.

But — and here was the splinter, the hook, the thing that worried at her — Voss hadn't sounded like she was lying. She'd sounded like a woman reporting weather. And Pip had known liars her whole life, brilliant ones, desperate ones, and the best of them always had a flicker somewhere, a hitch in the rhythm, a sweat at the hairline. Voss had nothing. Either she was the greatest liar Pip had ever met, or —

Or she believed it. Every word. Which was somehow much, much worse.

Because Pip had listened carefully, past the comfort and the blanket and the warm bread, and she'd caught the thing underneath, the thing Voss probably thought a child wouldn't hear. It was in the way she'd said *rebuild the protections properly, under proper stewardship.* In the way she'd said *the old blood will answer* — not with fear, but with something that had a shine to it, an appetite. In the way her whole body had gone quiet and attentive, like a dog watching a door, when she'd spoken of the dragon rising and thousands dying.

Sarel Voss didn't want to prevent a war.

Sarel Voss wanted one. Wanted it the way Pip wanted a full belly — with her whole self, with a hunger that had been fed on patience for years. All that talk of saving the realm, and underneath it, threaded through every word like wire through a candied apple: *let it come. Let it come, and let me be holding the sword when it does.*

Pip set down the spoon. She thought about Wick again, dead two winters now, coughing blood in a doorway while Pip held his hand because there was no one else. Wick had taught her the looking trick, and he'd taught her something else too, on his last lucid night: *Little Pip, when a rich man offers you kindness, count what it costs him. If it costs him nothing, it's worth nothing. And if it costs him nothing and gains him everything — run.*

What had Voss's kindness cost her? A blanket. A bowl of stew. A chair carried in by guards. Nothing. Less than nothing.

And what would it gain her? A small pair of feet walking out of a Warden stronghold toward four people who loved her, carrying — not a message, nothing so crude as a message — carrying a *doubt*. A little seed of doubt planted in the family's midst. *Did they abandon me? Do they know what they carry? Is Xanthe lying?* Doubt was cheaper than poison and killed just as sure.

Pip pulled the blanket around her shoulders and lay down on the bench, boots and all, and stared at the eleven cracks in the ceiling.

*I won't carry it,* she promised the dark. *Whatever she puts in my hands, I'll put it down. I'll walk out of here empty.*

But even as she thought it, she understood the shape of the real problem, the one that made her eyes sting despite everything. Voss didn't need her to betray anyone. Voss needed her to be *rescued.* Needed the family to come — Corin with his Warden knowledge, Marisol with her burnt-down magic, Xanthe with the flame's location written in her blood. A rescued child was a compass pointing straight at all of them. Pip was not a prisoner. Pip was bait on a hook, and the hook was love, and there was no trick she knew, no sleight of hand quick enough, to pick a hook out of your own heart.

The drip counted. Nine beats. Eleven.

Somewhere above her, muffled through floors of stone, a bell rang the third hour of the night watch, and Pip heard — or dreamed she heard, or maybe her gift-for-listening that wasn't magic, just attention, just the sharp ears of a small creature surviving — the sound of wings. Many wings. Far off to the north, a wind moving against the wind.

She pressed her face into the grey blanket that smelled of rosemary and snuffed candles, and she made herself one last promise, the smallest and hardest one:

*Hold on, small one. Be boring. Be useless. Eat their stew and tell them nothing and stay alive, because they are coming, and when they come, you'll be the only one inside these walls who knows what Sarel Voss really wants.*

*War. She wants war. And she thinks I'm going to hand it to her.*

Pip closed her eyes. Outside the tiny barred window, high above the courtyard, the sky was still starless — but at the northern edge of the black, so faint she might have invented it, something glimmered. Once. Twice.

Like a signal. Like a promise.

Like the first star coming home.
