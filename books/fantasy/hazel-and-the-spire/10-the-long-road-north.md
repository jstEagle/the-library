## 10. The Long Road North

The mountains north of the Spire had teeth.

Xanthe felt them in her knees by the third day — a grinding ache that climbed from stone into bone and settled there like an old debt. The road was barely a road anymore, just a scar of gravel where pilgrims had once walked to the Dragonfast shrines before the Wardens closed them. Frost furred the gorse in silver whiskers each morning, and the wind came down off the high passes with the smell of old snow and older grief.

She kept the vial against her sternum, wrapped in three layers of wool and one of Marisol's warding charms, and she caught herself touching it the way other people touched amulets. Half a heartbeat of another creature's life, stolen and stoppered. It was warm even through the cloth. It had been warm since they pulled it from the vault's iron dark, warm as a sleeping bird, and some part of Xanthe — the part that had spoken to beasts since she was small enough to fit inside a flour barrel — could feel it dreaming.

*Not yet,* she told it, when it stirred. *Not yet.*

Behind her, the others made their slow procession up the switchbacks: Brack hauling the packs as if they were filled with feathers instead of dried fish and rope, his grey-green shoulders dusted with frost; Marisol walking with her hands jammed in her armpits and muttering calculations about how much magic a warming charm cost versus how much dignity; Pip, quiet for once, walking close enough to Corin that their shadows overlapped.

Pip had been out of the Wardens' cells for six days now, and still the child flinched at boots on gravel, at the jingle of buckles, at anyone saying *hey* too sharply. Whatever Voss had done in that interrogation room, whatever comfort she'd offered with those soft, terrible hands, it lived in Pip like a splinter too deep to dig out.

Corin had traded everything he had left to get the child back. Old contacts, old favors, a letter written in cipher to a quartermaster who owed him a life from the Saltmarsh campaign — and in exchange, his name went onto a list. Not the list of wanted fugitives. The list beneath it. The one Voss kept for people who might still be useful, or might still be dangerous, or both.

He hadn't complained. He'd simply handed over the last coin of his old life and turned north with the rest of them, and Xanthe had watched him do it and felt something in her chest rearrange itself around the sight.

* * *

They made camp that night in the lee of a fallen watchtower, half-swallowed by the hillside, its stones furred with lichen the color of old blood. Brack built the fire with a stonemason's patience — a ring of fitted rocks, a hearth of dry pine, flames coaxed up small and hot rather than tall and hungry. "Smoke draws eyes," he rumbled. "Warmth draws friends. I know which I prefer."

Marisol rigged her charms in a perimeter — not wards exactly, more like tripwires of attention, threads of her own dwindling power strung between the pines. She sat back down heavily afterward, rubbing her fingers like a woman who'd counted coins and come up short.

"How much did that cost you?" Xanthe asked quietly.

"A question and a half." Marisol smiled, which didn't reach her eyes. "I'll earn it back. Magic's like bread. You bake more."

"You said burning your reserves in the vault set you back months."

"I say many things." She pulled her cloak tighter. "Eat your fish, smuggler."

The fish was terrible — salt-crusted, smoke-bitter, full of bones — and it was the best meal Xanthe had ever eaten, because she ate it warm, among living people, while the wind screamed harmlessly above the tower walls. Pip ate three portions and then fell asleep sitting upright, mid-sentence, head tipping against Corin's arm. He went very still, the way he always did when the child touched him, as if afraid any movement might break something fragile — or prove something true.

"Put her down," Marisol said gently. "In the bedroll. She'll sleep better horizontal."

"She fell asleep on me," Corin said, as if reporting a tactical anomaly.

"Children do that," Brack said, "when they've decided you're safe. It's a great honor. Also a great weight. Both literally." He reached over and lifted Pip with one enormous hand, careful as a man moving eggs, and tucked her into the shared bedroll near the fire. She didn't wake. Her hand stayed curled around a smooth river stone she'd carried since the vault — Xanthe had never asked why, and suspected she never would.

* * *

Later, when the fire had burned down to orange ribs and the stars — still missing, still gone, a blackness overhead so complete it felt like pressure — Marisol began to talk.

She talked the way tired people talk, in a low stream aimed at the flames. About learning hedge-craft from a grandmother who'd been burned out of two villages for less. About the year she'd spent pretending to be a simple herb-woman, brewing teas for Warden wives, memorizing which officer beat his horse and which beat his family. About the night she'd decided that if the world insisted on calling her kind criminals, she would at least be a competent one.

"I was good at hiding," she said. "That was my whole talent. Hiding in plain sight, smiling at people who'd see me ash if they knew. And then—" she gestured vaguely at all of them, at the fire, at the vial-shaped lump against Xanthe's chest, "—then I met a woman who talks to foxes and a captain who commits treason with his face completely blank, and I thought, well. There goes the neighborhood."

Brack laughed — a sound like boulders settling. "You think that's a story? I built the aqueduct at Cael Ford. Thirty years of my life. Cut every stone true, laid every joint plumb. And when the Wardens needed a fortress wall in a hurry, they tore out half my aqueduct for the masonry and called it requisition. Called it *duty*." He turned his hands palms-up, mason's hands, scarred and square. "So I stopped building things for them. Started building things for people who say thank you. Mostly that means fixing chimneys in free-towns. Sometimes it means holding a pass." He glanced at Xanthe, and his voice went quieter. "I'd hold this one too. All of you should know that."

Nobody answered right away. Some truths are best left lying in the firelight, unburied but undisturbed.

It was Corin who spoke next, and it surprised Xanthe, because he gave stories away the way misers give alms — rarely, and only under compulsion, and it changed the giver every time.

"Saltmarsh," he said. "You've heard me mention it."

"The campaign," Marisol said. "Where the quartermaster owes you."

"There was a girl there. A smuggler." His eyes found Xanthe's across the fire, and held. "Ran medicine through a blockade because the Wardens' own supply chain had rotted and the garrison surgeon was cutting limbs off men who only needed poultices. We caught her. I caught her. Orders were to hang smugglers during wartime." He paused. "I wrote a report instead. Said she was a local guide, essential to operations, under my personal supervision. Kept her in the camp for four months. She saved eleven men I know of, probably more I don't. Then she vanished one night, and I let the report stand, and I got promoted, and I told myself that was justice — that the system worked, if you worked it."

"And then the system asked you to hunt her," Xanthe said softly.

"And then the system asked me to hunt her." He looked into the fire. "I keep thinking about what I would have done if I hadn't known your face. If Voss had handed the commission to some stranger with a clean conscience. I'd be a story you tell, Xanthe. *There was this captain once.* That's all. And the thing that frightens me isn't what I almost did. It's how easy it would have been."

The wind moved through the pines. Pip murmured in her sleep and rolled toward the warmth.

"It wasn't easy," Xanthe said. "What you're doing now. Don't make it smaller than it is."

He looked up at her then, and the firelight moved in his eyes, and for a long moment neither of them said anything at all, because everything either of them wanted to say was too large for the space between two sentences.

* * *

Xanthe took the middle watch, as she always did, because the animals came easier to her in the dark hours and she liked knowing what moved in the night. Tonight the mountain was nearly silent — the starless nights had done something to the wild things, thinned them out or driven them deep — but a snow-hare let her feel its heartbeat from thirty yards off, quick and small and unafraid, and a family of marten chittered drowsily in a hollow trunk, and far below, in the valley, sheep dreamed in a fold, woolly and warm and oblivious to empires.

She was so absorbed in listening that she didn't hear Corin approach until he settled onto the rock beside her, close enough that their shoulders touched.

"Couldn't sleep?"

"Too busy being awake." He exhaled slowly. "I keep doing the arithmetic. Distance to Dragonfast. Days of food. How long Marisol's charms hold. How long until Voss stops sending scouts and starts sending the Kindly Ones." He shook his head. "And underneath all of it, one number I can't solve."

"What number?"

"What happens after." He turned to look at her, and his voice dropped to the register he used for truths. "If we get the flame home. If Ysolde lives. If the war doesn't come. What then? You go back to smuggling moon-wool through passes that aren't watched anymore? I go back to an order that has my name on two lists?" He huffed something that wasn't quite a laugh. "I've fought battles with worse odds than peace."

Xanthe was quiet for a moment. Below them, the hare had moved on; the martens slept. The vial pulsed faintly against her heart, patient as a banked coal.

"When I was nine years old," she said, "my mother sold me to a caravan master for a winter's grain. Not cruelly — she cried while she did it, and the master was decent enough, mostly. But I learned early that belonging is a thing that gets weighed and priced. Every place I've ever been, I've been there on sufferance. Useful, so tolerated. Tolerated, so kept." She looked at the dead stars. "Until a fox started talking to me, and a dragon started dying, and a bunch of fools who owed me nothing decided I was worth bleeding for. Do you know what I felt when Brack stood in front of me at Greytooth Pass? Not gratitude. Something worse. I felt *entitled*. Like it was mine. Like they were mine." Her throat tightened. "I don't know how to want things, Corin. I'm very good at needing them and terrible at wanting them."

"And what do you want?" he asked. Very carefully. The way you ask a question you've already paid for.

The answer rose in her whole body at once — in the skin of her arms, in the pulse behind her teeth — and she let it, for once, instead of caging it.

"You," she said. "Not the way I need you, which is tactical. The way I want you, which is ruinous. I want the mornings where you pretend you're checking the perimeter and you're actually watching Pip eat. I want your terrible reports and your worse jokes and the way you look at me like I'm a report you can't finish writing. I want—" her voice cracked, and she laughed at herself, wet-eyed, furious at the crack, "—gods, I want a house. I've never wanted a house in my life. I want somewhere with a bad roof that we fix together, and I want you in it, and I want it to be *mine*, ours, and I don't know what to do with wanting except tell you, because you taught me that telling the truth is survivable."

Corin Ashe, who had faced down a Warden vault and a High Warden's charm and the collapse of everything he'd believed, looked at her like a man looking at open sky after a lifetime underground.

"I wrote my resignation from the Wardens in my head three times," he said hoarsely, "before I ever committed treason. All three times, the last line was your name. I thought it was guilt. I thought I was cataloguing debts." He reached out and took her hand — calloused palm against calloused palm, smuggler's grip meeting soldier's grip. "It wasn't guilt. I just didn't have a word for it that my training allowed. So I used the wrong ones for a long time." His thumb moved across her knuckles. "I love you. There. Cleanest report I've ever filed."

Xanthe laughed — really laughed, helpless and bright, the sound startling a marten into indignant chatter — and then she kissed him, and the mountain wind went on howling somewhere far above them, and the fire burned low and gold, and for one whole hour the world contained nothing but warmth.

* * *

She woke before dawn to the vial screaming.

Not aloud — it had no mouth — but in the way she'd learned to feel it, a frequency behind her breastbone, a note of alarm like a struck wire. She was on her feet with a knife in her hand before her mind fully woke, scanning the treeline, the dark, the frost-silvered stones.

Nothing moved. But far to the south, back along the road they'd climbed, back toward the Spire and its blind black tower, the horizon glowed.

Not sunrise. Sunrise was hours away, and sunrise came gold. This light was white — hard, surgical, wrong — and it pulsed, slow and rhythmic, like something breathing. Like something vast turning over in its sleep beneath the city, testing the walls of its cage.

Beside her, Corin rose silently, following her gaze. Marisol emerged from her blankets a moment later, and her face, when the strange light touched it, had gone the color of ash.

"That's not the dragon," the hedge-witch whispered. "Ysolde's flame is red-gold. I've seen it. That's—"

"I know what that is," Corin said grimly. "I've seen it before. In the vaults. In Voss's private workrooms." His jaw tightened. "She's not waiting for us anymore. She's harvesting."

Against Xanthe's heart, the stolen heart-flame pulsed once — twice — and then went very still, the way a wild thing goes still when it hears the hunter's dogs.

Pip sat up in the bedroll, eyes huge and dark in the dying firelight, and said, in a small clear voice:

"She knows where we're going. She wants us to bring it to her."
