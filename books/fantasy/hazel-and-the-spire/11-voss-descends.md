## 11. Voss Descends

The High Warden of the Spire did not pray.

She had stopped, in the manner of most childhood things, somewhere around her twelfth year, when her mother died coughing blood into a rag in a room that smelled of tallow and wet stone, while the Wardens of that era walked the streets above in their silver-grey cloaks and did nothing, because plague was not a breach of the peace and the peace was all they were sworn to. Sarel Voss had knelt at the bedside with her small hands folded and her small voice reciting the Litany of the Nine Flames, and she had understood, with the terrible clarity that children sometimes achieve and then spend their whole lives trying to recover or to bury, that no one was listening.

So she did not pray. She *planned*.

She stood now at the window of her office at the crown of the Spire — the true crown, not the ceremonial one with its gilded balustrade where the Warden-General received ambassadors — and watched the sun come up over the city of Thornfell's heart. Below her, the morning bells were ringing the curfew's end. The streets unknotted slowly, cautiously, the way a body moves when it suspects it is being watched. Which it was. She had tripled the patrols since the night the stars went out, and the people of the city had learned, with the docility of sheep who have seen the dog's teeth, to move only when permitted and to look at nothing.

The stars had come back, mostly. The astronomers said the heavens had suffered some temporary obscuration — ash, perhaps, or an unusual conjunction of cloud — but Sarel knew better, and the knowing sat in her chest like a swallowed coal, warm and terrible.

She had seen the wards fall. Not from outside, like the frightened citizens who had spilled into the streets screaming about the dark — but from within, from the undercroft where the great ward-lattice terminated in its thousand silvered threads, each thread fed by a channel of fire that had burned without fuel for a hundred years. She had watched those channels gutter. She had watched the lattice go dark thread by thread, like a harp being strangled string by string, and she had felt — she would admit this to no living soul — a moment of pure, unguarded grief.

Then the grief had passed, as grief does when one has trained oneself out of it, and what remained was the arithmetic.

A knock at the door. Precise. Three beats, evenly spaced. Only one person in the Spire knocked that way.

"Enter."

Warden-Commander Ilsette Crane came in the way she did everything: efficiently, quietly, and slightly afraid. She was a tall woman with a long face and the grey-streaked braid of twenty years' service, and she had been Sarel's right hand since the purging of the Riverhold garrison, because Ilsette was competent and ambitious and had done a thing in Riverhold that could never be spoken of, which made her, in Sarel's experience, the most reliable kind of officer.

"The interrogation report," Ilsette said, laying the folder on the desk. "The child."

"Summarize."

"Nothing. Four hours, nothing. She's — " Ilsette hesitated, which was unlike her. "She's not frightened, High Warden. That's the strange part. She asks *us* questions. She asked me if I had children. She asked the turnkey whether his mother knew he worked nights. By the end the turnkey was bringing her extra bread."

A smile touched Sarel's mouth. It did not reach anywhere else. "Charm is a weapon too. She's been trained by the streets, and the streets teach the same first lesson we do: make the other person like you, and they'll give you what you want." She opened the folder without reading it. "She saw our faces. We did not see hers. She'll hold. Children hold better than adults — they haven't yet learned that no one is coming. She still believes someone is coming for her."

"And is someone?"

"Yes," Sarel said. "The beast-speaker. And the traitor Ashe, if my information holds." She turned from the window at last. "Which brings us to the matter of the north road. Sit, Ilsette."

Ilsette sat. There was only one chair before the High Warden's desk, and everyone in the Spire understood what it meant that there was only one.

"Report."

"Three sightings of the fugitives on the pilgrim road past Greytooth. A trollish man, a hedge-witch, a child matching our prisoner's description — though that's impossible unless they've doubled back, which — "

"Which they have, because the child was the price of their escape and they are the sort of fools who pay their debts. Go on."

"A man answering Captain Ashe's description bought horses at the Millford post, using a Warden requisition seal. A *valid* seal." Ilsette's jaw tightened. "Someone inside is still feeding him."

"Someone inside is feeding him because I am letting them." Sarel watched the realization move through her commander's face like weather. "The old quartermaster's clerk, Ferren. He was Ashe's batman for six years. He'd cut off his own hand for that man. I could arrest him, and I would gain one clerk and lose the thread. Instead I let him pass what he finds, and every scrap he passes tells me where my traitors believe they're going." She folded her hands on the desk. "Do you know where they're going, Ilsette?"

"The Dragonfast crater. It's the only — " Ilsette stopped.

"Say it."

"It's the only place the flame could be returned. But returning it would — " The commander's voice dropped, as if the walls had ears, which they did. "It would wake her."

"*Her*," Sarel repeated, softly. "You see? A hundred years of doctrine, and the first word out of a Warden-Commander's mouth about the creature beneath our feet is *her*." She let the silence do its work. Ilsette's hands had gone still in her lap. "That is not a rebuke, Commander. That is the entire design. Sit with it a moment."

* * *

Sarel descended the Spire that afternoon, as she did every seventh day, into the undercrofts.

It was not an inspection, officially. The official record showed that the High Warden reviewed the ward-lattice monthly for signs of degradation, a duty inherited from her predecessors and performed with scrupulous diligence. The unofficial truth was simpler and stranger: Sarel Voss went down to see the dragon the way a miser goes down to count his gold. Not because the counting changes the gold. Because the counting is the pleasure.

The stairs went down nine turns, past the old garrison levels, past the flooded archive where the histories of the Dragonfast era moldered in sealed chests — she had read every one, twice — and into the deep cold where the stone changed character entirely. The Spire's foundations were not built on the mountain. They were built *into* it, and the mountain had not always been stone.

The ward-lattice chamber opened around her like the inside of a bell. A thousand silvered threads ran from the walls and floor and ceiling to a single point, and the single point was a cage of white iron and old oaths, and in the cage was the dragon.

Ysolde. Last of the fireborn. A hundred and forty years in the cage, though the records said a hundred, because the records had been adjusted, as records are, by men who preferred tidy numbers to true ones.

She had once, the oldest accounts agreed, been the color of dawn — rose-gold, copper, the red of the first hour. Now her scales had gone the grey of banked ash, and where the light touched them they gave back no shine at all. She lay with her skull resting against the iron, her eyes closed, her breathing so long and slow that a watcher could stand for ten minutes and believe her dead, until the vast ribs moved once, like a hill deciding to be a lung.

The channels of fire that fed the ward-threads had run dry four weeks ago. The lattice was dark. The border wards of Thornfell were held now by nothing but habit and the enemy's ignorance.

And yet the dragon lived. Barely. Because the High Wardens who came before Sarel had not merely caged her. They had *milked* her. A century of siphoning the heart-fire through iron needles and oath-channels, feeding it out thread by thread to hold the borders, to light the beacon-wards, to warm the Warden citadels through the worst winters — and always, always, skimming. Every High Warden for a hundred years had skimmed. A vial here. A phial there. Enough to extend a life, sharpen a mind, hold off age. Sarel's predecessor had died at ninety-four with the face of a man of sixty, and the College of Wardens had called it a blessing of the Nine Flames.

Sarel had found his stores after his death. Eleven years of skim, hoarded in a vault beneath the treasury, in vessels of warded glass.

She had added to them.

She stood before the cage now, her boots silent on the frost-rimed stone, and looked up into the dark that was the dragon's face.

"You're smaller," she said. Conversational. She often spoke to Ysolde; it was not madness, whatever the chaplains whispered, but bookkeeping. A creditor addresses the debtor. "You were smaller last month, and the month before. Another year, perhaps two, and the arithmetic resolves itself."

The dragon's eye opened. It was the size of a shield, and it had gone the pale milk-blue of a cataract, and yet it found Sarel and fixed on her with an attention that would have buckled a lesser woman's knees. There was no hatred in it. That was the thing no one believed when she tried — once, long ago, drunk, to a lover she had since arranged to be posted very far away — to explain it. There was no hatred in the dragon's gaze. There was something far more useful.

*Pity.*

"Say what you always say," Sarel murmured. "I know you can't. The oath-iron took your voice a century ago. But you say it anyway. I can hear it in the shape of your silence." She tilted her head. "*Little thief. Little thief of fire.*"

The eye did not waver.

"Your fire kept the borders for a hundred years," Sarel said. "Do you understand what that means? It means the peace is not a policy. It is not the Nine Flames' grace, whatever the litany says. It is *you*. Your suffering, spun out into wards, holding nine kingdoms from one another's throats. The realm has been at peace for a century because you have been in agony for a century." She smiled, and it was, for one unguarded instant, the smile of the girl at the plague-bed — the smile of someone who has looked directly into the world's machinery and found it obscene. "I am the only honest person in the entire edifice, Ysolde. Everyone else believes the litany. I read the ledgers."

The great ribs moved. Once. The sound of the breath went through the chamber like wind through a canyon.

"So here is what happens now," Sarel said, and her voice changed, took on the cadence she used in the Council hall, the pulpit-cadence, the one that made hardened garrison commanders sit straighter. "The beast-speakers have stolen what remains of your heart-flame. That is the finding of my inquiry, and it will be the finding of the tribunal, and it will be written in the annals, because I will write the annals. The wards did not fail of age. They were *sabotaged*. The stars did not go out by conjunction. They were *extinguished* — by the old magic, the beast-magic, the magic that the Nine Flames themselves condemned when they cast down the fireborn at Dragonfast."

She began to walk the chamber's rim, slowly, her hands clasped behind her back, and the dragon's eye tracked her, and it occurred to Sarel — not for the first time — that this was the only congregation she had ever wanted.

"For ninety years the realm has been fraying. You've felt it, even down here. The free-towns that won't pay tithe. The hedge-witches and their little rebellions. The beast-speakers, breeding in the borderlands like weeds, whispering to animals, answerable to no oath and no flame. Nine kingdoms and not one of them loves the others. Every Warden-General before me has tried to hold it together with garrisons and grain prices and marriage alliances, and every one of them failed, because you cannot knit a realm together with *thread*." She stopped, directly before the cage, and her reflection was faint and ghostly in the milk-blue of the dragon's eye. "You knit it with fire. You give them an enemy."

The word fell into the cold like a stone into a well.

"A holy enemy," Sarel said. "Not a kingdom — kingdoms make wars that end in treaties. A *corruption*. The old magic, rising again. The beast-speakers who murdered the wards and stole the sacred flame and darkened the very stars. Every village that ever harbored a hedge-witch will denounce its hedge-witches. Every free-town that ever traded with a beast-speaker will burn its own registers and swear it was deceived. They will come to *us*, Ilsette's precious garrisons will swell with volunteers, and the nine kingdoms will stand shoulder to shoulder for the first time in a century, shoulder to shoulder against the dark." Her voice softened, and this was the masterstroke, and she knew it, and she let herself feel the pleasure of it, cold and clean as a blade going in. "And the beautiful thing, Ysolde — the *exquisite* thing — is that it will all be true. The wards did fall. The stars did go out. The flame *was* stolen. I have not had to invent a single thing. I have only had to arrange the order in which the realm learns the truth."

She turned away from the cage and walked toward the stair, and paused at the threshold, as she always did.

"When you die," she said, without looking back, "and it will be soon — weeks, perhaps — the last of the fireborn passes out of the world, and the age of magic ends, and the age of the Flame begins. Your heart-fire will be in a vault. And I will carry it into battle at the head of the holiest army this realm has ever raised, and when they see it burn, they will weep, and they will kneel, and they will *obey*." A beat. "You kept the peace with your suffering for a hundred years. I will keep it with your death for a thousand. History will call us both saviors. The difference between us, old mother, is that I know what I am."

The dragon's eye closed.

Sarel climbed the nine turns of stair, and by the third turn she had put the girl at the plague-bed away again, and by the ninth she was the High Warden entire, serene and silver-cloaked, and when she emerged into the evening light the first stars were pricking out overhead, and she looked at them the way a jeweler looks at stones he intends to reset.

* * *

Ilsette was waiting in the map room, and she was not alone.

The man by the hearth was broad, weathered, dressed in the undyed wool of a hill trader, and he had the flat, patient eyes of the Warden's deep-service — the eyes of men who spent years being no one in particular. He rose when Sarel entered and inclined his head exactly as far as protocol required and not a degree more.

"High Warden." Ilsette's voice was carefully level. "This is — "

"Master Corvane," Sarel said. "Of the wool trade. Whose wool, at present, is following three fugitives and a disgraced captain up the pilgrim road." She crossed to the great map of the northern reaches that dominated the room, its parchment scarred with a decade of pins and annotations. "Report, Master Corvane. And do leave nothing out. I find omissions insult me more than bad news."

"The fugitives reached the shrine at Coldbrook two nights past," Corvane said. "They've taken on provisions. The witch is spending her strength freely — she warded their camp three nights running, which she can't sustain. The troll does the watching. The child — " a flicker, there, in the flat eyes, quickly mastered — "the child is with them. Alive. Walking."

"And Ashe?"

"Ashe is the problem." Corvane came to the map, and his blunt finger came down on the northern passes. "He's not running like a man running. He's running like a man who knows the pursuit. He's using dead ground, moving at the bells, rotating watches. He had us made at Millford and shook us in the fells without losing an hour. High Warden — I trained half the men he's commanding. If you want him taken quietly, I need more than wool-traders. I need the Grey Company, and I need license to be *ungentle*."

Sarel studied the map. The pilgrim road wound north through the high passes, and beyond the passes, marked on no map but engraved on her memory, the Dragonfast crater sat in its ring of shattered mountains like a cup that had once held the world's fire.

"No," she said.

Corvane's jaw tightened. "High Warden — "

"No Grey Company. No ungentleness. Not yet." She traced the road north with one fingertip, and her voice took on the pulpit-cadence again, the one that made Ilsette straighten her spine without knowing why. "Think, Master Corvane. What do I want? I do not want three fugitives in a cell — cells make martyrs of no one, and I will need martyrs. I want them *running north*. I want them to reach the crater. I want them to stand at the place where the fireborn died, with the stolen flame in their hands, and I want witnesses — my witnesses, at distance, with glass and paper — to see them attempt to wake the last dragon." She lifted her finger from the map. "A theft can be argued. A resurrection cannot. When the realm sees beast-speakers raising the ancient horror at Dragonfast, the holy war writes itself, and I will not have spent a decade of patience to have some eager captain strangle it in a ditch at Coldbrook."

"Then what are my orders?" Corvane asked.

"Follow. Record. Do not be seen, and above all do not be *felt*." Sarel turned from the map, and the firelight caught her face and made it, for a moment, almost warm. "Let them run, Master Corvane. Let them believe, for a few more weeks, that they are winning. Hope makes people careless, and love — " the word came off her tongue without a flicker of contempt, which was somehow worse than contempt — "love makes them *predictable*. They will do anything for that child, anything for each other, and anything, in the end, will be exactly what I need them to do."

She dismissed them with a nod and stood alone in the map room as the lamps were lit, and her shadow lay long across the northern passes, across the crater, across the whole cold spine of the world.

It was then that the runner came — a boy out of breath, a dispatch tube in his fist, seal unbroken. Sarel cracked it and read the three lines it contained, and read them again, and stood very still.

The report was from the Millford post. A requisition of horses, grain, and cold-weather gear, signed with Captain Ashe's stolen seal — dated *this morning*. The fugitives were not doubling back. They were not slowing.

They were provisioning for a journey of six hundred leagues.

North — past Dragonfast, past the crater, past every mapped edge of the Warden's reach — to the ice-coast. To the old dragon-roads. To places where, if the moldering archives beneath the flooded level told the truth, the fireborn had once gone to *lay their eggs*.

Sarel Voss read the dispatch a third time, and the swallowed coal in her chest turned over, and for the first time in eleven years the High Warden of the Spire felt the specific, exquisite chill of a plan meeting a variable it had not priced.

"They're not running," she said softly, to the empty map room, to the nine kingdoms, to the dying god in her cellar. "They're *recruiting*."

She rang for the runner. "Wake the Grey Company after all," she said. "And Master Corvane rides with them. License to be ungentle."
