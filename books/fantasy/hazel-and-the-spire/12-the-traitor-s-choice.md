## 12. The Traitor's Choice

The letter came with the morning frost, slid under the door of the waystation where Corin had slept badly on a bench that smelled of old pine and older secrets.

He knew the hand before he broke the seal. He'd seen it a thousand times across a decade of duty rosters and commendation orders — that precise, upright script, each letter standing at attention like a soldier on parade. *Dain Merrick.* The name alone pulled him back ten years, to mud and blood and the screaming wind of the Saltmarsh campaign, when Corin Ashe had been somebody and Dain Merrick had been the brother he'd have died for.

The wax was Warden-grey. Of course it was.

Corin sat down slowly on the bench, the letter unopened in his hands, and listened to the waystation breathe around him. Somewhere above, in the loft, Pip was still asleep — the child had come home to them three nights ago, thin and silent and clutching a bruised wrist, delivered by a smuggler who claimed to know nothing except that "a man with honest eyes" had paid for the girl's release. Corin knew his own eyes better than that. He knew what they'd cost him: every favor he'd banked in fifteen years of service, called in all at once, spent like a drunkard's wages to buy one small life out of Voss's cells.

It had been worth it. He kept telling himself it had been worth it. Some mornings he even believed it.

He broke the seal.

*Ashe —*

*I'll keep this short, because I know how you hate a long letter and because I don't know whose eyes fall on these things anymore.*

*There's a path back for you. I've argued for it. Others have too — men who remember Saltmarsh, who remember what you were before you threw it away. Deliver the beast-speaker to the High Warden's justice and your badge is returned. Full reinstatement. Your name cleared of the granary business. A captaincy again, if you want it, and gods know the realm needs honest captains now more than ever.*

*You know where she is. You've always known. Don't insult us both by pretending otherwise.*

*Come to Thornfell within the fortnight. Bring her or bring your answer.*

*Your friend, if you're still that,*
*D.M.*

Corin read it twice. Then a third time, because his hands had begun to shake and he wanted to be sure the shaking was rage and not something weaker.

Outside, the frost was burning off the hedgerows in slow silver wisps. Marisol's voice drifted from behind the stable, low and rhythmic — she was teaching Pip the hedge-cantrips, the little ones, the ones that warmed water and mended cloth and made moths dance. Brack's deep humming underlay it like a bass note, the trollish stonemason repairing the waystation's sagging porch as though the world weren't coming apart at its seams. And somewhere north, past the passes, Xanthe Vane was walking toward a crater full of dead dragons with a vial of stolen fire and a plan that would either save everything or end it.

And Dain Merrick wanted him to put a rope around her neck and call it honor.

* * *

They'd met at seventeen, Corin and Dain, two farm boys washed into the Wardens by the same lean harvest year. Dain had been all elbows and laughter then, quick to fight and quicker to forgive, the kind of soldier who gave away his rations when a younger recruit went hungry. It was Dain who'd dragged Corin out of the flood at Saltmarsh when the causeway collapsed — Dain who'd held his head above the black water for a quarter mile of current, laughing the whole time, saying, *Not like this, Ashe, not like this, you great useless ox, swim, damn you.*

Corin had owed him his life since before either of them could grow a proper beard. He'd never once thought about what that debt might someday cost.

He walked out into the yard. The morning light caught the mountains to the north and turned them gold and merciless. Marisol looked up as he passed, and her eyes — those knowing, unhurried eyes — flicked from his face to the letter in his hand and back again. She said nothing. Hedge-witches, he was learning, saw more than they said.

Pip did not see anything at all, being fully occupied with making a moth the size of a sparrow loop-the-loop over Brack's head while the big man pretended, very badly, to be annoyed.

"Captain." Marisol's voice stopped him at the gate. Not *Corin*. She still used the title sometimes, and he'd never decided whether it was respect or irony. "Where are you walking?"

"Nowhere."

"Mm." She came closer, moth-light still clinging faintly to her sleeves. "Letters that make a man walk nowhere are usually letters that deserve an answer given carefully. Sit with it a while. Answers written in frost melt wrong."

"I know what the letter says, Marisol."

"I expect you do." Her gaze was steady and without judgment, which somehow made it worse. "I expect it offers you back everything you lost, in exchange for one small thing. They always do. Power is a miserly merchant — it never buys anything large. Only hearts, and only cheap."

He didn't answer. There wasn't an answer that wasn't already written in his bones.

"Xanthe will be back through here in four days," Marisol said quietly. "She's coming south for Brack's sake — his wounds festered worse than he let on, and she won't leave him behind again. So whatever you decide, decide it before then. Decide it *cleanly*, Captain. Half-decisions kill faster than knives."

She turned back to the child and the moths, and Corin stood at the gate a long moment, watching the mountains, feeling the letter weigh more than any armor he'd ever worn.

* * *

He wrote his reply that night, by candlelight, in the waystation's cold back room, and it took him six attempts.

The first attempt was anger — three lines of it, blunt and unforgivable, the kind of letter a man regrets by dawn. He burned it. The second was pleading: *Dain, listen to me, the wards were fed by dragon-fire, the High Warden stole it herself, the whole war is a lie built to crown her—* He burned that one too, because he knew exactly how it would read to a man still inside the grey walls. Treason dressed up as revelation. Madness dressed up as truth. He'd have read it the same way, a month ago.

The third attempt he actually finished. It was careful. It laid out everything — the fox, the undercrofts, Ysolde dying in the dark beneath the Spire, the vial, Voss's vaults, the interrogation of a child no older than nine. Evidence, argument, appeal after appeal to the man Dain had been. *Come and see,* it ended. *Come north and look with your own eyes, brother, and if I'm wrong, arrest me yourself.*

He read it over by candlelight and understood, with a sick settling in his stomach, that it was the most dangerous thing he could possibly send. Because if there was even a chance Dain believed it — even a spark — then Corin would be signing his oldest friend's death warrant alongside his own. Voss did not forgive doubt. Doubt, in the Spire, was a disease, and they burned the infected.

He fed the third letter to the candle flame and watched it curl and go to ash.

The fourth attempt was blank. He sat looking at the empty page for the better part of an hour, listening to the house settle, thinking about what love actually required of a man. He had spent his whole life believing duty was the highest form of it — duty to the realm, to the order, to the brothers who'd bled beside him. Duty as love wearing armor. But duty had sent Pip into a cell. Duty had watched refugees starve behind curfew lines while the Wardens' granaries stayed full. Duty had fed a dragon's heart, drop by drop, into a woman's ambition, and called the theft *protection of the realm*.

And Xanthe — Xanthe, who talked to foxes and feared her own gift and carried fire she could so easily use and refused, refused, to become the thing that fire wanted —

What did duty ask of him there? Nothing. Duty had no claim on her at all. Only love did. Love, which had no rank, no oath, no seal of grey wax. Love, which asked simply: *will you stand beside her, yes or no.*

He picked up the pen.

*Dain. My answer is no.*

*Not the way you think — not pride, not stubbornness. I'm telling you plainly: the woman you want me to deliver is innocent of what they say, and guilty only of telling the truth. I've seen the thing beneath the Spire. I've heard it breathing. If you deliver her, you deliver the last dragon's death and a holy war on top of it, and they'll pin a medal on you for both.*

*I won't come to Thornfell. I won't bring her. And I won't pretend, anymore, that I can serve two masters.*

*You saved my life at Saltmarsh and I have never forgotten it and I never will. This is me paying it back the only way left to me: by warning you. Get clear of Voss's shadow. Whatever she's building, whatever she's promised you, it ends in ash — hers or everyone else's, I've stopped caring which. When it comes apart, and it will, be somewhere she can't reach you.*

*I'm sorry, brother. For all of it. For choosing wrong when we were young and right too late.*

*Ashe*

He read it once. Then he did the thing that made his hands go cold and steady at the same time: he signed it with his full name and rank — *Corin Ashe, Captain, Warden Order (dismissed)* — because a warning sent anonymously protected no one, and a bridge worth burning deserved to burn with his name on it.

* * *

The problem was the delivery.

A letter to a serving Warden officer could not travel by common post; Voss read the posts. It needed a courier Corin trusted absolutely, and his list of those had grown tragically short. In the end it came down to one name: Tam Osgood, a retired quartermaster with a bad hip and a good heart who ran a livery in the river town of Millbrook and owed Corin for looking the other way, years ago, over some smuggling that had kept Osgood's daughter fed through a famine winter. Tam didn't ask questions. That was the arrangement, and it had held for eleven years.

Corin rode for Millbrook alone, telling the others he had "business," and Marisol's eyes followed him out of the yard with a weight he chose not to examine.

Millbrook was quiet in the way of places trying hard to stay unnoticed. Curfew notices papered the tavern wall — new ones, Voss's grey seal on every sheet, offering rewards for information regarding "beast-speaker agitators." Someone had scrawled *LIARS* across one in charcoal and someone else had scrubbed at it, half-heartedly, leaving the word legible underneath like a bruise.

Tam Osgood met him in the stable's back aisle, between two plow horses, and his face when he saw Corin was not the face of a man greeting an old friend. It was the face of a man greeting a corpse.

"Gods' teeth, boy." The old quartermaster gripped his forearm hard enough to hurt. "Do you know what you're worth? Dead or alive, and the *alive* price is higher. There are Warden riders on the north road twice a day now. Twice a *day*. What in the hells are you doing riding in here?"

"Sending a letter."

"To who?"

"A dead man, probably. Or a living one, if there's any mercy in it." Corin pressed the sealed packet into Tam's weathered hands. "Thornfell garrison. Captain Dain Merrick. By your own hand, Tam, nobody else's. Can you do it?"

Tam turned the packet over, weighing it as he weighed everything, and Corin watched the old man arrive at the answer before he spoke it. "This is the one that burns your boats, isn't it. Whatever's in here — once it's in Merrick's hands, there's no road back for you."

"There hasn't been a road back since the granary."

"No." Tam tucked the letter inside his coat, against his chest, with the tenderness men of his generation reserved for things that mattered. "No, I suppose there hasn't." He looked up, and his eyes were wet and furious. "Eleven years I've wondered if you'd ever come needing something, boy, and I'll tell you what I told my girl: debts of bread don't rot. Go on, then. Get out of Millbrook before sundown. And Corin—" His voice dropped. "Whatever you're running with, whoever she is — you run *well*, you hear me? You run like a man who means to live."

* * *

He meant to ride straight back. Instead, at the crossroads above the river, he reined in and sat his horse for a long time, looking south toward Thornfell.

He could almost see it, in his mind's eye: the garrison walls, the drill yard, the long grey barracks where he'd slept for fifteen years. Dain would get the letter tomorrow or the next day. Dain would read it standing at his window the way he read everything, jaw tight, and then — and then Corin genuinely did not know. That was the terrible arithmetic of it. He had spent his last coin on a wager he couldn't calculate: that somewhere inside the loyal officer there still lived the boy from the flood, the one who gave away his rations, the one who laughed in black water and refused to let go.

If Corin was wrong, Dain would carry the letter to Voss, and Voss would read it, and everything in it — the dragon, the undercrofts, the route north — would become a hunting map. He had just handed his enemies a confession. He had just drawn a circle around every person he loved and labeled it in his own handwriting.

If he was right, Dain would burn the letter, and maybe — *maybe* — start asking the questions that loyal men are not permitted to ask.

Either way, the bridge was ash. Either way, Corin Ashe was done being a Warden in anything but memory. He found, examining himself honestly, that he felt lighter. Frightened, yes — frightened down to the marrow — but lighter, the way a man feels setting down a load he'd forgotten he was carrying. The badge had been heavy. He'd simply worn it so long he'd mistaken the weight for a spine.

He turned his horse north.

* * *

The others were waiting when he rode into the yard at dusk, and he knew before anyone spoke that something had changed. Brack stood at the gate instead of the porch, arms crossed, watching the road. Marisol had every lamp in the waystation lit, which she never did — light like that was a beacon, a waste, a risk. And Pip sat on the fence rail, knees drawn up, and the child's face wore an expression Corin had learned to dread in fifteen years of command: the look of someone carrying news too big for their body.

"You sent it, then," Marisol said. Not a question.

"This morning. Tam carries it." He dismounted stiffly. "Why are the lamps lit? What's happened?"

"Riders," Brack rumbled. His voice, always soft for such a mountain of a man, was softer still now, the way it went when he was afraid for someone. "Warden riders, an hour past. Didn't stop — just passed on the high road, heading north. Six of them. Grey cloaks." He paused. "Voss's elite wear grey cloaks, ja? Like the ones what took the little one."

Corin felt the evening go cold around him. "North. Toward the crater."

"Toward the crater," Marisol agreed quietly. "They're not searching anymore, Captain. They know where she's going. Which means someone told them." Her eyes rested on him, gentle and terrible. "Or someone *will* tell them. Soon."

The letter. His letter, in Tam's coat, riding toward Thornfell even now — and if Dain faltered, if Dain so much as hesitated at his window with the seal broken in his hands, then every word of warning Corin had written would become a blade pointed at Xanthe's back. He had tried to save his brother and might have doomed them all in the same stroke, and there was nothing — *nothing* — he could do now but ride.

"We leave tonight," he said. "All of us. Brack, can you travel?"

Brack rolled his massive shoulders, wincing at something in the wound beneath the bandages, and grinned the gap-toothed grin that had first made Corin trust him. "I can travel angry. Angry travels fast."

"Pip—"

"I'm ready." The child jumped down from the rail, chin up, and Corin saw that under the fear there was steel now, tempered in a Warden cell, and it broke his heart and steadied him all at once. "Let's go get her before they do."

Marisol snuffed the lamps one by one, plunging the yard into starless dark — the stars still hadn't returned, and Corin had stopped expecting them — and as they saddled in silence, he looked north one last time, toward the mountains where a woman who spoke with beasts was walking into a trap he might have built.

"Hold on, Xanthe," he murmured, to no one, to the dark, to whatever gods presided over traitors and fools. "We're coming. Whatever it costs."

Behind them, far to the south, a single signal fire bloomed on a Thornfell watchtower — grey smoke rising straight into the starless sky, a message climbing toward the Spire.

Someone had opened a letter.
