## 13. Where Dragons Once Slept

The road forgot itself a mile before the crater did.

Xanthe felt it first in her boots — the packed earth of the old trade way crumbling into scree, then into nothing, as though the world had simply decided to stop pretending there had ever been a path here. Then the trees changed. Pines gave way to birch, birch to blackened snags that had stood dead for a hundred years and never fallen, their limbs raised like the charred fingers of drowned men reaching for a surface they would never touch.

And then the land opened its mouth.

The crater of Dragonfast spread before them like a wound the sky had once made in the earth — three miles rim to rim, ringed by cliffs of fused glass-green stone where the heat of some ancient conflagration had melted rock into weeping shapes. At its center, half-drowned in a lake of impossible clarity, lay the bones.

Even from the rim, even at this distance, Xanthe could see them: ribs arched like the rafters of a cathedral built by something that had never needed shelter, vertebrae strung along the lakebed like pearls too large for any mortal throat. A skull the size of a granary rested on its side, one empty eye socket aimed forever at the northern sky. Moss had claimed it now, softening the horror into something almost gentle — a green velvet laid over the past, the way grief is eventually softened by the years that follow it.

The animals had told her about this place long before she ever saw it. *Do not go where the fire slept,* the old badger beneath Thornfell's walls had warned her, years ago, when she was young enough to think warnings were suggestions. *The ground there remembers being angry.* She hadn't understood then. She understood now. Standing at the rim, she could feel it through her gift like a hand pressed flat against her sternum — not silence, exactly, but a held breath. The whole crater was holding its breath. Had been holding it for a century.

"Gods," Marisol said beside her, squinting against the wind that came up off the glass cliffs and smelled faintly of sulfur and older things. "It's beautiful. It's terrible. I can't decide which."

"Both," Xanthe said. "That's rather the point of it."

Pip scrambled up onto a shelf of rock to get a better look, and Brack caught the child by the collar before the wind could make its opinion known. Even the trollish stonemason — who had hauled blocks heavier than Pip's entire body up scaffolding for thirty years without so much as a grunt — went quiet when he saw the bones. He took off his hat. Held it against his chest. Xanthe had seen him do that exactly twice before: once at his mother's grave, and once when they'd passed the burned-out farmstead outside Havenhollow. It was how Brack prayed, or whatever Brack did instead of praying. He honored largeness. And nothing in the world was larger than what lay below them.

Corin stood apart, the way he always did when something mattered, arms crossed, jaw set, watching the crater the way a man watches a door he knows will open eventually whether he knocks or not.

"You've been here before," Xanthe said to him.

He didn't answer right away. The wind pulled at his cloak, at the gray in his hair that hadn't been there two months ago, when all of this began.

"Once," he said finally. "As a recruit. They brought us up here on the anniversary of the Fall. Made us stand at the rim while an old Warden — Halvard, his name was, dead twenty years now — told us about the last day of the war." His voice had gone flat, recitation-flat, the voice of a man reciting something he'd been forced to memorize and never permitted to feel. "How the dragons broke here. How the Wardens drove them into the crater and sealed the wards above them, and how the realm called it peace." He paused. "Halvard called it mercy. We all repeated it back to him. There was a test afterward."

"And you believed it?"

"I was nineteen," Corin said. "I believed everything. That was the whole problem with me." He looked at her then, and there was something raw under the flatness, something that had been waiting a long time for permission. "I don't believe it anymore. But I'll tell you what I remember, Xanthe, because you should hear it from someone who stood here wearing the uniform. When Halvard finished speaking, one of the recruits — a girl from the coast, Brenna was her name — asked him why, if it was mercy, the dragons had screamed for three days before the wards closed. He said she was too young to understand necessity." Corin's hands tightened on his own forearms. "She washed out that winter. Necessity. I've carried that word like a stone in my boot for twenty-five years, and I only just now figured out whose boot it was put in."

Xanthe turned back to the crater. Below them, the lake lay still as poured mercury, and the bones slept beneath it, and somewhere far to the south — days away, behind them, beneath the Spire — Ysolde lay in her cell of roots and stone with half a heart, dreaming whatever dragons dream when they have been dying slowly for a hundred years.

And in the iron-and-glass vial against Xanthe's breastbone, wrapped in wool and warded with every hedge-charm Marisol knew, the other half of that heart burned on. Patient. Dimming. Waiting to be either returned or lost.

* * *

They made camp on the rim itself, because descending felt like a decision none of them were ready to make, and because Marisol said — after walking the perimeter twice, tasting the air, muttering to her charms — that the crater's edge was neutral ground. Old treaty-ground. Whatever agreements had ended the war had been signed here, in sight of the bones, so that no party could later claim they hadn't understood the cost.

"The flame wants down there," Marisol said, sitting cross-legged by the fire with her palms up, reading it the way she read weather and moods and the small tremors in people's voices that meant more than their words. "I can feel it pulling. Like a dog at the end of a rope. Every hour we sit here it pulls harder."

"Of course it does," Xanthe said. "It's homesick."

"It's not homesick, love. It's hungry. Homesick doesn't get stronger the longer you wait." Marisol fed a twist of dried sage to the fire and watched the smoke curl north, which was wrong — smoke should have curled up, but here it bent sideways, drawn by currents no living wind made. "I need you to understand what returning it would mean. Not morally. Physically. I've been carrying this thing's weight in my own reserves since the vault, burning myself hollow to keep it shielded, and I'm telling you: that vial isn't a keepsake. It's a fuse. Half a dragon's heart, compressed into a space smaller than your fist, and the only thing keeping it from detonating back to full size is the fact that it has nowhere to go. Give it a body — give it *her* body — and it goes home, and it grows, and it becomes what it was."

"What it was," Brack rumbled from where he sat sharpening his maul, "was a weapon."

"What it *is*," Xanthe said, "is half of a dying woman's heart."

Brack didn't argue. He never argued. He just set the whetstone down and looked at her with those deep-set eyes that had watched mountains get quarried and friends get buried, and waited for her to say the next thing, because Brack had learned long ago that the next thing was always harder than the thing before it.

Pip broke the silence, because Pip always broke the silence, because silence was the one enemy the child had never learned to stop fighting.

"When we get her out," Pip said, "will she be big again? Like the bones?"

Nobody answered. Pip looked around the fire, reading faces the way Pip read everything — fast, sharp, missing nothing. "You're all doing the face. The face where you know something bad and you're deciding who tells me." Pip pointed at Marisol. "Marisol always tells me. Marisol thinks lying to children makes them stupid."

Marisol winced. "Thanks, Pip."

"So tell me," Pip said, folding skinny arms. "If we give the fire back, does the dragon come out of the Spire and start burning things? Yes or no."

"Yes," Marisol said quietly. "Probably. Almost certainly. Not because she's wicked — because she's *wounded*. You take half a creature's heart away for a hundred years and then hand it back, you don't get gratitude, Pip. You get a creature remembering everything that was done to it, all at once, with the full strength of a heart that hasn't beaten properly since before your grandmother was born." She looked at Xanthe. "You've felt it. Tell them."

Xanthe had felt it. That was the worst part. On the road north, on the cold nights, she had taken the vial out and held it in both hands — a smuggler's habit, checking her cargo — and through her gift the flame had spoken to her. Not in words. In pressure. In images that arrived like blows: the Spire's undercroft, the chains, the century of dark. The taste of ash. The specific, catalogued memory of every Warden who had ever carried a branding iron down those stairs. And underneath all of it, vast and slow and patient as geology, a single feeling that Xanthe had no better name for than *owed*. The flame believed it was owed. Blood, ruin, a reckoning measured in cities.

She had told herself it was just the flame's fear. Just pain talking. Pain talked like that; she'd heard it from foxes and horses and once, terribly, from a drowning man. Pain demanded payment from whoever happened to be nearest.

But the flame wasn't in pain anymore, exactly. It was *whole*, or nearly. It was power with nowhere to go, and power with nowhere to go did not stay gentle.

"I've felt it," she said now, to the fire, to all of them. "And I won't lie to you, Pip, because you're right, lying to children makes them stupid and lying to anyone makes me worse. If we return the flame to Ysolde, she rises. She comes out of that mountain whole for the first time in a hundred years, and the first thing a whole heart does is finish what it started." Her voice cracked on the last word and she let it. "There are people in the Spire who deserve it. Voss deserves it. But there are ten thousand people inside those walls who don't — bakers and lamplighters and children Pip's age who've never done anything but live under a government that lied to them. And the flame doesn't sort. I've held it. It doesn't sort."

Pip hugged both knees to chest and stared into the fire. "So we don't give it back."

"If we don't give it back—" Xanthe stopped. Swallowed. Started again. "If we don't give it back, it dies. And when it dies, magic dies. All of it. Every hedge-charm, every beast-bond, every ward, every healing, every small miracle that ordinary people have leaned on for a thousand years without ever knowing its name. Marisol's craft. My gift. The wards that keep the border beasts out of the villages — except they won't need to keep anything out anymore, because the beasts will lose their wildness too, or the land will lose its *aliveness*, or both, and I honestly don't know which is worse." She laughed, and it came out jagged. "I've spent six weeks running toward a choice, and now that I'm standing in front of it, it turns out there's no door. There's just a wall with two kinds of fire painted on it."

* * *

Corin walked the rim alone that evening, and Xanthe followed him, because she had learned that when Corin Ashe walked alone, he was not avoiding company. He was making room for a thought too heavy to carry in front of witnesses.

The glass cliffs sang in the wind — a low, keening harmonic, the sound of air moving over surfaces that had been liquid once and remembered flowing. Below them the lake had gone silver-black with the coming dusk, and the great skull's eye socket filled with the first stars, which had returned two nights ago, faint and trembling, as if the sky itself were testing whether it was safe to come back.

"They used to fly," Corin said, without looking at her. "Before Dragonfast. My grandmother saw one, when she was a girl. Over the Saltmarshes. She said the shadow of it crossed the whole estuary, and everyone ran indoors, and her father stood in the doorway and cried — not from fear. From joy. He'd waited his whole life to see one." He turned to face her. "I think about that man sometimes. Standing in his doorway, crying with happiness, watching a shadow. He didn't know he was looking at the last generation. He didn't know the sky was already closing. Nobody told him, because telling him would have meant admitting what was being done."

"Do you think Ysolde remembers flying?" Xanthe asked.

"I think she remembers nothing else." Corin's voice dropped. "A century in the dark, half a heart, and the one memory that would keep a creature like that sane is the memory of open sky. And they took the sky too. That's what the Spire is, Xanthe. It's not a prison. Prisons hold bodies. The Spire holds the *sky* — hers, and ours, if Voss gets what she wants." He was quiet a moment. "When I was a captain, I thought duty was the highest thing a person could serve. I've been unlearning that since Greytooth Pass. But standing here, on the ground where my order committed the crime it was founded to deny — I keep thinking that maybe duty was never the point. Maybe duty was just the shape obedience takes when it wants to feel like courage."

Xanthe reached out and took his hand. He let her. His fingers were cold from the rim-wind and rough from a lifetime of swordwork, and they closed around hers like a man gripping a rope over a drop.

"I keep trying to find the good path," she said. "That's what I do. That's what I've always done — read the signs, ask the animals, find the trail that doesn't cost anyone. And there isn't one. Do you understand? There *isn't one*. Every path leads through fire. Return the flame and Ysolde burns the realm free and burns the innocent with it. Keep the flame and watch it gutter, and take every wonder in the world down into the dark with it. Give it to myself and become the thing I've spent my whole life running from — a vessel of rage wearing a kind face." She pressed his hand between both of hers. "I stood at the rim today and I looked at those bones and I thought: a hundred years ago, someone stood exactly here and faced exactly this, and they chose the wall instead of the door, and we've all been living in the rubble of their choice ever since. And the truly unbearable thing — the thing that's been sitting on my chest since dawn — is that whoever it was probably chose it *kindly*. Probably chose it grieving. Probably stood here with someone's hand in theirs and said *there has to be another way* and then couldn't find it, and picked the least terrible fire, and called it peace."

Corin was silent for a long time. The glass cliffs sang. Somewhere below, in the drowned dark, the bones kept their counsel.

"Then maybe," he said at last, "the question isn't which fire to walk through. Maybe the question is who walks through it with you." He turned her hand over, traced the old smuggling scar across her palm with one thumb. "You told me once that your gift isn't talking to beasts. That it's listening. That the difference matters. So listen now — not to the flame, not to the crater, not to the ghosts. Listen to the five people sitting around that fire behind us. A stonemason who bled at the pass for strangers. A witch who burned her own magic hollow to buy us thirty seconds. A child who wouldn't break in a Warden cell. And a disgraced captain who chose you over the only life he'd ever known, and hasn't regretted it for one single morning." His voice roughened. "I don't know what the third path looks like, Xanthe. I genuinely don't. But I know who's going to be standing on it when you find it, and I know it won't be found by one woman alone at the rim of a crater trying to out-think a hundred years of grief."

Behind them, down at the camp, the fire had settled to coals, and Marisol was teaching Pip a charm against nightmares, and Brack was humming — tunelessly, enormously, the way mountains might hum if mountains could — and the whole small fragile architecture of them glowed against the dark like a single coal refusing the night.

Xanthe looked at it for a long moment. Then back at the bones. Then at it again.

*Every path leads through fire,* she thought. *But fire spreads. Fire has always spread. That's the whole nature of it.*

She didn't say it aloud yet. It wasn't a plan yet. It was barely a seedling, barely a thread of warmth in the cold machinery of her dread. But she held it the way she held the vial — carefully, close to the bone, shielding it from the wind.

* * *

They descended at first light.

Not all the way — Marisol insisted on wards first, and Brack wanted to test the crater floor's stability, and Corin wanted the high ground covered before any of them set foot on ground that had been angry for a century. But Xanthe went down ahead of them, alone, as far as the first terrace of fused glass, where the birch snags gave way to bare stone and the lake came close enough to touch.

She knelt at the water's edge. Behind her, faintly, she could hear her family breaking camp — Brack's low rumble, Pip's laughter, the clink of Marisol's charm-bottles. In her breastbone, the vial pulsed like a second heartbeat, faster now, insistent, straining south toward the bones and east toward the Spire and everywhere at once, a compass needle with too many norths.

The lake lay utterly still. In its mirror she could see the whole sky, the returned stars fading into dawn, and the great skull rising from the shallows like an island, and her own small face floating among all of it — a smuggler's face, tired, lined at the corners now in ways it hadn't been two months ago.

She spoke to the bones. Quietly, in the way she spoke to all things, which was less like speech and more like opening a door and leaving it open.

"I don't know yet," she told them. "I want to be honest with you, because everyone else who ever stood above you lied. I don't know what I'm going to do. I have half a heart in a bottle and a whole war in my chest and five people behind me who trusted me to find a way through, and I am asking you — you who paid the price of the last choice — to tell me if you know something I don't."

The lake did not answer. The glass cliffs sang. The wind moved east, toward the Spire, and carried with it, very faintly, very far away, a smell that did not belong to any fire she had ever known.

Smoke.

Fresh smoke, on a wind that had crossed forty miles of empty highland to reach her — which meant it had not drifted. It had been *sent*. Or it marked something moving toward them through the passes behind, something that burned as it traveled.

Xanthe stood slowly, her hand pressed to the vial, and turned to look north along the rim — where the dawn light was just beginning to pick out, tiny and distant and unmistakable, a line of mounted figures cresting the far ridge.

Wardens. Too many to count. Moving fast.

"Pip!" she shouted, already running. "Wake the camp! *Wake the camp!*"
