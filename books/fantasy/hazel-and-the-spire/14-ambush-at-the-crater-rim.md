## 14. Ambush at the Crater Rim

Brack had been carving stone since before most of these Wardens were born, and he knew a thing or two about how things broke.

Stone broke honestly. You struck it wrong and it told you so — a crack, a chip, a grumble of protest along some fault line you'd failed to read. Stone never lied to you. It never crept up quiet. When a wall was going to fall, it groaned first, sighed first, gave you time to stand clear or put your shoulder to it. Brack had spent sixty years listening to stone talk, and in that time he had learned the second great truth of his trade: that everything strong is strong for a reason, and every reason can be found and broken.

People were just stone that moved.

He crouched at the mouth of the pass with his hammer across his knees and watched the dawn pick out the line of riders on the far ridge, and he did the arithmetic he had been doing all night without letting himself finish it. Forty riders. Maybe fifty. Elite — you could tell by how they sat their horses, by the grey-and-silver of their cloaks, by the way they came down the scree in a column without a single loose stone clattering. Voss's own. The kind of soldiers who didn't make noise because noise was a confession.

Behind him, the camp was waking in a storm of small sounds: Pip's voice cracking on a shout, Marisol's low curses as she kicked dirt over the fire, Xanthe's commands sharp as chisel-taps. And under all of it, quieter than any of them, Corin's voice saying what needed saying in the fewest words.

They could not outrun fifty mounted Wardens through broken highland carrying a wounded fox, a half-trained hedge-witch running on fumes, and a vial of dying dragon-fire.

Brack had known that since midnight, when the wind brought the smoke. He had said nothing, because saying it early would only have cost the others sleep, and sleep was going to be scarce enough soon. Instead he had spent the dark hours doing what he always did when something was going to fall: reading the fault lines.

The pass was called the Shepherd's Throat, though no shepherd had used it in living memory. It was a slot between two shoulders of the crater rim, maybe thirty feet wide at the mouth and narrowing to ten where the walls pinched together a hundred yards in. On either side rose slopes of shattered basalt — old lava, old grief, the mountain's own scar tissue from the day the dragons died. Loose rock everywhere. Unstable everywhere. A mason's nightmare and a mason's gift, depending on which side of the falling you stood.

Fifty riders could not come through that throat abreast. They would have to come four at a time, five at a time, funneling themselves into the narrow like water into a crack. And water into a crack, given time, will split anything.

Unless someone stands in the crack.

* * *

"You're not hearing me," Corin said, for the third time. He had Brack by the forearm now, which for Corin was close to shouting. "They're not here to capture us, Brack. Voss sent her elite. That means kill orders. You stand in that gap alone and they will *kill* you."

"Probably," Brack agreed.

Corin stared at him. In the grey light the captain looked older than his years, hollowed out around the eyes, and Brack thought — not unkindly — that the man had been carrying his duty like a millstone for so long that he'd forgotten what it weighed until he'd finally set it down, and now he kept trying to pick it back up out of habit.

"That's your whole answer? *Probably?*"

"It's an honest answer." Brack rose. It took a while; it always took a while, like watching a cliff decide to be a cliff. When he was fully upright he stood a head taller than Corin and wide enough that the captain had to step back to keep talking to him. "You want me to lie to you instead? Say I'll dance through it untouched? I'm a stonemason, Captain. I don't do decorative work."

"I can't let you—"

"You can't *stop* me, is what you mean, and we both know it, and I'd thank you not to waste my last hour arguing about whose fault it is." Brack said it gently, the way he said most things, and saw the words land anyway, the way heavy things land. He turned and looked down the slope at the others.

Pip was trying to saddle the pack-mule one-handed and arguing with it, which was a losing fight on both fronts. Marisol sat cross-legged with her palms flat on the earth, drawing up what was left of her reserves, and even from here Brack could see the cost of it in her face — she'd burned herself hollow in the vault escape and hadn't refilled since, and the well she was drinking from now was down to mud. Xanthe stood apart, hand pressed over her chest where the vial hung, staring north at the riders with an expression Brack recognized from quarries: the look of someone watching a wall lean and knowing there wasn't enough timber in the world to shore it.

And the fox lay in the cart on a bed of blankets, breathing in shallow stitches, its ruined flank wrapped in Marisol's poultices. Still alive. Still stubborn. Brack had carried it three miles two nights ago when it couldn't walk, and it had growled at him the entire way, weak as a kitten, fierce as ever, and he had liked it enormously for that.

A month ago, he had known none of these people.

That was the part Corin didn't understand, standing there grinding his teeth. Brack had lived sixty years among strangers. Trolls weren't welcome in most towns — too big, too grey, too many old stories about what trollish hands had done in the Troll Wars, never mind that Brack's grandfather had died building walls *against* his own kin because somebody had to hold the line and the line didn't care who held it. Brack had spent his life being hired for his arms and dismissed at the gate after. He had built cathedrals whose doors wouldn't admit him. He had carved saints into lintels above taverns that wouldn't serve him a drink.

Then a pickpocket child had climbed onto his shoulder in Greytooth Pass like it was a hill made for her, and a hedge-witch had stitched his split knuckles without asking what he was, and a smuggler with fire in her eyes had looked at him — not past him, not through him, *at* him — and said, *we need someone who knows how things break.*

We need you. Nobody had said that to Brack Dravenmoor's face in six decades. He had decided, somewhere in the second week of knowing them, that he would bleed for these people if it came to it, and he had told no one, because it wasn't a decision that required announcement. It was mortar work. Quiet. Structural.

"They'll need twenty minutes to get the cart through the narrows and down the far side," Brack said. "The goat track's bad. Give me those twenty minutes."

"And then?"

"Then I'll be along. Slowly. In the manner of stones."

"*Brack.*"

"Captain." He caught Corin's shoulder, careful, the way he handled anything smaller than himself, which was nearly everything. "You've spent your whole life believing duty means standing where you're told. I've spent mine learning that sometimes it means standing where nobody asked. Let me have this one. It's the only kind of monument I'll ever get."

Corin's jaw worked. For a moment Brack thought the man might actually argue — he was stubborn, their captain, stubborn as bedrock in his own guilty way — but then Pip came pelting up the slope with the mule's lead rope and news in her voice like sparks off a grinder.

"They're splitting! Half of 'em are cutting west along the rim — they're gonna try to get behind us!"

Brack felt it land in his chest like a keystone dropping into place. Of course. Voss's elite wouldn't blunder into a defile head-on if they could flank it. Which meant the twenty minutes had just become fifteen, and the plan had just become simpler: not to hold the pass forever, but to make the pass so expensive that the flanking party became the main effort, and the flanking party had to go on foot, and foot-travel through the western scree was slow, slow, slow.

"Fifteen minutes," Brack said. "Get them moving."

* * *

He chose his ground the way he chose all his ground: honestly.

Not at the mouth of the throat, where he'd be visible from the ridge and they'd simply shoot him full of arrows from horseback. Not deep in the narrows, where he'd have no room to swing. Halfway in, where the walls pinched to twelve feet and a fallen slab — one he'd noticed on the way up, a basalt block the size of a haywain, wedged against the eastern wall — gave him cover at his back and a choke point at his front. One man's width of approach. Any Warden who wanted to reach him had to come to him one at a time, on footing Brack had spent the last hour improving.

Improving was perhaps a generous word for what he'd done. He had spent that hour pulling stones loose. Carefully. Lovingly, almost. Reading each one's fault lines, finding the ones that wanted to fall, and leaving them balanced on the edge of wanting. The slope above the narrows was now rigged like a harp with cut strings — one good blow and forty tons of basalt would come down on the first rank of riders entering the throat. He couldn't set it off remotely; he wasn't a witch. But he could set it off with his hammer, and his hammer was going to be exactly where the first rank arrived.

The rest — the fighting itself — he tried not to think about too precisely. Brack was not a violent soul. He had swung his hammer at stone for sixty years and at people only twice, both times with regret that still sat in him like a splinter. But he had also watched Marisol burn herself empty buying the others three seconds in a vault corridor, and he had watched Pip give up her supper to the fox every night without being asked, and he understood something now that he hadn't at fifty: gentleness isn't the absence of force. Gentleness is force that has decided what it's for.

His hammer was for this.

He heard them before he saw them — hooves on scree, then the jingle of tack, then voices, clipped and disciplined, ordering the dismount. He put his palm flat against the cold basalt beside him, the way he did before every difficult cut, and said the mason's prayer his grandfather taught him, the one that wasn't really a prayer so much as a promise: *Stand true. Stand true. What you hold, hold.*

The first rider came around the bend at a trot, sword already drawn, and Brack stepped out of the shadow of the slab and hit the horse.

Not the rider — the horse, a flat-handed shove along its neck that turned it sideways into the wall, and the rider went off over the opposite shoulder into the rocks with a sound like a dropped sack. Brack was already moving, already swinging, and the second rider's blade rang off his hammer-head and the counterblow took the man's shield and most of the arm holding it and folded him backward over his saddlebow. The third and fourth reined up hard, shouting, and above them Brack heard the slope begin to whisper — the beautiful, terrible whisper of forty tons of stone remembering gravity.

He swung at the wedge-stone.

The mountain came down.

It was not like anything he had ever built or broken. It was louder than thunder and more personal than a landslide had any right to be — he felt it in his teeth, in the roots of him, the whole slope exhaling at once, and the first four riders vanished under it, horses and men and steel and dust, and the throat of the pass filled end to end with roaring white cloud and the deep settling groan of a mountain rearranging its grief.

When the dust thinned, the narrows were sealed. Ten feet of tumbled basalt, sheer on both faces. No rider getting over that today. No rider getting *around* it except on foot, west, through the scree, slow as a funeral.

Fifteen minutes bought. More, maybe.

Brack allowed himself three breaths of something that wasn't quite triumph, and then the arrows came.

* * *

They had crossbows. Of course they had crossbows; Voss's elite traveled with crossbows the way other soldiers traveled with rations. The first bolt took him high in the left shoulder and spun him half around, and the pain was white and total and almost interesting, the way a quarry blast is interesting right up until the debris lands. He got the slab between himself and the second volley and stood there breathing, feeling the bolt grind when he breathed too deep, doing the new arithmetic.

The western flankers would reach the far side of the pass in — call it ten minutes, if they hurried and the scree fought them, and the scree would fight them because Brack had loosened that path too, yesterday, casually, the way a man whittles. Ten minutes. He had to keep the crossbowmen honest for ten minutes so they couldn't mass and ride the rim itself, east, and come down on the cart from above. Every minute they spent shooting at him was a minute they weren't riding.

So he gave them something to shoot at.

He came out from behind the slab roaring — actually roaring, giving them the full trollishness of himself, the thing he had spent sixty years apologizing for — and he charged the nearest cluster of dismounted Wardens and waded in like a flood into a dry creek bed. He didn't try to be graceful. Grace was for people built for it. He was a wall that walked, and walls don't parry, they *endure*, and he took cuts across the forearm, across the thigh, one bright line of fire along his ribs, and he gave back worse, hammer sweeping in short brutal arcs, and men fell away from him the way rubble falls away from a demolition — not bravely, not cowardly, just obeying physics.

Somewhere in the middle of it he heard a horn. Two notes, high and urgent. The signal to break off.

He straightened, swaying, blood sheeting warm down his side, and saw them pulling back — all of them, riders and footmen alike, withdrawing west with the discipline of professionals who had gotten their orders and knew better than to die for pride. And he understood, with the cold clarity that comes when the body starts shutting doors, what the orders must be. *Leave him. He's done. Take the flanking route and catch the cart.* They had decided he wasn't worth the time, and they were right, and the knowledge should have frightened him and instead settled over him like a finished job.

Because the horn meant the others were clear. The horn meant fifteen minutes had been enough.

Brack laughed. It hurt to laugh. He did it anyway.

* * *

He made it two hundred yards toward the goat track before his legs filed their formal complaint.

He sat down against a boulder — lowered himself, rather, with the enormous care of a man placing a final stone — and took stock the way he'd take stock of a damaged wall. Left shoulder: bolt through, deep, bad. Ribs: at least two cracked, probably three. Right thigh: gashed to the bone in one place, weeping steadily. Forearms: shredded. Everything else: bruised past cataloguing. He was bleeding from more places than he could press at once, and the ground under him was getting warm and red, and the sun was fully up now, lying gold across the crater rim where dragons had once slept, and it was, he thought, a fine morning to be alive on, whichever amount of it remained.

He got the shoulder bolt out. That was the worst of it, worse than the cuts — the grinding, the wet resistance, the way his vision went to sparkles at the edges. He bound it with strips torn from his shirt, clumsy, one-handed, using his teeth. Mason's field surgery: stop the leaks, brace the cracks, hope the structure holds till proper hands arrive. Marisol's hands. She'd grumble. She'd fix him anyway.

*If* they came back for him. He hoped they wouldn't. He'd told Xanthe, before the lines closed: *if it goes wrong, you go, and you don't look back, and that's an order from the only person on this mountain bigger than you.* She'd glared at him. She'd promised. Both things could be true.

But he knew found families by now. He knew they came back.

Footsteps on the scree. Slow ones. Deliberate ones. Not the hurry of a rescue.

Brack reached for his hammer and found his fingers wouldn't entirely close around the haft, and so he settled for sitting up straighter, squaring his shoulders against the boulder, arranging himself the way he'd arrange a cornerstone: load-bearing, whatever else failed.

Three figures came around the rocks. Not Wardens' grey — darker, plain, moving with the soft economy of people paid by results. The leader carried a crossbow leveled at Brack's chest and wore the patient expression of a man who had been sent back specifically because someone upstairs wanted a prisoner, and had drawn the short straw.

"Well now," the man said. "Big fellow. You'll fetch a pretty price, you will. High Warden's offering gold for any of your lot breathing."

Brack looked at him for a long moment. Then, slowly, painfully, he smiled — the smile he used for clients who tried to haggle after the contract was signed.

"Son," he said, "you've made the same mistake everyone makes with stone. You've assumed that because it's cracked, it's finished." His hand found the haft after all, closing finger by finger, joint by joint, the way a wall closes around a keystone. "Cracked stone under load does one thing."

The crossbowman's eyes flicked down. Too late.

"It *bites*."

Brack came off the boulder like the mountain coming down, and the last thing he heard before the world went white and roaring was the man's shout, and beneath it — faint, impossible, wonderful — Pip's voice screaming his name from the top of the goat track, and the drumming of feet, his family, *his family*, coming back for him after all.
