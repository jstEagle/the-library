## 15. The Guttering

The flame began to die on the third night after the crater.

Xanthe felt it before she saw it — a thinning in the air around the iron lantern where Marisol had coaxed it to rest, like a hearth going cold in a house where someone had just been singing. She woke with her hand already outstretched toward it, fingers curled as if she could cup warmth back into being, and found the others stirring too, drawn from sleep by the same wrongness. Even the horses were awake, ears pricked toward the little cage of glass and iron at the center of their camp.

"It's guttering," Marisol said. She was kneeling beside the lantern, her palms hovering over it without touching, and in the starless dark her face looked carved out of old candle wax. "It's been guttering since we took it. I thought — I hoped it was only adjusting. A fire needs a hearth. This—" she touched the lantern's cold rim, "—this is not a hearth."

Inside the glass, the heart-flame of Ysolde burned no larger than a thumbnail now. A week ago it had filled the vial like a caged sunrise, throwing shadows that moved wrong, that bent toward people instead of away from them. Now it was a coal. An ember the color of an old wound, pulsing slow and arrhythmic, and each pulse came fainter than the last, like a heartbeat counting down to something none of them wanted to name.

"How long?" Corin asked. He stood at the edge of the firelight with his arms crossed against the mountain cold, and he had not slept properly since Brack fell. None of them had. But there was something else in his voice tonight, something careful, the way a man speaks when he has already done the arithmetic and does not like the sum.

"Days," Marisol said. "Perhaps less. It isn't starving for fuel — I've fed it everything I know how to feed a flame. It's starving for *vessel*. Fire wants to live inside something. That's what fire *is* — life wearing light like a coat." She looked up, and her eyes went around the circle of them: Corin, Pip curled gray-faced and feverish under two blankets, the mule dozing on its feet, the dark trees leaning in. Her gaze snagged on Xanthe and stopped. "It can only live in a willing vessel. That's what the old texts say. The dragon's own flesh was its first vessel, willingly given, egg to bone. Voss stole it into glass, and glass is not willing, and so it burns itself down trying to escape." She swallowed. "A person could hold it. If the person chose it. Truly chose it, all the way down, with nothing held back."

The silence that followed was very large, and very cold, and full of everyone not looking at anyone.

"No," Corin said.

Nobody had proposed anything yet. That was the thing about Corin Ashe — he heard the shape of a conversation before it happened, the way he'd once heard ambushes in the creak of a saddle leather, and he moved to cut them off the way he used to move to cut off retreat. He crossed to the lantern and put his body between it and the rest of them, as if the ember inside might lunge.

"We are not feeding a person to a dragon's heart," he said. "We are taking this north, or east, or wherever Ysolde can reach it, and we are putting it back where it belongs, and that is the whole of the plan. It was the plan at Greytooth Pass. It is the plan now."

"The plan was made when we thought the flame would keep," Xanthe said quietly.

"Then we make a new one."

"With what? Corin." She rose, stiff-legged, her knees aching from days of hard riding and worse sleeping. "Look at it. Look at Pip." She nodded toward the small shape under the blankets, the child's breath coming quick and shallow, fever-bright even in sleep. "Pip took a Warden's boot to the ribs getting out of Havenhollow and hasn't complained once, because Pip never complains, but a child can't ride another week like this. And Brack is back there at the crater rim with half his blood left in the snow, and every hour we spend running north is an hour Voss spends tightening the net. The flame was supposed to be our answer. It's dying in a bottle while we argue about geography."

"Then we find Ysolde faster—"

"She's beneath the Spire, Corin. Beneath *Voss*. We ran *away* from her. That was the choice — remember? At the crater, when we saw what returning the flame would mean. What she would do with it. What war would look like with a dragon's grief at the front of it." Xanthe wrapped her arms around herself. The memory of that moral vertigo still lived in her chest like a stone she'd swallowed: standing at the rim of Dragonfast crater with the vial warm against her sternum, understanding that every path forward led through fire, and choosing the path that led *around* it, if around existed. "I promised her freedom. I meant it. But freedom delivered as a weapon is just another kind of cage — it cages everyone else."

"So you'll cage yourself instead," Corin said, and his voice cracked on the last word, and that was how she knew he had seen it too — had seen it hours ago, maybe days ago, had been lying awake watching the ember shrink and doing the same terrible arithmetic she had. "That's what you're building toward. I can hear it. You've been too quiet since Havenhollow."

"I haven't decided anything."

"You've decided. You're just waiting for the right moment to tell us you've decided."

"*Corin.*"

"Don't." He stepped closer, and up close she could see how hollowed he'd become, the gray in his stubble, the bruised skin under his eyes. "Don't stand there with your jaw set like that. I've watched you walk into burning buildings for strangers. I've watched you talk a cornered wolf off a cliff edge with nothing but your voice. You have never once in your life chosen the safe thing, and I love you for it, and I am begging you — *begging* you — to choose it just this once."

The word landed between them like a dropped torch.

*I love you.*

He had said it before, once, at the campfire on the long road north, in the low voice men use for confessions they've rehearsed a hundred times and still can't make sound casual. She had said it back. They had agreed, without ever saying so aloud, that the word was a promise about afterward — about some hypothetical country at the end of the road where there would be time for it, room for it, a house with a door that locked and mornings that belonged to no one but them. Saying it here, now, in the present tense, in front of the dying fire — that was different. That was a man spending his last coin.

Xanthe reached out and took his hands. His fingers were freezing.

"I'm not deciding anything tonight," she said. "But I need you to hear me, because if it comes to it, I need you to be able to live with it. That flame is the last of something older than every kingdom in this realm. Older than the Wardens. Older than the war Voss wants so badly. If it goes out — truly out — then every hedge-witch's charm fails, every beast-speaker goes deaf, every ward on every border crumbles to dust, and the world doesn't end, it just gets *smaller*, forever, and everything wild in it dies alone. I have spent my whole life listening to things other people say can't speak. I will not be the woman who let the last voice go silent because she was afraid of the price."

"And if the price is you?"

"Then it's a price," she said. "Not a certainty. Let me learn which it is before you bury me."

He held her gaze for a long moment. Then he did the thing that broke her heart most thoroughly: he nodded. Once. A soldier accepting orders he believed were fatal, because the woman giving them had earned the right to give them.

Marisol had turned away to hide her face. Pip, feigning sleep, had not fooled anyone for some time.

* * *

She went to the lantern alone, in the hour before dawn, when even the mule had given up pretending to watch.

The forest around them was silent in the way it had been silent since the stars vanished — not peaceful silence but *held* silence, the hush of animals who had lost the sky and didn't trust the ground either. Only once, far off, a single owl called, three notes, questioning, and got no answer. Xanthe knelt in the frost-rimed grass and lifted the lantern into her lap and looked into the glass at the last heart-fire of the last dragon in the world.

It was smaller than it had been at moonset. That was the truth she couldn't outrun. When they'd stolen it from the vaults it had roared inside its vial, furious and alive, and she had felt it through the glass like a second heartbeat laid over her own. Now it barely stirred. Its light reached perhaps a handspan past the lantern and no farther, and the dark pressed in on that little circle of gold the way winter presses in on a window.

"Hey," she whispered. Foolish, probably — it wasn't a beast, not exactly, not something with ears. But she had learned long ago that speaking to a thing honestly was never wasted, whether or not it understood. "Hey, old fire. I don't know if you can hear me. Marisol says you're more than fire. Says you're the part of Ysolde that remembers being young. The part that loved the world enough to burn for it."

The ember pulsed. Slow. Faint. But it pulsed — and Xanthe would have sworn, would have staked her gift on it, that the pulse leaned toward her voice the way a sunflower leans toward the last light of evening.

"I know what you want," she said. "You want out. You want sky. You want the crater and the thermals and a hundred years of grievance paid back with interest. I felt it that night at the vault, just holding the glass. You're rage, old fire. Rage and love, all tangled up together so tight nobody could separate them with a sword." She laughed softly, and it came out wet. "I know how that feels. I've been carrying both since Thornfell."

The flame flickered. Dimmed. Steadied.

And then it *spoke*.

Not in words. Nothing so crude. It spoke the way the fox had spoken, the way the rats beneath the Spire had spoken, the way wind speaks to a sail — directly, pressure becoming meaning. And what it said was not rage. Not yet. What it said was:

*cold*

*so cold*

*small*

and underneath those, threaded through them like veins through marble:

*willing*

*it must be willing*

*will you*

Xanthe's breath stopped in her throat. The frost on the grass seemed suddenly very bright, very sharp, the world narrowed to the ember and the question hanging in the air between them.

"Will I what?" she whispered, though she knew. Of course she knew. She had known since Marisol said *willing vessel* and everyone in the circle had looked anywhere but at her.

The flame pulsed again, and this time the meaning came with sensation attached — a flood of images that were not hers: a vast winged shadow crossing a young sun; mountains that were green and crowned with dragons instead of bare and crowned with snow; a city of spires raised in friendship by many hands; and then fire, betrayal, chains, a century of stone ceiling overhead, and always, always, the slow draining, the siphon-needles of the Wardens drinking her heart a cupful at a generation—

Ysolde's memories. The flame carried Ysolde's memories, the way a river carries the mountain it was born from.

And beneath the grief of them, the offer, plain and patient as a hand held out:

*me or nothing*

*xanthe-vane-who-listens*

*will you carry me*

She sat back on her heels. Her hands were shaking. Somewhere behind her, at the edge of hearing, she heard Corin turn over in his bedroll and go still — awake, listening, giving her this hour even though it was killing him.

"If I take you," she said slowly, "what happens to me?"

The answer came as honest as the question deserved: a cascade of images, half warning, half promise. Strength like a forge-wind in the blood. Sight that pierced dark and lie alike. The weight of a century of dragon-grief settling onto human shoulders. And a countdown — the flame showed her that too, showed her a candle burning down inside her chest, because a mortal vessel was a mortal vessel, and a heart-flame was not made for hearts that stopped. It would change her. It would consume her gift, her beast-speech, the one thing that had been hers alone since childhood, burning it as fuel the way any fire burns whatever is nearest.

And it would whisper to her. Every day. Every hour. *Burn them. Burn the Spire. Burn Voss in her own righteous robes.* The flame could not help it. Rage was half of what it was.

"That's the bargain?" Xanthe said. "Your power, your memories, your anger — and my voice, my time, my—" she almost said *my soul* and changed course, "—my better judgment. Until I burn through."

The ember flared — the brightest it had been in days — and the meaning that came off it was almost amused, almost tender:

*you speak as if you have a better offer*

She laughed. It hurt. Everything hurt, and had for weeks, and she was so tired.

Behind her, boots on frost. Corin came and knelt beside her without a word, and Marisol after him, and then Pip, wrapped in a blanket, gray-faced and stubborn, refusing to be left out of anything ever again. They formed a half-circle around the lantern, around her, and no one spoke, because there was nothing left to argue. They had all done the arithmetic. They had all arrived at the same sum.

"There's another way," Corin said one last time, hoarsely. "There's always another way. We could—"

"Name it," Xanthe said gently. "Name one. Just one, Corin, and I'll take it."

He opened his mouth. Closed it. Looked at the ember, at Pip, at the starless sky where stars should have been, and Xanthe watched him arrive at the same empty country she had been walking through all night.

"I hate this," he said.

"I know."

"I love you."

"I know that too." She kissed him, brief and fierce, tasting salt. "Which is why you're going to help me up in a minute, because I don't think I can stand on my own."

Marisol was already moving, pulling chalk from her kit, muttering the old words of containment-and-release, drawing a circle in the frost around Xanthe's knees. "It has to be willing on both sides," the hedge-witch said, and her voice shook only a little. "The vessel must choose the flame, and the flame must choose the vessel. Speak to it. Tell it yes. And Xanthe — listen to me — when it enters you, it will show you everything it is, all at once. Don't fight it and don't drown in it. Find the part of yourself that talks to foxes, and hold on to *that*."

Pip pressed something into her hand: the child's one treasure, a brass button polished mirror-bright, stolen from a Warden captain's coat a lifetime ago. "For luck," Pip whispered. "Bring it back."

Xanthe closed her fist around the button. Opened the lantern.

The moment the glass swung wide, the flame surged — not toward escape, not toward the open sky it had yearned for across a hundred years of captivity, but toward *her*. It rose from the lantern like a thread of golden smoke, hung in the frozen air between them, and Xanthe felt it reading her, the way she had read a thousand beasts: testing the shape of her spirit, probing for fear, for hesitation, for the smallest seam of unwillingness.

She gave it none. She opened everything.

"Yes," she said aloud. "Old fire. Last light. I'm willing. Come home."

The flame struck her chest like a falling star.

* * *

There was no pain. That was the first surprise — she had braced for burning and instead there was only *fullness*, a pouring-in, the sensation of a drought-cracked riverbed receiving spring flood all at once. Light behind her eyes. Heat in her marrow. And then the memories hit, all of them, a century compressed into a single endless heartbeat: the green mountains, the wheeling flight, the friendship of the founding years, the first ward-stone lit by a dragon's willing gift — *willing, it had always been willing, that was the old covenant, fire freely given* — and then the turning, the fear, the spears, the chains, the needles, the long dark drinking—

And the rage. Gods, the rage. It rose in her like a second tide under the grief, vast and ancient and *justified*, and it knew names: Voss, Voss, the Spire, the Order, every hand that had ever held a chain, burn them, burn the city, burn the *world* if the world had learned nothing in a hundred years—

*Xanthe.* Marisol's voice, far away, from outside the inferno. *Find the fox-part. Hold on to the fox-part.*

She reached for it. For the memory of a dying fox's breath against her palm in the pass below Thornfell, the small desperate voice saying *help me, speaker, please, help me* — and the rage met that memory and recoiled, hissing, because rage cannot abide tenderness, cannot look at it straight on.

She held on. She held on the way she had held on to a cliff-edge in a gale once, with both hands and her teeth, and slowly, slowly, the flood settled into banks, and the fire settled into her, curling up behind her sternum like a creature finding its den.

When she opened her eyes, the world was *lit*.

Not with light — with seeing. She could see the heat of her friends' bodies through their clothes, the cold veins of frost in the earth, the distant orange smear of a Warden campfire four miles south through the trees, and beyond it, beyond everything, the black tower of the Spire on the southern horizon, and inside it, moving like a spider in a web of light, the cold bright wrongness of Sarel Voss and the stolen magic bound into her bones.

Xanthe stood. Frost steamed off her shoulders. Where her tears had fallen, the grass had thawed into a ring of impossible green.

"Well," she said, and her voice came out doubled, harmonized, hers and something older underneath it, "that's one way to wake up."

Corin stared at her. Pip's mouth was a perfect O. Marisol had gone pale as the frost.

"How long do I have?" Xanthe asked quietly. "Before the candle burns down. Before it takes my gift and then takes the rest. Be honest, Mari."

Marisol's eyes were wet. "Weeks," she said. "Maybe a month. Less if you use it hard."

Southward, four miles through the black trees, the Warden campfires began to move.

They had found them.

Xanthe flexed her hands and felt the fire settle deeper, patient and hungry, already whispering its first soft suggestion: *burn them, little vessel, burn them all* — and she smiled at her family, a smile that cost her everything, and said:

"Then we'd better run."
