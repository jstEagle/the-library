## 16. What Power Whispers

The fire had been quiet for three days.

Xanthe told herself that was a mercy. She woke each morning and pressed a hand to her sternum, where the heart-flame lived now beneath her ribs like a second heartbeat, slower than her own, older, patient in the way that deep things are patient. Three days of silence. She had almost begun to believe the worst of it was over — the fever-dreams of the first night, when she'd felt Ysolde's century of captivity pour through her like molten grief; the way the trees along the north road had seemed to lean toward her, whispering *burn us, burn us, we are so tired*.

Almost.

It was dusk at the waystation above the Ashenmere, an old drover's shelter with a sagging roof and walls of dry-stacked stone gone soft with moss. Marisol had wards strung across the door like garlands of thorned light, invisible unless you knew to look. Brack sat outside whittling a crutch for himself with a patience that would have shamed a saint — his leg bound from thigh to knee where a Warden crossbow bolt had punched through it at the crater rim, though he refused to let anyone say the word *maimed*. Pip was asleep by the hearth, curled around a stolen apple like a dragon around hoard, one hand still loosely gripping the knife Corin had finally, after much argument, agreed the child could carry.

And Corin was watching her.

He thought she didn't notice. He was wrong about that more often than he believed, and she suspected he knew it. He sat near the fire with his sword across his knees, honing it in long slow strokes, but his eyes kept lifting to her face the way a man checks the sky for weather. He had seen what happened at the crater rim. He had seen her stand over the captured scout afterward, and he had seen the look on her face before she mastered it.

She turned away from him and looked out the broken window instead, at the darkening mere, at the first stars pricking through — real stars again, or close enough. The sky had been healing since the night the wards fell. She did not find this comforting. She found it obscene, somehow: the heavens mending themselves while everything below them burned.

*Xanthe.*

There it was. Not sound. Something deeper than sound, a warmth behind her breastbone that shaped itself into words the way heat shapes glass.

*I dreamed of Thornfell,* the flame said. Its voice was Ysolde's voice, or an echo of it — vast and slow, with sorrow folded into every syllable like ash into snow. *I dreamed of the passes where my sisters flew. Do you know what they did to us, little speaker?*

"I know," Xanthe murmured. She kept her voice low, under the crackle of the fire, under Pip's breathing. "You've told me."

*They have not told me enough.* The warmth spread, feathering outward through her chest, down her arms, pooling in her hands until her fingertips glowed faintly gold in the dim room. *A hundred years in the dark beneath their Spire. Chains of cold iron and colder doctrine. They took my fire in a vial and called it holy. And you hold it now, and you ask me to be gentle.*

"You're asking me to be something else."

*I am asking you to remember.* And then it showed her.

That was the trap of it, the terrible genius. It didn't argue. It simply opened doors in her mind and let her walk through.

She saw the Dragonfast crater as it had been, two hundred years ago — not a ruin but a city, terraces of white stone climbing the caldera walls, dragons wheeling overhead in the amber light of evening like living banners. She saw the first Wardens arrive, humble then, pilgrims seeking protection from the wars of men. She saw the treaties signed in dragon-fire that could never be forged. And then she saw the turning: the slow accumulation of fear, the sermons that curdled reverence into suspicion, the night the first chains went on. She felt it all from inside Ysolde's memory — the weight of iron settling across her shoulders, the indignity of it, the *betrayal*, sworn brothers-in-arms standing in a ring with nets and lances while the dragon who had sheltered their grandmothers bled on the stones.

The vision shifted. Now it was Voss — Sarel Voss, younger, kneeling in the vaults beneath the Spire with the vial of stolen flame cupped in both hands like a relic, her face lit from below with a rapture Xanthe recognized because she felt its twin stirring in herself right now. *This is how they make them,* the flame whispered. *Not born. Made. Hand them power and a grievance in equal measure, and watch which one grows faster.*

"Stop." Xanthe's whisper came out ragged. Her hands were shaking. The glow wouldn't stop. "I didn't take you in to become your weapon any more than I'll let them keep using you as theirs."

*No?* A pulse of something that was almost laughter, if laughter could be sad. *Then why do your hands burn, little speaker? Why does your heart keep count?*

Because it did. That was the horror she hadn't confessed to anyone. Somewhere in the last three days, without deciding it, she had started keeping a ledger. Every refugee dragged from the Spire city in chains. Every hedge-witch Marisol named who had vanished. Brack's ruined leg. Pip's three days in a cell. The ledger grew, and the flame read it aloud to her each night, and each night the arithmetic whispered the same conclusion: *there are only so many of them, and you are fire now, and fire settles debts.*

"Xanthe." Corin's voice, quiet, from across the room. "You're glowing."

She looked down. Her forearms shone through her sleeves like coals under ash. In the hearth, the ordinary fire had risen up flat against the chimney stone, straining toward her like a dog toward its master.

Pip stirred. Marisol, dozing in her chair, snapped awake with a hedge-witch's instinct for wrongness and was already moving, already muttering, her fingers sketching a cooling sigil in the air.

"I'm fine," Xanthe said.

"No," Corin said, rising, "you're not."

*He fears you now,* the flame observed, gently, horribly. *Feel it. How it feeds me. Fear is fuel too, little speaker. They never understood that. They thought fire needed wood.*

* * *

She slept badly and woke before dawn with the taste of smoke in her mouth and no fire lit anywhere in the room.

The scout was in the old byre behind the waystation.

They'd taken him at the crater rim — a young Warden outrider, barely twenty, separated from his patrol, armed with a crossbow he'd surrendered so fast it was almost comic. Brack had carried him down the mountain slung over one shoulder like a sack of turnips, because Brack did not leave wounded men on mountains, even men sent to kill them. Marisol had healed the arrow wound in his shoulder. He'd been their prisoner for four days, tied in the byre among the rusted stalls, fed from their own stores, and he had said almost nothing the entire time except *thank you* when given water, in a voice so bewildered it broke something in Xanthe every time she heard it.

His name was Tam. He was from a farm village south of the Mere. He had a mother, he'd told Pip once, thinking the child harmless, who kept bees.

Xanthe stood in the doorway of the byre in the grey pre-light and looked at him, and the ledger opened.

He wore the uniform. That was the whole of the case, and it was damning enough: the grey-and-silver of the Wardens, the spire-and-star badge at his collar, the same uniform worn by the men who had chained Ysolde, who had emptied the granaries, who had put Pip in a cell and smiled while doing it. He was asleep, breath whistling softly through a healing rib. Young. Freckled. His mother kept bees.

*You see?* the flame said, waking with her, warm and eager. *It is so simple. One is nothing. One is a message. Let them learn what it costs to hunt us.*

"He's a child," Xanthe whispered.

*So was I. Once. So long ago even I have forgotten it. They did not spare me for being young.*

She crossed the byre floor. She did not remember deciding to. Her body moved the way a river moves, and she watched herself from somewhere slightly behind her own eyes as she knelt beside the sleeping scout, as her hand rose, as light gathered in her palm — not fire exactly, something purer and far worse, the raw essence of the thing that had once cracked mountains —

The scout's eyes opened. He saw her face. He saw her hand. And he did not scream. He just went very still, the way animals go still, and said, "Is it the dragon? Are you the dragon?"

And some small human part of her flinched — because he wasn't afraid of *her*, he was afraid of the story he'd been told, the beast-speaker monster of Warden broadsheets, and she was about to make the story true —

— and then hands closed on her shoulders and wrenched her back, and the light shattered against the byre wall in a spray of sparks that set the old straw smoldering.

Corin. He'd followed her. Of course he'd followed her; he'd been checking the sky for weather for three days. He wrapped his arms around her from behind, pinning her burning hands against her own chest, and she felt the heat of her skin scorch him through his shirt and heard him hiss with pain and *not let go*.

"Xanthe. Xanthe, look at me. Look at *me*."

She fought him. She was stronger than she should have been — the flame lent strength the way it lent everything, generously, poisonously — and she heard him grunt as she twisted, heard the straw catch properly now, flames licking up the old stall posts. Somewhere beyond the ringing in her ears, Tam scrambled backward into the corner, and Marisol's voice cut through the byre, sharp and rhythmic, dousing the fire with words older than the Wardens.

"Look at me," Corin said again, and this time she did.

His face was inches from hers. There was terror in it, and there was something else, something that frightened her more than her own hands: he was looking at her the way he must once have looked at the Wardens he'd served, before they taught him better. Like someone watching a good thing break.

"This isn't you," he said. "This is what they did to Ysolde. This is what they did to *Voss*. You told me — you told me yourself, in the pass, that power doesn't corrupt, it *recruits*. It finds the wound and offers to be the sword."

"The ledger—" Her voice cracked. "Someone has to pay, Corin. Someone always pays, and it's never them, it's never *them*, it's Brack bleeding and Pip in a cell and Ysolde in the dark for a hundred years—"

"And you'll collect?" His grip tightened. "One boy with a crossbow? And then the next, because one won't balance it, and then the next, and in ten years you'll be the High Warden with fire in your veins and a grievance you call justice. I've *seen* how that story goes, Xanthe. I gave it twenty years of my life."

The fight went out of her all at once, the way a snuffed candle goes out. She sagged against him. The glow beneath her skin guttered, dimmed, and died down to embers. Across the byre, Tam huddled in the corner with his wrists still bound, staring at them both, and Marisol stood over the blackened straw with her hands smoking faintly, looking at Xanthe with an expression she couldn't bear to name.

"I nearly killed him," Xanthe said. It didn't sound like her voice. "He thanked me for the water, Corin. He thanked me."

"I know."

"How did they fall?" she asked him, and meant the dragons, and meant something larger. "How does anyone fall? Was it all at once?"

Corin was quiet a long moment. Outside, the dawn was coming up grey and cold over the Ashenmere, and the first birds were starting, tentative, in the alders.

"It was never all at once," he said finally. "It was a ledger. Same as yours. They wrote down every wrong ever done to them, and they called the writing holy, and one day the book was bigger than they were." He released her slowly, keeping one hand on her arm as if she might blow away. "You don't burn the book, love. You put it down."

*Little speaker,* the flame murmured, deep within her, and for the first time since she had taken it in, it sounded afraid. *What happens to me, if you put me down?*

She had no answer. But before she could speak — before any of them could move — hoofbeats thundered up the drover's track outside, many horses, riding hard, and Pip's voice rang shrill from the waystation door:

"Wardens! Wardens on the road — and they've got Brack!"

* * *

Xanthe reached the yard in time to see the impossible: six mounted Wardens in full kit, and Brack standing between them and the waystation door on his one good leg, crutch raised like a warclub, roaring defiance at men whose lances were leveled at his chest.

At their head, grey-cloaked and smiling, sat a woman Xanthe had seen only in nightmares and wanted posters: silver-braided, serene, with a vial of something glinting at her throat.

Sarel Voss had found them.

"Well," the High Warden called pleasantly, as Brack's crutch splintered against a lance shaft and the great stonemason went down hard on his knees in the frost. "There you are, beast-speaker. And here I'd been told you were hiding."

Her eyes found Xanthe's across the yard — and dropped, deliberately, to Xanthe's chest, as if she could see the flame burning there.

"Give it back," Voss said, "and perhaps I'll let the big one live."
