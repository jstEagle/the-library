## 17. The Siege of Havenhollow

The rain had been falling on Havenhollow for three days when the crows came.

Xanthe Vane stood in the doorway of the Drowned Lantern inn and watched them settle on the rooftops along the market square — dozens of them, then hundreds, a black frost creeping across the slate. The townsfolk hurried past her with their heads down, hauling in crates of late apples, shuttering windows against a wind that smelled wrong. She alone stood still, listening, because the crows were talking to each other, and she could hear it: *riders coming, riders coming, riders coming.*

She had known for two days, really. Known since the hedge-witch Marisol had read the smoke over the southern hills and pronounced it the wrong color. Known since Corin Ashe had come down from the watchtower at dusk with his jaw set like quarried stone and said only, "They've found us." But knowing a thing and feeling it arrive were different animals, and the crows were the arrival itself — the sky filling with scavengers who always knew where death would be served.

"Xan." Pip appeared at her elbow, small and quick, still carrying the faint bruise-shadow at the wrist where the Warden irons had been. The child looked up at the birds with an expression Xanthe couldn't immediately read, and then she could: it was the look of someone counting. "How many?"

"More than we can fight," Xanthe said honestly. "Fewer than they think."

Pip nodded as if that were the most sensible thing anyone had ever said, and went back inside to help Brack bar the doors.

Havenhollow was not a fortress. That was the whole point of it. It was a free-town, one of the last — no Warden garrison, no tithe-house, no Spire banner anywhere except the faded one some merchant used as a floor rag. It was a town of tanners and beekeepers and a ferryman who charged what you could pay, and it had taken the family in without asking what they carried or what they'd done, because that was what free-towns did, or had done, once. Old Mother Casselin, who ran the Drowned Lantern, had looked at their mud-caked boots and their haunted eyes and said only, "There's stew. Sit."

Now the stew pot was cold and the whole town knew what was coming up the south road, and nobody had asked them to leave.

That was the thing Xanthe kept turning over in her chest like a river stone. Nobody had asked them to leave. She had gone to Casselin herself, ready to beg, ready to take her family out into the hills and draw the Wardens away — and the old woman had taken Xanthe's face in both flour-dusted hands and said, "Child, I buried a husband to the Spire's tax-men and a son to their conscription. You think I'll hand them a girl and a captain and a witch and a babe-brained stonemason and a pickpocket just because they come with pretty armor? Get away from me with that talk. Go help Marta bring her goats in."

So they stayed. And the town chose to stay with them.

* * *

Corin Ashe had spent eleven years learning how fortifications failed, and now he walked Havenhollow's muddy perimeter in the grey dawn light and saw every way this town would fail, and hated that he loved it anyway.

It had walls once — low ones, fieldstone, built to keep wolves out of the sheep pens, not soldiers out of streets. Gaps yawned where carts had knocked them down over the years. The southern gate hadn't closed properly since before the war. There was no moat, no ditch worth the name, no tower higher than the granary roof. Against a full Warden company with siege gear, Havenhollow would hold for perhaps an hour. Against Sarel Voss's picked veterans, less.

But Voss did not have a full company here. Corin had counted the fires on the southern ridge the night before — forty, maybe fifty men, riding hard, traveling light. They expected no fight at all. They expected a town to open its gates and hand over five fugitives the way towns always had, the way the Spire's writ said they must, the way fear had taught the realm to behave for a hundred years.

*They expect obedience,* Corin thought, standing at the broken south gate with rain running off his chin. *They have never once considered that people might simply refuse.*

He turned that thought over the way he'd once turned over tactical maps, and found it heavier and stranger than anything in his old profession. Because refusal wasn't a tactic. You couldn't drill it, couldn't supply it, couldn't command it into being. It came from somewhere else entirely — from a beekeeper deciding her hives mattered more than her safety, from a ferryman tying his boat to the dock instead of fleeing across the river, from an old innkeeper putting herself between Warden steel and strangers because someone had once put themselves between Warden steel and her son.

Corin had spent his life believing order was the highest good, that the Wardens' wall of law was all that stood between the realm and the old darkness. He had watched that wall rot from the inside, watched it become a weapon pointed inward, and he had burned his badge in a granary fire rather than serve it. But standing here, in this unfortified town of stubborn farmers, he understood something he'd never learned in any garrison: the wall had never really been made of stone. It had been made of people choosing each other. The Spire had merely claimed the credit.

"Captain." Marisol found him at the gate, hood up, her hands stained green to the wrist with some preparation or other. Behind her trailed six women and two men — hedge-witches, cunning-folk, the quiet magics of rural places that the Spire called superstition when it bothered to call them anything. "We need to talk about the walls."

"The walls won't hold," Corin said flatly.

"No," Marisol agreed. "But they don't have to hold long. And they don't have to hold alone." She lifted her stained hands. "Every hedge-witch within a day's walk is coming. I sent word by crow and moth and dream. They're bringing charms and weather and anger. We can make your hour into a day, Captain. Maybe more."

He looked at her — this small fierce woman who had burned half her own magic getting Pip out of a vault, who moved like someone perpetually surprised to still be alive — and felt something loosen in his chest that had been tight since Greytooth Pass.

"What do you need?" he asked.

"Your eyes," she said. "You know how soldiers think. Tell us where they'll hit us, and we'll be there first."

* * *

They hit at the south gate an hour after noon, under a sky that Marisol had spent the morning stuffing with cloud.

Sarel Voss's lieutenant — a broad man named Hesk with a voice like a millstone — rode up under a white cloth and demanded the town surrender the fugitives, citing the Act of Unified Protection, offering amnesty to all who complied, promising fire to all who refused. He gave them until the bell.

Old Mother Casselin climbed onto the gatehouse steps herself, seventy years old and four feet ten, and answered him in a voice that carried the length of the market square.

"You may tell the High Warden," she said, "that Havenhollow keeps its own hearths. And that she should remember what happened to the last empire that came north to teach us manners."

Hesk's face went through several colors. Then the white cloth dropped, and the horns sounded, and forty veteran Wardens came out of the treeline expecting a town that would fold.

What they got was weather.

Marisol and nine hedge-witches had spent the morning arguing with the sky, and the sky, grudgingly, had agreed. As the Warden column reached the mud flats before the gate, the rain turned sideways and cold enough to ache, and the ground beneath the Wardens' charge — ground that had been firm clay all week — remembered, suddenly and inconveniently, that it had once been a bog. Hesk's front rank went down in a tangle of horse and shield. From the rooftops, tanners dumped vats of lime-water; from the granary, boys with slingshots — actual children with shepherd's slings, which Corin had tried to forbid and been firmly overruled — rained stones on the floundering riders.

And on the broken wall beside the gate, enormous and unhurried, Brack the stonemason stood in the rain catching ladders.

Not breaking them. Catching them. Catching them and setting them gently aside, the way a man might move a branch off a path, while arrows sparked off the stone around him and one had already opened a red line across his shoulder he hadn't bothered mentioning. When a Warden sergeant actually gained the wall-top, Brack regarded him for a moment with those mild river-grey eyes and then simply picked him up and handed him back down to his comrades, the way you'd return a goat that had gotten into the garden.

"They keep climbing," Brack reported later, mildly puzzled, over supper. "I kept handing them down. I don't think they understood."

By dusk the assault had broken twice and stalled, and Hesk pulled his men back to the ridge to wait for whatever Voss would send next, and inside the walls the town did the most extraordinary thing: it sang. Someone started it — the ferryman, Xanthe thought, though she never learned for certain — a rough harvest song, and it spread street by street until the whole town was singing at the darkening ridge line, defiant and off-key and alive.

Xanthe stood in the square with the heart-flame's warmth banked low in her chest — she had learned to keep it quiet, a coal wrapped in ash — and listened, and wept without shame.

*This,* said something deep in her, in the flame, in the fox-voice of the world itself. *This is what the fire was for.*

* * *

Pip had found a job, and the job was roofs.

Nobody had assigned it. Pip had simply looked at the layout of the town, looked at the gaps in the defense, and appointed themselves runner-and-signal — small enough to cross the rooftops unseen, quick enough to carry word between the gate and the granary and the witches' station faster than any rider could ride it through the mud. By the second day Pip had memorized every drainpipe and loose shingle in Havenhollow and had opinions about all of them.

It was on the third night, crossing the tannery roof in the black rain, that Pip saw the second force.

Not from the south. From the east, moving without torches through the orchard rows — twenty shapes, maybe thirty, in the new matte-black armor the Spire had begun issuing to its special companies. Not an assault. A wedge, aimed at the eastern wall where there were no defenders worth the name, where the plan — everyone's plan — assumed nothing would happen because nothing ever happened there.

Pip did the arithmetic in about two seconds. Running the rooftops to warn the gate would take too long. Shouting would bring the whole infiltration force down on one small child.

So Pip did what Pip did. Pip stole the night.

There was a stack of tar-barrels by the tannery, left from autumn. Pip rolled one to the roof edge, waited until the wedge passed below, and dropped it into the lane behind them, where it burst and splashed burning pitch across the cobbles — and then lit a second, and a third, and screamed like something out of a story, screaming down through the rain: *"EAST WALL! EAST WALL! THEY'RE IN THE ORCHARD!"*

It shouldn't have worked. One child, some barrels, a lot of noise. But the hedge-witches had left watchers on the granary roof precisely because Marisol trusted no plan, and the watchers saw the fires, and the bells began, and by the time the black-armored wedge reached the eastern wall it found Brack waiting for it, having crossed the town at a dead run with a felled apple tree over his shoulder like a broom.

Afterward, Casselin fed Pip twice at supper and told everyone within earshot that the child had saved the town, and Pip, mortally embarrassed, hid under the table until Xanthe came and sat on the floor beside them in the dark and said quietly, "You know what you are, right? You're the reason the wall held."

"I'm the reason the barrels fell down," Pip said, but Pip's voice wobbled, and Xanthe took the small hand in hers and held it, and neither of them said anything else for a while, because some things don't need saying, only sitting-with.

* * *

On the fourth day, the sky cleared, and Sarel Voss came to Havenhollow herself.

Xanthe felt her arrival before anyone announced it — a pressure behind the sternum, the heart-flame flaring in its ash-wrapping like a live coal breathed upon. The fox, convalescing in a basket by the Drowned Lantern's hearth, raised its head and growled at nothing, and every animal in the town went silent at once, the way they had on the night the stars went out.

Voss rode to the ridge above the south gate with a banner and a hundred fresh troops and did not attack. She sat her white mare in plain view, in the clean sunlight, and her voice — amplified by some working woven into her own throat, a magic worn like jewelry — carried to every corner of the town.

"People of Havenhollow," she said, warm as a hearth, reasonable as a magistrate. "I do not come to punish you. I come to save you. The woman you shelter carries stolen fire — the last fire of the last dragon — and everything she touches burns. Your walls will fall. Your homes will burn. Give her to me, and go back to your lives untouched. This is mercy. I offer it once."

Silence. Long enough that Xanthe could hear her own blood.

Then, from the gatehouse, Old Mother Casselin again — but this time she was not alone. She stood surrounded by the town council, by the ferryman, by Marta the goatherd, by hedge-witches with green-stained hands, by Brack towering behind them all like a cliff that had decided to attend a meeting. And the old woman said, in a voice dry as seed corn:

"We heard you the first time. Our answer hasn't changed. Come and take us, if you can find us under all these neighbors."

And the town — the whole town, every rooftop, every window — answered her. Not with words. With sound: pots banged on doorsteps, children stamping on floors, the ferryman ringing his mooring bell, the smith striking his anvil in slow deliberate blows like a heartbeat. A wall of ordinary noise thrown against the High Warden's silver voice.

On the ridge, Xanthe saw Voss's composure flicker — saw, for one naked instant, the thing beneath the righteous face: confusion. She had offered mercy and been refused. She had offered fear and been met with song. Her entire architecture — the holy war, the harvested flame, the realm unified under her fist — rested on the certainty that people, given the choice, would always choose safety over each other.

Havenhollow had just told her, loudly, in pots and anvils and an old woman's cracked voice, that they would not.

* * *

That night, in the loft above the Drowned Lantern, the family gathered around a single candle because lamps were rationed, and took stock.

Brack's shoulder was stitched — Marisol's work, neat as basketry. Pip slept mid-story, mid-boast, mid-bite of bread, upright against Brack's side like a kitten against a warm wall. Corin cleaned weapons that didn't need cleaning because his hands needed doing. Marisol stared into the candle and admitted, in a voice gone thin, that her reserves were nearly empty, that she had been spending pieces of herself for days, and that she didn't regret a single piece.

And Xanthe sat by the window with the fox in her lap and looked north, toward the crater where dragons once slept, and felt the countdown in her chest — because the flame was changing her, and she had stopped pretending otherwise, and the longer it stayed in her the more it whispered, and the whisper was getting harder to refuse.

"It has to end soon," she said quietly, to all of them and none of them. "Whatever happens. I can't carry it much farther."

"Then we go tomorrow," Corin said. No argument, no debate, no weighing. Just that. "First light. North road, through the high pass. Voss will expect us to hide behind these walls until they break. So we won't."

"She'll follow," Marisol warned.

"Let her," said Pip, waking just enough to say it, with the absolute confidence of a child who had personally defeated an army with barrels.

Brack, half-asleep, added: "I will walk in front."

And outside, over the battered, singing, stubborn town, the clouds finally tore open — and Xanthe saw, for the first time in weeks, stars. Faint, few, trembling at the edges of the dark like coals at the edge of ash.

But there.

Something vast stirred far to the north, beneath the roots of the Spire, in the dark where a dragon lay dying in chains. Xanthe felt it through the flame in her chest like a pulse through a joined hand — felt Ysolde turning her great head toward the north road, toward the crater, toward the fire that was coming home.

Toward them.

The fox lifted its head from Xanthe's lap and spoke one word into the candlelit dark, the first word it had spoken since the wards fell:

"Soon."
