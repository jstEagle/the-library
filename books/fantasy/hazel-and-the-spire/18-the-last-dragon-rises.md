## 18. The Last Dragon Rises

She had forgotten the color of the sky.

Not the word for it — words were the last things to go, worn smooth as river stones by a century of turning them over in the dark. *Blue*, she told herself, in the deep hours when the chains grew heaviest and her heart-flame guttered like a candle in a doorway. *The sky was blue, and there were clouds like white mountains walking, and at evening it went gold and then rose and then a blue so deep it was nearly black, and the stars came out one by one like embers shaken from a burning log.*

But remembering a word was not remembering a thing. A century beneath the roots of the Spire had taught Ysolde the difference. She knew the names of ten thousand things she could no longer see: wind, rain, the particular green of new pine needles when the light came through them from behind. She knew the taste of goat and the taste of deer and the taste of fish taken live from cold rivers, though her last meal had been gruel shoveled through an iron door by hands that never lingered, that never trembled, that never once — in a hundred years of feeding a god in a cage — looked up to meet her eye.

She had been mighty once. That was not vanity; it was fact, the way stone is hard and water is wet. She had been Ysolde Stormwing, and the mountain passes had learned her shadow the way children learn their mothers' footsteps. When she flew over Thornfell, the wolves sang. When she landed at Dragonfast, the ground remembered her weight for a generation.

Now she was a rumor in a cellar. Now she was a story the Wardens told their recruits to make them feel important: *we keep the last dragon, and the keeping is holy.*

Her chains were warded iron, spelled by men long dead, renewed each solstice by men who had never seen a living dragon before they came down into the dark with their little tongs and their little prayers. They thought the wards held her because they were strong.

They held her because she was weak. There was a difference, and she hoped — in the small, stubborn chamber of herself that still hoped — that someday they would learn it at the worst possible moment.

* * *

The theft of her flame had happened ninety years ago, and she had felt every hour of its absence.

They had come with a vial of dragon-glass and a surgeon's patience, three High Wardens in succession across the decades, each taking a little more, each assuring her — in the gentle voices of men who believe their own kindness — that it was necessary, that the border wards must be fed, that the realm must be protected, that she should be proud to serve. As if a heart could be bled like a taproom cask and the bleeding called service.

She had raged, in the early years. The rage had cracked the undercroft walls twice, and both times they had doubled the wards and tripled the chains and taken a little more fire as penalty. So she had learned what the old ones had always known, the truth carved into the bones of every dragon since the first egg cracked open in the world's first morning: *rage is a fire that burns the one who carries it.* She had banked her rage down to coals. She had wrapped the coals in sorrow. And she had waited, because dragons are patient the way mountains are patient, and because somewhere in the arithmetic of her long dying she had calculated that the realm above her would eventually grow careless, or cruel enough to be predictable, or simply run out of wardens willing to climb down into the dark.

She had not expected hope to arrive on four small feet, smelling of blood and pine and stubbornness.

* * *

The fox had come to her first.

It had crept down through gaps no Warden knew existed — the undercroft was honeycombed with rat-runs and root-tunnels, and the small things of the world had always known Ysolde, had always brought her news in exchange for warmth. In the years when she still had warmth to give. Rats had sat in the crook of her foreclaw and gossiped about grain prices. Bats had hung from her horns and sung her the songs of the surface. When her fire dimmed, the visits thinned, but they never stopped entirely. The small things were loyal past all reason. It was one of the reasons she had loved the world, back when loving it had not hurt so much.

And then, one night — she did not measure nights anymore, but it was recent, recent as a fresh wound — the fox had come down the root-tunnels with a woman's scent clinging to its fur, and it had looked up at her with eyes like struck flint, and it had said:

*She's coming. The speaker. She's coming for you.*

Ysolde had almost laughed, and the laugh would have been terrible, a sound to crack stone. *Coming for me,* she had said. *Little brother, nothing comes for me. Nothing has come for me in a hundred years. Everything comes to take from me, and even that has stopped, because soon there will be nothing left to take.*

*She has your fire,* the fox said. *Or part of it. Stolen, and stolen again, and now it lives in her chest, and she is running north with it, and she does not know yet what she means to do.*

*Then she is a fool carrying a bonfire through a dry field.*

*Yes,* the fox agreed placidly. *But she is our fool. And she listens.*

Dragons do not weep. Their bodies are built wrong for it, all scales and hollow bone, and the grief stays inside where it calcifies around the heart like pearl around a wound. But something in Ysolde's vast chest had shifted at those words — *she listens* — and shifted again, later, when the fox returned with more: the woman's name was Xanthe Vane, and she smelled of moon-wool and honesty, and she traveled with a disgraced Warden who had chosen treason over duty, and a stonemason built like a gatehouse, and a witch who burned her own magic like kindling to save a child, and a child pickpocket who had given up nothing because the child had nothing left to give except courage, which turned out to be everything.

A found family. Ysolde knew the shape of it from the oldest stories, the ones from before the war, when dragons and the small folk had traded gifts instead of fire. Her grandmother had spoken of such bonds the way priests speak of heaven: *a hoard you cannot count in coin, little one. A hoard made of hearts.*

And now this Xanthe Vane carried Ysolde's own stolen heart-flame in her chest, and the flame had not consumed her, and she had not used it to buy power or safety or vengeance, and she was coming north through the winter passes with it cradled inside her like a bird with a broken wing.

*What does she mean to do?* Ysolde had asked the fox, on its last visit.

And the fox had looked at her for a long time with its flint-strike eyes, and had said: *I don't think she knows. I think that's the point. I think she's going to choose it when she gets there, and I think whatever she chooses will be something none of us — not you, not the Wardens, not the war itself — has imagined.*

Ysolde had turned that over in the dark for a long time afterward.

Because here was the truth she had never spoken aloud, the truth beneath the truth, the secret she kept even from the rats and the bats and the fox: *she did not want vengeance anymore.*

Oh, she had wanted it. For the first fifty years she had wanted it the way the starving want bread, wholly and without shame. She had composed the burning of the Spire a thousand times, wingbeat by wingbeat, had rehearsed the exact arc of her descent, had chosen which towers to take first and which to leave standing so the survivors would have somewhere to watch from. She had dreamed in ash. She had slept in the shape of her enemies' screaming.

But rage is a fire that burns the one who carries it, and after fifty years she had looked down the length of that road and seen where it ended: a world of cinders, a victory with no one left to share it, and herself alone again — alone as she had always been since the last of her kind fell at Dragonfast — but alone *by choice*, which was a loneliness deeper than any chain.

She did not want to be the last dragon who burned the world.

She wanted — and this want had surprised her when she finally let herself look at it, hunched and shy as a creature new-hatched — she wanted to see the sky again. She wanted to feel wind under her wings. She wanted to land somewhere soft and have someone be glad.

That was all. A century of captivity had boiled her down to that, and she was ashamed of how small it was, and then she was not ashamed, because it was not small at all. It was the largest thing in the world. It was the thing wars were fought over and never named.

* * *

The change came on a night when the Spire above her was full of shouting.

She felt it first as a loosening — the wards that fed on her fire suddenly slack, like a hand releasing its grip. Then the chains. The warded iron did not break; it simply stopped meaning anything, the way a fence stops meaning anything to a river. Magic needs a shape to hold, and the shape they had built to hold her required her fire to complete it, and her fire was gone — carried away north in the chest of a smuggler who had, at the crater rim, done something no one had ever done with dragon-fire in all the long history of dragon-fire.

She had felt it happen. Even half a realm away, even dying, she had felt it: her flame, her stolen heart, refusing the two roads everyone had laid before it — return and rage, or gutter and die — and choosing instead to scatter. To divide itself among a handful of mortal hearts, willingly given, willingly received. Fire spread thin across many hearths instead of hoarded hot in one.

It should have destroyed her, that choice. It was, in the oldest law, a kind of death: the flame would never return to her whole. She would rise from her chains diminished, a candle where she had been a conflagration, her long life shortened to a short one.

She lay in the settling dark of the undercroft and considered this, and found — to her astonishment — that she was smiling. If dragons can be said to smile. Something moved in the region of her mouth, anyway, and the rats fled.

*Diminished,* she thought. *Yes. Good. I have been enormous for three hundred years, and enormity is what made me easy to fear and easier to cage. Let me be small. Small things fit through doors.*

She rose.

It took a long time. A body that has not stood in a hundred years does not remember standing; it must be taught again, patiently, like a foal. Her joints announced themselves one by one, each a small betrayal, each a small triumph when it held. Scales sloughed from her flanks in sheets, gray and dead, and beneath them the old colors showed through — storm-blue and copper, dulled but present, like a painting recovered from under whitewash. Her wings unfolded against the undercroft ceiling and could not open fully, and she did not care. She would fly on half-wings. She would fly on will alone if she had to.

The chains slid from her like shed skins.

Above her, through fathoms of stone, she heard the Spire in uproar — bells, boots, the high thin voice of Sarel Voss rising over it all, and even muffled by rock Ysolde could hear the wrongness in that voice, the hunger dressed as righteousness. Voss had come down to see her only once, years ago, and had stood at the edge of the torchlight and looked at Ysolde the way a merchant looks at ore. Not hatred. Not fear. *Inventory.*

Voss had wanted her dead slowly, milked to the last drop, and then wanted her flame mounted on a weapon and pointed at the realm, and wanted the blame laid on beast-speakers so the realm would beg the Wardens for chains of its own. Ysolde had understood all of it in a single glance, the way one predator reads another, and had thought: *you are what my kind became, little Warden, when we forgot that fire is for warming. You are what we would have won the war and lost everything to become.*

And now Voss was above her, gathering her forces, marching on a free-town full of farmers and hedge-witches and one smuggler with a shard of dragon-heart in her chest.

Ysolde put her shoulder to the undercroft wall.

The wall had stood for a thousand years. It stood for perhaps nine more seconds. Stone burst outward and upward and she climbed through the ruin of it, through the vaults and the barracks levels and the archives, up and up, following the smell of night air the way a drowning creature follows light, and the Spire shook around her, and somewhere far below a Warden recruit dropped his sword and sat down on the stairs and began, very quietly, to pray.

* * *

She broke the surface at midnight, through the roof of the Spire itself, in an eruption of slate and snow and a hundred years of held breath.

The cold hit her like a blessing. The wind hit her like a mother's hand. She stood on the shattered crown of her prison with her wings flung wide and her head thrown back, and the sky — the sky was *there*, vast and wheeling and real, and the clouds tore open above her and the stars came through, every one of them, the ones the wards had eaten a year ago to feed themselves, all returned at once as if they had only been waiting behind a curtain.

Ysolde Stormwing opened her throat and made a sound that had not been heard in the world for a hundred years.

It was not a roar. Roars were for war. This was older than roars — a long, rising, aching note, the call dragons made at dawn in the years before men built walls, the call that meant *I am alive, and the sky holds me, and today is today.* It rolled down the mountains and across the frozen plains and into Havenhollow, where a smuggler with fire in her chest lifted her head from a barricade of carts and said, in a voice gone strange with wonder: "That's her. That's Ysolde. She's *out*."

In the city below, the Wardens poured into the streets and leveled their lances at the sky, and Ysolde looked down at them — at the tiny upturned faces, the trembling spear-points, the officers screaming orders no one followed — and she did not burn them.

That was the miracle, though no one watching would have called it that until much later. Every story they had been told, every nightmare the Wardens had fed them for a century, ended with fire falling from the sky. She could feel the expectation hanging over the city like weather, a hundred thousand people waiting to die or be saved, and she folded her wings — slowly, deliberately, the way one folds a letter — and let the moment stretch until it groaned.

*I am not here for you,* she said, and her voice was thunder made gentle, heard not through ears but through bones, through teeth, through the marrow of every soul in the city. *I am not here for vengeance, though I have earned the right to it a thousand times over. I am not here for war. I spent a hundred years learning what war costs, from underneath. I would not wish the tuition on anyone.*

She stepped off the Spire's crown and fell, and caught the air, and flew.

Half-wings, dulled scales, a heart that would never again hold its whole fire — and still the sky took her, still the wind bore her up, still the old magic woke in her bones like a sleeper hearing its name. She circled the city once, low, and the people below saw her clearly in the starlight: gaunt, scarred, chained-marked, magnificent not despite her wounds but *through* them, the way a candle is most beautiful in a dark room.

*One thing I ask,* she said, as she turned north. *One debt, owed to me by this realm, and I will collect it and consider us square. Stay your High Warden's hand. What she marches on Havenhollow to take — a fire that belongs to many hearts now, freely shared — she cannot seize, because it was never hoarded. But she will try, and trying will kill thousands, and I am too old and too empty to stop her alone.*

*I go north to witness. Not to fight. To witness what the small folk have done with my stolen heart — the thing my whole kind failed to do in three hundred years of burning. And if any of you have ever wondered whether the old stories were true, whether dragons can be anything other than what your Wardens told you—*

She beat her wings, and the downdraft scattered snow from a thousand rooftops like a blessing.

*—then watch me, and remember this night.*

* * *

She flew north through the small hours, and the land unrolled beneath her, and she wept — or did whatever dragons do that is weeping's nearest kin — at the sight of forests she had planted the seeds of, rivers she had drunk from as a hatchling, mountains that still carried the long-weathered scars of Dragonfast. The world had gone on without her. That was the sorrow and the mercy of it, braided together: the world had gone on, and it was still worth going on *to*.

Near dawn she crossed the ridge above Havenhollow and saw the smoke of Voss's army massing in the valley, rank upon rank, siege-engines gleaming, and at the center of it a banner she remembered from the last war, unfurled again, and her old rage stirred in its ashes and she gentled it back down. *Coals,* she reminded herself. *Keep them as coals.*

Below the town, on the crater road, she saw them: five small figures standing in the snow, looking up. The smuggler with her hand pressed flat to her own chest. The broken captain beside her, sword drawn but pointed at the ground. The witch, the mason leaning on a makeshift crutch, the child with snow in her hair and her chin tipped all the way back.

Xanthe Vane raised her hand. Just that. No spell, no plea. A greeting, offered to a creature out of nightmare, by a woman who carried a piece of the creature's heart and had never once asked what it cost.

Ysolde wheeled above them, one slow turn, and sent her voice down soft as falling snow:

*Little keeper of my fire. I have come to see what you have made of it. Show me — quickly now. Your enemy is coming up that road, and she brings enough chains for all of us.*

And far to the south, at the head of the advancing column, Sarel Voss looked up at the dragon crossing the winter sky, and smiled, and gave the order to march.
