## 19. Fire Against Fire

The Spire had no stairs to its crown. It had never needed them.

Xanthe climbed anyway — up through the ruined bell-tower, out onto the shattered shoulder of the tower where the great bronze bells had been melted in their frames a century ago and left hanging like dead wasps' nests, then up the outside of the tower itself, hand over hand on the old ward-chains that had once anchored the border magic to the sky. The chains were cold. They had been cold for a hundred years, and now she knew why, and knowing made every link feel like touching a corpse.

The heart-flame burned behind her ribs.

She had carried it for nine days now, and she had learned its moods the way one learns a lover's breathing in the dark: when it flared at cruelty, when it guttered near iron, when it sang — *sang*, low and wordless, like a hearth-song half-remembered from childhood — whenever she thought of her family below. Brack's slow heartbeat. Pip's quick one. Marisol humming over her charms. Corin, always Corin, steady as a banked coal.

Below her, Havenhollow's bells were silent and the free-town burned in patches, but the fighting had moved here, drawn upward like smoke finding a chimney. She could hear it even now: steel ringing on stone in the Spire's lower yards, Marisol's voice cracking across the walls in words of holding, Brack — *alive, still alive, she could feel him through the flame's strange new sense* — laughing his terrible trollish laugh as he threw a Warden siege-ladder back down the mountainside.

And above her, waiting where the stars should have been, Sarel Voss stood on the empty crown of the world.

* * *

The top of the Spire was a platform of black glass — sand fused by dragon-fire a century ago, the night Ysolde was taken. No railing. No cover. Just a circle of fused glass under an open sky, and the sky itself wrong: starless since the night the wards fell, a ceiling of unbroken dark like the inside of a closed eye.

Voss stood at its center with her hands folded, as if greeting a guest at tea rather than the end of everything.

She did not look like a monster. That was the horror of her. She was a tall woman gone grey at the temples with dignity rather than age, wearing the plain grey-and-silver of the High Wardens without ornament, without vanity. Her face was kind the way a well-made trap is well-made. Only her eyes gave her away — eyes that held light when there was no light to hold, two coals banked behind glass, stolen fire looking out of a stolen face.

"Xanthe Vane," Voss said, and her voice carried in the dead air as if the mountain itself had been asked to listen. "Beast-speaker. Smuggler. The woman who broke my wards."

"You broke your own wards," Xanthe said. Her voice came out steadier than she felt. Behind her ribs the flame pressed outward, eager, and she pressed back. *Not yet. Not for this.* "You starved them. A hundred years of feeding your prison off the border-fires, letting the passes go dark so you'd have an enemy to point at. You killed those stars yourself."

"I managed them." Voss tilted her head, and there was something almost wistful in it. "Do you know what the realm was, before the Wardens? Before the Binding? I've read the accounts. Kingdoms burning each other over dragon-hoards. Beast-speakers raising wolf-packs against villages. Hedge-witches cursing whole valleys over a slighted daughter. Chaos dressed up as freedom." She unfolded her hands. Light moved beneath her skin then, faint as fish under ice. "I have spent thirty years building peace out of fear, and you — a smuggler, a *girl who talks to foxes* — nearly undid it in a season."

"Peace." Xanthe laughed, and hated the sound, because the flame turned her laugh into something with edges. "Your peace is a curfew. Your peace is refugees in chains while the real thief sits in a tower. Your peace is a dragon dying alone in the dark under your feet for a hundred years."

"Yes," Voss said simply. "All of that. And yet no war." She stepped forward, and the glass did not creak under her, though it groaned under anything else. "You think I want the flame to burn things, child. I want it to *end* things. One final fire, lit against whatever comes next — the beast-clans arming in the north, the old pacts waking, every grudge of a thousand years coming due the moment people learn the Wardens lied. Give me the flame and I will make the realm strong enough to survive its own history. Keep it, and we all burn separately."

The terrible thing — the thing Xanthe would carry long after this night — was that some part of her understood. She had felt the flame's rage. She knew what vengeance weighed per ounce. And Voss had built her cathedral of fear on real ruins; the old wars had happened; the cruelties were real.

But understanding was not agreement, and pity was not surrender.

"You keep saying *give me*," Xanthe said. "You stole it first. From a sleeping creature who trusted the oaths your order swore. Everything after that is just theft explaining itself."

Something moved behind Voss's borrowed light. Not anger. Something colder and more dangerous: certainty meeting its first honest mirror.

"You're going to refuse," Voss said. Not a question.

"I'm going to offer you a third choice—"

"There is no third choice." For the first time the High Warden's voice cracked its porcelain, and the light under her skin flared bright enough to throw shadows upward, which was not how shadows worked, which was wrong, wrong the way the starless sky was wrong. "There has never been a third choice! There is power or there is weakness, there is the flame or there is the dark, there is *me* or there is the war! Every wise woman in history learned that lesson or died of it!" She breathed. Smoothed her sleeves. Became kind again, which was worse. "I am sorry, Xanthe. Truly. You might have been useful. Instead you'll be a martyr, and martyrs are so much easier to manage than witnesses."

She raised her hand, and the stolen magic came out of her like a wave breaking off a seawall.

* * *

Later — much later, in the telling — Xanthe would remember the fight not as combat but as an argument conducted in fire.

Voss struck first, and the strike was not a bolt but a *presence*: a fist of compressed ward-light, the same geometry that had caged a dragon, aimed to close around Xanthe's chest and simply *stop her heart*. Xanthe felt the cage-forming before it landed — the flame recognized its old enemy, the binding-shapes, and screamed — and she threw herself sideways off the black glass as the cage snapped shut on empty air with a sound like a mountain biting.

She rolled, came up kneeling, and let the flame answer.

It wanted more than she meant to give. That was the danger she'd been dancing with for nine days: the heart-flame did not distinguish between defense and vengeance, between Voss and the order Voss embodied, between the guilty and everyone who had ever looked away. When she opened herself to it, it rose through her like a tide with opinions, and for one white instant she saw what it wanted to do — unmake the woman in front of her, root and branch and memory, burn the name *Sarel Voss* out of the world's mouth —

*Xanthe.* Corin's voice, remembered, from a campfire on the long road north: *Fire doesn't get to choose what it burns. That's why it needs a keeper.*

*I'm the keeper,* she told the flame, and hauled it back like a dog straining at a leash, and shaped it instead into a single lance of gold-white heat that she drove at Voss's ward-shield.

The collision knocked them both backward. The black glass rang like a bell. Somewhere far below, the fighting paused — she felt hundreds of faces turning upward toward the light bleeding off the Spire's crown, gold against gold, the first true fire the sky had seen since the stars went out.

They circled. The glass smoked under their feet.

"You're holding it back," Voss observed, wiping a thread of blood from her lip. Her shield had a molten scar across it. "Interesting. The dragon never could. Is it love? Is that your leash?" She smiled, and it was almost gentle. "Love is a leash I can cut."

Then she stopped fighting like a Warden and started fighting like what she actually was: a woman who had spent thirty years quietly harvesting every loose scrap of magic in the realm and binding it into herself. She pulled ward-craft out of the air and wore it as armor. She pulled hedge-charms — Xanthe recognized the cadence, Marisol's tradition, *stolen, she steals everything, she even steals our smallness* — and threw them as curses. She pulled something older, something with teeth, and the starless sky above the Spire began to *bend*, darkness gathering into a descending spiral, the absence of stars given shape and appetite.

Xanthe burned through all of it. She had no technique worth the name; she had a dragon's heart and a keeper's grip and a family below her worth surviving for, and it was enough, barely, terribly — each exchange cost her skin and breath and years, and each one cost Voss nothing, because Voss had made herself a treasury and Xanthe was spending a single coin, however vast.

The flame flickered. Guttered. She felt it thinning inside her, felt the countdown she'd been living with accelerate, and understood with cold clarity: Voss didn't need to win. She only needed to wait.

So Xanthe stopped playing the treasury's game.

She stopped attacking the armor and went for the *seams* — the places where stolen magics met, ward-craft against hedge-charm, old teeth against new light. Every theft leaves a scar. Every binding has a join. She found the first seam by feel, the way she'd once found a dying fox in the dark by the sound of its pain, and she put the flame through it like a needle.

Voss screamed.

It was not a dignified sound. It was the sound of a hoard being audited, of thirty years of quiet theft catching fire along its fault lines. The bending dark above unraveled. The armor of many magics cracked into a mosaic of separate lights, each one briefly visible for what it was: *taken*. Taken from witches. Taken from beast-speakers. Taken, thread by golden thread, from a dragon.

"You can't—" Voss gasped, staggering, her composure burning off her like fog. "You cannot win this way, girl. Even broken, I am stronger than you. Even bleeding, I have more fire than you will ever—"

"That's the thing," Xanthe said, and her voice came out quiet, and the quiet carried further than any shout. "You keep counting. Fire isn't a count. It's a *hearth*. You built a vault. I built a home."

She raised her hand — not to strike. To *hold open*. The flame rose in her chest, and she did not aim it, did not leash it, did not spend it. She offered it forward into the space between them, blazing, and said:

"Come and take it, then. If you can hold what refuses to be held."

Because that was the truth she had learned in the crater and the guttering and the long dark road: the heart-flame lived only in willing vessels. It had lain quiet for a century in a vial because a vial does not consent. It had raged in Xanthe because she consented *imperfectly*, gripping it like a secret. But offered freely, openly, to a woman who took everything by force—

Voss reached for it. Of course she did. Greed was the load-bearing wall of her entire soul.

And the flame, feeling the shape of her reaching — the taking, the counting, the vault — recoiled like a hand from a stove, and in recoiling tore itself another inch free of the last binding Voss had woven around it, and the High Warden's own stolen fire turned in her veins like a knife being sharpened from the inside.

Voss fell to one knee on the black glass. The light under her skin strobed, wild, unbound, thirty years of theft trying to become thirty years of debt.

"Now," Xanthe breathed, "we finish the argument."

* * *

That was when the hatch at the tower's shoulder burst open, and Corin Ashe came up the last chain with blood on his face and his broken badge still hanging at his throat like an accusation, and behind him — impossible, glorious — Pip, small and singed and grinning like the end of the world was a dare.

"The stairs were guarded," Pip announced, to no one, to everyone. "So we used the window."

"Pip—" Xanthe's heart did something painful and enormous. "You were supposed to stay below."

"You were supposed to come back down." The child shrugged. "Everyone's disappointed tonight."

Corin reached her side, breathing hard, sword out, placing himself between her and the kneeling Voss with the easy inevitability of a man who had stopped choosing this and simply *was* it. Below them, the sounds of battle were changing — Marisol's rallying cry rolling along the walls, the hedge-witches answering, the Wardens' line buckling as the townsfolk of Havenhollow refused, publicly and at great cost, to hand over their guests.

"It's over, Sarel," Corin said. His voice was the voice Xanthe had fallen for: level, tired, immovable. "Your elite are routed. Your lie is on every wind. Ysolde is rising, and the whole realm is about to watch a dragon choose mercy. Put it down."

Voss lifted her head. Blood ran from her nose, her ears, her eyes — the price of a hoard foreclosing. And she *laughed*.

"Choose mercy," she whispered. "Oh, children. You think this is the end." Her hand closed around a shard of her own broken bindings — a splinter of the cage-magic, dense and dark and hungry. "The dragon rises. Yes. And when she finds her flame split among *you* — scattered, diluted, mortal — do you truly believe a century of captivity will look like gratitude?" She pushed herself upright, swaying, terrible, her borrowed light guttering around her like a storm lantern in wind. "I don't need to win tonight. I only need you to *choose*. Return the flame and arm a war. Keep it and watch it gutter. Either way, the age of fear continues, and I have already won—"

Xanthe looked at the flame in her chest. At Corin, whose hand found hers without either of them deciding it. At Pip, fierce and small. At the family below, holding the walls. At the starless sky, where — far off, impossibly, over the mountains — a single point of light had just appeared. Then another. Stars, returning, one by one, like a city lighting its windows at dusk.

"No," Xanthe said. "You've already lost. You just haven't understood the arithmetic yet."

She turned to Corin, and to Pip, and called down through the flame-link to the others — Brack bleeding but breathing, Marisol with her arms raised and shaking, all of them hers, all of them *willing* — and began, at last, to speak the third path aloud.

Voss lunged, shard raised, screaming that there was no third path, that there had never been—

—and the sky, overhead, filled with wings.
