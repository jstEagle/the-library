## 20. The Third Path

The wind at the top of the Spire tasted of ash and old iron.

Xanthe stood on the broken crown of the tower with the heart-flame burning in her chest, and the whole world lay beneath her like a map drawn in smoke. Far below, the city of Thornfell sprawled in the dark — curfew-fires guttering in the streets, Warden horns sounding and falling silent, the bells of the low quarters ringing in panic or defiance or both. The stars were still gone. They had been gone for a season now, and she had almost forgotten what it was to look up and see anything but a black seam of sky.

Sarel Voss rose from her knees across the shattered flagstones, and Xanthe felt the flame recoil inside her like a living thing pressing away from a door.

The High Warden's stolen magic smoked off her shoulders in grey ribbons. Whatever Corin and the others had done below — whatever price they had paid to carve a path up through three hundred feet of Warden steel — had cost Voss something too. Her white cloak was scorched black at the hem. Her beautiful face, the face that had charmed confessions out of children and pardons out of kings, was cracked along one cheekbone like glazed pottery, and through the crack shone a light that was not hers.

"You feel it," Voss said. She did not shout. She never shouted; that was the horror of her. "You feel how much there is. A hundred years of hoarded fire, and you're carrying all of it in one small body." She took a step forward, and the flagstones hissed under her boots. "It will eat you alive, beast-speaker. It ate Ysolde. It will eat me too, someday, and I have made my peace with that. But first—" her hand opened, palm up, and the grey ribbons of magic coiled toward Xanthe like smoke finding a chimney, "—first it will be *mine*."

"It isn't yours," Xanthe said. "It was never anyone's. That's the whole of it, Voss. That's what you never understood."

She thought of Ysolde then — she could not help it; the dragon's presence brushed against the flame in her chest like a great wing against a window. *One vessel,* the dragon had told her, in the undercroft a lifetime ago, when Xanthe had knelt in the dark and promised her freedom. *One vessel is a prison. One vessel is a war waiting to be born.* The dragons had fallen because each of them carried their fire alone, vast and singular and therefore covetable, killable, stealable. And men like Sarel Voss had risen because they understood that singularity. They built vaults around single flames. They built empires on the idea that power must live somewhere it can be taken.

Xanthe looked down.

They had come for her. Of course they had come for her. Corin stood at the base of the last stair, sword drawn, holding the landing against two Wardens who would not stay down. Marisol crouched behind him, hands shaking, her reserves burned down to embers from the vault theft and the siege and everything since — a hedge-witch running on nothing but stubbornness. Brack was not there. Brack was lying in the courtyard below with a Warden spear-wound through his shoulder and Marisol's last healing poultice packed into it, alive because trolls were hard to kill and because he had held the gate so that the rest of them could climb.

And Pip — Pip was halfway up the outside of the Spire itself, clinging to the old lightning-rod chains, having refused — *refused*, in that flat, furious, eleven-year-old way — to wait below. The child's face appeared over the parapet now, dirt-streaked and wide-eyed, and Xanthe's heart broke and mended in the same breath.

Her family. Ragged, bleeding, impossible. Strangers a season ago. Strangers who had chosen each other in waystations and burned granaries and snowbound passes, who had traded pieces of themselves back and forth until none of them could say where one ended and the next began.

And the flame in her chest roared and raged and whispered of vengeance, and Xanthe finally, finally understood what it was asking for.

Not a throne. Not a war. It was asking what every captive thing asks: *let me out. Let me be more than one shape.*

"Corin!" she called, and her voice came out strange, doubled, ringing with harmonics that were not entirely human. "Marisol! Pip! Come here. All of you. Now."

Voss laughed. It was a lovely laugh, warm as a hearth, and utterly empty. "Calling your little found family to watch you die? How touching. I'll make it quick."

"I'm not dying," Xanthe said. "I'm *sharing*."

She reached into the fire.

It fought her, of course. It was rage and hunger and a century of captivity, and it poured visions into her skull like molten metal — Ysolde's fall, Thornfell burning, Voss's cracked face split open like an egg, the Wardens' spires toppling one by one into the sea. Xanthe let the visions come. She had spent a season learning this flame's language, and she knew now that its fury was only grief wearing armor. Grief for a sky full of dragons. Grief for a world that had caged its own wonder and called it safety.

*I know,* she told it, in the wordless tongue she had used with foxes and rats and dying things all her life. *I know. But we don't get to burn the world because the world hurt us. We get to find the ones who'll hold us.*

The flame hesitated. And in that hesitation, Xanthe did the thing no dragon had ever done, no Warden had ever imagined, and no thief had ever dared:

She began to pull it apart.

* * *

It hurt. She would remember that afterward, when memory was all she had left of it — that it hurt like being unmade, like pulling her own heartbeat into strands. The flame resisted division the way a river resists being forked, roaring, thrashing, flooding her mouth with light until she tasted copper and starless sky. Voss screamed something — a command, a threat, perhaps her name — but it reached Xanthe from very far away, through water, through wool.

Because the moment the first strand separated, the flame *changed*.

It stopped fighting her.

It had been one thing for a hundred years. One heart in one dark room, one vast loneliness wearing the shape of a fire. And now, for the first time since the Wardens had cut it from Ysolde's chest, it was becoming *many* — and the manyness sang through it like dawn through frost. Xanthe felt it understand, all at once, with the simple overwhelming joy of a creature released, what she was offering. Not a new cage. Not even freedom into the wild dark, where it would gutter alone.

A hearth. Many hearths. Hands that would hold it not because they owned it but because they had been asked.

"Xanthe." Corin's voice, close now, ragged with fear. He had abandoned the stair; he was crossing the broken crown toward her with blood on his jaw and terror written plain across the face she loved, the face that had once arrested her at spear-point and then, in a burned granary, chosen treason instead. "Whatever you're doing — your eyes are *on fire*, love. Tell me what to do."

"Hold out your hands," she said.

He didn't hesitate. That was the miracle of Corin Ashe, disgraced captain, traitor twice over: he never hesitated when it mattered. He dropped his sword — the sword of a Warden captain, the badge he'd lost and the duty he'd carried, clattering on the stone — and opened his arms.

The first shard went into him like a spark into dry tinder.

He gasped. His knees buckled. Xanthe caught him, and through her palms she felt it settle into him — not consuming, not commanding, just *warming*. A thread of dragonfire winding itself around his steady soldier's heart, and his steadiness winding back around it, so that the rage in the flame met, for the first time in a century, a man who had spent his whole life learning to hold his temper like a weapon he chose not to draw.

"Oh," Corin breathed. His eyes were wide, lit gold from within. "Oh, it's — Xanthe, it's *alive*, it's—"

"Yours," she said. "If you want it. Only if you want it."

"I want it." He laughed, wet and disbelieving. "Gods help me, I want it."

* * *

Marisol came next, because Marisol understood cost better than any of them.

The hedge-witch limped forward, gray-faced, her own magic spent down to fumes, and when Xanthe explained — quickly, in fragments, the flame pouring out of her faster than words could keep pace — Marisol's chin lifted in that defiant tilt Xanthe had first seen in Greytooth Pass, when a woman with nothing but herbs and nerve had stepped between Wardens and a stranger.

"A shard of dragonfire," Marisol said. "In a hedge-witch. In *me*." She barked a laugh. "Every hedge-coven from here to the sea has prayed for a fraction of that. And you're just — handing it out? To a woman who poisons rats for coppers?"

"To family," Xanthe said.

Marisol's defiance crumbled into something younger and rawer. She had spoken once, around a campfire on the long road north, of the coven that had cast her out for healing the wrong person, of thirty years of practicing magic alone in kitchens and barns, powerful enough to matter and poor enough to be ignored. She held out her hands now the way a starving woman holds out a bowl.

The second shard sank into her, and Marisol *bloomed*. Light ran up her arms like sap in springtime; her spent reserves flared back to life and then past them, kindled by something older and vaster than hedge-craft. She wept without seeming to notice, laughing, saying words in the old country tongue that Xanthe didn't know but understood perfectly anyway.

Pip didn't wait to be asked.

The child came over the parapet like a squirrel, dropped the last six feet, and planted herself in front of Xanthe with her fists on her hips and her chin trembling only slightly. "I heard," she said. "I've got good ears, everyone knows it. You're giving bits of the dragon to people." Her eyes — sharp, streetwise, starved for anything that had ever belonged to her — fixed on the light streaming from Xanthe's chest. "I want one."

"Pip." Xanthe crouched. "This isn't a locket. It might be heavy. It might be *scary*. It's a century of grief and rage, and—"

"I've been carrying worse since I was seven," Pip said flatly. "I slept in a drain, lady. I watched my brother get taken by the press-gangs. Give it here."

And what could Xanthe do, faced with that? She gave it gently — gentler than the others, a thin bright thread, a candle-flame's worth of dragon — and Pip gasped and staggered and then stood very still, her small face transfigured, tears cutting clean lines through the grime.

"It's warm," Pip whispered. "Nothing's ever been warm *on purpose* before."

* * *

Brack could not climb the Spire. So Xanthe sent the fourth shard down to him.

She cupped it in her hands — a knot of fire the size of a robin's egg, pulsing like a patient heart — and spoke to it as she had spoken to foxes and falcons and frightened things all her life, and the flame, four-fifths family now, listened. It leapt from her palms and poured down over the parapet like golden rain, down the Spire's flank, through arrow-slits and broken windows, and far below she felt it find him: felt, through the link they now shared, Brack the stonemason open his eyes in the courtyard, feel the warmth sink into his wounded chest beside Marisol's poultices, and smile his slow, tectonic smile.

Four shards. Four hearths. And the last of the flame — the largest piece, the oldest, the part that remembered the sky — remained in Xanthe's chest, and it was *singing*.

"No," Sarel Voss said.

Xanthe turned. She had almost forgotten the High Warden, and that, she realized, was the whole victory in miniature. Voss stood where she had stood, but her composure was gone, cracked through like her cheekbone, and the grey stolen magic around her writhed with something that in a lesser creature would have been called panic.

"No," Voss said again, louder. "You can't — power doesn't *work* that way. Power is *held*. Power is taken and kept and defended, that is the entire architecture of the world, that is why the Wardens exist, that is why *I*—"

"That's why you'll lose," Xanthe said quietly.

She walked toward Voss, and the last shard of the heart-flame rose with her, streaming from her chest like a banner. "You built everything on taking. Take the flame, take the ward-magic, take the blame and give it to refugees, take a child's fear in a cell and turn it into a confession. And every single thing you took, you had to hold alone — because the moment you set it down, someone like you would pick it up." She stopped an arm's length away. Up close, the light shining through the crack in Voss's face was cold and colorless, and Xanthe pitied her, and the pity was the sharpest blade she had. "You wanted a fire you could seize. Look around, Voss. There's nothing left to seize. It's in a soldier who chooses mercy. It's in a witch who heals for coppers. It's in a child who slept in a drain and still shares her bread. Try reaching into *that*."

Voss reached.

Xanthe saw it happen — saw the High Warden's stolen magic lash out, grey and grasping, plunging toward the scattered fires in Corin and Marisol and Pip the way a hand plunges toward a hearth-log to snatch it from the grate. And the fires simply... declined. Each shard turned in its bearer's chest like a fish in deep water, slipping the grasp, and the grasp tightened on emptiness, and the emptiness pulled.

That was the thing about hoarded power. It had no roots. It had never been given, only taken, and so it had never learned the one trick that mattered — the trick every found family knows without knowing it knows it — which is how to be let go of and come back.

Voss's stolen century tore loose all at once.

The grey light ripped out of her in a silent, screaming torrent — a hundred years of harvested ward-fire and dragon-embers and magics whose owners' names were lost — and it had nowhere to go, nothing to anchor to, no vessel willing to hold it. It dispersed. It thinned. It went out like breath on a winter window, and Sarel Voss, High Warden of Thornfell, architect of holy wars, stood suddenly, terribly ordinary on the broken crown of her Spire — a woman in a scorched cloak with a cracked face and empty hands.

She swayed. Corin moved, fast, catching her arm — because he was Corin, because even now — and Voss looked at his hand on her sleeve with an expression Xanthe would carry to her grave: not hatred. Bewilderment. The pure, childlike bewilderment of a woman discovering, at the very end, that the world had been playing a different game than she had.

"You should have taken it," Voss said to Xanthe. Her voice was small. "All of it. You could have been *unassailable*."

"I could have been you," Xanthe agreed.

Below them, boots on the stairs — Warden boots, dozens — rising toward their fallen commander. Corin's grip tightened on Voss's arm, and Xanthe saw the arithmetic run behind his eyes: arrest her, claim the victory, maybe buy mercy for all of them. The old dream. Reinstatement. An oath restored.

Then he looked at Xanthe, and at Pip, and at Marisol glowing like a lantern, and he let go of Voss's arm and stepped back to stand at Xanthe's side, plainly, publicly, where every Warden on those stairs could see him.

"Let her stand trial," Corin said, as the first helmets crested the stairwell. "Before the realm. Before the survivors of Havenhollow. Let *everyone* see what the High Warden was." His voice rang off the stones, and the gold light in his chest rang with it. "And write this down, all of you. The ward-fall wasn't a beast-speaker's crime. The wards died because you fed them on a prisoner's heart and called it duty. The dragon didn't betray Thornfell. *You* did. Every one of you who knew and kept the secret."

The stairwell was silent except for breathing.

And into that silence, from beyond the Spire's edge, came a sound that none of them — not the Wardens, not the family, not even Xanthe, who had been waiting for it with her whole soul — was truly prepared for.

Wings.

* * *

Ysolde rose.

She came up the side of the Spire like a mountain deciding to move, vast and scarred and gaunt, a hundred years of captivity hanging from her bones — and she was not burning. Xanthe had feared this moment above all others: the freed dragon, the avenging fire, the ancient war reborn in a single column of flame. But Ysolde climbed the night sky slowly, wings beating once for every ten heartbeats, and her eyes — ancient, amber, bottomless — swept across the crown of the Spire and found Xanthe standing amid the scattered light of her own divided heart.

Through the link — thinner now, a thread instead of a rope — Xanthe felt what the dragon felt.

She felt the four shards like four small suns on the horizon of her awareness. She felt that the ache in her chest, the amputation that had defined her century, had changed shape: the fire was gone from her, yes, but it was not *destroyed*, and it was not *owned*, and it was not *hers to mourn*. It was out in the world, in willing hearts, doing what fire had always longed to do.

Warming things.

Ysolde circled the Spire once, twice, her shadow sweeping over the upturned faces of Wardens and rebels alike, and when she finally landed — folding herself onto the ruined crown with a delicacy impossible for something the size of a cathedral — she lowered her great head until one ancient eye hovered an arm's length from Xanthe, and the eye was wet.

*Little speaker,* the dragon said, and her voice was thunder wrapped in velvet. *What have you done?*

Xanthe's legs finally gave out. Corin caught her. From somewhere impossibly far away, she heard herself answer — heard the words come out slurred and half-laughing, the exhaustion of a season finally collecting its debt:

"Gave it away. All of it. Every last—"

The Spire tilted. The starless sky wheeled. The last thing she saw before the dark took her was Ysolde's eye closing, slow as a sunset, and the dragon's head bowing over her — not in menace, not in judgment —

in something that looked, impossibly, like gratitude.

And the last thing she felt, sinking down through the warmth of Corin's arms and the small fierce grip of Pip's hand finding hers, was the flame — her shard, the final fifth, still flickering in her failing body — beginning, very gently, to dim.
