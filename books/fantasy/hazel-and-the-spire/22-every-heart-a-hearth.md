## 22. Every Heart a Hearth

The first star came back on a Tuesday.

Xanthe knew it was a Tuesday because Pip kept the calendar now — chalked on the slate above the kitchen door in a child's careful hand, each day crossed off with a flourish that got more extravagant as the week wore on. *Tuesdy*, it said, half-erased and rewritten twice, because Pip had opinions about spelling that no one had the heart to correct.

She was hanging wash when it happened. The line ran from the eaves of the new house to the lowest branch of the hearth-tree, which had grown — impossibly, wonderfully — from an acorn Marisol had pressed into the ash of the Spire's dungeons a year ago. It stood taller than the roof now, its leaves a strange bronze-green that caught the light like hammered copper, and in the evenings, if you sat beneath it with your palm flat against the bark, you could feel something warm moving in the wood. Not magic, exactly. Not anymore. Just warmth. The way a stone holds the sun after sundown.

The star appeared low in the east, over the mountains where Greytooth Pass cut its old scar, and it did not flicker or fade or apologize for being gone. It simply was there, one bright point in a sky that had been blank for a year, and Xanthe dropped the washbasket and stood in the wet grass with her arms wrapped around herself and her mouth open like a fool, and she did not say anything at all, because there was nothing to say to a star except *welcome back*.

By full dark there were eleven of them. By midnight, dozens. By the time Pip tumbled out of bed and into the yard in bare feet, shrieking loud enough to wake Brack three fields over, the whole river of the heavens had refilled itself, silver on black, so many stars that the darkness between them looked almost blue.

"They came back," Pip said, out of breath, gripping Xanthe's sleeve with fingers still sticky from some midnight theft of honey bread. "Xanthe. *Xanthe.* They came back."

"I see them, love."

"Do you think they know?"

"Know what?"

Pip squinted up, considering. "That we kept the fire going. So they'd have something to come home to."

Xanthe's throat went tight. She put her arm around the child's shoulders and they stood together in the cold grass, two small warm shapes under an enormous returning sky, and somewhere behind them the hearth-tree creaked its slow bronze-green creak, and the fire in the kitchen — banked but never out, never out, not once in a year — breathed through the chimney in a long contented sigh.

* * *

The fox came back in autumn.

It had been coming all along, if she was honest — glimpses at the tree line, a rust-colored shape at the edge of the barley field, amber eyes watching the house with an attention that felt less like animal curiosity and more like visitation. But in the weeks after the stars returned, the fox grew bold. It would sit at the fence post while Xanthe worked the garden, tail curled neat over its paws, and watch her with the particular patience of an old friend waiting for you to finish telling a story you've already told too many times.

And here was the thing — the thing she had not told anyone, not even Corin, because saying it aloud felt like tempting grief:

She could almost understand it again.

Not words. Her gift was gone; she had poured it out with everything else, ground it into the shards of flame she had pressed into six willing hearts, and what remained in her was silence where the world's voices used to be. When a bird sang now, she heard only a bird. When the horses blew through their nostrils at dawn, she heard only horses. She had mourned that silence through the whole grey end of winter, quietly, the way you mourn a limb.

But the fox — the fox made her feel things. Not heard. *Felt*. A pressure against her mind like a hand laid flat between her shoulder blades: steady, dry, faintly amused. When the fox was near, Xanthe knew without knowing how that it was well, that it remembered her, that it found her human fussing — the garden, the wash, the endless bread — fundamentally ridiculous and forgivable anyway.

"You're doing it again," Corin said one evening, leaning on the gate beside her. The fox sat on its post ten feet away, pretending with great dignity to be part of the landscape.

"Doing what?"

"That thing where you stare at each other and then you laugh at nothing."

"It wasn't nothing."

"What did it say, then?" He asked it gently, every time, though he knew the answer. He always asked gently. That was Corin — a man who had spent thirty years learning how to hold a sword and one year learning how to set it down, and who turned out to be better at the second.

"It didn't *say*—" Xanthe stopped. Started again. "It said the apples are ready. The ones on the south tree. And that I let them go too long last year and it was a disgrace."

Corin looked at the fox. The fox looked at Corin with an expression of such perfect innocence that Xanthe burst out laughing.

"Pip swears you understand," he said. "Pip tells everyone. Half the village thinks you're a witch, and the other half thinks you're a saint, and Marisol says both halves are wrong and you're just stubborn."

"Marisol would know."

"She would." He reached over and took her hand, callused palm to callused palm, and they watched the light go gold and then grey over the barley, and the fox watched them watching, and none of it needed saying. That was the year's great lesson, the one the flame had taught her as it left her: love does not need a language. It only needs a hearth.

* * *

They had built the house themselves, all of them, badly and then less badly, in the free-town shadow of Havenhollow where the walls had held and the people had chosen each other. Brack had quarried the foundation stones himself from the crater's rim — "good stone," he'd rumbled, "stone that's seen worse than us" — and carried beams that should have taken four oxen on one shoulder, humming his gravelly mason's songs until the whole frame stood square and true. Marisol had warded the chimneys and cursed the moles and brewed remedies for every scraped knee in the valley, charging most patients in gossip. Pip had stolen, by conservative estimate, a third of the nails.

Ysolde had come to the raising. That was a day Xanthe kept folded somewhere behind her ribs, to take out on hard evenings. The dragon had flown up from the rebuilt Spire — no longer a prison, its undercrofts opened to light and air, its dungeons now a school — and circled the valley once, wings wide enough to shade the whole harvest field, and landed with a gentleness that still seemed impossible for a creature whose footprints filled with rain. She had lowered her great grey head and breathed across the unfinished roof-beams, one long slow exhalation, and the wood had seasoned in an afternoon what should have taken a summer.

*Xanthe-of-the-many-hearts,* Ysolde had said — and Xanthe had felt it, even giftless, the way you feel thunder in your chest — *you have done what no dragon and no Warden and no god ever managed. You have made fire humble.*

Then the dragon had curled around the half-built house like a cat around a saucer of milk, and Pip had climbed onto her nose and declared it the best climbing tree in the world, and Ysolde had endured this with the weary majesty of a grandmother, and Brack had wept openly into his mortar, and it had been, Xanthe thought, the single finest day of her life, including the days she'd previously been certain were the finest days of her life.

Now Ysolde came at each turn of the season, no more, no less. Guardian of the realm by the new oaths — the Wardens' reformed order, sworn to service instead of suspicion, their badges recast with an open flame instead of a closed fist — but she guarded the way a mountain guards: by being there. Children rode her back at festivals. Farmers left her offerings of cattle that had died naturally, which she accepted with grave courtesy and ate in private, having some dignity left.

"She asks after you," Corin said, the night the stars returned, when the whole household lay out in the yard on blankets because no one could bear to sleep indoors under a sky like that. "Every visit. She asks if you are well, and whether the fire in your chest still troubles you."

"It doesn't trouble me." Xanthe laced her fingers through his. Above them the stars wheeled, thick as spilled salt. "It's quiet now. All of it. Even the shard I kept."

Because she had kept one. The smallest sliver, the size of a grain of wheat, the piece that would not leave her — that had sunk into the place where her beast-speech used to live and settled there like an ember in a banked grate. She hadn't chosen it. Or maybe she had, in the way you choose the person you can't stop loving: not with decision but with recognition. Six shards for six hearts — hers, Corin's, Marisol's, Brack's, Pip's, and the fox's, given through a touch of palms to a small wild skull, because the family had voted and the fox had been there and it had seemed obvious, afterward, that it had always been obvious.

Voss had never understood it. Xanthe thought about that sometimes, more charitably than she'd expected to. Sarel Voss had wanted one flame in one body, hoarded, weaponized, *hers* — and when the fire scattered itself across seven unremarkable hearts in an unremarkable valley, she had reached for it and grasped nothing, because you cannot seize what is freely held, and her own borrowed magic had guttered in her hands like a candle in a doorway. They said she wept at the end. Xanthe hoped it was true. Weeping would have meant there was still someone in there to save.

"What does it feel like?" Pip asked now, rolling over on the blanket, chin propped on both hands. "Your bit of fire. Does it hurt?"

"No, love."

"Does it do anything?"

Xanthe considered. Below the hearth-tree, the fox had emerged from the shadows and lain down in the grass, tail over nose, one ear cocked toward the conversation with shameless interest.

"It keeps me warm," she said. "When everyone else is cold, I'm not. And when I'm frightened—" she smiled, "—it reminds me I'm not alone. That's all it does. That's everything it does."

Pip nodded solemnly, as though this were a satisfactory answer, and rolled back over to count stars, and Corin's thumb moved slowly across Xanthe's knuckles, and the night went on being enormous and kind.

* * *

Winter came early that year, the first real winter since the wards fell — snow deep enough to squeak, the kind that shut roads and opened kitchens. The whole valley ended up at their table more nights than not: hedge-witches and stonemasons and reformed Wardens and the widow Tatchett with her six goats and her nine opinions, until Marisol started cooking for twenty as a matter of course and Brack built the second table without being asked.

On the longest night of the year, they hung lanterns in the hearth-tree.

It had been Marisol's idea, then Pip's demand, then everyone's quiet project for a fortnight — copper and glass lanterns, each one holding a candle, hung from the bronze-green branches until the tree burned overhead like a green and golden constellation of its own. The village came out to see it. Ysolde came out to see it, lying in the snowy field beyond the fence with her chin on her forepaws, her ancient eyes reflecting forty small flames, and when Pip asked her if she liked it, the dragon was silent a long moment.

*A hundred years ago,* she said at last, and her voice moved through the snow like heat through iron, *I was the last fire in the world, and I was in a cage, and I believed that was how fire was meant to be. Kept. Guarded. Owned.* Her breath melted a circle in the frost. *You have taught me otherwise, little thief. Fire is not meant to be kept. It is meant to be shared, and tended, and passed hand to hand until no one remembers whose it was in the beginning.*

Then she sang — low, wordless, a sound like the mountains remembering — and the lanterns swayed, and the snow fell soft through the light, and Xanthe stood in the yard with her hand on Corin's arm and the ember warm under her ribs and the fox a small bright-eyed shape at her feet, and she thought: *this. Whatever the flame was, whatever it cost, it was for this.*

Later, when the guests had gone home through the blue snow-dark and the household had drifted to bed one by one — Brack's snore like distant quarrying, Marisol's cat asleep in the woodbox, Pip smuggled into the big bed between them as happened most nights now, though officially they were much too old for that — Xanthe lay awake a while, listening.

The fire in the kitchen ticked and settled. The tree creaked. Somewhere far off, a wolf sang to the returned stars, and she heard it as just a wolf, just a song, beautiful and mute and enough.

But under her breastbone, small and patient, the ember pulsed — once, twice — like a heartbeat keeping time with another heartbeat, somewhere out in the white dark. And she smiled at the ceiling of the house they had built, in the town that had held, under the sky that had come home, and let the warmth have the last word.

In the morning there would be bread to bake and apples to store and a fox on the fence post waiting to be laughed at, and Pip would cross another day off the slate with scandalous flourish, and none of it would be magic, and all of it would be.

The stars stayed. The fire stayed. Every heart a hearth, and every hearth lit, and the long road north — the long road everywhere — warm all the way down.
