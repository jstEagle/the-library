## 1. The Night the Stars Went Out

The mule went first.

That was how Lena Amberdale would remember it, afterward—the small things before the large ones, the way a house comes down: not all at once but in the order of its weakness. The mule, old Bramble, stopped mid-stride on the switchback above the Greywater ford and would not move again, no matter how Halvden cursed or tugged or pressed his shoulder against her flank. He was a big man, Halvden, a carter with forearms like knotted rope, and when even he could not shift her, Lena knew to listen. Animals heard what people had trained themselves out of hearing.

She stood at the head of the line, lantern raised, and looked up.

It was past midnight in the high passes of Thornfell, the kind of hour where the mountains stopped being geography and became something closer to architecture—black walls rising on either side of the trail, their summits lost, their faces faintly silvered by moonlight that came down the valley like water finding its level. The cargo run was a good one, an honest one as these things went: salt pork, iron nails, lamp oil, a crate of midwife's tools bound for the villages past the divide, where winter came early and stayed late and the nearest garrison was three days' ride and did not care. Six mules, two carts, four hired hands who grumbled about the cold but took her coin without questions, because Lena's coin never came with questions attached. That was the arrangement. That was the whole of her reputation, built over seven careful years: *the woman who gets cargo through*, nothing more, nothing less. No name spoken above a murmur. No history asked after.

Seven years ago there had been a different name, and an order, and a god.

But that was seven years ago, and the stars were still out, and she had work to do.

"Leave her," Lena said quietly, and set her hand on Bramble's neck. The mule's hide twitched under her palm like something swimming just beneath the surface of it. Her ears were pinned flat. Her breath came fast and shallow, and her eyes rolled white toward the dark above the trail—not toward the carts, not toward the men, not toward the smell of wolf that sometimes haunted this stretch in autumn, but *up*. At nothing. At the empty black.

"She'll come round," Halvden said. "She always does."

"Not tonight." Lena lifted her hand away. "Cut her loose from the trace. We'll walk her through."

He grumbled, but he did it, because he had learned—in the way of men who worked for her—that when Lena Amberdale said a thing softly, it was already decided. The harness buckles clinked in the cold. Somewhere below them the Greywater muttered over its stones, a sound like someone turning in sleep.

And then the first star went out.

Lena saw it happen the way you see a candle die in another room: not the flame itself but the absence arriving, a small hole punched in the corner of her eye. One moment the sky above the eastern ridge held its familiar scatter of lights—the Shepherd's Yoke hanging low over the pass, the long pale river of them that the villagers called the Road of Souls—and the next, a single point simply wasn't. Not dimmed. Not clouded. There had been no cloud that night; the air was knife-clean, the kind of cold that made the lungs ache and the stars burn hard. It was simply gone, the way a word is gone from your mouth the instant you finish saying it.

She told herself it was a meteor that had finished falling. She told herself her eyes were tired. She told herself the mountain had moved, which mountains did not do, but which she preferred to every other explanation available to her.

Then a second star went out. Then a third and fourth, close together, near the Yoke's handle-star, and this time Halvden saw it too, because he stopped cursing mid-word and stood with his mouth open, and Tam the younger dropped a coil of rope he'd been coiling for ten minutes without dropping it once.

"What is that," Tam said. Not a question. Questions wanted answers, and there were none, and everyone on that trail understood instinctively that they had stepped out of the country where answers lived.

"They're going out," the older hand, Merrit, said. He was sixty if he was a day, missing two fingers to a snare from his poaching years, and he had crossed these passes in every season for forty of them. "Stars don't go out. I've seen eclipses, I've seen the northern lights boil red as a wound, I've seen comets with tails across half the sky. Stars *don't go out*." He said it the way a man testifies. "Not like that. Not one by one, west to east, like something walking along a shelf and pinching candles."

West to east. Lena made herself look, made herself count, because counting was what she did instead of praying—had done, ever since prayer stopped working. The extinguishment moved in a line, orderly as a harvest, each star winking out a heartbeat after its neighbor, and the darkness behind it did not fill back in. It stayed. The sky was being erased in strips, like paint scraped from plaster, and beneath the scraped places there was not deeper sky, not the greater dark full of lesser lights that sailors navigated by. There was only flat, absolute, featureless black, a black that had texture somehow, a black that seemed to sit *closer* than the sky should sit, pressing down on the ridgelines like a lid.

The moon lasted longer. It hung in the southwest, gibbous and cold, throwing its thin silver down the valley, and for a while the extinguishing line crawled toward it the way weather crawls, slow and inevitable, and Lena found herself watching it with the detached attention of a woman watching someone else's funeral procession pass. Then the line reached it, and the moonlight did not fade—it *stopped*, cut off like a door closing on a lit room, and the valley dropped into a darkness so complete that the lanterns, all six of them strung along the cart train, suddenly seemed to carve holes in the world rather than light it.

No one spoke. Even the wind held off, as though it too were waiting to see what happened next.

Lena became aware, slowly and then all at once, of two things.

The first was the silence.

Her god had been silent for seven years. She had gotten used to it the way you get used to a missing tooth—the tongue keeps returning to the gap, learning its shape, until the shape becomes part of you. Vess, the Quiet God, the Unseen Hand, keeper of Thornfell's borders and patron of everyone whose survival depended on not being seen: his silence had been the constant weather of her adult life. She had prayed into it every night for the first year, then every week, then only on the road, then only when she was frightened, and finally not at all, because praying into silence is a kind of bleeding and she had needed her blood for other things. The order had taught her that Vess spoke in stillness, in the space between heartbeats, in the moment a sentry looks away. Seven years of nothing had felt, at worst, like abandonment. At best, like a god sleeping.

This was not that. This silence had weight. It arrived the way deep water arrives when you dive past the point where light gives up—a pressure on the ears, a flattening, a sense of vast attention withdrawn all at once from somewhere very far overhead. The stars going out and the silence deepening were the same event. She understood that without knowing how she understood it, the way you understand a trap has sprung by the click, not by the teeth.

The second thing was the scar.

Left wrist, inner side, just below where the pulse lives. Every sworn servant of Vess carried one—a thin white line, the mark of the oath-knife, given in the shrine at Harrowgate the day she turned nineteen, when she had knelt on cold stone and sworn her sight, her hands, and her silence to the god of the hidden borders. For seven years it had been exactly what it appeared: a scar. Dead tissue. A souvenir.

Now it was cold.

Not cold like skin in mountain air. Cold like a key left out overnight in January—cold that radiated, that had a center, that sat in her wrist the way a stone sits in a shoe. She wrapped her fingers around it without deciding to, the way you grab a railing when the floor tilts, and the cold climbed. Up the forearm, into the elbow, settling behind the sternum like a swallowed chip of ice. Her breath came out visible even by lantern-light, and for a moment she honestly could not tell whether she was shivering from the temperature or from something else, some other kind of temperature entirely, some thermometer in her that read a scale she hadn't known she owned.

*The wards,* she thought. The border wards. Everyone in her old life had known about them the way everyone knows about the load-bearing wall in their own house: the woven lattice of old power anchored into the passes, the fords, the standing stones, invisible until you tried to cross it wrong, at which point it was very visible indeed. It was why Thornfell's smugglers ran the routes they ran, why the Concord's soldiers marched where they marched, why the whole country fit itself around a pattern no one drew anymore. And the wards had anchors. Living ones, once. Sworn servants, poured full, plugged into the lattice like keystone into arch.

She had believed, for seven years, that she had been released from that. Discharged. Cut free. The order had told her so, in the last days before it scattered—or rather, the order had told her nothing at all, and she had constructed *released* out of the silence because the alternative was unbearable.

The cold in her wrist said otherwise. The cold in her wrist said: *you are still attached. You have been attached this whole time. Something at the far end of the line has just let go.*

Or been made to.

"Lena." Halvden's voice, low, careful. He had known her five years and had never once asked her business, but he could read a face like a freight manifest. "You know what this is."

It was not a question either. Nobody on that trail was asking questions anymore. Merrit had sat down on a rock with his hat in his hands, an old man's gesture of helpless piety, mouthing prayers to saints whose names he'd half forgotten. Tam the younger had wedged himself between the carts as if the cargo could protect him. The mule, Bramble, stood quiet now—quieter than she'd been all night, head drooping, as though whatever she'd been hearing had finally stopped, and the stopping was worse than the hearing.

"No," Lena said. Which was true, and also the most useful lie available, because the truth was a shape she refused to look at directly: that somewhere south, in the cities, in the seminaries, in the marble halls of the Radiant Concord, men in white and gold had spent years preparing for this night, and the stars were not dying—they were being *put out*, deliberately, in order, like lamps in a house being closed for the season. That the wards were failing. That the age of hiding was ending, and everything she had built her life inside—every false ledger name, every unmarked trail, every kindness done in the dark—was about to be dragged into a light that did not forgive.

What she said instead was: "We make the ford tonight. We move faster, we talk less, and anyone asks, we're a salt run out of Duncarrie and I'm nobody's guide, I'm a freight clerk. Can you all do that?"

They could. Frightened men can do almost anything you give them a procedure for, and she gave them one, and it held, and the train creaked forward down toward the water with the sky above it scraped bare.

* * *

The ford crossing took an hour, because everything took an hour now. Fear slows hands. Lena walked the shallows first with a pole, testing depths that had not changed in the eleven years she'd crossed them, and the water was blacker than black water should be—no star-reflection, no moon-path, nothing on its surface but the lanterns' trembling gold. Above the southern ridge, where the sky had been stripped cleanest, she could see actual terrain now that had never been visible before: the faint gray bones of ridgelines against a void that gave nothing back. The world had become a charcoal drawing of itself.

Halfway across, the oath-scar flared.

There was no better word for it. Cold all evening, it suddenly burned—burned *cold*, which should be impossible and was, and Lena staggered in the current and caught herself on the pole and stood there hip-deep in the Greywater with her jaw clenched shut on a sound she refused to make in front of the men. For three heartbeats she felt—she had no framework for this, no doctrine, nothing from the shrine teachings that covered it—she felt *pulled*. Tugged from inside her own wrist, toward the south, toward the lowlands, toward Cinderhollow and the border and whatever was happening there, the way a hooked fish feels the line go tight. An attention moving through her. A vast, starving, desperate attention, brushing past her insides like a hand searching a dark room for a candle it remembers owning.

Then it was gone, and the scar went back to cold, and she stood in the river breathing steam and understanding, with total and terrible clarity, that she had just been touched by her god for the first time in seven years—and that the touch had felt like drowning.

Vess was not sleeping. Vess was *dying*, or fleeing, or being devoured, and whatever he was doing, he had reached for her in passing the way a man falling reaches for a branch.

She got the train across. Of course she did. That was the thing about her, the thing the order had built into her and the seven years had hardened: the work came first, the fear could wait its turn. She got the mules up the far bank, got the carts greased and rolling, got the men moving with short words and steady ones, and if her left hand shook inside her glove where no one could see it, well, gloves existed for reasons.

They made the high bench above the valley an hour before dawn, the place where the trail crested a shoulder of the mountain and the whole southern approach to the pass lay open below: miles of descending forest, the thread of the lowland road, and far off—twenty miles, maybe twenty-five, a smudge of woodsmoke against the last of the western dark—the town of Cinderhollow, where they were due to deliver, where there was an inn with a stable yard and a woman who sold eggs and never asked names.

Lena stopped the train there and looked south, and what she was looking for she could not have said, except that the pulled feeling in her wrist pointed that way like a compass needle, and compass needles exist to be followed whether or not you like the destination.

The east was beginning to gray. Dawn came up strange that morning—she would think about this for years, the strangeness of it, the way the light arrived without any of the colors it was owed. No rose, no gold. A pale, washed, exhausted gray, as though the sun were rising over a country that had already been surrendered. And below her, spread out in that gray light, the lowlands lay quiet, smoke rising straight from a hundred chimneys, the world looking exactly as it had looked yesterday and the day before.

Except for the birds.

There were no birds. On a dawn in late autumn in the Thornfell lowlands there should have been ravens working the scree, finches in the hedgerows, a hawk somewhere holding its circle. The trees stood silent. The fields stood silent. And along the lowland road, catching the first gray light, she could see them—small, distant, orderly, wrong: figures on the road. Columns of them. Moving north, toward the pass, toward Cinderhollow, in the white and grey livery that she knew the way you know a scar: the Radiant Concord, marching where the Concord had not marched in force for a generation, because the wards that kept them out—

The wards that she could feel, cold and dead and *empty*, around her own wrist—

"—were down," she said aloud, to no one, to the gray morning, to the god who had brushed past her in a river and kept falling. "They're down."

Halvden came up beside her and squinted down the road, and squinted harder, and took off his hat, which was what he did when he didn't understand something and wanted the world to know he was taking it seriously.

"That's an army," he said.

"It's a work crew," Lena said, because the professional calm was all she had left and she intended to spend every coin of it. "Come on. We've a delivery in Cinderhollow, and I want to see with my own eyes what's happened to my country overnight."

She led the train down off the bench, down into the gray light and the birdless trees, toward the town and the smoke and the columns on the road, with a dead star-field overhead and a cold key of ice locked around her wrist where her oath used to live—walking, as she had walked for seven years, toward the thing she most dreaded, because it had never once occurred to her that there might be a direction worth walking that wasn't straight through.

By noon, she would learn what the silence had cost. By noon, she would see the writs nailed to the customs-house door in Cinderhollow, and her own name on them, and the signature beneath it of the one person alive whom she had never managed to stop loving.

But that was noon. This was dawn, and the stars were out—no. The stars were *out*, the way a lamp is out, the way a fire is out, the way a voice is out when the throat that made it has been closed.

The night the stars went out was the night Lena Amberdale stopped being invisible.

Nothing, in the seven years since her god had gone quiet, had ever frightened her as much as being seen.
