## 2. Smuggler's Luck

The road down into Cinderhollow was the kind of road that forgave nothing and remembered everything.

Lena knew it the way she knew her own hands—by its scars. The switchback where a mule had once slipped and dragged a whole season's profit into the gorge. The stand of black pines where she'd waited out a Concord patrol for two days, chewing pine bark until her jaw ached, listening to men who would have hanged her discuss their supper. The shallow ford where the water ran so cold it sang against the stones.

She came down that road in the last grey hour before dawn, leading three loaded mules and carrying a cargo manifest written in a cipher only four people alive could read. Salt pork, iron nails, lamp oil, and—tucked into false-bottomed crates marked as dyestuffs—forty pounds of fever-bark that some village upriver would need before the month turned, or half its children would not see spring.

That was the work. That was what smuggling meant in Thornfell, whatever the Concord's writs said: not gold, not glory, just medicine moving through mountain passes while men in white-and-gold uniforms counted everything and understood nothing.

The oath-scar on her wrist still ached with cold. It had ached since the stars went out, since Vess's silence had stopped being silence and become something with weight and teeth—a pressure behind her ribs like a held breath that no longer belonged to her. She'd wrapped the scar in wool against the morning chill and told herself it was the altitude. She was good at telling herself things. Seven years of practice.

Cinderhollow announced itself first by smell: woodsmoke and wet slate and the sour-sweet reek of the tannery at the town's edge. Then by sound—or rather, by the absence of sound. No mill wheel turning. No dogs. No bell ringing the hour, though the hour was due.

Then, cresting the last rise, she saw why.

The town lay in the cup of the valley like water in a broken bowl, and through it moved lines of soldiers. Not garrison soldiers—the thin, tired border watch she'd traded with for years, men who looked the other way for a cask of brandy and never asked what was under the dye. These were Radiant Concord regulars in their sun-white tabards, moving in files of eight, boots crunching frost in unison, bayonets catching the first light like a field of crooked stars.

At the town gate, where the old ward-stone should have stood—a squat granite pillar carved with Vess's spiral, worn smooth by generations of travelers' touching hands—there was only rubble. And around the rubble, a ring of soldiers, and around them, a crowd of townsfolk kneeling in the frozen mud.

Lena stopped the mules in the shadow of the pines and stood very still.

*The wards are down.* She had known it since the stars died; she had felt it in her scar like a nail driven into bone. But knowing a thing and seeing it were different animals. Knowing let you keep your distance. Seeing put the truth in your face, breathing on you.

The ward-stone had not crumbled. It had been *broken*—she could see that even from here, even at this distance. Chiseled. Deliberately, methodically chiseled apart, the spiral-face shattered, the fragments gathered into a pile like bones after a battle. Someone had wanted it done slowly enough to be thorough. Someone had wanted the townsfolk to watch.

And they had watched. They were watching still, kneeling there in the mud, and the sight of it did something to Lena that she couldn't name and didn't try to. It was the feeling of standing at a deathbed you arrived too late for. Everything important had already happened. All that remained was the aftermath, and the aftermath was being organized by men in white tabards with ledgers and rope.

A sergeant's voice carried up the slope, flat and bored, the voice of a man reading from a list:

"—by decree of the Radiant Concord, all persons within the territory of Thornfell are hereby subject to census, tithe, and lawful conscription. Resistance is heresy. Heresy is death. You will be processed alphabetically—"

Processed. Like wool. Like ore. Like anything else that passed through Cinderhollow and came out the other side worth less than it went in.

Lena's hand found the knife at her belt without her deciding to reach for it. Then she caught herself, because instinct was a liar, and the arithmetic was simple and ugly: one mercenary, three mules, forty pounds of fever-bark, versus a garrison. There was no version of this where she rode down the hill with her sword out and anything happened except her dying on the same mud as everyone else, and the fever-bark rotting in a confiscated crate, and some village full of children learning what the new order cost.

Self-preservation wasn't cowardice. She'd learned that young, in the years when Vess still spoke, back when she'd believed that faith meant charging at the dark with a torch. He had taught her otherwise, in his quiet way. *Survive,* he'd said, the last clear words she ever heard from him. *Survive, and be kind where kindness can live. The rest is noise.*

So she did what smugglers do. She turned the mules off the road onto the drover's path that looped wide around the town's eastern edge, toward the waystation inn that sat outside the walls—the Broken Compass, run by a widow named Odile who owed Lena three favors and asked no questions, which between the two of them amounted to a sacrament.

She was fifty yards along the path when she heard the screaming.

It came from the direction of the millrace—a child's voice, high and furious rather than frightened, the way a cornered animal sounds when it decides to spend its fear as anger. Then a man's voice, thick with authority and something meaner underneath. Then the crack of a short baton against something soft.

Lena told herself to keep walking. She said it out loud, actually, to the mules, as if saying it to them made it binding. *"Keep walking."*

The mules kept walking. She didn't.

She stood in the frost-killed grass with the reins slack in her fist and listened to a child get beaten half a mile away, and she thought about the arithmetic again—one against a garrison—and then she thought about Vess's last words, *be kind where kindness can live*, and she understood, with a clarity that felt almost like the god's hand on the back of her neck, that this was exactly the place where kindness could live. Small. Hidden. Half a mile from the main force, in the fog off the millpond, with no witnesses but her and whatever gods were left to see.

"Damn it," she said, to Vess, to herself, to the morning. She tied the mules to an alder, drew her hood forward, and went.

* * *

The millrace ran fast and brown with snowmelt, loud enough to drown a confession. Along its bank, behind the tannery sheds, Lena found them: four Concord soldiers and a boy.

He was small—ten, maybe eleven, all elbows and hollow cheeks, with a shock of hair the color of rust and a face already smeared with blood from a split lip. His wrists were bound in front of him with cord, and a soldier was hauling on the cord's end like a man leading a stubborn goat, while the boy dug his heels into the mud and screamed curses at them with a fluency that would have embarrassed a dockworker.

*"Conscription,"* the big one was saying, patient as a stone, pulling the cord hand over hand. *"You're on the list, boy. Your mother signed the census. Whole family's liable. Now walk, or I drag you, and dragging's harder on everyone."*

"I don't *got* a mother on any list, you lying—" The boy spat blood. "She's dead! You know she's dead, you *counted her grave*, you were *there*—"

"Orphans go to the Foundling Companies. Labor corps. Learn a trade." A shrug in the voice. "Better than starving."

"I wasn't starving!"

"You are now."

The other three soldiers watched with the loose, entertained posture of men whose work had paused to become a show. One of them had the boy's cap in his hand and was turning it over, examining the lining, checking the seams the way soldiers check everything—for hidden value, for contraband, for anything worth taking.

Lena took inventory the way she took inventory of every room she entered: exits, weapons, distances. Four soldiers. Two swords drawn, two sheathed—they hadn't expected trouble from a child, and their boredom said they didn't expect it now. The millrace at their backs, loud. The tannery sheds to the east, deep shadow between them, and the fog coming off the pond in slow white tongues. Twenty yards of open ground between the sheds and the soldiers, and she'd have to cross all of it.

Her body had already decided before her mind finished counting. That was the thing about instinct—it didn't wait for permission. Her feet were moving, silent on the wet stones, and her hand had found the sap-globe in her coat pocket, one of the little resin balls she carried for starting fires in wet country, and her thumb had already cracked its shell so the pitch inside bled sticky over her fingers.

She crossed the open ground low and fast, through the thickest tongue of fog, and came up behind the shed nearest the soldiers before any of them had turned around.

What happened next took eleven seconds, and she would remember all of them for the rest of her life, however long that turned out to be.

She stepped out of the fog directly behind the soldier holding the cord, and she hit him—not with the knife, never with the knife unless there was no other choice—with the weighted end of her belt, a strip of leather with a lead bar sewn in it that smugglers called a *politeness*. It caught him across the temple and folded him into the mud without a sound louder than a sack of grain dropping.

The second soldier turned toward the noise and got the sap-globe full in the face. Pitch spread across his eyes, sealing them, and he went backward into the millrace shrieking about his eyes, and the race took his shrieking and swallowed it, and the cold water did the rest.

The third one—the one with the cap—was quick. He dropped the cap, drew steel, and came at her with a lunge that would have opened her throat if she'd been a half-second slower. She wasn't. She'd spent fifteen years learning one lesson above all others: never fight the blade, fight the wrist. Her politeness cracked across his forearm and the sword spun away into the mud, and then her knee found his sternum and his breath left him in a whump and he sat down hard and stayed sitting.

The fourth soldier looked at his three companions—in the mud, in the river, on the ground—and made the calculation that sane men make. He ran. Lena let him. A runner carried a story, and the story he'd carry was *witch in the fog, strikes like lightning, took three of us in ten heartbeats*, and stories like that kept other soldiers cautious, and caution bought time, and time was the only currency she had.

Then it was over, and the fog closed back around the scene like a curtain, and Lena stood in the churned mud with a bleeding boy beside her and her heart hammering so hard she could hear it in her ears, in her teeth, in the oath-scar on her wrist, which had gone from cold to burning as if the god under her skin had felt every second of it and had opinions.

The boy stared at her. Blood on his lip, cord still binding his wrists, fury still burning in his eyes—but underneath the fury, suddenly, a wariness much older than his years. He looked at her the way street children look at adults who appear out of nowhere: as a problem before a person.

"You're not Concord," he said. It wasn't a question, exactly, but it wasn't trust either.

"No."

"You're not from the tannery."

"No."

"Then who—" He squinted at her hood, at the mud on her boots, at the politeness dripping in her hand. "You're a *smuggler*. I've seen you. You come through with the mules, autumn runs. Odile feeds you pie." His voice climbed with something like accusation. "You never talk to anybody."

"It's safer that way."

"Well it didn't look safe just now."

No, it hadn't. Lena knelt, took his bound wrists, and cut the cord with two passes of her knife—carefully, angling the edge away from his skin, because a boy who flinched at rescue was a boy who'd been hurt by rescuers before, or near enough. The cord fell away and left red grooves in his skin, and he rubbed them and glared at her, and she found she liked the glaring. Glare was honest. Gratitude was often a performance, but glare never lied.

"What's your name?" she asked.

"Pip." He said it like a challenge, chin up. "Pip Ferrow. And before you ask—yes, my ma's dead, two winters back, fever. Yes, I've got no family. Yes, I steal, mostly bread, sometimes better. And *no*, I'm not going to any Foundling Company, because I heard what happens in those, and it's not learning a trade, it's—" He stopped himself, jaw working. "It's bad. It's worse than bad. People come back wrong or they don't come back."

"I believe you."

That landed on him strangely. She watched it land—the flicker in his eyes, the half-step of surprise, a boy braced for argument receiving agreement instead. Nobody had believed him recently. Maybe nobody had believed him in years.

"They'll send more," Pip said, jerking his chin toward the unconscious soldier in the mud. "For me, and for you. That one's going to wake up with a headache and a story, and Inquisitor's men love stories." He looked at her sidelong, and beneath the bravado something scared moved, quick as a fish under ice. "You should run. I would. Running's what I'm best at."

"I know. I watched you try it." She stood, scanning the fog line, listening past the millrace for boots. Nothing yet. The river-shrieker would have been fished out by now, blind and furious, adding his own chapter to the story. "Where were they taking you?"

"The muster yard. By the chapel." Pip spat, delicately, away from her boots. "They're processing people. Alphabetical, the sergeant said, like we're sacks of barley. Everyone between fourteen and fifty goes to labor companies. Everyone younger goes to Foundling. Everyone older—" He shrugged, and the shrug was worse than the words would have been. "Nobody says. The older ones just get taken to the chapel and the chapel door stays shut."

The chapel. Where the ward-stone fragments had been gathered, maybe. Where the kneeling townsfolk had been marched. Lena felt the cold in her scar deepen, spreading up her forearm like frost climbing a windowpane.

"The priest," she said. "Father Aldous. Old man, bad hip, keeps bees—"

"Gone. They took him the first night. Before the stone even came down, they took him." Pip's voice dropped, and for the first time there was no bravado in it at all. "My ma's friend Marta saw them load him into a wagon. He was praying the whole time. Loud. Not scared-loud. *Angry*-loud. Telling them the mountains would remember." He paused. "Marta says the mountains did remember. Something happened that night. Lights under the ground, she said. Like the whole valley hummed. Then it stopped, and it's been quiet ever since. Wrong-quiet."

Wrong-quiet. Lena knew wrong-quiet intimately. She had been living inside it for seven years, and for one night it had ended, and she hadn't even been here to hear it.

*Something happened that night.* Something had answered Father Aldous. Something under the mountains had hummed with a dying priest's defiance, and then gone silent again—and here she was, standing in the wrong-quiet with a god's cold scar on her wrist and a boy's blood on the mud and no idea what any of it meant, except that it meant everything.

"We need to move," she said. "Not to the muster yard. Away. There's a path along the millrace that comes out past the tannery, and from there—"

"I know the paths." Pip was already moving, light on his feet, ducking under the low lintel of the tannery shed like a bird into a hedge. "I know every path in this town better than the soldiers do. Better than *you* do." He shot her a look over his shoulder, equal parts challenge and plea. "Take me with you. On your mules. I'm useful. I can drive mules, I can read a little, I can fit in small spaces, I don't eat much, and I will *never* be conscripted, not while I've got legs."

Lena looked at him—at this scrap of a child, this furious orphan with his split lip and his rust-colored hair and his absolute refusal to be cargo in anyone's ledger—and felt the last of her self-preservation argue its case and lose.

Taking him was madness. Taking him doubled her risk, slowed her pace, added a mouth, added a liability that couldn't fight, couldn't vanish into a crowd, couldn't pass a checkpoint. Every professional instinct she owned said *leave him, point him at the hills, wish him luck.*

But she had watched four soldiers try to drag him to a fate he understood better than they did, and she had watched him fight them with everything his small body had, and she had heard him say *I wasn't starving* with the particular desperation of someone defending the only life anyone had ever let him keep. And she remembered, with a pain that was almost physical, that she had once been small and alone and certain that no adult would ever choose her over their own safety.

Someone had chosen her anyway. A god, as it turned out. A god who had then gone silent and left her to wonder if the choosing had been real.

Maybe this was how you answered that question. Not with prayer. With a boy on a mule.

"Up the drover's path," she said. "There's a gray mule, gentle as milk. Can you handle her?"

"I can handle anything with four legs and no opinions."

"She has opinions."

"Then we'll negotiate." And Pip grinned—split lip and all, blood on his teeth, grinning like the morning belonged to him—and something in Lena's chest that had been clenched for seven years loosened, just slightly, just enough to notice.

* * *

They reached the mules without incident. The fog was lifting, which was bad, and the bells of Cinderhollow had begun to ring—which was worse, because they weren't ringing the hour. They were ringing alarm, a fast irregular clangor that sent birds up from the pines in ragged clouds.

Pip, halfway to the gray mule's back, froze. "That's the raid-bell. They ring it when someone's escaped custody." He looked at her with dawning, horrified delight. "*You're* someone who's escaped custody."

"Technically, they escaped mine."

"That is the best thing I have ever heard and I am going to say it to everyone forever." He swung up onto the mule—clumsily, wincing where the cord had burned his wrists, but up—and grabbed a handful of mane. "Which way? Soldiers will check the south road first, always do. North goes to the high passes but the passes are watched since the wards fell. West is river country, lots of hiding, lots of water. East is—" He stopped. His face changed. "East is nothing. East is where my ma's buried, and past that, nobody knows. Past that's just Thornfell getting darker."

Lena mounted her own mule and sat for a moment, letting the animal's warmth soak through her cold thighs, looking north at the high passes where the stars used to be visible at night and weren't anymore, and west at the river country where hiding was possible but winter was coming early—everyone said so, the frost had come weeks ahead of itself—and feeling the oath-scar pulse its slow cold rhythm like a second heart.

Somewhere in those mountains was a reason for all of this. A map, a shrine, a Door—rumors, only rumors, the kind of rumors smugglers collected the way other people collected coins. Somewhere in this ruined country was an answer to the question her scar had been asking since the night the sky went black: *what happened to you, Vess? What did they take? What's left?*

She didn't have the answer. But she had a boy who knew every path in Cinderhollow, three mules, forty pounds of fever-bark, and a head start measured in the length of one soldier's headache.

"West," she said. "River country. We move now, we move quiet, and we figure out the rest after sundown."

"And then?" Pip asked, falling in beside her as the mules picked their way down toward the water. "After we hide. After we're safe. What then?"

Lena looked north one more time, at the passes, at the empty sky beyond them, at the whole vast silenced country that had been hers and Vess's and was now theirs no longer.

"Then," she said, "we find out what they broke. And we decide whether it can be fixed."

Behind them, Cinderhollow's alarm bells rang on, and somewhere in the white-and-gold machinery of the Radiant Concord, a clerk dipped a pen and wrote a name on a list—a name that would travel faster than any mule, and arrive before they did.

Hers.
