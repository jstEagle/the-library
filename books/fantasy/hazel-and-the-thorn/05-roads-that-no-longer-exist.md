## 5. Roads That No Longer Exist

The road died a mile out of Cinderhollow.

Lena felt it before she saw it—the way the ground under her boots changed its mind, the packed clay of the drover's track giving way to raw turned earth where the Concord's engineers had plowed it into furrows and salted them with ash so nothing would grow through. Beyond the furrow, the bridge over the Millwater was already gone. Not burned. *Unmade.* The stones had been pulled apart with a care that offended her more than fire would have, each block stacked in neat rows like a mason's graveyard, waiting to be carted off and used for something holier.

"They're not chasing us," Sefa said beside her. She stood at the edge of the ruined span, hood back despite the cold, reading the destruction the way other people read scripture. "They're erasing. Look—they took the bridge apart instead of breaking it. They took the ford markers down. They'll take the mile-stones next." Her gloved hand traced the air above the empty river gap. "My father used to say you can't rule a country whose roads you don't control. I thought he meant taxes."

"He meant this," Lena said.

Pip crouched at the near abutment, poking a stick into the dark water below. "Can we swim it?"

"You can swim it," Lena said. "I'm not carrying the maps."

"I'd carry the maps."

"You'd drown the maps." She caught his collar as he leaned too far and hauled him back with one arm, an motion that had begun to feel automatic, which worried her more than the Concord did. Three days ago she'd have let him learn about riverbanks the hard way. Now she was pulling him back from edges without thinking, and every time she did it, some small door inside her chest that she'd kept barred for seven years swung a little wider on its hinges.

Brannoc came up the bank from downstream, moving with the deliberate economy of a big man who had learned that size draws arrows. He didn't look at any of them when he reported. He hadn't managed eye contact since Cinderhollow, since the fence-post man's description, since they'd all silently agreed to let him walk with them anyway for reasons nobody had said aloud.

"Ford's two miles south," he said. "Shallows, gravel bed. Old drovers used it before the bridge went up."

"Old drovers," Sefa repeated. "You were Concord. What do you know about old drovers?"

"I know what the quartermasters burned." His jaw worked. "Every garrison keeps a ledger of local crossings, fords, shepherd paths, smuggler cuts—everything the army might need or might want closed. When the wards fell, the order came down to burn the ledgers and close the roads behind the advance. I carried the torch to three of them." He finally looked up, and his eyes were the flat grey of a winter sky deciding whether to snow. "So yes, mapmaker's daughter. I know exactly what we're walking into, because I helped make it."

Nobody answered that. The river ran cold and quick beneath the absence of its bridge, and somewhere north, faint as a bad memory, Lena could feel the Concord's advance like pressure against a bruise—the silence where Vess should have been, pressed thinner here, stretched tighter, as if the godless country were a fist slowly closing around what remained of him.

She flexed her left wrist. Under the wrappings, the oath-scar stayed cold. It had been cold for seven years, but lately the cold had texture, direction, weight—a compass needle pointing away from everything she loved.

"All right," she said. "South ford. Single file, weapons dry, and if anyone sees a red sash, you drop and you don't get up until I tell you. Pip walks in the middle. Sefa, your maps stay oilcloth-wrapped until we're clear of the water."

"And you?" Sefa asked.

"I walk last." Lena adjusted the strap of her crossbow and started south along the bank. "Somebody has to make sure the past doesn't catch up."

* * *

The ford was exactly where Brannoc said it would be, which Lena chose not to think about too closely.

They crossed at first light, when the mist sat on the Millwater like a held breath and the far bank was only a rumor of alders. The water came to mid-thigh on her and to Pip's ribs; she made him lock his arm through hers and felt him trembling—not fear, she realized, but the particular rigid excitement of a boy who had never been trusted with anything important and was now trusted with crossing a river in a country being erased. She remembered being eleven. She remembered the first time Maren had put a knife in her hand and said *don't cut yourself, cut what needs cutting*, and how the trust had felt like sunlight.

She pushed the memory down with the rest of them and kept walking.

On the far side, the land changed. South of the Millwater the Concord's erasure had passed like a scythe; here the old roads still existed—but only just, only as suggestions: a sunken lane grown over with hazel scrub, a drystone wall with a stile worn smooth by generations of hands that no longer had anywhere to go, a waymark stone carved with the spiral of the Thornfell drovers' guild, its meaning known now to perhaps forty living people. Sefa walked with her father's case strapped across her back and her fingers trailing along the waymark spiral as they passed, like touching the shoulder of an old friend.

"My father charted this lane," she said quietly. "When I was small, he'd take me out on survey weeks and I'd hate every minute—cold feet, wet bread, his terrible singing. And then he'd find something like this." She nodded at the sunken way. "A road everyone else forgot. He'd say, *Sefa, look. The world tried to lose this and couldn't quite manage it. Everything worth keeping is like that.*"

"Your father sounds like a man who'd have hated the Concord professionally," Lena said.

"He'd have hated them *competently*. There's a difference." Sefa almost smiled. It was the first almost-smile Lena had seen from her, and it changed her whole face, and Lena looked away from it quickly, the way you look away from a fire you're trying not to warm your hands at.

The lane climbed all morning through hazel and grey rock, following no logic that a soldier would recognize, switchbacking where a straight line would do, dipping into stream-cuts where a ridge route offered itself. That was the point. Smugglers' roads were built by people assuming pursuit—every bend chosen to break sightlines, every rise placed so a rider on the military track below couldn't see the top of your head. Lena had run cargo on these ways for six years and knew them the way she knew her own scars: by feel, by sequence, by the particular smell of a place at a particular hour.

By late afternoon they reached the first of the caches—an old hollow oak with a loose bark panel—and found it stripped. Not robbed. *Stripped*: the wax-sealed packet gone, a chalk mark left on the trunk in the guild's own cipher. Lena read the mark twice and felt the bottom drop out of her professional calm.

"What does it mean?" Pip asked, watching her face.

"It means someone got here ahead of us and took the supplies." She kept her voice level. "And it means whoever took them knows the code well enough to leave a receipt."

"Friends of yours?"

"That depends." She traced the cipher with one finger. The mark was genuine—old Hesta's hand, if she guessed right, the ferry-woman at Glassford, seventy years old and the most reliable criminal on the eastern slopes. But the second stroke was new, added after, a single line through the spiral. In guild cipher, a line through the spiral meant one thing: *the roads are closing. Use nothing twice.*

"The Concord isn't just burning bridges," she told the others. "Someone's warning us off the whole network. Every cache, every safehouse, every friendly window—we're supposed to assume they're all compromised."

"Then what do we do?" Pip asked.

Lena looked at him—at this scrap of a boy the Concord had tried to press-gang, standing in a dead smuggler's road asking practical questions with dust on his chin—and felt the strange, unwelcome warmth again, the one that had no survival value whatsoever.

"We do what the roads were built for," she said. "We stop being predictable."

* * *

They made camp that night in a shepherd's bothy that existed on no map Sefa owned and no ledger the Concord had ever burned, because it had never been written down anywhere at all. Lena had it in her head: a low stone shelter under a shelf of cliff, half-swallowed by a hillside, with a turf roof and a hearth that drew clean if you knew which of the two flues to open. She opened the right one, got a fire going small and smokeless, and pretended not to notice that Pip had fallen asleep sitting up against Brannoc's shoulder, and that Brannoc had gone very still, like a man afraid to breathe in case he woke the sleeping thing and it moved away.

Sefa spread her father's charts by firelight, weighting the corners with stones, and studied the route ahead with her lips moving—counting miles, counting hours, counting the things between them and the drover's hut in the high country where the archive waited.

"Two days," she murmured. "If the weather holds."

"The weather won't hold." Lena fed a stick to the fire. "There's a wrongness in the air. You feel it? Like the sky's holding its breath."

"I feel it." Sefa glanced up. "You always know weather?"

"Smuggler's trick. Weather kills more couriers than soldiers do." Lena wrapped her arms around her knees. Through the bothy's low doorway she could see a slice of sky, starless, black as spilled ink. Seven years of that now. Seven years of navigating by dead reckoning and memory because the heavens had gone out like lamps in a house whose owner had died. People in the lowlands had stopped looking up. Children born after the Silence thought stars were a story, like dragons, like honest priests.

She wondered—not for the first time, but with new sharpness—if Vess had ever seen stars. If whatever he was, before the Concord chained him silent, had ever looked up at his own handiwork.

The oath-scar pulsed cold, once, like a heartbeat in reverse.

"Lena." Sefa's voice, low. "Your commander. Maren. The writs had her seal."

"Mm."

"You served under her a long time?"

"Eleven years." The fire cracked. "She taught me to shoot. Taught me to read terrain, read men, read lies. When I took the oath to Vess—quietly, illegally, the way half the border folk did—she looked the other way. I always thought..." Lena stopped. Started again, flatter. "I always thought she looked the other way because she understood. Now I think maybe she was just waiting for orders that made looking away unnecessary."

"And if you meet her out here?"

"Then one of us is better at her job than the other." Lena met Sefa's eyes across the fire. "That's not bravado. It's arithmetic. She has riders, writs, and a godless army. I have four people, one mapmaker, and roads that don't exist. If it comes to a fair fight, I've already lost—which means my entire job is making sure nothing is ever fair."

Something shifted in Sefa's expression—respect, maybe, or recognition. Two women who trusted no institutions, sizing each other up across a small fire, finding the measurements agreeable.

Behind them, Brannoc spoke for the first time in hours. "It won't be fair tomorrow either way."

Lena turned. He was awake, of course; he slept like a cat, in shallow laps, and Pip had migrated to the warm spot he'd left. The big man sat with his back to the wall, sword across his knees, and his face in the firelight was the face of a man rehearsing something.

"There's a gorge," he said. "Tomorrow, on your route—the Drover's Cut, the narrows above the old packhorse bridge. Your smuggler's road crosses the military road there for half a mile. There's no way around it; I checked the ledgers before I burned them, and there's no way around." He swallowed. "And the Concord knows it. It's the obvious chokepoint between Cinderhollow and the high country. If I were planning the sweep—and I helped plan sweeps like it—I'd have watchers in the gorge by tonight."

"Then why are you telling me now?" Lena asked quietly. "Why not lie awake on it like a decent man?"

"Because—" His hands tightened on the sword grip. "Because if we're ambushed tomorrow and you find out I knew, you'll think I led you into it. And I need—" His voice cracked and he hated it, she could see him hating it. "I need one person in this company to believe that I'm not still theirs. Just one. Even if it's the child."

Pip, asleep, snored gently against the wall.

Lena looked at him for a long moment. A deserter who had carried the torch to three ledgers. A man who confessed like a man paying out rope, hoping the knot would hold.

"Here's what believing you costs me," she said at last. "If you're lying, tomorrow I die in a gorge. If you're telling the truth, tomorrow I fight a battle I already know is coming, which is the only kind of battle worth winning. Either way, the information changes what I do at dawn, not whether I watch you." She lay back against her pack. "Sleep, Brannoc. You're on second watch. Wake me at moonset—except there's no moonset anymore, so wake me when the cold gets worse. You'll know."

He almost laughed. It came out broken, but it came out. "Yes, captain."

"Don't call me that."

"Yes, Lena."

From across the fire, Sefa's voice, drowsy and dry: "For the record, I believe you. The torch-carrying part makes you stupid, not treacherous. Stupid I can work with."

Brannoc stared at her. Then he bowed his head over his sword, and if the firelight glinted on something in his eyes, the fire kept his secret, the way good fires do.

* * *

The ambush came an hour after dawn, and it came wrong—or rather, it came exactly as Brannoc predicted, which was somehow worse.

The Drover's Cut narrowed as promised, the smuggler's lane converging with the old military road between walls of grey stone hung with dead ivy. Lena had them strung out in file, crossbow up, breath steaming, when she heard the thing no soldier hears and every smuggler dreads: the specific quality of silence upstream. Birds don't stop singing for weather. They stop for people.

"Down," she breathed—and the first bolt took the space where her throat had been and sparked off the stone behind her.

Then the gorge was noise. Four hunters in the rocks above—Concord irregulars, red-sashed, half-trained, dangerous the way frightened men are dangerous. Bolts slapping stone. Sefa dragging Pip into the lee of the packhorse bridge's stump while arrows chipped the old mortar. Brannoc roaring and charging uphill with a sword bigger than sense, drawing two of the four onto himself like a man who had decided, in advance, exactly how much punishment he was owed.

Lena killed the first man cleanly, from cover, a shot through the gap in his sash as he reloaded. The second she took with the knife when he came around the rock expecting a fleeing woman and found a professional instead. It wasn't fair. She'd promised herself nothing would be.

The fourth hunter nearly had Sefa—came off the rocks above the bridge stump with a short sword raised over the mapmaker's daughter, and it was Pip who screamed the warning, Pip who threw a stone, a nothing-throw, a boy's throw, that caught the man on the ear and turned his swing wide, and it was Sefa—Sefa, who had never held a blade in her life—who drove her father's brass-bound surveying spike up under his ribs with both hands and all the fury of a daughter of erased roads.

Then it was quiet, except for breathing.

Four bodies in the gorge. One nicked arm (Sefa, refusing to look at it). One bruised shoulder (Brannoc, who had taken a pommel to the collarbone and called it fair trade). One boy shaking with delayed terror who insisted, through chattering teeth, that he was fine, that the throw had been intentional, that he'd aimed for the ear.

Lena crouched by the nearest body and went through the pouch with steady hands. Rations. A token of the Radiant Concord, sun-faced, cheap tin. And papers—a patrol order, signed with a seal she knew better than her own name.

Maren's seal.

Not a general sweep. A directed hunt. *Amberdale, L. — take alive. The Bearer is wanted intact.* The words sat in her palm like a coal.

"Take alive," Sefa read over her shoulder, and went pale. "Intact. They don't want you dead, Lena. Why wouldn't they want you dead?"

"I don't know yet." She folded the paper away, and her voice came out steadier than she felt. "But I intend to find out before it matters."

They moved fast after that, off the chokepoint, up into the high tracks where the snow had begun to fall in fat, windless flakes that muffled the world and covered the blood in the gorge behind them. Pip walked in the middle. Sefa walked with her hand resting, briefly, once, on Lena's shoulder as they climbed—no words, just weight, just proof of another human being choosing proximity.

And Lena, climbing into the white silence with her family-shaped company strung out behind her, understood that something had happened in that gorge that had nothing to do with survival. Somewhere between a boy's thrown stone and a mapmaker's brass spike and a deserter's borrowed courage, four strangers had become a unit, and a unit was one heartbeat short of a family, and families—

Families were the one thing the Concord couldn't burn, and the one thing she could least afford to love.

Ahead, through the thickening snow, the dark shape of the drover's hut crouched on its ridge like a patient animal. And beside the trail, half-buried in fresh powder, stood a waymark stone with the drovers' spiral cut deep—and a single line struck through it, in chalk too fresh for the snowfall.

Someone was still ahead of them. Someone who knew the code.

And the line through the spiral had been drawn in blood.
