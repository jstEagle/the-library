## 6. Cinders in the Snow

The snow came down like ash, and Brannoc Vey knew ash when he saw it.

He had spent eleven years of his life making things fall out of the sky in exactly that way—slow, suffocating, beautiful if you didn't think about what it buried. The Concord's liturgists had called the ward-collapse ritual *the Unveiling*, as though peeling the sky off Thornfell were a kindness, as though the gods beneath were bandages over a wound rather than the only thing holding the country's bones together. He had been twenty-four and brilliant and hungry to be seen by people who wore white robes and spoke of mercy in a language with no word for *no*.

Now he was thirty-five, sitting at the edge of a smuggler's fire he hadn't earned, watching a woman who used to be his enemy sharpen a knife she would be within her rights to put through his throat.

The cave mouth framed a world gone white. Somewhere behind them, the old salt road they'd taken out of Cinderhollow was already burning—the Concord did not leave bridges standing, literal or otherwise—and somewhere ahead lay three days of high country that Sefa's maps called passable and Brannoc's memory called a graveyard. They had made camp in a shepherd's bothy half-swallowed by the hillside, its turf roof furred with frost, its single hearth drawing well enough to warm hands but not enough to warm anything truer than that.

Pip slept against the far wall, curled around the boy's stolen knife like a barnacle around rock. Sefa sat cross-legged with her father's maps spread across her knees, copying them by firelight onto fresh paper, because Sefa trusted no original anywhere near danger. And Lena Amberdale sat nearest the door, as she always did, whetstone whispering along steel, her oath-scarred wrist turned toward the flame as if she could warm whatever had gone cold inside it.

Brannoc cleared his throat. It felt like clearing rubble from a collapsed mine—knowing what you'd find underneath and doing it anyway.

"I need to tell you all something," he said, "and I'd take it kindly if you let me finish before anyone decided anything permanent."

The whetstone stopped.

Sefa looked up first. She had the mapmaker's habit of reading terrain before faces, and Brannoc watched her do it now—the slope of his shoulders, the fault line under his words—cataloguing him the way she catalogued everything, for hazards and water sources and places where the ground might give.

Lena didn't look up at all. That was worse.

"Eleven years ago," Brannoc said, "I wasn't a soldier. I was a ritual engineer. Third Circle, Liturgy of Boundaries. You won't have heard of us—we weren't the ones who marched. We were the ones who drew the lines the marchers walked on." He turned his hands over, palms up, as though the proof were written there. It might as well have been. "Wards. Seals. Anchor-lattices. Every god in the Thornfell range is tied to the land through a lattice of oaths—thousands of sworn servants, each one a thread. I helped design the loom."

The fire popped. Outside, wind moved through the pines with a sound like a congregation shuffling in its seats.

"The Concord's doctrine says the gods are parasites," he went on, quieter now. "That they feed on worship the way ticks feed on blood, and that a country can't be free until every last one is starved out. Most of us believed it. I believed it. I could recite the proofs in my sleep—I *did* recite them in my sleep, for years, because Grand Inquisitor Halveth liked his engineers devout." His jaw tightened. "Halveth. You'll hear that name soon enough, if the mountains are kind, which they aren't. Serath Halveth. The man who wrote the Unveiling. I was one of six hands that built it."

Now Lena looked up.

Her eyes in the firelight were the color of wet slate, and there was nothing soft in them, and Brannoc had known this moment was coming since the day he'd deserted—had rehearsed it on the road, in the dark, in the voiceless hours before dawn—and still, meeting it, he found he had prepared entirely the wrong speech.

"You," she said. Not a question. A weighing.

"Me," he said. "The border wards didn't fail, Lena. They were *unmade*. From the inside, by design, in an operation I spent four years drafting. The stars going out—that's not damage. That's the first verse. There are nine verses total, and we've sung one."

Sefa's pen had gone still. Ink gathered at its nib in a fat black drop, trembling. "Nine," she repeated. "And the other eight?"

"Each one unmakes another layer of the old bindings. The wards at the border were the outermost ring. Then the road-wards, then the shrine-seals, then the deep anchors in the mountains themselves. By the ninth verse, there will be nothing left in Thornfell for a god to stand on. No consecrated ground. No oath that holds. No—" he hesitated, and hated himself for hesitating, because hesitation was a luxury of men whose hands were clean, "—no silence like the one your Vess has been living in, either. Even that gets erased. A silenced god can still be woken. An unmade one is just weather."

Pip stirred against the wall and did not wake. Small mercies.

"Why tell us this now?" Sefa said. Her voice was flat, controlled, the voice of a woman measuring a distance she might have to run. "You've traveled with us five days. You've eaten our food, stood our watches, carried Pip across a river when the ice broke. Why confess tonight, in a hole in the ground, with the snow coming down like the sky itself is trying to bury us?"

"Because tomorrow we cross the Col of Nine Stones," Brannoc said, "and the Col of Nine Stones is where the second verse begins. And because you deserve to know that the man guiding you through it is the man who surveyed it."

Silence. The wind rose and fell. Lena set down the whetstone with exaggerated care, the way a person sets down a thing instead of throwing it.

"Say the rest," she said. "There's more. Men who've said everything don't look like you look."

Brannoc laughed—a short, ugly sound with no humor in it, the laugh of a man finding a bone in his own soup. "No. There isn't more. There's only the part I've been circling for five days because saying it makes it real." He leaned forward, elbows on knees, and made himself meet her eyes. "The Unveiling has a flaw. Halveth knows it has a flaw. The ritual unmakes the lattices, yes—all of them, thread by thread—but a lattice isn't just threads. At the center of every great ward-work there's a knot. A living knot. An oath-bearer. Someone who swore so completely, so *wholly*, that their oath became load-bearing. The threads can be cut. The knot has to be opened."

"Opened," Sefa said. "What does opened mean?"

"It means," Brannoc said, and his voice came out lower than he intended, roughened by the guilt that had ridden him from Cinderhollow like a second rider, "that Halveth needs a person. Alive. Intact. Sworn. The final verse of the Unveiling isn't cast on stone or sky—it's cast *through* someone. You take an oath-bearer, strap them into the extraction rite, and you pour them out. Everything they swore, everything their god hid in them, drawn off like marrow from a bone. One bearer, fully emptied, gives the rite enough force to reach the deep anchors. To unmake the mountain-shrines. To finish silencing every god left in Thornfell in a single night."

"And how many oath-bearers are there?" Sefa asked. "In the whole country?"

"Four that the Concord ever identified. Two died in the first purge, seven years back, when Vess went quiet. One fled south over the border before the wards fell—we lost the trail in the free cities." Brannoc paused. The firelight moved on his face and he let it, because looking away now would cost him everything he was trying to buy. "The fourth is listed on the execution writs posted in every garrison town from here to the sea. Except the writs lie. I've read the orders underneath the writs. *Capture alive. Do not harm the bearer. Deliver to the Inquisitor's site at the Door.*"

Lena had gone very still. Stillness, Brannoc thought, was a language she spoke fluently—he'd seen her use it on ambushes, on negotiations, on dying men—and the stillness she wore now was the deepest he'd ever heard from her.

"Whose name is on the writs," she said. It wasn't a question either. But he answered it anyway, because she deserved to hear it said plainly, by him, in front of witnesses, so that later—whatever later held—she could not be told she'd imagined it.

"Yours," he said. "Lena Amberdale. Formerly of the Order of the Quiet Vigil. Sworn servant of Vess-of-the-Borderlands." He swallowed. "Halveth doesn't want you dead, Lena. Dead, you're just another grave. Alive, you're the key to the last eight verses. The Concord has been hunting you for seven years, and everyone who told you it was about your desertion, your smuggling, your sins—they were lying, or they were lied to. It was always about the oath. It was always about what's *inside* you."

The fire cracked. Snow ticked softly against the turf roof, patient as sand in an hourglass.

Lena looked at her wrist. The scar there—an old brand, the sigil of the Quiet Vigil, faded to a pale seam—caught the light. When the stars had gone out, she'd told them, the scar had gone cold, cold as riverbed stone in January, and it had not warmed since. Brannoc knew what that meant. He had written the theory of it in a monograph nobody outside the Third Circle would ever read: *the anchor-thread does not sever; it contracts.* Whatever lived in her had not abandoned her. It had pulled in close, the way a man pulls in close to a fire that is dying, and waited.

"Seven years," Lena said at last. Her voice was even. That was the frightening part. "Seven years I thought my god had left me. That I'd failed some test, or that he'd never been listening at all. Seven years I built a life on top of that grief like building a house on a grave." She raised her eyes to Brannoc's. "And you're telling me he didn't leave. He *hid*. In me. Without asking."

"I'm telling you what the design implies," Brannoc said. "I never saw the contraction happen. I don't know what he chose or why. I only know what Halveth believes is in you, and what Halveth intends to do about it." He spread his hands. "That's the whole of it. That's everything. I helped build the machine, and I ran from it, and running wasn't enough, so now I'm handing you the blueprints and hoping to God—" he almost laughed again, at that, at hoping to any god in a country being emptied of them, "—hoping it buys you all one honest day's warning that I never got."

Sefa rolled up her maps with slow precision. When she spoke, her voice had the careful flatness of a woman walking a rope bridge. "You understand the problem," she said. "You've just told us the most dangerous secret in Thornfell. Which means either you're telling the truth, in which case you're worth more to us than a wagonload of gold—or you're a lure. Sent to walk us gently toward the place where they want her delivered. Alive. Intact. Sworn." She let that sit. "How do we know which?"

"We don't," Brannoc said simply. "That's the honest answer, and I'll not dress it. Trust isn't a thing I can prove. All I can offer is this: tomorrow, at the Col of Nine Stones, there will be a Concord relay post—twelve men, a signal pyre, and a scrying-glass keyed to Halveth's own seal. If I wanted to sell you, I'd need less than a candle's burn to light that pyre. Instead I'll walk you past it through the blind ravine on its north side, a route that exists only in survey documents I drafted myself and burned copies of when I deserted. Kill me before we get there, if you decide to. But kill me *after* the col. Let my guilt at least be spendable currency before you write it off as counterfeit."

Lena stood. She did it slowly, the way she seemed to do everything—no wasted motion, no flourish—and crossed to the cave mouth, where the snow fell silver through the dark. For a long time she said nothing, and the others let her, because there are silences that demand filling and silences that forbid it, and hers was the second kind.

When she finally spoke, she didn't turn around.

"When I was sixteen," she said, "an old priest told me that Vess keeps his face turned from the proud and his hands under the falling. I asked what that meant, and he said: *you'll know when something catches you that had no reason to.*" Now she turned, and the firelight and the snow-light met on her face, and Brannoc saw that the slate of her eyes had shifted—not soft, never soft, but decided. "You had no reason to catch us, Brannoc Vey. You had every reason to sell us. So either you're the best liar the Concord ever made, or you're a man trying to hand back a debt with interest." She came back to the fire and sat, and picked up her knife, and resumed the long whisper of steel on stone. "You guide us through the blind ravine. You stand third watch. And the first time I catch you looking north toward that pyre with anything like longing, I won't ask questions."

"That's fair," Brannoc said, and meant it, and was grateful for it in a way he couldn't say aloud.

Sefa snorted softly and bent back to her copying. "Third watch," she muttered. "Gods preserve us. We've acquired a conscience with insomnia."

But it was Pip—awake, Brannoc realized, and had perhaps been awake the whole time—who settled the matter, in the way of children, who cut through the knots adults tie themselves into. The boy sat up, hair full of straw, eyes enormous in the firelight, and looked at Brannoc with the frank appraisal of someone who had already survived a press-gang and therefore considered himself a judge of dangerous men.

"You cried in your sleep two nights ago," Pip said. "You said *not the children, not the children*. Soldiers who fake that kind of dream are better actors than any I've met." He lay back down, decisively, pulling his knife close. "So. Fine. You're ours now. Don't make it weird."

Brannoc stared at him. Something in his chest that had been clenched for eleven years loosened by a single degree—not much, barely enough to notice, but enough that breathing hurt differently after it.

"Aye," he managed. "Ours."

Later, on third watch, alone at the cave mouth with the snow sifting down through the dark like the ash of everything he'd burned, Brannoc took out the last thing he owned from his old life: a scrap of vellum, folded eight times, bearing a fragment of the Unveiling's ninth-verse notation in his own handwriting. He had carried it out of the White Citadel intending, he'd told himself, to destroy it. He had carried it for three years without destroying it, which told him something about intentions.

He fed it to the watch-fire and watched the notation curl and blacken and rise.

And it was as the last corner flared—as the ink that described the opening of a living oath-bearer bloomed briefly gold and then was gone—that Brannoc saw, with the clarity that comes only to men at the end of their strength, the detail he had missed in all his careful confession. The detail that turned his stomach to ice water and sent him scrambling back into the cave, waking them, all of them, with snow melting down his collar and his voice cracking like a boy's:

"The writs," he said. "Lena—the writs. I told you they wanted you delivered to the Door. I read the order wrong. Gods forgive me, I read it *wrong*." He gripped the doorframe, breath smoking. "It's not the Inquisitor's site at the Door. The order says *deliver the bearer to the Door*—capital letters, the true name, the old name. Halveth isn't taking you to the ritual, Lena. The ritual is already moving. And it's moving *toward wherever you are*."

* * *

Outside, far to the north, beyond the Col of Nine Stones, a signal pyre caught the darkness and began, very quietly, to burn.
