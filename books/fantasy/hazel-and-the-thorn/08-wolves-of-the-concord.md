## 8. Wolves of the Concord

Sefa had learned to read rivers the way other people read faces.

Her father had taught her that before he taught her letters: *a river tells you where it's been, girl, and if you're patient, it'll tell you where it's going.* The Vellwater was telling her nothing good this morning. It ran high and grey with snowmelt from the high passes—too early for snowmelt, which was its own kind of wrong—and it carried things downstream that made her stomach tighten. A fence post. A shutter torn from a window. A dead goat, bloated and turning in an eddy like a slow, obscene dance.

The ferry crossing at Marlow's Bend was gone. Not abandoned—*gone*. The rope still hung from the far-side mooring post, but it had been cut on the near bank, and the flat-bottomed barge that should have been tethered beside it was splinters somewhere south by now. On the far shore, half-hidden in the frost-killed reeds, stood a marker Sefa recognized from her father's work: a white-painted post with three black bands.

Concord survey markers. Her father had drawn them into his maps for twenty years, always with a small contemptuous flourish, as though ink could mock what wood and paint enforced.

"They've been here," Brannoc said quietly behind her. He crouched at the treeline, his deserter's coat pulled tight, his breath steaming. "Cut the crossing so anyone coming through has to use the bridge at Kettering. And there's only one reason to funnel travelers toward a bridge."

"An ambush," Lena said.

"A checkpoint," Brannoc corrected. "Worse. An ambush you can run from."

Pip peered out from between Sefa's knees, where the boy had taken to standing whenever danger was discussed, as though she were a wall built for exactly this purpose. She wasn't sure when that had happened. She wasn't sure she minded.

"Can we swim it?" Pip asked.

Sefa looked at the water. Grey-green, fast, studded with ice shards that clicked against each other like teeth. "You'd be dead before you finished screaming," she said, then winced, because Pip was nine and she kept forgetting to soften herself around him. He didn't flinch, though. He'd stopped flinching at most things somewhere between Cinderhollow and here, and that frightened her more than the river did.

"There's a ford." She heard herself say it before she'd decided to say it, and hated the small traitor part of her that wanted to be useful, wanted to be *needed*, because being needed was how her father had died—useful to people who came asking with soft voices and hard hands. "Six miles upstream. The Hollow Ford. It's not on any Concord map because my father never sold them one."

Lena turned. In the flat white light of the false winter, her face was all planes and shadow, and the oath-scar on her wrist showed livid even from six feet away, like something under her skin trying to get out. "Your father's maps again."

"My father's maps," Sefa agreed. "It's the only thing I have left of him, so we may as well spend it."

She didn't tell them the rest. That the ford was real but narrow, that the footing was shale and lies, that her father had marked it with a single notation in his private cipher—*cold, quick, unforgiving; go at dawn or not at all*—and that dawn had come and gone two hours ago while they'd hidden from a Concord patrol that smelled of incense and oiled steel.

Some debts you paid in coin. Some you paid in hours. She was learning that hers were paid in both.

* * *

They reached the Hollow Ford as the light began to fail, which meant they reached it wrong, which meant everything after happened wrong too.

The ford was exactly as her father's cipher promised: a ragged spine of shale breaking the river's back, barely wide enough for one person at a time, the water on either side running deep and mean. Beyond it, the far bank rose into pine shadow, and beyond *that*, if Sefa's memory held, lay three days of ridgeline country where no cart road went and no Concord column could follow without axes and time.

Time was the one thing the sound of hooves said they didn't have.

Brannoc heard it first—he was always hearing things first, a habit Sefa suspected had kept him alive through years of being hunted by his own former brothers. He raised a fist, and they froze among the pines, and Sefa listened until her ears ached.

Hooves. More than four. Moving fast along the old drover's track on their side of the river, moving with the particular disciplined rhythm of riders who did not gallop but *advanced*, because men who served the Radiant Concord believed haste was a form of doubt.

"How many?" Lena breathed.

Brannoc's jaw worked. "Eight. Maybe ten. Light horses." He paused, and Sefa watched him arrive at the same arithmetic she had. "We can't outrun them to the ford. Not all of us."

"Not all of us," Lena repeated, flat.

"I can hold them at the water," Brannoc said. "Long enough."

"No," Sefa said, before she understood she was going to speak. "No one holds anything. We cross together, we scatter in the trees on the far side, and we meet—" She cast desperately through her father's routes. "The charcoal-burner's clearing. Two days north. Everyone knows it."

"The hunters will know it too," Brannoc said gently. "If they've got your father's maps, they've got every clearing in Thornfell."

"They don't have his maps." Her voice cracked on it. "I burned the duplicates myself. Every one."

That was true. That was the truest thing she owned. After the wards fell, after the stars went out and the world tilted, Sefa had sat up three nights feeding her father's duplicate charts into the stove of a safehouse in Lowmark, page by page, watching forty years of secret knowledge curl into ash, because a map in the wrong hands is a knife laid against a nation's throat. She had wept doing it. She had thought she was saving lives.

The hoofbeats grew louder.

And then, from the trees behind them, a voice called out—young, male, carrying the bored authority of someone reading from a list:

"Sefa Vane. Daughter of Aldous Vane, cartographer, deceased. You are named on a writ of information. Come out and you will be treated fairly. Your companions are named on writs of execution. They will not."

Sefa's blood went cold in a way the season couldn't account for.

*A writ of information.* Not execution. They wanted her alive, wanted her the way you want a key you haven't yet found the lock for. Which meant they knew about the archive. Which meant somewhere in the Concord's machinery, someone had already decided that Aldous Vane's daughter was worth more breathing than dead, and every instinct she had—the ones her father had honed, the ones that had kept her alive through seven years of smuggling secrets in map-tubes and hollowed book spines—screamed that being wanted alive by the Radiant Concord was a slower and crueler death than any writ of execution.

"They know my name," she whispered.

"They knew all our names," Lena said. "Mine's been on walls since Cinderhollow."

"It's different." Sefa's hands were shaking. She pressed them flat against her thighs. "Execution writs are for making examples. Information writs are for—" For people who have something the Concord needs. For people whose fathers charted doors out of dying countries. For people who are useful.

The riders emerged from the treeline.

There were eight of them, and they were not the garrison soldiers Sefa had seen pressing townsfolk in Cinderhollow—those had been farm boys in borrowed armor, scared and cruel in the way scared boys are. These were something else. Their armor was matte black lacquered over mail, unmarked by any sigil, because the Inquisitor's hunters wore no colors: they were supposed to be indistinguishable from night itself. Their horses moved in perfect formation. And at their center rode a woman in grey with a face like a closed door, holding a lantern that burned without flickering in the wind.

Witchlight. Concord witchlight, the kind that cost more per drop than a village earned in a year.

"Last chance," the young voice called. "Come out, Sefa Vane."

"We split," Lena murmured, so low Sefa felt it more than heard it. "Brannoc draws them east along the bank. I take Pip across the ford. Sefa—"

"No." Sefa heard her own voice come out hard and strange. "Nobody splits. Nobody draws anybody. We cross *now*, all of us, and we don't stop."

"You don't understand what those riders are—"

"I understand perfectly." She was already moving, sliding down the shale bank toward the ford, gravel biting through her gloves. "My father spent thirty years mapping ways out of this country for people the Concord wanted broken. I'm not going to let eight horsemen make liars of him. *Move.*"

For one suspended moment, nobody moved. Then Lena swore softly—a smuggler's curse, almost affectionate—and shoved Pip toward the water, and Brannoc came down the bank behind them, and the four of them hit the ford in a clatter of stone and spray just as the grey woman's lantern swung toward the river and the hunters wheeled.

The water was worse than her father's cipher warned. It grabbed at their legs like living hands, cold enough to burn, and the shale rolled underfoot with every step. Pip went down once, wholly under, and Sefa hauled him up by the collar with strength she didn't know she had, his gasp tearing the air, and behind them the first crossbow bolt hit the river with a flat slap and sank.

"Hold!" the grey woman's voice rang out, clear as a bell over the water. "Hold fire. The Vane woman comes out alive. The others are forfeit."

*Forfeit.* A beautiful word for it. Sefa climbed, slipped, climbed again, pushing Pip ahead of her, and the far bank came up slow as a mountain, and they were halfway—halfway, the deepest water behind them, the shallows rattling under their boots—

—that was when Pip screamed.

Not a scream of cold. Sefa turned and saw a hunter already in the river—not wading, *riding*, his black horse plunging chest-deep through the ford with the insane confidence of a beast trained for exactly this—and the man leaned down from the saddle with a hooked pole, the kind used to pull drowning bodies from water, and hooked it around Pip's waist, and lifted.

It happened in perhaps three seconds. Sefa would spend the rest of her life replaying those three seconds, frame by frame, looking for the moment she could have moved faster, chosen better, been braver. Pip's hand caught hers for one instant—small fingers, chapped knuckles, the boy who had survived a press-gang and a magical winter and her own terrible cooking—and then the pole tightened, the horse turned, and the river took her empty hands and gave her nothing but spray.

"Pip!"

She lunged back into the deep water and Lena's arm slammed across her chest like an iron bar, dragging her back, and Sefa fought her—actually fought her, clawing, screaming, the river roaring, the hunters' formation closing around the boy on the far shore like a wound closing over a splinter—and it took Brannoc too, both of them, hauling her onto the shale while Pip's scream thinned into the distance, carried off toward the drover's track and the grey woman's unhurried lantern.

"He's alive," Lena was saying, gripping Sefa's face in both cold hands, forcing her to look. "Do you hear me? They don't kill children. They *keep* children. He's alive."

Alive. The word was a plank thrown to a drowning woman, and Sefa clung to it because the alternative was walking back into that river and dying for nothing.

* * *

They ran.

There was no discussion. The remaining hunters were fording behind them, slow and deliberate, and the pine dark swallowed them mile by mile, and Sefa ran on legs that belonged to someone else, her mind circling one fixed point: *they keep children.*

Which meant Pip would be taken to Cinderhollow, or wherever the Concord kept its leverage. Which meant he was, for the moment, valuable rather than expendable. Which meant there was time. There had to be time. Time was the only currency she had left, and she had just watched eight horsemen rob her of everything else.

They stopped at midnight in a hollow beneath a rock overhang, where Brannoc built no fire and Lena stood watch and Sefa sat with her satchel in her lap and shook.

It was the satchel that finally broke her open.

Because inside it, wrapped in oilcloth, sewn into the lining where no searcher would think to look, was her father's master route-book—the original, the one true copy, the thing she had lied to the others about having destroyed. She had told them she burned everything. She had burned everything *except this*, because this was him, this was thirty years of his handwriting, his little marginal jokes, his cipher notes in the corners—*cold, quick, unforgiving*—and she had not been able to put it in the stove, and now a child had been dragged out of a river because the Concord believed the Vane maps were worth killing for, and here they were, whole and safe and warm against her ribs, purchased with a nine-year-old boy.

She didn't cry. What she did was worse. She took the route-book out and held it over the little pile of kindling Brannoc had gathered, and her hands stopped shaking, and she said, very calmly, in a voice she didn't recognize:

"If I burn this, they can't ever get it off his body or out of my pockets. If I burn this, the archive is the only copy left in the world."

"Sefa." Lena crouched across from her. In the starless dark—there was no starlight anymore, there would never be starlight again until someone did something about the hole where the sky's heart used to be—her eyes were the only points of light. "What are you thinking?"

"I'm thinking they took Pip because they think I'm the way to my father's work." Each word came out like a stone set in a wall. "So I'm going to give them a reason to think they were right. I'm going to lead them on a chase across half of Thornfell following roads that don't exist anymore, roads my father invented, roads that end in cliff faces and flooded valleys. And while they're chasing ghosts, we're going to Cinderhollow, and we're going to get him back."

"That's not a plan," Brannoc said quietly. "That's a funeral with extra steps."

"Then it's a good thing," Sefa said, "that I'm very good at drawing maps of places that don't exist."

She fed the route-book to the darkness instead of the flame—couldn't burn it, not yet, maybe not ever—but she made the decision with her whole body, and the decision changed her shape. She felt it happen. Somewhere between the riverbank and this cold hollow, the woman who had spent seven years hiding, trading, keeping her head down and her father's work out of sight, had drowned in the Vellwater along with a boy's hand slipping from hers.

What stood up out of that river was her father's daughter.

Above the overhang, the sky was a smooth black lid. No stars. No moon. Just the Concord's false winter pressing down like a thumb on a bruise, and somewhere north, a boy alone in a wagon or a cell, being told that no one was coming for him.

Sefa pressed her palms against the frozen ground and made a promise to the dark, in the language of her father's ciphers, in the language of roads that led somewhere:

*I'm coming, Pip. Learn the walls. Count the guards. Steal whatever you can reach.*

*Your family draws better maps than they do.*

Far to the south, faint on the wind, she thought she heard a church bell begin to ring—and ring, and ring, and not stop.
