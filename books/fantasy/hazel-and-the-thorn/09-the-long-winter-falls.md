## 9. The Long Winter Falls

The snow began at dusk, and it began wrong.

Lena had guided caravans through the Thornfell passes for eleven years. She knew the grammar of mountain weather the way she knew the weight of her own sword—the fat, lazy flakes that meant a storm would spend itself by morning; the needle-sleet that came sideways off the Kestrel Col and stripped paint from cart-boards; the high, thin silence before a whiteout, when the air itself seemed to hold its breath. She had trusted that knowledge with her life a hundred times, and it had never once lied to her.

This was none of those things.

The snow fell straight down, in flakes too large and too regular, each one identical to its neighbor, as if someone had cut them from paper with the same dull scissors. It did not drift. It did not swirl. It descended with the patience of something following instructions, and where it touched the ground it did not pile—it *accumulated*, rising in smooth, unbroken shelves, erasing stone and scree and the twisted pines of the treeline with the thoroughness of a hand wiping slate.

"It's not natural," Sefa said. She stood at the head of the mule, her father's coat buttoned to her chin, one bare hand held out to catch the falling white. A flake landed on her palm and sat there, whole and perfect, refusing to melt. "Lena. Look at it."

"I'm looking."

"Snow melts on skin. Even up here. Even in the dead of winter." Sefa closed her fist around the flake and opened it again. It lay in the cup of her hand like a chip of glass. "This isn't weather. This is a *decision*."

Behind them, Brannoc made a low sound in his throat—not quite a word. He had been quiet for two days, since the river crossing, since Pip. He walked with his hood up and his hands buried in his sleeves, and when he spoke at all it was in the flattened voice of a man reading from a confession he hadn't finished writing. "The Concord called it the Long Winter," he said now. "In the ritual texts. I thought—I told myself it was metaphor. Inquisitors love their metaphors." He watched the snow erase the road behind them, mile by mile, closing the passes they'd climbed. "It's not a metaphor. It's a siege engine. You don't have to burn a country out if you can starve it under six feet of obedient snow."

Pip should have been walking beside him, complaining about the cold, asking how much farther, doing the small relentless work of being eleven years old in the mountains. The space where Pip walked was just space now. Lena kept catching herself turning to check on the boy—her eyes dragging to the left rear of the column out of pure habit—and finding nothing there but falling snow. Each time it was a small knife going in at an angle she hadn't braced for.

*We're coming for you,* she thought at the snow, at the Concord, at the whole white silent country. *Hold on.*

They climbed. There was nothing else to do. The lower passes were closed—Sefa's maps said so, and the smoke rising from three separate valleys confirmed it, greasy black columns that were bridges burning, waystations burning, the smugglers' roads being unmade behind them with bureaucratic thoroughness. That left the Shepherd's Stair, a route so old it predated the maps, a goat-track that switchbacked up the shoulder of Mount Vael through country where even Lena had only been twice, and never in winter.

By full dark the snow was knee-deep and still falling, and Brannoc was stumbling, and the mule was done—Lena could see it in the animal's ears, the flat miserable set of them, the way it planted its feet at every rise. They needed shelter or they needed to stop pretending they were going to survive the night, and Lena had spent enough years keeping people alive to know which lie she preferred.

"We need walls," she said. "Tonight."

"There's nothing on the Stair," Sefa said. "My father charted it twice. There's a shepherd bothy at the third switchback, but it's a roof and three walls at best, and it'll be drifted shut—"

"There's something better than a bothy." Lena pointed up and to the left, where the mountainside went dark against the darker sky. "See that notch? There's a village up there. Hollowmere."

Sefa stared at her. "That's not on any map."

"No."

"Lena. My father charted this entire range. If there was a village—"

"Then your father never found it, because Hollowmere doesn't want finding." Lena adjusted the mule's lead and started up. "Ward-touched," she said, when Sefa didn't follow. "People the old wards marked. Children born during ward-tides, shepherds who heard the stones singing, that kind of thing. The shrines used to send them food in hard winters and the Concord called it witchcraft, so they came up here and built their own village and let the mountain keep their secrets." She glanced back. "I carried grain up this track once, seven years ago. Before Vess went quiet. They paid me in cheese and never asked my name."

"And you think they're still there?"

"The snow's wrong everywhere else," Lena said. "Maybe it's right here."

* * *

Hollowmere existed, and the snow did fall wrong there—but wrong in the other direction.

They came over the last rise near midnight, half-frozen, the mule led by main force, and looked down into a bowl of a valley where perhaps forty houses huddled around a frozen millpond, and the snow lay soft and ordinary and deep, drifted against doorways, crusted on thatch, catching the orange light that leaked through shuttered windows exactly the way snow was supposed to. Smoke rose from chimneys in honest, wind-torn columns. Somewhere a dog barked, twice, and stopped, as if satisfied.

At the valley's edge, where the Shepherd's Stair dropped in, the snow simply ended—a clean line across the track, like the edge of a tablecloth. Beyond it, the Concord's paper-flakes kept falling into darkness. Inside the line, real snow, real night, real cold.

Brannoc stopped so suddenly that Sefa walked into his back. "A ward," he whispered. "A living one. Someone is *holding* this—" He turned to Lena, and in the light spilling up from the valley she saw his face was wet, though whether from wind or something else she couldn't say. "Do you understand what this means? The Concord pulled down the great wards, the anchored ones, the ones gods died holding up. But the small ones. The local ones. The ones woven by hand, by people, without any god at all—they can still hold. They're holding *right now*, above us, while the whole sky comes apart."

"Then we knock politely," Lena said, "and hope they remember me."

They remembered her.

Not her name—she'd never given it—but her face, or rather the shape of her: the tall woman with the scarred wrist who'd brought grain the year the shrine-stone cracked, who'd taken no payment beyond cheese and a night by the fire, who'd listened to old Marta sing the warding songs without laughing. Word ran ahead of them up the track as they descended—young voices shouting through the snow, doors opening, light blooming window by window until the whole bowl of the valley glowed—and by the time Lena reached the first house, an old woman was already standing in the doorway with a lantern held high, squinting through the falling dark.

"You," the woman said. "Grain-woman."

"Hello, Marta."

Marta looked at her for a long moment. Then at the others—the mapmaker with her father's coat, the big broken soldier, the mule, the empty space where a child should have been walking. Something moved behind her eyes, some arithmetic of need and risk, and Lena watched her do it and waited, because it was not her arithmetic to do.

"Inside," Marta said finally. "All of you. Bring the mule to Byram's barn. And grain-woman—" Lena paused on the threshold. "Whatever you're running from. It doesn't come in here. Understand? Whatever it is."

"It won't," Lena said. "I'll see to it."

* * *

They fed them first. That was the law of the house, Marta said, older than any other law: no talk, no questions, no bargains until bellies were full. So Lena sat on a bench beside strangers and ate barley stew with winter roots and a heel of dark bread, and drank tea brewed from pine needles and dried rowan berries, and felt the warmth go down into her like water into cracked ground, filling places she hadn't known were empty.

The house was Marta's, and it was full—not only of Marta but of two other families who'd crowded in for the evening, the way mountain folk did in deep winter, sharing fire and food because a shared fire cost less than two fires. Children peeked at the strangers from behind loom-frames. An old man mended a net by the hearth with fingers gone stiff and precise. Nobody stared rudely. Nobody asked anything. But Lena felt their attention the way she felt weather—the ward-touched sense of things, maybe, or just the sharp attention of people who had survived seventy years of being hunted by noticing everything.

When the bowls were empty, Marta sat down across from Lena, folded her hands, and said, "Now. Tell me why the sky is dying."

So Lena told her. Not all of it—she kept Vess inside her own chest, kept the oath and the anchor and the Door folded away in the private room of her heart where she kept such things—but she told the shape of it. The wards fallen. The stars gone. The Concord pouring through the passes, conscripting towns, posting execution writs. The snow that obeyed orders. Pip, taken at the river. The Door, and the map, and the road they still had to walk.

She did not mention the Door. She mentioned the road, and needing to cross the high country before the passes sealed entirely, and Marta listened with her head tilted, and when Lena finished, the old woman was silent for a long time, feeding a stick to the fire.

"The Concord came to the foot of our valley in autumn," Marta said at last. "Two riders, white cloaks, very polite. They said the old ways were ended, that the wards were lies, that any who clung to them were enemies of the Radiant Dawn." She smiled, and it was not a warm smile. "I gave them tea. Rowan berry. Then I showed them the edge of our snow-line and invited them to step across it, and told them what my grandmother told me happens to those who cross uninvited." She shrugged. "They rode away. We haven't seen them since. But they'll come back, grain-woman. With more than two riders, next time. Wards woven by hand are strong, but they're a lamp in a dark field. Everyone can see where they burn."

"How long has yours held?"

"Since the tides broke, four nights past. My granddaughter wove it—Tam. She's got the touch strongest of anyone since my grandmother." Marta's voice did something complicated on the word *granddaughter*, and Lena understood: the girl was exhausted, holding a valley against the sky, and would hold it until she dropped. "She sleeps two hours a night and stands at the boundary-stone the rest of it, singing. The snow listens to her. For now."

Lena looked into the fire. She thought about Pip in a Concord cell in Cinderhollow, about Sefa's burned maps, about Brannoc sitting by the wall with his head in his hands, carrying the design of the world's unraveling in his skull like a swallowed stone. She thought about the Door, and the choice waiting at the end of the road, which she had not yet said aloud to anyone and which sat in her like a second heart.

And underneath all of it, quieter than any of it, she heard herself think: *I could stay here.*

The thought arrived fully formed, wearing comfortable clothes. Stay. Take a corner of this valley, a share of this fire. Teach these people to fight, to hide, to move cargo through the white country the way she'd moved it for eleven years. Run grain instead of secrets. Watch Tam's ward hold, season after season, while the Concord's empire of ash froze solid outside the snowline. Warmth as rebellion. Bread as defiance. A life that asked nothing of her except to keep showing up, keep feeding people, keep the lamp lit.

She had spent seven years building a life of small illegal kindnesses. Here was a whole village built of nothing else.

"You're thinking of staying," Marta said.

Lena blinked. "Am I that easy to read?"

"You've got the look. Every runner gets it eventually, the ones with good hearts. They find a place like this and they start measuring it for a bed." Marta leaned forward. "Let me tell you what I know about the look. The ones who stay, stay because they're running *from* something, and the valley becomes a hiding place, and hiding places rot you slow. The ones who leave, leave because they're running *toward* something, and the valley becomes a memory, and memories keep you warm on the road." Her eyes, pale and clear as ice-water, held Lena's. "Which are you, grain-woman?"

Lena didn't answer. Across the room, Sefa had fallen asleep sitting up, her father's coat wrapped around her, one hand still curled around the strap of her map-case even in sleep—as if the maps might be taken from her the way everything else had been taken. Brannoc slept too, finally, deeply, the sleep of the truly spent, and even sleeping his brow was furrowed, as if the ritual texts were chasing him through his dreams.

A child crept close to the fire—an actual child, a girl of six or so with Tam's same dark hair—and studied Lena with the frank stare of the very young. Then she crossed the floor, solemn as a little priestess, and pressed something into Lena's hand: a carved wooden bird, small enough to hide in a fist, its wings swept back mid-flight.

"For the road," the girl said. "Tam says travelers need something that remembers how to fly."

Lena looked down at the toy in her palm, and her throat closed, and for one long dangerous moment the thought of staying rose in her again like floodwater—stay, stay, grow roots, be somebody's neighbor, carve birds for children—

But the bird had wings swept back. Mid-flight. Not perched. Not resting.

"Thank you," she managed, and the girl nodded once, gravely, and went back to her mother.

* * *

Near midnight, Marta woke her.

Lena had taken the watch by habit, sitting by the banked fire with her sword across her knees, and Marta came and sat beside her without ceremony, two old soldiers of different wars sharing a quiet hour.

"Your friend," Marta said. "The mapmaker. She's awake. She's standing at the edge of the ward-line looking at the snow." A pause. "You might want to see what she's seeing."

Lena found Sefa at the boundary-stone, breath steaming, staring out into the Concord's paper-dark. Beyond the line, the false snow had changed. It no longer fell straight down. It had begun to *pile*—fast, deliberate, rising in smooth white ramparts along the valley's rim, sealing the passes, walling the high country in. And down in the far distance, faint as a scratch on glass, red light flickered against the clouds. Cinderhollow, burning, or something worse.

"They're not trying to starve us anymore," Sefa said quietly. "Look at the shape of it. They're building a lid. They're closing the country." She turned, and in the light of Tam's ward her face was grim and awake and certain. "Lena—the Door. Whatever it is, wherever it is—we have days, not weeks. Maybe less. And Pip is down there, under that light."

Lena stood beside her at the stone, the little wooden bird tight in her fist, and watched the lid close over Thornfell.

"Then we leave at first light," she said.
