## 12. The Devil You Swore To

The snow stopped falling at dusk, and that was the first wrong thing.

Lena noticed it the way a sailor notices the sea go flat—no wind, no sound, the flakes hanging in the air like ash over a fire that had forgotten to burn. The village of ward-touched outcasts had given them a loft above the shrine-house, straw mattresses and a brazier of peat, and Sefa had fallen asleep mid-sentence with her father's map still folded in her fist. Pip was two days free of Cinderhollow and slept like something drowned, curled against Brannoc's snoring bulk. Outside, the false winter pressed its white silence against every seam of the world.

Lena lay awake and listened to her own blood.

That was the other wrong thing. For seven years she had lived inside Vess's silence the way one lives inside a walled garden—aware of the walls, fond of them even, but no longer hearing what moved beyond. Now the silence had texture. It breathed. When she closed her eyes she could feel it behind her sternum, vast and patient and *hungry*, and it felt less like absence than like something holding very, very still so she would not notice it was there.

She should have slept. Her body ached from the river crossing, from the gorge, from carrying Brannoc half a mile with an arrow through his shoulder before they'd gotten him to shelter. But sleep came for her anyway, the way debt comes for the poor—suddenly, and all at once, and without asking.

* * *

She dreamed of the pass where the stars had gone out.

Not as it had been—as it might have been, if the night had kept its promises. Stars in their thousands, wheeling slow and silver over the black teeth of the mountains, and beneath them the road she'd walked a hundred times, familiar as the lines on her palm. She stood on it barefoot in the snow and felt nothing, which was the third wrong thing, because in dreams you always feel the cold.

"Hello, Lena."

The voice came from everywhere and nowhere, the way a voice comes when someone speaks through water, or through stone, or through seven years of deliberate quiet. It was not the voice she remembered. The god she had sworn to at nineteen had sounded like winter wind through a high pass—clean, cold, impersonal, a voice made for oaths. This voice was hoarse. Frayed. It sounded like a man who had been shouting into a locked room for a very long time and had finally learned no one was coming.

"You're dead," she said, to the stars, to the road, to whatever wore her god's silence like a stolen coat. "Brannoc said you were silenced. The Concord cut you out of Thornfell like a tumor. I watched the wards fall. I watched the stars die. You are not here."

"No," the voice agreed, and there was something terrible in how gently it said it. "And yes."

The snow around her feet began to move. Not melt—*move*, swirling upward in a slow spiral, gathering, taking shape the way frost takes shape on glass: fern-patterns first, then limbs, then a suggestion of shoulders, a head bowed under a weight she couldn't see. A figure made of snowfall and starlight-that-wasn't, translucent as breath on a window. Where its face should have been there was only a deeper dark, and in that dark, faintly, the shape of antlers—or scars where antlers had been.

"I know what Brannoc told you," it said. "He told you true, as far as he knows it. They came for me with their ritual and their knives of consecrated glass, and they did not find me, because I was not where they looked." The figure raised its head. "They looked in the sky."

Lena's oath-scar woke.

It had been cold since the night the stars went out—a band of old ink and older magic circling her left wrist, the mark of her swearing, dormant for years. Now it burned like a brand, and through the burning she felt something turn over inside her chest, enormous and slow, like a whale sounding in deep water. Something that had been sleeping in her for seven years and had just heard its name called.

"No," she said again, and her voice cracked on it. "No. That's not—I would have *known*. I would have felt you. Seven years, Vess. Seven years I talked to a wall. I lit candles at your shrines and got nothing back, not a whisper, not a warmth, nothing, and you're telling me you were—" She pressed her hand flat against her own sternum, against the turning, the vast slow weight of it. "You were *in here*. Sleeping. While I grieved you."

"Yes."

The word fell between them like a stone down a well. She waited for more—for explanation, for apology—and understood, with a fury that made her vision swim, that none was coming quickly. Gods did not apologize. Gods explained themselves the way avalanches explained themselves: after the fact, and mostly to the survivors.

"Why?" The word tore out of her. "Why me? Why without asking? Do you know what I did with those seven years? I built a whole life on top of your grave. I learned to be *orphaned*. I got good at it. And the whole time you were squatting in my ribs like a—like a smuggler's cargo, like contraband, hiding in the one place they'd never search because they'd have to open *me* to find you—"

"Yes," the snow-figure said again, softer. "That is exactly what I did. And I am ashamed of it."

Lena stopped. Of everything the voice had said—that was the thing she had not prepared for. In seven years of silence she had imagined every possible reunion: the cold greeting, the divine indifference, the god who would explain that mortals were candles and gods were wind and what had happened was simply weather. She had rehearsed her anger against indifference. She had no armor for shame.

"You don't get to be ashamed," she said, but it came out wrong, came out small. "Shame is for people who could have done otherwise."

"I could have done otherwise." The figure took a step toward her, and where it stepped the snow did not crunch—it *listened*. "I could have let them take me. I could have scattered myself across the wards the way my brothers and sisters did, thin enough that the ritual found only mist. I chose instead to pour what remained of me into my last sworn servant—the one whose oath ran deepest, whose faith had never once wavered, whose body I knew better than my own temples. I chose you, Lena Amberdale, and I did not ask, because I knew what you would say."

"You didn't *know*—"

"You would have said yes." The dark where its face should be tilted, almost tender. "You would have said yes because you loved me, and because you were twenty-three years old and brave in the way that gets people killed, and you would have said yes and then spent the rest of your life wondering if you'd mattered at all. I could not bear to make you a martyr. So I made you a hiding place instead, and I told myself it was mercy." A pause, long as a held breath. "It was not mercy. It was theft. I stole the shape of your life and filled it with myself, and then I slept, because sleeping was the only way to be quiet enough to survive, and I left you alone in a world that thought you were nothing but a smuggler with a scar."

The stars overhead shuddered. Somewhere far off in the dream, thunder rolled—not real thunder; the sound of the Concord's engines, maybe, or the sound of a country being unmade, carried even here.

Lena sat down in the snow. She hadn't decided to; her legs simply ended their argument with her. She sat with her knees drawn up and her branded wrist cradled against her chest, and she stared at the god-shaped absence standing over her, and she wept—not the clean grief of the funeral she'd held for Vess in her heart seven years ago, but the ragged, furious weeping of a woman who has just learned her mourning was wasted.

"I lit candles," she whispered. "Every restday. At every shrine I passed, even the desecrated ones. I talked to you. I told you about Sefa's maps. About Pip learning his letters. I told you things I never told anyone living, because you were the only one I trusted with them, and you were *asleep*."

"I heard none of it." The figure knelt across from her, and the snow of it pooled like spilled milk. "And that is the second theft. You gave your secrets to a wall. I am sorry. I have been sorry for seven years, in the thin dreaming place where sorry was all I had room for."

"What do you want?" She wiped her face with the heel of her hand, rough, soldierly. "Gods don't visit dreams for apologies. What do you want from me?"

The figure was quiet for a moment. When it spoke again, the hoarseness had changed quality—less like exhaustion now, more like hunger. She recognized it despite herself. It was the hunger she'd heard in Brannoc's voice when he described the extraction rite. It was the hunger of something that had been starving politely for a very long time.

"The truth first," it said. "Then the choice. And I will give you the choice honestly, which is more than any god has ever given anyone, and more than I deserve to give you now."

"Truth."

"They are going to finish it." The stars flickered overhead, dimming by degrees, as if the words themselves were smothering them. "The Grand Inquisitor. The ritual Brannoc helped design was never meant to kill us—we cannot be killed, only thinned, scattered, starved. It was meant to make us quiet enough to be *found*. Every god in these mountains is now either scattered mist or a starving ember hidden somewhere the Concord is hunting for, inch by inch, valley by valley. And they have a machine, Lena. A great engine of glass and scripture, built in the Door's antechamber, that can open anything. Locks. Wards. People." The dark face turned toward her wrist. "Oath-bearers."

"I know. Brannoc told us. They want to open me and pour you out."

"They want more than that." The voice dropped, and the snow around them grew colder, though she still could not feel it—only knew it, the way you know things in dreams. "An opened bearer gives them one god. But the Door—the last Door out of Thornfell—is keyed to the oldest ward-anchor still living. To me. If they open you at the antechamber, they do not merely capture a starving god. They capture the key. They close the Door forever, and Thornfell becomes what the Inquisitor has always dreamed: a sealed vessel, godless and gateless, where no one ever leaves and no god ever answers and the snow falls until everyone forgets there was ever anything else."

The false winter outside the dream seemed suddenly very close. Lena thought of the village below, the outcasts feeding strangers with no questions asked. She thought of Pip's laugh, rusty from disuse, surfacing more often now. She thought of Sefa's hands smoothing the map flat, over and over, the way you touch a thing you love to prove it's still there.

"And the choice?" she said.

The figure rose. It was taller than it had been—or she was smaller, kneeling in the snow of her own dream—and when it extended a hand of falling starlight, she saw that the palm was cracked, fissured, like old glaze on a broken pot. Starving. Dying, maybe, if gods died slowly enough that dying looked like this: a long fading, a patience running out.

"I can be poured back into Thornfell," it said. "Through you. Your oath was the vessel that hid me; your oath, freely returned, can carry me home. Into the wards. Into the stones and the passes and the old shrines. I would burn through the false winter like sun through fog. The stars would return. The borders would wake. The Concord's engine would find itself trying to unmake a country that was, once more, defended." Its hand trembled, and the cracks widened, and Lena understood with sick clarity how little of it was left—how close it had come, in its seven-year sleep inside her, to simply winking out. "But oaths are not coin, Lena. They are not something you spend and keep. An oath poured out is *everything*. Every memory bound into the vow. Every person you swore beside, fought beside, loved. The road. The names. The faces. All of it goes into the fire with the god, because that is what the oath is *made of*, and there is no other fuel."

The dream went very still.

"You're telling me I forget them," Lena said. Flat. Careful. The voice she used for delivering bad news to families.

"I am telling you that you will not know them." The figure's voice frayed to a whisper. "Sefa. Pip. Brannoc. The village that fed you. The roads you learned. The woman you became on those roads—all of it was built on the oath, and the oath must burn entire. You will walk out of the wards reborn into a country you saved, and you will not remember saving it. Strangers will thank you and you will not know why. Children will speak your name and it will mean nothing. You will be a woman with empty hands and a full country."

"And the alternative."

"Walk through the Door." The stars wheeled, indifferent, beautiful, restored for the length of a dream. "Take Sefa's map. Take Pip's hand. Leave Thornfell to its winter and live. Live long, live warm, live *remembering*—that is the one thing the Concord cannot take from you if you run. And I will fade. Slowly. Perhaps another year. Perhaps less. And the Door will close when I go, and the country will end, and you will carry that too—but you will carry it *whole*, with your memories intact, which is more than most survivors of endings ever get."

Silence. The kind of silence she knew intimately—the silence of Vess, seven years of it, and now she finally understood it had never been absence at all. It had been a god biting down on a scream.

"You said you wouldn't choose for me," she said. "Last time you didn't ask. This time you won't choose. Which is it, Vess? Are you a thief or a coward?"

The figure flinched—actually flinched, the snowfall of it scattering and regathering—and when it spoke, the hoarse voice was almost human in its weariness.

"Both. I was a thief, and I am sorry, and I will spend what remains of eternity being sorry. And now I am a coward, yes, because I have seen what happens when gods decide for their servants. Look at the Concord. Look at Maren Kestrel, who was promised her dead god back if she delivered yours, and who believes it, because a god once decided for her too, long ago, in ways she has never questioned." The dark face lowered. "I will not add my choice to the pile of choices that broke this country. I hid in you without consent. I will not leave you without it. Ask me anything, hate me as long as you need, and then choose—and whichever you choose, I will not argue, and I will not haunt you, and I will not call it betrayal."

"That's not mercy either," Lena whispered. "Making me carry it alone."

"No," the god agreed. "It isn't. I have very little mercy left. I spent most of it staying quiet while you mourned me."

* * *

She woke with tears frozen on her lashes and the brazier burned to gray ash, and for a moment she did not know where she was—which was absurd, and frightening, and a small cold rehearsal of the thing she had just been offered.

The loft. The village. Straw and peat-smoke and Brannoc's snore. Through the frost-feathered window, the false winter hung motionless, flakes suspended in air like a held breath, and beyond the mountains, where the stars should have been, nothing.

Her wrist burned. Inside her chest, something vast turned over in its sleep and murmured, in a voice like wind through a high pass, like a man shouting into a locked room:

*I'm sorry. Choose well. Or don't. Either way, I will love you, and I will not follow you.*

Lena lay in the dark and did not sleep again. Below her, in the shrine-house, she could hear the outcast women moving quietly through the pre-dawn cold, banking fires, setting out bread for strangers. Saving people who would forget them, if she chose one way; abandoning a country that had fed her, if she chose the other.

When dawn finally came—gray, starless, late—it brought hoofbeats on the frozen road, and a rider at a gallop, and Sefa bursting up the ladder with her father's map clutched in both fists and terror written across her face in the language Lena had learned to read on the road.

"Lena," she gasped. "Pip's clerk sent word. We know where they're keeping him—and Lena, it's the same place. The ritual site. The Door. It's all the same place."

Lena looked at her—at the chapped hands, the map-creased fingers, the girl who had burned her inheritance to save them—and felt the god stir beneath her heart like a tide answering a moon.

She had until the Door to decide.

She was afraid it would not be long enough.
