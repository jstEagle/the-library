## 13. Ambush at the Glass Bridge

The gorge had a name once, Sefa thought, though no one remembered it now. The maps her father drew called it the Serpent's Throat—a wound in the mountains so deep that the river at its bottom ran black even at noon, and the only way across was a bridge of ice the smugglers called the Glass.

She saw it first as a seam of light. They had climbed since before dawn through pine forest silvered with rime, and when the trees finally broke apart, there it was: a frozen fall of water spanning the gorge like a blade laid edge-up between two cliffs. The sun caught it and set it burning, blue-white and terrible, and for one long breath Sefa forgot to be afraid.

Then she remembered what beautiful things were for, in Thornfell, in this winter, with the Concord's hunters on their trail, and she stopped forgetting.

"It won't hold all four of us at once," Brannoc said. He stood at the lip of the gorge with his hood back, gray-streaked hair whipping in the wind that funneled up from the black water below. His face was drawn; he had not slept well since Cinderhollow, since Pip's name had been spoken aloud between them like a debt come due. "Ice that clear is ice that's thin. It sings when it's sound. Listen."

Sefa listened. The wind moved over the bridge and the bridge answered—a high, glassy note, almost sweet, like a wet finger circling the rim of a cup.

"Sound," Lena said quietly. "Good."

"Go one at a time," Brannoc said. "Spread your weight. Don't stop in the middle, whatever you see, whatever you hear. The middle is where it dreams."

"The middle is where it *what*?"

But Brannoc was already uncoiling rope from his shoulder, checking knots with hands that had done this ten thousand times, and Sefa understood he would not answer questions he considered beneath the business of staying alive.

They argued about order—of course they argued; they were four people who had learned to trust each other precisely because none of them trusted easily—and settled on Sefa first, because she was lightest and because someone had to fix the far anchor line. Then Brannoc. Then Lena, with Pip's small weight of absence riding her shoulders like a second pack. Pip was three days gone now, taken at the river crossing while Sefa burned her father's duplicate maps into ash and drifting embers, and none of them spoke his name more than necessary, because speaking it made the space where he should have been too large to bear.

Sefa crossed the Glass with her eyes fixed on the far cliff and her father's voice in her head—*weight is a promise you make to the ground, girl; keep it small*—and the ice sang under her boots the whole way, one long held note that rose near the middle into something almost like a word, and she did not look down, and she did not listen to what the word might be.

On the far side she drove the pitons, set the anchor, and turned to watch the others the way you watch people you love do something dangerous: with your heart in your teeth.

Brannoc came next, steady as a mason. Lena came last, and halfway across, the world ended.

* * *

It began as a wrongness in the light.

The morning had been hard and clean, sun on snow, shadows sharp as knife-cuts. Then the shadows on the western rim—the rim behind them, the rim they had just left—began to move against the sun. Figures detaching from figures. Black cloaks drinking the brightness. Sefa counted six, then eight, then lost count, and her stomach dropped through the floor of her body.

"HUNTERS!" she screamed, and her voice cracked across the gorge like a whip. "LENA—RUN—"

Lena ran. Sefa would remember that always—not hesitation, not the freeze of fear, but Lena Amberdale hitting the ice at a dead sprint with the anchor rope paying out behind her, boots striking sparks of frost, the bridge singing under her in a shriek now instead of a note.

The hunters reached the near lip. And at their head, unhurried, walking as though she owned the morning and everything it touched, came a woman in white-and-gold plate beneath a traveling cloak, her helm carried under one arm, her hair cropped short and iron-gray at the temples.

Even at that distance, even across two hundred feet of singing ice, Sefa saw Lena stop dead.

Saw her turn.

Saw her say a name that the wind tore away—but Sefa read the shape of it on her lips, and later, when Lena finally gave the name its weight, Sefa would know she had guessed right:

*Maren.*

"Maren Voss," Brannoc breathed beside her. He had gone the color of old tallow. "Gods below. She came herself."

"You know her?"

"I *designed half her weapons*." His voice was flat with horror. "Sefa, listen to me. That's not a hunting party. That's the Inquisitor's own—those are the Hollow Men behind her, and where Maren Voss walks, the extraction rite follows. They didn't come to kill Lena." His throat worked. "They came to *open* her."

Below, Lena had started moving again—not toward them. Toward the middle of the bridge, where the ice sang loudest, where she planted herself with her sword drawn and her back to the far cliff, facing the woman she loved like a sister and could not outrun.

"No," Sefa said. "No, no, *no*—" She grabbed the anchor rope. "Brannoc, we pull her out, we—"

"She'll never make it. The Hollow Men will be on the ice before she's halfway." Brannoc was already moving, rope over his shoulder, pitons in his fist, and there was something in his face Sefa had never seen there before—not fear, not guilt exactly, but the terrible calm of a man doing arithmetic he'd been dreading his whole life. "Get to cover. Both of you. Now."

"What are you—"

"*Now*, Sefa."

He went over the cliff edge onto the bridge before she could stop him.

* * *

Later, Sefa would try to reconstruct those minutes and fail. Memory broke them into shards, the way the Glass itself broke under fire.

She remembered Brannoc crossing fast, recklessly fast, the ice screaming under him, and the Hollow Men stepping onto the bridge from the far side in perfect silence, eight figures in gray that seemed to drink the sound of their own footsteps.

She remembered Maren Voss raising one gauntleted hand, and the hunters halting, and the whole gorge going still enough that Sefa could hear the black river muttering far below.

She remembered Lena shouting—words torn to ribbons by wind, but the shape of them unmistakable. *Maren. Maren, please. You knew me. You taught me. You held my oath-scar when I took it and told me it was an honor.* And Maren standing at the bridge's end with her helm under her arm and her face like carved stone, answering in a voice the wind mostly stole, except for the last words, which arrived across the ice cold and complete:

"—the Concord promised me my god back. What did your silence ever give you, Lena? Walk away from them. Come home."

And Sefa remembered thinking, with a clarity that felt like blasphemy: *she means it. She isn't cruel. She's grieving.* Two women broken by the same silence, pointed like knives in opposite directions.

Then Brannoc reached the middle of the bridge, and everything became arithmetic.

He didn't go for the Hollow Men. He went for the ice.

Sefa watched him drive both pitons into the bridge at its singing point, watched him kneel and set something against the ice—a charge, alchemist's work, his own design, gods help him, his own design—and watched him look up and find her on the far cliff, and smile.

It was not a brave smile. It was an apologetic one. It said: *I'm sorry. I'm sorry. This is the only currency I have left, and I am spending all of it.*

"BRANNOC!" Lena's voice, raw, understanding one heartbeat too late.

"For Thornfell!" he roared, and it wasn't a battle cry, it was a confession, it was the words he should have said seven years ago when he helped write the ritual that killed the stars. "For every god they silenced—for every door they locked—FOR PIP—"

He struck the charge.

The Glass Bridge did not shatter. It *sang*—one enormous, crystalline chord that filled the gorge wall to wall, that Sefa felt in her teeth and her sternum and the backs of her eyes—and then the middle hundred feet of it came down all at once, silently, beautifully, like a chandelier falling in a dream.

Brannoc went with it.

So did three of the Hollow Men. The rest fell back from the edge, and Maren Voss stood at the broken brink with her hand still half-raised, staring down into the gorge where the black water swallowed the ice and the man upon it, and Sefa—watching through tears she did not remember starting—saw something cross the commander's face that was not triumph.

Lena screamed. Sefa heard her scream from across the whole width of the sky, one long tearing sound, and then Lena was running again, running the last stretch of bridge toward the far side, toward Sefa, toward the anchor line, while behind her the hunters regrouped and Maren's voice rang out across the ruin, no longer gentle:

"Seal the south rim! They'll run for the high pass—take them alive, the Bearer must not be marked!"

Hands seized Sefa's shoulders—she shrieked, struck out—then Lena's voice, close, real: "It's me. It's me. Cut the rope, we're going, *cut it*—"

They cut the anchor and ran.

* * *

They ran until the gorge was a gray memory behind them and the light went copper and then blue and then dark, until the trees closed around them and the hunters' horns faded into wind. They ran past the point of exhaustion into the country beyond it, where the body moves on borrowed time and the mind goes strangely quiet, and when they finally stopped—in the hollow root-cell of a lightning-struck fir, snow sifting down through the broken crown like ash—Sefa discovered that she was holding Lena's hand and had been for hours, neither of them acknowledging it, neither letting go.

Lena sat with her back against the roots, staring at nothing. Snow gathered on her shoulders and in her hair and she did not brush it away. Her sword lay across her knees, still drawn, as if part of her expected the fight to resume at any moment and the rest of her had simply forgotten the war was paused.

"He's not dead," Sefa said.

Lena didn't move. "You saw him fall."

"I saw him fall into water. Moving water, Lena. Black, deep, freezing—but moving." Sefa heard her own voice climb and forced it down. "Brannoc has survived things that would've killed a mountain goat twice over. He designed that charge himself—he'd have known exactly how the blast would throw him. If anyone in this cursed country could fall a hundred feet into a river and walk out the other side of winter, it's him."

Silence. The wind moved through the firs above them, and somewhere far off a horn sounded once and died.

"You saw her face," Lena said at last. Her voice was gravel and ash. "At the bridge. When he went down. Tell me what you saw."

Sefa hesitated. "Grief," she said. "Or something wearing grief's coat."

"Maren doesn't grieve for enemies." Lena's thumb moved over the hilt of her sword, back and forth, a small mechanical motion, like a prayer worn smooth. "Seven years I served beside her. She pulled me out of the Whitefall avalanche with her bare hands. She taught me the oath-words. When I took the scar, she held my wrist and cried, and I thought—that's what faith looks like. That's what love of something bigger than yourself looks like." A breath that shook on the way out. "And now she hunts me for the same reason. Because she loves something bigger than herself, and it told her I'm the enemy. The Concord didn't break her, Sefa. It *aimed* her."

"And us?" Sefa asked quietly. "What did it do to us?"

Lena looked at her then—really looked, the way she rarely let herself, green eyes catching the last gray light—and something passed between them that needed no words. We happened to ourselves, the look said. Badly, slowly, with scars. But ourselves.

"We get him back," Lena said. "Brannoc, and Pip. Both of them. Whatever it costs." She sheathed her sword at last, and the sound of it was very final in the dark. "I spent seven years being silent, Sefa. Being careful. Being nobody. I watched stars go out and told myself survival was enough." Her jaw tightened. "Maren wants to open me like a letter she thinks is addressed to her god. Fine. But she's about to learn what else is written inside."

Sefa unrolled her father's map by feel, the two halves aligned now, the route to the Door traced in a dead cartographer's patient ink. In the dark she couldn't read it, but she didn't need to. She had walked it in her mind every night since the shrine.

"The ritual site is three days north," she said. "Through the pass the outcasts showed us. If the Inquisitor begins the unmaking before we arrive—"

"Then we arrive first." Lena rose, pulling snow from her hair like a woman shedding a skin. Above the trees, through a tear in the clouds, a single star glimmered—false, maybe, or a planet, or a piece of the old wards flickering where Vess stirred beneath Lena's heart. Sefa chose to believe it was a star.

"One more thing," Sefa said, and her voice betrayed her, cracking on the words. "The bridge. What Brannoc did. He didn't hesitate, Lena. He looked at me and he *smiled*, and I will hear that smile until I die, and I need—I need to know we're going to make it mean something. Tell me we're going to make it mean something."

Lena crouched, took Sefa's chilled face in both scarred hands, and pressed their foreheads together, the way soldiers do, the way family does.

"We make it mean something," she said, "starting now. Three days, Sefa. Three days, and then we take back everything they've stolen—one boy, one map, one god at a time."

Far to the north, past the black teeth of the pass, a light that was not a star kindled briefly against the clouds—gold-white, rhythmic, pulsing like a heartbeat or a countdown—and neither of them saw it.

But the mountains did.
