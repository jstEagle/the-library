## 15. The Godless City

Pip had learned to count in the dark.

Not sheep, not prayers — the Concord didn't like children praying to anything but the Radiant One, and Pip had never liked anything the Concord liked. He counted instead: the drips that came through the ceiling of the Cinderhollow holding cells, one every eleven heartbeats when it was cold and one every nine when the furnaces below stoked up. He counted the boots on the night watch, twelve pairs, one of them dragging its left heel because a man named Corporal Vessick had a limp he was too proud to have seen by a healer. He counted the days since they'd taken him at the river crossing, scratching them into the underside of the bunk with a bent nail, until the nail broke and then he scratched them into his memory, which was harder and which was maybe the point.

Forty-one days.

He was not going to cry about it. Crying got you moved to the small cell where the light never came on, and the small cell made you forget things, and Pip had decided early — day three, maybe, when the clerk with the ink-stained fingers had slid him an extra heel of bread without looking at him — that forgetting was the only thing he would not do. Not Sefa's maps burning orange in the river wind. Not Brannoc's laugh, big as a barn door. Not Lena's hand on his head, heavy and warm, saying *stay low, stay smart, we're coming*.

They were coming. He'd made himself believe it the way you believe in spring: not because there was proof, but because the alternative was unbearable and Pip had a talent for refusing the unbearable.

The transfer came on a morning with no morning in it. Cinderhollow's sky had been grey wool for weeks — the Concord called it the Veiling, said it was holy, said the stars had been sinners all along — and the prisoners were marched out under lanterns into air that tasted of ash and cold iron. Twenty of them, roped wrist to wrist, most of them ward-touched folk from the villages, people whose eyes did a faint silver shine in lamplight like coins at the bottom of a well. Pip walked in the middle of the line because middles were safer than ends. He kept his head down and his ears open, which was how he'd survived everything so far, and it was why he heard the wagon axle crack before anyone else did.

It cracked on Cobbler's Hill, where the road out of Cinderhollow narrowed between two burned-out granaries, and the lead wagon lurched sideways, and the escort sergeant swore in the particular bored way of men who thought the war was already won.

That was the first mistake. The second was that only six soldiers were watching twenty prisoners, because the other eight had drifted toward a smell — someone, somewhere ahead, was roasting chestnuts over a brazier, and even Radiant soldiers were still men with stomachs.

The third mistake belonged to the city itself.

Later, Pip would hear a hundred versions of what happened next, and none of them would agree, and that was how he'd know it was true. A riot doesn't have a first moment; it has a hundred small ones, all leaning on each other like drunks. But for Pip it began with a chestnut seller — a round woman in a patched coat, face like a dumpling, whom he'd seen before through the cell bars being questioned about her brother, who was ward-touched and missing. She stood at the edge of the square with her brazier, and as the prisoner line passed she looked straight at Pip, and Pip looked back, and something passed between them that had no words in it except maybe this: *well? are we doing this or aren't we?*

She kicked her brazier over.

Chestnuts went skittering across the cobbles like startled beetles, coals spinning sparks, and the soldiers flinched — trained for ambushes, not for snacks — and in that flinch the whole hidden city exhaled. A shutter banged open above the granary. A flowerpot followed it down and caught a soldier on the helm with a sound like a dropped pot, because it was one. Someone screamed *the wards are coming back!* from a rooftop, which wasn't true yet, but lies like that, shouted loud enough, have a way of becoming true out of sheer stubbornness. Doors opened along the whole street — doors that had been shut and barred for forty-one days of occupation — and ordinary people came out of them carrying the tools of ordinary lives: cleavers, boat hooks, boiling water, a rolling pin wielded by a grandmother with the technique of someone who had raised seven sons.

The rope around Pip's wrists went slack. He looked down and found the clerk beside him — the ink-fingered one, Joss, who brought him bread and pretended it was contraband — sawing through the knots with a stolen guard-knife, hands shaking so hard he could barely hold it.

"Go," Joss whispered. "Cell block's open. I unlocked everything last night. I don't know why I'm telling you that, I've never done anything brave in my life—"

"You're doing it now," Pip said, and shoved him behind a rain barrel as a crossbow bolt buried itself in the barrel lid with a *thock*.

Then Pip ran, because staying low and staying smart also meant knowing when the smart move was speed.

* * *

He found them at the old tannery gate, because of course he did. Sefa had taught him on the road: if you're ever lost in a town, find the water and follow it upstream, because smugglers always nest near water, near walls, near ways out. The tannery sat on the millrace, and leaning against its gate with a hood up and a knife out was a silhouette Pip knew better than his own heartbeat.

"Lena!"

The hood came back and there she was — thinner, harder around the eyes, a scar on her jaw he didn't remember giving her time to earn — and she crossed the distance and grabbed him by the shoulders hard enough to bruise, turning him around, checking him like cargo, like he was something worth inspecting for damage.

"Forty-one days," Pip said, breathless. "I counted."

"I know," Lena said. "I could see the tally marks on your arms when they paraded you past the wall." Her voice did something strange on the last word, a crack running through ice. "We've been here nine days. Sefa's been mapping the tunnels off her father's notes. We were coming for you tomorrow night."

"Tomorrow's boring," Pip said. "Today there's a riot."

Behind her, Sefa emerged from the gate shadow, and the look on her face when she saw him was the look of someone finding a thing they'd told themselves they hadn't lost. She didn't hug him. She took his chin in her hand and examined his eyes, his teeth almost, like a horse trader, and her hands were trembling, and Pip understood that this was how Sefa hugged: with her whole ferocious attention.

"They fed you?" she demanded.

"Joss the clerk fed me. There's a whole thing. I'll explain later. Also—" he grinned, and it hurt his face because he hadn't used those muscles in weeks, "—I stole something. Well. Memorized something. The Inquisitor's ritual site. It's under the Cathedral of the Unveiled Sky, and the Door's key-stone is there too, in the reliquary vault. I heard the clerks arguing about it. They keep it close because they're scared of it."

Sefa went very still, the way she did when a map suddenly drew itself in her head. "You're certain?"

"Ma'am, I've been counting drips for forty-one days. I am *extremely* certain."

Brannoc wasn't there. Pip noticed the shape of his absence immediately — the group had a Brannoc-shaped hole in it, the way a mouth has a tooth-shaped hole — and when he looked at Lena, she gave a small shake of her head that closed the subject like a door. Later, then. Grief kept its own schedule.

"Can you get us in?" Lena asked. "Through the tunnels?"

Pip rolled his shoulders like a man accepting a commission. "I've been listening to guards complain about the drains for six weeks. The old tannery race runs under the cathedral district — my father helped dig half of Cinderhollow's cellars before the Concord came. I know where the walls are thin." He paused. "Also the city's kind of on fire right now, so we should probably move while everyone's busy."

Above the rooftops, smoke was standing up in columns, and bells were ringing wrong — not the Concord's measured peal but a chaotic clanging from tower to tower, the old alarm pattern, the one that predated the Radiance, that grandmothers remembered and bell-ringers had been waiting years for permission to use.

Lena looked at the smoke, then at Pip, and something in her face shifted — the mercenary mask sliding aside for just a moment to show the person underneath, the one who pulled children out of press-gangs on instinct.

"Lead on, then," she said.

* * *

Under Cinderhollow was another Cinderhollow, older and truer.

Pip led them down through the tannery's drain-mouth, into brickwork older than any of the warring gods' quarrels — arches laid by masons whose names nobody remembered, channels cut when the city's business was leather and dye and the honest stink of work. The millrace ran dark and quick beside their feet, loud enough to cover voices. Sefa moved with her father's map unrolled in her mind, correcting it aloud in a whisper — *he had this junction wrong, the flood of '61 collapsed the east passage* — and Pip realized with a jolt that she was trusting him, actually trusting him, letting his knowledge braid with hers, and that trust felt heavier and warmer than any coat.

They weren't alone down there.

At the first junction, a lamp swung into view and a voice hissed *"Who goes?"* — and it was a dyer's apprentice Pip half-recognized, a boy of maybe sixteen with blue-stained hands and a kitchen knife held like he'd only ever held carrots. At the second junction it was two women from the fishmarket with cudgels and a hand-cart full of stones. At the third, a whole family was camped against the tunnel wall — father, mother, three kids, bedrolls and a cookpot — refugees of the street fighting above, and the mother pressed hot flatbread into Pip's hands as he passed without asking who he was, because that was the rule tonight and everyone seemed to know it: *feed whoever comes, ask nothing, the Concord takes inventory and we refuse to be inventory.*

"It's spreading," the dyer's apprentice told them, falling into step. "Started at Cobbler's Hill and now it's everywhere. The weavers came out. The coal-heavers came out. Old Mother Anthea walked into the garrison square with her walking stick and dared them to arrest her and there were too many of us watching, so they *didn't*, and once they don't arrest one person they can't arrest anybody, do you see? Fear only works while it's private. Tonight everybody's fear got public."

Pip walked faster after that, because his throat had gone tight and he didn't want anyone hearing him swallow.

That was the thing he'd understood in the cell, counting drips, and it was the thing the whole city seemed to understand at once tonight: the Concord's power wasn't in its soldiers. It was in the agreement — the quiet, tired agreement of ten thousand people to be afraid separately. And agreements could be broken. All it took was one chestnut brazier kicked over, one grandmother with a stick, one clerk unlocking doors he'd been hired to lock, one child memorizing the location of a key-stone because a child with nothing left to lose was the most dangerous creature alive.

Ordinary people, choosing each other. Pip carried that phrase in his chest like a coal.

* * *

They surfaced in the cathedral district through a coal chute behind a chandler's shop, and the world above was a storm made of people.

Fires burned in barrels at every corner. The Concord's banners — white sun on black — hung torn from the customs house, and someone had painted over the sun with a crude circle of stars, badly, gloriously, in whitewash that dripped like melting snow. Soldiers held the cathedral steps in a shield-wall, but the crowd around them wasn't attacking; it was simply *there*, thousands strong, singing — singing! — an old threshing song that every child in Thornfell knew, the kind of song that had no god in it at all, just hands and harvest and the stubborn arithmetic of getting through winter together. The song bounced off the cathedral's stone and came back doubled.

"The Inquisitor will be below," Sefa murmured, studying the building's flank. "If the ritual starts with the city in chaos—"

"It's already started," Pip said quietly. "The ground's been humming since yesterday. Didn't you feel it?"

And they had all felt it, he realized — a vibration in the soles of the feet, deep and rhythmic, like a heartbeat under the floor of the world. Like something enormous turning over in its sleep. Or waking up.

Lena's hand had gone to her oath-scarred wrist. In the firelight Pip saw the scar shining faintly, silver as the ward-touched folk's eyes, pulsing — Pip would have sworn it pulsed — in time with the hum.

"He's down there," Lena said. Not a question. Her voice sounded like it was coming from very far away and very close up at once. "Vess. Under the cathedral. I can feel him pulling."

"Then we go down the way I showed you," Pip said, "and we get the key-stone before the Inquisitor finishes whatever he's finishing." He looked up at her, at Sefa, at the burning sky and the singing crowd, and he planted his feet the way he'd watched Lena plant hers before every fight. "I counted forty-one days in the dark so I could be here for this. Nobody's sending me back to the cells."

For a long moment nobody spoke. Then Sefa put her hand on his head, briefly, roughly, the way you'd steady a compass needle.

"No," she agreed. "Nobody's sending you anywhere."

Lena drew her blade and nodded toward the coal chute, toward the humming dark beneath the godless city that had stopped being afraid.

"Stay low, stay smart," she said — the old words, the road words, home words.

"We're coming," Pip answered, and led them down.
