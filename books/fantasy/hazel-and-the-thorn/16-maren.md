## 16. Maren

The tunnels beneath Cinderhollow smelled of wet stone and old smoke, and somewhere above us the city was burning in the good way—the way a forge burns, the way a hearth burns when someone has finally decided to come home. Pip's directions had been scratched into the wall in charcoal at every junction, a child's shorthand that Sefa had learned to read in an afternoon: *three lefts past the broken cistern, then down where the rats gave up.* We moved through the dark like water finding its old channels.

I told them I was going alone.

Sefa argued. Of course she argued. She had her father's coat pulled tight around her shoulders and the two halves of the Door-map folded against her heart like a second heartbeat, and she stood in the torchlight with her chin up and her jaw set and told me that we did not split up, that this was the rule, that I had made the rule myself somewhere on the frozen roads between Cinderhollow and the Glass Bridge.

"I'm not asking," I said.

"You're not asking," she repeated, flat. "You're walking into the arms of the woman who signed your death writ, alone, in a city full of soldiers, the night before we breach the Door. That's not courage, Lena. That's just a slower way of dying."

Behind her, Brannoc's absence stood in the room like a man. Nobody had filled the space he'd left. Nobody would.

"She knew me," I said. "Before. Before the silence, before the writs, before all of it. If the Inquisitor's ritual needs an oath-bearer, then Maren knows better than anyone alive what's inside me. And if something goes wrong tomorrow—if I don't come back—someone has to know what she knows." I looked at Sefa, and then at Pip, who was trying very hard to look like a soldier and mostly looking like a boy who had spent a winter in a cell. "You two get to the antechamber. That's the mission. Maren is mine."

Pip said, "You come back," in the voice children use when they are not asking permission either.

"I come back," I said, and I was surprised to find that I meant it, and more surprised still to find that meaning it felt like a vow—not the old kind, sworn to a silent god with cold iron on my wrist, but a new kind. A warmer one. The kind you make to people who will notice if you break it.

Sefa pressed a knife into my hand. Not my knife. Hers, with the worn bone grip her father had carved. "Then take this," she said. "And take this too." She tapped my sternum, once, hard. "The part of you that came back for us at the river. Bring that. Leave the rest."

I went down where the rats gave up.

* * *

The old shrine district of Cinderhollow had been the first thing the Concord burned when they took the city, seven years ago. They had toppled the statues, salted the garden-plots where offerings used to grow, and raised their white barracks on the foundations. But the undercrofts remained—vaulted chambers beneath the ruined temples where smugglers had once stored contraband wine and where, I suspected, the Concord now stored things it did not want its own soldiers thinking about.

I found her there, in the undercroft of a temple whose god I had never learned the name of. There was no god in it anymore. There was a table, and a lamp, and Maren.

She was sitting with her back to the entrance, which told me two things: that she had heard me coming from thirty paces, as she always had, and that she had decided to let me. Her sword lay on the table in front of her, unsheathed, the blade catching the lamplight like a strip of frozen river. She had not changed. Seven years, a war, an execution writ with my name on it—and she sat the same way she had sat at a hundred night-fires on the border, elbows on knees, watching the dark for whatever came out of it.

"You always walked too loud for a scout," she said, without turning. "I always told you. Heel to toe, Lena. Heel to toe."

"You always listened too hard for a commander," I said. "I always told you. Some of us like our ghosts."

She turned then, and the lamplight found her face, and I made myself look at what the years had done. Grey in her close-cropped hair. A scar I didn't know, running from her ear down into her collar. And her eyes—her eyes were the same. That was the cruelest part. The same grey-green, the same way of looking at a person as if measuring the distance to their throat and their heart and deciding, every time, to aim for neither.

"You got my letter," she said.

"I got your writ."

"Same paper." She almost smiled. It was a terrible almost. "Sit down, Lena. Or stand. You were never any good at being still."

"I'm good at it now." I stayed by the door, the way you stay when you've learned that rooms with Maren Vale in them have exactly two exits and she's already counted them. "Seven years, and you never came for me yourself. You sent other people. Why not you?"

"Because I asked them not to succeed." She said it simply, the way she'd once reported the weather on the high passes. "Every hunter I sent after you was the best I had and the least I trusted to do it properly. Brannoc was supposed to be the exception. I'm sorry about Brannoc."

The grief went through me like cold water through a cracked jug. "You don't get to be sorry about him."

"No," she agreed. "I don't. But I am, and I've learned the Concord has no use for that, so I spend it where I can." She pushed the lamp a little to the side, and the light fell across the table between us, and I saw what else lay there. Not papers. Not orders. A small wooden figure, hand-carved, worn smooth by years of a thumb worrying it—a figure of a woman with her arms raised, holding up a sky. "Do you know who this is?"

I knew. My whole body knew before my mind caught up. "That's Vess."

"That's Vess," she said. "The way the border-folk carved him, before. Before the Concord came. Before the silencing." Her thumb moved over the figure's raised arms, back and forth, back and forth, a gesture so familiar it made my chest ache, because I had watched her do it a thousand times over a thousand night-fires and never once asked what she was holding. "You think I don't remember him? You think any of us who served the border wards could forget? He was in the wind off the pass. He was in the way the wards sang when the wolves came. We all felt him go quiet, Lena. All of us. The whole border went deaf the same night, and the Concord told us it was a mercy. That a dead god's silence was cleaner than a dying god's screaming." Her thumb stopped. "I was the one who reported the wards' collapse. Did you know that? I wrote the report that let them do everything that came after."

"Maren—"

"They promised me." She looked up, and her eyes were wet and furious and absolutely steady, all three at once, which was the most Maren thing about her. "They came to me after, the Inquisitor's people, and they said: the god is not dead, Commander. He is *hidden*. And we know where. And when our work is finished—when every false god in these mountains is unmade and Thornfell is clean—we will pour him back out of his hiding place. We will restore him. Whole. Alive. Singing in the wards again." She laughed, and it was the worst sound I had heard since the Glass Bridge. "And I believed them. Do you understand? I sat in their white chapels with their white candles and I believed them, because I wanted it so badly that belief was the only thing left in me that still worked."

The oath-scar on my wrist went cold. It had been cold for seven years, but this was a different cold—the cold of a thing being *named*.

"They're not going to restore him," I said. "You know that now. That's why you're down here in a dead god's basement with a sword on the table and your back to the door."

"I know that now." She said it like a confession torn out with the roots. "I've known it since the winter. Since the starving villages. Since I saw what they did at the ritual sites to the ward-touched children they captured. A god they meant to restore, they would not torture his people. You don't break what you're building a house for." She stood, finally, and she moved like she always moved—like a blade being drawn slowly, so as not to startle anyone—and she came around the table and stopped an arm's length from me, close enough that I could see the lamplight trembling in her eyes. "So. Here we are. You came down here alone, into my hands, the night before the biggest operation of your little smuggler crusade. Either you're the finest fool I ever trained, or you came for something. What did you come for, Lena?"

"The truth," I said. "And I think you just gave it to me. But there's one more thing."

"Ask."

I drew Sefa's knife. I saw her register it—saw the flicker in her eyes that said *so this is how it ends, then*—and I did not point it at her. I set it down on the table, beside the little carved god with his raised arms, and I stepped back from both.

"You signed my death writ," I said. "And I've had seven years to hate you for it, and I did, I hated you so thoroughly and so professionally that it was practically a second career. And now I'm standing here with a knife in reach and a reason older than the writ, and every lesson you ever taught me is screaming at me to end this before it ends me. Because that's what you trained me for, Maren. That's what the order was. We were the people who did the necessary thing before it became the possible thing."

"Lena—"

"But I'm not the order anymore." My voice cracked, and I let it. "I spent a winter learning that. I learned it from a mapmaker who burned her father's life's work to save a boy she'd known a month. I learned it from a deserter who died buying us ten minutes with a broken body and a smile. I learned it from a child who stole the location of the most guarded place in Thornfell with a stolen pen and a clerk's boredom. They taught me something the order never did, Maren. They taught me that the necessary thing and the right thing are different things, and that the distance between them is where you live, if you're lucky. If you're loved."

She hadn't moved. She was looking at the knife on the table, and then at the little wooden god, and I watched her understand, all at once, what I was offering. Not mercy exactly. Mercy was for the defeated. This was something else. This was a door, held open, with no promise on the other side except that it was not a cell.

"They'll execute you for what you're about to do," she said quietly. "Whatever you're about to do. You know that."

"I've been executed on paper for seven years. I'm told the paperwork is very thorough."

That almost-smile again, and this time it made it all the way to her eyes, and it was terrible and beautiful and it was *hers*, and I thought: there she is. There's the woman who taught me heel to toe. The Concord didn't make her. The grief made her. The Concord just found her grief and gave it a uniform and a lie to live in.

"The Inquisitor begins at dawn," she said. "The antechamber beneath the old citadel. There are three ward-rings around the ritual floor and the outer two answer to Concord command-words, but the inner one—" she paused, and I watched her weigh the last thing she had, the last coin of her old life, "—the inner ring answers to me. I set it myself, six months ago, when I still believed I was building a house for a god. It will open for my voice and no other. That's what you came for. That, and the truth. And you have both, and now I have nothing, so." She squared her shoulders, the old parade-ground square, and lifted her chin. "Say what you came to say, Lena. That I'm under arrest. That I'm your prisoner. Take me up the tunnel in chains and hand me to your mapmaker and your boy and let them decide what to do with the woman who signed your writ."

"No," I said.

Her brow furrowed. In seven years of war I had never once seen Maren Vale confused, and I filed it away, selfishly, as a treasure.

"No chains. No prisoner. You walk out of here beside me, up the tunnel, into a room full of people with every reason to hate you, and you tell them what you told me. About the inner ward-ring. About the children. About the lie they built you out of. And then, when it's done—when the Door is open or the wards are burning or whatever tomorrow turns out to be—you decide what you are. Not the Concord. Not the order. Not me. You."

"And if I decide wrong?"

"Then you decide again," I said. "That's the whole trick, Maren. Nobody ever told us. You just keep deciding, until one of the versions of you is someone you can live with."

She stood there a long moment, in the lamplight, in the dead god's house, with her sword on the table and her god in her hand. And then she did something I had never seen her do in all the years I had known her, in all the years of frost and border-fires and the singing wards: she reached out and took the little wooden figure of Vess, and she closed her fist around it, and she bowed her head over it the way you bow over a wound.

"He was in the wind," she said, so quietly I almost lost it. "I want to hear him again. Even once. Even at the end. I want to hear him in the wind."

"He's in the wind," I said. "I promise you that much. He's closer than they know. He's closer than *I* knew, most of my life." I held out my hand. "Come and see."

She looked at my hand for a long time. Then she sheathed her sword—slowly, deliberately, a blade being put away rather than drawn—and she took the lamp in one hand and my hand in the other, and her grip was steady and warm and shaking, all three at once.

"Heel to toe," she said.

"Heel to toe," I agreed.

We went up together, out of the dead god's undercroft, into the burning-good burning of a city choosing itself, and behind us the little temple stood empty at last, which is not the same as godless—Sefa would have told me that, Sefa with her maps and her father's coat—and ahead of us the tunnels ran dark toward the old citadel, toward dawn, toward the Door.

And as we climbed, in the wind that moves through even the deepest tunnels, the one that comes from nowhere and goes everywhere, I heard it—or I dreamed I heard it, and I have stopped believing there is a difference.

A heartbeat. In the stone. In the wind. In my wrist, where the scar lay cold no longer, but *warm*.

Maren stopped on the stair. Her head came up, sharp as a hound's.

"Lena," she said. "What is that?"

"That," I said, and I was smiling, and I could not have stopped if the Inquisitor himself had been standing behind me, "is the sound of tomorrow going wrong for the Concord. Come on. We've got a Door to open."

And far below us, beneath the old citadel, the Inquisitor's great machine began, in the dark, to sing.
