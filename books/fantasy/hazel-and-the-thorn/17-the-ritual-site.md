## 17. The Ritual Site

The mountain had a throat, and we were crawling down it.

That was how I thought of the tunnel—my father's maps called it the Gullet, a lava tube from some eruption older than Thornfell itself, running down beneath the citadel like a swallowed secret. The walls sweated warmth that had no business existing in a winter like this one, and every few paces the rock gave off a faint mineral smell, iron and sulfur and something else underneath, something almost sweet, like incense burned down to its last bitter inch.

I walked with my father's map held open against my chest, both halves of it now, stitched together along a seam of candle-wax and grief. Brannoc's coat still hung on my shoulders. I hadn't washed out his blood. It felt like the kind of thing you weren't supposed to wash out.

"Talk to me, Sefa," Lena said behind me. Her voice was low, pitched to carry in stone. "What's ahead?"

"Fork in four hundred paces. Left branch dead-ends at a cistern—the Concord flooded it when they took the citadel. Right goes down another half-mile and opens into what my father labeled *the Vestibule*." I tapped the old ink with one finger. "He drew this forty years ago, before the Concord ever dreamed of the Door. He wrote one word across the antechamber."

"What word?"

"*Threshold.*"

Lena was quiet for a moment. In the dark I could hear her breathing, steady as a metronome, and under it—faint, so faint I might have imagined it—that heartbeat sound, the one that had started in her wrist on the stairs and hadn't stopped since. It got louder the deeper we went. Like walking toward a drum.

"And Pip's information?" she asked.

I heard rather than saw Pip straighten up somewhere behind her. The boy had not stopped talking since Cinderhollow—not really talking, more narrating, filling the silence the way children fill silences, because silence is where the bad things live. But when he spoke about the ritual site, he spoke differently. Carefully. Like someone reciting scripture he'd stolen.

"The wards are keyed to *song*," Pip said. "The clerk told me. Not words—nobody sings words into a ward, that's how they catch you. It's a hum. Three notes, low ones, the kind you feel in your teeth. The guards have to hum them going through or the ward takes the heat right out of their blood." A pause. "He showed me. He thought I was just a dumb kid who liked music."

"You are a dumb kid who likes music," I said.

"Yeah," Pip agreed comfortably. "But I'm a dumb kid who likes music that opens doors."

Brannoc would have laughed. The thought arrived whole and uninvited, the way thoughts of the dead do, and I let it pass through me without stopping, because if I stopped for it I would stop for all of them, and there wasn't time. There was never again going to be enough time.

We came to the fork. The left passage breathed cold and wet; the right breathed something else entirely. Warmth, yes—but a wrong warmth, a furnace warmth, and threaded through it a sound I felt before I heard: a vast, slow vibration rising through the soles of my boots, through my shinbones, through the roots of my teeth.

The machine. Singing.

My father had charted mountains his whole life. He used to say that a mountain was just a slow argument between fire and stone, and that a good mapmaker listened for which side was winning. Standing at that fork, listening to the Concord's engine grind away beneath the earth, I understood that they had found a way to make the argument themselves. To hurry it along. Stone losing. Fire winning. Everything patient in the world being rushed toward an ending.

"Right," I said, and folded the map away, and led us down into the throat of the thing.

* * *

The tunnel opened without warning.

One step we were in blackness and dripping stone; the next, light—light everywhere, sourceless and white, pouring down from a ceiling so far above us it might have been sky. We stood at the mouth of a gallery carved into the living rock, a crescent of a chamber wide as a cathedral nave, and below us, down a sweep of steps broad enough for a hundred pilgrims, lay the antechamber of the Door.

I had imagined it my whole life. My father had drawn it from secondhand accounts, from a smuggler's drunken description, from a fragment of pre-Concord scripture—and even so, his drawing had been too small. Too human. The Door itself stood at the far end of the chamber: not wood, not stone, not metal, but a standing absence, a shape cut out of the world, taller than three men and edged in a thin gold light that moved like water finding its level. Around its frame ran carvings worn nearly smooth by centuries of hands. People had touched this. Thousands of them. People who wanted out, or wanted in, or only wanted to put their palms against something older than their fear.

And between us and it, the Concord had built their altar.

It sat in the center of the floor like a wound: a ring of black iron perhaps thirty feet across, inscribed with channels that glowed a sullen red, and within the ring—gods forgive me, gods, if any of you are left to hear me—within the ring knelt the oath-bearers.

There were nine of them. I counted twice because the first count made me sick and I didn't trust myself. Nine figures in gray shifts, kneeling in a circle around a central pillar of glass and iron, each one bound at wrists and throat with bands that pulsed in time with the machine's song. They were alive. That was the horror of it—they were alive, and awake, and their mouths were moving. Praying, maybe. Or singing back. Their oath-scars shone through their sleeves like brands seen through cloth, and with every pulse of the machine, light traveled *out* of them, up the channels, toward the pillar, toward—

Toward the Door. Which flickered. Which guttered, like a lamp in wind.

"They're drinking the Door," Lena whispered beside me. I hadn't heard her come up to the lip of the gallery. Her face in the white light was terrible and calm. "They can't close it, so they're starving it. Feeding it nothing but silence until it forgets how to be open."

Above the altar, on a platform of black stone, stood the Grand Inquisitor.

I had expected—monsters always disappoint me; I've read enough of my father's marginalia to expect horns, or at least theatrical robes. The Inquisitor wore plain gray. A small person, narrow-shouldered, with a voice that carried anyway, that carried the way ice carries across a frozen lake, cracking outward in all directions:

"—and when the threshold is quiet, we begin the counting. Every god in these mountains has hidden itself inside a servant. Every servant will be opened. And when the last name is unwritten, Thornfell will be *clean*."

Clean. As if the country were a floor. As if everything living in it were dirt.

Beside me, I felt Lena go very still—the particular stillness of a drawn bow.

"There," she breathed. "The pillar. Do you see what's mounted in it?"

I looked. Set into the glass column at the altar's heart, cradled in iron claws like a heart in a ribcage, was a stone. Small. Unremarkable. Gray as river slate.

Except it was the only thing in the entire chamber that cast no reflection.

"The key-stone," I said. "Pip—"

"I know," Pip whispered. "Third level of the pillar housing. Clerk said it comes out with a counterweight release, but the release is *inside* the ward perimeter." His jaw set, and for one moment he looked so much like a soldier that my chest hurt. "Inside the circle. Where the kneeling people are."

* * *

We made our plan in the dark of the gallery, heads together, voices under the machine's song.

Maren knew the guard rotations—she'd helped write them, she said, in a voice like chewing gravel, another debt she was paying off in installments. Two sentries on the gallery above us, four on the floor, twelve in the outer corridors, changing at the bell. The bell rang every quarter hour. She could hear it coming before it sounded; she'd been listening to Concord bells since before Pip was born.

"We take the gallery sentries silent," Maren said. "Then the floor. Then Sefa and the boy go for the stone while Lena and I hold the stairs."

"No," Lena said.

Everyone looked at her.

"Lena—" I began.

"It knows I'm here." She held up her wrist. Through the skin, faint as a coal under ash, the scar-light pulsed. "It's known since the shrine. Maybe since the stars went out. The moment I step onto that floor, every ward in this room is going to turn and look at me, and the Inquisitor is going to get exactly what she came for." She looked around at us, and her voice dropped to something almost gentle, which from Lena was more frightening than shouting. "So I'm going to be loud. I'm going to be the loudest thing in this mountain. And while every eye in the room is on me, my family is going to steal a rock."

"That's suicide," Maren said flatly.

"That's Tuesday," Lena said. "Ask them."

Pip laughed—one sharp, terrified bark of a laugh—and then pressed both hands over his mouth. And I understood, standing there in the white dark with a dead man's coat on my shoulders, that this was what my father had died without ever finding on any map. Not the Door. This. The moment when people decide, together, quickly, without ceremony, that they will spend themselves for one another. No cartographer charts it. It doesn't hold still long enough.

"The bell," Maren said quietly. "Two minutes."

I kissed my father's map—both halves, the seam, the whole impossible road of it—and put it away forever.

"Positions," Lena said.

* * *

The first sentry died without knowing it. Maren's knife did its work the way snow does—softly, completely. The second turned at the last instant and Lena caught him mid-turn, hand over mouth, and lowered him down like setting down something breakable. I watched her do it and thought: *this is what seven years of silence taught her hands.* Grief makes such strange skills.

Then the bell rang, and the floor changed shifts, and for ninety seconds the antechamber belonged to no one.

We went down the steps in a line, hugging the wall, and the machine's song rose around us until I could feel it rearranging my heartbeat, trying to sync me to it, trying to make my blood keep its time. The oath-bearers knelt in their ring, mouths moving, scars shining. Close up I could see their faces—young, old, one of them no older than Pip—and I made myself look at every single one, because someone should. Because looking was a kind of witness, and witness was the only weapon I had brought.

At the base of the pillar, the counterweight housing waited: a hatch of black iron, unmarked, humming.

Pip slid forward on his belly like he'd been born in gutters, which, in fairness, he mostly had. His fingers found the release mechanism by touch alone, eyes closed, lips moving—counting notes, I realized. Humming the three low notes under his breath, feeling for the false one, the lock-note, the note that didn't belong.

"There," he breathed. "There it is, the ugly one—" And his small hand pulled, and the hatch swung open on oiled hinges, and the key-stone sat in its cradle six inches from his fingers.

Which was when Lena stepped out onto the center of the floor, alone, arms spread, and said in a voice that cracked across the antechamber like the ice on a spring river:

"Inquisitor. You wanted an oath-bearer. Here I am. Come and count."

Every head turned. Every ward flared. The machine's song stumbled—for one glorious, staggering moment, the whole vast engine of the Concord lost its rhythm—and in that gap of silence, Pip's fingers closed around the key-stone.

The Inquisitor looked at Lena for a long, long moment. Then she smiled, and it was the worst thing I saw that night, worse than the kneeling, worse than the dying light of the Door. She smiled like a woman whose trap had finally, after years, caught exactly the animal it was built for.

"Anchor," she said softly. "Open her."

The wards came down like a net.

* * *

I don't remember deciding to run. I remember the key-stone burning cold in my fist, and I remember Pip's hand in mine, and I remember screaming Lena's name as the light took her—as bands of white fire snapped around her wrists, her throat, her oath-scar, dragging her toward the altar while she fought, while she fought like ten soldiers, while the oath-bearers in the ring lifted their heads as one and *looked* at her with recognition, with hope, with hunger—

The Door flickered. Once. Twice.

And then, through the machine's roar, through the Inquisitor's chanting, through the chaos of Maren's blade meeting three guards at the stair-foot—I heard it. Faint. Rising. From the direction of the upper galleries, from the tunnels, from the whole wounded body of the mountain above us:

Bells. Not Concord bells.

Cinderhollow's bells. The revolt had found the way down.

Pip squeezed my hand hard enough to hurt. "Sefa," he said, "the stone's getting *hot*."

In the pillar's empty cradle, the wards were already beginning to fail—and the Door, the great patient Door my father had spent his life chasing, began, slowly, impossibly, to swing inward.

On the other side of it, something was waiting.
