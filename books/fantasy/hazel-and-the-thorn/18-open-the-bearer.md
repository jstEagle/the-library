## 18. Open the Bearer

They took her boots first.

Lena noticed that, later, when she had nothing left to notice but small things. The Inquisitor's acolytes had unbuckled her boots with careful, almost tender hands before they strapped her into the chair of black iron and pale bone at the heart of the ritual chamber, and the loss of them—stupid, human, the cold stone under her bare soles—had frightened her more than the manacles. Boots meant roads. Boots meant running. Without them she was not a smuggler or a mercenary or an oath-bearer. She was cargo.

The chamber itself was older than the Concord. That much even Lena could read in it, and she had never been a scholar. The walls were fitted stone gone glassy with age, veined with channels that might once have carried water and now carried something else—a slow silver light that pulsed in time with her own heartbeat, because of course it did. Everything in this room beat with her. She was the drum this whole cathedral of pain had been built around.

Above her, carved into the vaulted ceiling where a god's face should have been, someone had chiseled the Concord's sunburst and filled the grooves with gold. It caught the light of a hundred tallow candles and threw it back down at her like a smirk.

Her wrists were bound in iron cuffs lined with wool. She understood why—the wool, the care, the padded leather straps across her chest and thighs and throat—and understanding made it worse. They were not torturing her. Torture would have been simpler. They were *harvesting*, and you do not bruise the fruit.

"You're awake." The voice came from behind her head, unhurried, almost kind. "Good. I prefer my bearers awake for this. Sleep is a kind of lie, and I have sworn off lies."

The Grand Inquisitor came around into her field of vision, and Lena saw him properly for the first time since the bridge, since the nets, since Brannoc's blood on the ice. He was smaller than his reputation. A narrow man in dove-grey robes, clean-shaven, with the soft hands of someone who had never carried anything heavier than a pen. His eyes were the color of weak tea, and they looked at her the way Sefa looked at an unfinished map: measuring, delighted, already seeing what could be made of the blank spaces.

"Do you know," he said, drawing a stool close and sitting, folding his hands, "how long I have waited for a bearer of Vess? Not one of the little gods—the river-whispers, the hearth-mothers, the things the mountain folk mistake for saints. Those we have opened before. They come out like pus from a wound, and the wound closes, and the town forgets by spring." He tilted his head. "But Vess. The Warden. The Silence that was a shield all along. Seven years your god has hidden inside you, Captain Amberdale, and in seven years no rite of ours has found so much as an echo of him. Do you know why?"

Lena said nothing. Her throat was raw from screaming on the march, and besides, she had learned long ago—in Maren's company, of all places—that silence is a weapon you keep sheathed until the last possible moment.

"Because he isn't hiding *in* you," the Inquisitor continued, warming to his subject like a lecturer. "He *is* you. Or rather—" he raised one finger, correcting himself with scholarly precision, "—you are the shape his absence makes. When he poured himself out of the wards, he needed a vessel that would not merely contain him but *be* the containment. An oath-bearer. Someone who had given him everything—name, history, future. You gave him your girlhood, didn't you? Hazel." He smiled at her flinch. "Yes. We found the records. A novice named Hazel, who swore herself to a silent god at fourteen and walked out of the cloister as someone new."

"I walked out," Lena rasped, "because your order burned the cloister."

"And because there was nothing left there for Hazel. Only for you." He leaned forward, and his tea-colored eyes were gentle, which was the most obscene thing about him. "I want you to understand that I take no pleasure in what follows. What I do tonight, I do to save this country from the tyranny of unseen masters. Every god is a chain, Captain. Every prayer a leash. Thornfell will be free—not free the way smugglers mean it, free the way a cell is free of windows—but free. And you will be our proof that divinity can be *extracted*. Refined. Put to use."

"Put to use," Lena repeated.

"The wards were his work. His power still runs in the old stones, sleeping. With him unmade and rendered—distilled—we can rebuild the border ourselves. A wall no god controls. A wall *we* control." He stood, smoothing his robes. "You should feel honored. Out of every soul in Thornfell, you are the keystone."

He snapped his fingers, and the acolytes moved.

* * *

They had told her, in the order, what extraction felt like. They taught it to novices as a warning, the way you teach children about wolves: *the opening of a bearer is the death of two souls, the god's and yours, and no oath survives it.* Maren had described it once, around a watchfire, in her flat commander's voice: *It doesn't hurt the way a blade hurts. It hurts the way drowning hurts—slow, and from the inside, and worst right before the end.*

Maren had been wrong about one thing. It was worse than drowning, because drowning takes only your breath, and this took everything else.

It began with the channels in the walls waking up. The silver light quickened, ran faster through its veins, and began to sing—a thin, high note that Lena felt in her teeth, in the roots of her hair, in the wet architecture of her lungs. Then the acolytes laid their palms against the arms of the chair, and the iron cuffs warmed, and something reached *into* her.

Not into her body. Into the space beside her body. The space where Vess lived—or where Vess's absence lived, the hollow shaped exactly like a god, the hiding place she had been for seven years without knowing it. She felt fingers there. Not flesh-fingers; something older and colder, patient as winter, methodical as a clerk counting coin. They found the first seam and pulled.

And a memory tore loose.

Not faded. Not slipped. *Tore*—with a sound she heard from inside her own skull, a wet ripping like canvas in a gale, and then it was simply *gone*, and what remained was the hole where it had been, ragged-edged, bleeding meaning into the rest of her mind.

She screamed. She couldn't help it. The straps held her down and the scream went up and broke against the golden sunburst overhead and fell back on her like ash.

The memory had been small. That was the horror of it—it hadn't started with anything large. It had been Pip, last autumn, sitting on the tailboard of the cart outside Cinderhollow, swinging his legs, telling her with tremendous authority that the best apples were the ones with a worm in them, because the worm knew. She could see the cart. She could smell the dust. She could hear his voice going bright and certain—

—and then she couldn't. There was a boy-shaped hole in her autumn, a gap in the road between one town and the next, and she knew—I *know* there was someone, she thought, I know there was a child, I can feel the shape of him like a handprint in flour—and she could not remember his name. Could not remember his face. Could not remember whether she had ever saved anyone at all.

"That's the way of it," the Inquisitor said softly, watching her from his stool. He had not touched the rite himself; he presided, the way a man presides over an execution he has ordered but does not wish to sully his gloves with. "The small ones go first. The mind protects what it loves most, so we must strip away the ordinary before we reach the treasure. You'll lose a great many apples, Captain, before we find your god."

Another pull. Another tear.

Sefa this time—or the idea of Sefa, because what ripped away left the word *mapmaker* floating loose in her head with nothing attached to it. She remembered ink-stained fingers. She remembered arguing about a route, being argued with, losing the argument and being glad of it. She remembered trusting someone enough to sleep while they kept watch, which for Lena Amberdale had been the rarest gift she owned. And the person at the center of all of it dissolved like sugar in rain, leaving only the trust behind, orphaned, pointing at nothing.

*Who did I trust?* she thought, and the question echoed in an empty room.

The third one was Brannoc, and she knew it was Brannoc because it went easier—heavier, somehow, like a stone sliding into deep water instead of tearing free. She saw snow. She saw a confession offered like currency. She saw a man trying to buy back a sin with his life, and paying, and her own voice saying something to him at the end that she would now never remember saying. The grief stayed. The man it belonged to drained out of it, and she was left holding grief like a coat someone had stepped out of, too big for her, smelling faintly of woodsmoke and guilt.

She stopped screaming after that. Not because it hurt less, but because she had discovered something worse than the pain: she was forgetting that she was forgetting. Each loss closed over smooth, and the person she had been an hour ago—who had walked into this chamber with a family stitched into her ribs—was becoming a rumor even to herself.

*Hold the names,* she told herself. *Whatever happens, hold the names.*

But the names were going. She could feel them loosening like teeth.

* * *

Time lost its edges. The candles burned down and were replaced without her marking it. The silver light in the walls beat faster now, hungry, and the cold fingers inside her worked with increasing confidence, having learned the geography of her. They had stopped taking memories one at a time. Now they peeled whole seasons.

She lost the road. All of it—the passes, the waystations, the smell of pine smoke at dawn, the particular silence of high country under snow. It went as a single sheet of ice calving off a glacier, and with it went the reason she had ever been good at her work, and she felt herself become smaller, a woman with skills and no origin for them, a tool nobody had finished making.

She lost the shrine. The dying priest's hands pressing the half-map into hers. The heartbeat in the stone. *A door opens both ways,* someone had warned her—she kept the words and lost the face and the voice, and the words turned strange in her mouth, a prophecy addressed to a stranger.

She lost the village in the snow. Warmth as rebellion. Bread broken by ward-touched outcasts who asked nothing. She lost the feeling of having been loved by strangers, and that was the cut that went deepest so far, because it took with it the belief that she deserved such a thing.

And through all of it, in the hollow where a god should have been, Vess said nothing.

That, more than the pain, more than the peeling, was what nearly broke her. Seven years of silence she had forgiven, because silence was his nature, because the Warden keeps quiet watch. But now—now, while she was being unmade stitch by stitch, while the family she had chosen was being stolen out of her skull by men in dove-grey robes—now he kept his silence, and it felt less like a nature and more like an abandonment.

*Say something,* she begged, into the hollow. *Anything. Curse me. Leave me. Just—say something.*

Nothing. The hollow breathed cold.

Then the Inquisitor stood, and the acolytes paused, and he came close and crouched beside the chair so their faces were level, and he studied her with genuine tenderness.

"You're deeper than any bearer we've opened," he murmured. "Do you feel it? He's wrapped around the oldest parts of you. Around the name. We'll reach him soon, and when we do, I suspect we'll find he's been drinking from you all along—that every memory we've taken tonight, he took first, and hid himself in the taking." He smiled. "You've been a larder, Captain. A well-stocked cellar. And your god has been eating quietly for seven years while you wondered why he wouldn't speak."

Something in Lena went very still.

Because it wasn't true—she knew it wasn't true, the way you know a map is wrong, the way you know north—and yet the lie slid into the hollow where Vess lived and *fit*, and for one terrible moment she hated him. Hated the silent god who had used her, hidden in her, let her carry him like a disease while she prayed to him in the dark and got nothing back. The hatred felt warm. The hatred felt like relief. And she understood, with the last clear part of her mind, that this was the rite's cruelest instrument: not the tearing, but the turning. Make the bearer hate the god, and she will open her own doors to get away from him.

*Don't,* she told herself. *Don't you dare. Whatever he is, whatever he took—you chose him. Fourteen years old and you chose him, and choosing again is the only thing left that's yours.*

The hatred receded, grudgingly, like a tide.

"There she is," the Inquisitor said softly, watching her fight. "There's the spine. Good. Hold onto it, Captain. You'll need it for what comes next, because the small memories are gone, and now—" he straightened, and nodded to his acolytes, and the silver light in the walls flared white— "now we open the deep vaults."

The next pull was not a memory.

It was a *name*.

Hazel.

It came up from the bottom of her like a drowned body rising, and she felt it go—felt the girl she had been before the oath, before the silence, before everything—lift out of her with a sound like a bell cracking, and for a moment she was shown it whole, one last mercy-cruelty of the rite: a hazel copse behind a cloister, a child's hands full of nuts, a mother's voice calling a name across a green field, *Hazel, Hazel, come in before dark*—

—and then it tore, and the field was empty, and the name was gone, and Lena Amberdale sat in the iron chair and could not remember who had ever called her anything at all.

She hung in the straps, sobbing without sound, emptied past emptiness. The chamber spun. The golden sunburst overhead swam. Somewhere far away the Inquisitor was saying something triumphant, and the acolytes' palms glowed against the chair-arms, and the cold fingers inside her found the final seam—the one beneath the name, beneath the girlhood, the seam where a god had folded himself small enough to hide in a mortal soul—and they gripped it, and pulled.

And the hollow *moved*.

Vess rose.

Not gently. Not gratefully. He came up through her like a flood through a mine, vast and starved and furious, seven years of compressed silence detonating outward, and the last thing Lena felt before the world went white was not pain and not peace but *presence*—a god filling every torn and hollowed place in her at once, pouring himself into the wounds the rite had made, wearing her ruined memory like armor.

And through the roaring, through the white, she heard him at last.

Not words. Something older than words, pressed directly into what was left of her:

*They are coming. Hold, little warden. Your family holds the door—and they are coming for you.*

The chamber doors burst inward off their hinges.

Through the smoke and the shattered wards and the falling gold dust of the desecrated sunburst, three figures waded into the light—one small and fast, one tall with ink still on her hands, and one in Concord grey with a drawn sword pointed, at last, the other way.

Lena, strapped in the ruins of the rite, could not remember their names.

But her heart, stubborn thing, knew them anyway—and it broke open with joy.
