## 21. Spring, Remembering

The thaw came to Cinderhollow the way forgiveness comes—slowly, and all at once.

Sefa heard it first from the roof of her father's old workshop, where she had gone at dawn to check the snow-gauges out of a habit that no longer served anything. The eaves were dripping. Not the slow, grudging drip of false spring that had tricked them twice already this season, but a steady, insistent percussion, water finding its way down through ice that had forgotten how to be anything but ice. She stood there with her bare hands on the cold shingles and listened until her fingers ached, and somewhere in the middle of listening she realized she was crying, and could not have said for which grief or which relief.

For seven weeks the sky over Thornfell had been black. Not clouded—*empty*, the particular wrongness of a sky with nothing in it, no stars, no moon, no pale wash of far galaxies. The wards had held since the night Lena Amberdale walked backward through the Door and gave herself away, and the Concord's engineered winter had broken like a fever breaking, all at once in the small hours, so that people woke to find their breath no longer smoking and did not trust it. But the stars had been slower. They came back one by one over the following weeks, as if climbing up out of some deep water, and the whole country watched them return the way you watch a sick child sleep—afraid to look away, afraid to hope too loudly.

Tonight, Sefa knew without checking, every star would be home.

She climbed down. In the workshop below, the lamps were lit and the smell of ink and hot wax was exactly what it had been when she was nine years old, before her father started charting roads that officially did not exist, before the Door, before everything. Pip sat cross-legged on the drafting table because Pip still sat on furniture meant for other purposes, mending a net with the fierce concentration of someone who had decided that if his hands were busy they could not shake. He'd stopped shaking mostly, these days. Mostly.

"You're dripping," he said, not looking up.

"It's called spring."

"It's called you stood on the roof again. Brannoc used to say mapmakers fall off roofs more than soldiers do."

"Brannoc," Sefa said, "said a great many things." She said it lightly, and Pip let it be light, and that was the treaty they had between them now: they carried the dead gently, and only mentioned them when the carrying got heavy enough to need two sets of hands.

Outside, Cinderhollow was waking into its first honest morning in half a year. Smoke rose straight from a hundred chimneys. Someone was arguing cheerfully about seed potatoes in the lane. And down at the mouth of the pass road—the road that led up toward the Door, toward the shrine, toward everything—a small figure had appeared out of the morning mist, walking with the careful, deliberate gait of someone who had learned recently that the ground could be trusted but wanted proof.

Sefa went very still.

She would know that walk anywhere. She had spent eleven days on the road watching it: the slight roll of the shoulders, the weight kept low, the walk of a woman who had crossed a hundred borders and expected each one to cost her. But the woman coming up the pass road walked like that and *did not know why*, and when she lifted her head and looked at the town—at the smoke, the roofs, the ordinary miracle of Cinderhollow standing—her face was open in a way Lena Amberdale's face had never once been. It was the same face. Sharp jaw, wind-burned cheeks, eyes the grey-green of the sea over slate. And behind those eyes, nothing. A house with the lamps out and the furniture gone.

"She came back," Pip whispered. He had come to stand beside Sefa at the window, and his knuckles had gone white around the netting needle. "Sefa. *She came back.*"

"No," Sefa said softly. "Someone came back. We have to make her *Lena* again. That part's ours to do."

* * *

They found her in the market square, standing in front of the well, and the whole square had gone quiet around her the way a room goes quiet around a fire that might be either warmth or danger.

She was looking at the well because someone had hung garlands on it—first-thaw garlands, rowan and early crocus—and she was touching one of the flowers with two fingers, carefully, the way you touch something in a dream you're afraid of waking from. Her hands were empty. No sword, no pack, no knife at the belt. Just a worn oath-scar on the left wrist, faded now to a thin silver line, and clothes that had been good leathers once and were now patched by hands that hadn't known whose they were.

"I know this place," she said, when she saw Sefa and Pip stop at the edge of the crowd. Her voice was rough with disuse. "I don't know why I know this place. I woke up on the pass road three days ago with—" she turned her wrist, showing the scar, as if it explained something, "—with this, and a feeling like I'd left something somewhere, and my feet just... kept choosing south."

"What's your name?" Pip asked. His voice cracked in the middle of it, and he pushed through anyway, because someone had to ask and everyone else in the square was too frightened or too reverent.

She looked at him for a long moment. Something moved behind her eyes—not memory, Sefa thought, not quite, but the *shape* where memory had been, the way a missing tooth leaves a tongue restless.

"I don't have one," she said. "I've been calling myself nothing. It didn't seem right to pick one. It felt like—" she searched, "—like wearing someone else's coat."

The silence stretched. Somewhere a mother pulled her child back by the sleeve, and the stranger flinched at the sound of it, a small trained flinch, and Sefa felt her heart crack along a seam she'd been holding together since the Door.

Then Sefa stepped forward, took the stranger's empty hand in both of hers, and said, clearly, so the whole square could hear:

"Your name is Lena. Lena Amberdale. You are a smuggler, a mercenary, an oath-bearer, and the reason the stars came back. And you are going to come home with us, and we are going to tell you everything, as many times as it takes, until you believe us."

The stranger—Lena—looked down at their joined hands. "Why?" she asked. Not suspicious. Genuinely asking, the way a child asks. "Why would you do that? You don't know me."

"We know you better than anyone alive," Sefa said. "You just don't know yourself yet. That's all right. That's what family is for."

* * *

They told the stories in the workshop, over stew, because Sefa had decided on the walk home that the stories should never be told in a church voice. Churches were the Concord's territory, and besides, Lena had given everything in a place of stone and ritual; she deserved to get herself back in a place that smelled of ink and onions.

So they told them like cargo-run stories, like road stories, laughing in the wrong places and going quiet in the right ones.

Pip told the first one, because he had claimed it weeks ago, rehearsing it against the day. He told her about the press-gang in Cinderhollow's first bad week, the soldiers with their white-and-gold sashes, and the boy who had run, and the mercenary who had stepped out of an alley with no plan at all except that the boy was not going to be taken.

"You hit a sergeant," Pip said. "With a fish. A smoked herring. It was frozen."

"That doesn't sound like me," Lena said slowly.

"It's *exactly* you," Pip said. "That's the whole point. You didn't even like me yet. You told me later you saved me because it was 'operationally inconvenient' to watch a kid get pressed. You lied. You were terrible at lying about the things that mattered."

Lena looked at him, and her brow furrowed, and Sefa watched her reach for something that wasn't there and find, instead, the outline of it. "Pip," she said, testing. "Your name is Pip."

"Yeah."

"Pip." She said it again, softer, like setting down something fragile. "When you say it, my chest does something. Like a door opening onto a warm room. I can't see the room. But I know it's warm."

Pip burst into tears, laughed at himself for it, wiped his face on his sleeve, and kept talking, faster, about the waystation and the ward-touched village and the winter, and Lena listened with her whole body leaned forward, hungry, a starving woman being fed one spoonful at a time and told, patiently, *there is more, there is always more.*

* * *

It was Sefa who told the hard ones. She had decided that too. Pip's stories were the sweet ones; the bitter ones belonged to her, because she had earned them on the riverbank burning her father's maps, and on the glass bridge, and in the antechamber of the Door.

She told her about the cartographer's daughter who had bargained with a mercenary for escort, both of them trusting no one, and how the bargain had turned into something neither of them had signed for.

"You hated me, at first," Sefa said. "Politely. You thought I was reckless, trading safe routes for a fool's errand into the mountains. You told me my father's archive was probably a legend and I was going to get us all killed chasing it."

"Were you?"

"Yes. Reckless, I mean. It wasn't a legend. And you went anyway, even though you thought it was, because—" Sefa paused, choosing, "—because Pip needed feeding and I needed guarding and that was reason enough for you. You used to say you were in it for the coin. Nobody ever found the coin."

Lena's hand had drifted, without her noticing, to the silver scar on her wrist. "And this?" she asked. "What did I pay?"

And Sefa told her. All of it. Vess poured into his last sworn servant; the anchor; the extraction rite; the straps and the peeling-away; the memories tearing loose like roots torn from soil. She told it plainly, without mercy and without cruelty, because she had learned on the road that Lena Amberdale could survive any truth but resented being protected from one.

"You chose it," Sefa finished. "Nobody made you. Maren opened the Door for us, and the revolt broke the chamber, and you could have walked out free. Everyone would have understood. Instead you walked backward through the threshold and poured your whole oath back into him. Every memory of us. That's what bought the winter's end. That's what bought the stars."

Lena sat very still for a long time. The lamp guttered. Outside, the meltwater ticked against the window like a patient clock.

"I remember none of it," she said at last. "But I remember *choosing*. Isn't that strange? There's no picture, no sound. Just—" she pressed her fist briefly against her sternum, "—a weight. Like I set something down somewhere very important, and I can't remember what, but I remember that my hands were steady when I did it. Does that mean anything?"

Sefa had to look away for a moment, out the dark window, toward the mountains where a god was wearing a dead woman's love like a coat of embers.

"It means everything," she said. "That's the truest thing you own."

* * *

They fell into a rhythm, the three of them, as the days lengthened. Mornings, Lena worked—she could not bear idleness, that much had survived the hollowing, and she hauled timber and fixed fences and taught the Cinderhollow militia footwork with a patience that startled everyone including herself. Afternoons and evenings, the stories. Pip took the funny ones and the brave ones. Sefa took the ones with blood in them. Neighbors began drifting in to listen, then travelers, then strangers off the reopened roads, until the workshop's evenings became something the town quietly organized itself around: the telling of Lena Amberdale back to Lena Amberdale, piece by piece, like raising a barn.

Some nights it hurt her. Sefa learned to read the signs—the stillness, the hand drifting to the scar, the moment when the hunger in her face curdled into something like grief for a self she couldn't reach. On those nights Sefa did not push. She just refilled the bowl, or put another log on the fire, and said, "Tomorrow there's more," and Lena would nod, and stay.

One night, near the end of the third week, Lena stopped her mid-story.

"The commander," she said. "Maren. You've told me about the bridge, and the ambush, and how she turned her blade against her own order at the last. Where is she now?"

"South," Sefa said. "Under a truce-banner, answering for the Concord's crimes. Some days I think she'll hang. Some days I think the country owes her a statue. Both things might be true."

"And our dead god. Vess." Lena's voice went careful. "Where does he live now?"

"In the wards. In the mountains. In—" Sefa hesitated, then decided she had earned the truth too, "—in you, a little, I think. You carry his scar. Maybe he carries yours."

Lena nodded slowly, absorbing it, and then said the thing that Sefa would turn over in her mind for many nights after:

"When I was on the pass road, before I reached the town—I passed a shrine. Broken roof, moss on the stones. I went in because my feet made me. And there was a sapling growing up through the cracked altar floor. Young hazel, maybe knee-high. And I stood there and I cried, and I have no idea why, except that it felt like—" she frowned, hunting the word, "—like greeting. Like something in me knew that tree before I did."

Sefa went cold and warm at once.

Because she had not told her that. No one had told her that. The sapling had been planted by no human hand that anyone could name—it had simply appeared in the restored shrine's ruins the first week of thaw, green and impossible in the ash, and the ward-touched had begun making pilgrimage to it, whispering that Vess had left a sign, a promise, a *seed of something*—

Or perhaps not Vess at all. Perhaps something older. Perhaps the god had kept, hidden deep in his anchor, one memory he could not bring himself to spend—one name, one girl-before-the-oath, planted like a seed in the only soil he had.

"Hazel," Sefa said quietly, almost to herself.

Lena's head came up sharply. "What did you say?"

"Nothing. A guess. A—" Sefa steadied herself. "There's a story we haven't told you yet. The oldest one. About who you were before you ever swore an oath to anyone. Before the mercenary, before the smuggler, before the scar. We don't know it ourselves—not all of it. But I think," and her voice caught, and she let it, "I think the shrine knows. I think the tree knows. And I think, if you're willing, it's time we all went and asked."

Lena stood. She looked at her empty hands, then at the two people who had spent three weeks handing her back to herself story by story, and something in her face—something that had survived gods and rituals and the long erasure—settled into resolve.

"Then get your coat, mapmaker," she said, and for one flickering instant her smile was the old smile, sharp and warm at once, the smile from the road. "And you, herring-boy. We're going to meet whoever I was."

They went out together into the spring night, under a sky so full of returned stars it seemed to be spilling over—and ahead of them, up the dark pass, the mountains waited with a name.
