## 1. The Night the Stars Went Out

The bell above the door of Galloway's Charms & Curios had not rung all day, and Emrys had come to think of its silence as a kind of weather — a low pressure settling over Greyhollow that pressed everyone indoors and made even the bravest souls clutch their keys like talismans. They stood behind the counter polishing a brass compass that did nothing but point north, which in this city was the only honest magic left for sale.

Outside, Lamplighter's Row was going amber with dusk. The gaslamps hissed awake one by one, and beyond them the wards hummed — or they were supposed to. Emrys could feel them the way a sailor feels a storm three days out: not with the ears, but with the teeth, the marrow, the old scar tissue behind the sternum where the keeper's oath lived coiled like a second heart. Three years since they had sworn it. Three years of selling luck-charms that were mostly brass and mostly lies, and of walking each midnight to the sealed gate beneath the city to whisper the words that kept the veil taut as drumskin.

Tonight the hum was wrong. Thinner. Like a singer gone hoarse mid-hymn.

Emrys set down the compass and told themselves it was nothing. They had gotten very good at telling themselves things.

* * *

The child came in just before the last light failed, pushing the door open with both hands because she hadn't the weight for it. She couldn't have been more than eight, wrapped in a coat two sizes too large, her breath coming in little white flags. Emrys knew her by sight — Maren Ostler, daughter of the chandler on Tallow Street, the one whose shop had burned in the spring under circumstances no one would name aloud.

"Are you closed?" the girl asked, though she was already inside, already standing on the worn diamond of floorboard where every customer eventually stood, as if the shop itself arranged them there.

"For you? Never." Emrys came around the counter and crouched to her height, an old habit from a life before Greyhollow, when crouching to children's height had been a matter of courtesy rather than camouflage. "What's troubling you, Maren?"

The girl's eyes went to the shelves — rows of little glass bottles filled with colored sand, iron nails bent into rings, dried rowan berries strung on red thread. All of it inert. All of it theater. Emrys had spent three years perfecting the theater, because a city that outlawed magic would forgive a shopkeeper who sold superstition, but never one who sold the real thing.

"Mama says the stars are going to go out," Maren said. "She said it to Papa after she thought I was asleep. She said the Warden bells rang wrong at noon and the dogs won't stop howling and—" Her voice snagged. "And Da's ship is due back on the tide tomorrow, and if the stars go out how will he find the harbor?"

Emrys felt the oath behind their sternum turn over, slow and cold, like something waking in the bottom of a lake.

"The stars aren't going anywhere," they said, and heard the lie land soft and practiced. It was the same voice they used for the charms. "But I'll tell you what. There's a charm for finding your way home. Old kind. My grandmother's recipe."

They crossed to the shelf by the window and took down a small tin locket — one of the few pieces in the shop that wasn't entirely theater. Inside, pressed against a scrap of lodestone, was a thread of Emrys's own hair, charmed with the smallest working they dared: enough warmth to hold through a cold night, enough pull to nudge a lost foot toward home. Harmless. Undetectable unless you knew what to look for, and Emrys had spent three years making sure no one in Greyhollow knew what to look for.

They pressed it into the girl's palm and folded her fingers over it.

"Now listen. You don't tell anyone what's inside. Not your mama, not the priest, especially not any Wardens. You wear it under your shirt, and when you're scared, you hold it and think about your da's face, and it'll keep a little lamp lit in you. That's all a luck-charm ever really does."

Maren looked up at them with the terrible gravity of children who have already learned the world can break. "Did it work for your family?"

The question went through Emrys like a needle finding the gap between ribs.

"My family is a long way off," they said. Farther than the girl could imagine. Farther than the harbor, farther than the horizon — through a veil stitched shut with oaths and silence, into a realm that had been dying slowly since the Sundering, while Emrys stood on this side selling brass and pretending the wound wasn't theirs to tend.

"That'll be four coppers," they added, because the theater required prices, and then, when the girl dug in her pocket and came up with three and a button, "the button covers it."

Maren smiled — a small, cracked thing, but real — and let herself out into the darkening street. Emrys watched her go until she turned the corner toward Tallow Street, one small lantern of a person carrying a piece of a keeper's secret under her coat.

Then they turned the sign to CLOSED and stood a long time in the empty shop, listening to the wards hum their thinning song.

* * *

Midnight came the way it always did in Greyhollow: with the great clock in Warden's Square tolling twelve, and the city holding its breath between the twelfth stroke and whatever came after. Emrys had walked this hour a thousand times — down the stairs behind the shop, along the undercity galleries where the old kingdom's bones still stood, to the gate.

It was a door, though no one who saw it would call it that. A slab of grey stone standing free in a vaulted chamber older than the city above it, carved over every inch with covenant-script that glowed faintly when Emrys spoke the keeping-words. Behind it, the other realm. Ahead of it, this one. And Emrys in between, the hinge, the lock, the lonely hand upon the latch.

They lit no lamp. They didn't need one; the script gave its own pale light, and tonight—

Tonight the script was flickering.

Emrys stopped on the last stair, their hand flat against the cold wall, and watched the covenant-script stutter like a guttering candle. The hum they'd felt all day in their teeth was nearly gone now, replaced by a sound they had never heard in three years of midnights: a thin, high whine, the sound of a thread about to part.

"No," Emrys said aloud, foolishly, to a door. "No, you don't."

They took the last steps at a run and pressed both palms to the stone. It was warm. It had never been warm. The keeping-words rose in them unbidden, the oath answering the body's panic before the mind could shape it — *hold, hold, holdfast, I am the keeper, I am the seal* — and for a moment, one long moment, the flickering steadied. The whine softened. Emrys sagged against the door, forehead to the stone, breathing hard.

And that was when the stars went out.

Not dimmed. Not clouded. *Out.* Emrys felt it happen rather than saw it — a sensation like a great lung exhaling somewhere far above the sky, like every candle in creation pinched between two fingers at once. Through the high slit-window near the vault ceiling, where on clear nights a sliver of heaven showed between the undercity stones, there was nothing. No moon. No stars. No darkness even, properly speaking — darkness implies something behind it. This was absence, total and absolute, pressing against the window like water against glass.

In the same instant, the door beneath Emrys's hands shuddered.

It was not opening. Emrys would have known — the keeping-words would have screamed through them. But it was *moving*, trembling like a struck bell, and through the stone came a sound that Emrys had heard only once before, three years ago, on the worst night of their life: voices. Faint, layered, countless, like wind through a forest of throats. Voices from the other side. Voices calling.

And beneath them, distinct now, cutting through the chorus like a blade through reeds — a tearing sound. Somewhere along the veil's vast length, the fabric was ripping. Not here at the gate, not yet. But somewhere. Something was coming through, or something was trying to leave, and the wards above — the Guild's great iron lattice that had held Greyhollow safe since the Sundering — were failing all at once, everywhere, like a net giving way.

Emrys slid down the door until they were kneeling, and laughed — one short, broken note with no humor in it at all.

Three years. Three years of brass compasses and colored sand, of lying to children about luck, of whispering to a door in the dark and calling it duty. They had built a life the way you build a house on a frozen river, telling yourself the ice was thick enough, would stay thick enough, if only you walked softly. And now the thaw had come all at once, in a single midnight, and everything they'd made was already sliding toward the water.

They simply hadn't known it yet. That was the cruelest part. An hour ago they had been a shopkeeper closing up for the night, counting coppers, thinking about tea. Now they were what they had always been — the last keeper of a failing seal — and the life above was already a ghost.

Above, muffled by fathoms of stone, the Warden bells began to ring. Not the measured peal of the hours. The alarm. Over and over, ragged and frantic, and beneath it, rising from the streets of Greyhollow, the sound of ten thousand people waking to a starless sky and beginning, all together, to scream.

Emrys pressed their forehead to the warm stone one last time.

"Hold," they whispered — to the door, to themselves, to whoever might be listening on the other side. "Hold just a little longer."

The door trembled. The voices swelled. And somewhere in the dark of the upper city, in an alley off Lamplighter's Row, something tore through the veil with a sound like the world's fabric splitting at the seam —

— and began, joint by too many joint, to hunt.
