## 2. A Stranger Through the Hedge

The veil tore like wet paper, and Hazel Thorne fell through the wound.

There was no grace in it. No shimmering descent, no gentle unfolding between worlds — nothing like the stories her grandmother had told, back when stories were still allowed. It was a ripping, a birth gone wrong, and she came out of it the way a stone comes out of a sling: fast, blind, and screaming.

She hit cobblestones. Her shoulder took the worst of it, and the breath left her in a single punched-out gasp that fogged the cold air of an alley she had never seen before. Somewhere above her, between the leaning walls of brick and timber, there should have been stars. There were none. Only a low grey ceiling of cloud, and beneath it the smell of smoke and tallow and wet stone — the smell of a city, a real city, with chimneys and people and bread baking somewhere out of sight.

*Other side,* she thought wildly. *I made it to the other side.*

Then the pain arrived, all at once, and she curled around it on the frozen ground with her arms wrapped over her head like a child sheltering from hail.

In her right fist, clenched so hard her knuckles had gone white and bloodless, was half of a broken covenant-stone. It was warm. It was always warm now, ever since the night she'd pried it from its shattered setting while the sky behind her went dark ridge by ridge, mountain by mountain, star by star. It hummed against her palm — not sound exactly, but something older than sound, a vibration that lived in the bones of her wrist and traveled up into her jaw until her teeth ached with it.

And through that hum, faint as voices heard through a closed door, she could hear them again. Her mother. Her aunts. The whole hedge-line of Thorne women who had stayed behind when she ran.

*"Go,"* they had said. *"Take the half and go. One of us has to reach the gate."*

She hadn't wanted to go. She wanted that remembered too, written somewhere in whatever ledger kept accounts of such things: *Hazel Thorne did not want to go.*

A sound came from the tear behind her.

It was not a tearing sound this time. It was a clicking — a dry, jointed clicking, like a beetle the size of a horse working its way through a crack too small for it. The veil's edges had begun to knit themselves closed behind her; she could see them doing it, the ragged hole shrinking with a slow peristaltic motion, flesh healing over flesh. But something was forcing itself through anyway. Something patient. Something that had been starving on the far side of a dying sky for longer than Hazel had been alive.

She knew what it was. Everyone in the hedge-lands knew, though no one said its name anymore, because names fed things like that. The old women called them *joint-walkers*, and the rule was simple: you did not look at where the joints bent, because the places where they bent were not places joints should bend.

Hazel got up. Her shoulder screamed. She got up anyway.

The alley ran both directions into shadow. To her left, the walls rose higher and ended in a wooden walkway strung with laundry — sheets, nightshirts, a man's coat, all of it stiff with frost. To her right, the alley opened onto a street where lamplight spilled yellow across the cobbles, and she could hear a cart, a dog, a woman calling someone home to supper. People. Witnesses. Wardens, maybe — she'd heard about the Wardens' Guild even beyond the veil, in the last desperate councils before everything stopped mattering except the stone. *Greyhollow outlawed magic after the Sundering.* That was the one fact every refugee carried like a coin sewn into a hem: *do not be caught being what you are.*

Behind her, the clicking stopped.

That was worse. That meant it had come all the way through, and was standing in the dark of the alley with its head tilted at an angle that made her stomach turn over, listening for her heartbeat the way a fox listens for mice under snow.

Hazel did the only thing her hands knew how to do. She was a hedge-witch, seventh daughter of a seventh daughter, raised on charms against chilblains and hexes for sour milk — small magic, kitchen magic, the kind that had never once impressed anyone. But her grandmother had taught her one thing of weight, one inheritance passed hand to hand down the female line like the stone itself: *when you cannot fight and cannot run, hide yourself inside the world's forgetting.*

She pressed her palm flat against the frost-stiff sheet hanging from the walkway above and whispered the words her grandmother had traded three teeth to learn.

The charm took hold the way small magic always does — imperfectly, grudgingly, like a mule deciding to move. The frost on the linen bloomed silver-white, and the sheet's shadow deepened, and the space beneath the walkway where Hazel stood became the kind of place eyes slide off of. Not invisible. Just *uninteresting*. A gap in the world's attention.

The joint-walker came around the corner of the tear's remains.

She didn't look at where the joints bent. She looked at its feet instead — or what served as feet, four of them, arranged along a body that moved like a centipede wearing the suggestion of a wolf. Its skin was the grey of things that have lived a long time without light, and where its face should have been there was a smoothness, a blankness, and set into that blankness a single opening that opened and closed, opened and closed, tasting the air.

It tasted the air where she had fallen. It tasted the blood from her shoulder, which had soaked through her cloak and dripped, she realized with distant horror, a trail of bright spots across the pale cobbles.

The blank face turned toward the laundry line. Toward her.

The opening widened.

And then — bells. Distant at first, then nearer, a pattern of three notes repeated over and over, ringing from the direction of the lit street. The joint-walker froze. The opening narrowed to a slit. And Hazel, watching through the gaps in her own fraying courage, saw the creature do something she had never seen one do in all the stories: it flinched.

It poured itself backward up the alley wall, joints folding in directions that she firmly refused to see, and flowed over the rooftops like spilled ink running uphill, and was gone.

The bells rang on. Three notes, three notes, three notes.

Hazel let out a breath she felt she'd been holding since the moment of her birth, twenty-six years ago. Her legs gave out. She sat down hard on the frozen ground beneath the walkway, among the shadows of hanging shirts, and held the covenant-stone against her chest, and shook.

* * *

"You're bleeding on my street."

The voice came from the mouth of the alley. Low, level, neither kind nor unkind — the voice of someone who had learned to say alarming things calmly because calm got better results.

Hazel's head came up. Standing at the edge of the lamplight was a figure in a long dark coat, hood back, holding a shuttered lantern low so its glow fell on the cobbles rather than upward toward any face. She couldn't tell age or much else — only that the figure stood very still, the way people stand when stillness is a habit rather than a choice, and that their eyes, when the lantern-light caught them, were fixed not on her face but on her right hand.

On the stone.

"I'm not—" Hazel started, and stopped, because every lie she reached for was transparent even to herself. *I'm not hurt.* Blood was pooling under her shoulder. *This isn't what it looks like.* It was exactly what it looked like: a bleeding stranger crouched in the dark clutching a humming fragment of the oldest magic in either world. "Who rang the bells?" she asked instead.

"I did." The figure stepped closer, boots quiet on the stone. "Three notes means stray animal. It clears the street and brings no Wardens. Four notes would bring Wardens." A pause. "You're welcome."

"You knew," Hazel said. Her voice came out rougher than she intended. "You knew what was in the alley. You rang the bell to scare it off."

"I know what walks when the wards go quiet." The lantern lifted slightly, and light climbed the stranger's face at last: young-ish, sharp-boned, tired in a way that sleep doesn't fix. "And I know what you're holding. So here is what happens next. In about ninety seconds, a Warden patrol finishes its round two streets over, and if they find you first, no charm you know will save you. They have iron that eats hedge-magic like it's bread. So you can sit there bleeding and proud, or you can come with me, and we can both pretend this conversation never happened."

Every sensible part of Hazel screamed that this was a trap. Strangers who appear precisely when monsters flee are strangers with plans. But the stone hummed against her sternum, and through the hum she could feel — not hear, *feel* — something she hadn't felt since the sky went dark: a second note. A resonance. As though somewhere beneath this city, deep down in the dark under the cobbles, another half of something was singing a harmony to the piece she carried.

Her grandmother's voice, in memory: *the stone knows its own, and it knows doors.*

"Who are you?" Hazel asked.

"Nobody. A shopkeeper." But the stranger's hand had drifted, unconsciously, to rest flat against their own chest — the exact gesture Hazel made when the stone sang too loudly, the gesture of someone accustomed to feeling something hidden beneath the ribs of the world. "Emrys. Emrys Galloway. I keep a charm shop on Candlemaker's Row. Harmless things. Luck-charms, mostly."

"Harmless things," Hazel repeated. She looked down at her own hand, at the cracked half-stone leaking warmth and voices, and then back up at Emrys Galloway, and something settled in her chest — not trust, not yet, but recognition. The specific, weary recognition of one person who keeps secrets finding another person whose pockets are full of them. "You lie the way I lie," she said. "Badly, but with excellent posture."

For the first time, something flickered across Emrys's face that might, in better light, have become a smile. "Get up, Hazel-who-hasn't-told-me-her-name. Ninety seconds."

"It's Thorne. Hazel Thorne." She rose, swaying, and the alley tilted. Emrys crossed the distance between them in two strides and took her arm — and the moment their hands touched, the covenant-stone flared, one bright pulse of heat, and Emrys went rigid.

"What—" Emrys stared at the stone. At her. At some middle distance where calculations were visibly failing. "Where did you get that?"

"From the other side of the sky," Hazel said. "And there's something coming through the tear I just made that wants it back. And your bells bought us minutes, not hours."

Emrys closed their eyes for one long breath — the breath of a person watching three years of careful, hidden, harmless life come apart in their hands — and opened them again.

"Cellar door's behind the shop," they said. "Walk like you're drunk. Drunk gets ignored. Everything else gets reported."

They went together out of the alley, past the frost-stiff sheets, into the lamplit streets of a city that had outlawed everything Hazel was — and behind them, high on the rooftop where the joint-walker had vanished, something with too many joints unfolded itself patiently, and began, without hurry, to follow.
