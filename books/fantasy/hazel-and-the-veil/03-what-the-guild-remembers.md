## 3. What the Guild Remembers

The knock came at dawn, and it was not a knock so much as a verdict.

Three blows, iron-shod, spaced with the patience of men who had never once been made to wait. Emrys stood in the middle of the charm shop with a broom in their hands and Hazel's half of the covenant-stone burning cold against their chest, tucked beneath their shirt where it had spent the night humming like a trapped wasp. Above, through the floorboards, they could hear her moving — or trying not to. She had insisted on the cellar when the grey light came, and now every creak she failed to make was louder than any she might have.

Emrys set down the broom. Smoothed their apron. Arranged their face into the mild, slightly bored expression of a shopkeeper whose only crime was selling dried lavender to the gullible.

Then they opened the door.

Warden-Captain Ilsa Vane filled the frame the way frost fills a window — quietly, completely, and all at once. She wore the Guild's slate-grey coat with the silver chain of her rank looped twice about her shoulders, and behind her stood four Wardens in matched grey, their faces the blank faces of men who had learned that curiosity was a billable offense.

"Master Galloway." Her voice was pleasant. That was the worst thing about it. "Lovely morning."

"It's overcast," Emrys said.

"Every morning is overcast now." She smiled without warmth and stepped inside uninvited, her boots leaving damp prints on the floorboards Emrys had scrubbed only yesterday. "Strange night we had. Stars go out all at once, wards fail on the north quarter, and by some accounts there was a *tearing* sound near Miller's Row. You heard nothing, I suppose."

"Nothing," said Emrys, who had heard everything.

Vane drifted along the shelves, trailing one gloved finger across jars of salt and bundles of rowan — harmless things, blessed things, things the Guild itself had approved for sale after deciding that superstition was safer than sorcery. Her finger stopped at the luck-charms. She picked one up, turned it in the light.

"You sell these," she said. "Knotted thread and river-stone. No power in them at all. Do you know why the Guild permits that, Master Galloway?"

"Because there's no power in them?"

"Because hope without power is a leash." She set the charm down precisely where it had been. "People who buy knots against bad luck have already decided magic isn't real. They've done our work for us. It's the ones who still believe — the ones who come asking for something that *works* — that we watch." Her eyes lifted, pale as skimmed milk, and settled on Emrys with the weight of a hand on the back of the neck. "You've never asked me for anything that works. In three years, you've never once given me cause to look twice at you. I find I miss it."

"I'm flattered?"

"Don't be clever. Cleverness is a smell, and I follow smells." Vane moved toward the back of the shop, toward the curtain that hung over the cellar stair, and Emrys's heart performed a slow, deliberate act of sabotage against them. "We're searching the quarter. House to house. A stranger was seen — a woman, mud to the knees, running from the hedges off Miller's Row shortly before midnight. The same hour the sky went dark. You'll forgive the thoroughness."

"The hedge-rows are full of strangers this time of year. Pickers, vagrants—"

"*Running*, Master Galloway. From something." Vane paused at the curtain. Her glove hovered an inch from the fabric. "There were tracks too. Not hers. Something with too many joints in its legs, the witness said. Something that left the ground scorched where it stepped." She looked back over her shoulder, and for one unguarded instant there was something beneath the pleasantness — something old and tired and afraid. "You're a sensible man. Sensible men tell me what they know before I find it myself. Finding it myself is always worse."

*She*, Emrys thought, and did not correct her aloud. *She. A woman. Hiding under my floor with half a broken god in her pocket.*

"I know nothing," Emrys said, and heard how badly it landed — flat, a half-beat late, the words of a man reading from a script he'd never rehearsed. Three years of small talk with this woman, three years of charm-shop pleasantries, and when it finally mattered, Emrys Galloway lied like a child with jam on their face.

Something flickered in Vane's expression. Not certainty. Not yet. But a note taken, filed, folded away.

"Hm," she said.

And then the screaming started three streets over, and everything changed.

It came thin through the walls — a woman's voice, then a man's, then the crackle of something burning wet and green. One of the Wardens at the door swore and ran toward it; another blew a whistle that split the morning. Vane's head snapped toward the sound, and for a moment the whole machinery of her attention wrenched away from the curtain, from the cellar, from Emrys standing there with guilt written across them in letters a child could read.

"Stay here," she said. "Don't make me regret being merciful."

She swept out into the street. The door hung open behind her. And Emrys stood alone in their shop, listening to the screams get closer, understanding with terrible clarity that whatever had followed Hazel through the veil had not stopped following.

* * *

They went down the ladder two rungs at a time.

The cellar was small and smelled of tallow and old turnips, and Hazel was pressed into the corner behind the shelving with her knees drawn up and the stone-half clutched against her sternum, her eyes huge in the dark. At the sight of Emrys she started upright, one hand flying to the knife at her belt before recognition caught up with fear.

"They're searching house to house," Emrys whispered. "There's a thing loose in the streets — the one that chased you, I think. Vane's gone to it, but she'll be back, and she'll come back *certain*. I lied to her badly. Gods, I lied like a first-year clerk."

"Then we go. Now, out the coal-chute—"

"And run where? Every gate out of Greyhollow has Warden posts since midnight. Listen to me." Emrys crouched, keeping their voice below the scream-thread coming through the floorboards. "This cellar has a second door. My predecessor built it — the keeper before me. There's a passage that runs to the old cisterns, and the cisterns run under the whole quarter. If we can reach the undercity—"

"*Predecessor?*" Hazel's eyes narrowed. "Keeper of what, exactly? Because last night you talked like a man who sells soap and superstition, and this morning you're talking like—"

"Like someone who's going to explain everything, but not while the Guild is peeling the roof off my neighborhood." Emrys was already dragging the shelving aside, fingers finding the hairline seam in the stone that no one else had ever found, because no one else had ever known to look. "Help me. Please."

Together they heaved, and the false wall groaned open onto black air and the smell of deep water. Hazel stared into it, then at Emrys, and something in her face shifted — the wariness rearranging itself around a new fact.

"You really are him," she said quietly. "A keeper. I thought — I hoped, but I thought maybe I'd dragged a shopkeeper into the end of the world."

"I sell charms," Emrys said. "Mostly."

* * *

While Hazel gathered herself in the dark mouth of the passage, Emrys stood in the cellar with a candle and let the memory come, because it always came when the Guild knocked, and because they needed, just once, to look at it plainly instead of around it.

Three years ago. The Sundering was older than that — thirty years old, the wound in the world that everyone pretended was weather. Emrys had been nineteen when the old keeper, Aldous Merrin, had found them: a starving apprentice scrivener with a talent for sigils and nowhere to use it, copying invoices in a counting-house. Aldous had bought them dinner and told them a story.

He told them about the covenant. About the gate beneath Greyhollow, older than the city, older than the Guild, a door between the world of humankind and the other world — the one the old books called the Verdant Reach, where the stars burned different and brighter, where Hazel's people had kept their own side of the bargain since before either nation had a name. Two realms, one door, and a covenant binding both: each side would keep the veil gentle, would pass through it rarely and reverently, would tend the boundary together. And the price of the door was a keeper. One soul, bound to the gate, sworn to hold it shut against everything that hungered to come through unwelcome — and bound forever, because a covenant does not accept resignations.

"That's monstrous," young Emrys had said.

"That's balance," Aldous had answered. "I've held it forty years. You get used to the weight. Or you don't, and you die of it, and the door finds someone else."

And then, thirty years ago — no. Emrys corrected themselves in the dark. The Sundering was thirty years past, but the *breaking* had come later, in Aldous's time, in the years when the Guild grew rich and frightened and hungry for control. The Wardens had wanted the gate. Wanted it as a weapon, a lever, a threat to hold over both worlds. Aldous refused. So the Guild had done the next best thing: they had outlawed magic entire, burned the hedge-witches and the cunning-folk, driven the old knowledge underground — and in doing so, they had starved the covenant of its keepers. No one left to tend the wards. No one left to teach the rites. The veil, untended, had begun to rot.

The night it tore, Emrys had been twenty-six, standing in this very cellar beside a dying old man. Aldous had pressed the keeper's sign into their palm — a cold ring of braided metal that Emrys still wore hidden on a cord around their neck — and gasped out his last instruction: *Hold it. Hold it alone. Tell no one. The Guild must never learn the gate still lives.*

So Emrys had held it. Alone, in silence, for three years. Selling luck-charms to children. Nodding to Ilsa Vane in the market. Watching the Guild drag hedge-witches through the streets in chains and saying nothing, because saying something meant the Guild learning what lay under the city, and Aldous's last word had been *silence*.

And now the stars were out, and the veil was tearing, and a woman with half a broken stone had fallen through a hole that Emrys's silence had helped widen. Every witch burned, every rite lost, every year the wards went unsung — Emrys had watched it all from behind a shop counter and called it duty.

*You didn't build the hunt,* they told themselves. *You just kept the lamp lit while they hunted.*

But the lamplighter is complicit too. The thought arrived without mercy and stayed.

Above them, faintly, the screaming stopped. In its place came a silence worse than screaming — the particular silence of a street where the Wardens have won, or think they have.

Hazel touched their shoulder. "Whatever you're remembering," she said softly, "it can keep. We can't."

"No," Emrys agreed. "It can't." They snuffed the candle. "But you should know what you've walked into. The Guild doesn't just want you, Hazel. They want the stone. Both halves. And if Vane gets them, she won't mend the covenant — she'll *own* it. She'll hold the door over both our worlds like a knife."

"Then we get the other half first."

"The other half is in the Guild vault," Emrys said, and watched her face change in the dark. "Under the Citadel. Behind wards I helped design the mundane parts of, three years ago, because a good hiding place needs a respectable citizen building it."

Hazel was quiet a moment. Then, unexpectedly, she laughed — a low, cracked, exhausted sound.

"A keeper who lies badly and builds locks well. Fine. Undercity, then. Lead on, Master Galloway."

Emrys stepped through the false wall into the breathing dark of the old waterways, and behind them, far above, came the sound of the shop door opening again — and Vane's voice, pleasant as frost, calling their name.
