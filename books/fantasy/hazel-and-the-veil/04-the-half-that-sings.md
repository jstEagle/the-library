## 4. The Half That Sings

The cellar beneath the charm shop smelled of dust and old candlewax, and Hazel Thorne had never felt safer in her life.

She sat on an upturned crate with her knees drawn to her chest, wrapped in a blanket Emrys had pressed into her arms without a word. Above her head, floorboards creaked as the shopkeeper moved through the evening's routine — banking the hearth, latching the shutters, turning the little painted sign that read *Charms for Luck, Love, and Safe Passage* face-down against the glass. Hazel knew that sign. She had seen its like in a hundred windows on the other side of the veil, back when there had been other sides at all, back when the worlds had still bothered to pretend they were separate.

Here, though, magic was a crime. Here, Emrys Galloway sold luck-charms that were — what? Hazel turned the question over while she waited. Harmless. That was the word the woman upstairs used when she spoke of them. *Harmless.* Dried lavender sewn into red cloth. Iron nails bent at dawn. Beeswax candles poured under a waning moon and blessed with nothing stronger than a wish.

Hazel almost laughed. On her side of the veil, such things would have been called toys. Children's games. The kind of magic you gave a child so it would stop crying, knowing full well it would do nothing at all.

And yet.

Her hand found the stone in her pocket before she could decide to reach for it. She had been trying not to touch it — trying, these last hours, to be only a fugitive, only a girl in a cellar, only someone who needed soup and sleep and not to think. But the stone had opinions about being ignored. It warmed against her palm like a living thing, and then, soft as breath, it began to hum.

Hazel went still.

The hum was not one voice. It was many, braided: her grandmother's low contralto, the one that had sung the planting songs; her mother's lighter thread woven through it, always a half-step behind, always laughing; her brother Rill's cracked boy-soprano chasing them both. They were singing the harvest-round from home, the one that began *Turn the wheel, turn the year* — except they were singing it wrong, or singing it true, because the verses had changed since Hazel had last heard them. There was a new line now, where the old song had praised the sun:

*No star to steer by, no light to keep,*
*we sing in the dark, we sing ourselves to sleep.*

Hazel's throat closed. She pulled the stone out and held it cupped in both hands, and the voices rose to meet her, faint and far and unmistakably alive. Not ghosts. Not echoes. Her family, singing across a distance no song should have been able to cross, carried in the grain of a broken covenant-stone half the size of her fist.

They couldn't see her. She understood that now, understood it the way you understand a thing by breaking your own heart with it. The stone was a door-crack, a keyhole, a held breath between worlds — and through it, her people sang into the dark hoping someone, anyone, might hear. They had been singing since the stars went out. They were singing still.

"I hear you," Hazel whispered. "I hear you. I'm sorry."

The humming swelled, just slightly, the way a fire swells when you feed it. Then it settled, patient, and waited.

* * *

Emrys came down the cellar stairs near midnight, carrying a tray: bread, hard cheese, a clay pot of stew that smelled of rosemary and long simmering. Hazel watched their face in the lamplight — watched them set the tray down carefully, watchfully, the way a person sets down something they're afraid might be more than it seems.

"You've been talking to it," Emrys said. It wasn't quite a question.

"To my family." Hazel didn't look away. She was done, she decided, with looking away. "Through the stone."

Emrys lowered themselves onto the second crate, leaving a careful arm's length of shadowed air between them. In the lamplight their face looked older than it had in the shop — or perhaps simply unguarded, which amounted to the same thing. "I heard it," they said quietly. "From up here. Through the floor. I thought at first it was rats in the walls, singing." A small, strange smile. "Rats don't sing in harmony."

"No," Hazel said. "They don't."

For a long moment neither of them spoke. The lamp hissed softly. Somewhere above, Greyhollow settled into itself — cart wheels, a drunk singing badly two streets over, the ordinary machinery of a city pretending everything was fine.

Then Emrys said, "Tell me how you got it."

And Hazel told them.

She told them about the realm beyond the veil, the one the old books in Greyhollow called the Far Country and her people simply called *home*. She told them about the night the stars went out there first — a full turn of the moon before they died here, a warning shot fired across the border of the world. She told them about the cold that came after, the slow gray suffocation, crops that grew pale and tasteless, rivers that ran thick as spit, children born who never learned to cry properly because there was nothing in the sky left to answer them.

"We thought it was temporary," she said. "Everyone said temporary. The elders performed the rites. We fed the bonfires. We sang to the dark until our throats bled, and the dark did not care, because the dark wasn't the problem. The door was. Something had gone wrong with the door between us and you, and all our light was leaking out through the crack, and nobody on this side even noticed."

Emrys flinched. Hazel saw it — a small motion, quickly mastered, but real. She filed it away and kept talking, because once she had started she found she could not stop, and did not especially want to.

"The covenant-stone sat in the heart-shrine in our village. You've seen half of it. It was whole once — a stone the size of a loaf, veined with silver, warm to the touch. It was the anchor. The thing that held our end of the bargain. When the Sundering happened — when your Guild broke the old covenant and sealed the gate — the stone cracked down the middle. Half stayed in the shrine. Half—" she closed her hand around it, "—half fell to me. Or I stole it. Both are true. I'll say both and let you choose which to believe."

"Which is it?" Emrys asked.

"It's mine," Hazel said. "I took it because it was dying and I couldn't watch. Because when the stars went out, the stone started singing with everyone's voices inside it — everyone who'd ever touched it, everyone whose family had kept it for nine generations — and I could hear my mother asking it if I was safe, and I could not bear to leave the answer behind." Her voice cracked and she let it. "So yes. I stole it. From a dying house, with the roof already falling in. And I came through the veil while it was tearing, while the wards you people put on it were failing, and I ran through an alley with something behind me that had too many joints, and I would do it again."

Silence. The lamp hissed.

"You could have sold it," Emrys said at last. "Something like that — a fragment of the old covenant — there are buyers in this city. Rich ones. Desperate ones. People who'd pay enough to buy a new life ten times over."

"For what?" Hazel asked. "A purse full of coin in a city that hates me? While my mother sings herself to sleep through a rock?" She shook her head. "You don't understand yet. Maybe you will. This isn't treasure. It's a *voice*. It's the last thread I have to everyone I love, and every day I hold it, I can hear whether they're still alive. Do you know what that's worth? Do you know what it costs?"

Emrys looked at her for a long time. Then, slowly, they reached into the collar of their shirt and drew out a cord, and on the cord, a key. Old iron, black with age, worn smooth at the grip by thumbs that were not Emrys's — or rather, by one thumb in particular, belonging to whoever had kept the gate before them.

"That's not a charm-shop key," Hazel said.

"No," Emrys agreed. "It isn't."

* * *

What Emrys told her then, Hazel would carry for the rest of her life, in whatever life she had left.

They told it plainly, without flourish, the way people tell things they have rehearsed alone too many times. Three years ago, Emrys Galloway had been named keeper of the gate beneath Greyhollow — the true gate, the Sundered Door, the seam in the world the Wardens' Guild pretended was a myth and the city's oldest maps marked with a blank space and the words *here be nothing*. The previous keeper, an old woman named Maren Ostwick, had chosen Emrys out of a hundred candidates, trained them in secret for a year, and then died, leaving Emrys the key, the oath, and the loneliness.

The oath was simple and terrible: *keep the gate sealed, tell no one, trust no one, and when the wards fail, hold the door alone.*

"I've spent three years telling no one," Emrys said. "Three years selling harmless charms and checking the wards twice a night and lying to the Wardens' Guild with a smile. Three years watching the stars get dimmer and telling myself it was weather. And then three nights ago they went out, and I knew — I *knew* — that it was my fault. That I'd failed. That somewhere on the other side of my locked door, a whole world was going dark, and I was downstairs selling lavender sachets to frightened children."

Hazel heard the guilt in it, raw and self-inflicted, and recognized it, because she had been feeding on the same poison since the night she ran. *I should have done more. I should have stayed. I should have died with them instead of running.* Two people in a cellar, drowning in the same flood, each convinced they were the only one wet.

"The gate is real," Emrys said. "It's real, and it's failing, and it's mine to guard. Mine alone, according to the oath." They turned the black key over in their fingers. "But here's what Maren never told me, because she died before she had to: the gate doesn't want a keeper. The *covenant* wants a keeper. One person, bound alone, forever. That's the old law. That's the shape of the prison we built." Their jaw tightened. "I used to think the loneliest part was the secrecy. It isn't. The loneliest part is that the law itself says *alone*, and I have been alone so long I forgot it was a choice anyone ever made."

Hazel looked at the stone in her hand. It hummed, faintly, patiently — her mother's voice braided through her grandmother's, holding the note, holding on.

"And if the halves were joined?" she asked. "If the covenant were reforged? What happens to the keeper then?"

Emrys was quiet for a moment. "Bound," they said. "To the gate. For good. That's the price written into the old law, and the old law doesn't negotiate."

Hazel nodded slowly, and something settled in her chest — not peace, exactly, but purpose, sharp-edged and clean. "Then we should be clear with each other," she said. "Because I think you already know what I'm going to say, and I think it's the same thing you're going to say, and one of us is going to have to be faster than the other."

Emrys almost smiled. "That sounds like a bargain."

"It sounds like a war," Hazel said. "A polite one. Between two people who'd rather die than let the other pay alone." She held out the stone-half, humming, glowing faintly silver along its broken edge. "Your gate. My stone. Your oath. My family. Neither of us gets to be a martyr in private anymore. Agreed?"

Emrys looked at the stone. At her. At the space between those two things, where a whole world's survival hung on a word.

"Agreed," they said.

And the stone, in Hazel's palm, sang one clear, bright note — like a bell, like a beginning — and above them, muffled by floorboards and distance and the whole sleeping weight of Greyhollow, something answered. A sound from below the city. Deep. Patient. A door, remembering it existed.

Emrys stood, and their face had gone pale and resolute. "That's the gate," they said. "It knows the stone is close." They picked up the lamp, and their hand was steady, and their eyes were not. "We need to move tonight. Because tomorrow morning, the Wardens' Guild comes to this street asking questions — and I just remembered what else is buried down there besides the door."

"What?" Hazel asked, rising.

Emrys blew out the lamp.

"The other half of your stone," they said, "is in the Guild's vault."
