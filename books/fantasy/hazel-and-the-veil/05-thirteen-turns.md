## 5. Thirteen Turns

*Emrys*

The cellar had never felt small before. Three years Emrys had kept it — swept stone, jars of pickled things, a cot for the nights when the gate's hum grew too loud to sleep above. Tonight it was a coffin with shelves.

They had told Hazel the truth about the vault and then immediately regretted it, because Hazel had gone very still in the way of people deciding whether to run. So Emrys did the only sensible thing left, which was to light every candle they owned and put the kettle on a spirit-stove among the preserves, as if tea could hold back the end of the world.

It was while the water muttered that the ghost arrived.

Emrys knew it first as a coldness behind the left ear, an old familiar ache — the gate's keeper-sense, the thing that lived in them like a splinter of winter. Then the candle flames all leaned sideways at once, toward the far wall, where no draft came from, and the shadows there thickened into the shape of a woman.

She was old the way rivers are old. Her dress was a style from before the Sundering, high-collared and silver-grey, and her face had the terrible patience of someone who has waited out centuries on purpose. She did not so much appear as *condense*, the way frost forms on glass.

"Keeper," she said. Her voice was dry leaves and door hinges.

Emrys set down the kettle with exaggerated care. "Grandmother Wren."

Hazel startled. "You *know* her?"

"I know of her." Emrys's mouth was dry. "Every keeper does. She was the first. The one who made the bargain." They turned back to the shape in the shadows and made themselves bow, which their knees objected to. "You've never come up before. In three years you've never come *up*."

"The rules were different three years ago," said Wren. "The veil was whole. Now it is torn, and torn things let through more than hedge-witches and hungry beasts." Her eyes — pale as moonstone, and just as cratered — moved to Hazel, to the satchel at her hip where the stone-half slept. "And they let through *that*. So I have come to tell you what your oath-givers were too frightened or too proud to write down."

"Which is?" Hazel asked.

Wren smiled, and it was not a comforting sight. "That you are already dying. Both of you. Both *worlds*."

* * *

*Hazel*

Hazel had grown up on stories of the Sundering, but the stories in her realm were different from the ones here. Over there, the Sundering was the night the stars began going out — one by one, like lamps in a house where someone was walking ahead of you snuffing candles. Here, apparently, it was the night magic was outlawed. Same wound, two scar-tissues. She was beginning to understand that the wound itself had never been treated at all.

Now she sat on an upturned preserve crate with the stone-half warm against her ribs, listening to a dead woman explain how she was going to die.

"The covenant is not a wall," Wren said. She paced as she spoke, though her feet made no sound and disturbed no dust — she glided an inch above the floor, and Hazel decided not to mention it. "A wall keeps things apart. The covenant is a *lung*. It breathes between the realms — starlight this way, root-magic that way, each feeding the other. When your forebears sealed the gate and called it safety, they did not stop the breathing. They only stopped it being *tended*."

"And now it's failing," Emrys said quietly.

"Now it is failing. And when the moon completes its thirteenth turn—" Wren raised one translucent hand, and in the air above her palm the shadows gathered into shapes: a disc of dark, then a sliver, then a full moon, turning and turning, each turn a little faster than the last. "—the veil will harden. Not tear. Not fade. *Harden*, like a scab over a wound that needed air. Nothing crosses after that. No light, no magic, no messenger, no mercy. Each realm will be sealed inside its own skin."

"For how long?" Hazel asked.

"There is no *how long*," Wren said. "That is the cruelty of it. A sealed jar does not open itself. Your realm is already suffocating — you told him so yourself, girl. Starless skies, fields that won't wake, children born grey. This realm will follow more slowly, because its lungs are bigger. But it will follow." The moons above her palm spun faster. "Thirteen turns. Count them. You have already spent four since the wards fell."

Hazel did the arithmetic and felt the floor tilt. Nine turns. A moon turned in a little under thirty days. Nine months — less, if the turns were quickening, and the spinning shadows suggested they were.

"That's not enough time," she said. "To find the other half, get into the Guild vault, reforge the covenant—"

"It is exactly enough time," Wren said, "if you stop wasting it on fear. Fear is a fine fuel but a poor map." She closed her fist and the moons went out. "The second half lies in the Guild's vault beneath their chapterhouse — the Vault of Quiet Things, we called it, before there was a Guild to keep it. They took it during the Sundering, when they took everything. They believe it is evidence. A relic. A trophy." Her moonstone eyes fixed on Emrys. "They do not know it is half a heart."

"And if we reunite the halves," Emrys said slowly, "at the gate. Then what? The old law still stands. The covenant demands a keeper bound forever. One keeper. Alone. That was the original price, wasn't it? That was *you*."

The silence stretched. Somewhere above them, Greyhollow's bells rang the third hour, muffled and distant, like a sound coming through water.

"Yes," Wren said at last. "That was me. Four hundred years alone at a door in the dark, holding a lung open with my hands. I do not recommend it." Something moved across her face — not quite pain, not quite humor. "But I will tell you a secret, keepers, since you are kind enough to be the first company I've had in a century: the law that demands one keeper was written by frightened people. And frightened people write small laws. Whether the covenant can be made to accept a larger one—" her smile again, thin as paper, "—is a question no one has ever been brave enough to ask."

* * *

*Emrys*

After the ghost faded — not departed, Emrys noticed; *faded*, the way a lamp dims rather than goes out, as if she had simply stepped back behind the wall of the world — neither of them spoke for a long time.

Emrys cleaned the spirit-stove. Hazel rewrapped the stone-half in its cloth. Ordinary motions, performed in the ringing quiet, while nine moon-turns counted themselves down somewhere overhead.

"We'll need plans of the chapterhouse," Emrys said finally. "The vault sits three levels down, behind warded iron. I know the street-level layout from selling charms to the clerks — gods, I sold a *warding charm* to a vault-warden once, the irony could kill me — but below ground I'm guessing."

"You're talking like we're doing this," Hazel said. Not accusing. Testing.

Emrys looked at her — at the hedge-witch with ash in her hair and her family's voices humming against her ribs, who had crossed a dying sky on the strength of a stolen half-stone and a stubbornness Emrys recognized the way one recognizes one's own handwriting. Three years ago, Emrys would have said the smart move was to put her on a cart out of the city and guard the gate alone until the thirteenth turn took them, as oaths required. But Wren's face was still hanging in the memory of the candlelight, and *four hundred years alone* was written under everything now, and Emrys found they could not say the smart thing.

"We're doing this," they said. "I've spent three years keeping a secret instead of keeping a promise. I'm done with the first one."

Hazel nodded slowly. Then, practical as a hedge-witch must be: "Your friend Tam. The one who sells you Wardens' surplus. Can he get us plans?"

"He can get us *arrested*," Emrys said. "Whether he gets us plans first depends on how good my luck-charms actually are." They stood, joints aching, and blew out all but one candle. "We move tomorrow night. Which means today we do nothing — nothing unusual, nothing memorable. I open the shop. You stay down here and sleep, and eat, and talk to your stone. And when the bells ring compline—"

The shop bell upstairs rang.

Not the street door. The little brass bell above the *cellar hatch* — the one Emrys had rigged years ago to warn of anyone coming down the outside stair. It gave one bright, cheerful, catastrophic jingle.

Emrys and Hazel looked at each other in the candlelight.

Footsteps on the outside stair. Heavy boots, several pairs, descending with the unhurried confidence of people who had already decided what they would find.

"Wardens," breathed Hazel, hand flying to the satchel.

"No," Emrys whispered, heart slamming, mind racing through the inventory of the cellar — the gate-hum too strong to mask, the stone singing, nowhere to hide, *nowhere*— "Wardens knock. Wardens announce. This is—"

The hatch above them opened, and lamplight spilled down the stairs, and a voice Emrys knew — rough, tired, and utterly unexpected — called softly into the dark:

"Galloway. It's Tam. Don't hex me." A pause, and then, lower, urgent: "Vane's got your name on a warrant and the chapterhouse on lockdown by dawn. Whatever you're hiding down there — you've got until morning. And Galloway? I saw the warrant's second page." The footsteps started down. "They're not hunting a witch. They're hunting a *stone*. And I think I know why."
