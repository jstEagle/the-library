## 6. Smoke Over Greyhollow

The knock came at dawn, and it was not a customer's knock.

Hazel knew the difference the way she knew weather in her bones. Customers knocked twice, apologetic, half-afraid of waking whatever lived behind the charm shop's door. This was three blows, evenly spaced, struck with the flat of a gauntlet — the sound of an institution that had never once apologized for existing.

She was already moving. Down the ladder from the loft where Emrys had insisted she sleep — *the cellar's too damp for you, you'll catch your death* — past the shelves of bottled luck and knotted thread, to where Emrys stood in the gray light of the front window, very still.

"Back door," Hazel whispered.

"Blocked since midnight." Emrys's voice was flat as slate. "They cordon first. Always. I taught half of them that trick myself."

Through the window, Greyhollow's street unrolled in the early murk: lamplighters snuffing their wicks, a baker hauling his shutter, and between them, moving with terrible unhurried purpose, six figures in Warden grey. At their head walked a woman with iron-shot braid and a face like a closed ledger.

"Vane," Emrys said.

"You know her."

"I trained her." Something flickered across Emrys's face — not fear, Hazel realized. Shame. "Three years ago I would have called her a friend."

The knocking came again, harder. A voice through the wood: *"Wardens' Guild. Open by order of the Sundering Statutes."*

Emrys turned from the window, and what Hazel saw then frightened her more than the raid did. It wasn't panic. It was grief arriving early, already finished with itself — the look of someone who had done this arithmetic in the night and hated the answer.

"Hazel." Emrys took her by the shoulders. Their hands were steady; their eyes were not. "The cellar. There's a hatch behind the salt barrels. It drops into the old drainage run. Take the stone. Whatever happens up here, whatever you hear — you don't come back up. You go east until you reach the cistern junction, and if I'm not there by the third bell—"

"No." The word came out of her before she could dress it in sense. "I didn't crawl through a dying world and lose—" Her hand closed over the stone-half at her throat, warm as a held bird. "I'm not doing this alone again. That's the whole point. That's been the point since the alley."

For a moment Emrys just looked at her, and something in their face came apart and reassembled into something else. Not surrender. Recognition.

"The lamp oil," Emrys said quietly. "Under the counter. All of it. And the spirit-wax from the back shelf. Bring them here."

Hazel stared. Then she understood, and understanding felt like swallowing cold water.

"You're going to burn it."

"I'm going to burn everything they want." Emrys was already moving, pulling ledgers from beneath the floorboards — three years of careful, patient records, every charm sold, every coin earned, every small kindness logged in a neat hand. "Vane isn't here for me. She's here for proof that magic survived the Sundering. Ledgers. Stock. Witnesses. If she finds a single bottle of luck-water intact, she'll have grounds to turn this whole quarter inside out looking for more. But ash—" Emrys's mouth twisted. "Ash is honest. Ash says: there was nothing here worth keeping."

"And you?" Hazel asked, because she had to. "What does the ash say about you?"

Emrys went still, one ledger in hand, and for a heartbeat looked older than any living person Hazel had ever seen.

"That I finally stopped pretending," they said.

* * *

They worked fast after that, and there was a strange mercy in the speed of it. Hazel hauled oil while Emrys stacked the stock — the little carved doors that kept nightmares out, the red thread for safe roads, the salt-and-iron charms mothers bought for cradling infants — everything built charm by charm, sale by sale, into a life so quiet and harmless that even Hazel had almost believed it real.

The knocking had stopped. That was worse. Now came the sound of the door being persuaded otherwise: iron on hinges, the groan of wood giving up its arguments.

"Down," Emrys said. "Now. I'll light it and follow."

Hazel hesitated at the cellar hatch, stone-half burning against her collarbone, and looked back once. Emrys stood in the middle of the shop with a taper in hand, surrounded by three years of careful nothing, and the look on their face was not the look of someone destroying evidence.

It was the look of someone lighting a funeral pyre.

The hatch fell shut above her. Darkness, the smell of wet stone and old salt. Then, muffled through the boards, a soft whump — and orange light bloomed through the seams of the floor like dawn coming up wrong.

She heard the front door burst open. Heard Vane's voice, sharp with fury, cut short by the roar of flame. And under all of it, running through the dark toward the drainage run, Hazel heard the stone-half singing — low and urgent, her grandmother's voice threaded somewhere in its hum, saying words she couldn't parse but understood anyway:

*Run, little hedge-witch. Run.*

* * *

The cistern junction was a cathedral nobody prayed in anymore.

Water dripped from vaulted brickwork black with centuries of soot, and the air tasted of iron and old rain. Hazel reached it with her lungs full of knives and no memory of the journey — only the stone's song guiding her left when the passages forked, humming louder when she chose right, her family's dead voices serving as compass points.

Emrys was not there.

She waited one bell. Two. She told herself stories about locked doors and clever lies and Wardens who cared more about fire than fugitives. On the edge of the third bell, footsteps echoed down the junction tunnel — two sets, uneven, one dragging — and Hazel pressed herself against the wet brick with her knife out before her mind caught up with her hand.

A lantern swung into view. Behind it, Emrys, soot-streaked, one sleeve burned to the elbow, alive. And beside them—

A Warden.

Grey coat, Guild badge torn half away, a young man maybe twenty years old with a bloodied temple and his hands raised, palms out, the universal grammar of *don't*.

"He followed me down," Emrys said. "Or rather — he let me see him following, which is worse, or better. Hazel Thorne, meet Tam Voss. Formerly of the Wardens' Guild, currently of nowhere at all."

"I pulled him out of the smoke," Tam said. His voice cracked on the second word. He cleared his throat. "Your shop. I was posted on the cordon. When it caught — everyone else moved to contain the fire. I moved to find whoever lit it." He swallowed. "I've read the old statutes. The real ones, from before the Guild rewrote them. My gran used to say the Sundering wasn't magic failing. It was men being afraid of it." He looked at Hazel's throat, at the faint glow seeping between her fingers where she gripped the stone. "Is it true? Is the veil really dying?"

Hazel looked at Emrys. Emrys gave the smallest nod — *your story, your choice* — and something in Hazel's chest, wound tight since the alley, since the tearing, since the world with no stars, loosened by one degree.

"It's true," she said. "And when the moon finishes its thirteenth turn, both my realm and yours die with it. Unless we fix what your Guild broke."

Tam exhaled slowly, like a man setting down something heavy he'd carried a long way. Then he tore the rest of the badge from his coat and let it fall into the black water, where it sank without ceremony.

"Then I'm your man," he said. "Whatever's left of me."

* * *

They walked east through the undercity while Greyhollow burned its morning above them.

Hazel learned the tunnels as she'd once learned hedgerows — by touch and trust. Tam knew them better than either of them; three years of patrol maps lived in his head, and he talked as he walked, nervous, filling silence the way young men do when they've just amputated a piece of themselves. Emrys walked half a step behind, silent, and Hazel kept glancing back at them.

She had seen grief before. She'd worn it herself for months, walking out of a dying country with a stolen relic and no goodbye. But Emrys's grief had a different grain. Hazel had lost a world that was already ending. Emrys had lost a world that worked — a shop that opened at eight, customers who trusted them, tea gone cold on the counter, a life assembled from ten thousand harmless hours. That was a harder thing to burn. Anyone could be brave about ruins. It took a rarer courage to strike the match yourself.

At a junction where an underground stream sang over stones, Emrys finally spoke.

"I had a chair," they said, apropos of nothing. "By the window. Secondhand, lopsided. I sat in it every evening and watched people walk home, and I thought: *this is enough. This is what safety looks like.*" They laughed, and it was not a happy sound. "Do you know what I feel now? Standing in a sewer with a defector and a fugitive and half a rock?"

Hazel waited.

"Lighter," Emrys said. "Gods forgive me. Lighter."

Hazel reached back without looking and found their hand. Emrys's fingers were still warm from the fire.

"That's not forgiveness you need," she said. "That's just what it feels like when you stop carrying something that was crushing you."

Ahead, Tam had stopped. He stood at the mouth of a wider passage, lantern raised, and his voice came back to them stripped of all its nervousness, gone hard and flat.

"You need to hear this," he said. "Before we go another step. The vault where the Guild keeps the confiscated relics — the deep vault, under the Citadel — I stood guard on it for a year. And there's a piece in there that hums. All night, every night. The old-timers call it the Quiet Thing, and they won't say what it is." He turned, and the lantern light caught his face, pale and certain. "But I know a song when I hear one. And I think it's been waiting for her."

At Hazel's throat, unbidden, the broken covenant-stone began to sing.
