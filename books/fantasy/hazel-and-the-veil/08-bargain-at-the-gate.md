## 8. Bargain at the Gate

The gate did not look like a gate.

Hazel had imagined doors her whole life — her grandmother's cottage door with its iron latch worn smooth by a hundred years of hands, the moon-door in the veil itself that she had slipped through on the worst night of her life. She had expected something with hinges. Something that opened.

What lay beneath Greyhollow was older than doors. It was a ring of standing stones sunk into the earth like teeth, each one taller than two men and carved so deep with covenant-script that the grooves held shadow even when Tam's lantern swung close. The stones leaned inward, converging over a floor of black glass where the halves of the world had once been stitched together. And at the center, set into the glass like a heart into a chest, was a socket — a hollow shaped exactly like the stone Hazel carried against her sternum, the half that sang.

She could feel it singing now. Not the family voices, not tonight — those had gone quiet since the vault, since the second half had come within reach, as if the stone knew it was nearly whole and was holding its breath. What she felt instead was a low, patient hum, the sound of a thing that had waited three years under a city that had forgotten it existed.

Emrys stood at the ring's edge, lantern raised, their face lit from below in a way that made them look both younger and infinitely older than the shopkeeper Hazel had met in an alley nine turns ago. Nine turns. The moon had wheeled overhead nine times since the stars went out, and four remained.

"Four," Emrys said quietly, as if reading her thought. "We have four."

"Then we shouldn't waste them arguing," Hazel said, and stepped onto the black glass.

Her boots made no sound on it. That was the first wrongness — the floor drank sound the way the dead drank memory. The second wrongness came when she knelt at the socket and drew out her half of the stone. It was warm. It had always been warm, since the night she'd torn it from the covenant-shrine in her dying realm while the many-jointed things screamed behind her. But now it was *pulling*, leaning toward the socket like a plant toward light, and the socket was pulling back, and between them the air had begun to shiver with a frequency she felt in her teeth.

She set the half into the socket.

For one heartbeat — one long, ringing heartbeat — the black glass beneath her lit from underneath with veins of gold, script crawling up the standing stones, the whole buried ring waking like a body remembering it had blood. Hazel's breath caught. Through the floor she could feel the other side: cold air, starving dark, the press of creatures who had waited three years at a door that would not open. They were *there*. She could feel their hunger through the stone like frost through a windowpane.

Then the halves refused.

It was not dramatic. There was no explosion, no shriek of rejected magic. The gold veins simply dimmed, the way a face goes still when someone decides not to answer. The stone-half sat in its socket and did not join to anything, because there was nothing to join — the second half was still wrapped in Tam's coat in Emrys's pack, and the covenant, whatever ancient will lived in the script around her, would not reach for it alone.

"It needs both," Emrys said. "And then it needs more than both."

Hazel looked up. Emrys had come onto the glass without seeming to decide to, drawn the way she had been drawn, and they were reading the script on the nearest stone with the particular stillness of someone translating something they had hoped never to translate aloud.

"What does it say?"

Emrys was quiet for a long moment. When they spoke, their voice had the flat carefulness of a person handling broken glass.

"'One willing keeper, bound forever. The gate opens for a keeper's hand and closes over a keeper's heart.'" They lowered the lantern. "That's what my oath was, Hazel. Three years ago I swore a lesser version of this — a keeping, not a binding. Watch the wards. Tend the seal. Tell no one. I thought I understood what I was agreeing to." A short, bitter breath of a laugh. "I didn't. Nobody does, at twenty-three."

"And the full version?" Hazel asked, though she already knew, the way you know a wound before you look at it. "The full version is forever."

"The full version is forever."

* * *

They argued the way storms argue — in circles, gaining weight with every pass.

Hazel offered herself first. Of course she did. She had rehearsed it for nine turns without admitting she was rehearsing it, turning the speech over in the dark of safehouses: *my realm is already dying, my family is already beyond the veil, I came here carrying a stone I stole, I am the one who broke the quiet.* It was clean arithmetic. One life, already half-spent on the wrong side of the sky, weighed against two worlds. She had watched her mother do this kind of arithmetic all her childhood — hedge-witch arithmetic, the kind where you give away your own warmth to keep the fire going and call it prudence.

"I'm the obvious choice," she said. "You heard the ghost. The binding wants willingness, and no one is more willing than someone whose world is already gone. You have a city. You have a life. You rebuilt one from nothing once — you can do it again without me."

Emrys set down the lantern. The gesture was very small and very final, like a card placed face-up on a table.

"No."

"Emrys—"

"You don't get to spend yourself because you've decided you're cheap currency." Their voice cracked on it, just slightly, and Hazel realized with a jolt that she had never once heard Emrys Galloway raise their voice — not with Vane, not in the burning shop, not in the vault with alarms howling overhead. This was as loud as they got, and it was louder than shouting. "Three years ago I told myself the same story. *My silence costs nothing. My small life protects everyone. I am the obvious person to carry this alone.* Do you know what that arithmetic bought? It bought Vane's hunt. It bought a burned shop. It bought a girl bleeding in an alley with a stolen stone and no one to run to, because the one person in Greyhollow who knew the truth had decided his cowardice was a form of virtue."

"That's not—" Hazel started, and stopped, because some traitorous part of her had been counting her own silences the same way. All the letters she hadn't sent across the veil. All the nights she'd hummed to the stone and told herself the family voices were only echoes, only memory, so she wouldn't have to admit they might be *prayers*.

"I'll take it," Emrys said. "Me. I'm the keeper's line. The oath is already mine; we're only completing the sentence. You go free. You find your family when the veil opens — it *will* open, that's the entire point — and you live the life you crossed a dying realm to reach. That was the bargain you made the night you ran. Let me be the bargain I made three years ago."

"And what life do I live?" Hazel heard her own voice go strange and thin. "In a world with a door in it and you on the wrong side of the door? You think I ran across the veil to be *safe*? I ran because everyone I loved was dying and I couldn't watch. And now you're asking me to stand on the living side of a gate and watch *you*."

The words came out and hung there in the lantern-light, and Hazel heard what she had actually said a half-second after saying it, the way you hear thunder after lightning.

Emrys heard it too. She could see it land — see it in the way their shoulders dropped, in the way their eyes, which had been flint-hard through the whole argument, suddenly weren't.

"Hazel," they said, very carefully, "that sounded like—"

"I know what it sounded like."

"Do you mean it?"

The question sat between them on the black glass like a third person. And Hazel, who had faced down Warden-Captain Ilsa Vane with a stolen stone in her coat and a lie in her mouth, who had walked into the Guild's vault knowing it might be a trap, found that this was the hardest thing she had ever been asked to answer honestly.

Because the truth was worse than yes or no. The truth was that she didn't know when it had happened. Somewhere between the alley and the cellar and the fire and the vault — somewhere in the nine turns of running — duty and love had braided themselves together so tightly she could no longer tell which strand was which. She had told herself she was protecting Emrys because the mission needed them. She had told herself she was staying because the stone needed both of them. Every true thing had been wearing another true thing as a disguise, and now, kneeling on the floor of the world with four turns left on the moon, the disguises were all burning off at once.

"Yes," she said. "I mean it. I love you. I think I've loved you since you lied to Vane badly, with your hands shaking, to buy me one more hour. I think I decided to die for you somewhere around the cellar, and I dressed it up as strategy because I'm a coward about the important things." She laughed, wet and unsteady. "We're both cowards about the important things. That's probably why the stone picked us."

Emrys crossed the black glass — three steps, soundless — and knelt in front of her, and took her face in both hands the way you hold something you are terrified of breaking and more terrified of losing.

"I love you," they said. "I have been in love with you since the night you told me the stone sang with your family's voices and you didn't ask me to fix it. You just let me hear it. No one has ever let me just *hear* anything." Their thumb traced her cheekbone. "So we're not doing the arithmetic. Neither of us. Not tonight."

"Then what? The covenant wants one keeper. It doesn't want two. It won't—"

And that was when the stones answered.

Not in words. In pressure. The gold script flared along the standing ring, and the voice of the covenant — the old ghost's voice, vast and dry as pages turning — moved through the buried dark:

*ONE WILLING KEEPER.*

"One," Emrys breathed. "It insists on one."

"Then one of us has to say it," Hazel whispered. "One of us has to say *me* or *you*, and the other one has to let them."

Above them, muffled by forty feet of earth and stone, the moon began its turn.

* * *

Later — much later, though it could not have been more than a minute — Hazel would remember that neither of them answered. That they knelt on the black glass with the covenant waiting and the clock running and said nothing at all, because saying nothing together was its own kind of vow.

But the covenant did not accept silence.

The script blazed white along every stone, and the voice came again, closer now, almost gentle, almost cruel:

*A KEEPER MUST BE NAMED BEFORE THE TURN COMPLETES. NAME ONE, OR THE VEIL SEALS AND BOTH REALMS SUFFOCAATE.*

And in the sudden terrible quiet that followed, Hazel felt Emrys's hand slide into hers — and felt, through the stone in the socket, through the floor, through the whole patient ancient machinery of the gate, something shift. A seam. A clause written so long ago it had been forgotten by everyone except the stone itself, glimmering at the edge of the script like a word half-erased:

*TWO MAY HOLD WHAT ONE CANNOT BEAR — IF THE LAW ITSELF IS BROKEN FIRST.*

Hazel's head snapped up. "Emrys. Look at the stone. *Look at it.*"

The last syllable of the covenant-script was flickering. Unwriting itself. As if the law had been waiting three thousand years for someone stubborn enough — two someones — to read to the very end.

Above them, faint through the earth, the moon slid toward the top of its arc.

"We have minutes," Emrys whispered. "Maybe less."

"Then we break it," Hazel said. "Together."

The white light gathered itself like a held breath — and struck.
