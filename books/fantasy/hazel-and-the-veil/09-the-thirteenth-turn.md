## 9. The Thirteenth Turn

The light struck like a verdict.

Emrys felt it pass through them — not around, not over, but *through*, the way cold water finds every seam in a coat. For one endless instant they stood inside their own bones and saw them lit from within: three years of small careful lies, the charm-shop's dust motes, the oath spoken alone in the dark beneath Greyhollow when they were twenty-two and certain that solitude was simply what duty cost. All of it written in light, all of it read.

Then the white receded, and Emrys was still standing. Still whole. Hazel's hand was locked around theirs so tightly that the knuckles had gone bloodless, and the covenant-stone — both halves now pressed together in their joined grip — was singing.

Not humming. Singing. A sound like a choir heard through a wall, like the memory of music rather than music itself, rising out of the seam where the halves met. The old script along its faces was rewriting itself letter by letter, the ancient clauses dissolving into threads of silver that wound together into something new, something no scribe had ever set down:

*TWO KEEPERS. ONE DOOR. NEITHER ALONE.*

"It's listening," Hazel breathed. Her eyes were wide, fever-bright, tears cutting clean tracks through the grime on her face. "Emrys, it's actually — it's taking us seriously."

"Then we'd better be serious back." Emrys's voice came out steadier than they felt. Beneath their feet, the buried gate was waking. They could feel it through the soles of their boots — a slow tectonic pulse, the heartbeat of a door that had been locked for three thousand years deciding whether to open or to seal forever. "Hazel. Once we say the binding, there's no version of this where we walk away unchanged. I need you to understand what I understand. What it costs."

"I know what it costs." She turned to face them fully, and in the stone's light her face looked carved from something older than fear. "I've known since the hedge. Since I came through carrying half my family's voices in my fist because it was the only piece of home they'd let me save. You think I don't know about cost?" Her laugh cracked in the middle. "I've been paying interest for years."

Above them, muffled by fathoms of earth and stone, the moon reached for the top of its arc.

* * *

There was no time left for ceremony, so they made do with honesty.

Emrys spoke first, because someone had to, and because they had spent three years practicing silence and could not bear to practice it a moment longer. "I kept the gate," they said, "and told no one. I sold luck to frightened children and let them believe the world was only as big as its walls. I thought keeping the secret was the same as keeping the faith. It wasn't. Keeping the faith would have meant finding you sooner." They swallowed. "I bind myself to this gate. Not as its prisoner. As its keeper. With her."

"With him," Hazel said, and her voice did not waver at all. "With them. With whatever we are when this is done. I crossed a dying sky to get here. I carried a stone that sang with my mother's voice because I couldn't carry my mother. I am done running from doors." She lifted her chin. "I bind myself too. Both of us. Or neither."

The stone flared.

The seam between the halves sealed with a sound like a struck bell heard underwater, and the covenant-script blazed down its length — but it was different now, Emrys saw. The old law had been a chain, clause after clause, each link forged for a single throat. What burned before them now was a braid. Two strands, endlessly crossing, neither complete without the other, and where they touched the letters glowed gold instead of white.

And then the gate began to open.

It did not groan or grind. That was the terrible, beautiful thing — it opened the way a flower opens, silently, inevitably, petals of black stone folding back to reveal not a passage but a *presence*: the veil itself, visible at last, a shimmering membrane stretched across the threshold like the skin of a drum. Beyond it, dimly, Emrys could see the other realm's night — starless, vast, starving. And pressing against the membrane from that side, shapes. Many shapes. The things that had been scratching at the world's edges for thirteen turns, drawn by hunger older than language.

"They'll come through," Hazel whispered. "When it opens fully—"

"They'll come through *carefully*," Emrys said. "That's what the binding is for. Not a wall. A doorway with two people standing in it, every day, forever, saying: gently. Gently. There's room. There's time." They squeezed her hand. "A wall just makes them hungrier. We're going to teach them patience instead."

"That's either the most beautiful thing I've ever heard or the stupidest."

"Both," Emrys said. "Definitely both."

The veil thinned. The membrane began, thread by luminous thread, to part—

And boots rang on the stair.

* * *

Warden-Captain Ilsa Vane came down into the dark like winter coming into a warm room.

She was alone. Tam must have slowed the others above — Emrys sent a silent debt toward the surface, to be paid in whatever currency survived tonight. Vane's grey coat was scorched at one shoulder, her jaw set in the particular way of a woman who had decided something irrevocable and hated herself only moderately for it. In her gauntleted hand she carried an iron net folded small, and in her eyes she carried something worse: certainty.

"Step away from the gate," she said. "Both of you. The Guild claims this threshold. By writ of the Sundering Accord, magic passes to Guild custody, and the covenant reforges under Guild seal. The gate becomes an instrument of the city. That is the law."

"The law is being rewritten right now," Hazel said, "by something considerably older than your accord."

"Then it will be rewritten again." But Vane's gaze had caught on the stone — on the braided script, the gold-lit seam — and Emrys watched, with the strange clarity of the final minute, as that gaze snagged and held. Watched her read it. She had spent her life memorizing covenant-law; Emrys had seen her interrogation notes, had seen the margins of her confiscated texts filled with a scholar's furious annotations. Ilsa Vane had never wanted to own the gate. She had wanted to *understand* it, and ownership was the only form of understanding the Guild permitted.

"You wanted it reforged," Emrys said quietly. "You said so in the vault. But look at it, Captain. Look at what it chose. Not a garrison. Not a vault. Two keepers who volunteered. The covenant has been waiting three thousand years for a binding that doesn't rot the binder. Are you going to tell it it's wrong?"

"The covenant," Vane said, "is a weapon waiting for a hand."

"It's a *door*," Hazel said, "waiting for a household."

Above them — above everything — the moon reached the top of its arc and hung there, balanced, the thirteenth turn completing itself in perfect terrible silence. The veil shuddered. Threads of it began to harden at the edges, turning from shimmer to glass, glass to stone. Minutes. Perhaps less. Whatever was decided here would be decided now.

Vane looked at the sealing veil. At the stone. At the two of them, hands locked, standing in the exact posture of the braid itself — two strands, endlessly crossing.

And Emrys saw the moment she chose. Saw it arrive in her face like weather: the iron net lowering, inch by inch, until it hung dead at her side. The certainty in her eyes didn't break — it *turned*, the way a blade turns when the hand holding it finally understands what it was forged for.

"My whole career," Vane said, very quietly, "I believed the Sundering happened because someone loved magic too much. That the answer was fewer hands on the wheel." She exhaled, long and slow. "Perhaps it happened because someone stood alone at a door exactly like this one, and no one stood with them."

She stepped aside. All the way aside, until her back met the stairwell wall, and she folded her arms — a sentinel's posture, a guard granting passage.

"Bind it," Warden-Captain Ilsa Vane said. "I'll watch the stairs. If the Guild comes looking for its weapon, it will find me first."

* * *

They turned back to the veil together.

The last unsealed threads were going rigid at the edges; the center still breathed, thin as morning mist, and beyond it the hungry dark pressed close enough now that Emrys could feel it against their skin like the approach of a large animal. Somewhere in that dark, faint and fraying, a voice that might have been Hazel's mother was calling a name.

"Hazel—" Emrys started.

"I hear her." Tears again, but Hazel was smiling through them, fierce as sunrise. "Later. Letters first. Trade routes second. Family visits third. We agreed on the order, remember?" She turned her hand in theirs until their palms pressed flat, fingers laced, and the covenant-stone settled into the cradle of their joined grip as if it had always belonged there. "Now. Together?"

"Together."

They spoke the binding as one voice — not the old words, which were ash now, but the new ones, the ones the stone had written for them, simple as a held breath:

*TWO KEEPERS. ONE DOOR. NEITHER ALONE.*

The stone dissolved.

It went up through their linked hands like dawn going up through a window, flooding bone and blood and breath, and Emrys felt the binding take — felt it as weight and wings at once, as a door settling into its frame, as the exact moment a house stops being walls and starts being home. The gate flared gold. The veil, at the very last instant before the thirteenth turn hardened it to stone forever, *parted* — cleanly, wholly, a curtain drawn back by two hands working in concert — and the starving dark beyond it met, for the first time in three thousand years, not a wall and not a void but two people standing in the opening, saying with their whole joined selves:

*Gently. Gently. There's room. There's time.*

The pressure receded. The shapes beyond the threshold stilled, and one by one — Emrys felt each one land like a bell-note in the new-made bond — they withdrew into the deep of their own realm, patient now, waited-for, expected.

And overhead, through fathoms of earth and stone and the whole sleeping weight of Greyhollow, the stars came back.

They felt it before anyone in the city saw it: a shiver through the binding, a thousand small lights reigniting in the far realm's sky, and then — impossibly, wonderfully — answering lights kindling here, in this sky, in this world, as if the two nights had remembered they were sisters. The veil hummed between them, open, guarded, alive.

Hazel made a sound Emrys had never heard from her — half laugh, half sob, entirely undone — and leaned her forehead against theirs. The stone-light faded from their skin, leaving only warmth, leaving only the bond, quiet now, steady as a hearth.

"We did it," she whispered. "We actually—"

Boots on the stair again. Many boots. And Vane's voice, rising from below with a new note in it that Emrys had never heard the Captain carry — wonder, worn like an ill-fitting coat:

"You'll want to see this. Both of you. The sky over Greyhollow is full of stars — and there's a woman at the top of these stairs who says she's Hazel's mother, and she says—" a pause, and something almost gentle in it, "—she says dinner's ready on both sides of the door."
