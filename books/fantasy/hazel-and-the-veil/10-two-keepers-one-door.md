## 10. Two Keepers, One Door

The first star came back on a Tuesday.

Hazel knew it was a Tuesday because Emrys had spent three months teaching her the Greyhollow calendar — market days and tithe days and the strange civic holidays of a city that had once burned witches and now, tentatively, sold them bread. They were standing in the doorway-house's unfinished frame, arguing about where the window ought to go, when the sky above the eastern ridge flickered like a lamp catching its wick.

One point of light. Small, stubborn, white.

Hazel dropped the hammer she'd been holding. It rang against a floorboard, and neither of them moved to pick it up. She stared at that single star until her eyes watered, and then she felt it — not saw it, *felt* it — as the second one kindled, and the third, somewhere over the veil, over the dying fields of home she'd fled through a tearing hedge with half a broken stone clutched to her chest.

"They're coming back," she said. Her voice came out cracked, like she'd been the one sealed behind glass for thirteen turns of the moon instead of the whole sky.

Emrys didn't answer right away. She had learned, in these months, to read their silences the way she'd once read weather: the set of their shoulders, the way their thumb pressed into the scar on their palm where the binding lived. The shared binding. The covenant they'd shattered and reforged with their own hands while the moon finished its turn above them and Vane stood aside with her sword still sheathed, choosing, at last, not to own the door but simply to let it exist.

"Both sides?" Emrys asked quietly.

Hazel closed her eyes and listened — really listened, the way her grandmother had taught her before the starlessness took the old woman's voice away entirely. Through the stone at her throat, through the thread of covenant that ran from her heart to Emrys's and out through the buried gate beneath the city, she could feel the far realm breathing again. Cold still. Wounded still. But breathing.

"Both sides," she said.

Emrys sat down on an upturned crate amid the sawdust and the smell of new-cut timber, and laughed — a short, startled sound, like something escaping a cage it had forgotten was open. Then they put their face in their hands and wept, quietly and thoroughly, the way people do when grief and relief arrive in the same carriage and can't be told apart.

Hazel sat down beside them and let her shoulder rest against theirs. Two keepers, one door. That was the whole of the law now, written in no book, sworn to no guild. Just the two of them, bound equally, neither alone.

* * *

The letters started arriving before the house had a roof.

They came by ordinary post at first, which made Hazel laugh every time — the vast machinery of two realms' reconciliation, funneled through a boy on a bicycle who left envelopes wedged in the doorframe between the hinges, because the doorway-house did not yet have a functioning door, only a curtain of oiled canvas that snapped in the wind.

*Dear Keeper Thorne,* began the first one, in a hand so careful it looked printed. *I am writing on behalf of my mother, who crossed before the Sundering and has not seen her sister in forty years. We understand visitation is now possible under supervised conditions. We do not know how to ask properly. Please tell us how to ask properly.*

Hazel read it aloud to Emrys twice, because the second time her voice failed.

There were others. A merchant guild in the far realm offering cold-iron tools in trade for Greyhollow wool. A scholar requesting permission to study the gate's threshold-stones "for purely academic purposes" — Emrys wrote back a polite refusal with a smile in every line. And once, folded small and unsigned, a letter containing only a pressed sprig of rowan and a child's drawing: two stick figures holding hands beneath a scribbled sky full of stars. Hazel pinned it above the hearth and never learned who sent it, and decided she didn't need to.

Not all the letters were easy. Some were angry — families who had lost people to the Sundering, to the Wardens' purges, to the slow suffocation both realms had suffered while the veil hardened. *Where were you,* one demanded, *when my brother was taken? Where was the great keeper of the gate?* Hazel wrote back the only honest answer she had: *We were not there. We are here now. I am sorry is not enough, but it is what I have.* She never learned whether it helped. She kept writing anyway.

Emrys handled those harder. Not because they felt less — Hazel could feel the guilt move through their bond like weather through a valley — but because they had carried it longer. Three years of silence. Three years of selling luck-charms and pretending the gate beneath the city was someone else's burden. They answered every accusation with patience and truth, and never once defended themselves, and Hazel loved them for it in a way that frightened her sometimes, how deep it went, how quiet.

* * *

Her family came through on the first day of spring.

She heard them before she saw them — the particular chaos of her mother's voice carrying across the threshold, already mid-argument with her father about whether they'd brought enough preserves, and her little brother Denny shouting that he could see *everything*, the whole grey city spread out below the hill like a map someone had spilled.

Then they rounded the gatehouse arch and stopped, all four of them, staring at her.

Hazel had prepared a speech. She'd worked on it for weeks — explanations, apologies, the whole story of the stone and the hedge and the thing with too many joints that had chased her through. She got exactly none of it out. Her mother crossed the space between them in four strides and held her so tightly that Hazel's ribs creaked, and her father wrapped around them both, and Denny burrowed into the middle of it like a burr, and for a long time nobody said anything at all.

"You're taller," her mother finally managed, wetly, into her hair.

"You're real," Hazel said, which was not the same thing, and everyone understood that.

When they finally untangled, her mother turned to Emrys, who was standing awkwardly by the doorframe with the expression of someone unsure whether they were intruding on a miracle. Her mother looked them up and down — this person her daughter had written about, guarded, bound to, chosen — and then did the most terrifying thing imaginable.

She hugged them too.

"Thank you for keeping her," she said simply.

"I — she kept me, mostly," Emrys managed, ears going red, and Hazel's father barked a laugh, and just like that the strangeness broke, the way ice breaks on a river in thaw — all at once, and everywhere.

Denny spent the afternoon interrogating Emrys about the Wardens ("Did you fight any? Did you *win*?") and the gate ("Can I touch it? Can I *lick* it?") while her parents walked the unfinished rooms, touching walls, exclaiming. By evening they'd all ended up around a fire in the yard, eating preserves that had indeed been sufficient, and her mother told the story of the night the stars went out on the far side — how the whole village had stood in the fields, silent, watching the sky heal itself one light at a time, and how her grandmother, blind for ten years, had lifted her face and said, *"Ah. There you are."*

Hazel looked across the fire at Emrys, lit orange and gold, laughing at something Denny had said, and thought: *there you are.*

* * *

The Guild changed slower than the sky.

Vane herself came to the doorway-house in early summer — alone, out of uniform, which was its own kind of message. She stood in the yard holding her helmet under her arm like a man at a funeral, and said, without preamble:

"The Council wants oversight. Inspections. A Warden presence at the gate."

"No," Emrys said.

"That's what I told them." Vane's mouth twitched, almost a smile. "I also told them the keepers would sooner seal the gate than surrender it, and that sealing it would kill us all, so perhaps we might try *trust* instead. They're considering it. Slowly. Councillors consider things the way stones consider rolling downhill."

"What do you want, Captain?" Hazel asked.

Vane was quiet a moment. "Tam asks after you. He's working the eastern patrols now — his choice; he says he's done with doors. But he wanted me to tell you he doesn't regret the vault. Even the cost." Her jaw tightened around the memory of what that cost had been — the years Tam had given, the sentence he'd served for opening the way. "And I wanted to see it. The house. Everyone says it's strange — a building grown around a gate like a shell around a pearl."

"It's not strange," Hazel said. "It's a door that finally has a home."

Vane looked at the doorway-house for a long time — the wide threshold, always open, always watched; the lanterns burning on both sides of it, grey-realm oil and far-realm glowglass together; the stream of visitors, traders, letter-carriers, homesick pilgrims passing carefully through, each one checked by wards that hummed rather than harmed. Then she nodded once, put her helmet back on, and walked down the hill toward the city, and Hazel understood that something had been laid down that would not be picked up again.

* * *

On the anniversary of the night the stars went out, Hazel woke before dawn to find Emrys gone from their bed.

She found them on the hillside above the house, sitting in the grass, looking east. The sky was doing what it did now every clear night — healing, glittering, *full*. Thirteen turns of the moon since the dark, and the stars had not wavered once. Somewhere beneath them, the gate hummed its low contented note, the sound of a covenant rewritten: *two keepers, one door, neither alone.*

"You couldn't sleep either," she said, sitting down beside them.

"Every year on this night I used to stand in the shop and count the wards failing," Emrys said. "Three hundred and sixty-four nights pretending everything was fine, and one night knowing it wasn't. I don't miss the shop." They paused. "That's a lie. I miss the counter. And the kettle. And Mrs. Aldery complaining that my charms were overpriced."

"She writes, though. She bought a luck-charm off the trader caravan last month and swears yours were better."

"They weren't. They were hollow. I knew it and she didn't." Emrys turned to look at her, and the starlight caught the silver threading their eyes now — the mark of the binding, matched by the same silver in Hazel's own. "This isn't hollow. That's the difference. Everything costs something, Hazel. This cost everything. And it's the first thing I've ever built that I'd build again."

Hazel leaned her head against their shoulder. Below them the doorway-house slept, lanterns glowing on both sides of its open door, her mother's visiting-cloak hanging on a peg inside, Denny's carved wooden star on the windowsill, letters waiting on the table to be answered in the morning — more every week, more every season, two realms learning slowly, clumsily, wonderfully, to speak to each other again.

"One year," she said.

"One year," Emrys agreed. Then, softer: "And however many come after."

Far to the east, past the ridge, past the veil, past the healed and hungry dark, a shooting star crossed the sky — and on the other side of the gate, Hazel felt someone she loved wish on it too.
