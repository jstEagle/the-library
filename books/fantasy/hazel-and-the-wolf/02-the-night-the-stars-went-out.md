## 2. The Night the Stars Went Out

The wards broke at dusk, and Marguerite knew it before anyone told him — not by sound or by sight, but by the ink.

He was at his desk in the western scriptorium, third window from the corner, grinding a fresh batch of iron-gall into the little stone mortar he'd worn smooth over eleven years of service. It was a task he did with his hands and half his mind; the other half was already three pages ahead, composing the evening's work: two copies of the Ashenmere Ward-Charter, Renewal Formula VII, for the temple's quarterly filing. He had copied that formula one hundred and forty-one times in his life. He could have written it blindfolded, upside down, drunk on temple wine, or dead. Sometimes, on bad days, he suspected he had done all four at once.

But tonight the ink behaved strangely.

It sat in the mortar like something listening. When he drew the quill through it, it clung to the nib too long, reluctant, as though it had somewhere better to be. And when he set the first stroke of the charter's opening line — *In the name of the Nine Wards, sealed and sleeping* — the ink bled outward from the stroke in fine dark filaments, spidering across the vellum like frost climbing glass.

Marguerite stopped. Lifted the pen. Watched the filaments continue to spread without him.

"That's not right," he said aloud, to no one. The scriptorium was nearly empty at this hour; only old Brother Cassian remained at the far benches, snoring gently over a ledger he would never finish auditing. Outside the tall windows, dusk was coming down over the valley the way it always did — slow, grey, smelling of woodsmoke and the cold off the mere — and the first lamps of the lower town were blooming one by one along the shore road.

Then the light changed.

Not dimmed. *Changed.* It went the color of old bruises, green-grey and wrong, and every lamp flame in the scriptorium stood up straight and rigid, as if pulled by strings. Marguerite felt it then — a sensation he had no vocabulary for, because no one in Ashenmere had needed such vocabulary in three hundred years: a pressure behind the eyes, a taste like copper coins and lightning, a hum in the fillings of his teeth. The air itself seemed to lean against him.

Brother Cassian woke with a strangled noise. "What — what is—"

The windows rattled in their frames. Somewhere beyond the eastern ridge, beyond the vineyards and the shrine-stones and the border markers where the valley's magic ended and the world's began, there came a sound Marguerite would spend the rest of his life trying to describe and never quite manage. It was not thunder. Thunder comes and goes. This *arrived* — a single vast note, low and crystalline, like a bell struck inside a mountain, like ice shearing off a glacier face, like the world clearing its throat after three centuries of silence.

Every pane of glass in the scriptorium cracked at once. Not shattered — cracked, each in a delicate web, and through those webs the bruise-colored light poured in and lay across the desks and the drying racks and Brother Cassian's horrified face.

"The wards," Marguerite said. His voice sounded very small and very calm, which surprised him, because inside his chest his heart was going like a smith's hammer. "Cassian. The wards just broke."

"They can't break." The old brother gripped his ledger like a shield. "They're wards. That's what they *do*. They've held since the Founding. They held through the Plague Years, they held through—"

Marguerite wasn't listening. He had gone to the window, pressing his palm flat against the cracked glass, looking east toward the ridgeline where the ward-shrines stood — those modest, mossy cairns he passed on his twice-yearly walks to collect reed-pens from the fen-farmers, never once thinking about what slept inside them. Even from here, even in the failing light, he could see that the shrines were lit. Every one of them burned with a pale silver fire that cast no heat and no shadows, and even as he watched, the fires guttered, flickered, and went out — all together, all along the ridge, like candles pinched by an invisible hand.

And above them, above the whole valley, the first stars of evening were coming out.

Except they weren't.

Marguerite frowned. He knew the winter sky over Ashenmere the way he knew the Ward-Charters — by heart, by rote, by long habit of looking up when the copying made his eyes swim. The Hunter should be rising now, belt-first, with the Shepherd's Lamp trailing behind. Instead there was nothing. A hole in the sky where the Hunter ought to be, black and absolute, and beside it another hole where the Lamp should burn, and overhead more holes opening one by one as the darkness deepened, stars winking out not like lights failing but like lights being *taken*, methodically, in some order he couldn't read.

"Cassian," he said again, and his calm was fraying now, threads of it snapping. "Look at the sky."

The old man looked. For a long moment neither of them spoke. Then Cassian began to pray, in the old formal cadence, the invocation to the Nine Wards — and got exactly four words in before his voice failed him, because everyone in Ashenmere knew, in the wordless way one knows one's own name, that the Nine Wards were no longer listening.

* * *

By full dark, the temple bells were ringing the Assembly — all nine of them, out of order, which had happened only twice in recorded history and both times preceded catastrophe — and Marguerite was running down the cloister stairs with his ink-stained sleeves flapping, swept up in a river of brothers and sisters and lay-servants all pouring toward the Great Hall.

The hall was chaos wearing ceremony's skin. Elder-Scribe Veyra stood at the head of the room on the dais beneath the great bronze astrolabe, and Marguerite had never seen her look afraid. She looked afraid now. Her hands were steady — Veyra's hands were always steady, they had corrected his letterforms for a decade without a tremor — but her face above them was the face of a woman doing arithmetic she didn't like.

"Quiet," she said, and the hall quieted, because you did not disobey that voice. "Here is what we know. At dusk, the border wards failed. All of them. Simultaneously. This is not possible, and yet it has occurred." A ripple ran through the crowd; she talked over it. "The ward-shrines are dark. The anchor-stones are cold. Prelate Ondrel has gone with a party to the eastern markers and will send word. Until then—" her eyes found the back of the hall, found Marguerite, and something in them sharpened, "—the temple functions. The temple *always* functions. Scribes to the dispatch room. Now."

Marguerite went. Of course he went. Eleven years of debt-bondage had built obedience into him deeper than bone; when Veyra's voice snapped, his body simply complied while his mind caught up, arriving several steps later with its arms full of questions.

The dispatch room was a narrow chamber off the archive, normally used for copying proclamations about tithes and festival schedules. Tonight it held six scribes, three lamps, and a growing pile of messages coming in from the valley towns — and, increasingly, from outside the valley, which should not have been possible, because who was left out there to send them?

He learned the answer within the hour, and the answer was refugees.

The first dispatches were routine panic: Harrow Ford reporting its well-water tasting of metal; the mill villages asking whether the tithe carts should still run; someone in Lowermere insisting the mere itself had frozen edge-to-edge in a single hour, in autumn, which was absurd, except that three separate messages said it. Marguerite copied them all in his beautiful, disciplined, dead-inside hand, the hand that had won him his bond-price reduction twice, and he kept his breathing even, and he did not look at the cracked windows where the starless sky pressed in like a face.

Then the messages started coming from beyond the mountains.

The first was dictated by a relay rider, half-mad with exhaustion, and the scribe taking it down kept glancing up as if to check that the words were real. Marguerite heard it secondhand, passed down the row of desks in whispers, and then heard it firsthand when the rider himself was brought in to repeat it for the record:

"From Thornhollow, past the pass. Population six hundred. There is no population. The village is empty. Doors open, meals on tables, beds unmade. No bodies. Snow fell there yesterday — deep snow, midwinter snow — and in the snow there are tracks leading away from every house, all in the same direction, all made barefoot, and the tracks do not end. They just stop being visible, partway across the fields, as if the people walked until they were... taken up. Or until walking stopped mattering." The rider drank water with shaking hands. "There's a thing following the snow. Walking with it. The folk in the high passes call it the Winter-that-Walks, and they've started leaving their doors open and setting out chairs, because they say if it's going to come anyway, better to seem — to seem *invited*."

Silence in the dispatch room. Somewhere a lamp guttered and no one moved to trim it.

"Superstition," said Sister Anhel, but her voice cracked on the second syllable.

"It's what they said," the rider answered dully. "I'm telling you what they said. I'm not telling you it's true. I'm telling you I rode through three villages tonight and in the last one there was a man standing in the square in his nightshirt, barefoot in the snow, looking up at the empty sky, and when I called to him he turned around and his eyes were full of silver light, like moonlight with nowhere to come from, and he smiled at me and said, *'It's so much quieter now, isn't it,'* and I rode, messengers' oath, I rode until my horse bled."

Marguerite wrote it all down. His hand did not shake. Later, examining the page, he would notice that the letters had come out perfect — flawless, elegant, machine-perfect — and that this frightened him more than trembling would have. Some deep scribe's instinct in him had taken over, the instinct that says *record everything, beautifully, accurately, because the record is the only weapon we have*, and it had seized his body and worked it while the rest of him stood inside watching the sky go dark.

Around midnight, they brought the refugees themselves through the temple gates.

* * *

He heard them before he saw them — a murmur swelling up from the forecourt, dozens of voices, and the heavy grind of the gate-bars being drawn. Marguerite set down his pen. No one ordered him to the windows; he went anyway, because curiosity had always been the crack running through the foundation of his obedience, the flaw Veyra despaired of ever correcting.

The forecourt was filling with people. Perhaps two hundred of them, maybe more, streaming in from the shore road with carts and bundles and children and livestock, and they were wrong in a way Marguerite took several heartbeats to identify. They were quiet. Two hundred fleeing people, and almost none of them weeping or shouting. They moved like sleepwalkers, like a congregation, faces tilted upward, and Marguerite followed their gaze and felt the breath leave his body.

There were no stars. None. Not one. From horizon to horizon the sky was a smooth, seamless, utter black, blacker than any night he had ever seen, blacker than the inside of a closed ink-box, and it was not the black of absence. Absence has texture. This had none. It looked less like a sky without stars than like a lid drawn over the sky, and Marguerite understood, with a certainty that bypassed thought entirely, that the stars had not died.

Something had eaten them.

"There it is," said a voice near him — one of the refugees, an old woman with frost in her eyebrows, standing at the gate as the last of her people filed through. She wasn't looking up. She was looking east, toward the pass, toward the mountains. "There it is. Coming down the pass. Told you we'd beat it here. Told you."

Marguerite looked where she looked, and saw nothing — only darkness, only mountains — and then he saw that the darkness over the pass was moving. Flowing. Pouring down the slopes like smoke poured from a jug, slow and vast and patient, and where it touched the peaks the peaks stopped being visible, and the snowfields below them went silver-white and then grey and then dark, and the temperature of the wind coming off the mere dropped so suddenly that the puddles in the forecourt cracked audibly, all at once, like applause.

"Inside," someone shouted. "All of you, inside, get *inside*—"

The temple swallowed the refugees the way it swallowed everything — inefficiently, in corridors, with much shouting and triage. Marguerite lost the thread of it. He found himself carrying a child he did not know, a girl of perhaps five with snow in her hair that would not melt, delivering her to the hall where blankets were being distributed, and the girl looked up at him with enormous serious eyes and said, "The quiet lady is coming. Mama says don't look at her when she asks your name."

"Your mama's right," Marguerite said, and gave her to a sister, and stood there a moment with his empty arms, feeling the cold of the child's snow still lingering against his chest.

Then he went back to the dispatch room, because the record was the only weapon, and sat down, and copied.

He copied until his wrist screamed. Dispatches, manifests, a preliminary census of the refugees that Veyra dictated in a voice like a blade being honed. The numbers were bad. The stories were worse. Village after village beyond the pass, empty or silent or worse-than-empty; the walking winter advancing at the pace of a funeral procession, unhurried, unstoppable; and threaded through every account, the same detail, repeated like a refrain: *it asks names.* The survivors said it in whispers. The quiet lady, the still man, the winter-that-walks — whatever it was, wherever it passed, it asked people their names, and the ones who answered were never seen again, and the ones who refused grew strange and silent and eventually walked out barefoot into the snow.

Names. It wanted names.

Marguerite sat back from his desk, flexing his aching fingers, and felt something turn over in his mind — the scribe's instinct again, but darker now, keener. Because he knew about names. Names were his trade's secret heart, the craft beneath the craft: true-name script, the old covenant letters, the art of writing a thing so precisely that the thing became bound. Every ward-charter he had ever copied was, at bottom, a name — the valley's name, written large, written safe. Ashenmere had survived the mage wars because someone, three hundred years ago, had written its name so perfectly that magic itself could not cross the lines.

And now something was crossing the world asking for names, and the valley whose name was written safest of all had watched its wards shatter like sugar-glass in a single evening.

The arithmetic of that did not close. Marguerite turned it over and over, and it would not close, and the not-closing of it filled him with a dread that was, underneath, horribly, undeniably, a kind of awe. Because if the wards could break — if the impossible could happen, if the sky could be eaten — then the world was larger and stranger and more terrible than eleven years of copying had led him to believe, and some traitorous chamber of his heart, the same chamber that forged medicine-chits and dreamed of the world past the mountains, whispered: *at last.*

He hated that whisper. He wrote dispatches until dawn to drown it out.

Near morning, Veyra came through the rows of exhausted scribes and stopped at his desk. She laid a message slip before him — fresh, urgent, sealed with the prelate's mark.

"Copy this first, and copy it clean," she said. "It goes to every town in the valley by noon."

Marguerite broke the seal. The prelate's hand was usually immaculate; this was scrawled, slanting downhill, the writing of a man writing while walking fast or running. It was short.

*Wards did not fail. Wards were broken. Cut from the outside, all nine anchors, one stroke, one hand. Something crossed the border in the moment of breaking. It is not the winter. It is ahead of the winter. Find it.*

Marguerite read it three times. Then he dipped his pen, squared his shoulders, and began to write — and it was only halfway down the page, in the middle of the phrase *one stroke, one hand*, that he remembered the refugee child's snow that would not melt, and the rider's story of the man with silver-light eyes, and he glanced up, involuntarily, toward the temple doors.

Where, he would learn soon enough, something had just been carried in out of the dark — something wounded, something human-shaped, something with a word already waiting on its lips for the one scribe in Ashenmere who would know what to do with it.

But that was hours later. For now there was only the pen, the page, the dying lamplight, and the words marching out beneath his hand like soldiers: *find it, find it, find it.*
