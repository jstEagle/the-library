## 3. What Came Through the Wards

The third day of the dark, Marguerite stopped counting the refugees.

He had begun with a tally-slate and a steady hand, as the elders commanded — one stroke per soul through the temple gates, grouped by family, by village, by the direction from which they had come. By noon of the first day he had abandoned the grouping. By dusk he had abandoned the slate itself, because his ink-stained fingers had gone numb with cold and because the numbers had stopped meaning anything. Four hundred souls. Six hundred. A thousand, pouring down the mountain road in a river of grey faces and greyer breath, all of them fleeing a thing that walked behind them at the pace of winter.

Now they filled the temple courtyard like snow filling a ditch: silent, drifting, settling into every hollow. They slept standing against the colonnades. They huddled under the eaves of the alms-hall where the braziers smoked and gave little heat. Children who should have been crying were not crying, which frightened Marguerite more than weeping would have. Weeping was a fire that eventually burned down to ash and sleep. This silence was something else — a held breath, a valley-full of people waiting for the sky to finish whatever it had begun.

"Brother Marguerite." Sister Anselm's voice cut across the courtyard, thin and precise as a stylus. She stood on the scriptorium steps with a bundle of dispatches under her arm, her wimple rimed with frost. "The elders want copies of the appeal to the high temples at Vael Corrinth. Three fair hands. The courier leaves within the hour."

"The passes are closed," Marguerite said. It was not disobedience; it was arithmetic. "No rider reaches Vael Corrinth before midwinter now."

"Then the courier will die trying, and the letter will be found on his body, and it will say that Ashenmere did its duty." Her eyes, pale as skimmed milk, held no malice — only the terrible tidiness of a woman who had never once doubted an instruction. "Three fair hands, Brother. And mind your margins. If the world is ending, it will end in good order or not at all."

He took the dispatches. He always took the dispatches.

In the warmth of the scriptorium — the one warm room left in the temple, because the elders ruled that ink must not freeze even if flesh might — Marguerite sat at his desk and copied the appeal three times. *To the Most Radiant Conclaves of Vael Corrinth, greetings. Be it known that on the eve of the Feast of Lanterns, the border wards of the Valley of Ashenmere did fail—*

*Did fail.* His pen hovered over the phrase. He had copied those wards ten thousand times. He knew their charter-verse better than his own name: the sevenfold ring, the star-anchors set into the mountain stone, the covenant-script that turned away everything hungry from beyond the pass. For twenty-two years he had written out their words in beautiful, useless, immaculate lines, and never once — not once — had the words done anything but dry on the page. Magic died at the valley's edge. Everyone knew it. The wards were not spells here; they were *history*, preserved like a saint's fingerbone, potent somewhere else, inert here.

And yet they had failed. Not faded. Not frayed. Failed, all at once, on the same night the stars went out, as though something had reached up and snuffed them along with the sky.

Marguerite looked down at his own hand, at the faint ghost of the forged dispensation still buried under layers of legitimate work — the medicine-chit he had written for his mother, signed with a prebendary's hand so perfect that the prebendary himself could not have sworn it wasn't his. That forgery had kept her lungs open through two winters now. It was the only true magic Marguerite Thornbury had ever performed, and it worked because it was made of nothing but ink and nerve.

*If the wards failed,* he thought, copying *did fail* in his fairest hand, *then something came through them. Wards do not break themselves.*

He finished the third copy. He blotted it. He squared its edges with the others, because Sister Anselm would check.

* * *

It was near vespers — though vespers meant nothing now, with no stars to order the hours and the sun going down at noon behind walls of cloud — when the soldier came through the gates.

Marguerite saw him because Marguerite was the one carrying soup. This had become his second duty of the dark days: ladling out the temple's barley broth to refugees too numb to complain about the barley. He was good at it in the way he was good at everything — quietly, precisely, without being thanked — and so he noticed things the other servers missed. He noticed that the river of arrivals had slowed to a trickle. He noticed that the trickle had changed character: these last ones came alone, or in pairs, moving with the desperate economy of people who had outrun something and knew they hadn't outrun it far.

And he noticed the soldier.

The man came through the gate leaning on a spear that was more splinter than shaft, and Marguerite's first thought was not *wounded* but *wrong*. Everything about him was wrong in small ways that added up to a large one. His uniform was mountain-troop grey, correct to the last button, but it hung on him like clothing on a scarecrow — as though the body inside it had been poured in hastily and hadn't finished setting. He walked like a man crossing ice, testing each step. And though the courtyard was full of people groaning and shivering and calling out for sons and mothers, this man made no sound at all. No breath steamed from his lips. In air cold enough to crack stones, he cast no breath at all.

Marguerite told himself he had imagined it. He was tired. Everyone was seeing omens in everything; a woman yesterday had sworn the soup-pot whispered to her.

Then the soldier crossed the courtyard — past the braziers, past the queues, past a hundred pairs of eyes — straight to the foot of the temple steps, and fell.

Not staggered. *Fell*, the way a marionette falls when the hand holding the strings opens. He went down full-length on the frozen flagstones with a crack that Marguerite heard across the whole yard, and lay there, and did not move.

The crowd did what crowds do: drew back, murmured, formed a ring. Marguerite set down the soup-ladle — he would remember, later, that he had set it down with care, parallel to the pot's rim, because some part of his mind refused to stop being a scribe even as the world ended — and pushed through the ring of onlookers.

Up close the wrongness was worse. The man was young, maybe thirty, with a trooper's close-cropped hair and a jaw stubbled grey, but his skin had the waxy look of a candle burned down to its last quarter-inch. There was blood on him — a great deal of it, soaked through his side and stiffening black — but when Marguerite knelt and pressed fingers to the man's throat, the pulse beneath was slow and vast and deep, like something turning over in sleep far underground. Not a wounded man's pulse. Not any man's pulse.

"He needs the infirmary," someone said behind him.

"He needs a priest," said someone else.

"He needs nothing," said a third voice, older, and Marguerite turned to find Elder Cassian standing above them on the steps, robed in temple-grey, his face folded into its expression of judicious sorrow. "Look at him, children. A soldier of the outer provinces, come through the broken wards. You know what walks out there now. You know what the refugees say follows them." He let his gaze travel around the ring of frightened faces, gathering them like sheaves. "This man may be exactly what he appears. Or he may be a door left open. Until we know which, he goes to the undercroft. Bound. Held. Watched."

Murmuring. Nodding. The crowd's fear arranging itself gratefully into obedience, the way water arranges itself into a channel.

Marguerite stayed kneeling beside the fallen man. "Elder," he said, "he'll die on these stones if he isn't bled and bound properly. Whatever he is."

Cassian looked down at him with mild surprise, the look of a man noticing a piece of furniture has spoken. "Then see to it, Brother Marguerite. You have steady hands. Bring him below."

* * *

The undercroft of the temple of Ashenmere had been built, four hundred years ago, to store ward-charter stones — the great carved anchors of the border covenant, before they were moved to their mounts in the mountains. It was a room of pillars and iron rings, cold as a crypt, and the old covenant-marks still crawled across its walls in bas-relief: spirals and star-forms and long lines of script that Marguerite had spent his childhood tracing with forbidden fingers, dreaming of the day he'd understand them.

He understood them now, mostly. Understanding had turned out to be worth exactly nothing.

Two novices helped him carry the soldier down and bind him to a pillar — wrists to the iron ring, properly, with the padded manacles used for the raving and the possessed. The man did not wake during it. His head lolled. His breath — Marguerite checked twice, and a third time — still did not steam in the freezing air.

"Should we send for Elder Cassian when he wakes?" one novice asked, hanging back by the stairs.

"When he wakes, I'll be sitting right here writing down that he woke," Marguerite said. "Go."

He did not intend to sit vigil. But the elders wanted a record — *in good order or not at all* — and so Marguerite fetched paper and ink and a stool, and settled at the foot of the pillar with the patient's chart on his knee, and began the watch.

Hours passed. The novices' footsteps faded overhead. The temple bells rang some hour or other, muffled by stone. Marguerite wrote: *Prisoner brought below at approximately vespers. Wounds to left flank, dressed but seeping. Pulse slow, strong, irregular. Skin cold. No fever.* Then, after a moment, because he was honest even in records no one would read: *Skin colder than the room. Subject does not breathe visibly. Possibly dying. Possibly already—*

He stopped writing.

Because the soldier's eyes were open.

They were silver. Not grey, not pale — *silver*, molten and bright, lit from within like coins at the bottom of a well with a lamp held over it, and in that light Marguerite saw that the pupils were wrong too: not round but slitted, vertical, a wolf's pupils, contracted to threads against a light that existed nowhere in the dark room except in the eyes themselves.

Every hair on Marguerite's body rose. Every rational explanation presented itself for inspection and was found wanting. Candlelight — there was no candle. Fever-glare — there was no fever. His own exhaustion painting monsters on a dying man's face — but the light fell on the pillar, on the floor, on Marguerite's own hands and the chart page, casting shadows, *casting real shadows*, and imagination casts no shadows.

The soldier's cracked lips moved.

Marguerite should have run. He was the temple's property, debt-bound, a man whose entire survival strategy for twenty-two years had consisted of not being noticed; running was the first law of his nature. Instead he heard himself ask, in a whisper scraped nearly flat:

"What are you?"

The mouth worked again. No sound came — the throat produced only a dry click, like a lock turning in an empty house. The silver eyes fixed on Marguerite with an intensity that had nothing of the invalid in it, nothing of the beast either. It was the intensity of a scholar reading a difficult text. Of a man checking a signature.

The lips shaped a word. Soundlessly at first. Then, on the second try, on a thread of voice so hoarse it barely qualified as sound, the soldier said:

"Scribe."

Not a question. A recognition.

Marguerite's pen had stopped against the page, leaving a blot. He stared at it — the blot, the spreading bloom of ink — and then at the man, and the fear in his chest did something unexpected. It did not grow. It *curdled*, thickened, and separated, the way milk separates, into two distinct substances: terror, which said *run*, and underneath it, heavier, older, stronger — curiosity, which said *yes. And?*

"You know what I am," Marguerite said slowly. "How."

The silver eyes closed. Opened. The man's chest rose — finally, visibly, a breath, as though breathing were a courtesy he had forgotten to perform until reminded — and when he spoke again the voice was a shade stronger, rough as a file drawn over stone.

"They told me the valley would blind me. They were right. Everything here is... muffled. Distant. Like hearing a feast through a wall." A pause; the throat clicked. "But you. Ink on your fingers. Ink under your nails. Chart-verse cadence in the way you speak — you think in lines, scribe. I can almost hear it." The slitted pupils narrowed further. "Twenty years I've crossed the world looking for one man who thinks in lines. And the wards fail, and the stars go out, and I crawl through a hole in the end of everything, and the first thing the dark hands me is a *scribe*."

"I don't know what you're talking about." Which was true, and also the most dishonest sentence Marguerite had spoken in years, because some buried part of him — the part that forged signatures, the part that traced covenant-marks by night — understood every word.

"No," the soldier agreed. Something that was almost a smile touched the ruined mouth, without warmth and without cruelty — the smile of a chess player watching an opponent make, unknowingly, the correct move. "Not yet. But you will. Ask your elders what broke the wards. Ask them why the answer isn't in any charter they've ever let you copy." The eyes were dimming now, the silver guttering like a lamp running dry. "Ask them what a true name is worth, Marguerite Thornbury."

The name went through him like a blade of ice. He had spoken it to no one. It was written nowhere in the temple — debt-bonds recorded only debtor-numbers, never names, because names were *leverage* and the temple did not leave leverage lying about.

"How—" he started.

But the light in the soldier's eyes had gone out altogether, and the man's head dropped forward, chin to chest, breathing that vast slow underground breath, asleep or shut down or simply *waiting*, and the undercroft was dark and cold and utterly silent except for the scratch of Marguerite's own pen as, without deciding to, without any memory of having decided, he wrote on the chart:

*Subject spoke. One word. Knew prisoner's name unasked. Eyes luminous, silver, vertically slit. Recommend immediate review by—*

He stopped. Looked at what he'd written. Any elder reading it would have him confined for hysteria, or worse, would move the prisoner somewhere Marguerite could not reach him.

Carefully, methodically, with the same flawless craft he had used to forge a prebendary's hand, Marguerite struck the line through. Above it, in a bland and dutiful clerk's script, he wrote: *Subject delirious. Said nothing of note.*

Then he sat back on his stool in the dark, heart hammering, ink drying, and realized that he had just committed his second forgery in defense of a stranger with a wolf's eyes — and that he had done it without hesitation, without regret, and without the slightest intention of telling anyone.

Above him, muffled by four hundred years of stone, the temple bell rang the midnight hour.

The soldier did not stir. But as Marguerite gathered his papers and rose to go, he could have sworn — though he would never write it down, not in any record, not in any margin — that the man's lips moved once more in the dark, shaping a wordless syllable that sounded, impossibly, like the beginning of a bargain.
