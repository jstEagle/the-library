## 6. The Road Out of Ashenmere

*Marguerite*

The laughter died in his throat a mile past the border stones, and what replaced it was worse.

It was the cold. Not the ordinary cold of an Ashenmere winter, which he had known all his life — the honest, dumb cold of stone and snow and breath-fog, the cold that bit your fingers and numbed your toes and asked nothing of you but endurance. This cold came *in*. It slid through the seams of his gloves and the laces of his boots and settled against his skin like something with intentions. Like something *curious*.

"Is it supposed to feel like this?" he managed, between one step and the next.

The Wolf walked ahead of him through the knee-deep drifts with the unhurried stride of a man who had crossed this country when its roads were roads. The soldier's body moved stiffly on the left side, favoring the hip, and Marguerite had already learned to read that stiffness as weather: some nights it was barely there, and some nights — like tonight — it dragged at him like an anchor rope.

"It's not supposed to feel like anything," the Wolf said. "For most people it doesn't. You're not most people."

"I'm a scribe."

"You're a scribe who can feel ward-script from forty paces without touching it. I've met archmages who couldn't do that." He said it flatly, the way he said everything, as though he were reporting the depth of a river ford. "What you're feeling is the world remembering itself. The valley starved you of it for thirty years. Now you're drinking after a drought. It'll pass."

Marguerite did not want it to pass. That was the terrible thing, the thing he would not say aloud, the thing that sat in his chest like a swallowed coal: he did not want it to pass, ever. All his life he had copied wards he could not cast, traced circles whose power lived somewhere behind his fingertips and never in them, a man pressing his ear to a door forever locked. And now the door stood open and the wind of it was blowing through him, and every hair on his arms stood up beneath three layers of wool, and somewhere under his breastbone something ancient and patient had begun to hum.

He hated how much he wanted it. He hated that the wanting felt like relief, like a fever breaking, like coming home to a house he'd never lived in.

They climbed the shoulder of the first ridge as the last grey light went out of the west. Below them Ashenmere lay in its bowl of mountains, lamplit and small, the temple spire a black needle stitching town to sky. Somewhere down there was the scriptorium with his desk and his ink-stained grooves worn into the wood. Somewhere down there were elders who would be discovering, about now, that their scribe was gone along with a dead man walking.

"They'll send someone," Marguerite said.

"They'll send debt-collectors. Debt-collectors are slow." The Wolf paused at the crest, and his silver eyes caught the dying light and held it a moment after the light was gone. "The things behind us are faster."

Marguerite turned and looked back along the road they had come, at the broken line of the border wards — and saw, for the first time with his own eyes instead of another man's memory, what the Wolf had been fleeing since the night the stars went out.

The dark between the mountains was not empty.

It moved. Slowly, the way smoke moves, the way water finds a level — a darkness deeper than night, pouring itself along the valley floor and up the slopes, and where it passed, the snow did not gleam. There was no moon tonight; there had been no stars since the wards fell; and yet the darkness ahead of them was somehow *less* dark than the darkness behind, because the darkness behind ate light rather than merely lacking it.

"What are they?" His voice came out smaller than he intended.

"Wights. What's left of the first war's soldiers, unmade and still marching." The Wolf resumed walking, downhill now, into the open country beyond the ridge. "They hunt warmth. They hunt names. You have both. Walk faster."

* * *

*The Wolf*

The scribe lasted four hours before his legs gave out, which was better than the Wolf had expected and worse than he needed.

They sheltered in the lee of a fallen boundary-marker, one of the old covenant-stones that had once ringed the whole continent, its script worn to ghosts by eight hundred winters. The Wolf set no fire — fire drew them — and wrapped the shivering man in half the blankets they carried and told himself, with the cold precision he applied to all matters, that this was asset maintenance. A frozen scribe wrote nothing. A dead scribe wrote nothing at all.

But he sat awake beside the boy while he slept, and watched the darkness pool in the valleys below like floodwater, and did his arithmetic again, and got the same answer he always got.

Midwinter. Twenty-six days. The covenant-stone beneath the old capital. A name-craftsman good enough to cut a true-name into living stone — and the boy was that, or could be, if the Wolf taught him fast enough and the wights were slow enough and the soldier's heart held out long enough. Three conditions, and he controlled none of them.

The host-body coughed, wet and deep. The Wolf pressed a hand to his own chest and felt the rattle there, the slow flooding, and noted it in the ledger he kept behind his eyes alongside everything else. Nine days of hard travel left in these lungs, perhaps twelve if he spent nothing on healing. Every spell cost the body years it did not have. He had stopped counting the cost the way a gambler stops counting after a certain point; there was only the final stake, and he had decided on that stake centuries ago, scratched into a watchtower wall in his own hand, waiting for him like an appointment.

Beside him, the scribe made a small sound in his sleep and turned toward the Wolf's warmth without waking, and the Wolf looked at him for a moment too long, and then looked away, and filed that too.

The darkness in the nearest valley shifted.

Not drifted — *shifted*, all at once, the way a sleeping animal shifts, and the Wolf was on his feet with his hand around the hilt of a knife that was nearly useless before the sound reached him: a low, vast exhalation, like wind through a throat of stone.

"Wake up," he said. "Now. Quietly."

* * *

*Marguerite*

He woke with the Wolf's hand over his mouth and the word *wights* already forming in the air between them, and for one absurd heartbeat Marguerite thought of the scriptorium, of Elder Corvane's voice saying *the wards hold, boy, they have always held* — and then the darkness crested the ridge above them and thought left him entirely.

There were seven of them. Or there was one of them in seven pieces; he could not tell. They were shaped like men the way a glove is shaped like a hand — the form without the life, tall and jointed wrong, and where faces should have been there was only smooth absence, and they did not reflect the starless sky so much as *subtract* from it. Where they walked, the snow went grey. Where they paused, the snow went grey and stayed grey, and he understood with a lurch that the greyness was frost, was death spreading outward from their feet like spilled ink on wet paper.

And he could *feel* them.

That was the worst part. The new sense the border-crossing had opened in him — the humming, hungry, wonderful sense — took them in too, and what it reported back was a silence shaped like a scream. They were holes. They were absences walking, and his gift read them the way his eye read a misspelled word: instantly, painfully, as *wrong*.

"The knife won't work," he whispered.

"No." The Wolf was watching them come down the slope with the stillness of a heron over water. "Steel slows them. Names stop them. Which is why," and here he turned his head, and the silver eyes found Marguerite's in the dark, and there was something in them Marguerite had not seen before — not fear exactly, but its close cousin, "— I am going to teach you your first ward-script right now, at speed, with poor light, while they walk here. Do you understand?"

"No."

"Good. Understanding comes later." The Wolf pulled a stub of charcoal from his coat and seized Marguerite's wrist, turning his palm upward, and began to draw on the bare skin with quick sure strokes. "This is the character for *boundary*. Not the temple version — the temple version is a copy of a copy of a copy, third-hand and fourth-rate. This is the root-form. Feel it."

Marguerite felt it. The moment the last stroke closed, the character *woke* — a faint prickling heat against his palm, a tiny assertion of *here and not further* — and the sensation was so beautiful that he almost missed the Wolf's next words.

"Now you. Same strokes. My wrist."

His hand shook. The first stroke came out crooked. "I can't—"

"You copy charters for a living. Your hand knows these shapes better than mine does. Let it know them." The Wolf's grip tightened. "Again."

The second attempt was straighter. The third was clean, and when the final stroke closed, the character flared against the Wolf's skin with a soft gold light that hurt to look at, and the nearest wight — six paces away now, close enough that Marguerite could hear the frost crackling as it stepped — *stopped*.

It stopped the way a man stops at a closed door. Its absent face turned toward the light on the Wolf's wrist. And then it raised one grey arm, and from somewhere inside the sleeve of its absence came a sound like a name being spoken backward, and the light guttered.

"That buys us minutes, not safety," the Wolf said. "They learn. Everything from the first war learns. Run when I run, write when I write, and whatever happens—" his hand closed briefly, hard, on the back of Marguerite's neck, a gesture that was half command and half something else entirely, "—do not let them touch your mouth. They take names out through the mouth. Understood?"

"Yes."

"Then we go."

They went. Down the slope, through the waist-deep drifts, the wights flowing after them with that horrible smooth patience, and the Wolf shouted characters over his shoulder and Marguerite wrote them in the air with a finger trailing fire he did not know he had, each glyph blooming gold and dying, buying seconds, seconds, seconds. His lungs burned. His gift burned hotter. Every ward he cast lit up the hunger in him like a lamp in a dry field, and he hated it, he loved it, he was laughing again or sobbing, he genuinely could not tell which—

—and then the ground fell away beneath them, and they were sliding down a scree chute in a roar of ice and stone, and the wights did not follow, because wights do not fall. They simply stood at the top of the chute in their seven silent bodies and looked down, and waited, and the frost spread grey beneath their feet like a signature.

The Wolf picked himself up at the bottom, spat blood — bright red, worryingly red — and started walking north without a word.

Marguerite followed. His palm still glowed faintly where the first character had been written, and he found he could not make himself wipe it off.

"How long," he said finally, "until they find a way around?"

"Not until dawn. Maybe." The Wolf glanced back at him, and there was blood on his teeth and something almost like approval in his face. "You cast nine wards in ninety seconds, untrained, in the dark, while running. In the first war they gave medals for less. In the first war they also burned people for less." He faced forward again. "Sleep when we stop. Learn when we don't. That's the arrangement."

"And what do I call you?" Marguerite asked, because his mouth had always outrun his caution. "The soldier has a name on his tags. But you—"

The Wolf walked three more steps before answering.

"Hazel," he said at last, so quietly the wind nearly took it. "My name was Hazel. Don't use it. Names are currency out here, and mine spends badly."

* * *

*Marguerite*

Dawn came the color of old pewter, and with it came the mountains of the interior, rising white and endless against a sky with nothing in it — no sun they could see, only a diffuse bruised glow behind the cloud, the light of a world whose stars had been put out. Marguerite stood on the heights above the scree chute and looked at that country, at the ruined watchtowers strung along the ridgelines like beads on a broken necklace, at the far white smudge that might have been the capital or might have been cloud, and felt the magic humming in his blood like a second pulse.

He should have wanted to go home. Some dutiful, temple-raised part of him knew that he should want to go home.

Instead he flexed his glowing palm, and thought about the character for *boundary*, and found that what he wanted — what he wanted with a ferocity that frightened him — was to learn the next one.

Behind them, at the top of the chute, the seven dark shapes were gone. But the frost they had left remained, grey and perfect, spelling out across the snow a single line of characters that Marguerite's new sight read as easily as scripture:

*we smell the scribe*

The Wolf read it too. He was quiet for a long moment.

"Well," he said. "They've never written before."

He turned north, toward the ruined towers and the buried capital and the covenant-stone and midwinter, and Marguerite Thornbury — lawless, debt-free, marked, and wanting it — followed him down into the wide white world.

That night, in the shadow of the first ruined tower, the Wolf said: "Tomorrow I start teaching you properly. Ward-script. Name-craft. Everything the temples were too afraid to give you." A pause. "Try not to be better at it than me. I have a reputation."

Marguerite laughed — and then stopped, because the Wolf hadn't smiled, and because in the tower window above them, faint as breath on glass, something had just begun to glow.
