## 7. Lessons in Borrowed Fire

The Wolf had worn many bodies across the long centuries, and he had learned to read them the way a sailor reads weather — by small signs, by the creak of timber before the mast goes. This one, Corporal Edran Voss of some provincial regiment whose banner he'd never bothered to learn, was failing faster than any he had ever borrowed.

He felt it first in the hands. Voss's hands had been steady things, calloused from spear-shafts and mess tins, but now they trembled at odd hours, a fine shiver like a plucked string that would not settle. Then the teeth. A dull ache along the jawline each dawn, as if the body were grinding itself down to powder from the inside. And beneath it all, deeper than muscle or marrow, the slow leak: every time the Wolf drew on his own magic to mend a wound or warm their camp, something in Voss went out of him permanently, like heat escaping a cracked window.

He did not tell the scribe.

Marguerite Thornbury walked beside him through the blue hours of night with his ink-stained fingers tucked into his sleeves and his mouth full of questions he was too proud to ask, and the Wolf let him believe the limp was from the fall at the temple steps. It was easier that way. The boy — man, he corrected himself; Marguerite was near thirty, though he carried himself like an apprentice caught thieving — needed to believe his guide was sound. Fear made scribes sloppy. Sloppy scribes died on the road to the capital, and dead scribes could not write him into the covenant-stone.

So they traveled by night, when the starless dark was thickest and the wights slept shallow in their hollows, and by day they hid in ruins and culverts and once, memorably, in the root-cellar of a burned-out shrine while a column of the Order of the Unbroken Star marched past singing hymns with no melody in them. And in those daylight hours, in the grey half-light under broken roofs, the Wolf taught him.

* * *

"Again," the Wolf said.

They sat in the shell of a waystation, its roof open to a sky the color of dirty wool. Marguerite had cleared a patch of floorboards with his sleeve and was scratching ward-script into the dust with a stick, because the Wolf refused to waste good ink on a student who might die before the pass.

"You've said 'again' eleven times," Marguerite muttered. "I've been counting."

"Good. Counting is the first discipline of name-craft." The Wolf leaned against the wall, arms crossed, and watched the stick move. "A covenant is only arithmetic performed on souls. If you cannot keep tally, you will lose track of what you owe."

"That's a comforting thing to teach a debtor."

"I'm told I'm comforting."

The stick paused. Marguerite looked up at him with that expression the Wolf was coming to know too well — the one where resentment and curiosity fought openly behind his eyes, and curiosity kept winning, which the Wolf suspected annoyed him more than anything else about this whole arrangement. "The third glyph," Marguerite said. "You gave me the closed form for binding, but you drew it open. The loop doesn't seal."

"It seals when spoken."

"Why?"

The Wolf considered lying. He was very good at lying; he had lied to kings and councils and, for three hundred years, to himself. But there was something about the scribe's flat, patient stare that made lies feel like work he hadn't the strength for today.

"Because a name is a door," he said. "Written, it is only the shape of a door — paint on wood. Spoken by someone with the right to speak it, it opens. The loop is the hinge. You close it with your voice, and whatever is on the other side of the name comes through."

"And what's on the other side of yours?"

The question landed harder than Marguerite could have known. The Wolf kept his face still, which after centuries of practice cost him nothing, and said, "Nothing you want to meet."

Marguerite held his gaze a moment longer — insolent, unafraid, or performing unafraid so well the difference had stopped mattering — then bent back to the dust. "Fine. Again, then."

He drew the binding-glyph. Open loop. Then, without being told, without hesitating, he added the counterstroke that anchored a ward to living ground rather than dead stone — a technique the Wolf had not yet taught, had planned to teach in another week, perhaps two, somewhere south of the river.

The Wolf went very quiet.

"What?" Marguerite said, not looking up. "It's wrong?"

"No."

"Then why are you looking at me like that?"

"Because I didn't show you that stroke."

Marguerite sat back on his heels. For a moment he looked almost frightened of himself. "I've copied wards my whole life," he said slowly. "Ten thousand of them. Charters, border-seals, funeral bindings, marriage-wards. I've written more magic than most mages have ever cast. I just — never got to see what it did." His thumb moved over the dust-glyph, not quite touching it. "It's like I've spent thirty years memorizing a language I was never allowed to speak. And now the words are just... falling out."

The Wolf studied him. In the temple at Ashenmere he had chosen this scribe for his craft and his desperation, in that order, and he had told himself there was nothing else in the ledger. Now, watching the man trace a counterstroke he had never been taught, the Wolf felt the first cold thread of something he had not allowed himself to feel in a very long time.

Not fear exactly. Recognition.

"You're gifted," he said, and heard how flat his own voice came out. "That's not a compliment. Gifted people die of it. The ones who survive are the plodders."

"I've never been accused of being a plodder."

"No," the Wolf agreed. "I imagine you haven't." He pushed off the wall, joints complaining — Voss's joints, thirty years old and feeling ninety, another bill the body was quietly running up. "We move at dusk. Sleep."

Marguerite lay down on the bare boards with his coat rolled under his head, and within minutes his breathing slowed into the deep rhythm of genuine exhaustion. The Wolf sat watch by the broken doorway and did not sleep, because sleep was another thing the body could less and less afford, and because watching the scribe sleep had become — he noted this with clinical distaste — a habit.

* * *

Three nights later, the wights found them.

The Wolf smelled them before anything else: a smell like snow given a fever, like cold that had learned hunger. He woke Marguerite with a hand over his mouth and pulled him down behind a fallen wall at the roadside, and together they watched the dark between the trees begin to move in ways dark should not move.

"How many?" Marguerite breathed.

"Four. Maybe five. They're young."

"Young? They're—" He stopped. Reconsidered. "Right. Of course. Everything here is wrong, why wouldn't the monsters have ages."

"They're what's left of people who walked out into the starless dark and kept walking. The dark keeps what it takes." The Wolf flexed his fingers. The trembling was worse tonight; he could feel Voss's heart stuttering like a bird against glass. Healing himself enough to fight four wights would cost the body days it did not have. "Can you draw a threshold-ward from memory? The one from yesterday?"

"The open-loop binding? Yes, but I can't—"

"You don't need to cast it. You need to write it large, in front of us, and you need to mean it." The Wolf pressed his knife into the scribe's hand. "Cut your palm. Blood carries intent better than ink. Old trick."

"That's disgusting."

"So is being eaten. Choose."

Marguerite chose. The Wolf watched him drag the blade across his palm with a hiss, watched him kneel in the frozen mud and write — fast, furious, the glyphs slightly crooked but alive, each one humming faintly with the borrowed warmth of his blood. The wights came out of the treeline like spilling smoke, and the Wolf stood up out of cover and gave them something to look at, pulling just enough of his old fire to make his eyes burn silver in the dark.

The lead wight recoiled. The others circled, hungry and cautious, and hit the blood-ward like moths hitting a lamp — and stopped. Not repelled. Confused. The ward held them at its edge, quivering, and the Wolf realized with a lurch that it wasn't holding because of its geometry.

It was holding because Marguerite had drawn the anchor-stroke again. Unbidden. Instinctively. And this time, bleeding and terrified, he had meant it with his whole mortal heart.

"Come on," the Wolf snarled at the wights, buying seconds, buying breath. "Come on, then."

They broke eventually — starved things rarely press a strong door — and melted back into the trees, and the silence afterward rang like a struck bell. Marguerite knelt in the mud, shaking, his cut hand cradled against his chest, staring at the glowing line of script as if it might bite him.

"I did that," he said. "That was me. That worked."

"Yes."

"I've never done magic in my life. I'm not allowed. There's no magic in Ashenmere, there's never been—"

"There has always been magic in Ashenmere," the Wolf said quietly. "There has only ever been a rule against it. Rules and absence are different animals, scribe. You of all people should know the difference. You've forged enough documents."

Marguerite flinched as if struck. Then, slowly, he began to laugh — a ragged, disbelieving sound, half hysteria, half joy. "Gods help me," he said. "It's the forgery, isn't it. All those years copying wards I couldn't cast, I was practicing. I was practicing the whole time and nobody knew. Not even me."

The Wolf looked at him — kneeling in the mud, bleeding, lit faintly by his own handiwork, laughing at the ruin of everything he'd believed about himself — and felt something shift in his chest that had no business shifting.

"Bind that hand," he said gruffly. "And next time warn me before you improvise. Improvised wards are how villages get flattened."

"Yes, master wolf," Marguerite said, with the ghost of his scriptorium insolence, and the Wolf turned away so the scribe would not see what his face was doing.

* * *

The nights grew colder as they climbed toward the pass, and the lessons grew longer, because neither of them wanted to stop.

It became the shape of their days: hide at dawn, sleep badly, wake at dusk, walk until the bodies complained, and then — wherever they sheltered, a culvert, a barn, the ribs of a burned church — sit knee to knee over a scrap of parchment or a patch of dirt, and the Wolf would teach, and Marguerite would devour.

The Wolf taught him the grammar of names: how a true-name was built in strata, surface, depth, root; how the common tongue was a shadow the true language cast; how every covenant since the first wars was a sentence in that older tongue, and most of them were lies. Marguerite absorbed it the way dry ground absorbs rain — greedily, silently, leaving no puddle of wasted effort. He asked questions that were not really questions but scalpel-cuts: *Why does the rite require a willing bearer? Who wrote the original clause? What does the stone actually weigh?*

"Liar's arithmetic again," the Wolf said, deflecting, always deflecting. "Ask me something I can answer."

"Fine." Marguerite sharpened his pen-knife against a stone, unhurried. "What's your favorite color?"

The absurdity of it stopped the Wolf cold. Centuries of interrogation, of councils and inquisitors and lovers-turned-enemies asking him the shape of his soul, and this ink-stained debtor wanted to know his favorite color.

"Silver," he said finally. "Before the exile. When the stars were mine to command." He glanced up. "Yours?"

"Red," Marguerite said. "Ink-red. My mother used to say I was born with it already on my hands." A pause, and something gentler moved through his voice. "She's still in the valley. She doesn't know where I am. She thinks I'm dead, probably, or worse."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be sorry. Get me to the capital, erase my debt, and be sorry on your own time." But there was no edge in it anymore. The words had become a ritual between them, a dance step they both knew, and the Wolf caught himself almost smiling in the dark and was appalled.

Appalled, because Voss's left hand had gone numb that morning and stayed numb. Because he had coughed blood into his sleeve twice in three days and burned the handkerchief both times. Because every lesson he taught, every fire he kindled, every mile he carried them both through the killing cold was another week shaved off the body, and he was spending it — he understood this now, with the clarity of the dying — spending it gladly, on these hours, on this stubborn brilliant infuriating man who laughed at his deflections and drew anchor-strokes he'd never been taught and asked about favorite colors as if the answer mattered.

The Wolf had planned his death for three hundred years. It was supposed to be clean. Solitary. A debt paid in private, witnessed by no one, mourned by no one, because no one remained who would know to mourn.

Now there was a scribe asleep three feet away, curled around his satchel of pens like a dragon around gold, breathing in the rhythm of a man who trusted the monster beside him not to eat him. And the Wolf found that the plan had developed a flaw, and the flaw had a name, and the name was currently smudged with ink up to both wrists.

* * *

On the last night before the high country, Marguerite caught him.

The Wolf had stepped away from the fire to cough — quietly, he thought, into his sleeve, behind the shelter of a fallen beam. When he straightened, wiping his mouth, Marguerite was standing in the firelight watching him with an expression the Wolf could not read, and the silence stretched between them like a wire.

"You're getting worse," Marguerite said. Not a question.

"I'm fine."

"You're lying."

"I'm very good at it."

"I know," Marguerite said. "I'm a forger. I know what lying looks like from the inside." He crossed the distance between them, and the Wolf — who had faced armies, tribunals, the judgment of gods — did not step back, mostly because stepping back would have confirmed everything. Marguerite took his wrist. Turned the hand over. Looked at the fine tremor in the fingers, the grey undertone of the skin, and the Wolf watched him do the arithmetic and arrive at the sum the Wolf had been hiding for weeks.

"This isn't exhaustion," Marguerite said quietly. "This is the body. Your magic is eating it. Every time you heal yourself, every time you light our fires, every time you stand between me and the dark—" His grip tightened. "How much of him is left? How much of *you*?"

"Enough," the Wolf said. "Enough to reach the stone. Enough to finish what I came to finish."

"And after?"

The fire cracked. Somewhere far off, the starless dark went on being starless.

"After," the Wolf said, "is not part of the plan."

Marguerite stared at him for a long moment. The Wolf braced for anger, for accusation, for the scribe to demand answers he was not ready to give — but what came instead was worse. Marguerite simply nodded, once, the way a man nods when a suspicion becomes a certainty, and released his wrist, and said, in a voice carefully stripped of everything:

"Then we'd better not waste any more nights. Show me the next lesson."

And he sat back down by the fire and opened his satchel, and waited, and the Wolf — hiding, as he had hidden for three hundred years, as he intended to go on hiding until the stone itself pried the truth out of him — sat down beside him and picked up the pen.

But that night, teaching Marguerite the deep-strata forms, the roots of names, the Wolf wrote one glyph he had not planned to write: the opening stroke of a crafted true-name, the first layer of the very rite he meant to perform upon himself. He told himself it was pedagogy. He told himself the scribe needed to understand the architecture.

Marguerite copied it down without comment, in his precise temple-trained hand, and looked at it a moment too long.

"Whose name is this?" he asked lightly.

"A dead man's," the Wolf said.

"Mm," said Marguerite, and said nothing more. But the Wolf saw how he filed the glyph away, careful and complete, the way he filed everything — and for the first time in three hundred years, the Wolf wondered whether he was the teacher in this arrangement at all, or merely the next lesson being learned.

Two days later, at the foot of the pass, they saw the village: a huddle of roofs under a sky gone black at noon, and above its gate, painted in fresh white strokes, a sign that read:

**THE SILENCE IS PATIENT. FEED IT, AND BE FORGIVEN.**
