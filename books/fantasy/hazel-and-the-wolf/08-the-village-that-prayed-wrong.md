## 8. The Village That Prayed Wrong

They smelled the village before they saw it: woodsmoke and tallow and something underneath both, a sourness like milk left too long in a warm room. The Wolf stopped at the crest of the hill and stood very still, the way he did when he was listening to something Marguerite could not hear.

"Smoke," Marguerite said. "That's good. Smoke means people, and people mean bread that isn't hardtack."

"People mean other things," the Wolf said.

The borrowed soldier's face had grown thinner since the watchtower — cheekbones pushing up through skin like stones through thawing earth — but his eyes were still silver when the light caught them wrong, and they were catching it wrong now, fixed on the valley below where forty rooftops huddled around a single stone structure that was far too large for the village it served.

"That's a temple," Marguerite said slowly. "Or it was meant to be. It's got the proportions of one." He had spent his whole life inside temples; he knew their bones the way a carpenter knows a house. "But look at the roofline. They've built it open to the sky. No ceiling."

"No ceiling," the Wolf repeated, and there was something in his voice Marguerite had learned to dread — the flat tone of a man reading a sentence he already knew the ending of.

The sun was going down behind the western ridges, and as it went, the sky did what it had done every night for weeks now: it simply failed. Not clouded over, not dimmed — *failed*, the way a lamp fails when the oil runs out, the blue draining out of the zenith first and then the gold along the horizon, until the whole dome overhead was a smooth and starless black, like the inside of a closed eye. Marguerite had seen it every night since the wards broke, and every night it made his stomach drop anyway, some animal part of him insisting that the sky was not supposed to be a *surface*.

Below, in the village, bells began to ring.

"They're not ringing an alarm," Marguerite said. He knew alarm-bells; Ashenmere had drilled him on their rhythms as a boy. "It's — vespers. They're ringing vespers. At sunset. To the dark."

"To what's *in* the dark," the Wolf said. "We go around."

"We've been going around things for six days. I'm hungry, you're limping worse than you'll admit, and that horse needs shoeing if we can find a smith who still has iron. We need this village."

"I don't like open-roofed temples."

"You don't like anything. You didn't like the last three villages either, and two of them were empty."

"The third one wasn't."

Marguerite decided not to ask. He turned the horse down the hill, and after a moment's silence he heard the Wolf's boots crunching through the frost behind him, which was how most of their arguments ended these days: with the Wolf choosing to follow rather than to win.

* * *

The village was called Hollowmere, according to the leaning signpost, though the second half of the word had been chiseled off by someone in a hurry. The houses were low and turf-roofed, windows shuttered against a cold that had held since autumn without once breaking, and the lanes were empty except for chalk marks on every door — spirals, drawn large and sloppy, each spiral centered on a small black circle.

Marguerite reined in and stared at them.

"What is it?" the Wolf asked.

"I know these marks." His voice came out strange. "I've copied them. A hundred times. They're from the *Litany of the Veiled Hours* — an old penitential text, pre-covenant, mostly suppressed. The spiral is the winding-down of creation. The circle is—" He swallowed. "The circle is the face of the god who has stopped speaking. You draw it on your door so that when the Silent One passes over, he sees himself reflected and moves on."

"And your temple taught you that?"

"My temple taught me to copy it accurately. What it *meant* was considered heretical commentary." He laughed, and the laugh came out brittle. "I used to think it was a dead text. Nobody's prayed like this in four hundred years."

"Somebody's praying like it right now." The Wolf's gaze swept the shuttered windows. "And recently. Look at the chalk — it's fresh, redone every few days. This isn't leftover superstition, scribe. This is a living congregation."

From the open-roofed temple at the village center, the bells fell silent, and in the ringing's absence they could hear singing — dozens of voices, low and unhurried, rising into the black sky through the missing ceiling like smoke looking for somewhere to go.

"We should go around," the Wolf said again, but without conviction, because the horse had gone lame two miles back and they both knew it, and because the smell of actual stew was drifting from a cook-fire somewhere near the temple, and because they were tired in the way that makes even bad ideas look like shelter.

"We should knock," Marguerite said, "and be very polite, and say nothing about the sky."

* * *

The man who opened the temple's side door was old, broad, and dressed in robes that had clearly been cut for someone else and taken in by hand — priest's vestments, Marguerite realized, remade from a farmer's coat. Behind him the singing stopped mid-verse, and forty faces turned toward the doorway with an attention that made the hairs rise on Marguerite's arms.

"Travelers," the old man said. It was not a question, and it was not entirely a greeting.

"Pilgrims," Marguerite said, before the Wolf could say anything at all. He bowed, hands folded in the gesture of a lay brother, a motion so worn into his body from twenty years of temple life that it required no thought at all. "We come seeking shelter from the cold, and leave offering whatever blessing our presence may carry."

Something flickered across the old man's face — suspicion, or hope, or the place where those two things meet. "You come at the Hour of Silence. Few walk in the Hour of Silence."

"We walked because we must. The road behind us is worse than the road ahead."

This was true, and the truth of it seemed to settle something. The old man stepped aside. "I am Elder Bramwen. Come in, then, and be quiet while the god listens."

They went in.

The temple's interior was lit by a hundred candles arranged in concentric rings on the floor, all of them burning downward into glass jars — no flame left open to the sky above, Marguerite noticed, no light offered upward, only light *contained*. The villagers sat in rows facing the open roof, where the starless black hung like a lid. In the center of the rings of candles stood a stone font, dry, and beside it a wooden frame draped with chains.

Marguerite looked at the chains for exactly as long as it took to understand them, and then looked away, and found the Wolf already looking at him.

*Supper first,* he mouthed.

The Wolf's expression said, with great clarity, *you are insane.*

*Supper,* Marguerite mouthed again, and smiled at Elder Bramwen like a man who had never noticed a chain in his life.

* * *

The stew was barley and salt pork and a root vegetable Marguerite chose not to identify, and it was the best thing he had eaten in a week. The villagers fed them in a side chamber, apart from the congregation, watched by a dozen silent eyes. The Wolf ate like a soldier, mechanically, keeping his scarred hands in view. Marguerite ate like a monk, slowly and gratefully, and between spoonfuls he asked questions in the soft, deferential voice he'd used on temple elders all his life.

How long had the god been silent? Since the stars went out — no, longer, Elder Bramwen said; the stars had been *fading* for a season, growing small and ashamed, and then one night they were gone entirely, and everyone understood. Understood what? That the Silent One had finished turning his face away. That the long listening had begun. That the world was being weighed.

"And the chains?" Marguerite asked gently.

Bramwen's hands folded around his bowl. "An offering is owed. The god who stops speaking still hears. When the weighing is done, something must be given up, or everything will be taken." He looked at Marguerite with terrible mildness. "You arrived in the Hour of Silence. Some here say that means you were sent. Sent things are acceptable offerings."

Across the table, the Wolf had gone very still, in the particular way of a man calculating distances to exits.

"Elder," Marguerite said, "may I see your scripture?"

Bramwen blinked. "Our scripture?"

"The Litany of the Veiled Hours. The text your marks come from. If I am to be weighed, I would know by what measure." He kept his voice humble, curious, a scribe's hunger for text deployed like a weapon. "I have copied holy books all my life. Let me read yours."

He saw the temptation work on the old man — the pride of it, the chance to display the village's sacred possession to someone who might actually understand its letters. Most of Hollowmere, Marguerite guessed, could not read at all. Their religion lived in chalk spirals and memorized song.

"Wait here," Bramwen said.

When he had gone, the Wolf leaned close. "Whatever you're planning—"

"I'm planning to keep us off those chains."

"With *what*?"

"Give me the book and ten minutes," Marguerite said, "and pray to whatever you pray to that my hand hasn't gotten rusty."

"I don't pray."

"Then start. It couldn't hurt."

* * *

The book, when Bramwen returned with it wrapped in oilcloth and handled like a sleeping child, was a genuine antique — four hundred years old at least, its pages soft as cloth, its ink brown with age. Marguerite opened it with a scholar's reverence and let out a slow breath of relief.

It was incomplete. Badly so. Whole quires were missing from the middle, cut or torn away, and the surviving text jumped from the Third Veil to the Seventh without warning. The villagers had been filling the gaps with oral tradition and guesswork for generations.

He could work with this.

"Read to us," Bramwen said, "from the Ninth Veil. The part about the weighing."

Marguerite bent over the page and read aloud, translating the archaic script smoothly into plain speech: *In the hour when heaven holds its breath, let no soul offer itself unbidden, for the Silent One does not accept the coerced gift, and a false offering calls down the Unveiling.*

None of which was actually on the page. The page discussed agricultural tithes.

The villagers listened with their mouths slightly open. Marguerite turned a leaf with great care, buying time, scanning the genuine text beneath his finger like a musician sight-reading while improvising the melody.

*Let the community weigh itself,* he continued, inventing freely, grounding each invention in a phrase or cadence he'd genuinely lifted from the visible text, *for the god's silence is a mirror, and in a mirror only truth may stand. If any among you harbor doubt, let him speak it now, for doubt concealed is the only sin the Silent One punishes, and doubt confessed is the beginning of the Eighth Veil, which is the veil of mercy.*

"MERCY," he said, and let the word ring. "Elder Bramwen, does your litany teach mercy?"

"It teaches—" Bramwen faltered. "The old words are hard. We keep what we can."

"Then hear the Ninth Veil entire." Marguerite rose, holding the book open on his palms like an offering plate, and gave them the performance of his life. Twenty years of copying scripture had taught him how sacred language moved — where it slowed, where it climbed, where it paused to let awe land. He built the forgery the way he'd built forged ward-charters: on a foundation of real technique, every lie load-bearing, every invented verse stitched seamlessly to a genuine fragment. *When strangers come in the Hour of Silence, receive them, feed them, and let them go, for the god sends travelers as tests, and the village that spills innocent blood fails the weighing, and the Unveiling comes for it first.*

Silence. Forty faces, candlelit, searching his own.

One of the villagers — a young woman with a baby on her hip — spoke first. "My grandmother always said the travelers were a test. She said the chains were for the *unworthy*."

"The chains were always for the unworthy," Marguerite said gravely, "never for guests under your roof. Check the Seventh Veil. It's there." It was not there. The Seventh Veil was missing entirely, which was precisely why he'd cited it.

Bramwen stared at the book in Marguerite's hands for a long, long moment. Then he said, quietly, "Show me."

Marguerite's blood went cold. But his hands did not shake — twenty years of forging under the eyes of suspicious elders had burned the shaking out of them — and he turned the pages with a scholar's unhurried confidence, angling the book so the gutter-shadow fell across the exact line where a genuine marginal note about hospitality ended and his own invention began, and read aloud in the archaic dialect, translating as he went, and the note *was* about hospitality, sort of, if you squinted, and Bramwen listened, and nodded, and wept a little, and said:

"Forgive us. We nearly failed the weighing."

* * *

They were given the elder's own bed, which Marguerite suspected was the warmest room in Hollowmere, and a breakfast of eggs that tasted like a miracle. Only when the door had closed behind them did Marguerite allow himself to sit down on the edge of the bed and discover that his knees were not entirely reliable.

"You lied to them," the Wolf said.

"I *saved* them."

"You lied to them with a holy book in your hands. You took their faith and rewrote it in front of them and they thanked you for it." The Wolf stood by the window, silhouetted against the starless dark, and his voice had an edge Marguerite hadn't heard since the evacuation. "Do you know what I did, in the war? I wrote things into the world that weren't true and made them true. Covenants. Edicts. Names rewritten. And every time, I told myself it was mercy, or necessity, or the lesser evil. And the world I'm walking through now — the winter, the wights, the waking dead — is what my mercies grew into."

"This is different."

"How?"

Marguerite opened his mouth and found, to his discomfort, that he didn't have an answer ready. He sat with it. Outside, faintly, the village was singing vespers again, thanking their silent god for the travelers he had sent and spared.

"They were going to kill us," he said finally. "Or me, probably. You'd have fought, and you'd have killed some of them, and they're farmers, they're frightened people with one old gun between them — I saw it over the door. Someone was going to die for a misreading of a broken book. So yes. I lied. I lied the way I've lied my whole life, because lying is the one craft they gave me that works outside the wards." He looked up. "Would you rather I'd let you do it your way?"

The Wolf was silent a long time. "My way leaves bodies," he admitted at last. "Yours leaves something else. I honestly don't know which is worse." He turned from the window. "You enjoyed it."

"I—"

"Don't. I watched your face. You enjoyed it. The power of it. Forty people hanging on your invented scripture." His silver eyes caught the candlelight. "I'm not condemning you. I'm warning you. Because I know what that feeling costs, and I know how it compounds, and one day you'll rewrite something that shouldn't be rewritten and believe, absolutely believe, that it's mercy."

Marguerite wanted to argue. What stopped him was the memory of his own heartbeat during the reading — the fierce, singing joy of it, the sense that for ten minutes he had been the most powerful man in the room, in the village, possibly in the world. He had felt that joy once before, he realized: the night he forged his mother's medicine charter, ink still wet, heart hammering, certain that his lie was better than the truth it replaced.

"Then teach me the difference," he said quietly. "You've had centuries to learn it. Teach me where the line is."

"There isn't a line," the Wolf said. "There's just you, afterward, deciding whether you can live with what you wrote." He picked up his pack. "We leave before dawn. While they still think we're blessed."

* * *

But dawn did not come.

Marguerite woke in the grey hour to a silence wronger than the night's, and lay listening, and understood: the birds had stopped. All of them, everywhere around the village, at once. He rose, crossed to the window, and looked out at a sky that had begun, very faintly, very terribly, to *move* — not clouds, nothing so natural as clouds, but a slow churning in the black overhead, like ink stirred in water, like something vast turning over in its sleep.

Down in the lane, Elder Bramwen stood alone in the frost, looking up, his breath steaming, his face transfigured with terror and rapture in equal measure.

"He's answering," the old man whispered, loud enough to carry. "After all these years — the Silent One is *answering*."

And far to the east, on the road they would have to take, the darkness gathered itself and began, deliberately, to descend.
