## 9. Hunters of the Covenant

The road north of the pass was called the Pilgrim's Spine, though no pilgrim had walked it in living memory. It ran along a ridge of black stone where nothing grew — not because the ground was barren, Marguerite decided, but because the ground had been *asked* something once, and had never recovered from answering.

He felt it through his boots. That was the thing he still could not get used to: feeling. In Ashenmere, magic was ink on vellum, an abstraction, a trade. Out here it was weather. It pressed against his skin like damp wool, pooled in the hollows of the ridge, and threaded through the dead wards in long silver filaments that only he seemed able to see now — a side effect, the Wolf said, of three weeks of lessons written directly onto his skin in ever-ink.

"You're staring at the ground again," the Wolf said.

"I'm *reading* the ground."

"Read faster. We're exposed on this ridge like a signature at the bottom of a confession."

Marguerite glanced sideways at him. The soldier's body moved with its borrowed, uneven gait — left leg stiff, right hand always half-curled as if around a sword it no longer owned — but the eyes above it were ancient and silver-rimmed and currently fixed on the northern sky, where the stars should have been and were not. Three weeks of travel had taught Marguerite to read those eyes the way he read script: the tightening at the corners meant calculation; the slow blink meant exhaustion; the flat, unblinking stare meant the Wolf was remembering something he did not wish to remember.

This was the third kind of stare.

"What is this place?" Marguerite asked. "Really. Not the pilgrim story."

The Wolf was quiet for so long that Marguerite thought he would not answer at all. Then: "A battlefield. One of the last. Before the covenant." He crouched, laid two fingers against the black stone, and lifted them away dusted grey. "They didn't bury the dead here. They couldn't. There were too many, and the ground wouldn't take them. So they asked the stone to hold the shape of what happened." A pause. "And it does. It always will. That's what asking costs."

Marguerite looked down at the ridge with new eyes. He had been reading it wrong — not wards, not wards at all. A record. Miles of it. Every crack in the rock a line of text, every fissure a paragraph, and beneath their feet, all the way down into the mountain's cold marrow, a book the size of a country.

"And you were here," he said. It wasn't a question. He had learned to hear the past tense in the Wolf's silences.

"I was here."

"Which side?"

The Wolf stood. His joints cracked audibly in the frozen air — the soldier's body was failing faster now, Marguerite had noticed, each dawn a little stiffer, each healing charm a little more expensive. "Does it matter?"

"It matters to me."

"Then ask me again when I can answer without lying." The Wolf turned north. "We make the ruined chapel by dark or we don't make it at all. Move."

* * *

*They are close.*

The Wolf smelled them before he saw them — not with the soldier's blunted nose, which could barely find a fire in a snowstorm, but with the older senses, the ones that lived in whatever part of him the soldier's flesh had failed to smother. Cold iron. Consecrated ash. And underneath both, faint as a watermark, the particular resonance of star-metal worked by hands that hated what they worked for.

*Order of the Unbroken Star.* Of course. He had known since the wards fell that they would come. You did not shatter a thousand miles of border-warding without leaving a signature, and the Order had never learned subtlety. They believed concealment was a sin. They announced themselves in fire.

He said nothing to Marguerite. There was no point yet. The boy — man, he corrected himself, though *boy* kept slipping out — was hunched against the wind, one hand pressed flat to the ridge-stone as they walked, unconsciously reading it, unconsciously *learning* it, and the Wolf found the sight both gratifying and unbearable. Three weeks. Three weeks and the scribe already wrote ward-script in his sleep, murmured binding-cantos under his breath while gathering firewood, had twice corrected the Wolf's own penmanship without realizing whose hand had set the standard he was correcting.

*You chose well,* whispered the old hunger in him. *You chose a vessel for the name.*

He silenced it. He was getting better at silencing it. He had had centuries of practice, after all, and only weeks of forgetting how.

The chapel appeared below the ridge just as the light died: a shell of pale stone, roofless, its eastern wall collapsed into a drift of rubble and snow. Once it had been a waystation for covenant-pilgrims walking to the capital. Now its altar was a slab of frost-slick granite and its congregation was the wind.

"We'll shelter here," the Wolf said. "One night. We move before first light."

Marguerite dropped his pack with a groan that was mostly theater and partly genuine, and began unpacking the flint. The Wolf watched him do it — watched the ink-stained fingers, the careful economy of motion, the way the young man's lips moved silently over some fragment of canto as he worked — and felt the strange, unwelcome warmth that had been growing in him despite every effort of sense and self-interest.

It was exactly then that the starlight came down.

Not real starlight. There were no stars. But the night above the chapel split open with a sound like a struck bell, and light poured through the wound — white, hard, absolute — and in that light stood twelve figures on the chapel's broken walls, robed in grey, faces masked in polished steel that reflected nothing.

"Wolf of the Western Wars." The voice came from the tallest figure, and it was a woman's voice, aged and iron. "In the name of the Unbroken Star, you are called to judgment."

The Wolf did not reach for power. There was little enough to reach for, here in the wardless cold, and less time. Instead he stepped in front of Marguerite, which was instinct, which was stupid, which he did anyway.

"Mother Cassiela," he said. "Still wearing your father's mask, I see. Still hiding behind his face."

"The face is the Order's. As the war was the Order's. As the *covenant* was the Order's—" She descended from the wall, boots crunching frost, her sisters fanning out behind her in a silent crescent. "You wrote it, exile. You and eleven others. And now the wards you helped bind are falling, and the dark you helped imprison is walking, and we have come to correct the oldest error in our books."

"By killing me."

"By killing you *before* you reach the stone." Her masked head tilted. "Did you think we wouldn't know? Did you think the road to the capital wasn't watched? We broke the border-wards ourselves, Wolf. We opened the valley. We let the winter in — because the winter follows *you*, and we needed you to run somewhere we could see."

Behind him, Marguerite had gone very still. The Wolf heard the flint stop striking. Heard the young man's breath change — the quick shallow rhythm of fear being converted, in that particular furnace of a mind, into analysis.

"You used Ashenmere as bait," the Wolf said.

"We used Ashenmere as a *net*. The godless valley, the wardless dark, the one place your power gutters to nothing. Where else would a wounded animal run?" Cassiela spread her arms, and in each gloved hand a blade of star-metal woke with light. "You are the last author of the covenant still living. When you die, the text dies with you. The dark will have no key, no reader, no renewal. Whatever comes next will be final, and final things cannot be betrayed."

"That isn't correction," the Wolf said quietly. "That's surrender with a liturgy."

"It's *faith*." For the first time, something cracked in the iron voice — grief, maybe, or its fossil. "You of all of them should understand faith, Wolf. You knelt where we kneel. You swore where we swore. Eleven authors, and ten of them died true to their oath, and you alone walked away with the whole text folded inside your skull—"

"Because someone had to carry the *price*," the Wolf snarled, and the word tore out of him with more force than he intended, more force than the soldier's throat should have held, and every sister on the wall went rigid.

Silence. Wind through the roofless chapel. Snow beginning to fall through the wound of light overhead, catching fire as it crossed the beams.

Cassiela's voice, when it came, was almost gentle. "Say that again."

The Wolf said nothing.

"Say it *again*." She took a step forward. "The price. What price? The covenant was freely given. The authors' oath. The binding of names. There was no price beyond service—"

"There was always a price." The Wolf's voice had gone flat and cold, the voice of the archive rather than the man. "There is always a price. Nothing holds back an army of the unquiet dead for nine hundred years out of *good intentions*, Cassiela. Your founders knew. Your founders *wrote it*. And then they buried the clause so deep in the cantos that no one has read it in nine centuries, and built a church on top of the grave, and called the silence holy."

"You're lying."

"I'm the only one left who *can't* afford to lie about it." He laughed, and there was no humor in it, only rust. "Ask yourselves why the covenant must be renewed at the stone. Ask why it needs a crafted name. Ask why I — the last author, the traitor, the exile — am the only threat your order has mobilized armies against in four hundred years. You think it's because I might *loosen* the bonds?" He shook his head slowly. "I'm the only one alive who knows what feeding them costs. And you need me to die before I can tell anyone what your church was built on."

Marguerite made a small sound behind him. The Wolf did not turn. He could picture the expression precisely — the scribe's face when a text betrays him, the fury and the vertigo together. He had seen it in the archives at the capital, centuries ago, on younger faces. On his own.

"Cassiela," he said, more softly. "Go home. Take your sisters home. Break nothing else. Because if you kill me on this road, the covenant fails *this midwinter*, and everything your order has ever protected dies screaming, and the last thing any of you will have accomplished is making sure no one was left alive who might have saved it."

For a moment — one breath, two — he thought it had reached her. The masked head bowed slightly. The blades dimmed.

Then she said: "The Order does not negotiate with heresy," and the light came down like a guillotine.

* * *

Later — much later, hours later, bleeding and gasping in the lee of the chapel's fallen wall — Marguerite would try to reconstruct the fight and fail. Memory refused to hold it in sequence. There was light, and there was cold, and there was the Wolf moving in ways the soldier's ruined body should not have permitted, spending years of that body's life in single desperate seconds, and there was Marguerite himself flat against the granite with his palms pressed to the stone and *words* pouring out of him that he had never learned, ward-script rising off the chapel floor like frost-flowers, because the chapel itself was a text and texts, he understood suddenly and completely, could be *argued with*.

He remembered Cassiela's blade coming down. He remembered the Wolf taking it across the shoulder rather than let it reach him. He remembered shouting something — a canto, half-invented, half-remembered, forged the way he forged everything, stitched from stolen authority — and the chapel floor accepting the forgery as genuine, because the floor was old and tired and wanted, perhaps, to believe.

And he remembered the moment the sisters withdrew: not defeated, but satisfied, Cassiela's steel mask turning toward him for one long instant before the light swallowed them, and her voice arriving not through the air but through the stone under his hands, written there in letters of burning frost:

*The last author dies either way, scribe. The only question is whether he dies knowing what he owes. Read your master's covenant before you love him further. Read what it cost the first time. Then ask him why he needs you.*

Then they were gone, and the wound in the sky closed, and ordinary darkness returned, and the snow fell like snow instead of like fire.

Marguerite lay with his cheek against the freezing granite and his heart slamming, and beside him the Wolf was propped against the altar slab, one hand pressed to his shoulder where the star-metal had opened the soldier's body nearly to the bone. Silver light leaked between his fingers. Not blood-light. Something older.

"You're hurt," Marguerite said. Idiotic. Obvious. His voice shook.

"I'm *spending*," the Wolf said. "Different thing." He tried to rise, failed, and laughed — a thin, cracked sound. "Well. Scribe. You've had your first lesson in institutional history. How does it feel, learning that the church is a locked box?"

Marguerite didn't answer. He was looking at the altar slab. At the frost-writing Cassiela had left there, already fading, but not fast enough — he had read it twice, three times, the way he read everything, compulsively, completely.

*Read what it cost the first time. Then ask him why he needs you.*

"Why *do* you need me?" he asked.

The Wolf went very still. In the darkness, his silver-rimmed eyes found Marguerite's, and for the first time since the temple steps — since *scribe*, whispered like a prayer or a sentence — Marguerite saw something in them he had no craft to read.

"Not tonight," the Wolf said.

"No," Marguerite agreed, and sat up, and began, with steady hands, to bind the wound. "But soon. Because I'm done being the last person in every conversation who doesn't know what the words cost."

Far to the north, past the pass, past the petrified armies, the ruined capital waited with its buried stone. And somewhere between here and there, the Wolf knew — had always known — stood the watchtower where he had scratched his own name into the wall four hundred years ago, in a moment of weakness he had never forgiven.

They would reach it within the week. He had hoped, absurdly, to arrive first and erase it.

The hope died quietly, there in the roofless chapel, while the scribe bound his wounds with hands that trembled only a little, and the snow fell on both of them like a page waiting to be written on.
