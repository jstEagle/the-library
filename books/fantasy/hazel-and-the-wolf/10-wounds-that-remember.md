## 10. Wounds That Remember

The watchtower stood where the Wolf remembered it standing, which was itself a small cruelty. Four hundred years, and the world had worn away roads and kingdoms and the names of dead kings, but it had not managed to unmake this one gray finger of stone pointing accusingly at a sky that no longer held stars.

He had hoped it would have fallen by now.

They came to it at dusk, or what passed for dusk in the walking winter — a dimming of the white air, a thickening of shadow that might once have been called evening if anything so gentle as evening still existed. The tower rose from a shoulder of frozen hillside, its upper third collapsed into itself, its door long since rotted to splinters that crunched under Marguerite's boots like old bones. Snow had drifted through every gap. Inside, the wind moved in slow circuits, polishing the walls, keeping nothing.

"Charming," Marguerite said, and then coughed, and the cough went on too long.

The Wolf did not answer. He was listening to his own body the way a man listens to a floor that has begun to creak in new places.

The soldier's body — Corvan, the name stitched inside the collar, though the man who had owned it was four days past gone now, kept animate only by the Wolf's will and a stubbornness neither of them would have claimed — had been failing since the pass. Failing faster. Each skirmish with the wights cost him; each healing spell cost more. He had stopped counting after the third, because counting required arithmetic, and arithmetic required admitting what each spell took: not blood, not flesh, but *years*. The body's allotted span, burned like oil in a lamp with a cracked glass. He could feel the wick shortening. He could feel, when he let himself feel anything, the particular hollowness of a vessel being spent from both ends.

"Fire first," he said instead. "Then I'll look at your hands. You split them on the ward-script last night."

"I didn't split them."

"You bled on my work."

"That's called annotation." But Marguerite was already gathering the broken door-planks, arranging them with a scribe's fussy precision, coaxing flame from the flint they'd taken off a dead militiaman two valleys back. His fingers were clumsy with cold. They were always clumsy with cold now, and he complained about it constantly, and the Wolf had discovered — with something adjacent to alarm — that he had begun to find the complaints restful. A litany of small grievances was, it turned out, a form of music. In four centuries he had heard empires sing their own praises into ash. This was better.

The fire caught. Light climbed the tower's inner wall, and the Wolf turned away from it before it reached the place he did not want lit.

Too late.

He saw Marguerite see it. Saw the scribe's gaze snag on the far wall, above the drift-line, where the plaster had peeled away in great pale sheets to expose the older stone beneath — and where, cut deep into that stone in a hand the centuries had not softened, letters waited in the firelight like a wound that had learned to read.

Marguerite went very still. Scribes, the Wolf had learned, went still the way other men drew weapons.

"That's—" Marguerite began, and stopped, because he knew what it was. Of course he knew. He had spent three weeks learning ward-script from a teacher who refused to write his own name anywhere, and a scribe does not need to be told what a true-name looks like when one is carved raw into a wall, deep enough to scar the stone, shallow enough to have been done quickly, in the dark, by someone whose hands were shaking.

*Whose hands were shaking.*

The Wolf sat down heavily on a fallen beam and found, to his distant irritation, that he could not make himself stand again.

"It's yours," Marguerite said quietly. It was not a question. "You wrote it."

"The wall wrote itself," the Wolf said. "I merely held the knife."

"When?"

He should lie. Lying was his native tongue; he had spoken it to councils and kings and to the thing wearing his exile like a second skin. But the fire was warm, and the body was dying, and the name on the wall was looking at him with all the patience of carved stone, and he was tired — tired in a way that had nothing to do with the soldier's failing heart.

"The night before the covenant," he said. "Four hundred years ago. There was a garrison here then. We held this road against the eastern host for eleven days, and on the twelfth we knew we would not hold it another, and I came up here alone because I wanted — " He stopped. Started again, in the flat voice he used for facts. "I wanted someone, somewhere, ever, to know who I had been. Before the covenant made me a footnote. Before they gave me a title instead of a name. War-mage. Exile. *Wolf.* Names for things people are afraid to look at directly."

Marguerite said nothing. He had moved closer to the wall without appearing to decide to, the way water moves downhill, and his fingertips hovered an inch from the carved letters without touching them. The gesture was so precisely reverent — the posture of a man before an unrecovered manuscript — that the Wolf felt something in his chest turn over and present its soft underside.

"You never erased it," Marguerite said.

"I tried. Twice. Once with magic, once with a hammer." He looked at the fire. "The stone keeps it. Some things do. It's why I needed a scribe and not a soldier. Magic can rewrite a covenant, but only craft can *read* one, and only craft remembers what magic wants forgotten."

"And you've carried it all this time." Marguerite's voice had gone strange — not pitying, which the Wolf would have endured badly, but something more careful than pity. "Your name. Scratched in a ruin in the middle of nowhere, waiting."

"Not waiting. *Kept.* There's a difference. Waiting implies expectation." He pulled the collar of the dead soldier's coat higher, though the cold did not touch him the way it touched living men; it touched him worse, through the body's thinning veil. "I came here tonight intending to burn it out. I told myself you'd never seen it. I told myself a great many things on this road, scribe. You may have noticed I'm fluent."

"I noticed," Marguerite said, and there was the ghost of the smile, the dry one, the one that undid four hundred years of discipline as thoroughly as any wight's claws. "You're a very bad liar for someone so practiced."

"My lies are excellent. You simply refuse to be lied *to*, which is a different skill entirely, and one I haven't found a counter for."

Marguerite laughed — a small sound, swallowed quickly by the wind — and knelt then, close, closer than he needed to be, and took the Wolf's left hand in both of his own. The scribe's fingers were ink-stained even now, even after weeks of road-dirt; some stains do not wash, and the Wolf suspected he understood that better than most. Marguerite turned the hand over, examining the split skin along the palm where the ward-work had bitten back, and began, without asking, to bind it with strips torn from his own shirt hem.

"You don't have to tell me the name," Marguerite said, working. "I'm not going to read it off the wall. That's yours. Whatever it costs, whoever it was written for — it's yours."

"It wasn't written for anyone."

"Everything is written for someone. Even the things we pretend are just for us." He tied off the knot, neat as a colophon. Then he looked up, and the firelight was in his eyes, and the Wolf thought: *ah. There it is. That's the thing I've been refusing to name since the chapel.* "Why did you carve it? Really. Not the version for councils."

The wind circled the tower. Somewhere below, snow slid from the ruined stair with a sound like a long exhale.

"Because I was afraid," the Wolf said. The truth came out of him the way an arrow comes out of a wound — fast, and leaving a hole shaped exactly like itself. "Because the next morning I was going to walk down into the capital and help write a covenant that would end the war, and I already knew — I knew even then, before they hid it from the others, before I agreed to keep the price secret — I knew what it would cost, and I knew I would pay it, and I wanted one mark in the world that said *I existed, and I was afraid, and I did it anyway.*" He flexed the bandaged hand. "A name is a small thing, scribe. It's the smallest thing there is. And it was the only thing I had that no council had ratified and no army had requisitioned and no god had claimed. So I put it in the stone. Where it couldn't be conscripted."

Marguerite was silent for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice was roughened at the edges.

"You know what I did with my name? For thirty years?" He nodded at the wall, at the carved letters, at everything they meant. "I signed it to a debt-bond. Every morning. Ink and vellum, over and over, selling pieces of it by the page. And you — you hid yours in a rock so it would stay free." A breath that was almost a laugh. "We're the same kind of stupid, Wolf. We just filed our names in different places."

Something broke open in the Wolf then — quietly, without drama, the way ice breaks on a river in spring: a crack, a shift, the whole surface rearranged around a warmth coming up from underneath. He did not say anything. There was nothing in his arsenal for this. Four hundred years of strategy, and no doctrine whatsoever for a scribe kneeling in front of him binding his wounds and calling them the same.

Instead he did the thing he had not done since the night he carved the wall. He reached out — slowly, giving Marguerite every chance to withdraw, the way one approaches a wild thing or a holy text — and set his bandaged hand against the side of the scribe's face. Cold fingers. Ink-stained. Alive.

Marguerite leaned into it. Just slightly. Just enough.

"You're burning up," Marguerite murmured, and the tenderness in it curdled instantly into something sharper, and his eyes went clinical, and the moment folded itself away like a letter returned unsent. "That's not fever from the cold. That's the spells. How much have you spent on this body? Since the pass. Tell me the truth."

"The truth is a currency I'm currently hoarding."

"*Wolf.*"

"Three major workings," he said, because it was Marguerite, and because the wall had already taken his dignity and might as well take the rest. "And a dozen small ones. The body had perhaps five years left when I found it. It has perhaps five months now. Fewer, if we're attacked again." He said it lightly, the way one reads a dispatch about a distant defeat. "It changes nothing. The stone is nine days' walk. I need the body alive nine days. After that—"

"After that, what?"

"After that," the Wolf said, and smiled the smile he used for councils, "the accounting resolves itself."

Marguerite stared at him. And the Wolf watched — with the peculiar horror of a man watching a lock he believed secure begin, very slowly, to turn — as understanding moved behind the scribe's eyes. Not all of it. Not yet. But a piece of it, a corner of the truth lifting like the plaster on the wall, showing older stone beneath.

"You're spending it," Marguerite said softly. "All of it. You've been spending yourself the whole way here, and you never intended to—" He stopped. His jaw worked. "What happens at the stone, Wolf? What's the rite actually *cost*?"

Outside, the wind changed direction. And beneath the wind, faint and wrong, from the north — from the direction of the pass, the petrified armies, the frozen field where history had been nailed shut — came a sound neither of them had heard before.

Not a howl. Not a wind.

A knocking. Slow, patient, rhythmic, like fists on the far side of a door. Like something that had waited four hundred years in the ice beginning, at last, to test whether the ice would hold.

The Wolf closed his eyes.

"They're waking," he said. "We leave before dawn."
