## 11. The Debt Collector

They had made it as far as the ferry crossing at Coldbrook when Marguerite saw the riders.

He knew them before he could name them, the way a scribe knows a hand before he reads a word. Three figures on grey horses, moving at that unhurried pace the temple enforcers used precisely because they never needed to hurry. Their cloaks were the undyed wool of Ashenmere's scriptorium, their hoods thrown back to show the tonsured scalps and the iron collars of office. At the lead rider's saddlebow hung a ledger in an oilcloth case, and Marguerite felt his stomach drop through the frozen ground, because he had bound ledgers like that one. He had sewn those very clasps, probably. He had pressed the temple's seal into the wax himself, a hundred times, with hands that shook only when no one was watching.

"Marguerite." The Wolf stood at the water's edge, wrapped in the dead soldier's greatcoat, watching the ferryman pole his barge across the half-frozen river. "We cross in ten minutes. The ice will hold until midday, no longer."

"They found us."

"I know." The Wolf did not turn. His eyes — silver when no one watched, and someone was always watching now — tracked the riders with the flat patience of a thing that had outlived every kind of hunter. "Three of them. They've been behind us since the village. I let them close because I wanted to see what they wanted."

"And what do they want?"

The Wolf smiled without warmth. "What temples always want. What is owed."

* * *

There was no shelter at the crossing, only the ferryman's hut with its smoke-blackened eaves and a fire pit outside where the old man roasted chestnuts he sold to travelers too frozen to haggle. It was there, beside that poor little fire, that the enforcers came upon them — and Marguerite understood, watching Brother Aldous dismount, that this had been arranged down to the smallest detail. The temple did not ambush. The temple *arrived*.

Brother Aldous had been the master of the binding-house when Marguerite was first inked. He was not cruel; that was the worst of him. He had held Marguerite's wrist steady for the debt-tattoo at nine years old, murmuring the calming liturgy, and had said afterward, kindly: *It doesn't hurt if you don't fight it.* He looked older now, frost in his beard, but his voice was the same voice that had read out the terms of a child's servitude as gently as a bedtime story.

"Marguerite Thornbury." He said the full name deliberately, and Marguerite felt it land on him like a hand on the back of his neck. A true-name craftsman knows what a name weighs. Aldous knew it too. That was why they sent him. "You are in possession of temple property beyond the bounds of your charter. You are instructed to return—"

"He's not property." The words came out of Marguerite before he had decided to say them. He heard his own voice crack on the second word and hated it.

Aldous turned to him with genuine sorrow. "I wasn't speaking of you, son."

Beside the fire, the soldier's body shifted. The Wolf rose slowly, and even wearing a dying man's flesh he made the space around the firepit feel smaller. "Say what you came to say," he said. "Quickly. My patience has been dead longer than you have been alive."

"You are the property," Aldous said simply. "Registered under writ of seizure, sealed by the elders of Ashenmere the night you were taken from our steps. An unregistered practitioner found within temple grounds becomes temple chattel under the Third Charter. You know this law. You may have helped write older laws than ours, but this one is ours." He gestured, and the second enforcer opened the oilcloth case and lifted out the ledger. "The scribe who witnessed the seizure attests to the mark. All that remains is the transfer of custody."

"And if we refuse?" Marguerite asked.

Aldous looked at him then, and for a moment the kindness fell away and Marguerite saw the accountant underneath, the man who had spent thirty years balancing souls against debts and had never once lost sleep over the arithmetic. "Then the temple invokes the bond of surety. Every debt you carry, Marguerite — your mother's medicines, your apprenticeship, your lodging, the interest compounded across eleven years — reverts to joint liability. Anyone who aids you inherits it. Anyone who shelters you. Anyone who travels with you." He paused, delicately. "Anyone who *frees* you."

The fire popped. Somewhere across the river, ice groaned.

"The Wolf can pay it," Marguerite said. "He's a war-mage. He could buy your whole valley twice over."

"With what coin?" Aldous asked, almost gently. "Magic doesn't cross into Ashenmere, boy. You taught me that, or rather I taught you. Whatever he is beyond those mountains, in the valley he is only a sick man in a stolen coat. And sick men cannot discharge debts." He opened the ledger to a marked page and turned it so the firelight caught the columns of figures. Marguerite read them the way he read everything — instantly, helplessly, completely. Eleven years of compound interest rendered in the temple's beautiful, merciless book-hand. The sum was not large. That was the obscenity of it. It was such a small number for a whole life.

* * *

Later — much later, when the horses were gone and the ferryman had retreated wisely into his hut — Marguerite sat on the riverbank with the pen still warm in his fingers and tried to understand what he had done.

Because he had done something. That was the thing that kept circling back. While the Wolf had stood rigid at the edge of the firelight, calculating escape routes and kill orders with equal coldness, Marguerite had stepped forward and said, "There's another way," and the words had come out steady, and everyone had known — Aldous, the Wolf, Marguerite himself — that they would be true.

"A supplemental instrument," Marguerite had said. "I'll draft it myself. Witnessed by your own clerk." He had taken the ledger from Aldous's hands — taken it, the way he'd once dreamed of taking things all his life — and turned to a fresh page. "The debt transfers to me. Joint liability converted to sole liability, secured against my person, term extended—" and here his voice had not shaken at all, and that frightened him more than anything else that day, "—indefinitely. Lifetime service, renewed at the temple's discretion. In exchange, the temple releases all claim on the traveler and withdraws the writ of seizure."

"That is not a bargain, that is a surrender," the Wolf had said quietly.

"It's a contract," Marguerite had said. "I know the difference. I've written both."

And Aldous, damn him, had watched with something like pride as Marguerite drafted the instrument in the snow-cold, his breath fogging the page, using the formal covenant-hand that only three living scribes could write correctly. Because that was the trap inside the mercy: the document had to be *true*. Not truthful — valid. A forged signature would void it, and the temple's clerks would find the flaw within the hour, because Marguerite had trained two of them himself. So he had written his own name — his real name, the one his mother gave him, the one he had guarded his whole life the way other men guard coins — and pressed his thumb beneath it, and spoken the witnessing formula aloud while the junior enforcer scratched her own mark beside his.

The ink had barely dried when the Wolf moved. Marguerite never saw the whole of it — just a blur of the dead soldier's body moving faster than a dying man should, and the sound of Aldous's sword clearing its sheath, and then the Wolf standing between Marguerite and the enforcers with his hand raised and nothing in it, which was somehow worse than a weapon.

"No," the Wolf had said. One word. It had come out of him like something torn.

"The document is executed," Aldous had said, backing toward his horse with his eyes on the Wolf's raised hand. "Sealed and witnessed. The temple honors its instruments, mage. Even the ones signed against our interest." And there had been something in his face — regret, or its accounting equivalent — as he gathered his reins. "You should have let him fight it, Marguerite. It doesn't hurt if you don't fight it."

Then the horses, and the riders, and the ledger going home to Ashenmere with Marguerite's life written in it more thoroughly than ever before.

* * *

Now the riverbank was empty except for the two of them and the dying fire, and the Wolf had not spoken since the hoofbeats faded.

Marguerite fed a stick to the coals and watched it catch. Across the river, the far bank waited, and beyond it the road to the capital, and beyond that the covenant-stone and whatever price it meant to take from them. He flexed his right thumb. The ink was already fading from the skin — witness-ink, temporary, the mark itself living now in the ledger and in the law — but he could still feel it, the way you feel a ring after taking it off.

"Say it," he said finally. "Whatever you're composing. Say it now and be done."

"You had no right." The Wolf's voice was low and absolutely level, which Marguerite had learned was how he sounded when he was closest to losing control. "I have watched empires fall, scribe. I have burned cities whose names are forgotten by the ash that replaced them. I have stood in councils where kings wept and I felt nothing. And I have not—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "Eleven years. You have given them eleven more years, indefinitely renewable, for a man you have known a matter of weeks. A man who was going to die anyway. Who *intended* to die anyway. Do you understand what you've done? You have purchased a few months of my company at the price of your entire life."

"Yes."

"That is not an answer."

"It's the only one I have." Marguerite turned to face him. The firelight caught the silver in the Wolf's eyes and held it there, and Marguerite made himself look at it, made himself not flinch. "You want me to tell you I did it for the mission. For the covenant. For the world. Fine — the world needs you at that stone, and the temple would have dragged you back to Ashenmere and worked you to death copying wards you couldn't cast. That's reason enough. But it isn't the truth, and I'm a scribe, and I don't write falsehoods in executed instruments." He took a breath. "I did it because it was you. Because when Aldous said they'd take you, something in me went quiet and certain, the way it goes when I'm writing something true. I didn't decide it. I recognized it."

The silence stretched long enough that the fire burned down a notch.

"You love me," the Wolf said at last, and it was not a question, and it was not tender. It was accusatory, the way a man might say *you're bleeding*.

"Don't make it small," Marguerite said. "Don't make it a wound I inflicted on you. I know what I am. I'm a debtor. I've been owned since I was nine years old, and I learned to survive it by making the ownership useful — by being so good at serving that they left me alone with the ink. And yes, maybe I did the same thing just now. Maybe I bought my way deeper in because it's the only move I've ever known how to make." He laughed, short and joyless. "But hear this much, at least. When Aldous held out that ledger, I had a choice. First one I've ever had with teeth in it. Run, and let them take you. Or sign, and stay free enough to keep walking beside you. I chose. Me. Nobody bound me, nobody tricked me, nobody forgave a debt to make it sweeter. For the first time in my life, the chain is one I picked up myself." He looked down at his thumb. "That's worth something. I don't care if you see it. *I* see it."

The Wolf said nothing for a long time. When he finally spoke, his voice had changed — stripped of the fury, down to something rawer underneath.

"Do you know why I need a scribe? Truly?" He crouched by the fire, and for once the borrowed body looked exactly what it was: a dying man's frame, worn like a coat past its season. "The covenant-stone will not accept power. It has seen power; power is what broke it. It accepts *name*. Crafted name. Willing name. I told you I needed your craft, and that was true, but I let you believe it was your skill with wards, your forgery, your cleverness. It isn't. It's this—" he gestured at Marguerite's hands, "—this willingness of yours to put your name on things. To stand behind what you write. No one else I have ever known does it without flinching." He looked away, toward the darkening river. "I chose you in the temple because you were skilled. I keep choosing you because of this. And now you've chained yourself to a corpse walking toward its own execution, and I am furious, Marguerite, because I cannot decide whether it is the most foolish thing I have ever witnessed or the finest."

"Both," Marguerite said. "I'm a scribe. I contain multitudes of bad decisions, beautifully executed."

Something happened to the Wolf's mouth then — brief, unwilling, gone in a heartbeat. On anyone else it would have been a smile.

"The ferry," the Wolf said, rising. "The ice won't wait, and neither will the things that hunt the dark. We cross now."

Marguerite got up, stamped feeling back into his feet, and shouldered his bag. At the water's edge he paused. The far bank was invisible already, swallowed in the early winter dusk, and somewhere out in that darkness the starless wights were waiting, patient as creditors.

"Wolf," he said. "When this is over — if there's any 'over' — the temple releases me. The covenant supersedes local bonds; you said so yourself, back in Ashenmere. Renewal of the covenant dissolves all lesser instruments. Including mine."

The Wolf went still.

"So you see," Marguerite said softly, stepping onto the ice, "you're going to have to live. You've become my debt now. And I collect."

Ahead of them, out on the black river, something that was not wind began to move against the current — and the ice sang under their feet like a struck wire.
