## 13. The City Under Snow

They came down out of the pass on the ninth day, and Marguerite's first sight of the old capital was a chimney.

A single stone chimney standing alone in the snowfield, smokeless, its top sheared off at an angle as though some enormous hand had pinched it. Then another. Then a whole ridge of them, dozens, hundreds, poking up through the white like reeds through ice — and beneath the reeds, beneath six feet of packed winter and nine centuries of forgetting, the city.

*Vael Correnth*, the histories called it. The City of Bells. Marguerite had copied its name into charter after charter, ward after ward, always in the formulaic past tense reserved for things destroyed: *as it was written when Vael Correnth stood.* He had never once imagined it still standing. Buried, yes — drowned in its own snow like a face under a sheet — but standing. He could feel it under his boots, a vast submerged architecture of vaults and colonnades, and he could feel something else too: warmth. Not the sun's warmth; there was no sun to speak of, only that bruised pewter sky. A human warmth, candle-warmth, hearth-warmth, rising up through the snowpack like breath through a blanket.

"People live here," he said. His voice came out cracked from days of wind.

"Underneath," the Wolf said.

The soldier's body was worse. Marguerite had stopped pretending not to notice somewhere around the fifth day, when the Wolf had begun leaning on his staff — a plain ash stave he'd cut himself, because whatever sorcery had once lived in his hands now needed wood and leverage like any beggar's crutch. The host-body moved with a lurching economy, spending each step like a miser spends coin. Its left hand had gone gray at the fingertips. When they camped, the Wolf sat with his back against whatever stone was available and did not move for hours, and his breathing had developed a wet rattle at the bottom of it, like water finding a low place.

"You knew they were here," Marguerite said. "The survivors."

"I knew someone was here." The Wolf's silver eyes tracked along the buried ridgeline, reading it. "Candle-smoke means organization. Organization means government. Government means—" He paused, and something crossed the ruined soldier's face that might have been distaste. "—committees."

"You're afraid of committees?"

"I'm afraid of what mages become when they stop fighting wars." He said it lightly, but Marguerite had learned to hear the floor under the lightness. "Come on. There'll be a gate. There's always a gate."

There was. It took them two hours of walking the snow-buried ramparts to find it — a tower door set below the drift line, iron-banded, marked with a sigil Marguerite recognized from a hundred copied charters: the Unbroken Star, its rays rendered not as the Order's harsh eight-pointed wheel but in the older style, soft-rayed, almost gentle, like a child's drawing of the sun. Someone had scratched it out. Someone else had scratched it back in. And someone else again had scratched it out so thoroughly that the stone around it was gouged to powder.

Beneath the defaced sigil, a rope hung down through a hole in the snow, ending in a bell.

The Wolf looked at the bell for a long moment.

"You could knock," Marguerite said.

"They'll have warded the door."

"You could unward the door."

"With what?" The Wolf flexed the gray-tipped hand, and even that small motion cost him; Marguerite saw the tremor run up the soldier's arm. "I'm running on fumes and a borrowed corpse, scribe. Whatever's behind this door, I'd rather meet it invited than bleeding." He took the rope and rang.

The sound was enormous. It went down through the snow and into the stone and kept going, bell answering bell answering bell, a whole buried city waking tier by tier beneath their feet, until the air itself seemed to shiver with bronze. Marguerite pressed his palm flat against the tower wall and felt the ringing in his teeth, in the roots of his hair, in the scarred wards of his own skin where the Wolf's lessons had sunk in. Somewhere far below, doors opened. Voices rose. And then, directly behind the tower door, boots — many boots, unhurried, disciplined, the boots of people who had done this before and were not excited by it.

The door opened inward, and lamplight spilled up the snow like honey.

Marguerite counted eleven figures in the doorway and gallery above: men and women in layered wool and quilted silk gone gray with age, armed with pikes that had been maintained with obsessive care, faces lit from below in the manner of people who spend their lives looking down at candles instead of up at skies. They were all looking at the Wolf. Not at Marguerite. At the Wolf, with expressions Marguerite needed a long moment to parse, because they were not the expressions of strangers meeting a stranger.

They were the expressions of people meeting a portrait come to life.

"Well," said the woman at the front. She was old — genuinely old, older than anyone had a right to be in a world where the Wolf's borrowed body was failing, and her age sat on her like jewelry rather than burden. Her hair was white and cropped short; her hands, folded atop a lacquered cane, bore seven rings, none of them decorative. "Nine hundred years, and you ring our bell like a tradesman."

"You know me," the Wolf said.

"We know your *work*," the woman said. "The face is new. The face is always new with you." Her eyes flicked over the soldier's body with clinical, unsentimental appraisal — the gray fingers, the rattling breath, the way he leaned on the ash stave — and something in her expression softened by a degree that was somehow worse than cruelty. "You're dying faster than usual. Come inside, Wolf. Before the dark notices you're here."

"And my companion?"

The old woman looked at Marguerite then, properly, for the first time. Her gaze was a scribe's gaze; it went to his ink-stained fingers first, then to the ward-scar on his wrist where the Wolf had signed him into the road, then to his eyes, and stayed there a moment too long, the way one reads a difficult passage twice.

"A valley-man," she said. "Ashenmere, by the look of him — you can always tell, they flinch at open sky." She turned back toward the stairwell, already descending, conversation concluded. "Bring him too. Hospitality is cheap. We have more rooms than people."

Her name, Marguerite learned on the long stair down, was Councillor Vess. There were nine councillors. They ruled perhaps four thousand souls in the undercrofts of Vael Correnth, and they had ruled them, Vess mentioned in passing, since before her grandmother's grandmother, because mages who renew themselves do not die, and mages who do not die do not relinquish.

* * *

The city underneath was beautiful in the way of things that have made peace with their own burial.

Streets became avenues became halls, roofed over with vaulted stone, lit by a thousand candles and oil-lamps and slow-burning sigil-flames that gave light without heat. Water ran in channels down the centers of the streets, fed by taps that had been running, Vess said, since the founding. Market stalls lined the wider crossings, selling mushrooms grown in racks, goat cheese, lamp-oil, paper — real paper, Marguerite realized, staring, made from rag pulp in workshops he could smell from fifty yards. Children ran past them shrieking, actual children, three generations deep in a city that had never seen a star.

He should have found it grim. He found himself grieving that it wasn't. These people had built something warm and complete in the belly of a dead capital, and they had built it to last forever, and forever underground looked exactly like this: comfortable, candlelit, and closed.

"The sky's gone wrong everywhere," Vess said, walking beside him while the Wolf was escorted — *escorted*, Marguerite noted, with honor guards fore and aft — along the processional avenue. "We felt it weeks ago. The old wards here are deep; they drank the shock before it reached us. But the hum started shortly after. You've heard it, I assume."

"It knows his name," Marguerite said.

"Mm." Vess tapped her cane once on the stone, thoughtfully. "It would. It was made by his students, after all."

Marguerite stopped walking. "His *what?*"

Vess smiled at him with genuine, terrible kindness. "Oh, scribe. What did he tell you he was, when he picked you up?" She didn't wait for an answer. "Never mind. It doesn't matter what he told you. It matters what we can offer you. Both of you. Rest. Food. A healer for that poor body he's wearing — a real healer, not his patchwork. And in exchange—"

"There it is," the Wolf said. He had stopped ahead of them, turning, and the honor guards had stopped with him, smooth as water closing. "In exchange for what, Councillor. Say it plainly. You never used to bother with the winding-up."

Vess's smile didn't move. "In exchange for nothing at all. Yet. Guests first, terms later — that's the old courtesy, and I keep the old courtesies." She gestured down a side hall, toward arched doors from which warm air and the smell of bread flowed. "Rooms. Baths. A meal. Your man will be shown to the guest quarters as well, Wolf. Separately. Protocol."

Something passed between the Wolf and the councillor then, brief and cold, a fencing match conducted entirely in eyebrow and stillness. Marguerite caught only the end of it: the Wolf's jaw tightening, Vess's small shrug, the wordless arithmetic of two people calculating how much force the other would spend and on what.

Then the Wolf looked at Marguerite, and all the calculation dropped away, and what was left underneath was exhausted and honest and frightened, and he said, "Don't sign anything."

"I don't intend to."

"That's what everyone intends." The Wolf held his eyes a moment longer. "Don't sign anything, scribe. Not even a receipt."

* * *

The guest quarters were magnificent, and that was the trap of them.

Marguerite's room had a bed with clean linen, a writing desk, an inkwell filled and waiting, a window — a *window*, glazed, looking out onto a candlelit interior court because there was no other kind of view left in the world. A copper bath was drawn for him. A meal arrived: bread still warm, mushroom stew, salted fish, a cup of wine dark as garnets. After nineteen days of hard travel and harder snow, it was the finest thing he had ever seen, and he ate all of it, and hated himself a little for how good it was.

The bath was better. He soaked until his fingers pruned, watching the candlelight shiver on the water, and let himself think, for the first time since the temple steps, in complete sentences.

*Don't sign anything.*

But here was the thing about Vael Correnth, Marguerite thought, sinking lower in the water. Here was the thing he'd understood within an hour of arriving and couldn't stop turning over: nobody here had chained him. Nobody had posted a guard at his door — or rather, the guards outside his door were so courteous, so unobtrusive, so perfectly positioned between him and every stairwell, that calling them guards would be an insult to the profession. He was free to walk the undercrofts. Free to visit the scriptorium — they had a *scriptorium*, forty desks, and an archivist who had nearly wept to learn Marguerite's craft. Free to eat, bathe, read, rest.

Free to leave?

He asked the archivist, casually, over a display of pigments. The woman's face did something complicated. "Leave," she repeated, as if the word were in a dead language. "Well. The surface roads are closed this season. Wights in the passes, the council says. Very dangerous. In spring, perhaps. The council reviews travel petitions in spring."

"When is spring?"

She laughed, kindly, at the joke.

That night, Marguerite lay in the soft bed listening to the bells mark the hours, and did the arithmetic he'd been avoiding. Nineteen days on the road, and the Wolf had spent them teaching him ward-script by firelight, hiding the cost of every lesson, and Marguerite had pretended not to see the gray creeping up the soldier's wrist because seeing it would have meant deciding something, and he hadn't been ready. Now the decision had been made *for* him, gently, by warm bread and locked gates. The council would heal the Wolf's body — of course they would; Vess had looked at that dying flesh the way a jeweler looks at a flawed stone, assessing what could be cut away. And they would heal it slowly, and conditionally, and meanwhile midwinter was coming, and the hum under the snow was getting louder, and somewhere in the archives of this buried city was a covenant-stone with the Wolf's death already written into it.

And Marguerite was in a cage made of comfort, and the door was guarded by politeness, and every hour he spent soaking in warm water was an hour the dark gained.

He got up. He dressed. He took the desk's inkwell — full, black, excellent — and poured it into the bathwater, and stirred, and watched the water go cloudy gray, and felt briefly, absurdly guilty about the waste.

Then he opened the door and smiled at the courteous guard.

"I can't sleep," he said. "I thought I'd visit the scriptorium. The archivist said I could work anytime."

The guard hesitated — and that hesitation was everything, Marguerite thought. A prisoner gets refused. A guest gets *accommodated*. "Of course, master scribe. Shall I send someone with you?"

"No need." He was already walking. "I know the way."

He didn't, quite. But the undercroft corridors were signed in the old imperial script, which he read fluently, and the scriptorium lamps burned all night, and the archives were through the third arch beyond it, and the archives, he had noticed that very afternoon, were catalogued — meticulously, lovingly, by someone with a true-name craftsman's training — and among the catalogue headings, in the section no one had visited in years, he had seen one word that had gone through him like a hook:

*Covenant.*

The archive door was unlocked. Of course it was. Nothing in Vael Correnth was ever locked; that was the genius of the place, and the horror of it. Why lock a door when the roads are closed and the council reviews petitions in spring?

Marguerite lit a lamp, found the shelf, and began to pull the ledgers down.

Behind him, far above, faint through nine centuries of stone, the bells rang midnight — and under the bells, under the city, under everything, the hum answered, patient and vast, saying its one word over and over into the dark.

*Wolf. Wolf. Wolf.*

Marguerite opened the first ledger, and began to read.
