## 15. What Marguerite Read in the Dark

They had left him alone with the archives on purpose. Marguerite understood that now.

The council's archivist — a soft-voiced woman named Ilyen whose fingers were stained the same permanent gray as his own — had unlocked the iron-banded door at the end of the third undercroft and gestured him through with a courtesy that had felt, even then, like a hand between his shoulder blades. *Take whatever time you need, scribe. The covenant texts are delicate. Best you work without interruption.* And then the door had closed behind him with a sound like a held breath, and he had heard the lock turn from the outside.

A cage made of paper. They thought him tame because he was ink-stained. They thought that anything which loved books could be kept by them.

Marguerite stood in the dark for a long moment before he lit the lamp they'd left him. The archives of the old capital breathed around him: shelf after shelf descending into the cold, scrolls in lacquered cases, codices chained to their desks by customs older than the valley of Ashenmere itself. The air tasted of dust and tallow and something else beneath it — the faint mineral smell of old magic, the way a hearthstone smells hours after the fire dies. Here, where the wards of the world had once been written, even the silence was saturated.

He had come for one thing. He told himself it was duty: the Wolf — Vael, he let himself think the name now, only in the privacy of his skull — had asked him to help craft a name worthy of sacrifice, and a scribe did not approach such work blind. He would read the original covenant. He would understand its grammar, its architecture, its price. That was all.

That was what he told himself while his hands shook as he unrolled the first case.

* * *

The original text was not what he expected.

He had spent his life copying ward-charters, and ward-charters were legal instruments: clauses, exceptions, seals, the dry scaffolding of obligation. He had imagined the covenant in the same register — a treaty rendered in light, signed by kings and archmages, all its cruelty dressed in ceremony.

It was nothing of the kind. It was a poem.

Or rather, it had been a poem once, and then men had turned it into law, layering commentary over commentary until the original verses showed through like bones through skin. Marguerite read by lamplight in the freezing dark, translating the high imperial script line by line into the working shorthand of his trade, and slowly, horribly, he began to see the shape beneath the shape.

*Two lights were lit against the long dark,* ran the oldest verse, *and the dark could not tell them apart.*

*What is given freely cannot be taken. What is named cannot be unnamed. Let the stone hold the name that holds the night.*

He read it three times before the meaning settled into him like cold water into cloth. The covenant was not a wall. It was not a treaty. It was a *substitution* — an act of sympathetic magic on a scale no one had attempted since, in which two crafted names had been bound into the stone so that the darkness, reaching for the world, would find the names instead and be satisfied. Fed. Held. The frozen war below the pass, the soldiers locked mid-scream in ice and stone — they were not casualties of the covenant. They were its *collateral*. Held shapes. Names the stone had borrowed and never returned.

And the covenant required renewal because names wear out. Because everything wears out.

Marguerite set down his stylus and pressed his palms flat against the reading desk until the wood bit into his skin. He had known, in the way one knows a debt exists without counting it — Vael had told him there was a price, had said *it happens to me* with the terrible calm of a man reading his own death warrant aloud. But knowing a price and seeing it itemized were different things. Here, in the margin of the fourth verse, in a later hand, some archivist had written:

*Sacrifice must be willing. Sacrifice must bear a name crafted for the purpose. A name made in haste will fail; a name made in love will hold.*

*A name made in love will hold.*

Marguerite laughed — one short, ugly sound that the archive swallowed whole. So that was why the Wolf needed a scribe. Not for translation. Not for access. For *craftsmanship*. All those nights on the road, the lessons in borrowed fire, the patient correction of his strokes, the way Vael had watched him work with something that was not quite pride and not quite grief — he had been sharpening the knife. Teaching the instrument of his own execution to write.

And Marguerite had been *gifted*. Had been flattered by how fast he learned. Had sat across the watchtower fire thinking *he believes in me* while the truth sat across the flames wearing a dying soldier's face: *he is preparing me to kill him well.*

His hands were steady when he picked up the stylus again. That frightened him more than shaking would have.

* * *

It was near the bottom of the second scroll-case, in a fragment catalogued under some dead archivist's numbering system, that he found the annotation.

The fragment was small — a single leaf, brittle as frost, salvaged from some older and more complete manuscript. The verse upon it was one Marguerite already knew, the invocation of the binding, but this copy predated the commentaries. It was close to the author's own hand. Close enough that he could feel the man — or the being — who had written it, the pressure of the stylus varying with breath and doubt.

And in the lower margin, in ink gone brown as old blood, a single line:

*Let it be me.*

Not a signature. Not a title. Just that — three words, written small, in the cramped leftward slant of someone who wrote quickly and hated wasting space. Marguerite knew handwriting the way other men knew faces. He had spent twenty years in a scriptorium learning to read the person inside the script: the impatience in a rushed ascender, the fear in a trembling baseline, the vanity in an overwrought flourish.

This hand was impatient. This hand was afraid. This hand had been young, or young in the way of the powerful, and it had written *let it be me* in the margin of the world's most terrible bargain, and then —

And then centuries had passed. And the war had ended, and the covenant had held, and the one who had offered himself had instead been exiled — cast out beyond the wards, into the valley where magic could not follow, sentenced to live. Sentenced to live while everyone he had saved forgot the price, built temples over the wound, taught children that the war had simply *ended*, that heroes had simply won.

Marguerite thought of Ashenmere's temple elders, with their clean hands and their charters. Thought of the Order of the Unbroken Star, breaking wards in the name of a wholeness they had never examined. Thought of every institution he had ever served, every seal he had ever pressed into wax, and understood, with a clarity that felt like fever, that the covenant's true text had never been hidden from him at all. It had been hidden from *everyone*. The price had been written out of the copies. He had spent his life copying a lie in a beautiful hand.

And Vael — Vael had carried the true text alone for four hundred years. Had walked back out of exile not toward salvation but toward a stone in the dark, dragging behind him a scribe he was teaching to make his death beautiful. *Let it be me.* Still, after all this time. Still offering. Still the same hand, the same slant, the same refusal to waste space on anything but the essential.

Marguerite put the fragment down very carefully, as if it might break, though it had already survived four hundred years of everything the world could do to it.

Then he sat in the lamplight and wept — not loudly, not beautifully, just the flat mechanical weeping of a body processing what the mind refuses, tears that froze faintly at the corners of his eyes in the archive cold. He wept for the boy who had written those words. He wept for the soldier-shell dying by inches on a cot in the council infirmary. He wept, most of all, for himself, for the fool kneeling in the snow at the pass saying *I'll learn anything you teach me,* while the lesson was always, only, ever: *how to bury me properly.*

* * *

When the weeping burned itself out, something else remained. It took him a while to recognize it, because it wore the face of calm.

Defiance.

It started, absurdly, with the forgery instinct. Twenty years of copying wards he could not cast had taught him one supreme skill: he could make a thing look true. He could take any hand, any seal, any style, and reproduce it so faithfully that the difference existed only in his own conscience. He had done it for his mother's medicine. He had done it for a village that prayed wrong. Every sin of his life had been the same sin — substituting his craft for the truth others claimed to own.

And here, in the margins of the oldest document in the world, was the oldest hand in the world, and Marguerite had studied it for the better part of an hour.

*Let it be me.*

He looked at the words for a long time. Then he took out a fresh sheet and began, stroke by stroke, to copy them — not the words, but the *hand*. The impatient ascenders. The leftward slant. The economy of a writer who feared running out of time. He copied it until his wrist ached, until he could produce the slant in his sleep, until the two samples side by side would have fooled the archivist, the council, the stone itself.

Because the covenant did not ask for a name. He had read it correctly, read it four times, read it past hope and past horror. The covenant asked for a *crafted* name, willingly borne, made in love. It asked for a text. And Marguerite Thornbury, debt-bound scribe of the godless valley, had spent his entire life learning the one art that mattered here: how to make a substitution that no authority could detect.

They wanted a sacrifice. Fine. But no one had ever specified *whose* name went into the stone. The rite required willingness — and Marguerite had never been more willing of anything in his life. It required love — and he had stopped pretending, somewhere on the frozen pass, that he lacked it. It required craft — and his craft was the best in two worlds, forged in resentment and mercy and twenty years of beautiful lies.

Vael had spent four hundred years arranging his own execution and calling it penance. He had chosen the role of the sacrificed so long ago that he had forgotten it was a choice. He thought the story was finished. He thought the text was fixed.

Marguerite smiled at the fragment, at the brown-inked plea in the margin, and his smile had no gentleness in it at all.

"You wrote *let it be me*," he said softly to the empty dark. "But you wrote it in the margin. And margins, my love, are where the revisions go."

He worked through what remained of the night. He drafted, discarded, redrafted. He built the name the way he had been taught — Vael's own lessons, turned inside out and aimed back at their maker, the finest irony of a life full of them. He wove in what he knew: the tower wall scratched with a true name by a desperate hand; the pass strewn with soldiers the stone still held; the dimmed and hungry dark pressing down on the city above. He made it strong. He made it *willing*. He made it, in the only sense the covenant recognized, loving.

Near dawn, the lock turned in the door.

Marguerite slid the fragment into his coat, over his heart, and covered his drafts with a blank ledger as Ilyen entered with her lamp and her soft voice and her soft chains of hospitality.

"Did you find what you needed, scribe?"

"Yes," Marguerite said, and stood, and gave her the obedient half-bow of a man still in bondage, and it cost him nothing now, because he knew what she didn't: that the most dangerous forger in the world was walking out of her archive carrying a secret in his own hand.

Above, through the ventilation shafts, the false dawn came gray and starless. Three days to midwinter. Somewhere in the infirmary, a dying soldier slept with another man's centuries on his face, dreaming — Marguerite was sure of it — of a stone and a name and a long-awaited end.

"Let it be me," the old text begged.

"No," Marguerite whispered, climbing the stairs into the cold. "Let it be *mine*."
