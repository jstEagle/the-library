## 18. The Price Split in Two

The covenant opened like a wound.

Not the clean seam of a page turned, not the obedient unfolding of a charter he had written himself four centuries ago — it came apart the way flesh comes apart, wetly and unwillingly, with a sound only he could hear: a long tearing note beneath the floor of the world. The Wolf felt every word of the old accord resist him, felt the names of the dead givers clutch at their places in the text like drowning men clutching driftwood. He tore anyway.

*Let go,* he told them, or thought he told them — it was hard to know, down here, whether he was speaking or screaming. *You've held this stone long enough. Let someone else carry it.*

Marguerite was still fighting him. That was the unbearable thing. The scribe's hand was locked in his, ink-black fingers gone white at the knuckles, and through that grip poured everything Marguerite was trying to give away — his name unraveling thread by thread into the stone, his warmth, his particular stubborn light, all of it streaming out of him and down into the dark beneath the citadel where the first war slept. The Wolf could feel the shape of what remained of the man, like a candle seen through paper. Half a candle already. Less.

He had spent four hundred years learning how things ended. Wars. Bodies. Promises. He knew the grammar of sacrifice the way Marguerite knew the grammar of wards — intimately, bitterly, from the inside. And so he knew, even as he tore, that what he was doing had never been done, because no one who reached this point had ever wanted to stop. The givers before him had walked to the stone alone by design. Solitude was built into the architecture. A willing sacrifice bearing a crafted true-name — singular. The covenant had never imagined two.

Good, the Wolf thought, with a ferocity that surprised even him. Let it be surprised.

"Marguerite." His voice came out of the dying soldier's throat like gravel dragged over ice. "Listen to me. I need your hand to write."

"I'm not—" The scribe's eyes found his, and they were already going strange, the pupils dilating as his name thinned, the way a face goes strange when you look at it across a great distance. "I'm not letting you take my half. You don't get to die for me twice."

"You misunderstand." The Wolf pulled him closer, close enough to feel the terrible cold coming off him now, the cold of a man becoming an inscription. "I'm not taking anything. I'm *adding*. There's a difference, and you of all people should know it. It's the whole difference between forgery and authorship."

And he pressed their joined hands against the stone.

* * *

What happened next, no one taught him. There was no lesson for it in the archives of the Order, no precedent in the burned libraries of the capital. The Wolf simply did the one thing he knew how to do better than any living creature on the earth, which was to write — and he wrote with Marguerite's hand and his own will together, carving into the living covenant a clause that had never existed:

*Let the price be divided.*

The stone fought him. Of course it did. Four hundred years of settled grief is not easily renegotiated, and the covenant was grief, he understood that now — it had always been grief given grammar, the war frozen mid-scream because no one could bear to finish the sentence. The old words closed around his wrist like teeth. Somewhere above them the starless dark pressed down through the broken vault, and the waking dead were climbing the stairs, and the snow outside burned with a fire that gave no heat, and none of it mattered. None of it had ever mattered except this: the man in front of him, and the arithmetic of what he was about to lose.

"Hold still," the Wolf said, absurdly gentle, the way you speak to something you are breaking. "This will hurt."

"It already hurts," Marguerite said, and laughed — actually laughed, a cracked and disbelieving sound, blood on his teeth from biting his tongue. "You're the worst bargain I ever made."

"You made three," the Wolf said. "I'm counting the one where you followed me out of Ashenmere without being asked."

"That one was free."

"No," said the Wolf, and drove the new clause home. "That one cost the most. That's why it works."

* * *

The magic took them both.

There was no other way to describe it, though later — much later, in the long convalescence that followed, in the gray dawns of a world with its sky mended badly — each of them would try, and each would fail, and they would end up simply not speaking of it, the way soldiers do not speak of the field. But the Wolf remembered, because remembering was the last thing the covenant left him, and what he remembered was this:

It reached into him and took his name first. Not the false one, not the soldier-name he had worn like a coat for a hundred years — his true name, the one scratched into a watchtower wall by his own younger hand, the one Marguerite had found and read aloud in the dark with such careful tenderness. It peeled that away the way you peel bark from a green branch, slowly, with a sound like tearing silk, and beneath it there was nothing. He had expected nothing. He had prepared for nothing. He had spent four centuries making himself into a vessel whose contents could be poured out entirely.

But then it kept going.

That was the part he had not planned for — that in tearing the covenant open he had also torn open himself, and the price did not distinguish between the giver and the intruder. It took the exile. It took the war-mage. It took the silver fire he had carried since before the valley of Ashenmere had a name, the power that had gutted at the ward-line and flickered in the soldier's failing body, all of it drawn out through the wound he had made and split, halved, apportioned with the blind fairness of a scale finding its balance. Half went into the stone. Half went — he felt it go, felt it leave like heat leaving a room — into the text, into the renewed accord, into the world.

And Marguerite's half — Marguerite's stolen, offered, refused-and-accepted-anyway half — met his in the middle.

He heard the scribe cry out. Not in pain, exactly. In recognition. Two halves of a price grinding against each other, refusing to fit, fitting anyway, the way two broken stones can be set together into an arch that holds. The covenant shuddered. The whole mountain shuddered. Above them, through the shattered vault, through the burning snow, through the dark that had eaten the stars —

— the stars came back.

Dimmed. That was the imperfection, the flaw in his forgery, the interest owed on a debt paid in installments. They did not blaze back the way the old songs promised, the way the Order's heresies feared. They returned the way coals return after a night of ash: one by one, uncertain, small, a scatter of embers across black velvet, each one burning at perhaps half its former strength, each one enough. Enough to see by. Enough to steer by. Enough to know the sky was a sky again and not a mouth.

Down in the streets of the buried city, the waking dead lay down.

The Wolf felt it happen the way you feel a fever break — a vast loosening, a sigh moving through the frozen pass and the petrified fields and the burning boulevards, ten thousand soldiers locked four hundred years in the posture of killing finally permitted to rest. Not dying. Sleeping. The covenant no longer froze the war; it cradled it, the way you cradle something broken until it can be mended properly, someday, by hands not yet born. He had rewritten the ending of the mage wars into something gentler and less final, and the cost of that gentleness was written on both their bodies.

Then the stone let go of them, and they fell.

* * *

He woke to silence and candlelight and the weight of snow-melt dripping somewhere far above.

For a long time he did not move. The soldier's body — what was left of it — lay on the cold flagstones beside the covenant-stone, and the Wolf lay inside it like a man lying in the wreck of a house he had lived in too long, taking inventory. Heartbeat: present, slow, laboring. Sight: dimmed, colors washed thin, as if the world had been left out in rain. And where his magic had been — where the silver fire had lived, that constant low burn he had known so long he had stopped hearing it, the way you stop hearing your own blood —

Quiet.

Not empty. That was the strange mercy of it. Not the dead silence of the valley, where magic went to starve at the border. Something smaller. A banked hearth instead of a forge. If he reached for it — and he did reach, wincing, curious, afraid — he found just enough to warm his hands. Just enough to mend a small wound, slowly, clumsily, the way a village healer mends them, with poultice and patience instead of power. Half a name. Half a life. Exactly as bargained, and nothing like what he had expected either half to feel like.

It felt, he realized with something between horror and wonder, like being mortal.

Beside him, Marguerite stirred.

The Wolf turned his head — even that cost him; the body protested, joints creaking like an old door — and looked at the scribe in the ember-light of the returning stars, which fell through the broken vault in thin silver bars. Marguerite's face was gray. His lips were cracked. His hands, when the Wolf took one up without asking, were cold as river-stones, and when the Wolf pressed his own ruined half-power into them, warming them the slow hard way, the scribe's eyes opened.

They were brown. Just brown. Whatever the covenant had taken from him, it had not taken that — the flat, unmagical, utterly human brown of a man born in a valley where wonders could not follow, who had crossed the whole wintered world anyway.

"You tore it," Marguerite rasped. His voice sounded scraped hollow, a bell rung too many times. "I felt you tear it. I told you not to."

"Yes," the Wolf said. "You did. At length. You're very articulate when you're dying; it's one of your more irritating qualities."

"I had it." Marguerite tried to sit up, failed, settled for turning his head, and glared with what strength remained. "I had it finished. My name was in the stone. It was *done*, and you — you couldn't just —"

"No," the Wolf said. "I couldn't."

Silence. The drip of meltwater. Far above, faint as memory, the sound of the city breathing again — survivors emerging from undercrofts, voices raised small and wondering at a sky with lights in it.

"You should have let me," Marguerite said, quieter now, and it was not anger in his voice anymore. It was something worse. Grief, maybe, for the clean death he had crafted for himself, the tidy ending he had spent chapters building letter by letter while the Wolf slept. "It would have worked. One of us. Whole."

"And the other would have spent what remained walking around a hole in the shape of you." The Wolf said it flatly, because the truth needed no ornament. "I have been a hole wearing a body for a century, Marguerite. I know the fit. I wouldn't wish it on you, and I especially wouldn't wish it on myself with you gone to prove it."

"That's not —" Marguerite stopped. Swallowed. Started again, slower. "That's not a reason. That's just loneliness with arguments."

"Yes," the Wolf agreed. "Most reasons are."

Marguerite stared at him. And then — the Wolf would replay this moment for years, mining it, unable to decide if it was forgiveness or surrender or simply exhaustion finding its level — the scribe began to laugh. Quietly at first, then helplessly, painfully, tears cutting tracks through the ash on his face, laughing at the ruin of them both, at the half-finished rite, at the dimmed and patched sky, at the sheer stupid audacity of two men who had each tried to die for the other and succeeded only in living.

"We broke it," he managed at last. "We broke the covenant. Four hundred years, and we broke it in one night, and the stars are — look at them. Look at them, they're *dim*, everyone will know something happened, scholars will argue about it for a thousand years —"

"They'll call it the Night of Half-Light," the Wolf said. "Or the Mended Sky. Some order of zealots will form around getting it wrong."

"The Order of the Unbroken Star," Marguerite said, and they looked at each other, and this time they both laughed, ragged and shocked and alive.

* * *

Getting out of the vault took most of a day, and neither of them could have done it alone.

That was the lesson of the stair, if the Wolf was honest — and he was trying, these days, at being honest; mortality seemed to demand it, the way a small boat demands bailing. The soldier's body could manage fifty steps before the legs shook. Marguerite could manage sixty before the cough started, deep and wet, the sound of a man whose name had been handled roughly. So they climbed in relay, leaning on each other, trading weight, cursing, resting on the landings among the sleeping dead — for the petrified soldiers who had waked and marched into the city now stood motionless in the corridors, faces slack, weapons lowered, and stepping past them was like stepping past a congregation at prayer.

At the top, the world.

Snow still fell, but it was only snow now, white and cold and ordinary, hissing on the fires that still smoldered in the lower quarters. The sky overhead was a scandal of small stars, each one shy, each one lit, and between them a darkness that was merely night rather than appetite. People stood in the streets looking up. An old woman was singing, tunelessly, the way people sing when they have forgotten the song but remember why. Somewhere a child was asking questions no adult could answer.

Marguerite stood beside him in the falling snow, swaying, holding himself upright by what the Wolf suspected was pure spite, and looked at the sky for a long time.

"It's not what it was," he said finally. "The stars. Before. I never saw them before — the valley, you know — but I read the old charts, I copied them a thousand times, and they were supposed to be —" He gestured vaguely upward, at the modest embers. "More."

"Everything is less than the charts say," the Wolf said. "Charts are written by people who haven't paid yet."

Marguerite glanced at him sideways. "Is that a proverb?"

"It is now. Write it down. You're a scribe; it's the only immortality either of us has left."

But Marguerite didn't laugh this time. He was looking at the Wolf's hands — at the fine tremor in them that hadn't been there a month ago, at the way the soldier's knuckles had begun, unmistakably, to look like an old man's knuckles. The borrowed body had been failing since Ashenmere; the covenant's tax had hurried it along; and whatever half-life the Wolf now carried, it was housed in flesh with perhaps a decade left in it, maybe less, aging visibly now like a candle burning at both ends of a very short wick.

"You're diminished," Marguerite said quietly. It wasn't pity. It was inventory. Scribe's habit.

"So are you. Your name has a hole in it — I can see it when you write. You'll drop letters. Forget words. Some mornings you won't remember what you dreamed, and some nights you won't dream at all." The Wolf flexed his damaged hands, watching the tremor. "Half a life, half a name. We bought a patched sky at a pawnshop rate, and we'll spend the rest of our lives noticing the missing pieces."

"Would you undo it?" Marguerite asked. "If you could. The split. Would you take it all back — make it whole again, one name, one death?"

The Wolf considered the question honestly, because the man deserved honesty and because he was no longer certain of his own answer, and that uncertainty itself was new, and strange, and not entirely unwelcome.

"No," he said at last. "A whole price paid by one is a wall. A price split in two is —" He searched for the word, and found it, and was embarrassed by it, and said it anyway. "A bridge. I've spent four hundred years on one side of every wall I ever built. I find I'd rather stand on a bridge. Even a bad one. Even in winter."

Marguerite said nothing. But he moved closer — half a step, no more, the smallest possible distance, the distance of a man testing whether the ground holds — and his shoulder came to rest against the Wolf's shoulder, and the Wolf discovered that a body without magic could still feel warmth, and that the feeling was not diminished at all. It was, if anything, the only thing in him that burned at full strength.

Above the pass, above the ruined capital, above the valley where the wards were even now beginning to reknit themselves strand by strand like a loom remembering its pattern, the dimmed stars burned on.

And far to the east, where the road ran down out of the mountains toward the temple lands and the scriptoriums and the debts and the old life — far off, at the very edge of seeing, a light was moving. Small. Steady. Coming toward them through the snow.

Torchlight. Many torches.

"Envoys," Marguerite murmured, squinting. "Or soldiers."

"At this point," said the Wolf, "the distinction is mostly a matter of paperwork."
