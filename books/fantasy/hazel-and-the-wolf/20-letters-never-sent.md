## 20. Letters Never Sent

### I. Marguerite

The scriptorium smelled the same, and that was the cruelest thing about it.

Wax and iron-gall ink, tallow smoke, the faint sourness of wet wool drying by the brazier — every note of it exactly as he had left it, as if the valley had held its breath for a season and exhaled nothing changed. The desks stood in their rows. The ward-charters lay stacked in their pigeonholes, waiting to be copied out in hands steadier than his had ever been. Outside the high windows, Ashenmere went about its morning: carts in the lane, a bell ringing the hour, snow sliding off the temple eaves with a soft, indifferent thump.

Marguerite sat at his old desk with a fresh sheet before him and could not remember how to begin.

His hand knew. That was the horror of it. His fingers curled around the quill with the same practiced ease they'd had since he was nine years old, when Brother Aldous first pressed a stylus into them and said, *You were made for this, boy — made to write what others do.* But knowing the shape of the work was not the same as believing in it. He looked down at the charter he was meant to copy — a boundary-ward renewal, third revision, standard formula — and saw instead the covenant-stone beneath the citadel, half-lit by burning snow, and two names carved into its face where one should have been.

Half a name. Half a life. His.

He flexed his fingers. They ached in cold weather now, all the way through, an ache that lived deeper than the joints. Some mornings he woke and could not recall his own middle name — just for a heartbeat, just long enough to be afraid — before it came back to him like water seeping into a dry well. The mages called it *diminution*. The priests called it sacrifice. Marguerite thought of it as rent: he had bought the stars back at installments, and the payments would come due for the rest of his life.

The debt-bond itself was ash. When the covenant reknit, every contract written under the old accord had burned with it — including the one that had bound him to Ashenmere since his father's ruin. He was, by law and by ink, free.

He had been free for four months, and he still flinched when anyone said his name without a title attached.

"Marguerite." Brother Aldous's shadow fell across the desk, thin and stooped as ever. "The eastern charters won't copy themselves."

"No," Marguerite said. "They won't."

He bent to the work. And that was the second cruelty: the work came easily. Too easily. His pen moved through the ward-formulae like a boat through still water, and somewhere around the third line he realized he was no longer copying — he was *improving*, tightening a binding clause here, smoothing a channel there, small corrections that would make each ward hold longer and burn brighter than any charter Ashenmere had produced in a century. Corrections learned on the road, from a voice like gravel and winter, in the space between one skirmish and the next.

He stopped. Stared at the page. The extra strokes sat there gleaming, unasked-for, alive.

*You were made for this,* Aldous had said. But Aldous had never meant this. No one in Ashenmere had ever meant this.

Marguerite scraped the corrections away with a knife, copied the charter plain and perfect and dead, and felt something in his chest close like a fist.

* * *

That night he wrote the first letter.

He did not decide to. He finished supper in the refectory, endured an hour of conversation about grain-tithes and the envoy from the lowlands who was expected in spring, and climbed to his cell with a candle, and there on the desk — where he kept no desk-things, because he owned almost nothing — lay a sheet of good paper and a stick of sealing wax that he had no memory of taking from the stores.

He sat down. He wrote:

*To the Wolf, wherever you are convalescing, which I am told is the east tower of the citadel, which I am told because I asked, which I asked because—*

He stopped. Crossed it out. Began again.

*I have returned to my duties. The scriptorium is unchanged. I am unchanged. This is a lie I am practicing.*

The candle guttered. He watched the flame bend and thought about the way fire had moved across the snow that night, and about silver eyes in a dying soldier's face, and about a voice that had said, hoarse and furious and broken open, *No — no, you fool, you magnificent fool, what have you done—*

*I dream about the pass,* he wrote. *Not the battle. The quiet after. You were laughing. I don't think I had ever heard you laugh before, and I don't think you had either, because you looked frightened of the sound.*

He wrote until the candle drowned in its own wax. Then he read it back, every word of it naked and unbearable, folded it with great care, addressed it to *The East Tower, Citadel of Vhalen*, and put it in the drawer with his spare nibs, where no one would ever find it, least of all a courier.

He slept better than he had in weeks.

### II. The Wolf

The east tower of the citadel had been a mage's observatory once, before the wars, before the snow. Now it was a sickroom with a view of a buried city, and the Wolf hated every stone of it.

Not the city. The waiting.

He had been mortal for four months, and mortality turned out to be a country with terrible roads. The body they had split him into — the remnant-body, the half-life, grown new around the half-name the covenant had left him — tired easily, ate enormously, and woke him before dawn with aches in places he had not previously known possessed the capacity for pain. His magic, what remained of it, came slow and dim, like light through deep water. He could warm a cup of tea. He could mend a torn sleeve. Once, in a fit of vanity, he had lit every candle in the tower at once, and then spent the next day in bed paying for it.

Hazel would have laughed at him.

The thought arrived uninvited, as it always did, and the Wolf set down his tea and looked out the window at the grey morning and let it sit there, because he had learned that fighting it only made it worse.

Four months, and neither of them had written. Which was absurd, because letters existed. Couriers ran between Vhalen and the passes twice a month now that the wards stood again; the world was knitting itself back together with the clumsy, eager stitches of survivors, and a letter from the capital to Ashenmere would take perhaps ten days. Ten days. He had crossed a frozen hell to reach a covenant-stone in less time than that.

But a letter required words, and every word he tried was wrong.

*Marguerite. I trust the valley treats you well. I trust the valley is everything you wanted, which is to say safe, which is to say small, which is to say a cage with the door painted on—*

No. He struck it through. He was not going to open a letter by insulting the man's home. Even if it was true. Even if he had watched Marguerite walk back through those border stones into the ward-shadowed valley and seen the exact moment the light went out of him — the same dimming, the same quiet, as a lamp carried behind thick glass.

*I owe you an accounting,* he tried. *By the old ledgers, half a life purchased jointly is held in common, and common property cannot be abandoned by one party without consent of the other. Therefore I require your consent to stop thinking about you, and I am writing to inform you that I will not be granting mine.*

He read that back three times. It was, infuriatingly, the closest he had come to the truth, and it was still a coward's way of saying it.

The truth was simpler and worse. The truth was that he had spent centuries arranging his death with the patience of a mason laying stones, and a scribe with ink-stained fingers and a talent for forgery had picked up the whole edifice and *rewritten* it — had overwritten the Wolf's name with his own on the covenant-stone while the world burned, and then, when the Wolf tore the rite open to split the cost, had *held on*. Had refused to let go. Had chosen, freely, with full knowledge of the price, to be bound to him — half-named, diminished, mortal — rather than let him die alone.

And now the Wolf sat in a tower drinking bad tea and could not compose a letter, because every letter implied an ending, and he did not want an ending. He wanted — 

He got up. He paced. Mortal bodies were built for pacing; it gave the restlessness somewhere to go.

Below, in the undercrofts, the council of ashes went on governing its candlelit kingdom, softer and crueler by degrees, and the Wolf answered their summonses when he must and ignored them when he could, because he was a hero of the restoration and heroes are difficult to discipline. They wanted him to stay. A relic, a symbol, a living proof that the old wars were over. He could have anything he liked, they said, within reason.

What he liked was four hundred miles west, copying charters in a valley where magic couldn't follow, and would not say so, and neither would he.

He went back to the desk. He took a fresh sheet. He wrote, in a hand that shook slightly and did not entirely belong to him:

*The candles here are poor. Everything here is poor, compared to—*

He stopped. Folded it. Addressed it to *M. Thornbury, Scriptorium of the Temple of Ashenmere*, and locked it in the tower's one surviving strongbox, which had not opened in three hundred years and was not about to start.

Then he sat by the window until dark, watching the sky over the pass, where the stars came up dimmed but burning, every single one of them a debt he shared with someone who wasn't there.

### III. Marguerite

Spring came wrong but it came. The snows pulled back from the valley walls in dirty ribbons, and the envoys began to arrive — first from the lowlands, then from Vhalen itself, grave men and women in travel-stained grey who spoke in the refectory of a world remade, of covenants rewritten, of a new accord being drafted that would need *scribes*, many scribes, scribes trained in the old true-name craft, which was to say: scribes like him.

Aldous brought him the invitation personally, glowing with reflected glory. "They ask for you by name, Marguerite. By name! Do you understand what that means? The Temple's standing — your standing —"

"I understand what it means," Marguerite said, and did not tell him that the name on the invitation had been written in a hand he knew better than his own, a hand that shook slightly and pressed too hard on the downstrokes, and that he had recognized it from across the room before he'd read a single word.

The Wolf had recommended him. Formally, officially, through channels. Had put his name forward into the wide world and then — nothing. No note. No message. Not even the courtesy of a signature above the seal, though the seal itself was the Wolf's: a wolf's head over a broken star, stamped in wax the color of dried blood.

Marguerite carried the invitation in his coat for six days. On the seventh he took it out, read it again, and understood, with the clarity that comes only after long stupidity, what was happening.

Neither of them would say it. Neither of them would *write* it. Two of the finest name-craftsmen alive, two men who had literally rewritten the fabric of the world with words, and they could not manage eleven honest sentences between them — because a letter could be refused, and a refusal would end things, and neither of them could bear the ending more than they feared the asking.

*Longing outrunning pride,* he thought. *Or pride outrunning longing. I genuinely cannot tell which anymore.*

That night he took out the drawer with his spare nibs. Inside were four letters now, unsent, accumulating like sediment: the first desperate one, and three more written on worse nights, each one truer and less sendable than the last. He laid them out on the desk in order, and read them all the way through, and by the end his eyes were hot and his hands were steady, which was, he supposed, the whole of his character in miniature.

The fourth letter ended: *I chose you on the stone and I choose you now and I am a coward in a valley, which is the worst kind, because valleys make cowards feel safe.*

He took the letters to the refectory hearth, when it was empty, and fed them to the coals one at a time. They burned quickly. He watched the ink flare green — iron-gall always flared green — and felt each sentence leave him like a splinter drawn from a wound.

Then he went upstairs and packed a bag.

It did not take long. He had never owned much, and the covenant-fire had taken the little he'd inherited. Two shirts, his good pens, the knife he used for scraping vellum, a block of sealing wax he absolutely had taken from the stores and would confess to eventually. The forged scripture from the village that prayed wrong — he kept that, as a reminder of what lies could build when aimed at mercy. And at the bottom, wrapped in oilcloth, a charter he had copied out himself, plain and perfect and dead, the very first thing he'd written after coming home — because he wanted to carry proof of what he was leaving, so he would know the difference when he found it.

At dawn he stood at the edge of the temple courtyard with the bag on his shoulder, looking back at the scriptorium windows going gold with early light. Somewhere inside, Aldous was already at his desk. Somewhere inside was a life that fit him the way an old glove fits: exactly, comfortably, and belonging to someone else.

"You were made for this," Aldous had said.

"Yes," Marguerite said aloud, to no one. "Just not here."

He walked west, toward the border stones, and did not let himself run until the temple was out of sight.

### IV. The Wolf

On the last morning of a wrong spring, the Wolf lost an argument with his own legs.

He had intended, you understand, merely to walk. To the border, yes — four hundred miles of it, alone, in a body that complained like a hired mule — but walking was all. He had a speech prepared. It was a good speech, dignified, restrained, offering the man a position on the new accord commission with entirely professional terms, and it had taken him three weeks to draft and contained not one honest word.

He crested the last rise above the border stones in the grey of early morning, wind in his face off the pass, and stopped.

There was a figure at the stones already. Small, ink-stained, travel-worn, with a bag on his shoulder and snowmelt in his hair, standing very still, as if he had been there some time — as if perhaps he had walked out of the safe valley, the first soul ever to choose the magical world over it, and then waited, because he had known, the way they both knew things now, half-named and wholly bound, that waiting would be enough.

The speech died in the Wolf's throat. All three weeks of it.

"Your handwriting has improved," Marguerite said, without turning around. "I saw the invitation. You pressed too hard on the downstrokes. You always press too hard when you're frightened."

"I was not frightened," the Wolf said. "I was composing."

"For three weeks?"

"For three weeks."

Marguerite turned then, and his face in the early light was thinner than the Wolf remembered and lovelier for it, marked now with the fine silver scarring that diminution leaves on those who pay it gladly. He looked tired. He looked terrified. He looked like a man who had burned four letters and packed a bag and walked out of a valley, and would do it all again.

"Well," Marguerite said. "Here I am. No bargain this time."

"No bargain," the Wolf agreed, and found that his voice had gone rough, and that his legs, traitorous mortal things, were already carrying him down the slope.
