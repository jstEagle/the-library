## 1. The Charm Seller's Hour

The bells of Highmoor rang the ninth hour, and Ivo Brightwater was already late.

She came down Cooper's Lane with her tray of charms slung across her chest like a breastplate — which, in a way, it was. Twelve years of selling knotted thread and river-stones had taught her that a woman with something to sell looked busy, and a woman who looked busy looked harmless, and a woman who looked harmless in occupied Highmoor lived to see another market night.

The charms themselves were honest work, mostly. Knots tied at the turn of the tide for luck. River-stones from the Weep, polished smooth and drilled through, said to keep nightmares off. Dried lavender against bad dreams, iron nails bent into hooks against worse ones. None of it worked, strictly speaking — or rather, none of it worked *magically*, which was the important distinction in a city where magic carried a fine of everything you owned and, if the witch-finders were feeling thorough, everything you were.

But Ivo's knots did work, a little, because she made them while thinking kindly thoughts at them, and kindness soaked into things the way rain soaked into brick. She'd never been able to explain that to anyone. So she didn't try.

"Charm-seller!" called a boy of maybe ten, weaving through the crowd with the confidence of someone who had never once been grabbed by a soldier. "Me da wants a luck-knot for the fishing boats."

"For your da, or for you?" Ivo asked, already knotting.

"Both," the boy said shamelessly.

"Then that'll be two coppers, and I'll throw in the third knot free." She tied fast, murmuring under her breath — *safe water, full nets, come home* — words so old they'd worn smooth as the stones she sold. The boy paid, grinned, and vanished into the press of bodies, and Ivo felt the small, familiar warmth of having done one small good thing in a city that made small good things feel like smuggling.

Market night in Highmoor ran on candlelight and rumor. The occupation had taken the daylight markets years ago — too easy to police, the soldiers said, when everyone could see everyone — but night markets sprang up like mushrooms after rain, in the crook of every alley where the lanterns didn't reach. Here the fishwives sold eels still writhing in their buckets. Here a knife-grinder worked his wheel by feel. Here a woman sold roasted chestnuts and opinions about Lord Cassian in equal measure, both of them hot and likely to burn you.

And here, at the corner of Tanner's Row and the Weep Stair, Ivo Brightwater set out her tray and became, for four hours, exactly what she appeared to be: a charm-seller. Plain, pleasant, forgettable. A woman whose face you couldn't hold onto five minutes after she'd gone.

That was the mask, and she wore it the way other people wore coats. Underneath — underneath was the thing she fed at midnight and told no one about.

* * *

The trouble started, as trouble usually did, with an animal.

A cart-horse stood at the mouth of Tanner's Row, harnessed to a wagon of salted fish, and he was terrified. Ivo could hear it before she saw it — not the sounds horses make that people notice, the stamping and the tossing, but the sound underneath. Horses spoke in pressure and breath, in the tightening of a flank, in the way an ear swiveled back like a door trying to close. This one was screaming in the only language he had left, the language of held-in fear, and half the market walked past him without hearing a word.

Ivo heard it the way you hear your own name shouted across a square.

He was a big bay, fifteen hands if he was one, with white socks and a scar over one eye, and his handler had gone off to drink with the fish-cart drivers and left him standing beside a brazier that spat and crackled like something hunting him. Every time a spark jumped, the horse flinched so hard the wagon creaked. His eye showed white all around. He was working himself toward the edge of panic, and a panicked cart-horse in a crowded night market was a disaster with hooves.

Ivo should have kept walking. That was the rule. Twelve years, one rule, carved into her like initials into a tree: *never let them see.* Not the witch-finders, not the neighbors, not the fishwives who'd sell your name for a discount on mackerel. Never let them see.

But the horse was looking at her.

Animals always knew. It was the first thing she'd learned, back before she'd learned to hide it — that beasts could smell the gift on her the way dogs smelled thunder coming. They found her in crowds. They turned their heads to her across fields. And this horse, drowning in fear in a sea of indifferent humans, had found the one person in a hundred who could hear him, and he was looking at her with an expression she'd spent twelve years pretending not to understand.

*Please,* said the look.

"Oh, hang it," Ivo muttered, and crossed the row.

She did it the way she did everything — with a cover story ready before her feet finished moving. She stopped by the wagon, made a show of admiring the salted fish, and then, casually, as if stretching, laid her hand flat against the horse's sweat-darkened neck.

And she breathed.

It wasn't words, not really. Words were for humans; horses thought in wider shapes than words. What she sent down her arm and through her palm was simpler: *no fire will touch you. No spark. You are safe. Stand still. Breathe.* She matched her own breathing to his until his ribs slowed under her hand, until the white left his eye, until the trembling went out of him like a storm moving off over water. It took perhaps two minutes. To anyone watching, it looked like a charm-seller petting a horse, which was the sort of thing charm-sellers did, harmless as spilled milk.

"There," she said aloud, for the benefit of anyone listening. "Good lad. Handsome fellow. Who's your owner, then?"

"Mine," said a voice behind her, thick with ale and suspicion, "and what's it to you?"

The handler had come back. He was a broad man with fish scales in his beard and the particular watchfulness of someone deciding whether to be angry. Ivo smiled her plainest smile.

"It's nothing to me, friend. Only that a spooked horse in a crowd is a broken leg waiting to happen, and I've got a soft spot for the handsome ones." She scratched the bay's jaw, and the horse leaned into her hand with a sigh that would have melted a stone. "See? He likes me. Animals always do. It's the lavender." She patted the little bundles on her tray. "Keeps flies off, calms the nerves. Want one for his bridle? Two coppers."

The handler squinted at her, decided she was nobody worth being angry at, and bought the lavender. Ivo counted that a victory twice over — once for the coin, and once because the best hiding place in the world was inside a transaction. Nobody remembered the woman who sold them something. They remembered the *thing*, and the price, and moved on.

She was three steps away when the patrol came around the corner.

* * *

There were six of them, witch-finders' men in Cassian's gray-and-black, and they moved through the night market the way weasels move through a henhouse — unhurried, certain, enjoying themselves. At their head walked a lean officer with a lantern on a pole, and the lantern was no ordinary light. It burned pale green, and where its glow touched, things showed true.

Ivo knew about those lanterns. Everyone in Highmoor knew about them, the way everyone knows about wolves — abstractly, until the night the howling comes close. The green flame drank glamour and gift alike. A hedge-witch's charm would gutter in its light. A gifted person's shadow, they said, would move wrong.

Her shadow was behaving itself. Her charms were only knots and stones and dried flowers. By every law of the occupation, Ivo Brightwater was exactly what she appeared to be.

But the horse.

The bay had gone quiet under her hands in a way that no ordinary petting explained, and the handler was telling anyone who'd listen about the charm-seller and her marvelous lavender, and the officer with the lantern had stopped, and turned, and was looking at the horse with narrowed eyes.

"Hold," the officer said. "That animal. Why is it calm?"

"Because she—" the handler began, gesturing at Ivo with his tankard.

Ivo's heart performed a maneuver that would have impressed a surgeon. She kept walking. That was the trick, the oldest trick, the one that had kept her alive through purges and raids and the year they'd burned the tanner's daughter on nothing but a neighbor's grudge: *keep walking.* Guilty people freeze. Innocent people are annoyed at being delayed.

She turned, brows raised in mild, put-upon curiosity, a woman mildly inconvenienced. "Something the matter, captain?"

"Not captain. Finder-Sergeant Corvel." The lantern swung toward her, and the green light washed over her face, her tray, her hands. In its glow her knots looked like knots. Her stones looked like stones. Her shadow lay obediently at her heels. "You touched this horse."

"I sell lavender, sergeant. I touched half the animals on this street. There's a dog by the chestnut cart wearing more of my product than the cart has chestnuts." This was true. The dog in question was wearing three bundles and looked deeply pleased about it.

Corvel studied her the way a man studies a lock he suspects but can't prove. "Horses don't calm for strangers."

"This one calmed for two coppers' worth of lavender and a scratch on the nose." She smiled — plain, pleasant, forgettable. "Unless you're telling me kindness is outlawed now too. Only I'd want to know, seeing as I'd need to change trades, and I'm told there's good money in informing."

That last bit was a gamble. But she'd learned long ago that witch-finders disliked two things above all: being laughed at, and being reminded that informers were a coin everyone could spend. A hint that she might know names — anyone's names — was often enough to make them decide a woman was more useful walking free.

Corvel's eyes flicked to the horse. The bay stood drowsy and content, one hind foot resting, breathing slow as a sleeping child. Then back to Ivo. Then to the lavender bundle in the handler's fist.

"Lavender," he said, in the tone of a man filing away a word he didn't believe.

"Or luck," Ivo offered helpfully. "Luck-knots are two coppers as well, if the lavender's not to your taste."

For one long moment — a moment she would take out later and examine in the dark of her room, turning it over like a stone with something underneath it — Corvel simply looked at her. Green light on her face. Her heartbeat loud enough to hear. Somewhere behind her ribs, the old terror stretched and yawned and bared its teeth.

Then a drunk upset a bucket of eels two stalls down, and the resulting commotion — shrieking women, slithering fish, one eel making a determined bid for freedom down a boot — broke the moment like a dropped plate.

"Eels," Corvel said disgustedly, and turned away. "Move along, charm-seller. And lose the mouth, or you'll find yourself explaining it at the Citadel."

"Wouldn't dream of it, sergeant," Ivo said, and meant: *I have dreamed of nothing else for twelve years.*

She walked away slowly, because innocent people are annoyed at being delayed, and only when she'd turned the corner by the Weep Stair did she let herself shake.

* * *

Her room was above a chandler's shop on Candle Lane, one window, one bed, one stove that smoked unless you bribed it with dry wood. Rent was cheap because the chimney drew badly and the chandler's wax smell got into everything. Ivo had stopped noticing the smell around year three.

She lit no lamp. Lampts were for people with nothing to hide; a dark room was a room that couldn't be watched through. She sat on the bed in the gloom and counted the night's takings: eleven coppers, a button, and a dried herring some fishwife had pressed on her in exchange for a nightmare-charm, on the theory that a charm-seller had to eat too.

Eleven coppers. On a good night, fourteen. On a bad night, three and a lecture from the chandler's wife about smoke.

Twelve years of eleven coppers a night, and never once a name spoken aloud to her in kindness. That was the arithmetic she didn't do, most nights, because doing it led to places with no floor. Instead she did the other arithmetic, the survival sum: rent, coal, bread, oil she didn't use, a copper saved here and there into a jar behind a loose brick. The jar was for running. It was always for running. In Highmoor you kept a bag packed and a jar filled and a story ready, and you thanked whatever gods the occupation hadn't outlawed yet that you'd woken up one more morning unremarkable.

She was reaching for the herring when she heard it: the small scrabble of claws on the roof tiles outside her window.

* * *

The cat was orange, enormous, missing half an ear, and had the general bearing of a debt collector. Nobody owned him. Everybody fed him, on the strict understanding that he belonged to no one, least of all them. The chandler's wife called him His Nibs. The knife-grinder called him Satan. Ivo called him nothing at all, out loud, because calling a cat to you in a voice anyone could hear was a small domesticity, and domesticity was a thread, and threads, pulled, unraveled masks.

But she'd been feeding him for six years.

He arrived through the window the way he arrived everywhere — as though the building owed him money. He landed on her bed, considered it briefly, and settled onto her blanket with the entitlement of a landlord.

"Evening, Your Majesty," Ivo murmured, too low for any ear but his. She broke the herring in half and set the larger portion down. "Don't say I never give you anything."

The cat ate with terrible elegance, then butted his skull against her wrist — the one place, Ivo sometimes thought, in the whole city of Highmoor, where she was allowed to be touched. Six years of this. Six years of a cat who came to her window and no further questions asked. If you'd asked her — if anyone ever *had* asked her, which they didn't, because nobody noticed her enough to ask — she'd have said the cat was a habit. A convenience. An arrangement between professionals.

She'd have been lying. She knew she'd have been lying. The cat was the closest thing she had to family, and the word *family* was a locked room in her mind she visited only at night, alone, in the dark, with the door shut.

"You nearly had us all killed tonight, you know," she told him, scratching the intact ear. "Standing next to that horse like a great orange witness. Anyone with a green lantern and half a wit would've wondered why the beasts all come to Ivo Brightwater like she's handing out supper."

The cat purred, which Ivo chose to interpret as disagreement.

Outside, the bells rang midnight, and the last of the market noise died away, and Highmoor became the other city — the silent one, the watched one, where every shuttered window might have eyes behind it. Ivo blew out nothing, because she'd lit nothing, and lay back on the bed with the cat a warm weight against her side, and did the dangerous arithmetic after all, just for a minute, just because the night was quiet.

One day, she thought, in the secret voice she used for nothing else. One day I'd like to walk through a market in daylight. Say hello to people who say hello back. Feed a cat where the neighbors can see, and let them think what they like.

One day. When the wardens wake, whispered a voice in her memory — her grandmother's voice, dead twenty years, saying words she'd been told were only stories. *When the wardens wake, the masks come off, little bird. All of them. Ours and theirs.*

"Stories," Ivo said softly to the ceiling, the way she always did, the way she'd done ten thousand times, the ritual that kept the words harmless.

The cat lifted his head.

And looked at her — not the lazy golden blink of a fed and sleepy beast, but a long, level, deliberate stare, pupils widening in the dark until his eyes were nearly all black, and when he opened his mouth, what came out was not a meow.

"*They stir,*" said the cat. "*Under the citadel. They stir, Brightwater, and the ink is already drying on your name.*"

Ivo Brightwater did not scream, though every part of her wanted to. She sat up very slowly in the dark, heart hammering, twelve years of careful silence cracking down the middle like river ice in spring.

"*Say that again,*" she whispered, "*and slower.*"
