## 2. A Face in Old Ink

The archives of the University of Highmoor smelled of dust, tallow, and the particular despair of scholars who had died before finishing their footnotes. Sera Vane loved it more than any place on earth.

She had claimed her corner of the third sub-basement eleven years ago, back when she was a first-year with ink-stained cuffs and an advisor who'd told her that prophecy studies was "a discipline for people who enjoy being wrong." She had spent those eleven years proving him wrong about that, at least, and if she hadn't yet proven anything else — well. The night was young. So to speak.

Tonight's prize sat open before her under a lamp turned low: *The Vellum Hours*, a century-old codex recovered from a burned farmhouse outside Hollowbridge, catalogued by some long-dead librarian as "miscellaneous devotional verse" and left to rot ever since. It was not devotional verse. Sera had known that from the moment she'd seen the marginalia — a looping, hurried hand annotating the psalms with dates, names, and small, precise astronomical diagrams.

Someone had used a book of prayers as a ledger of predictions. And someone else, later, had tried to burn it for it.

She dipped her pen and copied out the latest deciphered passage, mouthing the old Highmoor dialect as she went, translating by feel the way other people translated by grammar:

*When the grain is taxed twice and the bell tower leans,*

*a seller of small magics will walk the night market unafraid.*

Sera paused. Unafraid. In occupied Highmoor, where Lord Cassian's witch-finders walked the streets in their grey hoods and even hedge-wives hid their remedies? No one sold magic openly. No one was *unafraid*.

Unless the writer meant it as aspiration. Or unless—

She shook herself. This was exactly the trap her advisor had warned about: reading prophecy like scripture, hunting for yourself in every line. Prophecy was a mirror in a dark room. You saw what you brought in with you.

She kept copying.

*Beneath the leaning bell, in the hour of cheap candles,*

*she mends what men have broken, and the broken thank her wrongly.*

"Well, that's specific," Sera murmured to the empty archive. A charm-seller who healed things. In the night market. Under a leaning bell tower — Saint Aldric's, whose spire had been crooked since before the war, so that was no great feat of foresight. Still. Her pulse had picked up its pace without asking her permission.

She turned the page.

And froze.

There was a sketch in the margin.

It was small — three fingers tall, done in brown ink with the quick confidence of someone who drew often and well. A woman, standing behind a market stall hung with little charms: knotted cords, painted river-stones, bundles of dried herbs tied with red thread. She was slight, dark-haired, with her sleeves rolled up and her head tilted as though listening to something just out of frame. Her face was rendered in perhaps twenty strokes, and every one of them was right. The strong jaw. The faint scar through the left eyebrow. The wary set of the mouth, as if the artist had caught her mid-decision about whether to smile.

Sera knew that face.

Not from history. Not from any portrait gallery or genealogical roll. From the night market itself, from a stall between the pie-seller and the man who repaired kettles, where a quiet woman sold luck-charms and never once, in all the years Sera had passed her, made eye contact with anyone longer than courtesy required.

"Ivo Brightwater," Sera said aloud, and the archive swallowed the name whole.

Her hands were not entirely steady as she checked the sketch's caption. The old hand had dated his drawings — she'd noticed that weeks ago, another oddity, because why would a prophet date his own visions? Unless he wasn't dating visions. Unless he was keeping records.

The caption read:

*Ivo Brightwater, charm-seller. Drawn the day before the blood moon.*

Sera did the arithmetic the way you check a lock you already know is unlocked. The marginalia's internal calendar — which she had laboriously aligned with the modern one over four months of cross-referencing festivals, tax edicts, and one extremely helpful eclipse — put this drawing at...

Tomorrow.

Tomorrow there would be a sketch-perfect image of a woman who was alive and selling charms tonight, drawn a hundred years ago, dated the day after today.

"No," Sera said firmly, to herself, to the archive, to the dead annotator whose name she still hadn't pinned down. "No, absolutely not. Coincidence. Resemblance. The human face has limited real estate; statistically—"

She stopped, because she had turned the page again without deciding to, and there was more text beneath the sketch, in that same looping hand, but pressed harder now, the letters jagged:

*On the night of the blood moon the gate will burn,*

*and what sleeps beneath the citadel will hear it.*

*Woe to the city that chained its speakers.*

*Woe twice over to the woman whose name opens the door.*

Sera sat very still.

The lamp guttered. Somewhere overhead, the university bells rang the tenth hour, and she thought — absurdly, hysterically — of Saint Aldric's leaning spire, and whether leaning towers rang differently, and whether anyone had ever tested that, and how quickly she could get funding for a study if the world didn't end tomorrow.

Because that was the thing. That was the whole wretched, wonderful, terrible thing about her profession: prophecy was either nonsense or it wasn't, and there was no comfortable middle ground, and every fiber of her trained skepticism was screaming *nonsense* while the sketch of Ivo Brightwater's scarred eyebrow looked up at her from the page and said, in its silent, patient, hundred-year-old voice: *was I wrong about the jaw, too?*

"Right," Sera said. She stood, tucked the codex under her arm, and pinched out the lamp. "Right. Empiricism."

Empiricism meant observation. Observation meant going to the night market and looking at a charm-seller's eyebrow.

It was, she would reflect later, a remarkably flimsy foundation for the rest of her life.

* * *

The night market lived in the shadow of Saint Aldric's, in the crook of streets the city guard patrolled lazily and the witch-finders avoided when they could, because crowds were unpredictable and hooded men drew stares. Sera arrived early, wearing her second-best coat and carrying a basket as camouflage, and bought a pie she didn't want from the pie-seller as cover.

Ivo Brightwater's stall was exactly where memory said it would be. The woman herself sat behind it, mending — of all things — a child's wooden bird with a broken wing, working with a patience that seemed less like craft and more like conversation. Around her hung the charms: knotted cords, painted stones, herb bundles with red thread. Exactly as drawn. Exactly as drawn a hundred years ago.

Sera stood ten feet away, clutching her basket, feeling profoundly ridiculous.

*Say something,* she instructed herself. *You are a scholar. Scholars ask questions. Ask her about the weather. Ask her about the pie. Do not ask her about the blood moon; that would be insane.*

"You're staring," said Ivo Brightwater, without looking up from the wooden bird.

"I'm — shopping," Sera said.

"With a basket full of nothing and a pie you keep holding away from your coat like it owes you money." Ivo glanced up then, and her eyes were grey-green and sharp and very tired. "What do you actually want?"

Here was the moment where a sensible person asked about charms. Here was the moment where a cautious person walked away entirely. Sera Vane, eleven years in a basement, heard herself say:

"Do you have a scar through your left eyebrow?"

Ivo's hands went still over the broken wing. For a heartbeat her whole body changed — the wariness in her mouth resolving into something older and harder, a cornered stillness — and then it smoothed away so completely that Sera doubted she'd seen it at all.

"Old childhood argument with a rosebush," Ivo said lightly. "Why? Buying luck or buying scars?"

"Neither. Both. I don't—" Sera exhaled. "May I show you something? Not here. Somewhere with fewer ears." She nodded, very slightly, toward the grey-hooded figure at the far end of the lane who was pretending, badly, to be interested in kettle repair.

Something flickered across Ivo's face. Not fear, exactly. Recognition — the look of someone hearing a sound they'd learned long ago to dread.

"Archivist," Ivo said. It wasn't a question. "You've got the coat and the hands. University?"

"Third sub-basement. Prophecy and apocrypha."

A short, dry laugh escaped Ivo before she could stop it. "Prophecy. In this city. That's a fast way to disappear."

"That's rather what I need to talk to you about."

For a long moment Ivo Brightwater studied her, and Sera had the distinct sensation of being weighed — measured against some private scale the charm-seller kept behind her eyes, the kind that judged not what you said but what you'd do when the price came due.

"Saint Aldric's closes at midnight," Ivo said finally. "The sexton drinks. The vestry door sticks, but it sticks *outward*, if you take my meaning. Come at half past. If you're followed, don't come at all, and forget my face."

"And if I'm not followed?"

"Then I'll want to know," said Ivo Brightwater, "why a hundred-year-old book thinks it knows me better than I do."

Sera blinked. "How did you—"

"Lady," said the charm-seller, returning to the wooden bird with the air of a woman closing a door, "you walked up to my stall pale as whey, holding a pie like a shield, and asked about my eyebrow. Either you're the world's worst cutpurse or you've read something about me somewhere. I've survived twelve years in this city by knowing which questions to answer. Go on. Half past. Bring the book."

* * *

The vestry of Saint Aldric's smelled of candle wax and pigeons. Ivo arrived at half past precisely, slipping through the sticking door with the ease of someone who had clearly used it before, and Sera — who had spent the intervening hours alternately rereading the codex and talking herself out of the entire enterprise — spread *The Vellum Hours* open on a vestment chest and turned the lamp up.

Ivo looked at the sketch for a long time.

She didn't touch it. Sera noticed that — noticed how the charm-seller held her hands folded tight against her apron, as if the drawing might bite, or as if touching it might make it more true.

"That's me," Ivo said at last, quietly. "That's my stall. Those are my charms — the knot pattern on the third cord down, I invented that myself two years ago. Nobody sells that knot but me." She looked up. "Who drew this?"

"A man signing himself only as 'the Watcher at Hollowbridge.' He annotated a psalter for — I believe — the last six years of his life. Dates, names, sketches. Most of it's mundane until the final quarter, and then it stops being mundane very quickly." Sera turned pages with a scholar's careful hands. "Floods. Fires. The fall of the old king — he recorded that three days before it happened. And then the entries become... thematic. Repetitive. All about a gate, and sleepers beneath the citadel, and—" she hesitated, "—speakers."

"Speakers of what?" Ivo asked, in a voice carefully, deliberately flat.

Sera met her eyes. "Beasts. The old texts call them beast-speakers. The gift of speech with animals. It's why the prohibition laws exist — the official histories say beast-speakers started the last war. Called armies of wolves and ravens down on the free cities. That's the story every child learns."

"Yes," Ivo said. "That's the story." Something moved behind her eyes — grief, or guilt, or twelve years of swallowing both — and Sera, who had spent her life reading dead men's handwriting, found herself reading living silence just as fluently, and understanding, with a lurch, that she was sitting in a locked room with a criminal.

With a beast-speaker.

With the woman from the prophecy.

"The next passage," Sera said, because if she stopped to think about what she was doing she would stop doing it, and because terror shared promptly was terror halved, or possibly doubled, but at least honest. She turned to the jagged-inked page and read aloud:

"*On the night of the blood moon the gate will burn, and what sleeps beneath the citadel will hear it. Woe to the city that chained its speakers. Woe twice over to the woman whose name opens the door.*"

Silence. A pigeon resettled somewhere in the rafters.

"When is the blood moon?" Ivo asked.

Sera had done the calculation four times. She'd have done it a fifth, but her mouth was dry. "Nine days."

"Nine days." Ivo laughed — a small, cracked sound with no humor in it at all. "Nine days. Twelve years I've been careful. Twelve years of selling knots and stones and never saying one word where the walls could hear, and some dead farmer in Hollowbridge writes my name in a book, and now—" She stopped. Pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. "Now what? What does it mean, *whose name opens the door*? Opens it how? Opens it for *whom*?"

"I don't know," Sera admitted. "The Watcher's later entries degrade — his hand gets worse, the ink changes, I think he was dying when he wrote them. There may be more. There's a reference to a 'second voice' correcting him, but I haven't found it yet." She hesitated, then said the true thing, the scholar's confession: "Ivo. I've spent my career assuming prophecy was folklore with good posture. I told myself the sketch was coincidence while I was walking here, and I stopped believing it somewhere around the knot pattern. But I need you to understand what I'm certain of, versus what I merely fear."

"Tell me both."

"I'm certain the book is genuine, the dating is correct, and the sketch is of you. I fear everything else." Sera closed the codex gently, as if it might overhear. "And I fear something else besides. The Watcher wrote these entries in secret, but secrets leak. If I found this book in a basement, others could find copies. And there are men in this city paid very well to find exactly this kind of thing — names, faces, gifts."

Ivo had gone very still again. That cornered stillness. "Witch-finders."

"Witch-finders."

"They don't come to the night market," Ivo said. "Too many witnesses. Too much chaos. That's always been the rule — they sweep the rich quarters and the docks, and leave us alone because we're beneath notice." She was speaking faster now, thinking aloud, and Sera watched twelve years of careful architecture rearrange itself around a new fact. "But a named prophecy. A face. If someone informed—"

The church bell began to ring.

Not the hour. Not the half. An irregular, urgent clanging — the alarm pattern, the one Sera knew from drills she'd slept through — and beneath it, faint but unmistakable, came the whistle-calls of the city watch, three notes repeated, the signal every market vendor knew: *close your stalls. Wardens walking.*

Ivo was on her feet before the second clang. "That's not a routine patrol. Routine patrols don't ring the alarm bell." She grabbed Sera's wrist, and her grip was stronger than her slight frame suggested. "Which door did you use coming in?"

"The — the west sacristy—"

"Boots in the lane," Ivo breathed. Through the vestry's narrow window, grey shapes were moving along the street below — not two, not four, but eight of them, moving with purpose toward the market square, and at their center walked a figure Sera recognized from pamphlets and nightmares alike: tall, iron-haired, with a witch-finder's badge worked in silver on her breast. Captain Elske. The one they said could smell a lie through stone.

"They're not sweeping the rich quarters," Sera whispered. "They're coming *here*. To the market. To—"

"To me." Ivo said it simply, without self-pity, the way you'd report the weather, and something in Sera's chest broke and reformed around the shape of it. "Someone talked. Someone always talks." She turned, and her grey-green eyes fixed on Sera with terrible calm. "You came to me tonight. You can be gone by morning — university scholars travel, it's normal, nobody will question it. Go home, Vane. Forget my face, like I said."

"And the book?"

Ivo looked at *The Vellum Hours*, at her own face drawn a century ago, and her jaw tightened.

"Give it here," she said. "If they're burning my name out of the world, the least I can do is carry it myself."

But Sera found she was already shaking her head, already clutching the codex to her chest with both arms, already hearing herself say words she could not afterward account for:

"No. Absolutely not. I spent four months on this book. Besides—" and here her scholarly mind, that treacherous, faithful instrument, supplied the observation she least wanted, "—if they're searching for you, they'll search everyone connected to you, and a scholar who visited your stall tonight and then vanished will look like exactly what she is. Our best move is together, and *away*, and I know the sewers under Aldric Street better than any witch-finder alive, because I mapped them for a drainage thesis nobody read."

Ivo stared at her. "You mapped the sewers."

"For science."

"You're offering to flee the law with a wanted charm-seller based on a thesis *nobody read*."

"It was a very good thesis," said Sera Vane, with as much dignity as a woman could muster while a church alarm rang and armed men closed in around the only friend she'd made in eleven years. "Also, I have the book, and you have the face it's written about, and neither of us is leaving without the other. So. The sewers, Brightwater. Unless you'd rather wait for Captain Elske."

Below the window, a grey hood turned toward the church doors.

Ivo Brightwater took one long breath — the breath, Sera would think much later, of a woman setting down a weight she'd carried for twelve years and picking up a different one — and said:

"This way. And Vane? Whatever happens next — you should know the cat comes with us."

"...The *what*?"
