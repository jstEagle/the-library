## 3. Witch-Finders at the Door

The trouble with luck-charms, Ivo had long ago decided, was that they worked.

Not the charms themselves — she made those honestly enough, knotted thread and river-stone and a whispered word that meant nothing at all, or so her customers believed and so she let them. But the *selling* of them worked like a charm on her own nerves. There was a rhythm to market night: the lantern-light on wet cobbles, the smell of fried onions and tallow, old Marta haggling over a love-knot she'd bought three times already for three different grandsons, and Ivo smiling her careful smile, hands busy, mind empty of anything dangerous. For an hour or two each week, she was nobody. Just the charm girl from Tanner's Row who kept her head down and her voice soft and never, ever looked too long into anyone's eyes — because eyes were where the truth lived, and hers had a way of going strange when animals were near.

She was mid-haggle with a fishwife who wanted a storm-charm discounted because "the last one only half worked, we still got wet" when the horses screamed.

That was the thing about Highmoor under Lord Cassian. You learned to listen past the noise. Music, argument, a drunk singing off-key by the fountain — all of it faded into wallpaper. But fear in an animal carried like a bell through fog, and every horse tethered along the market row began screaming at once, high and rolling, the sound they made when fire came or wolves did.

Ivo's hands went still around the knot she was tying.

*Soldiers,* said the fear in the horses' blood, wordless but vivid as a picture held up to light. *Iron and dogs and men who smell of ash.*

"Charm not good enough for you either?" the fishwife demanded, misreading her silence entirely.

"Best charm in the market," Ivo said automatically, and then, because the fear was getting louder, closer, "though if you'll forgive me, mistress, I've just remembered I left my kettle on the stove."

"You haven't got a stove."

"I'm told it's very urgent."

She was already gathering her tray, fingers quick and steady the way they only ever were when panic sat on the other side of them. Around her the market went on laughing and haggling, oblivious, and Ivo felt the old familiar loneliness settle over her like a coat two sizes too small. Twelve years in this city. Twelve years of hearing terror before anyone else did, and never once being able to say so out loud.

Then someone grabbed her elbow.

She nearly dropped the tray. It was the scholar woman — the one from three nights ago, the one who'd stood at her stall far too long, asking odd questions in a voice pitched low, questions about her grandmother, about her mother's people, about whether Ivo had ever been *north*. She'd bought nothing. Scholars never bought anything; they just looked at you like you were a page they hadn't finished reading.

"You're Ivo Brightwater," the woman said now, breathless, spectacles fogged from the cold. Her grip on Ivo's arm was surprisingly strong for someone built like a bundle of scrolls. "You have to come with me. Now."

"I'm sorry, do I know—"

"There are witch-finders coming to this market. Tonight. For you." The woman swallowed. "There was an informant. A description. Your name. They'll be here in — I don't know how long. Minutes. Perhaps less."

The world did the thing it sometimes did, which was go very quiet and very clear, like the moment after ice cracks on a pond. Ivo heard herself say, calmly, "Who *are* you?"

"Sera Vane. University archives. I found your name in a book that's older than the city walls, and I know how that sounds, and I promise I will explain everything, but *not here*." Sera's eyes darted toward the mouth of the square, where the crowd had begun, at last, to feel the wrongness rippling through it — heads turning, conversations dying. "Please. I didn't bring your name to them. I need you to believe that."

And there it was — the question underneath the question. Ivo had spent twelve years becoming an excellent judge of faces, because she couldn't read minds but she could read everything else, and what she saw in Sera Vane's face wasn't triumph or greed or the flat satisfaction of a hunter closing a trap. It was fear. Raw, specific, guilt-edged fear, the kind a person wears when they've realized their cleverness has hurt someone.

Somewhere beyond the rooftops, a dog began to bay — not hunting-bay, but the eager, trained cry of a beast worked against its own nature, and beneath it Ivo felt something that turned her stomach: the dog's confusion, its misery, its terrible willingness.

"They're here," she said.

* * *

They went out through the fishmongers' lane, which was the only direction that smelled of escape rather than iron. Ivo knew the market's veins the way she knew the knots on her tray — twelve years of learning which alley narrowed, which gate stuck, which cellar door belonged to a widow who took bribes in pickled eels. Behind them the square erupted: boots, shouted orders, the crash of a stall going over, someone screaming a name that wasn't hers but might as well have been, because witch-finders rarely checked twice.

"The sewers," Ivo gasped, hauling Sera down a stairwell that stank of onions and old rain. "Don't ask. Don't gag. Walk fast."

"I read about the Highmoor drains," Sera said, with the tone of a woman clinging to scholarship the way other people clung to prayer. "Thirteenth century construction. Remarkable. Allegedly."

"Allegedly," Ivo agreed, and lifted the grate.

The dark below was total until Sera fumbled out a small hand-lantern, and by its trembling light they picked their way along the walkway beside the channel, water chuckling along below them with a sound that Ivo tried very hard not to interpret. That was the other burden of her gift, the one nobody warned you about: even in a sewer, everything had a voice. The rats chattered ceaselessly in the walls. The water muttered. Somewhere ahead, something small and blind sang to itself in the dark of the pipes, and Ivo fixed her eyes on her boots and thought about taxes instead.

"So," she said, mostly to keep from listening. "A book."

"A book," Sera echoed gratefully.

"With my face in it."

"With your — well. Not precisely your face. A sketch. Ink, quite degraded, the artist was no master. But the name beneath it is *Ivo Brightwater*, and the date written beside it—" Sera hesitated, and Ivo heard the hesitation the way she'd heard the horses scream. "The date is tomorrow."

They walked ten paces in silence. Water chuckled. Rats commented.

"That's impossible," Ivo said at last, conversationally, as though remarking on the weather.

"Yes," Sera agreed. "Entirely. Which is why I came to find you instead of reporting it, because I am a rational woman, and rational women verify their impossibilities before the witch-finders do it for them." She pushed her fogged spectacles up. "Except I appear to have verified it rather badly, since my inquiries are evidently what brought them to Tanner's Row in the first place. I asked after you at three shops. I was — thorough. Thoroughness is my worst habit."

"And your informant?"

"I don't know. Someone in the archive, perhaps. Someone watching me." Sera's laugh came out thin. "I study dead languages, Mistress Brightwater. Nobody watches scholars. We're the least dangerous people alive."

"You found my name in a prophecy," Ivo said. "You're dangerous enough."

Ahead, the tunnel forked, and Ivo stopped, because the dark ahead of them had changed texture. She couldn't have explained it to Sera — it was like explaining color to someone born without eyes — but the air itself had gone attentive. Listening. And then, from the junction of the pipes, two points of green light opened like doors.

"Oh no," Ivo said.

"What? What is it?"

"It's the cat."

* * *

The cat was a large, scarred, orange tomcat whom Ivo had been feeding illegally behind her lodgings for six years, on the grounds that he was bad-tempered, half-feral, and the closest thing she had to family. He appeared now at the tunnel fork with the unhurried confidence of a creature who owned every sewer in Highmoor and merely tolerated the city above it, and he sat down directly in their path, tail wrapped around his feet, and stared at Ivo with an expression she had never once seen him wear.

He was waiting for her to speak.

Cats always waited for that. It was the cruelest joke of her gift — that the animals who most wanted words were the ones she could least afford to give them. Six years of one-sided conversation, of murmured nonsense disguised as madness, of telling him her troubles in a whisper while he pretended to be asleep. Six years, and now he sat in a sewer with witch-finders somewhere overhead and a stranger holding the lantern, and he waited.

*Ivo,* said Sera nervously, "is there something—"

"Give me a moment," Ivo said, and knelt on the filthy walkway, and broke twelve years of discipline in a single breath.

*What are you doing here?*

The words weren't spoken. They never were. They arrived the way warmth arrives through a window — directly, wordlessly, a shape of meaning pressed against her mind. But this time the shape was different. Sharper. Older. As though the cat had been speaking to her all along in a language she'd refused to hear, and had finally raised his voice.

*Following you. Obviously.* The mental voice was dry as old paper and twice as judgmental. *Someone has to keep you alive. You have the survival instincts of a moth.*

"You can *talk*?" Ivo said aloud, forgetting herself entirely.

Sera made a small strangled sound. "To the cat. You're talking to the cat. And it — is it answering?"

*Of course I can talk,* said the cat. *I simply had nothing worth saying to a woman who spends six years calling me 'Mister Bristles' in a baby voice.*

"That was affectionate!"

*It was humiliating. Fortunately, I am a generous soul.* He rose, stretched elaborately, and looked back down the tunnel the way they'd come. His ears flattened. When he spoke again, the dryness had gone out of his voice, and what remained was something Ivo had never heard from any animal in her life: gravity. Weight. The tone of a creature delivering news it had carried a long way.

*Listen to me, charm-seller, because I will not enjoy repeating it. I am not what you think I am. None of us strays are. We are the leftovers of something older — the keepers' kin, scattered when the wardens went to sleep. And we have all felt it, these past months. Every rat in this city, every pigeon, every dog chained in Cassian's yards. Something beneath the citadel has begun to dream again.*

"Dream," Ivo repeated. Her mouth had gone dry. "Dreaming what?"

*Your name,* said the cat simply. *It dreams your name. And the witch-finders are hunting you, and the gate beneath the city is cracking, and you are standing in a sewer arguing about nicknames.* He flicked his tail toward the right-hand passage. *Now come along. The left tunnel floods at tide-turn, and I refuse to explain drowning to a woman who can hear me.*

For a moment Ivo didn't move. Terror and wonder were fighting for possession of her body, and neither was winning, and the result was that she simply stood in a thirteenth-century sewer being stared at by a scholar and a talking cat, thinking: *this is what it feels like, then. This is what the women in the old stories felt like, the ones who burned.*

*Ivo Brightwater,* the cat said, gentler now, in the exact tone she used on frightened horses. *Walk.*

She walked.

* * *

They emerged near the tannery district an hour later, through a drainage arch hidden behind a wall of blackberry bramble that Ivo had known about since she was nine years old and had never once had occasion to use. The night was colder up here, and quieter — the market raid had moved on, or finished, and somewhere across the city a watch-bell rang the hour with terrible indifference.

Sera sat down hard on a bollard, set the lantern between her feet, and removed her spectacles to clean them with hands that shook only slightly, which Ivo considered heroic.

"I have just," Sera said, "spent an hour following a woman and a cat through a sewer, based on a drawing in a book. I would like it noted that I was top of my year."

"Noted," Ivo said. "Why did you warn me?"

Sera looked up. In the lantern-light her face was pale and smudged and utterly honest. "Because whoever wrote that book wrote your name beside a burning gate and a blood moon, and I have spent three days telling myself it was coincidence, and then tonight I watched soldiers drag a fruit-seller off his own cart because he had the misfortune to be standing near your stall." She put her spectacles back on. "I don't know what you are, Ivo Brightwater. But I know what they think you are, and I could not stand in that square and let them be right about the only part that matters."

*Well said,* the cat observed, washing one paw. *For a hairless thing.*

"Did it just comment?" Sera demanded.

"He comments constantly," Ivo said. "Apparently he's been able to talk for six years and chose spite as the moment to mention it."

*Chose the moment you needed humility,* the cat corrected. He padded to the edge of the lantern-light and sat facing the dark, ears forward, and when he spoke again his voice had that grave weight in it once more, the weight of old things. *Rest here if you must. But not long. The finders have dogs, and the dogs are confused and unhappy, which makes them dangerous — a beast forced against its nature bites hardest at the wrong things. By morning they will have your scent from your stall, and by tomorrow night they will have your face on every gate in Highmoor.*

Ivo hugged her arms around herself. The market was gone. The stall was gone — the little tray of harmless knots, the careful smile, the whole small architecture of her hiding, collapsed in an evening. Twelve years of building a life out of silence, and it had taken one scholar and one informant to pull it down.

And yet.

She looked at Sera Vane, shivering on a bollard in the wrong part of town for the sake of a stranger. She looked at the scarred orange cat sitting sentinel in the dark, keeping watch over her the way he had, apparently, been keeping watch all along.

"You said the gate is cracking," Ivo said quietly. "Beneath the citadel. What's behind it?"

The cat was silent a long moment. When he answered, the words came slow, like stones turned over one at a time.

*Sleepers,* he said. *Old ones. Stone-made, oath-bound, set to guard this city back when the world was younger and crueler. Your kind woke them once, long ago — the speakers, the beast-folk, the ones your history books blame for the war. And your kind bound themselves to the gate so it could be woken again, if it ever had to be.* His green eyes found hers in the dark, unblinking. *The prophecy-book your scholar found is not the only copy of what's coming, charm-seller. The rats remember fragments. The birds sing pieces. And every scrap of every song agrees on one thing: the wardens are stirring, and they will rise for one voice only.*

The night held its breath. Somewhere far off, the watch-bell finished striking, and the last note hung in the cold air like a held chord.

"They want me," Ivo said. Not a question. She could feel it now, faintly, the way you feel weather changing — something vast turning over in its sleep beneath the city, murmuring a name it had been given centuries before she was born.

*They've been wanting you your whole life,* said the cat. *Tonight everyone else caught up.*

Sera had gone very still. "Ivo," she said carefully, "there's a line in the book. Under your name. I didn't tell you in the market because — because it seemed unkind, and I was hoping I'd mistranslated it." She pulled a folded page from inside her coat, ink-stained fingers trembling as she smoothed it open on her knee. "But I don't think I mistranslated it. The next line reads: *when the moon turns red as a wound, the gate shall know its keeper, and the keeper shall pay the gate's price.*"

Above the tannery roofs, through the ragged gap between two chimneys, a sliver of sky showed — and in it, already, the moon hung fat and pale, weeks yet from full, but climbing.

Toward red.

"Well," said Ivo Brightwater, whose entire strategy for surviving twelve years had been to be nobody, nowhere, nothing worth writing down, "it appears I'm somebody after all."

*Oh, you're somebody,* said the cat, rising, stretching, and setting off down the dark street with his tail high like a banner. *Come along, both of you. If we're going to break a prophecy, we'll want allies. I know a deserter who owes me a fish.*
