## 4. The Company of Strays

The tannery smelled like a crime against the nose.

Sera had catalogued a hundred terrible smells in her years in the archives — mold, rat droppings, the particular rot of books drowned in a cellar flood — but nothing had prepared her for three days in an abandoned tannery, where decades of rendered fat and lime and old hide had soaked into the very beams. The smell lived in her hair now. It lived in her teeth. She was fairly sure it would follow her to her grave, which, given recent events, might be sooner than she'd planned.

"You're making that face again," Ivo said. She sat cross-legged on a workbench near the broken skylight, the gray morning light catching the copper of her hair. The cat — who had not spoken since that night in the sewers, and whom Ivo insisted on calling nothing at all because "naming him makes him mine, and then I'll worry about him" — lay curled in her lap like a loaf of bread with opinions.

"I'm calculating," Sera said, "how long before we die of the smell instead of the witch-finders."

"Odji says the smell keeps people away. He calls it our first line of defense."

"Odji," said a dry voice from the doorway, "also says you two argue like sisters, and he'd know, having four." Odji came in balancing a tray of stolen breakfast rolls on his one hand with the ease of long practice, his sleeve pinned neatly at the wrist. He was perhaps fifty, with a locksmith's forearms and a face like a closed ledger. "Rolls. Two are stale. Don't fight over them."

Sera took a roll and tried not to think about how quickly she'd stopped questioning where Odji got things. That was the trouble with fugitives: morality became a luxury item, like soap.

Three days ago, this had been just her and Ivo, hiding in the dark under the tannery's drying racks while patrols walked the street outside, close enough that Sera could hear the creak of their armor. Three days ago, Ivo had whispered, *we can't do this alone*, and Sera — who had spent her whole life believing that alone was simply the correct way to be — had nodded.

Now there were five of them, counting the cat, and Sera still wasn't entirely sure how it had happened.

* * *

It had happened, mostly, through Bram.

They'd found him the second night, or rather he'd found them — a big man in a soldier's coat with the insignia torn off, standing in the tannery doorway holding a knife and bleeding from a wound in his side that had clearly gone bad. He'd looked at Ivo and said, "You're the charm woman. From the night market."

"And you're a deserter," Ivo had said, calm as anything, though Sera saw her hand drift toward her knife. "Cassian's army?"

"Was." Bram had swayed. "I'm not going back. And I'm not going to report you. I just need—" His legs had folded before he finished the sentence.

Odji had appeared out of the shadows ten minutes later, drawn by some instinct of his own, taken one look at the unconscious soldier, and said, "Well. This is either very stupid or very lucky," which Sera would learn was his response to nearly everything.

Bram had woken feverish, and for two days he'd lain on a pile of hides, sweating and muttering, while Maren — the last arrival, a quiet young woman with ink-stained fingers who'd been treating the poor in the river district until a patient's family asked one question too many about why her poultices worked better than they should — cleaned his wound and shook her head.

"It's infected deep," Maren told them on the third morning, in the low, apologetic voice she used for everything. "I can slow it. I can't stop it. Without a real healer, without clean instruments..." She didn't finish. She didn't have to. Everyone in the room understood the arithmetic.

"He saved my life once," Odji said quietly. "In the war, before. He doesn't remember me. Doesn't matter. He pulled me out of a burning granary when I was a boy in the quartermaster's train." He looked at his pinned sleeve, then away. "I'd rather not do the sums on what I owe him."

So they all stood around a dying deserter in a stinking tannery, and nobody knew what to do, and Sera thought: *this is what the end of the world looks like up close. Not armies. Just four strangers and a fever.*

Then Ivo knelt beside Bram, and did something none of them expected.

* * *

Later, Sera would replay it a dozen times, trying to understand exactly what she'd seen.

Ivo had put both hands flat on Bram's wound — over the filthy bandage, no ceremony, no incantation — and gone very still. Her eyes had changed. That was the only way Sera could describe it. They hadn't glowed, nothing so useful; they'd simply *focused*, the way a hawk's eyes focus, on something no one else could see.

And then she'd made a sound. Low, almost subvocal, a rising-and-falling hum that wasn't quite a word and wasn't quite a song. The cat had lifted his head. Somewhere outside, dogs had started barking — and then, strangely, stopped, all together, as if listening.

"What is she doing?" Maren whispered.

"I don't know," Sera said honestly. "But I don't think it's healing."

She was right. It took Sera another hour, watching, to work out what she was actually seeing — and by then it was over.

Because Ivo wasn't healing Bram. Ivo was *negotiating*.

"The infection," Ivo explained afterward, gray-faced and shaking, sitting on the floor with her back against the workbench while Bram slept a true sleep for the first time in days. "Fever's not a thing, exactly. It's... a war. Tiny soldiers fighting tiny soldiers. Most healers try to kill one side. I just talked to the ones already winning and asked them to hurry."

Maren stared at her. "You *talked* to his fever."

"To the small lives in it. They're not clever. They're like... like pigeons. You just have to give them somewhere to go." Ivo rubbed her face with both hands. "It's not healing. Healing's outlawed too, so it hardly matters, but — I want to be accurate. What I am is a beast-speaker. All beasts. Even the ones too small to see."

The silence that followed was the longest of Sera's life.

She watched each of them take it differently. Odji went still in the way of a man recalculating every debt he'd ever owed. Maren's hand crept toward her own chest, unconsciously, as if guarding something. And Sera felt the scholar in her sit up and start screaming questions even as the fugitive in her started screaming warnings.

It was Maren who broke the silence. She stepped forward, knelt across from Ivo, and rolled up her own sleeve. On her forearm, faint as watermarks, were lines of pale scarring — old burns, in patterns too regular to be accidents.

"My gift isn't much," Maren said. "Things I make with my hands heal faster than they should. Poultices. Splints. Bread, sometimes, if someone's starving — it feeds better than bread should. But every time I push it, it takes something back. It's why I hid. It's why everyone hides." She looked up, and her eyes were wet and furious at once. "You just saved a man's life with a gift that gets people burned. So no. I'm not horrified. I'm *relieved*. Do you know what it's like, thinking you're the only monster in the city?"

"Not a monster," Ivo said automatically.

"No," Maren agreed, with a small, fierce smile. "Neither are you."

Odji cleared his throat. When he spoke, his voice was gruffer than usual, which Sera was learning meant it mattered. "Twelve years I've fixed locks for smugglers and turned a blind eye to half the city's sins, and never once asked myself what I'd do if the day came. Well." He flexed his single hand. "Day's come. I'm in. Whatever 'in' means. But I want it said plainly: I'm not brave. I'm just done being careful."

"That's what brave is," Ivo said softly. "Mostly."

Sera said nothing. She was aware, distantly, that all eyes had turned to her — the scholar, the stranger, the one whose book had started all of this. Her whole life she'd believed knowledge was safest kept, that every secret shared was a secret endangered. She thought of the prophecy page hidden in the lining of her coat, the sketch of Ivo's face dated tomorrow, the words about blood moons and burning gates.

"I found your name in a century-old book," she said finally. "Before any of this. Before I ever met you. I brought the danger to your door, and then I brought you down a sewer, and now you've healed a soldier for people you met three days ago." She exhaled. "I should warn you that everything I touch tends to catch fire, metaphorically speaking. If you're wise, you'll send me away."

Ivo reached over and squeezed her hand. "Good thing none of us is wise," she said. "We're strays. We keep each other."

* * *

By evening, Bram was well enough to sit up and eat, and well enough to be deeply suspicious.

He listened to the whole story with the flat expression of a man being recruited into something, arms crossed over his chest, wound and all. "Let me get this straight," he said. "A charm-seller who talks to animals. A scholar with a forbidden book. A healer who poisons herself helping people. A thief with one hand. And a cat." He looked at the cat, who was washing his paw with tremendous dignity. "And you want to fight Lord Cassian."

"We don't want to fight anyone," Ivo said. "We want to *survive* Cassian. There's a difference."

"There is," Bram admitted. "I've been on the other end of it. His witch-finders don't investigate. They confirm. They decide you're guilty and then find the proof." He was quiet a moment. "I deserted because I was ordered to burn a village that had done nothing. I told myself I'd run far enough and disappear. Then I heard rumors about a charm-woman the finders were hunting, and I thought — maybe she knows a way out. Maybe there's a road that doesn't lead through Cassian." He looked around the circle of them, this ragged, impossible circle, and something in his shoulders loosened for the first time. "There isn't one, is there."

"No," Odji said cheerfully. "But we might build one."

That night, Sera couldn't sleep. She climbed to the broken skylight with the prophecy book and read by moonlight, and the cat came and sat with her, warm against her hip, and after a while Ivo climbed up too.

"Any closer to understanding it?" Ivo asked.

"Marginally. The script shifts dialects halfway through, which means at least two hands wrote it. Possibly more." Sera traced the lines of text with one finger. "Prophecies aren't usually collaborative. Someone corrected this one. Changed things."

"Changed them how?"

"I don't know yet. That's what frightens me." Sera looked at her — this woman she'd known less than a week, who fed stray cats and negotiated with fevers and kept a mask so polished most people never saw beneath it. "Someone wanted the future to say something different than it originally did. And I intend to find out what, and why."

Below them, faint through the broken skylight, came the sound of the night patrol passing — boots on cobblestones, the rattle of a witch-finder's iron chains. Five fugitives froze, out of habit, in five separate corners of the dark.

Then Ivo laughed — quiet, exhausted, real. "Listen to us. We scatter like pigeons and we don't even notice anymore."

"Pigeons," Sera mused, "at least have the decency to fly in the same direction."

"Give us time," Ivo said. "We're new at this. Being a company, I mean. Give us time."

Far off across the rooftops, past the walls of Highmoor, the sky had begun to change — a thin ring of red at the edge of the coming moon, barely visible, like a bruise forming. Sera saw it and felt the old dread stir in her chest, the scholar's dread, the certainty that the book was right and the calendar was turning.

The blood moon fair was eleven days away.

And somewhere in the citadel above the city, she was certain, plans were already being drawn that would decide whether any of them lived to see it.
