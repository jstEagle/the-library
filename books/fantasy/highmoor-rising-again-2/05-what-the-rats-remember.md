## 5. What the Rats Remember

The tannery smelled like a hundred years of regret, and Ivo had come to love it the way you love a coat that doesn't fit but keeps you warm.

She'd claimed the loft above the old lime pits as her own corner of it — a nest of sacking and straw where she could curl up with the cat, whom Sera had insisted on naming Professor on the grounds that he looked like he held strong opinions about other people's work. Professor currently occupied her pillow with the entitlement of a creature who had never once been wrong about anything.

Below her, the company was arguing about dinner. This was, she had learned, what the company did instead of sleeping.

"We are not eating the salt pork," Bram was saying, in the tone of a man who had defended fortifications with less conviction. "We are *saving* the salt pork."

"For what?" Odji asked. "A festival? A coronation? You want to be buried with it?"

"For when things get bad."

"Things got bad three weeks ago, deserter. That's why we're in a tannery."

"Which is exactly why we save the pork."

Ivo smiled into the dark and let their voices wash over her. It was strange how quickly noise had become comfort. For twelve years, silence had been her craft — the silence of never speaking to animals where anyone could see, of keeping her eyes down, of being nobody so thoroughly that even the witch-finders' lists forgot to include her. She had built her whole life out of quiet, and now here she was, lying in a ruined tannery above five strangers, listening to them fight about pork, and feeling — for the first time since her mother died — *warm*.

That warmth was exactly why tonight's errand scared her.

Because tonight she was going to tell them.

* * *

It had started, as most dangerous things did, with rats.

Rats were the best informants in Highmoor, if you didn't mind working with creatures who thought in smells and grudges rather than words. Their memories came to Ivo in fragments — a flash of a corridor here, a taste of grain there, stitched together like a quilt made by someone drunk. But they were everywhere, and Lord Cassian's soldiers fed them magnificently from the scraps of his supply depots, which meant the rats knew the usurper's logistics better than his own quartermasters did.

For two nights she'd sat in the cellar of the tannery, hands loose on her knees, letting the local colony climb over her boots while she listened.

What they remembered had kept her awake ever since.

There were the supply tunnels, first — that part was useful, almost cheerful. A whole hidden network running under the river district, built generations ago for merchants who'd wanted to move goods without paying the bridge tolls. Cassian's engineers had reopened them. Grain went down one way; weapons crates went down another. The rats had ridden in the wagons. The rats had opinions about the quality of the grain.

But underneath the tunnels, deeper than any merchant's greed had ever dug, something else was happening. The rats near the citadel hill spoke of it in the only way they could: fear without shape. A memory of dust falling from ceilings in long gray curtains. A memory of a sound — not heard with ears but felt in the teeth — a deep, slow grinding, like the city itself being opened with a spoon. And threaded through all of it, faint as a scent on the wind, something old. Something that made even the rats, who feared nothing smaller than an owl, huddle together and refuse to go further down.

The oldest rat in the colony — a scarred female missing half an ear, who Ivo privately thought of as the Duchess — had carried one last fragment up from the deep galleries before refusing to return. In it, Ivo had seen torchlight on wet stone, men in Cassian's colors prying at a wall, and carved into that wall, half-buried in centuries of mineral stain:

*A beast. Enormous. Stone. Its mouth open.*

And beneath it, worn nearly smooth by time, letters in a script older than the city.

Ivo knew that script. Every child in Highmoor knew it, or used to — it ran along the base of the old statues in Founder's Square, the ones the usurper had wrapped in scaffolding "for restoration." Her mother had taught it to her in whispers, tracing the shapes on a steam-fogged window: *the wardens' script*, the old tongue of the beast-speakers, back when beast-speakers had been something other than a word for *monster*.

The gate under the citadel wasn't a rumor. It was real, and Cassian was digging toward it, and Ivo was the only person alive who understood what that meant — because understanding required knowing what she was, and telling anyone what she was had been, for twelve years, the single rule of her survival.

She lay in the dark and listened to Odji lose the pork argument and thought: *if I can't trust them, I'll die alone anyway. And if I can trust them, then hiding this makes me a liar wearing a friend's face.*

Professor opened one eye, as if to say the decision had been obvious for days and he was embarrassed it had taken her this long.

"All right," Ivo said softly. "All right."

* * *

She called them together after supper — such as supper was — by lighting the good candle instead of the stub, which in their economy counted as ceremony.

They arranged themselves around the cold fire pit with the wary ease of people who had each survived something by reading rooms quickly. Bram sat with his arms crossed, wound-healed and restless. Odji perched on an overturned lime barrel, whittling a lock mechanism out of scrap wood purely to have something to do with his hands. Maren folded herself small against the wall, always positioned nearest the door, always positioned nearest whoever seemed most likely to need healing. Sera arrived last, carrying the prophecy book wrapped in oilcloth against her chest like a child, and settled beside Ivo with the particular attention of a scholar who could smell a primary source coming.

Even Professor attended, seated on the barrel beside Odji with the gravity of a chairing official.

"I found something," Ivo said. "Under the city. And before I tell you what it is, I have to tell you something else, and I need you to let me finish before anyone decides anything."

Bram's arms uncrossed slightly. That was as welcoming as Bram got.

"You all know what I sell. Sold. Luck-charms. Harmless little things — knotted thread, river stones, nothing that would interest a witch-finder." She turned her hands over, palms up. They were a charm-seller's hands, ink-stained and rope-scarred, utterly unremarkable, and she made herself look at them instead of at their faces. "The charms work because I ask very small favors. From animals. Rats, mostly. Birds, sometimes. Once, memorably, a goat."

Silence. The candle flame leaned in the draft.

"The law calls it beast-speaking," Ivo said. "The witch-finders call it the root-taint. My mother called it a gift right up until they took her for it, which is why I spent twelve years making sure no living soul knew I had it. I talk to animals. I've talked to animals my whole life. And for the past two nights I've been talking to the rats under this city, and they've shown me what Cassian is doing beneath the citadel, and I'm going to tell you about that now, and you can decide afterward whether you want a beast-speaker in your company or whether I should take Professor and disappear before dawn."

She made herself stop talking. Her heart was going like a drummer boy's.

Maren moved first — not away, but closer, settling onto the bench at Ivo's side as though taking a seat in a sickroom. "You said the charms worked," she said quietly. "On the cart-horse, market night. I saw you. I thought I'd imagined it."

"You didn't imagine it."

"Hm," said Maren, in the satisfied tone of a healer whose diagnosis has been confirmed.

Odji set down his whittling knife. "The goat," he said. "I want to hear about the goat."

"That's your question? Not — the treason, the outlawry, the—"

"Oh, the treason's obvious, we're all neck-deep in it. I want to know what the goat said."

"It told me where its owner hid the tax money. Under the third floorboard of the coop. Goats hold grudges, Odji. Never cheat a goat."

Odji nodded slowly, as though receiving intelligence of strategic value. "Noted."

Bram hadn't moved. He was staring at Ivo with an expression she couldn't read, and her stomach tightened — the deserter, the soldier, the man whose whole career had been built on hunting down exactly what she was—

"My sister," Bram said finally, "could do it. Small things. Calmed the geese, found lost lambs. Our village called her blessed until the law changed, and then they called her something else, and my family didn't defend her because defending her would have been admitting we'd known." His jaw worked. "I've spent eleven years being ashamed of that. So no, Brightwater. I don't want you to disappear before dawn. I want to know what the rats showed you."

Ivo discovered her eyes were burning and pretended very hard that they weren't.

Sera, meanwhile, had gone pale in the way she went pale when scholarship and dread shook hands. "The deep galleries," she said. "You said the rats won't go below a certain level. What did they feel?"

"Fear," Ivo said. "Old fear. And I saw the wall they're digging toward. There's carving on it — stone beasts, huge ones, mouths open. And writing in the warden-script. The same as the statues in Founder's Square."

Sera was already unwrapping the prophecy book with shaking fingers. "Show me — no, wait, I've seen it, I've *seen* it—" She flipped pages to her charcoal rubbing, the sketch of the gate she'd copied from the book's final plate weeks ago, and laid it on the floor between them all.

Stone beasts. Mouths open. The same worn script beneath.

Nobody spoke for a moment. Even Professor leaned forward to inspect the rubbing, which Ivo chose to interpret as scholarly rigor.

"They're not digging for treasure," Ivo said. "Or they don't know they're not. There's a gate under the citadel, and it's real, and whatever's behind it has been asleep for a hundred years, and Cassian's engineers are a few weeks of spoiling and shoring away from waking it up without knowing what they're doing." She looked around the circle — the deserter, the locksmith, the healer, the scholar, the cat. Five faces that should have scattered at the word *beast-speaker* and hadn't. "The rats also gave me the tunnels. All of them. Every supply route, every cache, every choke point between the river district and the citadel hill. If we're going to survive this usurper, I can draw you a map of his veins."

"And the gate?" Sera asked softly.

Ivo looked down at the rubbing, at the open mouths of carved beasts that had guarded her city for a century longer than anyone remembered why.

"The gate," she said, "is why I'm still alive. I think it's why any of us are. And I think Cassian is about to knock on a door that was locked for a very good reason."

The candle guttered. Somewhere far below them, through forty feet of clay and stone, Ivo felt — faintly, the way you feel weather coming in an old scar — the deep slow grinding continue.

And beneath it, so soft she might have imagined it, something vast turned over in its sleep and whispered a name.

Hers.

* * *

Later, when the others had banked the fire and curled into their bedrolls, Sera stayed sitting beside Ivo in the ember-light, the prophecy book closed on her knees.

"You waited," Sera said. "Twelve years, and three weeks with us, and you waited."

"I was waiting to see if you'd get each other killed first. It narrows the field."

Sera laughed — a real laugh, tired and bright — and bumped her shoulder against Ivo's. "For what it's worth, I'm glad you told us. And I'm glad you didn't tell the goat about us. Goats gossip."

"So do scholars."

"Mm. But we cite our sources."

Above them, in the rafters, Professor watched the door with his tail curled around his feet, and Ivo fell asleep listening to the rats below dream their broken dreams of torchlit stone — and to something older, deeper, patient as mountains, whispering her name like a hand reaching for a key it had dropped a hundred years ago.

By morning, she'd have the map drawn. By week's end, Sera said, they'd need to see the fair crowds for cover to move.

The blood moon was coming.
