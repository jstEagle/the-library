## 6. The Blood Moon Fair

Sera Vane had spent eleven years in the university archives learning to be invisible, and she had never once imagined the skill would save her life. Invisible was a thing you became by wearing grey, by speaking softly, by having opinions about marginalia that no one wanted to hear. It was not, she had assumed, a survival trait. And yet here she was, three weeks into hiding in a derelict tannery with four strangers and a cat, discovering that the most dangerous thing she'd ever done was walk into a fair wearing someone else's badge.

"Stop fidgeting," Odji murmured beside her. He had one hand — his left, broad and scarred, missing two fingers besides — and he used it now to adjust the satchel strap across her shoulder with the brisk efficiency of a man dressing a mannequin. "Archivists don't fidget. Archivists are bored. Bored is safe."

"I *am* bored," Sera said. "I'm bored of being lectured by a locksmith."

"A locksmith who made your credentials," Odji said, tapping the wax seal on the letter of introduction tucked into her breast pocket. It bore the sigil of Cassian's Office of Records, rendered so faithfully that Sera had felt a small, professional envy when she first saw it. "You're welcome, by the way."

The blood moon hung low over Highmoor's eastern quarter, swollen and rust-colored, and beneath it the fairgrounds blazed with a hundred lanterns. Lord Cassian's fair — announced by criers for a fortnight, *a celebration of the city's renewal under its rightful lord* — had swallowed the old harvest market whole. There were roast-chestnut carts and a fortune-teller's tent (Sera noted, with grim amusement, that the fortune-teller would be arrested within the hour if anyone checked her license), acrobats, a pen of fighting dogs, and everywhere the red-and-black livery of Cassian's soldiers moving through the crowd like oil through water.

And somewhere in the records pavilion at the fair's center sat the thing they had come for: the excavation ledgers. Cassian's engineers kept their plans close, but the fair required paperwork — supply manifests, labor rosters, requisition orders — and paperwork, Odji had said, always came home to sleep somewhere. The pavilion's field archive held copies. Copies were enough.

"You're certain about the dog?" Sera asked.

Bram appeared at her elbow, out of his deserter's uniform and into a laborer's coat, looking enormous and uncomfortable, like a bear invited to a dinner party. "She's certain about the dog," he said. "I'm the one who has to hold it."

"The dog is essential," Ivo said.

Sera turned. Ivo Brightwater stood at the edge of the lantern light in a market-woman's shawl, holding a covered basket, looking — as she always did — like a person assembled from other people's expectations: harmless, forgettable, pleasant. Only her eyes moved constantly, tracking things Sera couldn't see.

"Explain it again," Bram said. "Slowly. For me."

"The hounds at the gate are trained to alert on fear-scent," Ivo said. "But dogs gossip. If one of them tells the others we're boring, we're boring." She lifted the basket lid an inch. Inside, a pie steamed. It smelled, genuinely, magnificent. "Maren's recipe. Liver and bacon. No witchcraft involved, unless you count butter."

"And the dogs will just... eat it?"

"One of them will. The big one with the cropped ear — she's the one the others listen to. She's been fed garbage rations since spring and she's furious about it." Ivo's voice went soft, the way it did when she slipped into that other listening. "She says the handler skims their meat allotment. She says if we're friends, she'll tell the others the crowd smells like nothing worth barking at."

Sera watched her, as she always watched her, with that double vision she couldn't shake: the woman she knew — dry, careful, secretly kind — and the sketch in the prophecy book, inked a century ago, dated to a day that had already come and gone. *The brightwater girl stands at the burning gate.* They hadn't reached any gates yet. But the moon overhead was the color of a wound, and Sera's hands wouldn't quite stay warm.

"Bram," Ivo said, handing over the basket, "be charming."

"I've killed men," Bram said, taking it.

"Yes, but have you complimented a dog?"

* * *

The plan, such as it was, had been built across six nights of argument around the tannery's single candle, and it went like this:

Odji would pick the pavilion's back lock — a Corvane pattern, which he claimed insulted him personally. Sera would enter through the front as an archivist sent from the citadel to audit the fair's requisitions, because no one questioned paper, and paper questioning paper was the most boring event in the world. Bram would linger near the hound line with a pie and a laborer's slouch. Maren stayed home, tending the tannery and pretending not to worry, which fooled no one. And Ivo —

Ivo would stand in the middle of the fair and listen.

"Animals are weather," she'd told them, the night they planned it, turning her cup in her hands. "You can't ask a sparrow what time the patrol changes. But sparrows know where cats are, and cats know where rats are nervous, and rats know where boots come from. You read it all together, the way a sailor reads clouds. Give me an hour in that crowd and I'll know every patrol route on the grounds without asking anyone anything."

"It's also the most dangerous thing you could do," Sera had said.

"Everything is the most dangerous thing," Ivo had said, with a tired smile. "That's rather the situation."

Now, walking toward the records pavilion with her forged letter and her best bored expression, Sera felt the truth of it humming under the fair's noise. She passed the hound line and saw Bram crouched by the fence, offering the pie with the stiff dignity of a man making a formal apology to a large animal. The cropped-eared hound took the entire pie in one motion, delicately, like a duchess accepting a bribe, and then leaned against the fence and sighed.

*Good,* Sera thought. *One part working.*

The pavilion was a long canvas hall lit by oil lamps, lined with trestle tables stacked in neat, hateful order. A clerk with ink-stained cuffs looked up as she entered.

"Office of Records," Sera said, presenting the letter. "Audit of fair requisitions. I'll need the current manifests, the labor rosters, and access to the field archive for cross-reference." She let boredom seep into every syllable. "It will take some hours. You needn't stay."

The clerk looked at the seal, looked at her face, and visibly decided that this was someone else's problem. "The archive's through the back partition," he said. "Mind the dust."

*Mind the dust.* Sera nearly laughed. Eleven years of archives, and the password to all of them was always the same.

Through the partition, past shelves of requisition forms, she found the field archive: a locked iron-banded cabinet against the rear wall, exactly where Odji's information said it would be. On the far side of that canvas wall, faintly, she heard a scrape of metal. Odji, already at work from the outside. She positioned herself at the shelf nearest the partition, pulled out a manifest at random, and began to read.

It was worse than she'd expected. Grain shipments routed away from the river districts. Timber quantities that made no sense for road repair. And page after page of labor requisitions for the *citadel underworks* — miners, masons, engineers — signed off by a name she didn't recognize, in a hand that pressed hard enough to tear the paper.

*Cassian isn't repairing anything,* she thought. *He's digging.*

A cheer went up outside, distant, from the direction of the acrobat stage. Then another sound threaded under it — a low, rising murmur from the animal pens, dogs and geese and something larger, a ripple of unease passing through the fair's creatures like wind through wheat.

Sera's stomach tightened. She glanced at the partition. The scraping continued, steady, patient. Odji was nearly through.

* * *

Ivo stood by the chestnut cart with a paper cone of roasted nuts she had no intention of eating, and read the weather.

It was beautiful, in the way storms are beautiful. The fair's animals formed a second fair, invisible, layered over the first: the hounds drowsing fat and content along the fence line (Bram's pie had been, by the sound of the big female's mental purring, a diplomatic triumph); the carthorses dozing; the geese patrolling their pen with the self-importance of minor officials. Above it all the swifts cut the dusk sky, and the swifts were her high clouds — they showed her the whole shape of the grounds at once, the flow of crowds, the dark knots where soldiers gathered.

Three patrols, rotating on a quarter-hour. Gate guards bored. Officers' tent: wine, laughter, nothing sharp. All of it calm, all of it readable, and Ivo stood in the middle of it letting the currents move through her and thinking, not for the first time, how strange it was that the gift she'd hidden for twelve years fit so naturally around the people she loved. That was the word, wasn't it. She tested it again, carefully, the way you test ice. *Loved.* Sera bent over her papers behind the canvas. Odji's pick whispering in the lock. Bram feeding state secrets to a hound. Maren at home, worrying loudly at a kettle.

The cat sat on a barrel nearby, washing one paw, projecting at maximum volume: *You're smiling. Stop smiling. Smiling people get remembered.*

"I'm not smiling," Ivo murmured into her chestnuts.

*Your face is doing a thing. Do the boring thing instead.*

Ivo arranged her features into market-night blandness, and had just achieved it when the weather changed.

It came from the pens first — the geese going silent all at once, which geese never did — then the horses stamping, then the swifts wheeling up off the rooftops in a single startled sheet. And threading through it, from the direction of the west gate, a smell in the animals' minds that Ivo had learned to dread above all others: a cold, clean, chemical wrongness. Witch-finder's oil. The stuff they anointed their instruments with, the stuff that stung her gift like frostbite.

*Finder,* the big hound thought, and even her contentment curdled. *Two of them. Walking slow. Looking at faces.*

Ivo set down the chestnuts. She moved through the crowd without hurrying, because hurrying got you remembered, angling toward the pavilion, counting in her head. Two finders, west gate, walking the rows. Behind them, four soldiers. Not a raid — a sweep. Someone had marked the fair for a look-over, or perhaps someone had talked. Either way, the arithmetic was simple: Sera and Odji were inside a locked room with one exit, and the exit was about to acquire an audience.

She reached the pavilion's rear corner, where the canvas met the supply fence, and pressed her palm flat against it. "Sera," she breathed. "Finders. West side, coming down the vendor row. Minutes, maybe less. Get out the back."

There was a pause, then Sera's voice, low and controlled: "Odji's still in the lock."

"Tell him to hurry."

"He'll say hurry insults the lock."

"*Insult it faster—*" Ivo stopped. The swifts had wheeled again, and through their hundred eyes she saw why: the finders had stopped. Not at random. At the fortune-teller's tent, dragging its owner out by the wrist, and the crowd was pulling back in a wide, frightened circle, and the finders were working — reading her, testing her, the way they did before the arrest became a spectacle.

Every eye in the fair was turning toward the tent. Every guard too.

*Now,* the weather said. *Move while everyone looks.*

Behind the canvas, metal scraped, clicked, and gave a small triumphant chime. Through the gap in the partition Sera emerged with a leather document case hugged to her chest, pale as milk, eyes enormous. Odji slid out after her, relocking the cabinet behind him with a flourish only he could appreciate.

"Lovely evening for theft," he whispered.

"West side," Ivo said. "We go east, through the livestock lanes, split at the cider stall, regroup at the tannery. Don't run. Running gets you remembered."

They walked. It was the longest walk of Sera's life, forty paces through a festival with stolen plans against her ribs, while somewhere behind them a fortune-teller screamed denials and the crowd made the low hungry noise crowds make. Ivo kept her eyes forward and her gift wide, reading the lane ahead through a stable cat's lazy half-attention: clear, clear, clear—

Then the cat's mind flinched, and Ivo looked up, and Captain Elske was standing ten feet away, looking directly at her.

* * *

Later, Sera would replay that moment a hundred times, searching for what gave them away. Nothing had. That was the horror of it. Ivo had done nothing wrong — a market woman glancing up at a soldier was not a crime — and yet Elske stood there in her grey coat with her finder's insignia dull as lead, and her gaze settled on Ivo's face with the particular attention of a person solving a puzzle.

"Evening," Elske said.

"Evening, Captain," Odji said smoothly, touching his cap. "Lovely fair."

"Mm." Elske didn't look at him. She was looking at Ivo, and Ivo was looking back with perfect, empty pleasantness, and Sera understood with sick clarity that the captain was doing exactly what Ivo did — reading a face for what moved underneath it. "Enjoying the festivities?"

"First chance we've had," Sera heard herself say, stepping slightly forward, archivist's badge catching the lantern light. "We're from the Records Office. Auditing." She let the word fall with all its natural power to kill conversation.

Something flickered in Elske's eyes. Not belief. Filing. Sera watched the moment happen — watched the captain decide, for reasons Sera couldn't see, to let the file stay open rather than closed.

"Of course," Elske said. "Carry on." And then, as they turned: "The tall one. The market woman. What's her name?"

The question hung in the air like a dropped knife.

"Petra," Ivo said, without turning around. Her voice was light, easy, a stranger's voice. "Petra Marsh. From Tanner's Row."

"Tanner's Row," Elske repeated pleasantly. "I'll remember that."

They walked on. They did not run. They passed the cider stall, split as planned, and Sera forced herself to stroll the long way to the tannery with the document case sweating against her heart, and only when the door closed behind her and Maren's worried face appeared did her legs finally inform her that they were done pretending.

She sat down hard on a curing bench. Odji laid the case on the table and opened it, and there they were — the excavation plans, the underworks surveys, the warden-gate drawings, real and creased and smelling of lamp oil. Around the table, breathless, they laughed the shaky laughter of the recently terrified, and Maren poured something medicinal into cups, and for a few minutes it was triumph, pure and simple.

Then Ivo came through the door last, and stopped, and Sera saw her face in the lamplight and the triumph died.

"She marked me," Ivo said quietly. "Not with ink. With attention. She'll pull the Tanner's Row rolls tomorrow. She'll find there's no Petra Marsh." She sat down slowly, and the cat flowed into her lap, pressing close. "Twelve years, and I was careful for all of them. One night."

"That's not true," Sera said. "You were careful tonight. It nearly worked."

"Nearly." Ivo looked at her hands. "Do you understand what happens next? She doesn't report a name. She reports a *face*. Faces go on lists, and lists go to every gate in the city, and every finder learns it the way you learn a page of marginalia." She looked up, and there was something in her expression Sera had never seen there before — not fear exactly, but the moment fear becomes a decision. "I can't sell charms in the morning market anymore. I can't walk home the usual ways. The life I had in this city — the small one, the hidden one — it's over as of tonight."

No one spoke. Bram's fists were clenched. Maren had gone very still. Odji studied the ceiling beams with the intensity of a man checking them for structural failure.

"So," Ivo said at last, and something in her voice shifted — grief folding itself away into resolve, the way she folded everything away. "We should probably look at those plans."

They bent over the documents together, heads close, and Sera found herself watching Ivo more than the pages. The excavation drawings spread out under the lamp: the citadel underworks, the descending shafts, and at the bottom, rendered in an engineer's precise hand, a gate. Cracked. Half-buried. Surrounded by annotations in Cassian's hard-pressing orders — *stabilize, contain, report nothing*.

*The brightwater girl stands at the burning gate,* the prophecy said, and here was the gate, and here was the girl, and between her and it lay a captain who now carried her face in her memory like a coin she intended to spend.

Outside, the blood moon climbed higher over Highmoor, and somewhere across the city a list was already growing longer by one name that didn't exist — and one face that very much did.
