## 7. Maren's Quiet Fire

Maren had learned long ago that the body told the truth before the mouth did. She could read a lie in a tightened jaw, a fever in the glassiness of an eye, a grief in the way a man held his shoulders like a coat too heavy to wear. She had spent eleven years reading bodies in a city that would have hanged her for what her hands could do, and so she knew — with the same quiet certainty she felt when setting a broken bone — that the fire in her chest was not going out.

It was coming *in*.

She sat on the tannery roof in the blue hour after sunset, where the smell of old hides had faded into something almost like clean stone, and pressed two fingers beneath her collarbone. There it was. The warmth. Not the warmth of health — she knew that one intimately, the banked coals of a working body — but something brighter, hungrier, like a candle burning down from both ends at once.

Every healing took a little of her. She had known that since she was nine years old and mended a sparrow's shattered wing and slept for two days afterward while her mother wept over her and called it a wasting sickness. She had known it at fourteen, when she closed a fishmonger's gushing wound on the docks and his gratitude bought her family a winter of free cod, and she paid for it in a month of fevers no physician could explain. She had known it every year since, and every year she had done the arithmetic anyway, because what was the alternative? Letting people bleed?

But arithmetic was different when you did it honestly.

*Three months,* she thought, testing the words the way she'd test a poultice against skin. *Maybe four, if I'm careful. Six if I stop entirely.* And she laughed — a small, startled sound, because six months of doing nothing, of watching wounds fester and bones knit crooked and children burn with fever while she sat on her hands — six months of that would kill her faster than any gift ever could.

Below her, through the tannery's sagging floorboards, she could hear the company of strays being a company of strays. Odji was arguing with Bram about the best way to rig the back door to scream if anyone touched it — "A lock should *whisper*, deserter, not announce us to the whole ward" — and Bram was arguing back with the patient stubbornness of a man who had once been responsible for forty soldiers' lives and couldn't seem to retire the habit. Sera's voice threaded between them, dry as good paper, refereeing. And Ivo —

Ivo was laughing.

Maren closed her eyes and listened to it. That was the thing she hadn't planned for, back when this had all been strangers sharing a leaky roof out of necessity: the laugh. Ivo Brightwater laughed like someone who had only recently remembered how, a little rusty, a little surprised at herself, and every time she did it, something in Maren's chest that had nothing to do with dying fire went soft and warm.

Which made the conversation she was about to have considerably harder.

She climbed down the ladder — slowly, because her hands had been unsteady since the night she'd pulled the infection out of Bram's leg, and she'd gotten very good at hiding it — and came into the lamplight.

"There she is," Odji said, not looking up from the lock he was rebuilding. "Our resident miracle worker. Tell this ox-brained soldier that tripwires are for amateurs."

"Tripwires are for people who want to *live*," Bram said.

"I want to live *quietly*."

"You want to live smugly."

"Yes," Odji agreed serenely. "That too."

Sera looked up from her book — the prophecy book, always the prophecy book lately, its pages soft as moth wings under her careful fingers — and her gaze snagged on Maren and stayed there. Scholars noticed things. It was the most annoying thing about them.

"Maren," Sera said. "You've gone grey around the mouth."

"I have *not* gone grey around the—"

"She has," Ivo said quietly. She was sitting by the window with the cat — Marrow, because Bram had named him and nobody had managed to talk him out of it — draped across her knees like a bone-colored scarf. "I noticed yesterday. I thought maybe she wasn't eating."

"I eat plenty." Maren dropped onto the bench by the cold hearth, more heavily than she meant to. Four faces turned toward her. The cat opened one eye, assessed the situation, and apparently decided it was worth staying awake for.

"Well," Maren said. "Since we're all staring at me like I'm a lecture, I suppose I'll give one."

The room went still in the particular way rooms go still when people sense a wound is about to be shown.

"You all know I can heal things. Small things, mostly. Fevers, infections, cuts that should have killed. You've seen me do it." She looked at Bram. He had the grace to look uncomfortable; the scar on his thigh was hers, neat as stitching. "What you don't know is what it costs. Because I let you think it cost nothing, and that was cowardice, and I'm tired of being a coward in a house full of fugitives. It's embarrassing. We should be cowards *together*. It builds trust."

"Maren," Ivo said.

"The fire comes out of me and doesn't come back." Maren kept her voice level, the way she'd learned to deliver bad news to families — gently, but without giving them anywhere to hide. "Every healing burns a little of my own life to mend yours. Small healings, small burns. Big ones—" She shrugged. "I've been keeping accounts for twenty years, and last week, pulling the rot out of Bram, I finally read the ledger honestly. I'm spending faster than I earn. Months, not years. Fewer months if there's trouble." She gestured vaguely at the room, at all of them, at the entire shape of their lives. "And we are *extremely* trouble-shaped people."

Nobody spoke. The lamp guttered. Somewhere outside, Highmoor's curfew bells rang the hour, and even the bells sounded apologetic about it.

"That's it," Maren said. "That's the whole lecture. No dramatics required. I just thought you deserved to know why I nap so much."

Odji recovered first, because Odji's heart was armored in sarcasm and sarcasm, unlike flesh, healed instantly. "Well. Obviously the solution is that you never heal anyone again, and we all take turns getting infected like responsible adults."

"Obviously."

"Bram, you're on infection duty."

"I'll do my best," Bram said gravely, and then ruined it by looking at Maren with an expression she recognized from triage tents — the look of a man doing arithmetic he hated. "Months," he said. "You said months."

"Bram."

"No, I'm — I need you to understand that I remember that leg. I remember what it felt like before, and after. You gave me—" His jaw worked. "You don't get to hand me my life and then tell me the price like it's a receipt."

"It's exactly like a receipt," Maren said. "And I'm asking you not to argue with the merchant."

"Maren." Ivo hadn't moved. The cat had transferred itself from her lap to the floor at some point, which it only did when it sensed weather coming, and Maren supposed a confession counted. Ivo's voice was very quiet. "How long have you known? Really known?"

Maren considered lying. It would have been easy; she was good at it; she'd had practice. But she'd come down that ladder specifically to stop.

"Since Hollowbridge," she said. "Before that, honestly, but I let myself pretend until Hollowbridge. All those wounded refugees. Every time I reached for the fire, it reached back a little harder." She smiled, and was pleased to find the smile mostly real. "Healers make terrible patients. We know exactly which lies we're telling ourselves."

"And you didn't say anything."

"I was waiting until it mattered."

"It matters *now*," Ivo said, and there it was — not anger, worse than anger, that fierce hurt tenderness that Maren had watched her aim at stray cats and stray people alike. "You've been carrying this alone in a house where we agreed — didn't we agree? — that we carry things together?"

"We agreed to share rations and watch rotations," Maren said. "Nobody ratified emotional disclosure."

"I'm ratifying it now."

"You can't ratify things unilaterally, that's not how—"

"*Maren.*"

"All right." Maren spread her hands. "All right. I'm sorry. Truly. But here's the part I actually came down here to say, so let me say it before I lose my nerve, because I will, I'm very good at losing nerve, it's practically a gift too." She took a breath. "What I need from you — from all of you — is a promise. When it happens, whenever it happens, however it happens — you don't waste yourselves on it. No revenge rides. No storming witch-finder garrisons because Maren died and someone feels guilty. No burning down anything on my account. You've got a war to survive and a prophecy to outrun and each other to keep alive. My death doesn't get to spend your lives. Promise me."

Silence again. Outside, the wind worried at the loose shutter Odji kept meaning to fix.

Bram looked at the floor. Odji looked at his lock. Sera looked at Maren with an expression of scholarly horror, as though Maren had proposed editing the prophecy with a knife.

And Ivo stood up.

"Oh no," Maren said. "Don't stand up. Standing up is a whole speech."

Ivo crossed the room and sat down beside her on the bench, close enough that their shoulders touched, and looked at her with those steady eyes that had once talked a cart-horse out of bolting and had, apparently, decided to try the same technique on Maren.

"No," Ivo said.

"That was fast. You didn't even consider—"

"I don't need to consider it. I've heard that promise before. I've *made* that promise before — to myself, about myself, for twelve years." Ivo's voice stayed gentle, which somehow made it worse. "*Don't come looking for me. Don't risk anything for me. I'm not worth it.* I said it to the dark every night in Highmoor like a prayer, and you know what the dark said back? Nothing. Because it was a lie dressed up as humility. It wasn't humility, Maren. It was fear wearing humility's coat."

"This is different."

"How?"

"Because *you're* the prophecy girl! You're the one the book drew! If anyone's life is load-bearing, it's yours. Mine is—" Maren gestured at herself. "Mine is a candle. A nice candle. A well-loved candle. But candles burn down, Ivo, that's the whole arrangement, and I am asking you — I am *begging* you — not to set yourselves on fire to light my funeral."

"See, and that's the second lie," Ivo said. "The one where you decide in advance what your life is worth to other people. You don't get to do that. It's not yours to price anymore. You joined a company. Companies vote."

"On revenge rides?"

"On everything." Ivo's mouth twitched. "Especially revenge rides. Odji will insist on planning them meticulously."

"I will," Odji confirmed from across the room. "There will be diagrams."

"Odji, you traitor, you haven't even promised yet either—"

"I'm savoring the moment," Odji said. "Promises are like locks. Best admired before you turn them."

Maren looked around the room — at the deserter and the locksmith and the scholar and the charm-seller, this ridiculous collection of people the world had chewed up and spat into the same leaking tannery — and felt the fire in her chest flicker against something else, something that had no business coexisting with a death sentence and yet absolutely refused to leave.

"You're all impossible," she said.

"We're a family," Sera corrected mildly, turning a page. "It's the same thing."

"Sera, you're supposed to be the reasonable one."

"I'm reasonable about sources and citations. About people, I'm afraid I'm as unreasonable as the rest of them." Sera looked up, and her eyes were bright and fierce behind the tiredness. "I spent thirty years believing knowledge was the safest thing to love, because books don't die on you. Then I met a woman who talks to cats, and now I love five people who could all be dead by spring, and I have never once regretted the exchange rate. So no, Maren. No promise. I won't waste myself on vengeance — I give you that much, gladly, because vengeance *is* a waste, and I'll fight any of you who reaches for it. But I will spend myself on *you*, living or dying, and you cannot stop me. I've read the primary sources. Persistence is my whole discipline."

Maren turned to Bram, last hope of military discipline. "Soldier. Surely you understand strategic restraint."

"I understand," Bram said slowly, "that in twelve years of service, the men I served with who made it home were the ones whose friends refused to leave them behind. And the ones who died alone were the ones who'd ordered everyone away first." He met her eyes. "You ordered us away. Request denied."

Maren opened her mouth. Closed it. Looked at Ivo, who was watching her with the patience of someone who had once waited out a spooked horse for three hours in the rain.

"You're really not going to promise," Maren said.

"No," Ivo said. "But I'll promise you something better. I promise you won't face it alone — not the burning, not the slowing down, none of it. And I promise that whatever time you have, we're going to fill it so full of living that the fire will have to fight us for every day." Her voice caught, just slightly, and steadied. "And I promise that when the time comes, we'll grieve you properly, loudly, and at length, and then we'll get up the next morning and keep going, because that's what you'd order us to do, and we'll hate it, and we'll do it anyway. That's the only version of the promise I'll sign."

Maren stared at her. The lamp flame stood perfectly still, as if listening.

"That," she said finally, "is a terrible bargain."

"It's the market rate," Ivo said. "Take it or leave it."

And Maren — who had spent twenty years pricing other people's lives in burns and fevers, who had done the arithmetic a thousand times and never once included herself in the sum — found, to her enormous irritation, that she was crying. Not elegantly. Ugly, hiccuping, healer-on-a-bad-night crying, the kind she'd have prescribed bed rest and broth for.

Ivo put an arm around her and let her.

* * *

Later — much later, past midnight, when Bram snored in the corner like a landslide and Odji had fallen asleep mid-sentence over his lock and Sera read by a stub of candle she pretended not to be hoarding — Maren climbed back up to the roof, because some conversations needed a second audience.

Marrow followed her, which was unusual. The cat generally treated roofs as beneath his dignity unless food was involved.

Maren sat with her back against the chimney and let the night air cool the fire under her skin. Below, through the floorboards, she could hear Ivo murmuring — low, wordless, the sound she made when she talked to animals, except there were no animals up here except one cat, and the cat was sitting beside Maren, tail wrapped around his feet, ears angled toward the sky.

Toward the citadel, Maren realized. Toward the old dark pile on the hill where Cassian's diggers worked nights now, hauling up something old.

"What are they saying?" Maren asked, because she'd stopped pretending not to notice the way Ivo listened to empty air.

Marrow's whiskers twitched. When he answered, his voice was the dry rustle of leaves — a voice Maren had heard only twice, both times at night, both times followed by Ivo going pale.

"They're dreaming louder," the cat said. "The old ones under the stone. And something up there—" his ears flattened toward the citadel "—has started digging with iron that *hurts* them. The rats won't go near the tunnels anymore. Even the rats are frightened."

Maren looked at the fire glowing faintly along her own veins, at the cat with his ancient yellow eyes, at the sleeping house below full of people who had refused, to a soul, to let her die cheaply.

"Well," she said to the night, "isn't that just like this family. I finally make peace with dying, and the world decides to get interesting."

Down in the dark beneath Highmoor, something old turned over in its sleep — and Maren, healer, hoarder of borrowed time, felt the fire in her chest lean toward it like a plant toward sun.
