## 8. The Road Out of Highmoor

The riot began, as most useful disasters do, with a lie about bread.

"Grain carts at the Southgate," Odji announced, sliding into the tannery just before dawn, his one hand already busy unbuckling his tool-roll. "Cassian's quartermasters have been skimming the city stores for three months. Someone — and I want it noted that I am far too professional to say who — let a rumor slip that the next shipment is bound for the citadel's private granaries instead of the market."

"You didn't," Maren said.

"I absolutely did." Odji's grin was all teeth and no apology. "By noon there will be four hundred hungry people between the witch-finders' barracks and the Southgate wall, and not one of them will be looking at a tanner's cart full of very nervous fugitives."

Ivo stood by the window with the cat draped over her shoulder like a stole of bad temper, watching the first smoke rise from the direction of the granary quarter. Twelve years she had lived in Highmoor, and in all that time she had never once seen the city angry on her behalf. It was strange, standing here on the wrong side of every law Cassian had written, to realize the riot was the closest thing to a gift Highmoor had ever given her.

"It won't hold them long," Sera said. She had the prophecy book wrapped in oilcloth and strapped to her chest like a breastplate, which Ivo thought was either very practical or very telling. "Elske isn't a woman you lose in a crowd. She's a woman you lose in a country."

"Then we'd best start walking," Bram said.

* * *

The tanner's cart smelled exactly as you would expect a tanner's cart to smell, which is to say like something that had died twice and been sorry about it both times. They lay under salted hides in the dark while Odji drove, whistling, through streets gone mad with hunger.

Through the gaps in the hides Ivo could hear everything: the groan of the crowd, the crack of a quartermaster's whip, someone singing a song about Lord Cassian that was not complimentary and would have gotten the singer hanged in any other week. The horse pulling them — a gray mare named, according to her own opinion of herself, *Gray Mare*, because horses are not poets — kept up a low, anxious muttering in the back of Ivo's mind.

*Too many feet,* Gray Mare complained. *Feet everywhere. Why do humans make so many feet.*

*I know,* Ivo murmured back, careful to keep her lips still beneath the hide. *Be brave a little longer. There's oats at the end of it.*

*Lies,* said Gray Mare, but she leaned into the harness anyway.

They reached the Southgate as the sun climbed high enough to turn the riot golden. The gate guards were not guarding; they were pressed back against the wall with their batons drawn, faces pale, holding a line against a tide of grandmothers. Odji turned the cart toward the postern door beside the main gate — the one he had locked himself, years ago, when he was still on the right side of the law and had memorized every lock in the city out of what he called "professional enthusiasm."

The lock opened at his touch like a flower deciding to trust the spring.

And then, for one moment, one stupid, shining moment, Ivo made a mistake.

She looked back.

It was only a glance over her shoulder as they slipped through the postern — a last look at the market roofs, the citadel's black spire, the crooked bell tower where she'd once sold a love-charm to a girl who didn't need one. Twelve years of narrow rooms and careful silences, of feeding a stray cat in an alley and calling that enough. The city had never loved her. It had barely tolerated her. But it was hers, the way a scar is yours, and she was leaving it behind in a cart that smelled of death, going she hardly knew where.

"Eyes forward," Bram whispered, but gently.

Then the cat, wedged in the crook of her arm, dug its claws in and spoke — not in the half-formed impressions animals usually gave her, but in words, clear and cold:

*Behind. Fast feet. The hunter with iron in her boots.*

Ivo looked back again, properly this time, and saw Captain Elske come around the corner of the granary road like a blade being drawn from a scabbard.

* * *

Elske did not run. That was the worst of it. Witch-finders, Ivo would learn, rarely needed to run; they simply arrived, the way weather arrives, the way grief arrives. She came through the crowd and the crowd parted for her without knowing why, and she stopped at the postern door, and even from fifty yards Ivo could see her look down at the lock — at the lock with no mark on it, no scratch, no sign of force — and then look up, directly at the cart, directly, it seemed, at Ivo's face.

"She knows," Ivo breathed. "Odji, the lock—"

"The lock is perfect," Odji said, wounded. "That lock is my masterpiece."

"She doesn't need proof. She needs a direction." Sera's voice was tight. "Drive."

Gray Mare needed no second invitation. Whatever else could be said of horses, they shared a species-wide conviction that crowds were where people died, and she took the south road at a pace that rattled the hides and everyone hiding beneath them.

For an hour they heard nothing behind them but their own wheels. For an hour Ivo almost believed it.

Then the birds started.

Sparrows first, streaming past overhead in a ribbon of small panic, all of them saying the same thing in the same breathless way: *fast boots fast boots fast boots.* Then a farm dog chained by a crossroads kennel, barking not at them but past them, at the road behind. Then a plow horse in a field, head up, ears pinned, thinking clearly enough for Ivo to catch it whole: *riders. Six riders. Iron-shod. Coming along the river path.*

"They've found horses," Ivo said. "Six. River path. An hour behind us, maybe less."

Bram sat up so fast he cracked his head on a hide rail and swore with the fluency of a man who had been a soldier before he was a deserter. "River path cuts the corner. We can't outrun six riders with a cart."

"We don't outrun them," Odji said. "We *inconvenience* them. There's a toll bridge two miles ahead. Toll bridges have chains. Chains have locks. Locks—" he patted his tool-roll with his remaining hand, "—have opinions, and mine are well known."

"You're going to lock a bridge *against* them?" Maren said.

"I'm going to lock a bridge *beautifully* against them."

* * *

They made the bridge as the light went long and coppery over the water. It was a squat stone thing built for ox-carts and toll collectors, and Odji was out of the driver's seat and into its chain mechanism before the rest of them had fully stopped moving, working with his single hand and his knees and, at one point, his teeth.

"Chain's old," he muttered around a padlock. "Chain's rusted. Chain has been neglected by a municipality that should be ashamed of itself. Give me ninety seconds."

"Give him sixty," Ivo said, listening. "*Fast boots fast boots.* Less than an hour. Maybe much less."

"Forty-five," Odji amended, "if nobody talks to me."

So nobody talked to him, and the company stood on the far side of the river in the failing light — a deserter, a scholar, a healer, a locksmith, a charm-seller, and a cat — listening to the water and waiting for the sound of hooves.

They came sooner than anyone liked. Around the bend of the river path, six riders, and at their head a figure in a grey traveling cloak with a witch-finder's iron badge dull at her throat. Even at distance, Captain Elske sat her horse the way a needle sits in a seam: precisely, inevitably, pointed at you.

She drew rein at the near end of the bridge. Her riders fanned out behind her. And across the water, in the ordinary human voice of a woman reading a sign rather than shouting a threat, she called:

"The chain is locked."

"Good eye!" Odji called back, cheerful as a market crier. "Local craftsmanship!"

"Locksmith Odji Vell. One-handed. Discharged from the Guild under circumstances your file describes as 'creative.'" Elske's voice carried perfectly over the river, unhurried, almost conversational. "You're harboring a beast-speaker and a thief of state documents. Every one of you is named in a warrant as of this morning. You cannot outrun me. I have never lost anyone I decided to find."

There was a silence. The river said what rivers say, which is nothing helpful.

"That's a very impressive speech," Sera called back, and Ivo felt a flicker of pride at how steady her voice was, "but you've read our files, so you know we're not the sort of people who respond well to authority."

Something that might have been, on a different face, amusement crossed Elske's. "Neither am I," she said. "That's why I'm good at this." She turned her horse. "Rest tonight. Run tomorrow. I'll be behind you the whole way to the free cities, and the free cities will not save you. Nothing saves anyone. Some things only delay."

Then she wheeled and rode back along the river path, and her riders went with her, and the last light caught the iron badge at her throat and made it flash like a small cold star.

"Well," said Bram, after a while. "She's pleasant."

"She's worse than unpleasant," Sera said quietly. "She's *patient*. People like that always win the first ten miles." She looked at the locked chain, then at Odji. "Can you unlock it?"

"Why would I? Let her climb over it. It'll cost her twenty minutes and some dignity."

"And if she finds another crossing?"

"There isn't one for eight miles, and the ford is chest-deep on a horse." Odji permitted himself a small, fierce smile. "We bought ourselves a night. Let's spend it well."

* * *

They camped in a hollow beech wood off the road, no fire, cold rations, the hides spread as blankets. Ivo took first watch because she never slept well anyway and because the watch gave her an excuse to sit alone with the dark and listen.

The night was loud, once you knew how to hear it. Owls trading gossip about mice. A fox trotting the wood's edge with a mouthful of chicken and a guilty conscience. Somewhere far off, cattle dreaming in the dumb, warm way cattle dream, all grass and no words. It should have been peaceful. Instead every voice felt like a thread pulled tight, because underneath the small talk of the night, the larger animals were uneasy.

The horses at the nearest farm hadn't settled since sundown. The dogs along the valley were barking in patterns, not at anything — rhythmic, relayed, passing something along the way soldiers pass orders. And once, just once, a wild thing Ivo couldn't name — something big, moving through the hills east of the road — had brushed the edge of her hearing with a thought too vast and shapeless to be a thought, more like weather: *many. coming. many.*

"You feel it too."

Maren had come to sit beside her without a sound, which was unnerving in a woman who limped. She handed Ivo a strip of dried apple and settled onto the root beside her.

"The animals," Ivo said. "Something's wrong east of here. Big something. The dogs are passing warnings like sentries."

"Cassian's army?"

"Maybe. Or maybe whatever makes an army necessary." Ivo chewed the apple without tasting it. "Maren, can I tell you something foolish?"

"You've told me several foolish things already. The one about the love-charms was my favorite."

"When I was running through that gate today, I looked back." Ivo watched the dark between the trees. "Twelve years that city ground me down, taxed me, outlawed me, would have burned me if it ever learned what I am. And I looked back like a girl leaving home." She laughed, short and unamused. "I don't know what's wrong with me."

"Nothing's wrong with you." Maren's voice was gentle. "You grieve what hurt you because it was also *yours*. That's not foolishness, that's just having a heart. Believe me, I'd know — mine's been breaking slowly for years and it still insists on doing its job."

Ivo glanced at her. Maren was looking at the trees, and in the dark her face was unreadable, and there was something in the phrasing — *breaking slowly* — that snagged like a nail in wool. But before Ivo could pull the thread, the fox came back.

It burst into the hollow at a dead run, dropped its stolen chicken as if the theft no longer mattered, and stood panting in front of Ivo with its amber eyes wide, and its mind hit hers like a wave against a seawall:

*Roads. East roads. Men beyond counting, banners like a red rash on the land, wagons of iron, and they are not turning toward the hills — they are turning SOUTH. Toward the free cities. Toward the river towns. Weeks, not seasons. Weeks.*

Ivo was on her feet before she knew she'd moved. The fox's fear poured off it like heat off a forge, and around the wood the night answered — a dog howling far away, another, another, the cattle lowing restless in their dreams, the whole countryside suddenly a single animal with its hackles up.

"What is it?" Sera was awake, the prophecy book already clutched to her chest. "Ivo, what did you see?"

"Not see. Heard." Ivo's mouth was dry. "An army. A real one, not a patrol — east roads, marching south, weeks away. They're making for the free cities." She looked around the hollow at the faces emerging from the hides — Bram reaching instinctively for a sword he no longer carried, Odji sitting up with his hand already curled around tools that wouldn't help, Maren very still. "Everything we ran from is the small part. Cassian isn't hunting us because we stole his plans. He's hunting us because we know what's coming, and he wants no one warned."

The cat rose from its nest in the cart's straw, stretched, and fixed Ivo with its green stare. When it spoke, its voice was the oldest thing in the wood.

*Now you understand why the wardens stir,* it said. *The gate weakens. The sleeper-stones crack. And the blood moon comes whether you are ready or not.*

Sera went white. "Blood moon," she whispered. "The next line. *A blood moon and a burning gate.*"

Above the beeches, far to the east, the horizon held a faint glow that might have been dawn and might have been something burning, and none of them could tell the difference, and none of them slept again that night.
