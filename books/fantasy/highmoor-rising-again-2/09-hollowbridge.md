## 9. Hollowbridge

The river announced Hollowbridge before the town itself did — a low, constant murmur that Sera felt in her teeth like a word she'd almost remembered. They had walked three days since slipping out of Highmoor, and her feet had filed formal complaints with every joint above them. Her boots, bought secondhand in her student years for library work, had never been intended for roads.

"You're limping on the left," Ivo said, not looking at her.

"I am *walking* on the left. There's a difference."

"Your stride shortened by half a hand around midday." Ivo glanced back, and there it was again — that look Sera was still learning to read, the one where concern and embarrassment wrestled to a draw. "The mule would carry you."

"The mule is a fiction you invented to talk to the actual horses without anyone noticing."

"The mule," Ivo said with dignity, "is carrying our cookpot. He can carry one more thing."

Sera looked down the road behind them. Bram walked at the rear as always, broad and watchful, his deserter's eyes cataloguing everything: sightlines, ditches, the number of crows on a fence post. Odji was ahead somewhere, having gone on alone an hour past because, as he put it, "a one-handed man walking into town with four strangers looks like trouble, but a one-handed man walking into town alone looks like a customer." Maren drifted beside the cart, gathering herbs from the roadside with the absent serenity of someone who found even weeds interesting.

And Sera carried a book wrapped in oilcloth against her chest, the way other people carried infants.

*Tomorrow,* the prophecy had said of Ivo's face, and then it had said worse things. Sera had spent three days of walking trying to make the ink say something else. It refused.

"Hollowbridge," she said aloud, mostly to hear something scholarly. "River crossing, grain trade, famous for eels. The university kept correspondence with their bridge-wardens' guild for two hundred years. Very meticulous record-keepers, apparently. One of them counted the eels."

"Counted the *eels?*"

"Annually. With notes on temperament."

Ivo smiled — quick, crooked, quickly hidden, the way all her smiles were, as though joy were another charm she wasn't licensed to sell. "Then let's hope they've counted the soldiers too."

They hadn't. That was the first thing wrong with Hollowbridge.

* * *

The town should have smelled of fish and tar and the particular green rot of river mud. Instead, as they came down the last hill, Sera smelled woodsmoke and unwashed crowds, and saw why: the meadow outside the west gate had become a city of tents and lean-tos, cooking fires, laundry lines strung between cart wheels. Refugees. Dozens. Then hundreds, as the road bent and revealed more — families from the eastern villages, from the free cities' borderlands, sitting among their salvaged belongings with the specific stillness of people waiting for news that could only be bad.

At the gate itself stood a notice board, freshly painted, and Sera read it twice because the first reading made no sense.

**BY ORDER OF LORD CASSIAN, PROTECTOR OF THE REALM:** The eastern war has been raised by beast-speakers — the abominations who whispered armies out of the wilds and broke the old peace. Any who harbor such creatures, or who speak with beasts, or who shelter those who do, share their guilt. Vigilance is loyalty. Report and be rewarded.

Below it, in different ink, someone had written: *whose peace?*

"Somebody's going to be punished for that," Bram said quietly.

"Somebody already was," Odji said, nodding at the dark stain on the boards near the bottom corner. He didn't elaborate. Nobody asked him to.

Sera became aware that Ivo had stopped walking. She'd gone very still, the way she did — Sera had noticed this — whenever the world confirmed her worst assumptions about herself. Her eyes were fixed on the words *beast-speakers*, and her face had closed like a shop shutter.

"It's a lie," Sera said. It came out sharper than she intended, loud enough that a woman nearby turned to look.

"Keep your voice down," Bram murmured.

"It's a *lie.* I have the book. I have—" She caught herself, because *I have proof* was exactly the sort of sentence that got scholars killed in occupied towns, and finished instead, more softly: "It's propaganda. Cassian needs an enemy older than himself so he doesn't have to be one."

"And everyone here needs bread," Bram said, nodding at the tents. "Scared, hungry people don't fact-check. They just need somebody to blame, and the notice hands them a name." His jaw worked. "I've seen this before. In the regiment. You don't argue with a crowd holding a torch. You get inside the walls before dark."

He was right. Sera knew he was right, and hated it, and followed him through the gate anyway, past two bored militiamen who cared far more about tolls than abominations. But she watched Ivo pull her hood lower, and thought: *she has been doing this her whole life. Walking past notices like that. Lowering her hood. Making herself small.*

The thought sat in Sera's chest like a swallowed stone for the rest of the afternoon.

* * *

They lodged at the Drowned Eel, which was less a name than a dare. The innkeeper, a wide woman named Petra with forearms like bridge cables, took Odji's coin, looked at the five of them — the soldier, the scholar, the healer, the locksmith, and the small gray woman who wouldn't meet anyone's eyes — and said only, "Back room. Supper's at six. Don't start anything."

"We never start anything," Odji said.

"Mm," said Petra, in the tone of a woman who had heard that before from people currently banned from three other establishments.

Supper was eel stew, which tasted better than it sounded, and Sera was halfway through her second bowl when the common room went quiet in that particular way rooms do — not silence, but a held breath. A man had come in from the rain, tall, gray at the temples, wearing a travel cloak over what was unmistakably a soldier's posture. He scanned the room with the unhurried thoroughness of a man counting exits, and Sera saw Bram's spoon stop halfway to his mouth.

"Oh no," Bram breathed.

"Do you know him?" Ivo asked.

"He knows *me.*" Bram set the spoon down with exaggerated calm, the way a man might disarm himself. "That's Captain Aldous Vey. My old commander. Before I—" He didn't finish. *Before I deserted* hung in the air anyway, plain as a banner.

Vey's gaze swept the room, snagged on their table, and stopped.

For a long moment nobody moved. The fire popped. Somewhere a dog shook itself. And Sera, watching, saw something extraordinary happen on Bram's face: fear, yes, but underneath it something rawer — the look of a man seeing home and knowing he couldn't go back to it.

Vey crossed the room. Every step was audible.

"Brammel Holt," he said. "Dead two years, according to the regiment's rolls. Yet here you sit, eating eel."

"Captain." Bram rose slowly. He didn't salute. Sera noted that; so did Vey, probably. "You're a long way from the garrison."

"Garrisons burn." Vey said it flatly, without drama, and Sera understood suddenly that this man had lost something too. "Cassian marched through the east. Those of us who wouldn't swear to him were given a choice between his colors and the road." His eyes moved across the table — Sera, Maren, Odji, and last, longest, on Ivo. "So I took the road. And now I find my best sergeant alive, sitting with strangers, in a town full of refugees, under a notice about beast-speakers." He leaned in slightly. "Which of you is it?"

The question dropped into the table like a stone into still water.

"Captain—" Bram started.

"Don't." Vey held up a hand. "Don't lie to me, sergeant. I taught you to lie badly and I'm looking at the results." He straightened, and his voice, when it came again, was quieter, and somehow worse. "I ask because there are men downstairs from the occupation office. Two of them. Asking questions, paying copper for names. If one of your companions is what the notice says, they'll be dragged into the square by morning, and anyone sitting with her will hang beside her. So I will ask once more, and I suggest you think carefully about whether you owe me truth or silence." He met Bram's eyes. "Which is it?"

Bram looked at Vey. Then — and Sera would remember this for the rest of her life — he looked at Ivo.

She hadn't spoken. She sat with her hands around her bowl, hood down now, gray-eyed, ordinary-looking, terrified and steady at once. And she gave Bram the smallest nod. *Your choice. Not mine to make for you.*

Bram exhaled.

"She's a charm-seller," he said. "Harmless charms. Luck-knots and traveler's blessings. Nothing more." It was, Sera realized, both a lie and completely true, which was the most dangerous kind of answer. "And she's under my protection, captain. Whatever that's worth these days."

Vey studied him for a long moment. Then he did something Sera did not expect: he pulled out a chair and sat down.

"Worth more than you know," he said. "Because the men from the occupation office are asking after a scholar, too. A woman. University-trained. Wanted for theft of restricted documents." His gaze slid to Sera, apologetic and unblinking. "There's a sketch. It's quite good."

* * *

Later — much later, after Vey had gone upstairs to his own room with a warning ("Leave before the market bell. I'll delay what I can, but I'm one man, and one man delays nothing") — Sera couldn't sleep. She sat in the inn's back courtyard under the eaves, the prophecy book open on her knees despite the damp, chasing marginalia by lantern-light, when she heard voices from the front of the building.

She shouldn't have looked. Scholars are trained to know when they shouldn't look, and trained, unfortunately, to look anyway.

Two men in Cassian's black-and-silver stood at the notice board by the gate, talking to a knot of locals. One held up a paper — a sketch, just as Vey had said — and Sera's stomach dropped, because even at this distance she recognized the drawing. Not Ivo's face as it was. Ivo's face as the witch-finders imagined it: gaunt, sinister, eyes too large. Propaganda didn't need likenesses. It needed monsters.

"—seen in the market district the night of the raid," the soldier was saying, voice carrying across the wet cobblestones. "Traveling with a deserter and a document-thief. The Protector offers ten crowns for information leading to the abomination's capture."

A murmur ran through the little crowd. Ten crowns was a year's wages for a laborer. Sera did the arithmetic the way she did all arithmetic — instantly, helplessly — and arrived at the obvious answer: someone in this town, soon, would do the math and take the money.

And then she heard herself say, "That's not her."

She was in the gateway before she'd decided to move. The crowd turned. The soldiers turned. Rain dripped off the notice board between them.

"What did you say?" the taller soldier asked.

Sera's heart was hammering so hard she could feel it in her ears. Every instinct she owned — the instincts of a woman who had survived universities, which are merely battlefields with better libraries — screamed at her to apologize, to laugh, to shrink. Behind her, she heard Bram's chair scrape inside the inn. Too late. She'd already opened her mouth.

"I said, that's not her." She pointed at the sketch. "I'm a scholar. Portraiture and physiognomy are part of my training. This drawing shows a woman with a widow's peak and a scar on the left cheekbone. The woman traveling with us — whom you describe — has neither. Your informant has wasted your time and the Protector's copper."

It was a magnificent lie. It was also, technically, checkable, which made it either brilliant or suicidal, and Sera genuinely did not know which until the taller soldier stepped closer, close enough that she could smell wet wool and cheap wine.

"And how would a scholar come to be traveling with a charm-seller and a deserter, madam?"

"Because," Sera said, "scholars go where the records are thin, and right now the records are thin everywhere, thanks to your Lord Protector burning the archives of every town he takes. Somebody has to write down what actually happened. Even if it's inconvenient." She lifted her chin. "Even for you."

The silence stretched. Rain ticked on the cobbles. Somewhere in the crowd, someone coughed.

Then the shorter soldier laughed — a short, ugly bark — and elbowed his companion. "Archivists. Gods save us. Come on, we've a list of names longer than this one." They moved off toward the next street, and the crowd dispersed, and Sera stood in the gateway shaking so hard she had to grip the doorpost.

"That," said Ivo's voice beside her — she hadn't heard her approach, she never did — "was the single bravest and stupidest thing I have ever witnessed."

"I'm aware."

"You lied to armed men. About *me.*"

"Yes." Sera turned. In the lamplight spilling from the inn, Ivo's face was doing something complicated — wonder and fear and something warmer underneath, fighting for space. "Ivo Brightwater," Sera said, "you have spent twelve years making yourself invisible so that no one would ever stand up for you. I would like to formally inform you that the policy has been discontinued."

Ivo laughed. It cracked in the middle, halfway between laughter and something else, and she pressed the back of her hand to her mouth as if to hold it in, and failed. "You'll get yourself killed."

"Probably. But not tonight, and not for lying badly — I lied *beautifully*, and I'll thank you to note it for the historical record." Sera hesitated, then, because the moment seemed to permit it: "You'd have done the same."

"No, I wouldn't." Ivo said it simply, without self-pity, which somehow made it worse. "I'd have hidden. It's what I'm good at."

"Well." Sera picked up her lantern. "Fortunately for both of us, you're not the only one in this company with a talent. Mine happens to be arguing with people who outrank me. It's gotten me thrown out of two seminars and one country." She smiled. "Consider yourself archived, Ivo. Officially. Irrevocably."

Ivo looked at her for a long moment. "Irrevocably," she repeated, tasting the word. And then, quietly, like a charm being sold at last at its true price: "Thank you."

* * *

They left before the market bell, as Vey had instructed — but not before he found Bram in the stable yard, saddling the mule in the gray pre-dawn.

Sera was loading the cart and pretended not to listen, which is to say she listened to every word.

"Where will you go?" Vey asked.

"East. Toward the free cities."

"They'll close the passes by winter."

"Then we'll be through by autumn."

Vey grunted. For a while there was only the sound of tack and the river below the walls. Then: "The men I served with who ran — I hunted two of them myself, back when I believed the rolls mattered more than the reasons." He paused. "You had reasons, sergeant. I knew it then. I was too much of a soldier to say it." Another pause, longer. "If you're going east — if you're going to *stand* somewhere, rather than run — send word. There are perhaps forty men scattered along this river who wore my colors and won't wear Cassian's. Forty men is nothing. But nothing, aimed properly, has ended better wars than this one."

Bram's voice, when it came, was rough. "You'd follow me, captain?"

"I'd follow the *reason*, sergeant. You happen to be carrying it." Boots on stone, a clasp of arms — Sera heard it, the solid thump of it — and then Vey added, quieter: "Watch the bridges south of here. Cassian's engineers are surveying them. Wherever he's building, it isn't bridges."

He was gone before the bell rang.

They rolled out of Hollowbridge as the sky went the color of unpolished pewter, the refugee camp stirring behind them, the notice board receding with its defiant graffiti (*whose peace?*) shrinking to a smudge. Sera rode beside the cart with the book against her chest and turned Vey's last words over and over.

"Surveying bridges," she said finally. "Why would an army survey bridges it doesn't intend to cross?"

Nobody answered. Then Odji, riding slouched and half-asleep on the tailboard, opened one eye.

"Engineers don't survey what they want to cross," he said. "They survey what they want to *hold*. Or what they want to keep *other* people from using." He sat up a little, frowning at the road ahead. "Question is what's south of here worth holding."

Sera opened her mouth to speculate — and stopped, because Ivo had gone rigid on the cart bench, head tilted, listening to something no one else could hear. The horses on the lead line had pricked their ears forward, all of them, in perfect unison, staring south.

"Ivo?" Sera said softly. "What is it?"

Ivo's voice came out thin. "The birds coming up the river road," she said. "They're saying the soldiers aren't building bridges at all." She swallowed. "They're digging. South of here, past the marshes — something old. And whatever it is, it's *wakeful*."

Behind them, the market bell of Hollowbridge began to ring — late, urgent, wrong. Not the market bell at all.

The alarm.
