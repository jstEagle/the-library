## 10. The Prophecy's Second Voice

The tannery had stopped smelling like a tannery about a week ago, which Sera considered the single greatest achievement of their residency. Odji had spent three days hauling out the old lime pits and scrubbing the flagstones with lye until his one hand ached, and now the great drafty hall smelled mostly of woodsmoke, Maren's herbs, and Bram's boots, which was a different problem entirely.

Sera had claimed the corner beneath the broken loading window as her study. It had a table — a door laid across two barrels, which she privately considered the finest piece of furniture she had ever owned — a candle stub she guarded like a dragon guards gold, and enough lamplight from the street to work by after the candle died. On the table, wrapped in oilcloth inside a grain sack inside a second grain sack, lay the most dangerous book in the world.

*The Vessing Codex*, the archivists had called it. Sera had stolen it from the university herself, which still made her laugh in a slightly unhinged way when she thought about it at two in the morning. She, Sera Vane, who had once apologized to a librarian for breathing too loudly near the restricted stacks, had committed theft. Grand theft, probably. Grand theft of prophecy.

"You're doing the face again."

She looked up. Ivo stood in the doorway with two bowls of something steaming, her red hair escaping its braid, the stray cat — who had traveled with them out of Highmoor and refused to explain why — draped over her shoulder like a furry stole with opinions.

"What face?"

"The one where you forget to blink." Ivo set the bowls on the barrel-table and peered at the codex as if it might bite. It had bitten all of them, in a way. "You've been staring at that page for two days. Maren thinks you're ill. Bram thinks you're cursed. Odji thinks you're avoiding dish duty."

"Odji is correct on all counts except the first three." Sera pulled the bowl toward her without looking at it. Barley and onions again. She'd have wept with gratitude for barley and onions a month ago. "And I'm not staring at a page. I'm staring at a *margin*."

Ivo sat down on the upturned crate that served as her chair. "There's a difference?"

"There is an *enormous* difference." Sera turned the codex around with the reverence other people reserved for holy relics. "Look. The main text. The prophecy of the wardens, the one that says a beast-speaker's blood will wake them, the one that got twelve people burned in my grandmother's generation and got my whole discipline defunded. You've seen it."

"I've seen it." Ivo's voice went carefully flat, the way it did whenever the prophecy came up — the one with her face sketched in ink a century before she was born, dated the day the witch-finders came. Sera had learned not to push. Mostly.

"Now look at the margin." Sera held the candle close. "Here. Alongside the verse about the war of the old kings — the war the beast-speakers supposedly started, the reason your gift is a hanging offense. Do you see it?"

Ivo leaned in. The cat poured itself off her shoulder and onto the table, which was against every rule of Sera's study and which Sera permitted anyway, because the cat had a way of staring at pages that was unnervingly like reading them.

"I see... smudges?"

"Smudges." Sera laughed, and it came out slightly wild. "Yes. That's what I thought for two days. Smudges, water damage, a mouse with inky feet. But look at them under the light at an angle. They're not smudges. They're *letters*. Someone wrote in the margins and then someone else — or the same someone, later — tried to scrape it off. Palimpsest work. In the *margins*."

Ivo looked at her the way she looked at Sera whenever Sera got going, which was with the patient fondness of someone watching a dog chase a cart. "Sera. Slowly. You know I love you, but I don't have the word for whatever a palimpsest is."

"A palimpsest is writing written over other writing. Scraped away and reused. And the point — the *point* — is that someone, a hundred years ago, read this prophecy and disagreed with it strongly enough to write their objection in the margin, and then someone after them decided that objection was dangerous enough to destroy." Sera was on her feet now. She couldn't remember standing. "Do you understand what that means? A censor. A censor in a book that was already hidden away in a restricted archive. Someone was so afraid of what the margin said that they hunted it down *inside a book nobody was supposed to read*."

The cat put one paw on the open page. Sera shooed it. The cat ignored her with the serenity of a creature who had never once been wrong.

"And you can read it?" Ivo asked. "The scraped writing?"

"Maybe." Sera sat back down, hard. "That's the problem. Maybe. I've been working at it with raking light, charcoal dusting, the angle of the stylus marks — the original scribe used a dry point, you can see the grooves even where the ink was lifted. It's slow. It's like listening to someone whisper through a wall. But I got three words tonight before you came in, and Ivo—" She stopped. Her hands were shaking. She pressed them flat on the table. "I got three words, and they were '*they were guardians*.'"

The room went very quiet. Somewhere in the hall, Odji dropped a lockpick and swore in three languages.

"Guardians of what?" Ivo asked. Her voice had changed. Sera knew that voice. It was the voice Ivo used when she was very still because moving might break something.

"Of whom. Of *people*, if the grammar holds." Sera pulled the codex back and traced the line of the main text without touching it. "Listen to the verse as it stands. '*And the speakers of beasts did open the ways of the world, and the old kings made war upon them, and fire took the north.*' That's the verse. That's the verse they teach in every school in Highmoor, the one that justifies the outlawry — beast-speakers meddled, beast-speakers caused the war, beast-speakers are the reason magic is chained. Except." She tapped the margin. "*Except* that if the marginal hand says 'they were guardians,' then the sentence doesn't mean what the censor wanted it to mean. 'Opened the ways of the world' — that could be a crime. Or it could be *keeping the ways open*. Holding a gate. Standing watch. The same words, Ivo. The same words, and the whole meaning turns on whether you believe the beast-speakers were the arsonists or the fire brigade."

Ivo was silent for a long moment. The candle guttered. Outside, Bram was arguing with Odji about whose turn it was to fetch water, in the comfortable bickering rhythm of people who had, without ever discussing it, become a household.

"You're saying," Ivo said slowly, "that the book might be lying."

"I'm saying the book might have been *edited*." Sera heard her own voice sharpen and made herself soften it. "I'm saying that a hundred years ago, someone read this prophecy and wrote in the margin that the beast-speakers were guardians — protectors — and someone else decided the world was safer not knowing. And I'm saying that if that's true, then the reason your gift is illegal isn't because of what your kind did. It's because of what someone was afraid your kind might *remember*."

Ivo looked at the page. The cat looked at the page. For a heartbeat, Sera would have sworn they were wearing the same expression.

* * *

She worked through the night. She told herself she'd stop at midnight, and then at the second bell, and then at the third, and each time the grooves in the margin gave up another word like a miser parting with coins, and she couldn't stop. This was the feeling. This was the feeling she'd chased through seven years of university and a decade of dusty archives — the feeling that the past was a locked door and she had just heard a key turn on the other side.

The method was simple and maddening. She dusted the margin with powdered charcoal, swept it clean, dusted it again, and read the ghost-letters where the dry stylus had pressed grooves the censor's scraper had missed. The scraper had been thorough but not patient — done in haste, Sera judged, by someone working by bad light, someone who cared about the *words* and not the *writing*. That in itself was a story. A scholar's erasure would have taken the grooves too. This was a soldier's erasure, or a priest's. Someone who feared the meaning, not the manuscript.

By the second bell she had a phrase: "*...not the burning but the keeping...*"

By the third: "*...the wardens slept because the speakers bade them...*"

She sat back. Her eyes burned. The candle had died an hour ago and she was working by a sliver of moonlight from the broken window, and it didn't matter, none of it mattered, because the words kept coming and each one rearranged the world.

The wardens slept *because the speakers bade them*. Not because the speakers' sins had doomed them to sleep. Not as a punishment. As an *instruction*. A warden-gate that closed on command — which meant, which had to mean, that what closed could be *opened* by the same authority. And the prophecy's central verse, the one that had Ivo's face in it, the one that spoke of a beast-speaker waking the wardens beneath Highmoor — everyone read that as doom, as the last catastrophe, the war come again. The witch-finders read it that way. The council of the free cities read it that way. Even Ivo read it that way; Sera had seen it in her face a hundred times, the quiet certainty that her gift was a fuse and she was the spark.

But if the marginal hand was right, the verse wasn't a warning at all.

It was a *job posting*.

Sera put her head down on the barrel-table and laughed until her shoulders shook, quiet hysterical laughter in the dark, and the cat came and stood on her back with all four feet, which was either comfort or a demand to be fed, and with that cat it had always been impossible to tell.

* * *

She presented her findings at breakfast, because she was incapable of presenting them at any later hour, and because Maren had made actual bread — actual bread, with a crust, traded from a farmwife two valleys over for healing a goat — and Sera felt the occasion deserved bread.

She'd laid the codex open on the table with charcoal-dusted margins and her transcription beside it, her neat scholar's hand filling two pages, and the whole company crowded around: Bram with his arms crossed, Odji perched on a barrel turning his hook in the light, Maren with flour still on her hands, and Ivo standing back, always standing back, as if she expected the book to accuse her personally.

"Right," Sera said. "Scholar's preamble. I'll keep it short, which for me is a heroic sacrifice."

"Bram, bet you a copper it's not," Odji said.

"Keep your copper," Sera said, "because here it is: for a hundred years, the prophecy of the wardens has been read one way. The beast-speakers caused the old war. Their gift is a curse. The wardens waking is the end of the world. Every law in Highmoor rests on that reading. Every burning. Every hanging. Everything Ivo has survived for twelve years rests on it." She turned the transcription around. "And it's wrong. Or at least — it was made wrong. On purpose. By someone with a scraper."

She walked them through it, and to their credit — to her lasting, astonished credit — they listened. Bram asked the soldier's question: "Who edits a prophecy? Prophecies are what you can't control. That's the whole point of them." And Sera got to say the sentence she'd been waiting all night to say: "Someone who needed it to say something else. Someone who feared what people would do if they knew the truth. Ask yourself, Bram — if you were a king a hundred years ago, and the fastest way to control every gifted person in your realm was to convince everyone their gift was the *reason* for the last war, what would you do with a book that said otherwise?"

Odji whistled low. "You'd scrape the margins."

"You'd scrape the margins," Sera agreed.

"And the wardens?" Maren asked quietly. She was looking at Ivo as she said it. "The verse about waking them. Is that scraped too, or is that true?"

"That part's untouched. The wardens sleep, and a beast-speaker wakes them. That part the censor *kept*." Sera met Ivo's eyes. "Which tells you something, doesn't it? The censor wanted the waking to sound like catastrophe. Wanted it feared. But the marginal hand — the one who wrote '*they were guardians*,' who wrote '*the wardens slept because the speakers bade them*' — that hand thought the waking was the *point*. Ivo. The prophecy might not be a warning about you. It might be instructions *for* you."

The silence stretched. Ivo looked at the transcription for a long time, her jaw tight, and Sera watched her wrestle with it — watched twelve years of hiding fight against one night of charcoal dust — and finally Ivo said, in a small voice that didn't match her at all:

"Guardians." She laughed, short and unsteady. "I spent my whole life being told my gift was the disease. And you're telling me it might be the *antibody*."

"I'm telling you it might be," Sera said. "I'm a scholar. I don't tell you it *is* until I've verified the hand, dated the ink, cross-referenced at least three other manuscripts, and slept, in that order."

"She'll do none of those things," Odji said. "She'll be at it again by noon."

"I'll do all of those things," Sera said with dignity, "in an order to be determined."

But it was Bram, quiet Bram, deserter Bram, who asked the question that dropped the temperature of the room. He'd been staring at the transcription, and when he looked up his face was grim.

"If this is true — if the usurper's whole justification for the outlawry is built on an edited book — then Cassian can never let it see daylight. And neither can anyone who's read it." He tapped the codex, once, gently. "This isn't just a discovery, Sera. It's a target. We're all standing next to it."

Nobody argued. Outside, the wind came down the valley with the smell of far-off rain, and far to the north — Sera would remember this, would remember the exact quality of the silence — a crow on the tannery roof began to scream, over and over, a sound Ivo translated a moment later with a face gone pale as paper:

"She says there are riders on the road. A lot of riders. And they're asking after a red-haired woman and her books."
