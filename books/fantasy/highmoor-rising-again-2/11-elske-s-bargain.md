## 11. Elske's Bargain

Captain Elske Varn had interrogated forty-one gifted souls in her six years as a witch-finder, and she had learned one thing above all others: they always ran.

They ran when she knocked on doors. They ran when she said their names. They ran even when running made no sense — the miller's son who bolted from a room with two exits, choosing the one that ended in a culvert; the old weaver who fled so fast she left her shoes behind and Elske had stood there holding them, feeling something uncomfortably close to pity.

So when Ivo Brightwater did not run, Elske's first instinct was suspicion. Her second was curiosity. Her third, arriving late and unwelcome, was something she refused to name.

* * *

It happened in Thornfield, three days after the ambush. The company had scattered into the orchard country south of the river road, and Elske's trackers had lost them — all but one. The woman who called herself a charm-seller had doubled back toward the town, alone, and Elske had found her at dusk sitting on a fallen wall at the edge of an apple orchard, eating an apple, of all things, as though the world were not hunting her.

Elske came out of the tree line with her hand nowhere near her sword, because that was the trick of it. You didn't corner people like dogs. You walked up to them like a neighbor borrowing salt, and by the time they understood what you were, you were already close enough to matter.

"You're a long way from your friends," Elske said.

Ivo looked up. She had a round, unremarkable face, the kind of face that made itself forgotten on purpose, and eyes that were doing something Elske couldn't read — not fear, not defiance. Appraisal. As if Elske were the one being measured.

"Am I?" Ivo said mildly, and took another bite of the apple.

"I have eleven soldiers in those trees."

"That seems like a lot of soldiers for one small woman eating fruit."

"It is," Elske admitted. "But then, you're worth a lot." She let the silence sit. Witch-finding, she had learned, was mostly patience dressed up as menace. "Ivo Brightwater. Charm-seller of Highmoor. Twelve years selling luck-knots and fever-washes, never once arrested. Do you know how rare that is? Most of them slip within five. You were careful."

"I was invisible," Ivo said. "There's a difference."

"There is," Elske agreed, and it cost her something to agree, because she knew exactly what invisibility cost. She'd bought her own at fifteen.

She sat down on the wall. Not beside Ivo — a body's length away, close enough for conversation, far enough for dignity. The apple in her own satchel went untouched. She'd stopped enjoying food years ago; it interfered with the watching.

"Here's what I know about you," she said. "You speak to animals. Don't bother denying it; my informant saw you calm a cart-horse in the night market two months ago, and the horse told me everything after." That was a lie. Horses told her nothing. But liars expected lies, and it unsettled people beautifully when their interrogator seemed to know too much. "You've been feeding a stray cat for years. You healed a deserter's wound in a tannery. And three days ago you walked into a trap set for six people and walked out of it, which means either you're lucky or the birds warned you. I know which I'd bet on."

Ivo finished the apple and set the core neatly on the wall, like a small polite offering. "You've done your homework."

"My job is homework." Elske turned her head and looked at her properly. "Here's what I don't know. Why you're still here. Eleven soldiers, one exit behind you, and you're eating apples. Either you have a death wish, or you want something from me."

The appraisal in those eyes sharpened. Elske felt it like a hand on the back of her neck.

"Maybe I wanted to meet you," Ivo said.

"No one wants to meet me."

"Everyone wants to meet the thing they're afraid of."

Elske laughed before she could stop herself — a short, ugly laugh, surprised out of her. It was true, and she hated that it was true, and she hated more that this quiet little charm-seller had seen it in under a minute. Forty-one interrogations and no one had ever flipped the table like that. No one had ever sat across from her and acted as if *she* were the interesting one.

"Let's skip to it, then," Elske said, and laid out the bargain she'd carried from Highmoor in a sealed letter she'd burned unread, because Lord Cassian's instructions were for his ears and hers, and some part of her had already decided they were filth. "Surrender. Come back to the citadel. Testify before the tribunal against the men Cassian calls his rivals — the harbor-masters, the grain lords, the guild heads he's been starving out for two years. Name them. Give him their ledgers, their letters, their sins. In exchange, your company walks free. All of them. The deserter gets a pardon under a new name. The locksmith keeps his remaining hand. The healer goes wherever healers go. The scholar keeps her archive privileges. Everyone lives."

"And me?"

"You live too. Comfortably. A house, a stipend, a guard who watches your door from the outside instead of dragging you through it." Elske paused. "That's the offer. It's a good one. It's better than any of the forty-one got."

Ivo was quiet for a long moment. An owl called somewhere down the valley, low and questioning, and Elske watched the charm-seller tilt her head toward it — just slightly, just a degree, the way you lean toward a voice you know.

"What happens to the ones I name?" Ivo asked.

"They hang."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I take you now, and we hunt the others the slow way. Bram first. He's the easiest — deserters leave trails of guilt everywhere they go. Then Odji, then Maren, then the scholar. We'll find them. I'm patient, and I'm good, and I have the whole machinery of a usurper's paranoia behind me." She said it without heat. It wasn't a threat, exactly. It was arithmetic. She'd learned to say terrible things flatly, because flat things frightened people less and moved them more.

Ivo looked at the apple core. "And if I say yes?"

"Then tonight you ride with me, and the others get a day's grace while I 'lose' their trail. Which I will genuinely lose, because I keep my bargains. It's the only clean thing about me."

Another silence. The owl called again, and this time Elske caught herself tilting her own head toward it, and hated that too.

"You keep your bargains," Ivo repeated. "Interesting phrase from a witch-finder."

"I'm not a witch-finder," Elske said, and heard, immediately, how it sounded, and pressed on anyway because it was the truth and she was tired of carrying it crooked. "I mean — I am. Officially. It's my rank, my pay, my reputation. But I've never once believed the thing I'm paid to believe. That magic is a corruption. That the gifted are a rot in the wood of the world." She picked up the apple core, weighed it in her palm, set it back down. "I believe the gifted are people. Frightened people, mostly. People who got a strange inheritance and spent their whole lives paying taxes on it."

Ivo's eyes had gone very still. "Why do you believe that?"

Because, Elske thought, and here was the place where the bargain tilted sideways, where the script she'd rehearsed on the ride south cracked down the middle like river ice.

Because of the dog.

* * *

She hadn't meant to tell her. She had rehearsed this meeting on the road — the offer, the pressure, the deadline, the clean professional menace of it all. She had not rehearsed honesty. Honesty was not in the witch-finder's toolkit. Honesty was a liability. You gave people pieces of yourself only when the pieces would buy something.

But Ivo Brightwater had asked a question nobody asked, and Elske had discovered, somewhere in the last three years of this work, that she could not hear that particular question without answering it. *Why do you believe that.* As if belief needed a reason. As if she might be permitted one.

"When I was fourteen," Elske said, "we had a dog. A big ugly shepherd mix named Bracken, who belonged to my father and loved me best, the way dogs do — unfairly, completely, without paperwork. One winter he got sick. Bloat, the vet said, or something like it. Twisted gut. He was dying in our barn and the vet was two towns over and my father said we'd lose him before morning."

She stopped. The words came harder than interrogation words ever had. Interrogation words were tools; these were something else, something she kept wrapped in oilcloth at the bottom of her chest where the damp couldn't reach them.

"I went out to the barn," she said. "Sat with him. Put my hands on his side where it was swollen, because that's what you do, you touch the thing that hurts even when touching can't help. And I — talked to him. Not really talking. More like... listening, and then answering, in a way that wasn't words. I don't know how else to describe it. I told him to breathe differently. To turn. To let go of whatever was twisted. And he did. He shifted, and the swelling went down over about an hour, and by dawn he was standing up begging for breakfast like nothing had happened."

The owl called again. Down the slope, one of her soldiers coughed, and Elske flinched.

"He lived nine more years," she said. "And I never touched him like that again. Never spoke to anything like that again. Because my father came out to the barn near morning — I still don't know why, maybe he heard the dog bark, maybe he couldn't sleep — and he saw me kneeling there with my hands on Bracken's ribs, and he saw the dog do what I asked." Her jaw tightened. "He didn't say anything that night. He was a careful man. Three days later he reported me himself. Walked into the magistrate's office in our market town and denounced his own daughter, in writing, with witnesses. Said it broke his heart. Said it saved my soul."

Ivo had gone quiet in a way that was different from her other silences. Softer. Receptive, like ground after rain.

"The magistrates sent me to the Assizes of Correction," Elske continued. "Six months. They call it correction. It's fasting and scripture and a man named Brother Aldous who puts a hot iron near your skin — not touching, never touching, that would leave evidence — until you recite the Litany of Purity fast enough. I was fourteen. I recited it very fast by the end." She smiled, and it was not a warm smile, and it was not entirely a cold one either. It was the smile of someone who has survived something and refuses to pretend it didn't happen. "When I came home, Bracken was gone. Sold, my father said. I looked for him for two years. Every farm, every fair. Dogs remember voices. I'd stand at fences and whistle the way I used to and wait."

"Did you find him?"

"No." Something moved behind Elske's sternum, old and heavy. "Or — I found out what happened to him. A knacker's yard outside Corveth. They don't keep long-working dogs past ten. He'd have been twelve." She let that sit between them, unsoftened. "My father sold a dying gift's only witness to the glue-boilers rather than look at it again. And then he told everyone his daughter had been cured."

Ivo's breath had changed. Elske noticed such things professionally, but this noticing was different — this was the noticing of one swimmer watching another tread water in the same deep place.

"And you became a witch-finder anyway," Ivo said. Not accusingly. Wonderingly, almost. "After all that."

"Because of all that," Elske corrected. "Do you know what I learned in the Assizes? That fear doesn't cure. It just teaches you where to hide. I hid inside the hunt. If I was the one holding the lantern, no one would ever hold it up to me. If I was the most zealous finder in the company, my file would stay thin. Zeal is armor, Ivo. I built mine rivet by rivet, and it worked, and every year it worked I hated myself a little more efficiently." She rubbed her thumb along the seam of her glove, where a scar lay hidden. "Forty-one arrests. I've never tortured anyone. I've never falsified evidence. I've let half my suspects escape through doors I could have sealed, and filed it as incompetence, and taken the demerits, because demerits heal and pyres don't. I told myself that made me decent. Some nights I still believe it."

"Some nights?"

"Some nights I count the ones I didn't save. The weaver with the shoes. The miller's son in the culvert — they drowned him, did you know that? Found him facedown in three feet of water because he panicked in the dark. I was twenty minutes behind him. Twenty minutes." Her voice went flat, which was how she carried things that would otherwise break her stride. "So yes. I keep my bargains. It's the only penance I've found that actually costs me something."

The light was going. The orchard had turned blue-grey, and somewhere below, her soldiers were lighting no fires because she'd ordered none, because fire drew eyes and this conversation was not for audiences. Elske realized she had been talking for a long time. She realized she did not know when the interrogation had become a confession, only that it had, somewhere around the dog, and that she could not recall the route back.

"So," Ivo said gently. "Your offer. Surrender, testify, and my family goes free. You'd keep that bargain. I believe you would. But Cassian wouldn't. The moment I'm in the citadel, I'm leverage — against the men I name, against the free cities, against whatever the prophecy says I am. You can't protect me from your own lord. You must know that."

"I know it," Elske said. "I've written the protection into the terms and I know it's worth the paper. Cassian eats his instruments. I've watched him do it. Harbor-master Quill testified for him two years ago and died in a 'cellar collapse' eight months later." She met Ivo's eyes. "I'm offering you a bargain I don't fully believe in, from a man I don't respect at all, to save people I've never met. That's the honest shape of it. Take it or don't."

"Why offer it at all, then?"

"Because the alternative is hunting you, and I'm tired." She said it simply, without self-pity, and somehow that was worse than self-pity would have been. "Because every arrest I make well is a lie I maintain, and every one I botch is a person I grieve, and I have been walking that ridge for six years and my legs are going. Because you sat on a wall eating an apple while eleven soldiers circled you, and something about that made me want to sit down too."

Ivo looked at her for a long time. Then she did something Elske had not anticipated, though afterward she would wonder why not: she reached into her coat and produced a second apple, small and bruised, and held it out.

"You haven't eaten," Ivo said. "You've been watching me eat for an hour. That's a hard way to spend an evening."

Elske stared at the apple. It was absurd. It was the smallest possible gesture, a bruised piece of fruit from a stranger's pocket, and it undid her more thoroughly than any blade could have. She took it. Her hand was steady — her hands were always steady, that was training — but something behind them was not.

"Bracken," Ivo said quietly, while Elske bit into the apple. "What color was he?"

"Sable. Black saddle, tan legs. One ear that never stood up right."

"Mm." Ivo looked out at the darkening orchard, at the trees going soft and shapeless in the dusk. "There's an old farm dog buried under an oak on the Corveth road. Sable. Crooked ear. The farmer's children loved him; he used to walk them to school. He lived to fourteen." She said it lightly, offhand, the way you mention weather. "Could be a coincidence. Lots of sable dogs with crooked ears in the world."

Elske stopped chewing.

The owl called a third time, closer now, and its voice seemed almost amused. And Elske sat very still on a fallen wall in a darkening orchard, holding a bitten apple, doing arithmetic she had no training for: either this woman was lying to comfort her — which was its own kind of gift, and its own kind of cruelty — or she had listened to some animal, somewhere, sometime, and been told a story about a crooked-eared dog who got his extra two years after all.

Either way, Elske thought, I am lost. Either way, the armor has a crack in it now, and it's shaped like kindness.

"You could have lied better than that," she managed at last. "A real spy would have given me details. A name for the farm. A date."

"I'm not a spy," Ivo said. "I'm a charm-seller. I sell luck. Consider that apple lucky." She stood, brushed off her coat, and looked down at Elske with an expression that was not quite pity and not quite respect and was somehow more dangerous than both. "I won't surrender tonight, Captain. But I'm not saying no forever. Ask me again when you've decided whose side your gift is on. Because you have one. I could feel it the moment you sat down — it's leaking out of you like heat from a bad window. Fourteen years of boarding it up and it's still the loudest thing in this orchard. Louder than your soldiers. Louder than the owl."

"The owl—"

"Is yours, incidentally. Has been since you entered the trees. She thinks you're sad." Ivo stepped backward into the dusk, unhurried, outrageous. "She also thinks you should eat more. Goodnight, Captain. Tell your men the trail goes cold here. They'll believe you. You're very believable."

And then the dark took her, the way it takes people who were never really visible in the first place, and Elsat Varn sat alone on a wall with a bruised apple in her hand and a hole in her armor the exact size of a dog with a crooked ear.

She did not give the order to search. She told her lieutenant the trail was cold, and he believed her, because she was very believable.

It was nearly midnight when she finally stood, and the owl dropped out of the orchard dark onto her shoulder — bold as brass, talons gripping her pauldron, feathers smelling of mice and rain — and said, in a voice that was not words but was unmistakably speech:

*Well?*

Elske looked at the lights of Thornfield below, at the citadel beyond it where a usurper ate his instruments, at the south road where a charm-seller and her ragged family were even now walking toward a gate that whispered names.

"Well," Captain Elske Varn said aloud, to an owl, in an orchard, at midnight, knowing full well what it would cost her, "I'm deciding."
