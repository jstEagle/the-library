## 12. The Warden-Gate Below

The stolen plans said the way down began behind a wall of dead men's names.

Ivo stood before it in the dark of the old citadel's undercroft, holding Odji's shuttered lantern at arm's length, and read the carved lintel twice to be sure her eyes weren't playing tricks. *Here lie the keepers of the deep peace.* Below that, a list of names so worn by centuries of damp that only the first three survived: *Aldous. Verity. Maren-of-the-Hills.*

"Maren," she said softly.

Behind her, the other Maren snorted. "Don't look at me. I've never been a hill in my life."

"It's a common enough name," Sera murmured, though she was already sketching the lintel into her notebook with the fevered speed she reserved for things that made her hands shake. "But the placement — Ivo, this is a *keeper's* inscription. The plans called this a foundation wall. They didn't know what they were digging through."

"They know now," Bram said. He had his sword out, which Ivo thought was optimistic; a sword was about as much use against what waited below as a spoon against a flood. But he was Bram, and Bram felt better holding something, so nobody said so.

Odji crouched at the base of the wall, running his remaining hand along the mortar lines with the tenderness other men reserved for lovers or coin. "There," he said. "See how the stones sit proud here, then sink? It's not settling. It's a door dressed up as a wall. Somebody wanted it forgotten, and somebody else wanted it *findable*, and they were both very good at their jobs." He glanced up at Ivo, one eye catching the lantern light. "Your call, charm-seller. Once I start, it starts."

Ivo put her palm flat against the cold stone.

She hadn't meant to. It was habit more than decision — twelve years of touching things to hear what they'd heard, though usually her subjects were horses and housecats and once, memorably, an extremely opinionated goose. Stone was different. Stone remembered slowly, the way glaciers remember, but it remembered *everything*.

What came up through her arm was not memory. It was attention.

The wall noticed her. The whole wall, all at once, the way a sleeping dog notices its name.

"Ah," Ivo said, and stepped back, and her voice came out steadier than she felt. "Yes. Open it, Odji. Quickly, if you don't mind."

* * *

The door took him eleven minutes, which he later described as "an insult to my craft" and which everyone else described as terrifyingly fast for a lock older than the university itself. Behind the false wall was a stair, cut from living rock, descending at an angle that suggested the builders had expected people to come down it in reverence rather than a hurry. The air that rose to meet them smelled of dust and deep water and something else — something green, impossibly, like a meadow after rain, though no meadow had existed within forty feet of this rock since before Highmoor had a name.

They went down in the order they always went down anywhere: Bram first with the sword, Odji second with his picks and his pessimism, Sera third because she refused to be last and also refused to admit why, Maren fourth because someone sensible ought to be in the middle, and Ivo last, because the beasts below already knew where she was, and she wanted to be the one who decided when they met.

The cat rode on her shoulder. It had insisted, in the wordless way cats insist, and Ivo had stopped arguing with it somewhere outside Hollowbridge.

"You feel that?" Maren whispered, maybe half an hour down. Her healer's hands were pressed flat against the stair wall as they walked. "It's like — standing next to a sickbed. Something down there is hurt."

"Not sick," Ivo said. She had been turning the feeling over in her chest for the whole descent, trying to find words for it, and the words kept coming out wrong. "More like... you know when a limb falls asleep? Pins and needles? Imagine being asleep for a thousand years and waking up halfway, and everything is pins and needles, and there are strangers digging at your door."

Sera stopped walking. In the dark ahead, her breathing went quick and shallow. "The plans," she said. "Cassian's engineers. Their notes said the vaults were 'structurally unstable.' They've been *prodding* it. For months. With picks and blasting powder and—"

"And it's been dreaming through all of it," Ivo finished quietly. "Yes."

Nobody said anything for a while after that. The stair went down.

* * *

The vault opened without warning, the way cathedrals do — one moment a corridor, the next a space too large for the lantern to argue with. Odji unshuttered the lamp fully, and the light reached out and gave up, and what it showed them in that first failed reach was enough to make Bram swear in three dialects.

Pillars. Hundreds of them, marching off into darkness, each carved from top to bottom with figures: wolves and stags, ravens and river-serpents, bears with crowns of oak leaves, hares with swords. And between the pillars, floor after floor of worked stone — galleries, walkways, an underground city built for a population that had never been human.

And at the far end, past all of it, a gate.

Even half-broken, even cracked down its center like a dropped plate, it was the largest thing any of them had ever seen. Two doors of grey stone, each tall as the citadel above, each carved into the shape of a beast rearing — a wolf on the left, a stag on the right, their forepaws raised toward each other across the gap where the doors had split apart. Through the gap poured a light that wasn't lantern-light: pale gold, slow-moving, like honey in winter.

"Oh," said Sera Vane, scholar of the university, in a small voice Ivo had only heard from her once before — the night she'd shown Ivo the prophecy book and said, *this is your face, dated tomorrow.*

"I know," Ivo said.

"No, you don't. You can't. Do you know what this *is*?" Sera was walking forward now, notebook forgotten, hands outstretched like a woman approaching an altar. "Every text calls the wardens metaphor. Every single one. 'The wardens sleep beneath the mountain' — allegory for civic vigilance. 'The gate of the deep peace' — a legal formula. A thousand years of scholars deciding it was a story, because the alternative was admitting we built our city on top of a *door*." She laughed, high and slightly unhinged. "We were wrong. We were all so magnificently wrong."

"Sera," Ivo said. "Stop walking."

"Why?"

"Because it's listening to you."

Sera stopped. And in the silence that followed, Ivo let herself finally do the thing she had been refusing to do since the top of the stairs: she opened herself all the way, dropped every mask, every careful wall, and *listened*.

The vault roared.

Not with sound. With presence. The pillars were not decoration — they were *voices*, hundreds of them, each carved beast a vessel that something vast and patient had been pouring itself into across centuries, the way water fills every cup set out in the rain. And all of it, every pillar, every gallery, every inch of the broken gate, was turned toward her like ten thousand faces at a trial.

And underneath the attention, threaded through it, faint as a pulse under bandages:

*Ivo Brightwater. Ivo Brightwater. Ivo Brightwater.*

Her own name, whispered in stone voices, in the accent of a language that predated speech.

"They know me," she said aloud. Her voice sounded very small under the vault's ceiling. "Sera. Bram. Everyone. They know my name."

"That's — good?" Bram offered. "Presumably. That's probably good."

"The prophecy says a beast-speaker wakes them," Maren said. She had gone pale, and her hands were glowing faintly, the way they did when her gift rose unbidden. "Ivo, they're not asleep. Look at the gate. They're *half awake*. They've been half awake, and Cassian's been hammering on the lid, and they're reaching for the one thing the prophecy promised them, and that's—" She stopped. She looked at Ivo with an expression Ivo recognized, because she'd worn it herself in mirrors for twelve years. Pity. Fear. Both aimed at the same person.

"Don't," Ivo said.

"I'm not saying anything."

"You're saying it with your whole face."

* * *

They made camp in the shadow of the left-hand door, beneath the carved wolf's raised paw, because retreating up an hour of stairs in the dark seemed worse than staying, and because Sera refused to leave until she had transcribed the inscriptions ringing the gate. Ivo sat with her back against the wolf's foreleg — warm, inexplicably warm, like a sunned wall — and tried to breathe.

The whispering hadn't stopped. If anything it had settled into something almost companionable, the way a house settles around its inhabitants at night. *Ivo Brightwater,* the stones murmured. *Ivo Brightwater. Little speaker. Late speaker.*

"Late?" she muttered. "I'm twenty-six."

The wolf's paw seemed, very slightly, to warm under her shoulder blades. It might have been her imagination. It might have been laughter.

The cat padded across the vault floor — bold as brass, tail up, treating the ancient warden-gate as a piece of furniture of no particular consequence — and settled in Ivo's lap. Then it looked up at her with its green eyes and spoke, the way it had spoken once before, in the sewers, in the worst night of her life.

*They remember you,* the cat said. *Not you. Your kind. The ones who sealed the door and walked away and told the world it was a story.* Its tail flicked. *Stone holds grudges, charm-seller. Be glad you're pretty.*

"You're a comfort," Ivo said. "Truly."

*I'm a cat. Comfort is extra.*

Bram, standing watch at the corridor mouth, cleared his throat. "Do we want to talk about the fact that our escape route is a single staircase, our position is directly beneath the usurper's seat of power, and we have approximately zero plan beyond 'look at the big door'?"

"We have a plan," Odji said from atop a fallen pillar, where he was mapping the vault's side galleries by lantern light. "The plans Cassian's engineers stole — sorry, that *we* stole — show a secondary shaft. Old ventilation, runs up through the hillside, comes out in the orchard road. I can get us to it in twenty minutes if we need to run."

"And if they've found it?"

"Then I pick a different lock," Odji said, "and if that fails, Bram hits someone, and if that fails, Maren heals whoever Bram hit, and if that fails—" he nodded at Ivo and the cat, "—we unleash the goose."

"There is no goose," Ivo said.

"There's always a goose," said Odji, with the certainty of a man who had grown up in a port city.

Sera, meanwhile, had gone silent in the way that meant discovery. She was kneeling at the base of the right-hand door, tracing the ring of inscriptions, and her lips were moving through translation faster than her pencil could follow. "Ivo. Come look at this. Now, please."

Ivo went, cat and all.

The inscription circled both doors, and where the crack split them, the text broke off mid-line — a sentence interrupted by the fracture, hanging incomplete across a hundred years of silence. Sera's finger rested on the final legible characters.

"'When the moon bleeds and the gate burns,'" she read, "'the speaker shall stand in the door and give what cannot be taken—'" She looked up. "That's where it breaks. That's where the crack cuts through. Ivo, the fracture isn't damage. Look at the edges — they're smooth. Worn. This crack has been widening for *centuries*, slow as tree growth, and the prophecy was written *before* it reached this line." Her voice dropped. "The gate is still breaking. The sentence is still being written. And whatever comes next—"

The floor shuddered.

It was not an earthquake. Earthquakes are indifferent. This shudder had *direction* — a flinch, a full-body flinch of something enormous in pain, and the golden light pouring through the gap in the gate guttered like a candle in wind. Dust sifted down from a ceiling none of them could see. Somewhere far above, muffled by a hundred feet of rock, iron rang against iron: the citadel's alarm bells.

"They felt that," Maren breathed. "Whatever Cassian's doing up there — they felt it."

Then Ivo felt something else, and her blood went cold.

Boots. Many boots. Not beasts — boots, human, disciplined, descending the long stair with the particular rhythm of soldiers who had done this march before. And threaded among the footfalls, faint but unmistakable to a beast-speaker's ear, the nervous shifting and whickering of draft animals hauling equipment.

"Cassian's engineers," she said. "Coming back for another session." She was already moving, scooping up the cat, grabbing Sera's elbow. "Odji — the ventilation shaft. Now. Everyone move like you mean it."

"How close?" Bram asked, falling in beside her, sword drawn for all the good it would do.

"Close enough that I can smell the mules."

* * *

They ran. There was no dignity in it — Sera clutching her notebook to her chest like a life-vest, Odji leading them through side galleries by memory and instinct, Maren stumbling once on rubble and caught by Bram without either of them remarking on it. Behind them the boots grew louder, and torchlight began to flicker orange against the distant pillars, and voices echoed — engineers' voices, tired and profane, complaining about shift rotations and unstable ceilings, men who did not yet know that five fugitives and a cat were sprinting through their excavation site.

And the whole time, the whispering followed Ivo like a tide.

*Ivo Brightwater. Little speaker. Do not go. The door is breaking. The moon will bleed. We are so tired, little speaker, we are so tired, and the sentence is nearly written—*

At the mouth of the ventilation shaft — a black square in the rock, breathing cool air from the world above — Ivo stopped. Just for a moment. Just long enough to turn and look back at the gate, at the two great beasts straining toward each other across the widening crack, at the wounded golden light.

"I'll come back," she said. She hadn't planned to say it. It came out of her the way truth does — unbidden, total, terrifying. "I don't know how, and I don't know what it costs, but I will come back, and I will finish the sentence."

The vault went still. All of it — every pillar, every gallery, every whisper — held its breath like a congregation.

Then, from the gate, in a voice that was not sound but was unmistakably speech, the wolf and the stag together:

*Hurry.*

The crack in the gate ran another inch with a sound like ice on a spring lake, and the engineers' torchlight swung into the far gallery, and Ivo turned and climbed into the dark.

Behind her, faint through the stone, she heard a young engineer's voice, puzzled: "Sir? This dust — sir, the crack's moved since Tuesday. It's definitely moved."

Above ground, dawn was still hours away. But somewhere south, on a dark road, an owl was flying toward Highmoor with a message, and a captain who talked to owls was making her decision, and the sentence in the stone was writing itself toward its ending whether anyone was ready or not.

Especially not Ivo Brightwater, who had promised a door she would return — and who was beginning to understand, in the marrow-deep way of beast-speakers, exactly what kind of promise she had just made.
