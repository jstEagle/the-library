## 13. Ashes at Hollowbridge

Bram smelled the smoke before he saw the town.

That was the first wrong thing. Hollowbridge sat in a river valley, and the wind came off the water in the mornings, and the wind should have smelled of fish and mud and the particular sourness of a tannery that had never once been cleaned. Instead it smelled of char and wet ash, the way a hearth smells the day after a fire, when everything is cold but nothing has been forgiven.

He reined up on the ridge road and looked down, and his stomach did what it had learned to do during six years of soldiering — it went quiet and professional.

"Hollowbridge," Odji said beside him, unnecessarily. She had one hand on her mule's bridle and her remaining one — the good one — was already counting things, the way she counted everything. "The mill's gone. The granary's gone. The bridge itself is standing, which I'll admit is a point in favor of whoever built it."

"The church tower's standing," Sera said quietly, from Bram's other side. Her voice had gone flat and careful, the scholar's voice, the one she used when feeling would get in the way of seeing. "Smoke damage only. They burned outward from the market square."

"They burned *outward*," Maren repeated. "Not inward?"

"Outward." Sera didn't look away from the town. "You start a fire in the center of a town, it spreads unevenly. You want to burn a town properly, you set fires along its edges and let them close like a hand." She paused. "This was done by people who knew how."

Bram knew how too. He'd been one of those people once, or adjacent to them — a sergeant in an army that did not burn towns as policy but did not exactly forbid it either, an army where a captain might look the other way if the town in question had been unhelpful. He had told himself for six years that he'd left because of the pay, because of Odji, because of a bad winter and a worse commander. All true. None of it the truth.

"They're punishing it," he said. "For us."

Nobody answered, because there was nothing to answer with. Four days ago, Hollowbridge had fed them, hidden them, lied to Cassian's provosts about their direction, and given Bram's old commander — a man named Hesk with a face like a closed ledger — every reason to remember exactly which fugitives had passed through and who had helped them. The company had left believing the town's discretion would hold. Discretion always held, they'd told themselves. People were afraid, and afraid people kept their heads down.

They had forgotten, all four of them, that Lord Cassian did not punish fear. He punished the *objects* of it.

"We should have stayed," Maren said.

"No," said Bram, and then, because lying to Maren was like lying to a well — she just listened, patiently, until you heard your own echo — he corrected himself. "We couldn't have stayed. Staying would've meant fighting Hesk's garrison in a river town with no walls, and we'd have lost, and the town would still be burning, plus us."

"But we'd be ashes too," Odji said. "Ashes can't feel guilty. There's an economy to it."

"That's the bleakest thing you've ever said, and I once watched you negotiate with a corpse-robber over a lockpick."

"I got the pick," Odji said. "And half his boots."

Ivo hadn't spoken since the ridge. She stood apart, holding the strap of her pack with both hands, looking down at the smoke with an expression Bram had seen on horses once, in a burning stable — the look of something that knows exactly which way out exists and knows equally well that it isn't meant for everyone. She'd been talking to the birds since dawn. He'd watched her do it without moving her lips, the way she did, a small stillness in her face like a woman listening through a wall. Now whatever the birds had told her was sitting behind her eyes, and it was heavy.

"What are they saying?" he asked. "The crows."

She flinched. Not much. But Bram had spent years learning to read soldiers' flinches, and hers was the bad kind — the kind that meant the answer was worse than silence.

"Crows don't really do grief," she said finally. "It's not — they're not built for it. They do inventory. They count what's changed." She swallowed. "There were nine hundred and forty people in Hollowbridge four days ago. The crows count differently than a census does. But they think — they think most of them got out. Downriver, into the marshes. Hesk burned the town but he didn't have enough men to ring it. He wanted them to run. Running is the punishment. It tells the next town what happens."

"And the ones who didn't run?"

Ivo looked at him, and Bram understood that she was deciding whether to lie to him, and understood also that she wasn't going to manage it.

"Some are in the marshes. Some are in Hesk's carts, headed south. And some are in the church." Her voice dropped. "Wounded. Burned. The crows say the church is full of hurt people and almost nobody to help them, because the healer — the town's healer — died on the first night."

* * *

They went down into Hollowbridge the way you go down into cold water: fast, all at once, before the body can lodge its objection.

The town was worse up close and better up close, both at the same time, which Bram would later decide was the defining feature of disasters. Worse, because ash has a texture that photographs and paintings refuse — it gets into everything, it has a taste, it clings to the inside of your throat and stays for days. Better, because ruins are quieter than you expect. A burned street is not screaming. It is just very, very empty, in the way a room is empty after an argument.

They moved through it in a loose line, Ivo in front because the animals kept finding her — a singed dog trotted out of an alley within minutes of their entering the town and pressed itself against her leg like it had been waiting all week for a specific person, which, Bram supposed, it had. Behind her came Sera with the prophecy book wrapped in oilcloth against the ash, then Odji, who kept stopping to test doors, because Odji's grief expressed itself as locksmithing. Bram took the rear, out of habit. Rear guard. Watch the road. Watch for the thing coming that you cannot stop.

Maren walked in the middle and said nothing at all, and Bram should have paid attention to that, because Maren not talking was like a river not running. It meant pressure somewhere upstream.

The survivors were in the church, as the crows had promised. Forty-one of them, huddled among the pews and in the undercroft, and when the dog barked at the door and Ivo pushed it open, the sound that came off those forty-one people was not relief. It was the sound of people who had spent four days learning that hope was a bill that eventually came due.

Then a girl near the front — maybe ten, a bandage around her head, one eye swollen shut — saw Ivo's face and started to cry, and said, "You came back," and the sound changed.

Bram stood in the doorway while forty-one broken people remembered that other people existed, and he thought: *this is what we did.* Not the fire. Hesk had lit that. But this — this terrible arithmetic, where their escape had been purchased with someone else's town. He had done the sums a hundred ways on the road down from the ridge and they never came out clean.

"You shouldn't have come back," said a voice, and a man detached himself from the pews — gray-bearded, one arm in a sling, a miller by the flour still ghosting his sleeves. Aldous. Bram remembered him; he'd been the one who argued hardest for helping them, four days ago, banging the table in the taproom. "I mean it. You shouldn't. If Hesk learns—"

"Hesk burned your town because you helped us when he *didn't* learn," Bram said. "Staying away doesn't buy anything now. That price is paid."

"It will be paid again."

"Maybe." Bram held the man's eyes. "But not by you hiding in a church waiting for it. Look — I was a soldier for six years. I know how garrisons work, how they patrol, what they bother with and what they ignore. If you're going to survive in a burned town inside Cassian's reach, you need someone who knows the shape of the boot that's on you." He heard himself and almost laughed, because it sounded exactly like the speech a recruiter gives, and he hated recruiters. "I'm not asking anyone to fight. I'm saying somebody should stay who understands what's coming, so it doesn't come as a surprise twice."

Aldous looked at him for a long moment. Then, with the grim courtesy of a man accepting a delivery he'd already signed for: "The undercroft floods when it rains. We could use help moving the stores up."

Which was, Bram understood, a yes.

* * *

Maren worked for eleven hours.

Bram kept count because counting was the only thing he could contribute. He carried water, boiled bandages, hauled a collapsed pew out of the undercroft stair, and counted. Eleven hours, and in that time Maren moved among the wounded of Hollowbridge like a candle moving through a dark house, and everywhere she passed, the dark withdrew.

He knew what her gift cost. They all knew, in the vague way a family knows a thing nobody says aloud: that Maren healed faster and deeper than any herb-craft could explain, and that afterward she ate like a starving woman and slept like the dead and sometimes, on bad days, her hands shook and there was a new silver thread in her hair. She'd admitted it once, weeks ago, around a campfire, in the flat tone of someone reading a weather report: her gift drew on her own life, a little, every time. A candle burning from both ends, except the second end was her.

Eleven hours in a church full of burn victims.

By the fourth hour she had stopped eating between patients. By the seventh, Sera was following her with bread and Maren was taking bites without noticing, the way a horse drinks when led to water. By the ninth, Bram noticed that the silver in her hair had spread — not threads anymore but whole wings of it, white sweeping back from her temples like frost climbing a window.

"Maren." He caught her arm between patients. "Sit down. Ten minutes."

"There's a boy with a burned lung," she said. "If I sit down he—"

"He'll keep for ten minutes. He's breathing. I checked. I know what breathing looks like; I spent six years checking whether men were doing it." He steered her onto a pew. She went with a small, terrifying docility, like a puppet whose strings had been briefly cut, and that frightened him more than argument would have. He put the bread in her hands. "Eat. That's an order from your former sergeant, which means it's worthless, but eat anyway."

Her mouth twitched. "You were a terrible sergeant."

"I was an adequate sergeant. Ask anyone I didn't get killed."

She ate. Half the loaf. Then she looked up at him with eyes that were clear and calm and much, much older than they'd been a week ago, and said, "Bram. Don't waste the company on me."

"Don't start."

"I'm serious. Listen to me, because I'm going to say this once and then I'm going back to work." She set the bread down and took his hands, and her grip was still strong, which he clung to. "What happened here — Hesk burning this town — it's going to happen again, somewhere else, and again, and each time there will be more wounded than we can carry and less of me to heal them with. So the company needs to learn the arithmetic now, while it's cheap, instead of later, when it isn't." Her voice didn't waver. That was the worst part. "You cannot spend me on everyone. Choose. And when you choose, choose the mission, not the person. Promise me."

"I don't make promises I intend to break."

"Bram—"

"I watched commanders do that math for six years," he said, and his voice came out rougher than he'd planned. "Men were numbers to them. Wounded were a supply problem. And you know what? They were *right*, by the numbers. Every time. And every time, the army got a little smaller in a way that had nothing to do with casualties, because the living stopped believing anyone would come back for them." He squeezed her hands. "I deserted over that. Well — officially over the pay. Unofficially over that. I'm not going to build the thing we're building on the same foundation, Maren. I won't promise to let you die efficiently."

Maren held his gaze. Then, softly, with the ghost of her old humor surfacing like a fish in a pond: "You realize refusing to promise is itself a choice. You've chosen the person over the mission right now, with me sitting right here."

"I'm aware."

"And you're aware I could heal twice as many people if you'd just—"

"I'm aware of the numbers, Maren. I've always been aware of the numbers." He stood, because staying seated next to her was becoming unbearable. "I'm choosing to be wrong. Somebody in this company has to be allowed to be wrong about something, or we're just Cassian with better hair."

Behind him, from the direction of the door, Odji's voice drawled: "Speak for yourself. My hair is magnificent."

Maren laughed — a real laugh, brief and cracked at the edges — and got up off the pew, and went back to work, and Bram stood in the aisle of a burned church and understood that he had just lost an argument and won nothing, and that the white in her hair was going to keep spreading whether he liked the arithmetic or not.

* * *

They buried seven people the next morning, in the sodden ground behind the church, and the company stood with the survivors in a thin cold rain, and Sera — of all people — spoke the words, because the town's priest was among the dead and someone had to, and it turned out that a century of reading old books gave a woman a good instinct for old words.

Afterward, the company gathered in the taproom of the Drowned Eel, which had survived the fires by the simple expedient of being built of stone and stubbornness, and which now served as kitchen, infirmary annex, and war council chamber, depending on the hour.

It was there, over a stew that Odji pronounced "aggressively adequate," that Bram made his announcement.

"I'm staying."

Four faces turned toward him. Ivo's cat — riding her shoulder these days like a furry epaulette, and speaking, gods help them all, whenever it felt the conversation required its input — opened one eye.

"Staying," Sera repeated carefully.

"Here. In Hollowbridge. With Aldous and the others." Bram laid it out the way he'd lay out a position report, because the alternative was laying it out the way a friend does, and he wasn't sure he could finish it that way. "Hesk's garrison thinks this town is finished. That's our advantage. Finished towns don't get garrisoned — they get taxed and ignored. Which means this is the one place for fifty miles where people can organize without a witch-finder's boot on their neck. Someone who knows Cassian's army from the inside needs to be here, building that. Routes. Signals. Safehouses downriver. When the free cities finally move — and they'll have to move eventually — this valley is the road, and right now nobody's watching it except me."

"You'd be alone," Ivo said quietly.

"I'd be alone-ish. There are forty-one people in that church and at least six of them are angry enough to be useful. Anger's a resource. I know how to refine it." He managed a smile. "Besides, I've been the rear guard this whole journey. This is just rear guard with better stew."

Nobody laughed. He'd known they wouldn't, but he'd hoped.

It was Ivo who crossed the room and hugged him — Ivo, who touched people the way cats touch things, provisionally and on her own schedule. She hugged him hard, her face against his chest, and said into his coat, "The sparrows will find you. Anywhere in the valley. If you need us, tell a sparrow."

"A sparrow."

"They gossip terribly. It's their one flaw."

"And their one virtue," said the cat, from her shoulder, in its dry gravel voice. "We sparrows brought down three empires, historically. No one believes this, which is why it works."

Bram cleared his throat, which had developed an obstruction. "Right. Well. If the empires fall, I expect a full account."

Odji shook his hand like she was testing a hinge. Sera kissed his cheek and told him, with scholarly precision, that he was making a historically defensible decision, which from Sera was roughly equivalent to weeping. Maren simply looked at him across the table, and he looked back, and neither of them said anything, because they had already said everything, and some arguments you agree to lose forever.

He walked them to the bridge at dusk — the famous bridge, the one that had given the town its name, scorched but standing, because whoever built it had built for a longer war than this one. The company crossed it going east, toward the free cities and the council and whatever the prophecy wanted from Ivo next. Bram stood on the western end, on the side of the burned town, and watched them go until the rain took them.

Then he turned around, rolled up his sleeves, and went to find Aldous, because there were stores to move and a valley to organize and a garrison two days' ride north that believed it had already won.

Behind him, in the wet grass by the roadside, a sparrow landed, tilted its head, and waited politely for instructions.
