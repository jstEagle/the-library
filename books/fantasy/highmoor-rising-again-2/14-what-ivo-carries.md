## 14. What Ivo Carries

The fire in the hollow had burned down to a red heart by the time the others slept.

Ivo could hear them breathing in the dark — Bram's slow soldier's rhythm, Odji's occasional snore that he would deny to his grave, Maren's breath thin as thread. Even the beasts of the wood had gone quiet around their little camp, as if they knew something was being decided tonight and did not wish to overhear it.

Sera sat across the coals with the prophecy book open on her knees, as she did most nights now, chasing the second hand's marginalia through the old ink like a hound on a scent. Her hair had gone half out of its braid. There was ink on her left cheekbone where she'd pushed her sleeve against her face an hour ago. She looked, Ivo thought, exactly like a person who was about to be hurt very badly, and didn't know it yet.

"You're staring," Sera said without looking up.

"I'm memorizing."

That made her look up. "Memorizing what?"

"The way things are." Ivo turned her hands over the fire's warmth. "In case I need to remember them later."

Sera closed the book slowly, keeping her thumb in the page. In the months since Hollowbridge, since the fair and the trap at Thornfield and all the narrow roads between, Ivo had learned to read Sera Vane the way she read animals — not by what she said but by the small weather of her body. Right now the weather said: *she has noticed something is wrong, and she will not let it go.*

"Out with it," Sera said. "You've been carrying something for three days. You carry it the way Bram carried his pack after he wrenched his shoulder — too carefully, like it might spill."

"It might."

"Ivo."

The cat — old Grey-ears, who had followed them out of Highmoor and refused every suggestion that he stop — lifted his head from Odji's bedroll and looked at Ivo with one yellow eye. He said nothing. He had said little since the sewers, since *the wardens stir*, and when he did speak it was always in that dry, unimpressed tone of a creature who had seen a hundred years of humans making fools of themselves. But tonight even his silence felt like a witness.

"Walk with me," Ivo said.

They walked to the edge of the hollow, where the land fell away toward a stream running loud with spring melt. The moon was three nights from full and already fat and pale, hanging over the black shapes of the eastern hills — the hills they'd have to cross to reach the free cities, past Cassian's pickets, past Elske's patrols, past everything.

Ivo stopped where the ground leveled off and made herself turn around.

"When we were in the vaults under the citadel," she said, "and the gate cracked — you asked me afterward why I wasn't more frightened."

"You said you were."

"I lied." She kept her voice level. It took effort. "I've known since before Thornfield. Since before the council hall, maybe. The original prophecy — the first hand, before someone corrected it — Sera, I read it. All of it. Not just the parts about wardens rising."

Sera went very still. Scholars, Ivo had learned, went still the way prey animals did: total stillness, ears forward, every sense aimed.

"And?" Sera said.

"The gate doesn't open for power. It doesn't open for bloodline or armies or cleverness. The first hand is very clear." Ivo made herself say the words plainly, because she had rehearsed them plainly a hundred times in her head and if she dressed them up now she would lose her nerve entirely. "*The wardens wake only when a speaker gives freely what no law can take.* That's the line. A beast-speaker's willing sacrifice. Life given, not taken. That's the key the gate wants."

The stream filled the silence for a long moment.

"No," Sera said.

"Sera—"

"*No.*" Her voice cracked upward, and somewhere behind them in the dark, Bram shifted in his sleep. She lowered it, which somehow made it worse — a furious whisper, pressed flat. "No, that's — Ivo, that's the first hand. The first hand also claimed beast-speakers started the last war. We proved the first hand lies. We *proved* it. The marginalia—"

"The marginalia corrects the lie about the war," Ivo said gently. "It doesn't correct this. I've been over those pages forty times. The second hand leaves the sacrifice intact. If anything—" she hated saying it, hated the way the words tasted, "—if anything, the corrections make it clearer. The second hand isn't softening the price. It's insisting the price be paid *willingly*. Freely. That's the whole point. A gate built by guardians can't be forced open by a victim. It has to be opened by someone who chooses it."

"Then it can stay shut."

"And Cassian binds the wardens instead. And the free cities burn one by one, starting with whichever one he reaches first, and every gifted child in them goes to his witch-finders." Ivo spread her hands. "You've seen the refugees, Sera. You've heard what the animals remember coming out of the occupied towns. I've heard worse. I hear it in my sleep."

"So your answer is to die."

"My answer is to pay."

"They're the same thing!"

"Not to me." And here Ivo found, to her own surprise, that she was calm — calmer than she had been any night for weeks, because the secret was finally out of her chest and into the air, and air could be argued with. "Sera. Twelve years I hid. Twelve years I made myself small and harmless and quiet, sold luck-charms and never spoke so much as a word to a sparrow where anyone could see. I had one friend and he was a cat, and half the time I wasn't sure he liked me." Behind them, Grey-ears made a low sound that was unmistakably offense. "And then you found my face in a book, and everything burned, and I ran — and somewhere on that road I stopped hiding. I spoke through a hundred birds in front of the whole council. I let the whole world know what I am. Do you know what that was like?"

Sera's jaw was tight. "Tell me."

"It was like setting down a weight I'd forgotten I was carrying. For the first time in my life I belonged somewhere, and it wasn't a place, it was — people. You. Bram and Odji and Maren. Even that impossible cat." Her voice caught and she let it. "So don't tell me I don't understand what I'd be giving up. I understand better than anyone alive. That's exactly why it has to be me, and why it has to be willing. The gate won't accept less. And I won't give it less, because less wouldn't save any of you."

Sera turned away from her. Stared out at the moonlit hills. When she spoke again, her voice had gone dangerously quiet and precise, which Ivo recognized as the scholar's version of a drawn blade.

"How long."

"What?"

"How long have you known? Exactly how long have you been walking beside us, eating our bread, letting Maren spend her life healing Bram, letting Odji rig escape routes, letting *me* lose sleep over the marginalia — how long have you known you intended to walk into that gate and not come out?"

Ivo hesitated.

"*How long?*"

"Since the night after the vaults," Ivo admitted. "When the stone beasts whispered my name. Maybe — honestly, maybe since the sewers. Since Grey-ears said the wardens stir. Some part of me knew what 'stirring' meant. What waking costs."

"Six weeks." Sera laughed, and there was nothing warm in it. "Six weeks you've been planning your funeral and letting us love you."

"That's not fair."

"Isn't it?" She rounded on Ivo, and the moonlight showed her eyes bright and wet and furious. "You talk about belonging like it's something you finally earned, and then in the same breath you plan to leave it! You want to *keep* the memory of us — you said so, tonight, memorizing, in case you need to remember — like we're a market night you're storing up against a hard winter! We are not a charm, Ivo! We are not something you tuck away and carry! We are people who are going to be *here* — after the gate, after the war, after — and you are planning to make us bury you and call it peace!"

The words landed like stones. Ivo stood in them and didn't step back, because stepping back would only prove Sera right.

"If I don't," she said quietly, "there may not be an *after* for anyone. You told the council yourself — the corrected prophecy names a blood moon and a burning gate, and the blood moon is nineteen days away. Cassian's engineers are already widening the breach in the vaults. If he gets binding-runes onto those wardens—"

"Then we find another way!"

"There is no other way in the text!"

"Then we write one!" Sera's voice broke loose entirely now, echoing off the hollow's rim. "Do you know what scholarship *is*, Ivo Brightwater? It is the refusal to accept that the text is finished! Every correction, every marginal note, every argument in every archive for a thousand years — it's all people refusing to let the dead have the last word! You think the second hand corrected the first because they were feeling tidy? Someone fought that book. Someone bled over that book, a hundred years ago, trying to change what it said — and they changed it! They couldn't finish the job, but they started it, and I am going to *finish* it!"

"Sera—"

"I have spent my whole life," Sera said, and now the fury was shaking, "being told that old words are fixed and fate is fixed and the powerful get to decide what the truth means. Cassian builds his whole war on a prophecy he's edited. And you — you, of all people — you want to hand him the ending? You want to agree with the oldest lie in the book, that some people exist to be spent?"

"That's not what I—"

"Then what *is* it? Tell me what it is, if it isn't that."

Ivo opened her mouth. Closed it. Because the honest answer was ugly: part of her had always believed it. Part of her had grown up in a city that treated her kind as fuel for other people's safety, and some buried corner of her had absorbed the lesson so deep she hadn't even seen it until Sera was shouting it at her under a fattening moon.

"I don't know," she said at last, and it cost her more than the confession about the gate had. "Maybe some of it is that. Maybe I've been telling myself it's duty when it's — habit. Being the one who pays. It's the only role anyone ever gave me."

Sera's anger didn't vanish — Ivo doubted anything short of a decade would manage that — but it changed shape, the way a storm changes when it hits high ground. She came closer. Took Ivo's face in both ink-stained hands, the way you'd hold something you were afraid might bolt.

"Listen to me," she said. "You are not the price. You are not the payment. You are the person who fed a stray cat for twelve years in a city that would have hanged her for it, because kindness was worth the risk. That's who walks into whatever comes next. And she does it *alive*."

"Sera, the text—"

"The text has been wrong before. The text has been *lied in* before. And I am telling you, as a scholar of the University of Highmoor, that I will tear that book apart page by page, glyph by glyph, down to the sheep the vellum came from, before I let it spend you." Her thumbs brushed the tears off Ivo's cheeks — Ivo hadn't realized she was crying. "You confessed. Good. Now hear my vow, because I mean it the way you meant yours: I will find another way. Not to comfort you. To *beat* you. To beat the first hand and the second hand and every dead scribe who thought a life was a key."

Above them, wings stirred. Ivo felt it before she saw it — the faint pressure of many small minds waking along the ridge, owls and nightjars and one indignant crow, all turning toward the hollow. Toward her. They did this sometimes now; the birds had been gathering at the edges of her days ever since the council hall, as if word had passed through the sky itself that the speaker was on the move.

But tonight they weren't gathering to listen.

They were gathering to watch.

*Ivo,* said Grey-ears, from the dark behind them, and his voice was older than usual, and careful. *The stone ones below are not asleep the way the book says. Sleep is for creatures who dream of waking. Those beneath the gate dream of being* answered. *Ask your scholar what happens to a lock that has waited a century for a key — whether it truly demands the key's destruction, or only the key's surrender.*

Ivo went cold. "Grey-ears. Say that again."

The cat emerged into the moonlight, tail curled, unimpressed as ever. *Which part?*

"The part about surrender. Versus destruction."

*Surrender,* the cat repeated, tasting the word. *A giving-over. Not a giving-up. Your dead scribes wrote in a hurry, girl, and in fear, and fear makes poor translators. Ask the rats sometime. Rats have excellent memories and no reverence whatsoever.*

Sera had gone rigid, her scholar's mind visibly seizing hold of the thing. "Surrender," she whispered. "Not sacrifice. The first hand says *sacrifice*. But if the original tongue behind it was — if it was a rendering of an older word, a word that meant surrender of the *secret*, the self, the hiddenness — oh. Oh, I need the archive copy. I need the pre-correction folio in the free cities, the one the council sealed. Ivo. *Ivo.* If the translation drifted even one generation—"

"Then the gate doesn't want my death," Ivo said slowly. Wondering. Terrified to hope. "It wants — everything I've been hiding. Laid bare. Given over. Forever."

*A door opens for honesty,* said Grey-ears, *or it stays shut forever. Doors are simple that way.*

Sera grabbed Ivo's shoulders, and she was laughing and crying at once, and it was the least scholarly sound Ivo had ever heard her make. "Nineteen days. I need nineteen days and that sealed folio and every scrap of the second hand's notes, and I need you to promise me — *promise* me — that you do not go near that gate, do not offer that gate so much as a polite greeting, until I've finished. Promise me you'll fight the book with me instead of for it."

Down in the hollow, Bram snored. Odji muttered something about locks in his sleep. Maren's thin breathing went on and on, stubborn as a candle in wind.

Ivo looked at the moon, nineteen days from blood. She looked at the woman holding her shoulders like an anchor holds a ship. She thought about weights, and which ones a person actually had to carry alone, and which ones had only ever felt that way because no one had offered to share them.

"I promise," she said. "We fight it together."

And far beneath the earth, in the cracked dark of the warden-gate, something ancient turned over in its long waiting — and listened.
