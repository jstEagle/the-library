## 15. The Free Cities Council

The free cities announced themselves to Sera the way a good book announces itself: by smell. Ink and tallow and river-mud, salt off the harbor, roasting chestnuts from a cart on the quay — a hundred small honest smells layered over one another, none of them trying to be anything but what they were. After three weeks of ditches and hedgerows and Odji's cooking (which smelled like an apology), it was nearly enough to make her weep.

"Stop gawking," Bram said, hunched in his borrowed fishmonger's coat. "Gawkers get remembered."

"I am *observing*," Sera said. "It's a scholarly discipline."

"It's a way to get us all hanged."

"Those are not mutually exclusive."

Ivo laughed — a real laugh, unguarded, the kind Sera had coaxed out of her maybe six times since Hollowbridge — and even that was worth the road. The cat rode on Ivo's shoulder with the dignity of an ambassador, tail curled just so, surveying the free city of Vellamar as though he had personally commissioned it.

They had come, all this way, for a hall.

The Council of the Free Cities met in the old customs house, a building designed by someone who believed that intimidation was best achieved through architecture: pillars thick as oaks, a ceiling painted with the nine cities' crests, and a semicircle of seats raised so high above the floor that petitioners had to crane their necks like children scolded by geography itself. Sera had read about it. Reading had not prepared her for how *small* it made a person feel, standing on the marble below while nine delegations looked down.

They were not the only petitioners that morning. A delegation of wool merchants went first, arguing tariffs with the passion most people reserved for funerals. Then a harbormaster's grievance. Then, mercifully, their turn.

Sera climbed the petitioner's steps with the prophecy book wrapped in oilcloth against her chest, feeling its weight like a second heart. Behind her came Ivo, calm-faced, hands folded, wearing the mask she'd worn for twelve years in Highmoor — the pleasant nothing-expression of a woman who wanted no trouble. Bram flanked her left, Odji her right, both dressed as hired guards, which required no acting whatsoever since they kept scanning the balconies for crossbows anyway.

Maren had insisted on coming, pale as milk, leaning on a stick. Sera had argued. Maren had won. She usually did, in that quiet way of hers, by simply refusing to lose.

"Petitioners," said the Speaker of the Council, a broad man with a voice like a landslide, "state your names and business."

"Sera Vane, formerly of the University at Highmoor." Her voice carried better than she expected. Years of lecturing empty rooms would do that. "I bring warning of Lord Cassian's movements, and evidence concerning the true history of the last war."

A murmur ran the crescent of seats. Somewhere above, a door opened and shut.

"The Highmoor matter has been addressed," said a woman from the Aurel delegation, silk-voiced, bored. "We have Cassian's own envoys' assurances that—"

"You have his *lies*," Sera said. "Assurances are cheap. He gave Hollowbridge assurances too."

The name landed. She saw it land — a flicker in half a dozen faces, the way people flinch from a wound they've heard about but never seen. Good. Let them flinch.

She unwrapped the book and held it up. "This is a century-old prophetic text, catalogued in the University archives under seal. I have spent months on its marginalia. The primary hand — the version every scholar has quoted for a hundred years — says the last war began when beast-speakers broke the ancient wards out of ambition." She turned pages with deliberate slowness, letting the council watch her do it. "But there is a second hand in these margins. Older corrections, in different ink, by a different writer. And that hand tells another story entirely."

She read it aloud. The wardens were not broken by beast-speakers. They were *raised* by them. Guardians, not traitors. The war's origin had been edited — carefully, deliberately, by whoever wanted magic outlawed and beast-speakers feared — and the correction had been hidden in plain sight for a hundred years because no one reads margins.

Silence, then noise. Half the crescent erupted at once. The Aurel delegate was on her feet saying something about sedition; a young delegate from Corvess was shouting that he'd *said so*, he'd always said so; the Speaker pounded for order with a gavel that wouldn't have bruised an egg.

"They're going to talk themselves to death," Odji murmured behind her.

"They usually do," Sera murmured back. "Watch the ones who aren't talking."

She watched. Three delegates sat very still through the whole storm. One of them, a grey-bearded man from the inland city of Drossen, was looking not at her but at Ivo — long, measuring, the look of a man doing arithmetic with lives.

When order finally limped back, the Speaker rubbed his temples. "Extraordinary claims, Scholar Vane. Marginalia can be forged. Books can be planted. You ask nine cities to break a peace treaty, raise armies, and brand themselves liars to Cassian on the word of—" he gestured vaguely at the book, "—ink."

"I ask you to send riders to verify it," Sera said. "I ask you to look at the ink age yourself. I'll teach your own archivists the method in an afternoon."

"And the rest?" The Drossen delegate spoke at last, mild as milk. "You also claim, Scholar, that the wardens beneath Highmoor can be woken. That requires a beast-speaker. Are you proposing we take that on faith as well?"

The trap, Sera thought. There it is. Say yes, and Ivo becomes a specimen to be examined, owned, bargained over. Say no, and the whole petition collapses into theory.

"We propose you take nothing on faith," Sera said carefully. "We propose you consider that Cassian believes it. His armies march. His witch-finders burn towns. Men don't spend blood on things they think are fables."

"Convenient," said the Aurel woman.

"Yes," Sera agreed pleasantly. "Most true things are."

That got a surprised bark of laughter from the Corvess bench, quickly smothered.

The Drossen delegate leaned forward. "Then give us something better than ink, Scholar. Give us proof." He spread his hands, reasonable, almost kind. "You travel with five companions. One of them, my sources suggest, is rather more than a guard."

Every eye slid down the steps to Ivo.

Sera felt Ivo go still beside her — that particular stillness, the hunted stillness, the one she'd worn in Highmoor market squares for twelve years. Sera's hand found the edge of the book and gripped it. *Don't*, she wanted to say. *You don't owe them anything. We can walk out. We can find another way.*

But Ivo stepped forward instead.

And then, before anyone could speak — before Sera could even draw breath to object — the windows happened.

There were high clerestory windows along the dome, the kind built for light and never cleaned. As the council watched, they began to fill. Sparrows first, dozens, pouring through gaps in the stonework. Then starlings, a dark ribbon of them spiraling down into the rafters. Pigeons from the eaves, jackdaws from the harbor cranes, a single enormous gull that landed on the Speaker's podium and regarded him with the flat contempt of its kind. Within a minute the great hall held what Sera could only describe as a parliament — a hundred birds or more, perched along beams and balustrades and the shoulders of statues, and every head turned toward Ivo Brightwater.

The guards moved. Spears came up. Bram's hand went to his belt; Odji shifted to cover Maren.

"No," Ivo said clearly, and the word rang strange in the vaulted air, doubled somehow, echoed in a hundred small throats — *no, no, no* — a chorus of sparrows saying it in their thin voices, the gull booming it, the starlings rippling it down the rafters like wind through wheat.

The hall went silent in a way Sera had never heard a room go silent. Not respectful silence. *Prey* silence.

"My name is Ivo Brightwater," she said, and each bird said it with her, a hundred voices braided into one, so that the words seemed to come from the walls themselves, from the dome, from everywhere. "I am a beast-speaker. I have been one since I was seven years old. For twelve years in Highmoor I hid it, because your laws taught me that the only safe thing to be was nothing at all."

She wasn't shouting. That was the terrible part, Sera thought. She didn't need to. The birds made quiet into thunder.

"Cassian will tell you the wardens are weapons he can bind. Your ancestors' books will tell you beast-speakers started the last war. Both are lies, and I can prove the second one, because I have spent my life being exactly what those books say to fear." She lifted her chin, and Sera saw — really saw, for the first time — that the mask was gone. Not slipped. *Removed.* Set down like a heavy thing finally allowed to be put down. "Here is your proof. A hundred creatures in a room full of men with spears, and not one feather harmed. Ask yourselves what a frightened weapon would do."

The gull on the Speaker's podium shuffled its feet. It was, absurdly, the loudest sound in the hall.

"The wardens beneath Highmoor sleep," Ivo went on. "Cassian is digging toward them. If he wakes them bound to his will, no army of yours stops him. If they wake free — if someone wakes them *right* — they will hold the passes and the rivers and the gates, and your cities will stand for another hundred years. That is what I came to say. That is everything I am."

She let the silence sit. Then, gently, she nodded once, and the birds rose — all together, one motion, a soft thunder of wings — and poured back out through the clerestory windows into the cold bright morning, leaving the hall smelling faintly of feathers and open sky.

Ivo turned to the council, alone now, ordinary, just a tired woman on marble stairs.

"Well," she said. "Now you know what I am. The question is what *you* are."

* * *

They gave the company rooms in the customs house annex, which was either hospitality or house arrest depending on how generously one interpreted locked doors, and the council argued for eleven hours. Sera knew because she counted, pacing the corridor outside the chamber doors, listening to the argument swell and fade like weather.

Odji sat on the floor whittling a new lock mechanism out of a chair leg. "Eleven hours," he said. "For nine cities to decide whether to live. My grandmother decided faster about whether to eat a suspicious mushroom."

"What did she decide?" Bram asked.

"She ate it. Said regret was for people with time." He shaved a curl of wood. "Council's worse than the mushroom. At least the mushroom only kills you."

Maren slept in a chair by the window, grey-faced, and every hour or so Ivo checked on her without seeming to, the way she fed the stray cat back in Highmoor — quietly, sideways, as if kindness needed a disguise even now. But it didn't, anymore. That was the thing Sera kept noticing. Since the hall, Ivo walked differently. Lighter. As though the mask had been holding up more than her face.

Near dusk the doors opened and the Drossen delegate came out himself, grey beard and careful eyes, and asked to speak with "the speaker and her scholar."

They met him in the annex's little reading room, which contained four books, two of which were tax ledgers. He closed the door himself.

"You should know how the vote stands," he said without preamble. "Corvess, Sellet, and my own Drossen will fight. Aurel and two others want to send envoys to Cassian — appeasement, dressed up as diplomacy. The rest will follow whichever side looks likelier to survive." He folded his hands. "Your demonstration was remarkable, Mistress Brightwater. It also terrified half the council. Fear votes for appeasement more often than courage does."

"Then we have three cities," Sera said. "Against Cassian's war machine."

"You have three cities and a problem." He looked at Ivo. "The gate. If it must be woken soon — before Cassian reaches it — then the fighting cities need time to muster, and Cassian's vanguard is ten days from Highmoor. Perhaps less. Can you reach the gate before he does?"

Ivo was quiet a moment. When she answered, her voice was steady, but Sera heard the thing underneath it, the thing they'd spoken of once by firelight and never again: *the gate asks a price.*

"I can reach it," Ivo said.

"And the price the old texts mention?"

Ivo glanced at Sera — a quick, private look, *don't* — and said, "There's always a price. The question is who pays it."

The old man studied her for a long moment, and Sera realized he understood more than he was saying. "Then here is what the three of us can offer. Escort to Highmoor. A letter of passage. And our voices in the hall, for whatever they're worth, while you're gone." He rose, joints cracking like green wood. "One more thing, freely given. The Aurel delegation received a visitor last night. A woman. Grey cloak, soldier's bearing, face like winter. She spoke with their leader for an hour and left before dawn."

Sera's stomach dropped. "Describe her again."

"Tall. Scarred knuckles. Carried herself like someone used to being obeyed." He paused at the door. "The Aurel envoy called her Captain."

Elske. In the city. In the halls. Already working the vote.

Bram cursed fluently and at length when the delegate had gone. Odji stopped whittling. Maren woke and asked what was wrong, and nobody wanted to tell her, and told her anyway.

"She won't hurt the council," Ivo said slowly. "That's not her way. She'll make them *afraid* — show them what happens to cities that resist. She'll offer terms. She's good at terms." A ghost of something crossed her face — not quite warmth, not quite grief. "And she knows me. Knows what I am. If she tells the Aurel bloc what I intend to do at the gate..."

"Then we leave tonight," Sera said. "Before she can poison the well."

"Tonight," Bram agreed. "Grain wagons leave the east quay at moonrise. I've already made friends with the driver."

"You've been here two days," Odji said.

"I'm likable."

Outside the window, the sun went down over the harbor, and the first stars came out over the free cities — three of which, perhaps, would still be free by spring. Sera packed the prophecy book with hands that were steadier than she felt, and caught Ivo watching her across the little room.

"Whatever happens at the gate," Ivo said quietly, "thank you. For the margins. For reading what everyone else ignored."

"That's the whole job," Sera said. "Everyone writes the big text. History hides in the margins."

Ivo smiled — and it was the unmasked smile now, the real one, and it made Sera feel like they'd already won something no council could vote away.

Above the harbor, unseen, a single grey-cloaked figure stood on the customs house roof, watching the lit windows of the annex, and did not move for a long time.

Below, in the dark of the east quay, grain wagons began to roll.
