## 16. The Trap at Thornfield

Odji had counted the envoy's fingers twice that morning, and both times there had been ten of them, which was more than Odji himself could say. He considered this a fair warning about the man.

"Envoy Pell," he said, for the third time since dawn, "the road through Thornfield narrows at the mill. If we're ambushed anywhere, it'll be there."

Envoy Pell of the Free Cities Council smiled the way men smile when they have never once been ambushed and consider this a personal accomplishment. "Master Odji. I appreciate your concern. But we have six soldiers, two wagons, and the blessing of the council. Cassian's forces are days away."

"The rats said otherwise."

"The *rats* said otherwise." Pell repeated it to his fellow envoys across the fire, delighted, as though Odji were a traveling curiosity rather than the only reason any of them would see the free cities again.

Odji let it go. He had learned — mostly from watching Ivo swallow worse insults with better grace — that you could not argue a man out of his safety. You could only be standing next to him when it ran out.

He checked his tools instead. That was the ritual, and rituals kept a one-handed locksmith sane: picks in their oiled roll along his left forearm, tension wrenches sized by feel, the little hooked probe he'd ground himself from a wagon spring because no smith in three cities would sell to a man with a stump where his right hand used to be. Eleven tools. He could name each one's weight blind. He had once opened a council vault in Vellmark using four of them and a hairpin borrowed off a duchess, and he'd returned the hairpin polished, which was the kind of courtesy that got you remembered fondly in places that would otherwise remember you not at all.

"You're doing the thing," Maren said, settling beside him. She nodded at his hands. "The counting thing."

"I'm always doing the counting thing."

"I know. It's very soothing. Like watching someone pray."

"It *is* praying," Odji said. "Just with better results."

Across the clearing, Bram was teaching two of Pell's soldiers how to cook without turning oats into a war crime, and Ivo sat with her back against a wagon wheel, eyes half-closed, listening — really listening — to something in the hedgerow. A wren hopped down and stood on her boot like it was delivering a report. Sera was reading. She was always reading, the prophecy book open on her knees even now, even on the road, its cracked leather cover soft as an old dog's ear. She'd found the marginalia, the second hand correcting the first, and she guarded that book the way Odji guarded his picks: like the world had already tried to take it once and might try again.

Elske rode point. Captain Elske, late of Cassian's witch-finders, currently of nowhere in particular, wearing her defection like an ill-fitting coat she hadn't decided to keep. She didn't talk much. When she did, it was usually useful, which Odji respected the way he respected good lockwork: grudgingly, and without warmth.

"Mill ahead," she called back, reining in. "I'll ride through first."

"There," Odji muttered to Maren. "That's a woman who counts."

* * *

Thornfield was pretty in the way of places about to stop being pretty. The mill stood where the road pinched between a stone wall and the river, its wheel turning slow and fat and stupid in the current. Odji hated it immediately and completely. Good ambush country. He'd have picked it himself.

He caught Ivo's eye as they approached. She was frowning at the hedgerows, at the crows sitting too quiet on the fence posts, at a farm dog chained near the gate that wouldn't look at the road at all.

"Dogs know," she'd told him once, early on, back when the company still slept in shifts and nobody trusted anybody's cooking. "Dogs always know when bad men are coming. They just can't tell anyone who listens."

Odji listened now. The dog was staring very hard at a fence post, ears flat, making itself small.

"Elske," he said quietly.

"I see it." Her hand had gone to her sword. "Pell. Get your people off the road. Now."

Pell laughed. Actually laughed, a short bright bark of a sound. "Captain, if this is some test of loyalty—"

The hedge moved.

Not swayed — *moved*, the way a blanket moves when something under it turns over, and then the hedge was full of soldiers, crossbows first, and Odji learned later there had been forty of them folded into the green like a letter into an envelope, waiting on Pell's own route, on Pell's own schedule, which meant—

Which meant Pell had talked. Or written. Or simply been frightened enough, weeks ago in some candlelit corridor, to pass along what he knew to a man who promised it would keep his family safe. Odji didn't know yet. He would find out. But in the moment there was no time for finding out, because the first bolt took a soldier off his feet and the dogs started screaming and the whole pretty morning tore open.

"Off the road!" Bram roared, hauling a wagon horse around by its bridle. "River side! GO!"

What saved them — what saved most of them — was that Odji had spent the last mile being annoying. He'd noted the mill's side door, the one with the old iron padlock rusted to decoration. He'd noted the wall's drainage grate, barred, with bars corroded thin at the base. While Pell had been smiling at his own importance, Odji had been doing the counting thing, and the counting thing paid out now like a loan to a friend.

"Grate!" he shouted at Maren, pointing. "Bars are rotten, kick low!"

She kicked low. The bars went with a crunch, and Maren went through into the dark under the wall, and Odji shoved the healer Wenna after her, then turned back for the others.

It was noise and smoke and the terrible specific chaos of an ambush done well. Crossbow bolts hissed past like angry geese. One of Pell's soldiers froze entirely, which happened to good soldiers sometimes, and Odji grabbed the man's collar and dragged him bodily toward the grate, shouting at him with words he'd learned in dockside Vellmark that would have gotten him arrested in any respectable city.

Ivo was in the middle of it, and this was the part Odji would remember afterward, the part that made his chest hurt in a way he refused to examine: she wasn't running. She had her hands up, palms out, and she was *yelling at the horses*. The wagon team had panicked, bolts in their harness, screaming the way horses scream, and they were about to bolt straight through the crossbow line and die for it. And Ivo stood in front of them, small and furious and utterly unhidden, and said something in that voice she used — low, carrying, the voice of someone speaking a language older than fear — and the horses stopped. Stood. Turned, gentle as Sunday, away from the arrows, and carried the wagon and two wounded soldiers down to the river shallows instead.

"Go!" she screamed at Odji over their backs. "Get them out! I'm right behind you!"

She was lying. He knew she was lying, because behind her, at the head of the ambush, a figure in witch-finder grey was walking through the smoke with a drawn sword and a face like winter, and that face was looking at exactly one person.

"Elske!" Odji bellowed. "ELSKE! Ivo's boxed!"

And here was the thing about Elske, the thing Odji still couldn't fit together in his head: she turned. She cut two of her own former comrades out of Ivo's path — actually cut them, hard and economical, no flourish — and reached Ivo just as a net came out of the hedgerow, weighted lead weights whistling, and it wasn't aimed at Ivo at all.

It was aimed at Sera.

Sera had done the bravest and stupidest thing available, which was to stand over the fallen prophecy book with her arms spread like a mother goose over one egg. The net took her full in the back. She went down with a sound Odji felt in his teeth, and a soldier snatched the book off the grass before Bram could reach it, and Elske got a hand on Ivo's collar and physically threw her toward the river — threw her, captain's strength, no apology in it — and then turned back alone into the closing ring of grey coats.

"No—" Ivo scrambled up, mud to her elbows. "No, no, NO—"

"Grate. NOW." Odji caught her arm. She was strong with panic, stronger than him, and for one horrible second it was a real fight between them, her grief against his grip.

"They have her, Odji, they have the book, they have—"

"And if you stay, they have you too, and then it's all been for nothing, every bit of it, so MOVE."

Something in his voice got through — maybe the fury, maybe the fact that it was Odji, who never raised his voice, whose anger was a rare and serious weather event. She moved. Bram came last, bleeding from the scalp, dragging two soldiers between them, and Odji counted heads through the grate like a miser counting coins: Maren, Wenna, four soldiers, Bram, Ivo. Nine. Plus himself, ten.

Eleven had gone in that morning.

He did the arithmetic once and then made himself stop, because the number didn't change no matter how many times you counted it, and some numbers you carry instead.

* * *

They hid until dark in a culvert upstream, cold to the waist in shallow water, listening to Thornfield's dogs bark themselves hoarse. Pell was dead — Odji had seen the bolt take him, had felt, absurdly, almost sorry for the fool. Two of the council's soldiers were dead. And Sera Vane was in a witch-finder wagon heading east toward Highmoor with the corrected prophecy in enemy hands.

Ivo sat apart, soaked, silent, feeding crumbs to a water rat that had come to check on her. Even now. Even gutted with guilt, she couldn't not talk to something small and frightened. Odji understood that better than anyone knew.

"This is my fault," she said finally, to the rat, or the river, or possibly to him.

"Don't be stupid."

"She was taken because of me. Because of what I am. Because Cassian wants—"

"He was taken because of *me*," Odji said, and heard his own voice come out flat and strange. "I saw the mill. I saw the pinch in the road. I told everyone, and then I got us through the grate and out the other side, and I counted eleven going in and nine coming out, and I left her." His hand — his one hand, his good clever hand that had never failed him — closed on nothing. "I do locks, Ivo. Locks are honest. You push, they hold; you pick them, they open. People aren't locks. People are—" He gestured helplessly at the whole wet miserable day. "People are doors that lie about what's behind them."

"Pell lied," Maren said quietly. From her, the judgment landed soft but true. "Or he was frightened, and his fear lied for him. Either way, that's not your door, Odji."

"Feels like my door."

"That's because you're the kind of man who checks every lock in a house he doesn't live in," she said, and there was the ghost of her old warmth in it, tired but real. "It's a virtue. Mostly. Tonight it's just a wound."

Bram came back from scouting the road, moving like a man made of bruise. "Wagon tracks go east. Elske rode with them." He hesitated. "She cut down two of her own to reach Ivo. Then she let them take Sera anyway."

"Because taking Sera was the mission," Odji said. "Cutting down her own men was the discount she could afford."

Nobody answered that. Across the culvert, Ivo had gone very still, and Odji watched her look east along the dark road, toward Highmoor, toward the citadel, toward whatever cell held Sera and the book that said the wardens could rise — and he watched her arrive somewhere, some decision, the way you watch a lock finally give under steady pressure.

"We get her back," Ivo said. Not loudly. Not dramatically. Just a fact, set down on the table like one of Odji's picks. "Whatever it costs, however long it takes. We get her back, and we get the book, and then we finish what we started."

"How?" one of the surviving soldiers asked. "Highmoor's a fortress. We're nine wet people in a ditch."

Odji looked at his tools, all eleven of them, laid out on a flat stone to dry. Honest things. Things that worked.

"Every lock has a key," he said. "And every prison has a door somebody built, which means every prison has a door somebody can unbuild." He started rolling the picks back into their oiled leather, one by one, the counting thing, the praying thing. "We've got friends in Highmoor who owe me favors. Rats who know the drains better than the engineers do. And somewhere in that citadel is a frightened guard with a family and a price."

He cinched the roll tight and looked up at Ivo, and managed — barely, crookedly — the thing that was almost a smile.

"Trust me. I count everything."

Far to the east, thunder rolled over Highmoor, or perhaps it was only the sound of a great many boots, marching.
