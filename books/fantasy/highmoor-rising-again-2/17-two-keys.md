## 17. Two Keys

The cell in Highmoor's citadel had been built for someone much more interesting than Sera Vane.

She had established this by the second day. The walls were scratched with tally marks going back decades — some prisoner had counted four hundred and eleven days before giving up, and the marks simply stopped, which Sera tried very hard not to think about at night. There was a slot in the door for bread. There was a bucket she declined to describe even in her own thoughts. And there was a window, high and narrow, that looked out on nothing but sky, which was either a mercy or an insult depending on her mood.

Her mood, lately, leaned toward insult.

They had taken the prophecy book, of course. That was the worst of it — worse than the manacles, worse than the interrogation that had been less interrogation than a long, bored lecture about the consequences of sedition delivered by a witch-finder with dandruff. Somewhere above her, in Cassian's citadel, a book she had spent three years of her life deciphering sat in a soldier's evidence locker, being *misfiled*, probably. Alphabetized wrong. She had told them as much during the lecture. They had not appreciated it.

On the fourth day, the door opened without warning, and Captain Elske stepped inside.

Sera had seen her twice before — once across the chaos of Thornfield, once at a distance through the fair crowds — and neither time had prepared her for how *quiet* the woman was. Witch-finders in the stories were all bared teeth and burning brands. Elske moved like someone entering a sickroom. She carried a single lantern, set it on the floor, and sat down on the stool provided for the guard, folding her hands in her lap like a woman settling in for a difficult conversation with a relative.

"Scholar Vane."

"Captain." Sera remained sitting on the cot. It seemed important, somehow, not to stand for her. "You've come to finish the lecture? I have notes."

Something flickered at the corner of Elske's mouth. It might have been the ghost of amusement, or it might have been indigestion. "You're not afraid of me."

"I'm terrified of you," Sera said honestly. "But fear and respect are different rooms in the same house, and I find I can only furnish one at a time."

The flicker again. Longer this time. "The book you were carrying. The prophecy. My men say it's heresy. Lord Cassian says it's forgery — beast-speaker propaganda planted to undermine the realm."

"And what do *you* say?"

Elske was silent for a moment. In the lantern light her face was all planes and patience, a face that had learned to wait out other people's lies the way stone waits out rain. "I say Lord Cassian has never read a book he didn't burn. I say his scholars are chosen for their loyalty, not their eyesight. And I say that when my men brought me your effects, there was a page folded into your boot that they missed, because they searched for weapons and not for paper."

Sera's heart performed a small, undignified leap. She kept her hands still. "A page."

"A page torn from a larger work. Old. Covered in two different handwritings." Elske reached into her coat — slowly, deliberately, the way one reaches toward a strange dog — and drew out a folded square of vellum, worn soft at the creases. "I've had it four days. I've read it perhaps forty times. And I cannot sleep, scholar, because either it is a very clever forgery, or everything I have spent nine years enforcing is built on a lie."

*There it is*, Sera thought. *The crack in the wall.* She made herself lean back against the cold stones, made her voice come out bored, though her pulse was hammering somewhere around her ears. "And which do you conclude?"

"I don't conclude. I verify." Elske unfolded the page and held it up between two fingers. "This is your hand. The marginalia. Corrections to the original text — corrections dated, if I read the notation right, within living memory of the war itself. Tell me what they say. Not what you want them to say. What they say."

So this was the bargain, then. Not offered yet — tested first. Sera took a breath and let the scholar take the wheel, because the scholar knew these roads far better than the frightened woman did.

"The official history," Sera said, "the one taught in every school from here to the coast, is that the last war began when beast-speakers turned the old wardens against mankind. That is why the gift was outlawed. That is why children who talk to cats are dragged into squares and —" she stopped, adjusted, continued in a flatter voice, "— that is why the Purges happened. The prophecy book supports this reading. Its opening verses name the speakers as the source of the ruin."

"Yes."

"But the marginalia disagrees. Look at the ink — darker, iron-gall mixed differently, a later batch. This second hand belonged to someone with access to the original manuscript, someone close enough to *edit* it. And where the main text says the speakers *unleashed* the wardens, the margin says —" she recited it from memory, because of course she had it by heart, she'd had it by heart since the night she found it, "— '*not unleashed: unbound. They loosed the chains that bound the wardens to mortal will, that no king might ever ride them again. For this the kings called it betrayal, and wrote the histories in their own ink.*'"

The lantern flame bent in a draft from under the door. Neither woman moved.

"Read the rest," Sera said softly. "The part your men would have burned me for. The condition of the wardens' rising. Your lord believes the gate beneath this citadel can be *bound* — that a speaker can be forced to chain the wardens to his command. That is what the edited text promises. But the marginalia says the gate answers no master. It opens only to truth freely spoken. '*No chain, no crown, no blood but willing. The gate is a mouth, not a fist. It eats lies and gives back silence.*'" She met Elske's eyes. "Cassian is excavating a door that will never open for him. He's spending an army's worth of gold and steel digging toward a lock that requires the one key he has outlawed: a speaker who tells the truth."

Elske looked down at the page. Her thumb moved along its edge, back and forth, back and forth, a small restless motion entirely at odds with the stillness of the rest of her.

"Nine years," she said at last. Her voice had changed — lower, rougher, the sickroom voice gone, something rawer underneath. "I joined the finders at nineteen because my mother was taken in the second wave of the Purges. Not for anything she *did*. A neighbor heard her singing to the geese. Singing, scholar. The way farm women sing to geese. Someone reported it, and she was gone before supper, and I was told it was necessary, that the gift was a plague and the finders were the physicians." She laughed, short and terrible. "I became a physician so that no one could ever do to another family what was done to mine. Do you understand? I thought I was standing between the fire and the city."

"And now?"

"Now I have spent four days reading a dead archivist's handwriting, and I keep thinking about my mother's geese." Elske folded the page with great care, as though it were something wounded. "Cassian's engineers pulled three hundred tons of stone out from under the citadel looking for a weapon. If your marginalia is true, they dug up a *conscience* instead. Something that will judge him. Something that will judge—" her jaw tightened, "—all of us."

"Then we arrive at the actual bargain," Sera said. "Because I didn't survive Thornfield and a cell and your colleague's dandruff lecture just to recite marginalia to a woman who'll file it away and think about it for another four days."

Elske's eyebrows rose a fraction. "You're in no position to make demands."

"I'm in the *best* position to make demands. That's how demands work — they're made by people with nothing left to lose and something you need." Sera stood now, unable to help it, the scholar and the prisoner collapsing into one furious whole. "Here is what I know, Captain. Ivo Brightwater is alive. The free cities are arming. Bram of the Ninth is organizing every deserter between here and Hollowbridge, and half those soldiers would rather follow him than a usurper. And somewhere under your feet, three hundred tons of excavated stone is waiting for a truth-teller while Cassian plans to feed it a lie. You are standing on a hillside in a lightning storm holding the tallest object in the province, and the tallest object is *your career*."

"Go on." Elske's voice was flat, but she hadn't reached for her sword, and Sera had been around soldiers long enough to know that was the whole conversation happening underneath the words.

"Open this cell. Give me back my book. Walk me out of this citadel tonight, and when the reckoning comes — and it is coming, Captain, within the month — testify. Stand in front of whatever council survives this and tell them what the finders did in Cassian's name. Tell them about your mother and the geese. That's the price. Not freedom. *Truth.* Freely spoken." She paused, and gentled despite herself. "It's the only currency the gate accepts. Seems fitting we adopt its exchange rate."

For a long moment Elske said nothing at all. Then, quietly: "If I open that door and walk you out, there is no road back. Not to rank, not to the order, not to anything I've been for nine years."

"No," Sera agreed. "There's only the road forward. I'm told it's crowded. We pick up strays constantly. It's rather our whole method."

Something broke in Elske's face then — not dramatically, nothing so generous as tears, just a fine cracking, like ice on a pond at thaw, the sound of a long winter deciding to end. She stood, picked up the lantern, and looked at the door of the cell as if it were a gate of its own, smaller and meaner than the one under the citadel, but a gate nonetheless.

"There's a rotation change at the third bell," she said. "The east stair will be empty for six minutes. Your effects are in the evidence room, second floor, and I will personally misfile you both out of the building." She turned back, and her eyes were dry and steady and utterly changed. "One condition, scholar. When this is done — when the wardens rise or don't — I want my mother's name entered in the record. Lysa Elske of Fenrow. Taken unjustly. Let the corrected history carry *that* marginalia."

Sera felt her throat tighten. "Lysa of Fenrow," she repeated. "In ink that doesn't wash out. You have my word as a Vane — and we Vanes are insufferable about our words."

Elske almost smiled. It transformed her face entirely, the way a lamp transforms a room, revealing furniture you'd sworn wasn't there. "Third bell," she said, and was gone, and the lock turned behind her, and Sera sat back down on the cot and discovered her hands were shaking and that she was, absurdly, smiling too.

* * *

What Sera could not see from her cell — what she would not learn until later, breathless, in the evidence room's lamplight, with the prophecy book pressed to her chest like a returned child — was what the birds were doing that same night across the free cities.

She would hear it from Ivo afterward, told in Ivo's low, wondering voice, and she would write it down immediately because some things deserved better than memory:

That in a candlelit loft above a Hollowbridge chandler's shop, Ivo Brightwater had stood before forty people and spoken through forty birds at once — sparrows on rafters, a hawk on a windowsill, a fat and opinionated raven named Sergeant perched on Bram's shoulder — so that every listener heard her voice coming from the feathers nearest them, intimate and impossible, and not one of them doubted afterward that the gift was real, or that the woman who carried it was telling the truth.

That among the forty had been a midwife, two ferry-men, a girl of eleven who spoke to moths, an old soldier who confessed, weeping, that he'd understood his horse's terror for thirty years and pretended otherwise, and a Cassian tax clerk whose pigeons had been reporting to him on the usurper's grain shipments for a season — *unasked*, the clerk insisted, the pigeons had simply *liked him better*.

That Ivo had told them about the gate, and the wardens, and the truth that opened it, and had not promised any of them safety, and they had stayed anyway, pulling stools closer.

And that at the end of the night, the raven Sergeant had hopped onto Ivo's knee and croaked, in his gravelly way, a message he'd carried from the west: *Stone-woman speaks to the finder. The finder's heart turns. Two keys turn together.*

Ivo hadn't known what it meant. She'd written it down anyway, because ravens were rarely wrong about doors.

Six minutes after the third bell, a side door of Highmoor's citadel opened onto the night, and two women slipped out of it — one clutching a rescued book, one carrying nine years of doubt finally set down — and behind them, deep beneath the foundations, something ancient shifted in its sleep and listened for the sound of keys turning.

The march on the citadel would begin at dawn.
