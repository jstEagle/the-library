## 18. The March of the Usurper

The birds told Ivo first.

They always did. Before the council's runners, before the semaphore towers on the hills blinked their frantic orange, before the first refugee carts even crested the eastern road — a starling had landed on her windowsill at dawn, feathers slick with road dust, and poured a memory into her mind like water through cupped hands: smoke, and hooves, and a bridge burning.

She sat with that image for a long while, watching the light come up over the free cities' white walls, until Sera knocked and let herself in without waiting, which was how Sera knocked these days, carrying two cups of tea and the expression of a woman who had already read three books about whatever bad thing was happening and had opinions prepared.

"Vellin's fallen," Ivo said.

Sera set down the tea. "I know. The council's been in session since midnight."

"The council's been arguing since midnight. Falling is different." Ivo wrapped her hands around the cup. It was warm. She made herself notice that. "Vellin's outer wall was supposed to hold for a month. The birds say it held for four days."

"Four days," Sera repeated softly. She lowered herself into the chair across from Ivo, and for once she didn't reach for a book or a quill or any of the small scholarly armor she wore against a world that kept refusing to behave like a citation. "That's not an army anymore, Ivo. That's a flood."

Outside, the city bells began to ring — not the alarm pattern, not yet, but the low rolling toll they used for grief. Somewhere below, gates were opening to let in what was left of Vellin's people.

* * *

Ivo went down to the east gate because she couldn't not go.

The road beyond it had become a river. Carts and handbarrows and people on foot, dust rising off them in a brown haze that turned the morning sun the color of old parchment. Children carried children. An old man led a goat that had clearly decided, somewhere on the road, that it would rather die than take another step, and was being dragged with the particular patience of someone who has already lost everything else.

And there were animals. So many animals. Dogs on fraying leads, hens in baskets, a milk cow with an arrow wound in her flank walking with the terrible stoic steadiness of prey animals who have learned that limping gets you eaten.

Ivo moved among them, and the memories came whether she wanted them or not.

That was the part no one understood about her gift — the part the witch-finders' pamphlets never mentioned, all their lurid woodcuts of beasts whispering treason. It wasn't conversation. It was drowning. Every wounded animal was a window left open onto everything it had seen, and it could not close the window, and neither could she.

The milk cow showed her a farmyard at dusk, and men in Cassian's grey livery setting fire to a barn with the family still inside, not in battle, not in rage, but methodically, the way you'd clear a field for planting. The cow had stood at the fence and watched because cows watch everything, and now she would watch it forever.

A shepherd dog showed her a river crossing where soldiers had simply pushed people in. Not even hurrying. Laughing a little, the way tired men laugh at anything.

A one-eyed cat pressed against her ankle and gave her nothing but cold, and more cold, and a doorway that used to be warm.

Ivo knelt in the dust of the road with her arms around the cow's neck, doing the only thing she knew how to do — smoothing the terror down, taking some of it into herself so the animal could carry less. Her own chest went tight and gray with borrowed fear. Behind her, she heard Odji's voice cutting through the chaos, organizing, directing, his one hand moving with brisk authority as he got the weak and the hurt funneled toward Maren's aid station.

"You're doing the face," Odji said, materializing at her elbow.

"What face?"

"The one where you're absorbing half a farmyard's worth of horror and pretending it's indigestion."

"I don't have a face."

"Everyone has a face. Yours is just quieter than most." He handed her a waterskin. "Drink. You can't pour from an empty jug, and you, my friend, are down to the dregs."

She drank. It helped, a little. Around them the river of refugees kept flowing through the gate, and above it all the bells tolled their slow grief, and Ivo thought: *this is what his army does when it wins. This is what it does everywhere.*

* * *

The council convened at midday, and this time there was no argument worth the name.

Ivo stood at the back of the great hall — she'd stopped being formally barred from these sessions after the hundred-birds speech, though she noticed the councilors still arranged themselves as if hoping she might be persuaded to stand elsewhere — and watched men and women who had spent weeks splitting hairs finally see the whole head.

Councilor Ashe, who had argued longest and loudest for appeasement, stood with a list in his shaking hands. "Four thousand refugees by noon," he read. "The count isn't finished. Vellin's garrison — what survived — reports that Lord Cassian offered terms before the assault. Surrender the gifted among you, and the city is spared. They surrendered three names. Three. And he burned the city anyway."

Silence, then, the kind that has weight.

"He was never going to stop," Ashe said. "We were never going to be enough. I have spent this entire crisis believing we could pay him to go away, and I find I was negotiating with a fire." He looked around the hall. "I move that the free cities commit to full defense. All levies. All stores. Everything."

It passed without a dissenting voice, and Ivo felt no triumph at all. It was like watching someone finally call a healer after the wound had gone black.

Afterward, in the corridor, Sera caught up with her, prophecy book under her arm — the real one now, copied and recopied, its marginalia transcribed in a dozen hands so no single capture could destroy it again.

"You heard the rest?" Sera said quietly. "The war council's estimates. Cassian reaches the outer walls of the capital cities within the month. Six weeks if we're lucky."

"And the wardens are still asleep."

"The gate is worse than asleep." Sera's jaw tightened. "Bram's scouts brought back drawings from the citadel excavation. Cassian's engineers haven't stopped working. If anything, losing Thornfield made him faster — he knows we know, so he's stopped pretending. They're building something around the gate. Bindings. Chains of stone and ironwork, anchored into the vaults." She hesitated. "There's more. The cracks are spreading. Whatever's holding the wardens' prison together is failing on its own, Ivo. If the gate breaks open wrong — unguided, unbound —"

"Then what comes out isn't a guardian. It's just power with nowhere to go." Ivo had seen what the stone beasts whispered when she'd touched the gate. *Soon. Soon. Soon.* Like dogs scratching at a door. "How long?"

"The engineers' own notes say weeks. Maybe less."

Weeks. Cassian outside the walls in a month; the gate breaking beneath everyone's feet even sooner; and between those two closing jaws, a council that had only today decided to fight at all.

Ivo laughed — a small, exhausted sound that surprised even her.

"What?" Sera asked.

"Nothing. It's just — every door keeps closing, and there's exactly one left, and it's the one I've been standing in front of telling everyone I'll walk through it." She shook her head. "You promised you'd find another way."

"I'm looking."

"Sera."

"I said I'm *looking*." There was fire in it now, the scholar's fury that Ivo had learned to love and dread in equal measure. "The marginalia says the sacrifice is the secret, not the speaker. That's not a riddle, it's a translation problem, and translations can be *checked*. I need time."

"The gate doesn't have time. Cassian doesn't give time. He takes it."

Down the corridor, a runner was already hurrying toward the war rooms with fresh dispatches, and the bells were still tolling, and somewhere beneath the citadel in Highmoor, stone beasts were whispering *soon* to a woman a hundred miles away who could hear every word.

* * *

That evening, Ivo went to the stable behind the school — her school, though it still startled her to think of it that way, the little building with its mismatched desks and its seven students, each one gifted in ways small and strange and now, officially, legal.

The refugee animals had been housed there temporarily, and she walked the rows doing what she could. A lamed pony. A flock of geese huddled in bewildered silence. The milk cow from the road, whose name, Ivo had gathered from her memories, was Buttercup, and who had belonged to a girl named Tilly who was not among the living.

"You're going to be all right," Ivo told her, resting her forehead against the broad damp nose. "There's grass here. Good grass. No one will burn it."

Buttercup looked at her with the patient, bottomless eyes of cattle, and gave her a memory Ivo hadn't asked for: Tilly, small and gap-toothed, teaching the cow to come to a whistle. Then the barn burning. Then the road. Then nothing, because cows do not understand *after*, they only understand *now*, and *now* was a stranger's hand on her face saying words she couldn't parse but that sounded, in tone, like kindness.

Ivo stayed until her own eyes stopped stinging.

When she came out, the stray cat was waiting on the fence rail — old now, gray-muzzled, dignified as a magistrate. He had followed her out of Highmoor all those months ago and had never once explained why, and she had stopped asking.

"They're coming here next," the cat said. It wasn't a question. Cats deal in certainties.

"I know."

"The wardens wake soon. Or break loose. Either way, the ground will shake." He groomed one paw with maddening calm. "You should decide what you're going to do before the ground decides for you."

"That's the general consensus, yes."

"Mm." He finished the paw, considered the other. "For what it's worth — and I know you put no value on the opinions of cats — I've watched you since the market nights. You spent twelve years hiding what you were, and the hiding nearly killed you slower than any sacrifice would. Whatever door you walk through—" he flicked an ear, "—walk through it as yourself. Not as a secret wearing your face."

He leapt down and padded away into the dark, having delivered, as usual, precisely one sentence of wisdom and then refused to elaborate.

Ivo stood alone in the yard. Above her, the stars were coming out over the free cities, steady and indifferent, and far to the east she imagined she could almost hear it — the tread of an army, the groan of ancient stone, the two sounds converging on the same narrow point of time.

Every door closed but one.

She thought of Maren, spending herself in healing wards. Of Bram, staying behind in ashes to build resistance from nothing. Of Elske, somewhere in enemy lines, holding a copy of the marginalia and a choice. Of Sera, awake right now over her candles, hunting through a century of ink for a loophole in fate.

Of the gate, whispering *soon*.

"All right," Ivo said aloud, to the stars, to the cat, to the sleeping wardens, to no one. "All right. But we do it my way. We tell the truth. All of it. Even the ugly parts. Especially the ugly parts."

The wind moved through the yard, and in the stable the animals stirred, and every beast within a mile of her lifted its head at once — as if the word had passed among them, the way words pass through flocks and herds, quick as weather:

*She's ready.*

Far beneath Highmoor, in the dark of the cracked vault, the stone beasts felt it too.

And began, very softly, to sing.
