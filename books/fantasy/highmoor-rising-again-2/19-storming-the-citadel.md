## 19. Storming the Citadel

Bram had stood in battle lines before. He'd stood in them for eleven years, in fact, wearing Cassian's colors before he tore them off his own shoulders in a ditch outside Hollowbridge, and he had learned one thing about battle lines that no drill sergeant ever taught him: they were mostly waiting. The killing was brief. The waiting was where a man lived or died, and Bram had always felt he'd done more of his living in the waiting than in anything else.

Tonight the waiting was a rooftop across the processional way from Highmoor's citadel, and the man beside him was a one-handed locksmith eating a dried plum with the concentration of a scholar reading a difficult text.

"You're going to choke on that," Bram said.

Odji swallowed. "If I do, bury me facing the gate. I've walked under half the city to get here. I'd like to see the thing before I die of fruit."

Below them, the processional way was empty — swept clean, lamplit, patrolled by soldiers who walked in pairs and kept their eyes on the shadows. Above, the citadel rose against the blood-tinted clouds like a fist of black stone. Somewhere inside it, Cassian's engineers were working through the night on the warden-gate, and somewhere deeper than that, the gate itself was cracking, and somewhere deeper than that — Bram had to take this on faith, and mostly he did — the wardens were stirring, whispering a charm-seller's name through a hundred miles of stone.

"Any word?" he asked.

Odji tilted his head, listening the way they all listened now, the way you learned to listen when you traveled with Ivo Brightwater. Rats ran messages through the city's bones; pigeons carried scraps of news on the wing; even the citadel's own stable cats had been turned, quietly and without anyone's knowledge, into a network of informants who worked for sardines and affection. Bram had once asked Ivo whether it bothered her, being loved by so many animals for so little. She'd looked at him like he'd asked whether it bothered her to be loved at all.

"She says the east postern has four guards instead of twelve," Odji reported. "The engineers are arguing. Something about the third seal. And there's a dog in the kennels who says Captain Elske's horse is saddled."

Bram went still. "Saddled. Meaning she's leaving?"

"Meaning she's ready," Odji said, and smiled with half his mouth. "There's a difference. Ivo's word for it was *wavering*. Sera's word was *recruitable*. The dog just said the horse was saddled and then asked me for a sausage."

* * *

They gathered in the shell of a burned granary two streets back, and Bram looked around at what the last year had made of them and felt the old soldier's ache in his chest — the one that was equal parts terror and love, though it had taken him most of his life to learn those were the same feeling.

Ivo stood at the center, small and steady, a woman who had spent twelve years making herself invisible and now could not walk down a street in any free city without birds following her like a rumor. Sera was beside her with the prophecy book wrapped in oilcloth against her chest, the way she carried it everywhere now, like a second heartbeat. Maren sat on a grain sack, hands folded in her lap, and Bram's eyes went to her hands the way they always did — to the faint silver scarring that crept up her wrists a little further each month, the price her gift wrote on her body in its own slow ledger.

He'd stopped asking her to stop. She'd stopped pretending she might. They had an understanding, the two of them, and the understanding was that he hated it.

"Say it plain," Ivo was saying. "Once we're inside, everything depends on speed. Cassian binds the wardens at moonset. If we reach the gate before that, Sera thinks the binding fails — she thinks the gate won't accept a master. If we don't—" She didn't finish. Nobody needed her to. They'd all seen the excavation plans Odji had lifted from the blood moon fair, a year and a lifetime ago: the chains of cold iron, the binding-circles, the careful notes in an engineer's hand about *containment of the subject entities*.

"And if we're caught inside," Odji said, "we're caught inside."

"If we're caught inside," Ivo said, "run. All of you. That's not a suggestion. The gate needs me. It doesn't need—" She gestured at the room, at all of them, and Bram watched her struggle with the sentence the way she always struggled with it, because Ivo Brightwater had spent twelve years believing she was worth nothing to anyone and had only recently, and with visible effort, learned to say *I*.

"It doesn't need us to die for you," Bram finished for her. "We know. We're going to ignore it anyway."

"That's not—"

"He's right," said Maren, mildly. "We are."

Ivo looked at them, at the whole ragged lot of them — the deserter, the locksmith, the healer, the scholar, the turncoat captain waiting somewhere in the dark with her saddled horse — and something in her face did the thing it did sometimes, the thing that had taken Bram a year to name. It was the look of a woman watching a fire in a wet country. Astonishment that it could burn at all.

"All right," she said quietly. "All right. Moonset, then."

* * *

The east postern fell the way Odji had promised it would — quietly, almost apologetically, four guards who never saw more than a shadow and a hand over a mouth and Elske's own sergeant, a broad woman named Petra with a face like a closed door, saying *stand down, that's an order* in a voice that made standing down feel like the only sane act left in the world.

Elske herself met them inside the wall, and Bram watched her and Ivo regard each other across three feet of torchlight with the wary respect of two people who had each, at one point, held the other's life in their hands and chosen — for their own reasons — not to close their fist.

"Captain," Ivo said.

"Brightwater." Elske's eyes went over the company, counting, the habit of a lifetime. "Petra holds the postern with thirty of mine. We can give you perhaps an hour before the garrison notices the east quarter's gone quiet. The engineers' level is two flights down. Cassian is—" She paused, and something crossed her face that Bram, who had spent years under her command and knew her better than she'd ever have liked, recognized as old shame. "Cassian is with them. He doesn't trust the binding to anyone else."

"Good," said Sera, and everyone looked at her, because Sera Vane saying *good* in that particular voice was like a library catching fire. "I want him there. I want witnesses. Everything in that book, everything he's buried — tonight it comes up out of the ground, and I want him standing on it when it does."

Elske studied her for a moment. "You know," she said, "a year ago I'd have arrested you for that tone alone."

"And now?"

"Now I'm going to walk in front of you and arrest anyone who objects." She turned on her heel. "Keep up."

* * *

Bram had imagined the citadel's depths a hundred times and gotten them wrong every time. He'd imagined dungeons, and they were not dungeons. The stairs went down and down through stone that grew older as it went — newer masonry giving way to old, old to older, until the walls were fitted with a precision no living mason could match, and the carvings began.

Beasts. Everywhere, beasts. Lions and stags and serpents and things with too many wings, carved into the living rock, their faces worn smooth by centuries of air. And as they descended, Bram began to feel it — a pressure, a listening, the sensation of walking through a room where someone enormous was sleeping. The rats had gone silent an hour ago, Ivo had said. Not frightened. *Reverent.*

The beasts' eyes, he noticed, were all facing downward. Toward the gate. Toward her.

"Stop," Ivo whispered suddenly, hand up.

They stopped. Ahead, around a bend in the stair, torchlight flickered and voices echoed — engineers, a dozen at least, and above them a voice Bram had heard give orders on a hundred parade grounds, smooth and cold as well-water.

Cassian. Live, and close.

"—the third seal is *failing*, my lord, the iron won't take—"

"Then pour more iron. I did not spend six years and a treasury on *won't take*."

"My lord, the texts are clear, the wardens answer to—"

"The texts," Cassian said, "are written by dead men, and dead men answer to me now. Bind it. Bind it or I will find engineers who can."

Bram looked at Ivo. Ivo looked at Sera. Sera was already untying the oilcloth from the book, and her hands were not quite steady, and her voice, when she breathed it, was barely a sound at all:

"The marginalia. The second hand. *The gate opens to truth freely given.* He's trying to force a door that only opens to honesty. He's been trying for six years." A breath that was almost a laugh. "No wonder he's angry."

Ivo closed her eyes for one heartbeat — gathering herself, the way she did before she spoke to something frightened — and then she stepped around the bend into the torchlight, alone, hands open and empty, into a circle of drawn swords and a usurper's cold stare.

"Hello, Lord Cassian," she said. "I've come about the wardens."

* * *

What happened next, Bram would remember for the rest of his life in fragments, the way you remember a storm — not as a sequence but as a series of terrible bright instants.

Cassian's voice, silky with contempt: *the beast-speaker. The prophecy's little key. Seize her — gently, gentlemen, she's worth more unmarked.*

The engineers' circle breaking apart as Elske and Petra's soldiers poured down the stair behind them, and the shriek of steel, and Odji going over the barricade of tool-crates like a one-handed cat, and Bram himself roaring the old battle roar he'd sworn off a year ago, feeling it fit his throat like a coat he'd never truly put away.

Sera, in the middle of it, holding the open book over her head and *shouting* — shouting the marginalia, the second hand, the corrected truth, in a voice that cracked and did not stop: *the wardens were guardians, the beast-speakers were guardians, the war was Cassian's kind of lie—*

And Maren.

Bram saw her because he had always seen her, the way you see the sun without looking at it. She had not drawn a weapon. She had not run. She had walked to the center of the chamber, into the open ground between the fighting and the half-broken gate, and knelt, and put her silver-scarred hands flat against the stone.

And the stone had *answered*.

Light came up through the floor like water rising in a well — warm, gold, gentle as a hearth — and where it passed, the fighting faltered, because it is very hard to keep trying to kill someone when the ground beneath you is glowing like morning. A crossbow bolt meant for Ivo's back slowed, and drooped, and fell short. A soldier's sword-arm went slack with something that was not weakness but rest. The whole chamber filled with the feeling Bram had only ever known from Maren's hands on his infected wound a year ago in the tannery: *you are held. You are safe. Put it down.*

She was spending herself. He understood it the way you understand lightning, instantly and uselessly. Every yard of that light was Maren, poured out, and her face as she knelt was not afraid. It was the face of a woman setting down a load she had carried too long.

"MAREN!" His voice tore something on the way out.

She turned her head and found him across the chaos, and she smiled — that same mild, maddening, loving smile — and she mouthed something he could not hear and did not need to. *This is what it's for.*

The gate behind her was waking. The carved beasts along its face were moving — slowly, joint by ancient joint, stone grinding like a continent turning over — and their eyes were open, and their eyes were on Ivo.

* * *

Afterward there was the retreat, because Cassian's garrison had come at last in numbers no light could soften, and Elske's voice cutting through the din — *fall back, fall back to the postern, MOVE* — and Bram carrying Maren because she could not walk and would not have asked anyone to carry her, her weight in his arms no heavier than a bundle of laundry and a thousand times heavier than that.

They went up the stairs through the waking dark, through chambers where the carved beasts were stirring on every wall, their stone voices a low chorus that Ivo walked inside of with her chin up and her eyes wet and her hands still open, still empty, still *hers*. The garrison did not follow them up. The garrison, Bram noted distantly, had discovered religion, or at least the sensible fear of it, and was staying very close to the far walls.

At the postern, in the cold night air, Bram laid Maren down on a cloak, and Sera knelt beside her, and Odji stood with his one hand pressed over his mouth, and Ivo — Ivo sat and took Maren's hand and held it against her own cheek and did not say anything at all, because there was nothing in any language, spoken or otherwise, for this.

Maren's eyes were open. Her breathing was slow and shallow, like a tide going out, and her smile was still there.

"Did we get there?" she whispered.

"The gate's waking," Ivo said. Her voice was wrecked and steady at once. "It's waking, Maren. It heard you."

"Mm." Maren's eyes drifted toward the citadel, toward the deep places, and something in her face was peaceful in a way that frightened Bram more than any wound ever had. "Good. That was the last of me, I think. Don't—" Her hand tightened, faint as a moth. "Don't you dare waste it. Any of it. Any of you."

"We won't," Bram said, and his voice broke in the middle, and he found he did not care. "I promise. We won't."

"Then I'm off duty." Her eyes closed, and her breathing evened — not gone, never gone, Sera's fingers at her throat confirmed it, *she's sleeping, she's only sleeping, the gift is spent but she is HERE* — and Bram put his forehead against the cold stone of the wall and let the shaking take him for exactly as long as he could afford.

Which was not long. Because across the processional way, the citadel's highest tower lit with torches, and a horn blew three long notes — the signal Bram had known for eleven years, the signal he'd hoped never to hear aimed at him again.

*The garrison sorties. The lord commands it in person.*

Cassian was coming out. Cassian, with everything he had left, was coming for the postern, for Elske's thirty soldiers, for a sleeping healer and a scholar and a locksmith and a deserter — and beyond them, through the streets, toward the gate that was even now grinding open in the dark beneath his feet.

Ivo stood up. She looked at the horn-blowing tower, and at the gate's distant glow rising through the street-grates like dawn coming the wrong way, and Bram saw her make the choice he had known, since the granary, she would make.

"Get her out of the city," Ivo said. "All of you. Get her somewhere safe and don't let Sera write anything stupid about it." She was already moving toward the streets, toward the deep places, toward the gate that whispered her name. "I'm going back down. It's time."

And behind her, from the throat of the earth, from a hundred miles of stone, the wardens answered — a sound like mountains learning to speak — and the first of them rose.
