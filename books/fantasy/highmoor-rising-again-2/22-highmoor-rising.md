## 22. Highmoor Rising

The bell over the schoolhouse door had been cast from a melted witch-finder's badge, and Ivo never tired of telling people so.

"It rings crooked," complained Pip, who was nine and had opinions about everything. "It goes *dun* instead of *dong*."

"That's because it used to belong to a man with no ear for music," Ivo said, sorting slate pencils on her desk. "Or a heart. Take your pick."

Pip considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. "Can I ring it anyway?"

"You may ring it when the lesson is learned, not before."

He rang it immediately, of course. Nine-year-olds were constitutionally incapable of letting a bell sit unrung, and Ivo had long ago stopped pretending she minded. The crooked note rolled out over the schoolyard — a converted granary with new windows and an old soul — and down Copperlane Street, where Highmoor was busy rebuilding itself in ways that would have gotten everyone involved hanged, twelve years ago.

A woman two doors down was teaching ivy to grow in patterns. A cooper sang to his glue-pots, quietly, the way men did now that singing to things wasn't a crime but still felt like a secret worth keeping. On the corner where the night market had once scattered at the first sight of grey cloaks, someone had set up a stall selling luck-charms openly, badly, and at scandalous prices. Ivo bought one every month out of professional courtesy. They didn't work at all. She'd told the charm-seller as much, gently, and the woman had thanked her and raised her prices.

*Progress*, Sera called it.

Ivo looked out the window at her students — eleven of them, ages six to fourteen, each carrying something the old Highmoor would have burned them for. There was Pip, who could talk to locks, which was either very useful or a locksmith's nightmare depending on his mood. There was Wren, small and solemn, who heard what dead trees remembered. And there was little Annet, who hadn't yet spoken a word in class but whose shadow moved a half-second before she did, as though it knew what was coming and wanted to be polite about it.

They were loud, these children. Loud and strange and unafraid, and every morning when they came clattering up the steps Ivo felt something in her chest that took her a long time to name, because she had spent so many years not naming things.

It was pride. It was home. It was the sound of a city learning to hold its breath less often.

"All right," she said, clapping chalk dust from her hands. "Wren, tell us what the roof beam remembers."

"Termites," said Wren.

"Anything else?"

"A carpenter named Aldous who was in love with a woman who sold fish, and he carved her initials where nobody would see them, and he never told her."

The class made the soft collective *awww* that children made at love stories, and Ivo smiled and wrote ALDOUS on the slate, because history was made of people like him, and somebody ought to remember.

* * *

Sera found her at midday, which meant Sera had forgotten to eat again, which meant Ivo had brought enough bread and cheese for two and would be generous about not saying so.

Sera Vane came through the door of the schoolhouse the way she came through every door: already talking. "—and the third marginal hand isn't a copyist's error after all, it's a *correction of a correction*, which means there were at least three beast-speakers working on the text across maybe forty years, which means—" She stopped. Looked at the bread. Looked at Ivo. "You're an angel."

"I'm a former criminal feeding a scholar. It's practically traditional."

"The archive is nearly done." Sera sat, tore the bread, talked through the mouthful because some things never changed. "The true prophecy, the corrected text, Elske's testimony, the warden-gate surveys — all of it, copied fair, sealed, and shelved where any fool who wants the truth can read it. Do you know what that means?"

"That you'll finally sleep?"

"I mean *historically*." Sera waved the crust at her. "For a hundred years, one book in one locked room decided what everyone believed about your people. About *you*. Now it's just — knowledge. Ordinary. Available." Her voice did the thing it did when she was moved and trying to disguise it as enthusiasm. "Anyone can walk in off the street and read that beast-speakers guarded the wardens. That they died holding the gate. That the war was Cassian's kind of lie before Cassian ever learned to tell it."

"I know," Ivo said. "I've seen them do it. There was a boy last week, couldn't have been more than ten, standing in front of the prophecy case for an hour. I asked him what he was reading." She turned her cup slowly. "He said, 'It says my gran's grandmother was a hero.' And then he asked me if I was the Ivo Brightwater, and I said there's only one, and he said—" She laughed, helplessly. "He said, 'You're shorter than the drawing.'"

"The drawing is unflattering."

"The drawing is *accurate*, Sera."

"The drawing makes you look tall and mysterious."

"And I am neither, and the boy saw through centuries of ink in four seconds flat." Ivo shook her head, but she was smiling. "That's the part I can't get used to. Not the council, not the wardens. Being *known*. Walking down Copperlane and having people nod at me like I'm a person they're pleased to see, instead of a thing they're careful not to look at directly."

Sera reached over and squeezed her wrist. "Get used to it. You've got decades of being known ahead of you. Possibly centuries, if the wardens keep insisting you attend their councils."

"Don't remind me. Granite has no sense of time. I sat through a warden deliberation last month that lasted three days, and I'm fairly sure no actual decision was made."

"What was it about?"

"Whether the eastern wall should be repaired by stone or by stone." Ivo sighed. "They take their deliberations seriously. Four hundred years asleep and they wake up with opinions about masonry."

But she said it warmly, because that was the truth underneath: the wardens were uneasy protectors, awkward as giant dogs in a small room, terrifying to look at and oddly gentle. The city had feared them at first — of course it had; they rose out of the earth the size of hills, wearing the shapes of beasts no living creature had ever been. But the first time the river flooded, and the great stone bear simply walked into the water and held the bridge together all night while people evacuated, something in Highmoor's spine loosened. Fear became wariness. Wariness became, gradually, the gruff civic affection citizens reserved for things that were inconvenient, enormous, and theirs.

Children left flowers on the warden-gate now. Left *bread*, sometimes, which the wardens could not eat and politely pretended not to notice.

"They asked about you today, actually," Ivo said. "The gate-stones. While I was walking past."

Sera went still in the way she only went still for scholarship or worry. "What did they say?"

"*When does the speaker come back.* Every day, the same question. Like cats waiting at a door." Ivo hesitated. "I told them soon. I don't know if 'soon' means anything to stone."

"It means something to whoever taught them the word." Sera brushed crumbs from her fingers, and her voice gentled. "Your predecessors. The ones who died holding it. They built patience into the gate, Ivo. They built *waiting*. Whatever else happened to them, they expected someone to come back." She smiled, small and crooked. "Someone did."

Outside, the noon bell — the crooked one, rung this time by Wren, who had better manners than Pip — sent its lopsided note over the rooftops. Somewhere below, a cart-horse nickered, and Ivo caught the edge of its mood without meaning to: contentment, sunshine, hay. Twelve years of flinching away from that sense, and now she let it wash over her like weather.

*Home*, said the horse's mood, insofar as horses said anything.

Yes, Ivo thought. Precisely.

* * *

The cat was waiting on her windowsill when she got home that evening, which was nothing new, except that everything else about it was.

He was old now. His muzzle had gone silver, his leaps had become dignified descents, and his naps had achieved a duration previously recorded only in geological strata. He had followed her out of the sewers of occupied Highmoor, through the blood moon fair, along every road to Hollowbridge and the free cities and back again, and somewhere on those roads he had stopped being a stray and started being — well. Family. The first member of it, in fact, though he'd deny it with his whole body if cats could testify.

"There you are," Ivo said, unlatching the window. "You know, most cats wait at the *door* like civilized creatures."

*I wait where the sun is,* he said. His voice in her mind had gone creaky over the years, like a well-loved hinge. *The door is drafty.*

"The door is fine."

*The door is drafty and Odji installed that ridiculous bell on it. Ding-ding-ding every time anyone enters. As if we might otherwise forget that people exist.*

"He installed it because Maren liked it." Ivo sat on the sill beside him, and the old cat poured himself into her lap with the practiced ease of a creature who had done so a thousand times and intended to do so a thousand more. "Before she..."

*Before,* the cat agreed, softer.

Maren's garden was three streets over now, tended by the healers' guild she'd founded in her last years, and every spring it came up in flowers that bloomed slightly out of season, because the ground remembered what had grown there and loved it. Bram ran the veterans' hall next to it and cried at weddings, which he denied loudly and everyone permitted him. Odji had married a clockmaker and their house chimed on the hour in eleven different keys, on purpose. And Sera —

Sera had fallen asleep over the corrected prophecy manuscript twice this week, and Ivo had covered her with a blanket both times, and neither of them had discussed it, and both of them knew exactly what it meant.

The family she'd built without meaning to. Stitched together out of strays, the way she'd once stitched charms out of scraps. It shouldn't have held. Nothing that ragged should have held. But here it was, whole and warm and spread across half a neighborhood, and here she sat in the middle of it with an ancient cat on her knees watching the sun go down over a city that had tried to erase her and instead had learned her name.

*You're doing the thinking-too-much face,* the cat observed.

"I'm allowed. It's been a long day of being shorter than my own drawing."

*Mm.* He kneaded her knee once, claws carefully sheathed, an old courtesy between old friends. *Do you remember the sewer?*

"I remember everything about the sewer. Mostly the smell."

*Before that. In the market. You fed me fish when you had barely enough for yourself, and you never once asked anything of me, and you never spoke to me where anyone could see, even though you must have been so lonely.* His eyes, clouded now at the edges, regarded her steadily. *I decided then. Before the wardens, before the prophecy. I decided you were mine.*

Ivo's throat went tight. "You waited twelve years to say that?"

*I'm a cat. We deliver our affections on our own schedule.* He settled deeper into her lap. *Besides, you knew.*

She had. Of course she had. Some things never needed saying until suddenly they needed saying very much, and then they were best said plainly, in the window light, with bread and cheese still on the breath of the day.

"I'm glad you decided," she managed. "I'd have decided back. If you'd asked."

*Cats don't ask.* A yawn split his face, enormous and unembarrassed. *We inform.*

* * *

Later, when the lamps were lit and the dishes done, Ivo stood at the window a long while, looking out at Highmoor rising in the dusk.

Cranes against the last orange light. New roofs going on old bones. Down in the low town, the warden-gate glowed faintly, as it always did now, and she could feel the sleeping-not-sleeping attention of the stone beasts turned toward her like sunflowers toward the sun — patient, faithful, waiting for their speaker the way the cat had once waited outside a market stall for fish.

Tomorrow there would be lessons. Pip would try to pick the schoolhouse lock with his mind and she'd pretend not to notice. Wren would report what the floorboards remembered. Annet's shadow would arrive before she did, polite as ever. Sera would arrive mid-lecture with news and no lunch, and Ivo would have bread ready, because some things you learned to count on, and counting on things was the whole point of everything.

The cat purred on the sill. The crooked bell rang somewhere across the roofs, dun instead of dong, ringing in the evening shift at the cooperage, ringing in the lamplighter's round, ringing in a city that no longer required its people to hide what they were.

Ivo Brightwater leaned her forehead against the cool glass and let herself feel all of it — the loneliness that had ended, the masks she'd retired, the family assembled out of fugitives and scholars and one extremely opinionated cat.

Then, from the direction of the warden-gate, faint but unmistakable, the stone beasts spoke her name — not in question this time, not asking when their speaker would return.

In welcome.

And beneath the welcome, threaded through it like a new line of ink in an old margin, something none of them had ever felt before: the gate, waking further, humming with a purpose not even Sera's books had recorded — as if the wardens had kept one more secret, all these centuries, and had chosen tonight, with the city rebuilt and its speaker home, to finally share it.
