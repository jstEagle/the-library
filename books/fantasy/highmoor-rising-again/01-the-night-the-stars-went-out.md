## 1. The Night the Stars Went Out

The ledgers of the Guild of Impossible Things did not balance, and that was precisely what Edmund Reyes loved about them.

He sat in the counting room beneath the guildhall, where the candles never guttered and the ink never dried, reconciling the autumn quarter. Around him rose shelves upon shelves of ledgers bound in leather that had once been something else — a debt collector's coat, a widow's wedding dress, the skin of a promise broken so thoroughly it had to be made into a book just to hold it still. Each ledger recorded an impossible transaction: three years of borrowed luck repaid with interest by a fisherman who'd never learned to lose; a memory of a mother's face traded twice, once in grief and once in greed; a second chance sold at auction to a man who wasted it, then bought it back, then wasted it again. The numbers were madness. The numbers were beautiful. Edmund had spent eleven years learning to make them behave.

He was halfway through the column for *futures, harvested, unripe* when he noticed the entry rewriting itself.

Not smudged. Not corrected. *Rewriting* — the ink crawling like insects across the page, letters dissolving and reforming into new figures while he watched. A farmer's purchased rain, forty days of it, contracted for the price of his eldest daughter's laughter: the forty became thirty. Then twenty. Then nothing. In the margin, where the collateral should have been listed, a single word surfaced as though rising through deep water:

*forfeit.*

Edmund set down his pen. His hands were steady — they were always steady; it was the one thing Master Corvane had ever praised about him, back when praise had been a leash rather than a kindness — but a coldness moved up through his chest all the same.

"Ledger," he said quietly, "who authorized this revision?"

The book did not answer. Books in the counting room rarely did; they only answered the Auditor-General, or the guild's masters, or — on one occasion Edmund tried very hard not to remember — a debt that had grown articulate from being ignored too long. But the neighboring volumes had begun to rustle, pages turning on their own like birds restless before a storm, and when Edmund pulled the next ledger toward him he found the same sickness spreading through its columns. Borrowed courage, defaulted. Traded years, recalled. And there, running down the margins of a dozen entries like a vein of black ice through stone:

*the Crown calls in its debts. the Crown calls in its debts.*

He stood so fast his chair went over backward. The sound rang through the empty counting room, and no one came, because it was past the third bell and everyone else had gone home to beds that stayed where they were put, in a city where the stars —

Edmund crossed to the narrow window slit high in the wall, the one that let him track the seasons by the sky, and looked out.

The stars were going out.

Not clouded over. Not hidden. Going out — one by one, in no order he could name, constellations unraveling like picked seams. He watched the Shepherd's Lamp die mid-shimmer, and felt something in the wards that ringed the free cities *shudder*, a vibration he sensed not in his ears but in his teeth, in the ledger-ink on his fingers. Every hedge-witch's apprentice learned what the stars were for. They were anchors. They were witnesses. They were the countersignatures on every ward from here to the border, and they were being struck from the great contract of the sky.

*The border wards are falling,* he thought, with the terrible clarity of a man who has spent his life reading other people's catastrophes in columns of figures. *Someone is calling in a debt large enough to unmake the sky.*

Behind him, in his satchel, the grimoire began to bleed.

He heard it before he saw it — a soft, wet dripping against the floorboards — and when he pulled the stolen book out, ink was seeping through its cover the way blood seeps through a bandage. Master Corvane's grimoire. Eleven years gone from the hedge-witch's tower, wrapped in oilcloth, buried at the bottom of every satchel Edmund had owned since, carried like a splinter he could not dig out. He had told himself a thousand stories about why he kept it. It was leverage. It was insurance. It was the only proof that the things Corvane had done to him in that tower had been real, that he hadn't dreamed the boy who ran barefoot down the mountain with nothing but a book and a talent for numbers.

Now the book wept ink onto the counting room floor, and the ink moved. It crawled in thin black lines toward the open ledgers, toward the margins where *the Crown calls in its debts* was written, and where it touched, the entries flared briefly crimson before settling into a new and dreadful order.

Edmund knew handwriting when he saw it, even ink writing itself. This was a signature. Something was signing its name across the guild's books, and the guild's books were letting it.

*Run,* said the part of him that had run from Corvane's tower, the part that knew the exact weight of a satchel packed in terror. *Run now, run fast, don't look back—*

But the runaway's instinct, honed over eleven years, snagged on something. Because Edmund had read the whole ledger. All of it, every impossible entry, in eleven years of nights exactly like this one. And there was one entry — the oldest in the guild's keeping, kept in a case of iron and glass in the Auditor-General's own vault — that he had copied out long ago, late at night, because it was the most beautiful piece of accounting he had ever seen and the most obscene.

A king's soul. Pawned. Generations ago. Collateral held in perpetuity.

And tonight, if the sky was any indication, someone had come to collect.

* * *

They came for him at the fourth bell, and they did not knock.

The counting room door opened without opening — the hinges simply agreed, in the way of guild-crafted things, that a door could be two things at once — and three figures stepped through dressed in the gray of guild auditors, except auditors carry pens, and these carried wires fine as spider silk and sharp as regret.

"Brother Reyes." The lead figure's voice was pleasant, which was how Edmund knew he was going to die. "The Auditor-General requests the return of guild property."

"I keep the ledgers," Edmund said. His voice sounded far away to himself. "I *am* guild property, more or less. Depreciated, but on the rolls."

"The grimoire." The assassin extended a gray-gloved hand. "Master Corvane filed a claim of theft eleven years ago. The claim has been... activated."

Eleven years. Which meant Corvane had known where he was all along, had let him live and work and count, and had chosen *tonight* — the night the stars went out, the night the ink walked — to call the debt due. Which meant the grimoire mattered to whatever was coming. Which meant—

Which meant Edmund was already doing the arithmetic, because he could no sooner stop than stop breathing. Three assassins. One door that opened only for those who understood it. And between him and the door, the iron-and-glass case in the Auditor-General's vault, one floor up, holding the oldest page in the guild's possession.

"You can have the grimoire," Edmund said.

The assassin inclined his head, pleased.

"It's in my satchel," Edmund said, and threw the satchel at him.

It was not a good throw. It was, however, thrown by a man who had spent eleven years calculating trajectories of falling candlesticks and collapsing shelves and one memorable flood of liquid luck, and it caught the lead assassin across the face with the full weight of a bleeding grimoire and eleven years of guilt. The other two moved — they were fast, terribly fast — but Edmund was already through the door, already taking the stairs three at a time, already hearing behind him the sound of the counting room door deciding, under guild law, that it was now a wall.

The vault was not locked. That was the guild's arrogance: nothing in the vault needed locking, because no member would dare. Edmund dared. He broke the glass with his elbow, ignoring the pain, and took the page.

It was smaller than he remembered, vellum gone soft as cloth with age, and the ink on it was old gold, not black. *Aldric,* said the name at the top, in a hand that had signed away a kingdom's future. Below it, terms. Below the terms, the collateral. Below the collateral, in fresh wet ink that had not been there an hour ago:

*collection imminent.*

Below that, in a different hand entirely — small, precise, familiar in a way that made his chest hurt before he understood why — a note he had never noticed in eleven years of copying this page:

*If the debtor cannot pay, the holder of the page may stand surety.*

Serah's handwriting. Serah, who had taught him wards before he ever taught himself sums. Serah, whose shrine at the border he had not written to in nine years, because shame is a debt too, and he had always been better at owing than paying.

Boots on the stairs. Wires whispering against stone.

Edmund folded the page into his shirt, over his heart, where the grimoire used to sit, and went to the window instead of the door. It was a long drop to the canal. He had calculated it a hundred times, bored, on slow nights, the way other men counted sheep.

He jumped toward something, this time. For the first time in his life, Edmund Reyes ran toward something instead of away — toward a crumbling shrine on the fallen border, toward a woman who had every right to let the wards stay down around her heart, toward a debt with his name suddenly written all over it in someone else's hand.

Behind him, the last star over the free cities went out.

The dark was total, and in it, somewhere south, armies that did not need light began to march.
