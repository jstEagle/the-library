## 2. The Debt of Crowns

The road to the border was a wound in the world.

Edmund had walked it for nine days, and each day the land grew quieter, as though sound itself were being called in like a debt. Birds still sang in the lowlands, but by the fourth day their songs came thinner, half-remembered. By the seventh, his own footsteps made no echo off the hillsides. He tested it once outside a burned-out waystation — clapped his hands, shouted his own name — and heard nothing come back. The world had stopped keeping accounts of him too.

He kept the ledger page folded against his heart, wrapped in oilcloth, and he did not take it out except at night, when he could shield a candle's flame with his body and read it by slivers of light. The script was old guild-hand, the kind they used for contracts that outlived kingdoms:

*Received of Aldric, son of Aldric, King of Highmoor: one soul, whole and unencumbered, held in trust against payment due.*

Payment due. The words sat there like a knife left on a table. No sum named. No date. Just *due*, the way a debt becomes due when the creditor decides it is, and not one breath before.

His master's grimoire rode in his pack, heavier than any book had a right to be. It had stopped bleeding ink on the third day, but the stains had spread, blooming across the pages like bruises, and sometimes when he slept he dreamed the ink was writing something and woke with the shape of a word on his tongue that dissolved before he could speak it.

On the ninth day, he saw the shrine.

It stood where the map said it would, at the throat of the pass — a ring of standing stones around a well, the stones carved so long ago that weather had worn the wards upon them down to whispers. Once, Edmund knew from Serah's letters, there had been forty ward-keepers here, singing the border into being night after night. Now smoke rose from a single lean-to beside the well, and a single figure moved among the stones, touching each one in turn, and Edmund's chest tightened with a grief he had no right to feel.

Because he knew her walk. Ten years, and he knew her walk.

* * *

Serah felt him coming before she saw him.

That was the thing about keeping the last wards on the border of the world: you learned to feel what the dark felt. For nine days she had tracked a hole moving toward her through the silence — a place where the quiet didn't quite reach, a warmth where the cold should have pooled. A person-shaped absence in the unraveling. She had told herself it was a refugee, a lost child, anything but the shape her memory insisted on.

Then he crested the ridge, thin and travel-worn, clutching a satchel like a man carrying a wounded bird, and her memory won.

"Edmund Reyes." She said it flatly, the way you'd read a name off an invoice. "Ten years. You couldn't have written."

"I wrote." His voice cracked on the second word. "Three times. The guild intercepted—"

"The guild." She laughed, and there was nothing warm in it. "Of course. The Guild of Impossible Things. Where they keep ledgers of memories and luck and second chances, and where apparently my letters were filed under *impossible to deliver*." She turned back to the stone she had been tending, pressing her palm flat against the worn carving. "I used to think if I stopped hating you, the wards would weaken. That anger was load-bearing. Isn't that absurd? I built you into the border, Edmund. Every night for ten years, part of the song I sang was a curse with your name in it."

"That explains," he said quietly, "why the wards held so long."

She whirled on him, and for a moment she genuinely considered the knife at her belt. But his face — gods, his face hadn't changed. Still that look of a man doing arithmetic on his own guilt, arriving always at a sum too large to pay.

"Why are you here?" she demanded.

He took out the ledger page.

* * *

Edmund watched her read it, and watched ten years of fury curdle into something colder and more useful: fear.

"A king's soul," she said. "Pawned. By which king?"

"The entry doesn't say. The guild's oldest books use regnal cipher — the name is recorded as a number. But the debt has been *called*. Three nights ago, the same night the stars went out. Whatever wears Aldric's crown walked into a guild chapterhouse in the capital and presented itself as the creditor's agent. The auditors who came for my grimoire—" his voice dropped, shame making it rough— "they weren't sent to punish me for stealing. They were sent because the grimoire's pages are collateral. My master pawned them years ago, and the Crown is collecting everything the guild holds. Everything."

"And you ran." She looked at him almost tenderly then, which was worse than the anger. "You've been running since you were nineteen years old. From your master, from me, from every debt you ever signed your name to. And now you've run to the one person standing between the dark and the free cities, carrying the one page that can either save us or finish us. Tell me you understand what you're holding, Edmund. Tell me you know why the wards fell."

"The debt was called," he repeated. "And the wards—"

"The wards are *collateral*." She struck the nearest standing stone with the heel of her hand, and the hollow boom of it was the first sound the valley had echoed in days. "Every ward on this border is anchored in the guild's great ledger. We don't hold the dark back with songs, we hold it back with *bookkeeping* — the border is a lien, Edmund, a mortgage the guild took out on the world's edge generations ago. When the Hollow Crown called in the king's soul, the ledger rebalanced. Debts cascade. Collateral gets seized. The stars went out because someone closed those accounts, and the wards fell because the ledger no longer recognizes them as paid."

Edmund sat down slowly on a fallen stone, the page trembling in his hands. He was, despite everything, still an accountant at heart, and what she described was the worst arithmetic imaginable: a debt so large that reality itself had been entered as surety.

"There's a clause," he said at last. "In the transfer law. Any debt held by the guild may be transferred — with consent or conquest — to the debtor's kin. Or..." He swallowed. "Or to the holder of the page. Whoever physically possesses the record of the debt assumes right of first refusal."

Serah went very still.

"You're saying whoever holds that page can take the debt onto themselves."

"I'm saying the Crown knows that. It's why the assassins came for me and not just the book. They let me run, Serah. They *herded* me. A debtor who flees to the border carrying the page — that's not a fugitive. That's a delivery."

The wind moved through the stones, and for a moment all forty ghosts of the ward-keepers seemed to sigh at once. Serah looked at Edmund — at the boy who had abandoned her, the man who had stolen a grimoire and a future and carried both to her door like an apology ten years late — and felt the terrible, stubborn ember beneath her anger refuse, absolutely refuse, to go out.

"Then we don't deliver it," she said. "We spend it. There's a rite older than the guild — my order kept its texts hidden even from the ledgers. If the debt can be transferred, it can be *transferred twice*. Called in, and called back. But to do it, we need the debtor alive. We need the true king, Edmund. We need to reach Aldric before whatever wears his crown finishes collecting."

"The capital's besieged."

"I know what the capital is." She was already pulling her warding cloak from its peg, already banked the fire, already becoming motion. "You spent ten years learning to run, Edmund Reyes. Spend three more days running *toward* something. As guild auditors, we can walk through any gate in Highmoor — the Crown trusts the guild's seals. It trusts them because the guild built its bargain." Her eyes found his, and the resentment in them had caught fire at last, burning in the same direction as his own terror. "We go in as its own paper, and we burn the ledger from inside."

Edmund rose. Against his heart, the page seemed suddenly warm, like a coal deciding whose hand it belonged to.

"One condition," he said. "When this is done — when the debt is paid — you let me explain about the letters. All of it. Even the parts that make me look worse than you already think I am."

Serah shouldered her pack and turned up the pass without waiting for him, but over her shoulder she said, "Survive the capital first. Then we'll negotiate terms."

And Edmund, who had spent his whole life reading the fine print of other people's bargains, followed her into the dark, thinking: *that*, at least, sounded like a contract he might actually survive signing.
