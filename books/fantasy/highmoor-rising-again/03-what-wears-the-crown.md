## 3. What Wears the Crown

The capital had learned to be quiet.

Edmund felt it from the ridge road, before the walls even resolved out of the morning haze — a silence with weight to it, the hush of a city holding its breath around a wound. Highmoor's towers still stood, gilded and proud against a sky that had no stars left in it even at noon, but the banners above them hung dead-still in a wind that should have been blowing. The besieging army made no sound either. Tens of thousands of soldiers camped in the floodplain below the walls, and not one voice carried. They moved like figures in a ledger someone had stopped updating: present, entered, inert.

"They're not men anymore," Serah said beside him. She had her hood up and her warding-chalk already out, drawing small sigils on the inside of her wrist where a pulse would show. "Not most of them. Look at the fires. Count them."

He counted. It was what he did. "Four hundred and six."

"There were nine hundred last week, by the shrine's last bird. The Hollow Crown is *spending* them. Feeding the siege lines into itself to keep the bargain warm." Her jaw tightened. "A debt this size doesn't sit idle. It wants payment, and it eats whatever stands closest."

They went down through the orchards, past a shrine to some minor harvest god whose statue had wept black resin until its face was featureless, and reached the river gate an hour before dusk. The guild seals on their travel papers — Edmund had forged them himself, which felt less like theft now than like returning borrowed goods — got them past the outer pickets without a glance. The guards' eyes were wrong. Not empty, exactly. *Filed.* The look of entries that had been copied somewhere else and the originals discarded.

Inside the walls, Highmoor smelled of cold hearths and cut lilies. Someone had tried to keep the old rites going; garlands rotted on every doorpost, offerings to gods who could no longer see this place to collect them.

"The palace wardens are guild-trained," Serah murmured as they crossed the outer court, two auditors with ledgers under their arms, indistinguishable from a hundred functionaries who had come to witness the kingdom's accounting. "They'll know real seals when they see them. So don't flinch. Flinching is how auditors die."

"I've been flinching my whole life," Edmund said. "It's why I'm still alive."

She almost smiled. It was the first almost-smile in three days, and he hoarded it like coin.

* * *

The throne room doors were open.

That was the first wrong thing. The second was the light — candlelight everywhere, hundreds of candles, and none of them flickering. Each flame stood straight as a pin. In the middle of that rigid glow sat King Aldric on the throne of his ancestors, and Edmund's heart broke a little before his mind caught up, because Aldric looked *fine*. Healthy, ruddy, strong, dressed in coronation white, smiling at nothing with the serene contentment of a man at his own wedding.

Then Edmund looked closer, the way you read a ledger line, and saw the arithmetic underneath.

The king wasn't blinking. His chest rose and fell in perfect metronome intervals. And behind his eyes — Edmund had spent years learning to spot the moment a person's signature stopped being theirs — there was nothing looking back. A house with all the furniture removed. Something else lived there now, wearing the king the way the crown wore gold.

"You brought it," said the thing in Aldric's throat, and the voice was layered, a chorus speaking through one mouth. "The page. Good. We have waited four generations for an honest clerk."

Serah's chalk hand moved. Edmund stepped forward, ledger open, quill already inked, and began the transfer ritual the way the grimoire had taught him: name of debtor, name of creditor, sum owed, and the binding clause —

"Stop."

The candles all leaned toward him at once, flames bending like grass in wind that didn't exist.

"You should read your own guild's founding charter, clerk. Article One. Who do you think brokered the original loan?" The thing smiled with Aldric's mouth, and it was a terrible smile because it was warm, human, kind. "Your Guild of Impossible Things. Memories, luck, second chances — who do you imagine taught us the interest rates? We did not steal this king. We *foreclosed*, lawfully, per contract. And the transfer clause you're so bravely reciting? It transfers the debt to whoever holds the page."

The quill went heavy in Edmund's fingers.

Complete the ritual, and King Aldric walked free — and Edmund Reyes became the debtor. His name, his soul, his everything, entered into a ledger older than the guild, held by something that had just demonstrated it could wait four generations without getting bored.

Refuse, and Aldric stayed a puppet until the Hollow Crown ate the free cities too, and Serah's shrine, and Serah.

"There it is," the chorus said softly, watching his face. "The fine print. You of all people, clerk. Didn't you ever wonder why the guild let you keep the grimoire? Why they sent assassins after the book but never after *you*?"

Because he was collateral. He had always been collateral. Every step of his flight, every clever escape, had happened inside a margin someone else had drawn.

"Edmund." Serah's voice, low and steady at his shoulder. Her chalk sigils were glowing now, a thin blue lattice rising around them both. "Whatever you're calculating — stop. I can hold the wards. Buy you time. Do the ritual right or don't do it at all, but don't do it *fast*."

"That's very noble," said the Hollow Crown, "and very expensive."

It stood. Aldric's body unfolded from the throne with a grace no living king possessed, and the candlelight bent toward it like iron filings toward a lodestone. When it raised one hand, the air itself tore along seams Edmund hadn't known were there, and things began coming through — not soldiers, not shapes, but *entries*, debts given flesh, creditors' agents with faces like unpaid invoices.

Serah's lattice blazed. "EDMUND. NOW OR NEVER."

And Edmund — who had run from his master, run from the guild, run from her, run his whole life from every bill that came due — finally understood that there was no version of this where he survived clean. Justice wanted Aldric freed. Survival wanted the quill dropped. He could not have both.

So he chose both, badly.

He wrote. Fast, sloppy, the transfer clause half-finished, the names correct but the safeguards skipped, the wards of the ritual itself left gaping because Serah's shield was eating everything she had to keep the agents off his back. Ink hit parchment. The page against his heart burned like a brand. Somewhere beyond the lattice, Serah cried out — not in pain, in fury — and then her voice cut off mid-word, and the lattice collapsed, and hands that were not hands closed around her and took her into the dark between the candles.

The last thing the Hollow Crown said, as Aldric's body crumpled and the true king surfaced gasping inside it, was a whisper meant only for Edmund:

"*Received with thanks. Your account is open, clerk. We will call on you when the stars come back.*"

* * *

Edmund woke on the flagstones with a king weeping over him and no memory of falling.

Aldric — the real one, hollow-eyed, four generations late to his own life — gripped Edmund's collar with shaking hands. "What did it take? What did you give it?"

"Nothing yet," Edmund lied, because the alternative was true and unspeakable. His name felt strange in his mouth. Lighter. Like a page someone had already started erasing.

He got up. The throne room was wrecked, candles drowned in their own wax, and Serah was gone. No body. No blood. Just her chalk, scattered in pieces, and one long blue scorch-mark on the floor shaped like a door.

Outside, across the whole besieged city, the silent army had begun — all at once, ten thousand throats together — to scream.

And in Edmund's breast pocket, where the page had been, there was now a single line of fresh ink in handwriting he did not recognize:

**Balance outstanding. Payment deferred — pending collection. Bring the woman. She co-signed.**
