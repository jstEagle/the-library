## 4. A Page Hidden Away

Serah had always known the ritual would take something. The ward-keepers' oldest texts spoke of debts paid in full — of names struck from stone, from memory, from the world's long accounting. But knowing a thing and watching it happen were different griefs, and she stood in the vault beneath the Guild of Impossible Things with ink on her hands and Edmund's voice fading like a bell rung underwater.

He had insisted she stand outside the circle.

"You'll be tempted," he'd said, that last morning, when the capital still burned at its edges and the Hollow Crown's armies massed beyond the river like a tide deciding whether to come in. "You'll want to stop me halfway. Everyone does. That's how half-debts happen, Serah. Half-debts are worse than none."

"You could let Aldric pay it," she said. "It was his bargain. His grandfather's bargain."

"Aldric can't hold a quill. He barely holds his own eyes open in there." Edmund had smiled then — the crooked, arithmetic smile she remembered from a border shrine twelve years gone, when he'd tallied candle-stubs for her and called it courtship. "Besides. I did the math weeks ago. There's only one entry in all the guild's ledgers large enough to cover a king's soul. One name with enough credit behind it. Mine."

"That's not how credit works," she'd snapped, furious because he was right, because he had always been right about numbers and wrong about everything that mattered. "You don't have credit. You have *debt*. You stole a grimoire. You ran. You ran from me—"

"And now I'm running toward something," he'd said quietly. "Finally. You said it yourself, the night the stars went out. Terror sharpened into resolve." He'd touched her wrist, once, brief as a signature. "Read me back if you can. If you can't—" He hadn't finished it. He'd never been able to finish sentences about endings.

Now the circle blazed around him, silver script rising off the master ledger in slow spirals, and Edmund Reyes wrote himself out of existence line by line. She watched through the ward-glass as his name thinned. As the guild's records of him went blank, page after page, apprentices in the scriptorium below crying out as their memories sloughed away like snow off warm slate. Somewhere above them, the Hollow Crown screamed — a sound like a ledger being torn in half — and the thing wearing King Aldric's face collapsed into the true king's body like water finding its level.

The armies beyond the river faltered at dusk. By midnight they were a mob. By dawn, nothing but abandoned siege-works and the free cities' bells ringing out of order, joyfully, all across the lowlands.

And Edmund Reyes was no one. A man-shaped absence in the vault, holding a quill he no longer knew why he held, looking up at Serah with the polite blankness of a stranger.

"Do I know you?" he asked.

"No," Serah said, and her heart broke along an old seam, cleanly, almost professionally. "Not yet."

* * *

The free cities held. The histories would say the wards were rebuilt by unknown hands; that King Aldric woke weeping and grateful and unable to say for whom; that the Guild of Impossible Things closed its doors for a season of mourning no one could explain. Serah stayed through all of it, because staying was what ward-keepers did, and because leaving meant admitting there was nowhere left to go back to.

She kept the page hidden against her ribs, wrapped in oilcloth, where it had been since the night before the ritual. One page, copied in her own hand while Edmund slept in the shrine at Highmoor, back when the debt was still a theory and hope still had numbers attached. It was not much. A name — *Edmund Reyes, hedge-witch's apprentice, keeper of impossible accounts*. A debt of abandonment, entered against himself: *owes Serah Vane one apology, unpaid, interest accruing*. And at the bottom, in his careless looping figures, a small tally of everything he'd ever loved: candlelight on snow, the smell of ink, the sound of her laughing at his terrible jokes about compound interest.

A page is not a person. But a page is a seed, and Serah had spent her whole life learning how wards grew from small inscribed things.

She began in the spring, when the guild reopened and the new auditors — young, unhaunted, puzzled by gaps in their own archives — let her into the scriptorium because she claimed to be cataloguing damaged entries. In a sense she was. Every ledger in the guild bore the same wound: a shape torn out of thousands of pages, a silhouette of absence where a clerk's name should be. She traced those absences the way she'd once traced failing wards, looking for edges, for residue, for anything the erasure had missed.

Erasure is never perfect. That was the first law of her craft. Ink soaks deeper than fire reaches. Memory lives in more places than minds.

She found him in autumn, or rather she found the place where he had landed: a counting-house in the river quarter of Vell's Crossing, where a quiet man with careful hands did the books for a wool merchant and asked no questions about why he knew double-entry arithmetic no one had taught him. The guild's residual ledgers listed him as *unnamed clerk, hired [entry illegible]*, wages fair, habits steady, keeps to himself. He had taken a room above a chandler's shop. He liked candlelight on snow, though he didn't know why. Sometimes, the landlady reported, he woke at night convinced he'd forgotten something important, and sat by the window until dawn trying to remember what.

Serah rented the room across the alley.

She told herself she would go slowly. That reading a person back was delicate work, like rekindling a banked fire — too much breath and you smothered it, too little and it died. She started small. She left her window shuttered at the same hour he lit his lamp, so their lights faced each other across the dark like two entries in the same column. She took her meals at the pie shop he frequented and ordered what he ordered. Once, passing him on the bridge, she dropped her satchel of ledger-pages on purpose, and he helped her gather them, and his fingers paused — just a heartbeat — on the page where his own name was written in her hand.

"Sorry," he said. "I don't know why I stopped."

"It's an interesting name," Serah said. "Edmund Reyes. Sounds like someone who owes somebody an apology."

Something flickered behind his eyes — a sum nearly carried, a column refusing to balance. "Does it?" he said, and gave her the polite blankness she had learned to hate, and walked on.

But that night his lamp stayed lit past dawn.

* * *

Winter came down off the hills and the river froze at its edges, and Serah read to him across the alley by lamplight, aloud, knowing sound carries over ice better than most magic. She read the guild's old trade-routes. She read fragments of Highmoor's history. She read, on the longest night of the year, the page itself — standing at her window, holding the oilcloth-wrapped paper to the light, speaking every word clearly enough to cross thirty feet of frozen air and a man's ruined memory.

*Owes Serah Vane one apology, unpaid, interest accruing.*

His lamp guttered. Then steadied.

In the morning there was a knock at her door, and she opened it to find him standing in the snow with his hat in his hands, looking at her the way a man looks at a locked door he has suddenly realized he owns the key to.

"I've been having dreams," he said. "About a shrine. About candles, and someone laughing at my jokes. About a mistake I made that I can't — I can't see the shape of it, but I can feel the weight." He swallowed. "The landlady says my name isn't on any lease. She says I told her it was Edmund, but I don't remember telling her. Do you know who I am?"

Serah looked at him for a long moment. Twelve years of anger, one page of stubbornness, a winter of patient light.

"You're Edmund Reyes," she said. "Hedge-witch's apprentice. Keeper of impossible accounts. And you owe me an apology — though I warn you, the interest has compounded terribly."

He laughed — startled, rusty, real — and the sound was the last entry restoring itself, the final figure sliding into a column that had stood unbalanced for a year.

"Then I suppose," he said, stepping inside out of the cold, "you'd better tell me the principal."

She closed the door behind him. Outside, the snow went on falling over Vell's Crossing, over the free cities, over the rebuilt wards of Highmoor shining faint and steadfast under a sky where, night by night, the stars were coming back — one recovered light at a time, like names being read home.
