## 1. The Taster's Tongue

There are two ways to die in Highmoor, and I've made a modest living off both of them.

The first way is poison, which is the honest one. The second is magic, which is everything else. I taste for a living and sell charms for a dying — though not literally, thank the absent gods. If my charms killed people at the rate my customers' problems deserved, I'd have been hanged from the Salt Gate years ago, swinging alongside the fishmongers and the coin-clippers.

The morning began as mornings do in the queen's palace: with eggs.

Queen Isolde Vane takes her breakfast in the small solar, alone, because a queen who eats alone cannot be poisoned by conversation. She sits behind a screen of carved ashwood so that her tasters may watch her hands but never her face. I have tasted for Her Majesty six years now, and I could count on one hand the number of times I've seen her whole face at once. It's an arrangement that suits us both. There are things a face does that a voice can hide from, and vice versa.

"Blackwood." Maren, my fellow taster, slid onto the bench beside me in the antechamber, balancing two porcelain spoons like a juggler deciding which audience to disappoint. "You look like something the tide refused."

"I slept."

"Liar."

"Fine. I didn't sleep. There's a difference between lying and editing."

Maren snorted. She has the kind of laugh that makes guards look over and then pretend they didn't, and she knows it, and she deploys it like artillery. Six years we've shared this work — the spoons, the waiting, the particular silence of two people whose job is to be ready to die slowly in front of witnesses. You don't survive that without becoming the sort of friends who insult each other before dawn.

The tray came up from the kitchens under seal: quail eggs in celery salt, honeyed rolls, a compote of plums gone dark as bruises, and tea brewed from leaves so expensive each one had its own biography. I took the eggs first. Celery salt is a classic vehicle — bitter enough to mask most of the gentle poisons, cheap enough to be swapped by a lazy kitchen hand. I held the spoonful on my tongue and counted.

One. Two. Three.

Nothing but salt and yolk and the faint green ghost of celery. I swallowed. Maren did the rolls; I took the compote; we split the tea between us like a pair of superstitious twins, because you never know which half of the pot someone's paid to ruin.

Twenty minutes later, alive and unpoisoned, we watched through the ashwood screen as the queen ate what we'd already died for. Her hands were steady. Her rings caught the light. Somewhere beneath all that silk was a woman who trusted me with her life every single morning, and who would have me flogged if she knew what I kept in the false bottom of my flour chest.

That's the arrangement. That's always been the arrangement.

* * *

Here's what the edicts say, in case you're new to Highmoor: magic is outlawed. Has been for a hundred years, since the Purging, when the last god-speakers were driven out or underground and the old gods were sealed away behind dragon-fire, and the city decided — sensibly, everyone agrees — that prayers were safer left unanswered. The penalty for charm-work is the wall. Not hanging; the wall. They brick you into the old aqueduct arches and let the pigeons make their judgments.

So naturally, I sell charms.

Not many. Not loudly. And never — this is the line I draw, the line I redraw every night like a man tracing a scar — never anything that *works*. What I sell are comforts. Dried lavender sewn into linen for sleepless wives. A knotted red thread "blessed" for safe travel, which is to say, blessed by me, in my kitchen, while burning toast. A pinch of ash from my hearth in a twist of paper for a cough, which works only insofar as any grandmother's remedy works, which is to say, sometimes, and mostly through stubbornness.

Placebo peddler. Fraud, if you're feeling legal about it. Kindly liar, if you're feeling like me.

Because here's the thing nobody says out loud in a city where magic is death: people still *pray*. They pray the way they breathe — automatically, desperately, without thinking. And when no one answers, when the gods stay sealed and silent century after century, they come looking for someone to sell them the shape of an answer. I am that shape. I am the hollow reed they blow their hope through, and it costs them a copper or two, and it costs me nothing but the low hum of shame I've learned to sleep beside.

I tell myself it's harmless. I tell myself a lot of things.

* * *

The laundress found me after vespers, down in the scullery court where the steam rises and nobody looks at anybody.

Her name was Odile, though she gave it reluctantly, the way people do when they've learned that names are handles other people can grab. She was maybe forty, with forearms like ship rope and the reddened knuckles that lye gives you as a retirement gift. She'd heard — the way they always hear, God knows how, some whisper-network of desperate women that operates faster than any royal courier — that Blackwood in the tasting service could get things.

"Things," I said. "I'm a food-taster, mistress. I get things like dysentery."

"Don't play with me." Her voice cracked on the second word, and she hated that it cracked, and I pretended not to notice because that's also part of the service. "My boy. Tam. He's fourteen. He's got the lung-rot, the wet kind, the kind that—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "The physickers want eight crowns for the treatment. Eight. I've washed linens for this palace eleven years and I have three."

I looked at her hands. I looked at the steam. I did the arithmetic I always do, the one where I weigh a copper against a conscience and pretend there's a difference.

"Mistress," I said, gently, because the kindest thing I own is gentleness, "what I sell isn't medicine. It's—"

"It's hope in a bag. I know what it is." Her chin came up. "My mother bought charms. Before the Purging, when they were real ones. I know the difference between a prayer and a promise, taster. I'm asking for the prayer."

And there it was. The exact shape of the thing I sell, named by a washerwoman in three words, priced at nothing, worth everything.

I sold her the sleep-charm. Lavender and chamomile and a knotted thread, wrapped in blue cloth because blue is for lungs, everyone knows that, it's in none of the books but everyone knows it. Two coppers. I told her to brew it sweet, to sit with the boy while he drank it, to tell him stories, because the charm worked best with stories — which is true, incidentally, of nearly everything.

She pressed the coppers into my palm like they were hot from her own skin, and she said, "Bless you," and walked back into the steam.

I stood there holding two coppers and the word *blessed*, and I thought: there's no one left to bless anyone. That's the whole joke of this city. We're a congregation without a god, passing the collection plate in circles, and I'm just the man who skims the copper off the top of hope.

I went home. I burned the toast. I slept badly.

* * *

My rooms are above a chandler's shop in Tallow Row, which means I live in a permanent fog of beeswax and tallow smoke that has done more for my complexion than any charm I've ever sold. One room. A bed, a table, a hearth, and the flour chest with the false bottom, which holds my entire inventory and my entire inheritance: my mother's mortar and pestle, a bundle of dried herbs arranged by a taxonomy only she ever knew, and a ledger.

The ledger is my father's. Before the wall took him — before the bricks and the pigeons and the arch that still bears, if you know where to look, a scratched mark shaped like a wren — he kept accounts. Not of money. Of names. Names of everyone in our quarter who'd ever wanted a charm, and what they'd wanted it *for*, because he believed — foolishly, fatally, beautifully — that a charm-seller should know his customers' griefs better than his stock.

Wren is in that ledger. My sister Wren, seven years gone, sold — though I didn't know the word *sold* then, I thought *taken*, I thought *dead*, I thought every word except the true one — to the court beneath the mountain. But that's a story for later chapters, and I'm getting ahead of myself, which is a habit of mine when the alternative is sitting still with a feeling.

That night I sat with the ledger open on my knees and read the entry I always read:

*Tam, son of Odile. Lung-rot, wet. Mother prays.*

I hadn't written it yet. I dipped the pen and wrote it now, and my hand was steady, and I hated how steady it was. Six years of tasting poison has given me the steadiest hands in Highmoor, and they never shake for the right reasons.

Below it, older entries. Pages of them. Griefs in my father's cramped hand, griefs in mine. A city's worth of wanting, catalogued and cross-referenced, and not one prayer answered in a hundred years.

I closed the book. I banked the fire. I lay down and listened to the chandler's shop exhale below me, and I told myself the usual bedtime story: *you belong here, Ansel. The palace trusts you. The quarter needs you. You have a place.*

Both lies, and I knew both lies, and I told them anyway, because a man can live a long time on lies the way a man can live on broth — thin, but warm.

Here is the truth, which I will set down now so that later, when everything burns, I can point at this page and say I knew: I belonged nowhere fully. In the palace I was the servant who smelled faintly of the streets. In the streets I was the palace man who smelled faintly of secrets. I moved between the two worlds like water between stones, touching everything, held by nothing, and I called it freedom because the alternative was calling it loneliness, and I wasn't ready for that. Not yet. Give me a few more chapters.

Sleep took me eventually, the way it takes soldiers and liars — suddenly, gratefully, without inspection.

* * *

I woke to knocking. Not the polite knock of the palace summons, three measured raps. This was urgent, arrhythmic, the knocking of someone whose hands didn't care what they were doing.

Maren stood in the grey light of Tallow Row with her cloak over her nightdress and her face the color of porridge.

"Ansel." She was breathing hard, and Maren doesn't run, Maren considers running vulgar. "It's the cooper's apprentice. Joss. The one who owed the dyer money."

"What about him?"

"He's dead." She gripped my doorframe like the street might tilt. "They found him at dawn. His debt-collector was found with him. Dead too. And Ansel—" She swallowed. "The collector didn't bleed. There was no wound. There were *roots*. Growing out of his chest. Through his lungs. Like something planted him."

The cold went through me the way cold goes through a man who has just remembered every charm he's ever sold, every knot, every pinch of ash, every whispered *this will protect you* — and wondered, for the first time in six careful years, whether any of them ever actually worked.

"Roots," I said.

"Growing *out* of him, Ansel. In Highmoor. Where nothing grows but debts."

Somewhere across the sleeping city, in a house on a lane I could walk blindfolded, a woman was brewing lavender for her sick boy and telling him stories, exactly as instructed.

I reached for my boots.
