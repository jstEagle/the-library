## 2. A Prayer Answered Wrong

There are two kinds of silence in the palace of Highmoor: the kind that means you're safe, and the kind that means someone is deciding whether you are.

I'd learned to tell them apart the way a fisherman learns weather — not by thinking about it, but by swallowing enough bad days that my body knew before I did. Tuesday's silence was the safe kind. The queen was at her devotions, the under-steward was drunk on plum wine before noon as usual, and the only thing between me and a quiet afternoon was a plate of honeyed figs that might or might not contain arsenic.

I ate one. Waited. Counted to sixty while the kitchen boys pretended not to watch me, because watching the taster was the closest thing to theater they got on a weekday.

Nothing happened. The figs were fine. The arsenic — if there ever had been any, which I doubted; poisoning the queen would require ambition this kitchen didn't possess — stayed where it belonged, which is to say nowhere near me or anyone who mattered.

"Clean," I announced, and the figs went out, and I sat back down and thought about root vegetables.

This is the trouble with my life: even on good days, half my mind is always somewhere else. The taster's half sits in the palace, chewing carefully, counting to sixty. The other half runs a shop with no door, in a city where the shop itself is a hanging offense.

Magic has been outlawed in Highmoor for a hundred years. Not discouraged — outlawed, with the particular venom of a government that remembers what it cost them. The old gods were caged beneath the mountain, the dragon-fire was lit to keep them there, and every hedge-witch, charm-seller, and prayer-whisperer was given a choice between the pyre and the honest poor. Most chose honestly. A few of us chose poorly and profitably.

I sell charms. Small ones. Nothing that would embarrass a competent fraud, which is what I told myself they were for seven years. A knotted red thread to ease a fever. A pinch of grave-dirt in a sachet to settle a restless child. Words said over bread so it wouldn't go stale. I charged what people could pay and lied about what the charms could do, gently, the way you lie to a dying man about spring coming.

"You have to believe a little," I'd tell them, "or it won't take."

And they believed, and their fevers broke, and their children slept, and their bread kept — because fevers break and children tire and bread is just bread, and none of it was ever me. That was the arrangement. That was the covenant I'd made with the universe: I would traffic in harmless nonsense, and the universe would look the other way, and nobody would burn.

The universe, it turns out, did not sign anything.

* * *

His name was Odo Fenner, and he owed money to a man named Garrick Vole, which is a sentence that ends badly in every version of Highmoor ever built. Odo came to me on a wet Thursday with his hat in his hands and three coppers on my counter, which was all he had, and asked for something to make Vole leave him alone.

Now. A careful man says no. A careful man says, *I sell luck-knots and stale-bread blessings, friend, try the magistrate.* A careful man goes home and sleeps soundly.

What I actually said was: "What do you want him to feel?"

"Small," Odo said. "I want him to feel small. Like something bigger than him is looking at him."

So I gave him what I give everyone — a thread, a word, a lie. But that night, standing over my little brazier with the stock of charms laid out for restocking, I found myself doing something I hadn't done since I was a boy kneeling at my mother's knee: I prayed. Properly. With intent. I held Odo's thread in the smoke and I said the old words, the ones Sefa-the-herbwoman used to mutter when she thought no one listened, the ones that predate the outlawry and the cages and the fire, and I said them like I meant them:

*Old ones under the stone, look upon this small cruel man, and let him know he is seen.*

Then I blew out the candle and felt like an idiot and went to bed.

You have to understand — I want that on the record, whatever record survives me — I wanted Vole frightened. Frightened men collect debts slower. I wanted him lying awake feeling watched, jumping at shadows, maybe developing a superstition or two. That was the whole of my ambition. One man, mildly haunted, temporarily inconvenienced.

Three days later, Garrick Vole died in the middle of Tanner's Row at midday, in front of forty witnesses, screaming.

* * *

I heard about it the way I hear most things — through Maren, who hears everything, delivered over the chopping block with her sleeves rolled and her knife going like a metronome.

"They say roots grew out of him." She said it lightly, but her eyes weren't light. "Out of his *chest*, Ansel. Out of his mouth. Like the ground reached up and took a breath of him. They had to burn the body on the spot — couldn't bury it, it kept growing."

"That's horrible," I said, and sliced a pear for the queen's luncheon tray with hands that were, thank all the dead gods, steady.

"Mm." The knife kept its time. "They're saying it's sorcery. Real sorcery. First in a hundred years." The knife stopped. "Ansel."

"Maren."

"What did you do?"

And here is the thing about Maren — she asks questions like that the way other people ask *how are you*. It sounds casual. It isn't. She's been my closest friend for six years, my fellow taster, the woman who taught me which guards can be bribed with pie and which ones need coin, and she has never once asked me a question she didn't already suspect the answer to.

"I sold a man a red thread," I said. "For his debts."

Her face did something complicated. Pity and fury sharing a small room. "Ansel—"

"It's a *thread*, Maren. It's wool. I bought it off a peddler from Duskmarket for a farthing."

"And you said words over it."

"I say words over everything. I said words over your birthday stew. Words are free."

She looked at me for a long moment, and I looked back with the bland open face I use on the queen's physicians, and finally she shook her head and went back to her knife. But she said, quietly, without looking up: "If you're doing what I think you're doing, stop. Whatever it costs you to stop. Because they burned a woman in Coppersmith Ward last month for less than growing roots out of a man's chest, and I don't attend many funerals, Ansel. I'd like to keep it that way."

I promised her nothing, which is its own kind of answer, and she knew it, and we both chopped pears until the silence turned safe again.

* * *

That night I went to see for myself.

Tanner's Row still smelled of smoke. Where Vole had died there was a scorch-mark shaped like nothing, and around it — this was the part that put ice down my spine — around it, pushing up through the gaps in the cobblestones, green shoots. In Highmoor. In the packed heart of the oldest quarter of the city, where nothing has grown wild in living memory, something green was growing out of the stones, and it hadn't been there three days ago, and it was *reaching toward the scorch mark* like a plant toward sun.

I crouched down. I should not have touched it. Every instinct I own screamed that I should not touch it. I touched it.

The shoot was warm. Warm as skin. And when my finger brushed it, I felt — I don't have a better word, and I've tried several — I felt *attention*. The way you feel someone watching the back of your neck. Something enormous and patient and buried very deep had noticed Garrick Vole, and having noticed him, had noticed the thread of intention that led to him, and had followed that thread back along its length like a hound on a scent, past Odo's three coppers, past my counter, past my candle and my smoke and my mother's knee —

— and stopped. For now. Curled at the end of the thread like a cat at a door, waiting to see if I'd pray again.

The old gods haven't answered a prayer in a hundred years. Everyone knows this. It's the first thing they teach you: the gods are gone, the fire keeps what's left of them caged, and magic is dead, which is why magic is illegal — a law against a ghost. A hundred years of hedge-charms and knotted threads doing exactly nothing, sold by frauds like me to desperate people, and the whole rotten gentle economy of it humming along because the gods were *asleep*.

Someone had woken them up. Or something. Or — and this thought arrived whole and terrible, wearing my own voice — or they had never been asleep at all, only deaf, and somewhere in the last hundred years, underneath Highmoor, under the mountain, behind the fire, something had begun to *listen*.

And the first honest prayer in a century had come from me. A food-taster. A liar. A man who sells hope by the farthing and calls it charity.

I walked home the long way, checking my shadow twice.

* * *

Back in my rooms, I did the arithmetic of terror, which is the only kind I'm good at.

Item: one charm, sold, effective beyond all reason. Item: one corpse with roots in its lungs, publicly burned, publicly discussed. Item: the palace spymaster pays gold for names, and Odo Fenner knows whose counter he stood at. Item: Maren suspects. Maren always suspects. Item, and this is the item I kept circling back to like a tongue to a broken tooth: whatever answered me is still listening. Still curled at the end of the thread. And I had a whole drawer full of threads.

I tipped the drawer out onto the floor. Fever-knots, sleep-sachets, luck-threads, cradle-charms, seven years of harmless lies in dyed wool and dried herbs. Seven years of believing I was running a con on the universe and getting away with it.

I fed them to the brazier one at a time, and I made myself watch each one burn, and I did not pray, and I did not say words, and when the last of it was ash I sat on the floor with my back against the bed and laughed until it turned into something else, because here is the joke, the beautiful stupid joke of it:

Burning them changes nothing. I know that now. You cannot un-ring a god. Whatever heard me heard me, and it knows my name the way I know the taste of arsenic — intimately, professionally, forever. The stock wasn't the danger. The stock was never the danger.

I was.

Somewhere below the city, something old shifted its weight against a cage, and settled in to wait for the next prayer. And I lay awake until dawn cataloguing everyone I'd ever sold a charm to, every knot and sachet and whispered blessing, wondering which of them would come to my door next — and what, exactly, I would be feeding when I fed it.

I didn't have to wonder long. There was a knock at my window at first light. Three taps, soft, deliberate — the knock of someone who knows the difference between my door and my shop.

When I opened the shutters, the herb-woman Sefa was standing on the tiles below, gray as ash, holding a ledger bound in black leather, and she said the sentence I will hear for the rest of my life however long that turns out to be:

"Blackwood. Your family name is in this book. You'll want to sit down."
