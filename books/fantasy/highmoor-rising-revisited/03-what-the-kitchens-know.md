## 3. What the Kitchens Know

There are three ways to learn anything in Highmoor Palace, and none of them is being told.

The first is the servants' stair, which carries gossip upward faster than any herald carries news downward. The second is the laundry, where sheets are wrung of secrets along with the wash-water. The third — and this is the one I trust, because it's mine — is the tasting table. You would be astonished what a man will confess while you hold a spoon to his mouth and say, *swallow or don't, it's your funeral either way.*

I'm Maren Ostwick, second taster to Her Majesty Queen Isolde, and I have held that post for eleven years without once dying, which in my profession counts as a distinguished career. My colleague Ansel Blackwood has held his for seven, and he's never died either, but he manages it with a kind of elegance that irritates me. He tastes poison the way other men taste wine. He swallows arsenic like it's a compliment. And lately — and here is where my story turns sour, so pour yourself something — lately he's been swallowing things before I can count them.

But I'm getting ahead of myself. Rumors first. Rumors are how everything starts in this palace, and how most things end.

* * *

The first rumor arrived on a Tuesday, riding in on the shoulders of a scullery boy who'd heard it from a groom who'd heard it from nobody in particular, which is how the best rumors claim their parentage. The queen was dreaming again. Not the ordinary royal dreams of taxes and traitors, but dreams with teeth in them. She'd woken three nights running calling out a name no one recognized — *Vess,* or *Vesh,* or some such — and her lady-in-waiting swore the candles in the royal bedchamber had guttered all at once, all together, like a held breath let go.

"Candles do that," I said, trimming fat off a capon for the noon tasting. "Drafts exist. So does coincidence."

The scullery boy, whose name is Pip and whose entire strategic value lies in knowing everyone's business, gave me the look that boys give adults who are pretending not to be interested. "Her Majesty asked for the old rites read over her bed, m'mister. The ones from before the Ban. Cook's cousin works in the chapel and she says Father Aldery wouldn't do it, so they sent down to the city for someone who would."

"Someone who would," I repeated. "That's a phrase with a great deal of room in it."

"That's what I said!" Pip beamed, delighted to have said anything at all.

I want to be clear about why this mattered, because if you didn't grow up in Highmoor you might not feel the cold coming off it. Magic has been outlawed in this city for a hundred years — not discouraged, not taxed, *outlawed*, with the same enthusiasm the crown applies to treason. We don't burn witches anymore; we're civilized. We simply stop selling them flour, and then we stop selling them anything, and then one morning there's an empty house on Tanner's Row and the aldermen agree among themselves never to mention it. The old gods went with the magic, locked away behind whatever bargain the founders struck, and dragon-fire — the last dragon's fire, chained somewhere official and unspoken — keeps the lock turned. Every child learns the rhyme. *Sleep, old gods, beneath the stone; the last flame burns, and you're alone.* Children sing it skipping rope. That should have told us something about ourselves, honestly.

So when the queen started dreaming names, and asking after forbidden rites, the kitchen did what the kitchen always does: it cooked through the fear. There is no anxiety in this palace that cannot be improved by bread.

* * *

The second rumor came the following week, and it had feet.

Two servants gone in ten days. Not runaways — runaways leave debts and sweethearts and at least one dramatic note. These two left nothing. Tam Weller, a potboy with a girl in every ward and a mouth that never stopped moving, simply wasn't at his station one morning. Then Odile from the stillroom, who was forty years old and had served the queen since the queen was a princess with mud on her hem, missed her shift, and her room was found neat as a coffin. Bed made. Shoes paired. A half-finished letter on the desk that just stopped mid-word, as though the writer had been interrupted, or as though she'd lost interest in the future.

"The mountain took them," whispered the laundry women, who whisper everything, correctly, eventually. "There's a door under the mountain, and it's been sealed since the old king's day, and now it isn't."

"There is no door," said the steward, in the tone of a man reciting scripture he'd personally written.

"There's always been a door," said Old Hesta, who scrubbed the great hearth and claimed to remember three reigns, though she'd also claimed to remember the plague of frogs, which had happened forty years before her birth. "Sealed with lead and liturgy. My mother carried mortar for the sealing. She said the masons wept the whole time and wouldn't say why."

"And now?" I asked, because someone had to, and cowardice has never been my favorite dish.

Hesta looked at me with eyes gone pale as skimmed milk. "Now the wax is off the seal, dearie. Somebody's been reading letters meant for older hands."

I laughed. I want that on the record. I laughed, and I kept chopping, and I did not sleep well that night or any night after, which in a food-taster is practically a resignation letter, since our whole trade depends on a steady hand and a calm stomach.

* * *

Here is the thing about Ansel Blackwood, and I say this with love, or with whatever you call the feeling you have for the person who shares your death-table eleven hours a day: he has always been slightly elsewhere. Even when he laughs — and he does laugh, beautifully, at exactly the right moments — there's a part of him standing back, taking notes, keeping accounts. Seven years I've worked beside him and I could not tell you where he sleeps, whom he loves, or what he spends his coin on, only that he never seems short of it, and a taster's wage is honest but thin.

I told myself for years that this was grief. Everyone knows the story, or the shape of it: parents dead, a little sister lost in the fever years, a boy alone who clawed his way into the one palace post where caution is a virtue. It's a good story. It explains his carefulness, his neutrality, the way he refuses every faction and flatters every faction, the way he is liked by everyone and known by no one. I built that story myself, brick by brick, because I needed him to be knowable. You cannot sit across a tasting table from a mystery. You'd never swallow.

Then the rumors started, and the bricks began to come loose.

It was small things at first. Ansel burning something in his brazier after hours — herbs, I assumed, though the smoke smelled wrong, sweet and grave-dirty, like violets growing in a churchyard. Ansel declining wine he'd normally sip. Ansel looking at the sealed stairwell that leads down toward the mountain cellars with an expression I'd only ever seen on men reading their own death warrants. And once — once — I caught him with his sleeve pushed back, and there were marks on his forearm. Thin lines, silvered, like old scars in the shape of roots. Or veins. Or writing.

"Cat," I said.

"Mm," he said, pulling the sleeve down with the smoothness of long practice.

"You don't have a cat."

"I have many cats. They're distributed throughout the city for operational purposes." And he smiled, and I smiled, and we both tasted the queen's consommé, and neither of us died, and I lay awake until dawn doing arithmetic with everything I knew about him and finding the sums wouldn't close.

Because here's what a taster knows that a guard doesn't: we know bodies. We know what people look like when something is working through them — poison, plague, passion, it's all chemistry to us. And I had begun to notice that the people around Ansel had a way of getting *better*. The laundress with the wasting cough, hale as a horse within a fortnight. The under-cook's bad knee, healed. Small mercies, scattered through the lower rooms like coins from a torn purse. Charities nobody claimed. And charity, in a city where magic is outlawed, has a very particular smell.

* * *

The third rumor didn't come through the stairs or the laundry. It came through me, standing in the spice pantry with Ansel, counting cardamom pods into the queen's morning posset, and it came out of my own mouth before I could stop it, which is how the truest things usually arrive.

"They're saying there's sorcery in the palace," I said. "Real sorcery. Working charms. The kind that answer."

Ansel's hands did not pause. That was the first wrong thing. Men pause. Innocent men especially — innocence is always reaching for its credentials. His hands kept counting, even and slow, twelve pods, thirteen.

"Dangerous business, believing rumors," he said.

"Tam Weller believed rumors," I said. "Odile too, maybe. Two servants gone into a sealed mountain, Ansel, and the queen dreaming names, and half the scullery swearing the gods are awake and hungry, and you—" I stopped, because the next part was a cliff edge, and I've never been brave so much as reckless. "And you smelling of graveyard smoke and hiding marks under your sleeves and paying out miracles to laundresses."

Silence. The kind with weight to it. Somewhere above us a kettle screamed and was silenced.

When he finally spoke, his voice was gentle, and that frightened me more than shouting would have. "Maren. If I were the sort of man those rumors describe — the kind who sells charms, the kind the old gods might listen to — what would you do?"

"Report you," I said immediately, and hated how fast it came, and meant it anyway, because that's the job, that's the law, that's the wall we all agreed to stand inside. "I'd report you. And then I'd testify at your trial, and I'd bring flowers to your grave, and I'd hate myself for the rest of my life, and I'd still do it. That's what you'd want me to do. It's what you'd do for me."

He looked at me for a long moment, and whatever stood behind his eyes was further away than the mountain. "Yes," he said quietly. "It is." And then, softer, almost to himself: "Gods help us both, it is."

He took the posset up to the queen's table himself that morning. Volunteered. Which he never does, because the morning tasting is my rotation, and Ansel Blackwood does not break rotation, does not break pattern, does not break anything except, apparently, silence.

I watched him go up the servants' stair with the covered cup balanced in his careful hands, and I thought about doors under mountains, and names dreamed by queens, and a sister dead seven years whose grave I had once, drunk on festival wine, promised to visit and never had. I thought about how a man might feed something hungry if he loved it enough. I thought about root-shaped scars.

Then I went and found Pip, and I paid him a week's wages to follow Ansel Blackwood after dark and tell me everywhere he went and everyone he spoke to — and to keep his mouth shut about it, which for Pip is the expensive part.

The boy came back at moonset, white as flour, and could only manage four words before he was sick into the herb bed:

"He went to the walls."
