## 4. The God-Speaker in the Wall

The dead man's boots were the first clue.

Not the root-growth through his lungs — that had been the gods' work, or whatever passed for it these days. No, the boots. Debt-collectors in Highmoor wore soft-soled shoes so they could creep up on you in stairwells. This man had been wearing good leather, oiled and heeled, the kind of boots a man buys when he expects to live long enough to break them in. He hadn't expected to die in a tanner's yard with birch saplings sprouting from his open mouth like a green confession.

Which meant someone had prayed hard enough to be specific. And specific prayers, in my experience, came from people who'd been told — by me, usually — that charms were gentle things. A knot tied against fever. A word sewn into a hem to turn away bad luck. I had built seven years of careful neutrality on the promise that nothing I sold could do more than nudge the world.

Something had shoved.

So I did what any sensible outlaw does when the law of nature breaks: I followed the money backward through the city until it led somewhere impossible.

* * *

The charm in question had gone to Odell Fenner, a cooper with three children and a debt that grew teeth every quarter-day. I remembered him well — big hands, bigger worry, the particular desperation of a man who'd already sold everything sellable. He'd come to me behind the dye-works, pressed two silver bits into my palm, and asked for something to make the collector "leave us be." I'd given him a knotted cord and a lie: *this turns anger aside.* Anger, I'd said. Not men. Not lungs full of growing things.

I found him at his workshop near the Salt Gate, and I will say this for Odell: he didn't pretend. When I showed him my face at his door he went gray as unseasoned oak and let me in without a word, the way guilty men do, as if silence might make the whole thing smaller.

"Tell me exactly," I said. "Every word. What you said when you took the cord."

He sat on a barrel meant for wine and wrung his hands like laundry. "I did what you told me. Tied the knots. Said the words."

"All of them?"

A pause. The pause was the answer.

"Odell."

"There was — after. My girl's been sick, and the rent, and I thought—" His voice cracked down the middle. "I prayed. Properly. Like my gran used to, before the Ban. Kneeling and all. I asked the old ones to make him leave us be. I used your cord because it felt like — like a door left open."

A door left open. Seven years I'd been selling knotted string and calling it commerce, and it had never once been anything but string. Now, suddenly, the doors were real, and something on the other side was leaning its weight against them.

"What did you ask for exactly?" I kept my voice level. Taster's training. You learn to keep your face smooth over poison.

"That he'd never trouble another family again." Odell looked up at me, and there it was — not guilt, not yet, just the raw bewildered relief of a man whose prayer had worked. "And he hasn't. They sent no one else. Ansel, they sent *no one else.*"

I burned my stock that night. Every charm, every sewn word, every knotted cord — eleven years of inventory, gone in a brazier behind my rooms while I fed the fire page by page like a man destroying evidence, which is what it was. The smoke smelled of juniper and tallow and, underneath, something older, like a temple floor after rain. As if the gods had been standing close enough to smell it too.

*Watching,* I thought. *They're watching again.*

I needed to know who had opened the door wide enough for Odell Fenner's prayer to walk through.

* * *

It took me four days, and what it took was listening — really listening — to the city. Highmoor talks constantly and says almost nothing, but grief talks differently than gossip. Grief has a direction.

Three whispers, all pointing the same way. A fishwife on the Weir Steps who swore her drowned son had come to her in a dream and asked for bread. A widow on Candle Lane who'd stopped buying grave-wax because, she said, the dead didn't need it anymore; they were *warm*. And a beggar-child near the Gate of Chains who told anyone who'd stand still long enough that the walls were humming, that if you put your ear to the stones by the old postern you could hear something breathing.

Walls don't breathe. But hollow walls echo, and Highmoor's walls are old enough to have secrets in their bones. The city was built in three layers — the outer curtain, an inner ring from the war centuries, and between them, sealed and forgotten, a corridor of dead masonry that the maps stopped marking two hundred years ago. I knew this because palace kitchens know everything about buildings that food has ever passed through, and once upon a time the old wall's hidden ways had carried supplies during sieges.

I went at dusk, when the watch changed and the lamplighters were still arguing with the wind. The postern by the Gate of Chains had been bricked shut so long the mortar had gone the color of bone, but bricks are just promises, and promises fail. A section the size of a coffin lid sat loose — recently loosened, the edges still bright where stone had scraped stone.

Inside, the air tasted of cold ash and pigeon and, faintly, of juniper. The same smell as my burning stock. Someone had been burning offerings here, and recently, and often.

"Hallo?" I said, because I'm an idiot with excellent manners. "I'm looking for whoever's been answering prayers."

Silence. Then, from somewhere deep in the wall's throat, a woman's voice, dry as old vellum:

"You're the taster."

"I'm the taster."

"They said the taster's tongue would know. Come in, then. Mind the third step; it lies."

* * *

She lived in a chamber the size of a modest tomb, lit by oil lamps trimmed so low the shadows had texture. Shelves lined the curved stone walls, and every shelf held bones — not grisly heaps, but arranged, labeled, *tended*, each one wrapped in linen marked with faded sigils. A god-speaker's reliquary. I hadn't seen one outside a museum since the Ban.

The woman herself was sixty-ish and built like a question mark, with ink-stained fingers and eyes the pale gray of a sky deciding whether to snow. She looked at me the way I look at a dish I've been asked to identify blind: thoroughly, and with professional disappointment.

"Sefa," she said. "Formerly Speaker of the Third Order, formerly licensed, formerly employed. Formerly a great many things." She gestured at a stool. "Sit. You'll want to sit for what comes next, and I'd rather you didn't fall on my bones."

I sat. "You've been answering prayers."

"The gods have been answering prayers. I've been writing them down." She tapped a ledger on her table — a fat, grim thing bound in gray leather. "There's a difference, and the difference is the entire history of my disgrace. The Orders believed we spoke *for* the gods. We don't. We speak *to* them, and mostly they ignore us, and the honest ones among us wrote that down and got exiled for it." Her mouth twisted. "I was honest. I'm now a heretic living inside a wall. The career arc of truth-telling is not glamorous."

"But they're answering now. Why?"

"Because someone is feeding them." She said it flatly, the way you'd report weather. "Souls, taster. Fresh ones. The old gods sleep under the mountain, caged behind dragon-fire, and dragon-fire runs on sacrifice the way lamps run on oil. For a hundred years the flame's been fed on ceremony and memory and it's guttered down to a wick. Now somebody's giving it the rich stuff. Whole souls. And a god with a full belly wakes up hungry for more, and the first thing a waking god does is listen." She leaned forward. "You've noticed the listening. Your cooper's prayer went straight through. That cord of yours wasn't magic. It was just a door, and doors only matter when something's on the other side pushing."

My mouth had gone dry. "Who's feeding them?"

"That," she said, "is the wrong question. Here's the right one." She pulled the ledger toward her and turned pages with a scholar's economy, past columns of names and dates and prices, and stopped, and turned it to face me, and I understood why she'd wanted me sitting down.

Two entries. Same surname. Mine.

The first was dated seven years back, the week my sister died. *Blackwood, Wren. Age 19. Cause entered: fever. Disposition: court beneath the mountain. Brokered rate: dynastic.*

The second was newer, the ink barely brown. *Blackwood, Ansel. Royal food-taster. Immunity maintained per contract. Note: blood-mark active. Do not approach without authority.*

"Do not approach," I read aloud, because reading it silently made it too much like a dream. "Whose handwriting—"

"The queen's spymaster keeps the ledger. The queen sets the rates." Sefa's voice gentled, which somehow made it worse. "Your sister's soul was sold, taster. Sold to feed the flame, to keep the gods sleeping, to buy the crown another generation. And your immunity — the reason you can sip arsenic and smile — isn't a gift of the palace. It's a *payment*. They bought your tolerance the same year they bought her, and they've been topping it up ever since, because your blood carries the mark of her bargain and they want their investment alive and docile."

Seven years. Seven years I'd grieved a fever, blamed the physicians, blamed myself for the tincture I'd mixed too strong. Seven years I'd eaten the palace's bread and called it belonging, and the whole time I'd been a line item. *Immunity maintained per contract.*

"Wren's alive," I said. It wasn't a question. Souls don't burn; they're *kept*.

"She's in the vault beneath the mountain, with the others. And she won't be the only name in that ledger you know, if you keep reading." Sefa closed the book and rested her ink-stained hand flat on the cover, like a woman pinning something down. "Now here's the part where you decide what kind of man you are, because I've watched you since Odell Fenner's collector died, and you didn't run, and you didn't report, and you burned your stock instead of raising prices. So. There's a dragon under the palace whose fire is failing, and a vault under the mountain where your sister is shelved like tax records, and a queen who trades souls to keep her throne warm. I can show you the road down. But the road down doesn't come back up the same, and neither do the people who take it."

I looked at the ledger. At my name, priced and noted and filed. At hers above it, seven years cold.

"I want my sister back," I said, and heard my own voice go quiet and certain, the way it never did over the queen's breakfast. "Whatever it costs."

Sefa nodded slowly, unsurprised, and began wrapping bones into a satchel with the brisk tenderness of a woman packing for a journey she'd been dreading for decades.

"Then rest tonight," she said. "Eat well. Trust your kitchen. Because tomorrow I'm taking you to see the last dragon in Highmoor — and taster, when you see how little fire she has left, you're going to understand exactly why the queen is in such a hurry to keep feeding her."
