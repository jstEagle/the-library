## 5. Dragon-Fire Guttering

I have lived inside the walls of Highmoor for eleven years, and in that time I have learned that a city is mostly hollow. People think of stone as solid, as permanent, but stone is just a skin the city wears over its emptiness. I live in the emptiness. There are corridors between the outer and inner faces of the wall, wide enough for a thin woman and her regrets, lit by nothing, warmed by nothing, and I know every footfall of them the way other women know the lines of their mothers' faces.

So when Ansel Blackwood came to me with the ledger clutched in his fist like a man holding his own heart outside his chest, I did not take him through any door. Doors are for people who belong somewhere.

"Where are you taking me?" he asked, for the fourth time.

"Down," I said.

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only honest one I have." I ducked beneath a crossbeam older than the dynasty. "Everything else would be a kindness, and I've stopped being kind. It's expensive."

He followed anyway. That was the thing about Ansel Blackwood that I was already beginning to understand: he could be terrified and stubborn in the same breath, and neither canceled the other out. Most men pick one. He carried both like a taster carries two spoons.

* * *

The way down begins behind a cistern in the wall's third gallery, where the masons of the old kings left a seam they never mortared — whether from laziness or shame, no record survives, and I have looked. The passage beyond slopes at an angle that suggests it was never meant for feet. It was meant for something being led down on chains, or perhaps for something walking down willingly, back when willing sacrifice still counted for something in this city.

Ansel's lantern threw our shadows huge against the wet stone. He kept glancing at them as if they might say something.

"You said souls," he said finally. "Fed to the gods. Like — grain to geese?"

"Worse. Geese stay geese." I stepped over a rusted iron ring bolted into the floor, big enough to pass my whole arm through. "A soul given to an old god doesn't vanish. It's eaten, yes, but eating wakes. Every soul is a mouthful of morning to something that's been sleeping a hundred years under a mountain of law."

"And the queen knows this."

"The queen pays for it."

He stopped walking. In the lantern light his face did something complicated — the look of a man tasting a dish and finding, beneath the familiar flavors, something rotten that had been there all along. Tasters must be good at that. Finding the rot under the flavor.

"I serve her," he said. "I've tasted her food for seven years. I've stood between her and poison more times than she knows."

"Yes," I said. "And she's been standing between you and the truth the whole time. Everyone in this city stands somewhere. She chose to stand on top."

He didn't answer, which was its own kind of answer, and we went down.

* * *

You feel the dragon before you see it. That's the part no one believes, because no one alive has felt it except me and now him. It's warmth first — not fire-warmth, not hearth-warmth, but the warmth of a body so large that its blood has its own weather. Then a sound below hearing, a pressure in the sternum, like leaning against a door someone enormous is breathing behind.

The last gallery opens without warning. One moment we were crawling through a crack in the world; the next, the crack became a cavern, and the cavern became a throat, and the throat held a god's mistake and the realm's salvation, chained.

She is smaller than the old paintings show. They always paint dragons vast as cathedrals, wings spanning harbors, and perhaps she was once, when there were others. Now she lies coiled in the dark like the last ember in a cold grate, and the chains — nine of them, each thick as an oak, each anchored into the living rock by seals carved with the names of dead kings — hold her not because she is strong but because she is tired.

Her flame is what holds the gods down. Not her body. Her body is just the lamp. The fire is the lock, and the fire is going out.

"Oh," Ansel said. Just that. A single syllable, the way a man speaks when language fails him and only awe remains, before horror arrives to fill the vacancy.

"She's called Vess," I said. "Though the name hasn't been spoken aloud in a century, so she may have forgotten it herself."

"How long?"

"Look at her scales. See how they've gone gray at the edges? Like ash that hasn't decided to fall?" I crouched at the gallery's edge, where the heat reached us faintly, a dying hearth's ghost. "When I found this place five years ago, the gray came up to her shoulders. Last winter it passed her ribs. By midsummer—" I made myself say it plainly, because plainness was all I had left to give anyone. "By midsummer it will reach her heart. And when it does, the flame goes out, and the seal goes with it, and everything the old kings buried under their law gets up and puts on flesh."

"Flesh," he repeated. "They'd take bodies?"

"They'd take anything. Yours. Mine. The laundress you sold charms to. The queen. Gods don't knock, Ansel. They move in."

He was quiet a long moment. The silence in that place isn't empty — it's full, pressurized, the silence of a room where something immense is listening. I have spent eleven years talking to myself in the walls, and even I lower my voice here.

"So the souls," he said at last. "The queen trades souls to the gods to keep them asleep. To buy time until—"

"Until what? Until a miracle? Until a second dragon crawls out of the sea?" I laughed, and the sound came out wrong in that chamber, too small and too human. "There is no until. There's only delay, and the price of delay, and the price keeps rising. Seven years ago the court needed a large payment. Someone provided one. Several ones. Children, mostly. Sold through brokers who never asked why the coin smelled of the palace mint."

His hand went to the ledger inside his coat. Wren. I watched him not say his sister's name, watched him hold it behind his teeth like a coal he couldn't spit out or swallow.

"Why show me this?" he asked. "Why me? You've been down here alone for years. You could have stayed alone."

"Because alone wasn't working." I looked at Vess, at the gray climbing her throat like frost claiming a window. "I tried petitions. I tried the temples, before they stripped me of my office and my name. I tried writing letters to a queen who signs her replies with a spymaster's seal. Nothing. And then a charm-seller in the Sump sells a working that kills a man with root-growth, and suddenly the gods are answering prayers again — hungry, awake, listening — and I thought: there. There's a man the gods notice. Maybe they'll notice him twice."

"That's not a plan, Sefa. That's a prayer."

I smiled. My face has forgotten how, so it probably looked like a wound. "In Highmoor these days, what's the difference?"

* * *

We should have left then. I want that recorded, wherever such things get recorded — in whatever ledger the old gods keep, if they keep ledgers, if they can read. We should have turned around and climbed back into the skin of the city and let the end come the way ends come: slowly, officially, with committees.

But Vess opened one eye.

It was the color of a banked fire, orange gone deep as garnet, and it found us in the dark with terrible patience. Eleven years I had come to this chamber, and eleven years she had slept. Perhaps the ledger in Ansel's coat smelled of the vault. Perhaps souls leave a scent. Or perhaps she was simply done pretending, the way the dying stop pretending near the end.

She spoke without moving her lips. The voice arrived the way her warmth did — through the chest, through the bones, bypassing the ear entirely.

*"Taster,"* she said, and I felt Ansel flinch beside me, because the word was for him, not me. *"You carry a debt-name. Blood sold below. The mountain knows your family."*

"Don't answer her," I whispered. "Don't bargain with anything down here. That's how it starts. That's how it always starts."

But he was already stepping forward, past me, toward the edge of the gallery, toward the chained ruin of the realm's last lock, and when he spoke his voice was steady in a way that frightened me more than the dragon did.

"Then the mountain knows where my sister is."

*"Yes."*

"Then take me down."

The eye closed. The warmth in the chamber shifted — I swear it, shifted, the way a house shifts when someone inside turns over in bed — and somewhere far beneath us, deeper than the dragon, deeper than the mines, something ancient and patient exhaled.

Vess's flame guttered as she settled back into sleep. I saw it clearly: the light along her spine dimmed, steadied, dimmed again, like a candle deciding.

And in the gray of her scales, where the ash had been creeping for years, I saw — or I dreamed I saw, and I no longer trust the difference — a new patch of gray, fresh, spreading, fed by something. Fed by a payment recently made.

The queen was still buying time. And time, it seemed, had started buying back.

"We need help," I said to Ansel. "Not charm-seller help. Not wall-rat help. We need someone who can open the mountain road, and there's exactly one man in Highmoor with the keys, and he's palace guard, and he's owned."

Ansel looked at me across the dying light of the last dragon in the world.

"Then let's go make him an offer," he said, "he can't refuse honestly."

Which, I would learn, was the only kind of offer anyone made in this city anymore.
