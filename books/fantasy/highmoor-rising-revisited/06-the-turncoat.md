## 6. The Turncoat

There are three ways a man becomes a traitor. The first is ambition, which is just greed wearing a better coat. The second is conviction, which is greed that's learned to quote scripture. And the third — the one nobody warns you about — is love, which doesn't even bother to dress up. It walks in the front door of your soul in its nightclothes and starts rearranging the furniture.

I'd like to tell you I fell into the third category with some dignity. That I fought it. That when the queen's spymaster slid the little portrait of my daughter across his desk — Pip, missing her front tooth, scowling at the painter because she always scowled at painters — I threw him out on his ear.

What actually happened was that my hand moved toward the picture before my brain could stop it, and Master Corvane watched that small betrayal with the satisfaction of a man watching a lock turn.

"Captain Ivo," he said. "You have a reputation for incorruptibility. I find those are the easiest men to corrupt. They've never practiced saying no."

Corvane was not what people imagined when they imagined the queen's spymaster. People imagined shadows and knives. Corvane was a soft-bellied man with ink-stained fingers and the manner of a provincial tax clerk, which was infinitely worse, because tax clerks never raise their voices either, and they always get paid.

"The mountain road," he said. "The old service gate beneath the guardhouse. You hold the only key that isn't ceremonial."

"That road's been sealed since the last reign."

"And yet you keep the key polished." He smiled. He had small, even teeth, like a hymnal full of piano keys. "Sentiment, Captain?"

"Duty."

"Of course." He turned the portrait so it faced me properly. Pip, age six, mid-scowl. "Her mother's family has land in Vessany. Good air. Good schools. Far from Highmoor, where children of disgraced officers sometimes fall down wells."

"My daughter has never been near a well."

"No," Corvane agreed pleasantly. "Not yet."

Here is the thing about blackmail that no one tells you: it works best on good men. A bad man has already priced everything he owns; you can't surprise him with an invoice. But a good man keeps one thing off the ledger, one thing he's never once thought of as negotiable, and so when someone finally names a figure for it, he discovers he'll pay anything.

I looked at Pip's scowl for a long time. Then I said, "The gate opens for whom?"

"A taster. A woman from the walls. You won't need to know more than that."

"I need to know exactly that much," I said, "and nothing less."

Corvane's smile thinned by a degree. Spymasters hate specificity the way surgeons hate dirt. "A retrieval," he said. "Something the crown misplaced. The queen wants it back quietly, before certain parties notice it's gone."

"Certain parties being the gods."

The room went very still. Somewhere below us, muffled by six floors of palace, the kitchens were starting their morning noise — the great kettles, the chopping, Maren Hollis laughing at something at a volume that carried through stone. Ordinary sounds. Sounds from a world where the gods were a story told to frighten children and the worst thing a captain faced before breakfast was a drunk second lieutenant.

"You've been listening at doors," Corvane said quietly.

"I've been standing at doors," I said. "Listening is your trade."

He studied me then, really studied me, the way a man checks a coin for clipping. Whatever he saw, he decided it was spendable. "Yes," he said. "The gods. Her Majesty has kept them fed and drowsy for seven years at considerable cost, and something is waking them early. She wants the situation resolved before the fire fails entirely. You will open the road, the retrieval party will go down, and you will forget every word spoken in this office. In exchange—"

He tapped the portrait once, gently, the way you'd tap a horse you'd just decided not to shoot.

"In exchange my daughter learns her letters in Vessany instead of a well," I said.

"Captain. I would never threaten a child." He gathered his papers with small precise movements. "I merely observe that Highmoor is an old city built over older things, and accidents are traditional here."

* * *

Ansel Blackwood came to my quarters that night smelling of charcoal and river water, which was fitting, because the man was half smoke and half current himself — always moving, never quite where you'd sworn you'd seen him last.

We'd known each other eight years, the way a wall knows ivy. He'd pulled me out of a tavern brawl my first month as a lieutenant, not by fighting but by talking the whole room into forgetting why they were angry, which should have been my first clue about him. Since then: a thousand breakfasts in the guard corridor, chess games he let me win exactly often enough to keep me coming back, and never once — not once in eight years — a word about where he went after dark.

That kind of discipline isn't secrecy. Secrecy slips. That kind of discipline is craft.

"Wine?" I asked, though we both knew the answer. A royal food-taster who drinks other people's wine is a man with a death wish or a death sentence, and Ansel was neither. He took the chair by the window instead, sat with his hands loose in his lap, and looked at me with those calm brown eyes that had watched ten thousand spoons pass the queen's lips without ever once flinching.

"Corvane spoke to you," he said.

"He did."

"And you're going to open the road."

"I'm going to hear your offer first," I said. "Then decide whose lie I prefer."

Something flickered behind his face — not guilt exactly. Recognition. The look of a man hearing his own accent in a foreign country.

"Your daughter," he said. "Pip. She's how they're holding you."

It wasn't a question, and I didn't insult us both by pretending otherwise. "And you? What's Corvane holding over you?"

"Nothing." He almost laughed. "That's the joke of it. He thinks everyone has a price because he does. I'm doing this for free."

"Nobody does treason for free."

"Then call it something else." He leaned forward, elbows on knees, and for the first time in eight years I saw the exhaustion under the calm — the specific fatigue of a man who has been carrying something heavy in plain sight while everyone compliments him on his excellent posture. "Call it a debt. Seven years ago my sister died. Except she didn't die. Her soul was sold — sold to the court beneath the mountain, sold by this palace, signed and sealed and filed away like a shipment of pepper. She's been down there ever since. Fed to something hungry, a spoonful at a time, to keep old gods asleep and a queen comfortable." His voice stayed level. That was the terrible part. Level as a still pond, with the whole drowning underneath. "I'm going to get her back."

I've commanded men through sieges. I've written letters to widows until my hand cramped. I know every register of grief there is, and what I heard in Ansel Blackwood's flat, careful voice was the worst one: grief that has finished burning and cooled into architecture. Load-bearing grief. The kind that holds up a whole life.

"And the throne?" I asked. Because that was the word Corvane had used, and the word I needed to use, and the word we were both going to lie about. "When you pull your sister out of whatever vault they keep her in — the queen's bargains come up with her. Names. Seals. Signatures. What happens to all that?"

Ansel was quiet for a moment. Outside, the palace bells rang the ninth hour, and somewhere below, Maren Hollis was still laughing, and the world we were both about to break was going about its evening business, unaware.

"It stays buried," he said. "I swear it. This is a theft, Ivo, not a revolution. We go down, we take what's mine, we come up, and the mountain closes behind us. The queen keeps her secrets. You keep your daughter. Nobody burns."

He held out his hand to seal it, the way soldiers do, the way men do who've shared a thousand breakfasts and want the gesture to mean what it's always meant.

I took it. His grip was steady. Mine was too. Two liars, shaking on it, each of us certain the other was the honest one.

Because here's what I knew, holding that warm dry hand: Ansel Blackwood had spent eight years being neutral, and neutrals don't stay neutral. Neutrality is just a position you haven't been forced to defend yet. Sooner or later he was going to stand in front of that vault with his sister's name on the ledger and the queen's seal beside it, and he was going to have to choose between the truth and the promise he'd just made me, and I knew — the way you know weather coming, the way you know a bridge is rotten before the first plank gives — I knew which way he'd choose.

And here's what he knew, or guessed, looking at my face: that I'd already sent a letter to my wife that afternoon telling her to take Pip to her sister's in Vessany, quietly, without packing that looked like fleeing. That I intended to honor my bargain with Corvane right up until the moment honoring it stopped protecting anyone, and that I'd already decided — privately, in the officer's washroom, staring at myself in a spotted mirror — that if it came to choosing between the queen's secrets and a child's safety, I knew which way *I'd* choose.

Two compromised men, recognizing each other across a chess table. Neither of us calling the bluff. Both of us knowing.

"One condition," I said, releasing his hand. "You come back up. All three of you. If the mountain keeps any of you, I burn Corvane's arrangement and every secret in it along with him. Understood?"

"Understood," Ansel said, and stood, and paused at the door with his hand on the frame.

"For what it's worth," he said, not turning around, "I'm sorry about the well."

"There hasn't been a well."

"Not yet," he said, and was gone.

I sat alone with the chessboard for a long while after that, setting up the pieces for a game neither of us would play, and outside the window the mountain loomed black against the stars, and deep beneath it — deeper than mines, deeper than memory — something old rolled over in its sleep, and the last dragon-fire guttered like a candle in a doorway, and I began, very quietly, to plan my treason within the treason.

The gate key was in the third drawer, wrapped in oilcloth, polished every month for eight years.

Duty, I'd told Corvane.

Sentiment, he'd guessed.

Neither of them had come close. It was habit. Eight years of polishing a key for a door I'd never opened, waiting — without ever letting myself know I was waiting — for the night someone knocked and asked me to be brave.

That night had arrived. It smelled of charcoal and river water, and it had lied to me beautifully, and I loved it like a brother.
