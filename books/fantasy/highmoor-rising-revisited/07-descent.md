## 7. Descent

The mountain road, as it turned out, was a hole in the floor of a flooded mine, and Ivo looked at it the way a man looks at his own grave.

"This is it," he said. "The great secret way into the palace's foundations. A puddle with ambitions."

"It goes down," Sefa said. She stood at the lip of the shaft with her lantern held high, and the light fell into black water that swallowed it without comment. "Everything down here goes down. That's rather the point."

"Does it go down *dry*?"

"No."

Ivo sighed the sigh of a man renegotiating with the universe and losing. He'd been doing a lot of that since we'd met at the wall three hours past midnight — sighing, checking over his shoulder, touching the knife at his belt as if to confirm it hadn't defected to the queen's spymaster along with everything else about him. He wore no armor. Armor sings in water. Instead he had oiled leather and a face like a closed ledger, and when he'd sworn — again — that the theft wouldn't touch the throne, I'd watched his mouth make the shapes and thought: *there is a man lying with excellent posture.*

I was lying too, of course. My lie was just better dressed. I'd sworn the same oath, and every word of it had been true except for the one thing that mattered: I fully intended, if it came to it, to burn the whole rotten bargain down to its bedrock, queen included. But you don't put that in an oath. Oaths are like charms — they work on what's said, not what's meant.

"Ansel." Sefa touched my arm. "Before we go under. The dead will smell you."

"I've been told I have a memorable scent."

"I'm serious." Her eyes in the lantern light were the color of wet slate. "The courts below know your blood. Wren's bargain marked your family — marked *you*. They'll want to know why you've come smelling of a soul they helped bury."

"And what do I tell them?"

"The truth," she said, and smiled, thin as a coin's edge. "It's the only currency they respect. It's the only currency anyone respects anymore, which is why everyone's so poor."

So we went down.

* * *

Drowned mineshafts are not drowned all the way through. That's the cruelty of them. You wade chest-deep through freezing black water until your teeth chatter loose from their moorings, and then the passage climbs, and you stand dripping in air so cold it feels borrowed, and then you go down again. Down, up, down. The mountain breathing us in and out.

Ivo went first because he insisted, and because he had the sword and the shoulders and the particular stubbornness of a man who has decided that being useful is the only apology he can afford. Sefa went last because she said the dead come from behind. And I went in the middle, which is where I have always been — between the guard and the god-speaker, between the palace and the outlaw, between the life I performed and the one I actually lived. Middle is where tasters live. We are professional middles.

"You're shivering," Ivo said, without turning around.

"Tasters don't shiver. It's in our contract."

"What else is in your contract?"

"A dignified death by poison, should it occur. The crown provides the coffin. Very generous."

He laughed — a short, surprised bark that echoed wrong off the wet stone, too loud, too alive. Then he stopped laughing, because the echo came back and it had company.

We all heard it. A second set of footsteps, faint, arriving a half-beat after ours, like a child playing follow-the-leader with death's own patience.

Sefa raised her lantern. "Don't stop," she murmured. "Stopping is an invitation."

"What happens if we invite?" Ivo asked.

"They accept."

We did not stop. The footsteps walked with us for a hundred paces and then, offended by our manners or satisfied by our fear, faded into the drip and groan of the mountain's plumbing.

It was somewhere in the third hour, in a dry gallery where the ceiling hung low enough that we had to walk bent nearly double, that Ivo started talking. Men like him talk when the silence gets expensive. I recognized it because I do it too — fill the dark with words before the dark fills itself.

"My daughter thinks I'm at a garrison inspection," he said. "Two weeks. I told her two weeks."

"She'll be glad to see you early," I said.

"If there's an early." He ducked under a rib of stone. "You understand what I gave up to open this road. Not my honor — that was gone years ago, sold in installments. My *name*. If this goes wrong, there won't be a cell for me. There'll be a story mothers tell their sons about what happens to traitors."

"There's already a story about me," I offered. "In the kitchens. Ansel Blackwood once ate a plate of poisoned quail and asked for seconds."

"Is it true?"

"The poison was mild. The quail was genuinely excellent."

Another laugh, strangled quickly, but warmer this time. And then, quieter, in the voice men use for confessions in the dark where faces can't be seen: "Why are you really doing this? The sister. Is she worth dying for? You barely knew her before they took her."

I thought about how to answer that honestly, because Sefa had said the truth was currency, and because Ivo deserved better than a charm — a pretty verbal trinket sold to keep him compliant.

"She used to sleep with her shoes on," I said. "Seven years old, convinced the house would catch fire in the night and she'd need to run. So she slept ready. Every night. Ready to run toward whatever was burning." I watched my breath fog in the lantern light. "I've spent seven years being ready too. Just never toward anything. Away from everything. Neutral. Safe. I'm done being safe, Captain. She's worth it because she never got the chance to be anything, and I got seven years of chances and wasted every one."

The water dripped. Somewhere far below, something vast shifted its weight.

"That's the most honest thing anyone's said to me in ten years," Ivo said finally. "And I interrogate people for a living."

"Sefa says honesty is currency."

"Sefa," Ivo said, "is the kind of woman who charges interest."

From behind us, unoffended: "Only those who can pay."

And that — absurdly, in a drowned mine beneath a mountain full of hungry dead — was the moment it stopped being three strangers in a trench and became something else. Something with a shape. I didn't have a word for it then. I do now. The kitchens would have called it family, and the kitchens are rarely wrong about such things.

* * *

The first court found us at the fourth hour.

We knew before we saw them. The water went still — not calm, but *attentive*, the way a room goes quiet when someone important enters. Then the lantern light bent, subtly, toward the left-hand wall, where the stone had opened into a gallery of arched niches, and in the niches sat the dead.

They were not skeletons. Skeletons would have been a mercy. These were impressions — the shape a person leaves in a bed after rising, given hunger and patience. Dozens of them, seated in rows like a congregation, their faces smooth as unfired clay, all of them turned toward me.

Toward me. Not Ivo with his sword, not Sefa with her lantern and her office. Me.

"Blackwood blood," said the nearest one, and its voice was the sound of water finding a crack. "Sold blood. Bound blood. Why does it walk?"

I stepped forward before I could think better of it, which may be the first honest brave thing I ever did. "My name is Ansel Blackwood. Seven years ago my sister Wren was sold to the court beneath the mountain. I've come to take her home."

The clay faces rippled. Something passed through the congregation like wind through wheat — not hostility. Recognition. Grief, maybe, or its fossil.

"The ledgers remember," the nearest one said. "The ledgers always remember. Blackwood. Two names entered, one name paid." Its smooth head tilted. "You carry the unpaid half. Did you know that, little brother? Her bargain bled backward. You've been collateral since the day she signed."

The cold that went through me then had nothing to do with the water.

"I didn't sign anything," I whispered.

"No one signs. That's the beauty of it." The impression spread its clay hands, almost gentle. "But debts run in families like water runs downhill. Go down, little brother. Ask the keeper what your name cost. Ask him what remains to collect."

Sefa's hand closed on my shoulder — iron-firm, grounding. "They're telling the truth," she said quietly. "Gods forgive them for it. Ansel — your immunity. Your poison-tolerance. You always assumed it was natural."

"Everyone assumes it's natural."

"Nothing in Highmoor is natural. Nothing has been natural for a hundred years." She turned me gently toward the descending stair. "Keep walking. Don't thank them. Gratitude is also binding."

We walked. Behind us, softly, in voices like rain on a roof, the court began to sing — or count, or pray; I couldn't tell which, and I'm not sure they could either. Ivo stayed close after that. Closer than protocol, closer than tactics required. Once, in the dark, his hand found my shoulder and squeezed once, briefly, the way you steady a brother.

Neither of us mentioned it afterward. That's how family works, too.

* * *

The second court was worse, because it wasn't hostile at all.

They lined the walls of a vast dry cistern — hundreds of impressions, standing, and as we crossed the gallery they bowed. All together. A wave of bowing, moving ahead of us like parting grass.

"*The marked one,*" they murmured. "*The marked one comes down.*"

"I hate this," Ivo said conversationally. "For the record. I have fought bandits, rebels, and one extremely committed bear, and I hate this most."

"Noted for the chronicle," I said.

"Ansel." Sefa had gone very still, lantern raised. "Look at the marks."

On the cistern walls, scratched into the stone in lines old as the mine, ran thousands of tally-marks. Names, I realized. Names and dates and prices. And there, in a niche worn smoother than all the others, fresher than all the others:

**WREN BLACKWOOD — SOLD — PAID IN FULL**
**ANSEL BLACKWOOD — ENTERED — OUTSTANDING**

My name. Entered seven years ago. Outstanding still.

"They've been waiting for you to come collect yourself," Sefa said softly. "Or be collected."

And that was when we heard it — above us, back up the flooded galleries, a splash that was not an echo. A splash with a heartbeat and a guilty conscience attached.

Ivo's hand went to his knife. Sefa's lantern swung upward. And I stood beneath seven years of tallies with my own name carved over my head, and knew — with the terrible clarity of a man who has tasted poison professionally — exactly who had followed us down.

"Maren," I said.

The splash came again. Closer. And this time, unmistakably, accompanied by the small, furious sound of someone who has made up her mind trying very hard not to be heard.
