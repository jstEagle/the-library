## 8. The Court Beneath the Mountain

I want it on the record — my record, the one I keep in my head and recite to myself when I can't sleep — that I did not *follow* Ansel Blackwood into the mountain. I was already in the mountain. There is a difference, legally speaking, between trespassing and being where you were always going to be.

The difference mattered less when the drowned shaft swallowed the lantern-light of the three of them ahead of me: Ansel, Sefa the wall-witch, and Captain Ivo with his sword drawn like a man who expected the dark to apologize for itself. I'd trailed them from the kitchens at a distance I told myself was professional caution and not heartbreak. Seven years I'd tasted beside that man. Seven years of spitting poison into shared cups and splitting the hazard-pay like brothers. And he hadn't told me. Not about the charms, not about the gods, not about whatever had made him walk out of the palace at midnight with a god-speaker and a turncoat guard, moving like a man going to his own funeral and hoping it would be well attended.

So yes. I followed him. Or I was already there. Pick whichever lets you sleep.

* * *

The mineshafts below Highmoor are not empty. That's the thing nobody tells you, because nobody who knows comes back up to tell it.

We passed the first court an hour down — or they passed it; I hung back in a side gallery with my heart doing something unsanctioned. They were dead, the folk in that chamber, but not gone. Pale as candle-drips, seated in rows on stone shelves like a congregation waiting for a sermon that had been postponed a century. And when Ansel walked through, every head turned. Every one. The motion was smooth, synchronized, wrong in the way a clock is wrong when it ticks backward.

One of them rose. An old woman, her shroud still knotted under her chin, and she reached out and touched Ansel's wrist — just touched it, the way you'd touch a doorframe you remembered — and said, in a voice like water finding a crack:

"*Wren's brother.*"

I nearly gave myself away then. I bit through the inside of my cheek and stood very still among the drip-stones, tasting copper, thinking: *Wren.* Wren Blackwood, who'd died of marsh-fever seven years ago, whose funeral Ansel had missed because the queen's breakfast could not go untasted, who he never spoke of except once, drunk, when he said "she had a laugh like a dropped bell" and then cried into the dishwater and made me swear never to mention it.

They knew his name down here. His blood bought him something in these courts — recognition, deference, a mark. The dead parted for him like courtiers before a prince, and Sefa walked close behind him wearing the expression of someone watching a debt come due.

I followed the parting crowd. What else was I going to do? Go home?

* * *

The vault opened off a gallery where the air went warm, which was somehow worse than the cold. Warm means something is burning. Warm means dragon.

I didn't see the dragon. I heard it — a long, labored breath somewhere below us, like a bellows worked by a tired apprentice, each exhale carrying a faint orange glow up through cracks in the stone. Sefa stopped at the edge of that glow and pressed her palm flat against the rock, and even from my hiding place I saw her shoulders drop, the way yours do at a sickbed.

"Nearly done," she whispered. "Another season. Maybe two."

Ansel said nothing. Ivo sheathed his sword, which for Ivo is a prayer.

And then we came to the vault proper, and I stopped being able to hide behind my own cleverness, because what I saw made hiding feel obscene.

It was a hall of jars. Hundreds of them, thousands, clay and glass and things I had no names for, ranked on stone shelves that climbed into darkness, and each one held a light. Not firelight. A softer thing, a *person*-shaped thing, some dim and guttering, some bright as a struck match. Souls. Shelf upon shelf upon shelf of souls, and in the center of the hall, at a desk built from a single slab of black stone, sat the keeper.

He looked like a clerk. That's the horror of it. Grey robe, grey face, ink-stained fingers, spectacles perched on a nose that had clearly been negotiated rather than grown. He was writing. He looked up at Ansel with the mild impatience of any functionary interrupted at ledger-work.

"You're late," the keeper said. "But punctuality was never the family trait. Sit. You'll want to sit for the arithmetic."

I wedged myself behind a shelf of jars — close enough now to hear everything, close enough that if one of those lights had flickered toward me I'd have been caught — and I listened while the truth of Highmoor came apart in my hands.

* * *

Here is what the queen did. Here is what I learned crouched among stolen souls with my knees cramping and my whole understanding of the world I served bleeding out onto the stone:

Seven years ago, the dragon's flame began to fail early. The old gods stirred. The queen — young, newly crowned, terrified, *pregnant*, the keeper noted with clerical relish — was offered the old solution: feed the prison. Souls slow the guttering. Souls buy seasons. And the realm's law says magic is outlawed, so where does a queen get souls no one will miss?

She got them from people who wouldn't be missed. Paupers. Prisoners. Foreign sailors. And, when the panic grew sharp enough and the price rose high enough, from families desperate enough to sell.

The keeper turned the ledger around and read aloud, and I heard Ansel stop breathing.

"*Blackwood, Wren. Age nineteen. Cause recorded: marsh-fever. Vendor: mother, Ilse Blackwood. Price: remission of debts, one cottage, and continued good health to the surviving son.*"

"Continued good health," the keeper repeated, tapping the page. "Do you understand what your mother purchased, taster? Not coin. *Immunity.* Your body's tolerance. The poisons you swallow daily for your queen — arsenic, belladonna, the widow's-brew they tested you with in your second year, the one that should have killed you twice over. Your sister's soul paid for every cup you've survived. She has been dying in a jar beneath the mountain for seven years so that you could keep living above it."

I watched Ansel's hands. I've spent seven years watching that man's hands — steady over the queen's porridge, steady over the vials, steady always, because a taster whose hands shake dies of it. Now they shook. He gripped the edge of the black desk hard enough that I heard the tendons creak, and he said, very quietly, in the voice he used for reporting flavors:

"How much for her back?"

The keeper smiled. It was the worst smile I have ever seen, because it was kind.

"Now we arrive at the arithmetic," he said. "Souls are not coin, taster. They are weight. The vault must balance. If I release Wren Blackwood's soul, something of equal substance must remain." He folded his ink-stained hands. "You've been wondering why the dead know your name. Why the courts bow. Her bargain marked you — half a transaction, a soul sold with a lien attached. The lien is you. Your years of immunity. The tolerance your mother bought. Give it back, and the scales balance, and your sister walks free."

"And without it?" Ansel asked.

"You die at court within the month," the keeper said pleasantly. "The queen's food is a battlefield, and you have been armored all your life by a girl who loved you enough to be sold. Take the armor off her grave and hand it back, or leave her in the jar and keep swallowing. Those are the terms. I don't make them. I only keep the books."

Silence. Down below us, the dragon exhaled, and the orange glow through the floor dimmed a little more, and somewhere far above I imagined the queen sleeping soundly in a bed paid for with other people's children.

I should have stepped out then. I want to say I stepped out then, out of loyalty, out of righteousness. But honestly — and this ledger is mine, and I keep it honest — I stayed hidden because I was afraid, and because some small vicious part of me wanted to hear what he'd choose. Wanted to know if seven years of friendship weighed anything against seven years of survival.

Ansel laughed. One short, cracked note, nothing funny in it.

"She'd hate this," he said. "She'd absolutely hate this. She'd come back and haunt me specifically." He looked at the jar the keeper indicated — third shelf, fourth row, a light the color of a struck match, flickering like laughter — and his voice went steady again, the taster's voice, the voice that has lied politely to queens. "Terms accepted."

"No," said Ivo, stepping forward. "Blackwood—"

"I didn't swear to protect *me*," Ansel said. "Swear your oaths elsewhere, Captain. This one's mine."

And that's when I made my choice. Not the noble version — the real one. My legs were numb, my cover was about to be worthless anyway, and there was a man I loved about to sign himself into a grave, alone, because he thought he deserved to be alone.

I stepped out from behind the shelf of souls.

"Maren?" Ansel's face did three things at once, none of them flattering.

"The widow's-brew," I said, walking forward, keeping my voice level the way we're trained to, tasters' voices, voices that taste of nothing. "In my second year. It didn't almost kill me either, Ansel. I carried you to the infirmary. I held the bucket. Whatever immunity you think you're returning — it was never only yours. We split the hazard, we split the poison, we split everything. So you don't get to do this arithmetic alone."

The keeper's spectacles glinted. "Ah," he said, with the delight of a clerk finding an unrecorded asset. "A co-signatory. How *interesting*. The books do love a complication—"

Down below, the dragon's breath stuttered. The orange glow through the stone flickered — once, twice — and went thin as paper, and every soul-jar in the vault flared bright at once, hundreds of lights straining toward the surface like faces at a window, and the keeper rose from his desk for the first time in what I suspect was a very long time.

"Well," he said. "It seems the arithmetic has been moved up. Gentlemen, lady — the flame is going out tonight after all. Decide quickly what you're willing to pay, and pray the gods are still taking payment in honesty."

Behind us, far up the drowned shaft, boots splashed. More than two pairs. Coming down fast.

I looked at Ansel. Ansel looked at the jar with the match-flame soul inside it, and it flickered — and I swear on my own ledger, it flickered *toward* him, the way a person turns toward a familiar footstep.

"Wren?" he whispered.

The light leaned closer.
