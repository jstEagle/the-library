## 9. The Last Flame

The dragon's fire had been dying for a hundred years, but it chose that morning — the morning I stood in the vault with my sister's soul in a jar of black glass — to make its death a public affair.

You could hear it through the mountain. A sound like a bell rung underwater, then a silence worse than the sound. Sefa pressed her palm flat against the vault wall and said, "It's guttering. Ansel. It's *guttering*."

I knew what that meant. We all did, by then. When the last flame went out, the seal went with it, and the old gods — the ones who'd spent a century pressing their mouths against the underside of the world like children locked in a cellar — would come up into flesh. Into our flesh. The queen had known it too. That was the whole rotten arithmetic of her reign: feed the gods souls to keep them drowsy, keep the dragon alive just long enough to die on someone else's watch.

Seven years of stolen prayers. Seven years of people like Wren.

The vault keeper stood between me and the shelf where the jars slept in their rows, and it smiled with a mouth that had clearly learned smiling from watching mortals do it wrong. "The trade stands," it said. "Her soul for your years. Your tongue has swallowed every poison this court could brew, taster. The gods find such a body *nourishing*."

"I bet they do," I said. My voice came out steadier than I felt, which is the one trick seven years of tasting had taught me. You learn to swallow arsenic with a pleasant expression. You can learn to say goodbye to your own survival the same way.

Maren was white as flour beside me. "Ansel, don't—" she started, and Ivo put a hand on her shoulder, and neither of them said anything else, because what was there? I'd already drunk the keeper's draught an hour ago. I'd felt my immunity leave me like warmth leaving a bath — slowly, then all at once, a cold settling into the marrow that no fire would ever fix again. Somewhere up in that palace, there were dishes I could no longer touch. There was a whole life I could no longer survive.

Wren's jar was warm in my hands. It shouldn't have been. Glass doesn't hold heat like that unless something inside it is *alive*, and I suppose I'd known that too, in the place where you know things you refuse to look at directly.

"Hello, Wren," I said, because I am apparently the sort of fool who talks to glass when his sister might be listening. "Sorry about the delay. Traffic beneath the mountain is murder."

Sefa made a sound that was half laugh, half prayer. Maybe for a god-speaker those are the same thing.

* * *

We came up out of the mountain into the great hall of the palace, because Ivo's road let us out exactly where cowards don't want to be: in the open, before the court, at the hour of morning audience. The queen sat beneath the banner of her house with her spymaster at her shoulder, and the whole glittering room turned to look at three filthy people climbing out of a hole in the floor carrying contraband divinity.

There is a moment in every poisoner's career — every honest one, anyway — when you stop measuring doses and simply pour. I had spent seven years measuring. Seven years of half-truths delivered in careful sips: a charm here, a lie there, never enough of anything to kill me. Standing in that hall with my dead-then-living sister's soul against my chest, I poured.

"Your Majesty," I said, loud enough for the back benches, "seven years ago you brokered the sale of souls to the courts beneath the mountain. Mine among them. My sister Wren Blackwood, sold by crown brokers to keep the old gods sleeping and your dynasty breathing. The ledger is in the vault below. The keeper will confirm it. The gods themselves will confirm it, if we stand here much longer, because your seal is dying and you've known since the day you signed."

The hall did the thing halls do — that rippling hush, two hundred people deciding at once whether to be horrified or delighted. The queen rose. She looked older than her banners. "Taster," she said, and her voice was quiet and terrible, "you swore oaths to this house."

"I swore oaths to your *food*," I said. "Your food never lied to anyone."

Behind her, the spymaster moved, and Ivo moved faster — a turncoat only in the direction of his own conscience, it turned out, or possibly just a man who'd decided which daughter he wanted to come home to. Steel rang somewhere. I didn't watch. I was looking at the queen, and she was looking at me, and between us lay seven years of breakfasts I had tasted so that she could sleep at night, purchased with nights my sister didn't sleep at all.

"You bought your survival," I told her, "with other people's souls. So here is mine, freely given, honestly named. Let's see if the gods can tell the difference."

Because that was the last piece of it, the piece Sefa had whispered to me in the dark of the mineshaft: the gods had woken hungry, yes, but they had woken *listening*. A century of frightened prayers, and now, finally, someone telling them the truth about where their food came from. They were pressing against the failing seal not to punish us. They were pressing to hear who would own it.

The dragon-fire died while I was still speaking.

I felt it go — everyone felt it — a vast exhale through the bones of the mountain, and then the air above the court *thinned*, the way water thins over a rising fish, and something enormous turned its attention toward us from underneath everything.

I broke the jar.

Not dramatically. I'm a taster; I do things with my hands. I unstoppered the black glass and tipped it, and Wren's soul came out like smoke that remembered being a girl — it circled me once, warm, familiar, smelling of the plum orchards behind our childhood house — and then it went *home*, wherever home was, into whatever body or whatever country receives the dead when the dead get their property back.

And the gods took me instead.

Not violently. That's the part no one believes afterward. They came up through the floor of the world like light through water and they gathered around the space where my immunity used to be — that cold hollow the keeper's draught had left — and they filled it. Honesty, it turns out, is a flavor they'd nearly forgotten. First honest servant in a century, Sefa had called me, and the gods apparently agreed, because they claimed me the way you claim a thing you've been promised: thoroughly, gently, and forever.

The last thing I saw from inside my own life was Maren's face, and Ivo's, and Sefa on her knees with her arms raised, laughing and weeping and doing her job at last. The last thing I heard was my own heart, slowing.

Then Highmoor tilted, and the marble came up kind, and I thought — with the specific satisfaction of a man who has finally finished a very long shift — *someone else can taste the queen's breakfast tomorrow.*

* * *

Dying, for the record, is mostly paperwork.

There was a threshold. There was a queue, which offended me deeply even in that state. And there were the gods, plural, numerous, arranged around me like a council that had been waiting a hundred years for a meeting it could actually enjoy.

"You gave us truth," said the largest of them, whose voice was the sound a mountain makes deciding to be a valley. "Truth is rare. Truth, freely priced, is rarer. What would you ask of us, Ansel Blackwood?"

And here is the thing I should have anticipated, being a man whose entire profession was knowing what things cost: I hadn't negotiated. I hadn't asked for my immunity back, or my life, or vengeance on a queen. I opened my mouth to ask for nothing — genuinely nothing, the first time in seven years I'd wanted nothing for myself —

— and the gods laughed, a sound like harvest and landslide together, and the largest one said, "*That* is why we claim you. Go back, then. Mortal. Powerless. Immune no more. But ours — marked, heard, answered. Every prayer you speak, we will treat as spoken by one of our own."

Which sounds like a gift. It is also a leash. I've had time, since, to appreciate both.

I woke on the marble of the great hall with Maren's hands on my chest and Ivo shouting for a physician and Sefa saying, quietly, over all of it, "Let him breathe. He's earned the right to be ordinary."

Ordinary. Immune no more. A man who could now be killed by a badly cooked mushroom or a grudge in the kitchens, in the very palace full of people whose queen I had just publicly ruined. The guards were already moving through the hall with warrants. The queen was gone from her throne — fled, or taken, no one would tell me — and her spymaster was nowhere, which frightened me more than her absence.

Maren saw my eyes open and burst into tears and immediately pretended she hadn't. "You absolute idiot," she said, thickly. "You're going to need a new job. You can't taste anymore. One wrong plate and you're dead."

"I know," I said.

"And the city's going to tear itself apart. Magic's loose, the gods are awake, half the court wants your head and the other half wants to worship you."

"I know," I said.

She helped me sit up. Through the great doors, far off across the city, I could hear bells — not alarm bells, older ones, ones I'd never heard rung in my lifetime — and under them, faintly, a sound I knew better than my own heartbeat.

Someone calling my name. Not Maren. Not Ivo.

A woman's voice, hoarse from seven years of disuse, coming up the palace steps.

"Wren?" I said.
