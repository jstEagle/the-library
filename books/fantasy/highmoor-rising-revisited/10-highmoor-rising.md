## 10. Highmoor Rising

The first thing I learned about waking up after seven years is that nobody warns you about the mouth. Seven years of not having one, and the moment mine came back it was full of somebody else's spit, because a woman in a healer's apron was pouring willow-bark tea between my lips like she was watering a stubborn houseplant.

"Swallow," she commanded.

I swallowed. Then I said — in a voice like a hinge that had been left out all winter — "Where's my brother?"

Because here is what seven years of being a soul in a jar teaches you: you don't dream, exactly, but you hear things. Muffled, through glass, through gold, through whatever they keep souls in beneath a mountain. And the last thing I heard before the dark went warm and moving was Ansel Blackwood's voice saying my name like he was paying off a debt, and then a sound I can only describe as a door opening somewhere very large.

"He's alive," the healer said, in the tone people use when they mean *technically*.

"That's a lawyer's answer."

"You've been dead-adjacent for seven years. You'll forgive me for precision." She tucked the blanket under my arms with brisk tenderness. "Your brother gave something away to get you back. The gods took him to pay for it. And then—" she paused, choosing her words the way you choose footing on ice, "—then they gave him back too. Mostly."

*Mostly.* There's a word that follows our family around like a dog.

I sat up. My body fit like a coat borrowed from a taller sister — everything in the right place, nothing where I remembered it. My hands were a grown woman's hands. The last hands I'd owned were seventeen and ink-stained from copying out prayer-cycles for a god-speaker who'd promised me the work would protect our family. Sefa, her name was. I heard she lived in a wall now. I intended to have words.

"Take me to him," I said.

"You can't walk—"

"I spent seven years unable to walk. I have been *looking forward* to this argument."

She looked at me for a long moment, and then she laughed — the startled laugh of someone who has just realized the patient is going to be a problem — and went to find a wheelchair.

* * *

They wheeled me up through the palace kitchens, which is apparently where my brother lives now, or lived, or some tense I don't have grammar for yet. The city above had changed so much it made my teeth ache. Magic was legal again — there were charm-stalls on the corners with *licensed* painted on their awnings, licensed, as if the whole trouble hadn't started with licensing — and the old shrines at the crossroads had fresh bread on them instead of the bare stone I remembered. People left offerings without looking over their shoulders. A child was chalk-drawing a spiral sigil on the cobbles while her mother watched, unbothered, haggling over eels.

Seven years ago, drawing that spiral would have gotten us both branded. I pressed my palm flat against the wheelchair's arm until the wood creaked.

In the kitchens, a broad-faced man with flour to his elbows looked up from a mountain of dough and went white.

"You're her," he said. "You're Wren. He talks about you in his sleep."

"I'm told I'm mostly back."

"Maren," he said, wiping his hands uselessly on an already-ruined apron. "I'm Maren. I taste for the queen. Well. For whoever ends up queen, given recent events." He hesitated, then added, quieter: "He chose truth. You should know that. Everyone downstairs wanted him to keep quiet — the trade, the vault, all of it. He could have kept his post, his rooms, everything. He stood up in front of the whole court and named her anyway, with poison still in his blood and no immunity left to save him."

"I know," I said. "I heard the door open."

Maren blinked. "Right. Soul things. I'm going to need a very long time to get used to soul things." He jerked his chin toward a door at the kitchen's far end. "He's through there. Fair warning — he looks worse than he is. Or possibly better than he is. The healers argue about it daily."

The room beyond was small and sunlit and full of flowers, which struck me as funny, because the old gods are not flower people. They're root people, bone people, river-bottom people. But someone had filled every jar and cup with cut blooms, and in the middle of the bed, propped against pillows, lay my brother — thinner than I remembered, gray around the mouth, but awake, and watching the door like he'd been watching it for days.

"Wren," he said.

"Ansel." I got myself out of the chair on legs that shook and hated me and crossed the floor anyway. "You look terrible."

"You look seventeen."

"I look twenty-four, and you owe me seven birthdays." I sat down on the edge of the bed, careful of his ribs, and put my forehead against his, the way we did when we were small and hiding from the tithe-collectors. His skin was cold. Underneath the cold, faintly, like a coal banked in ash, something else hummed. "What did they do to you?"

"They offered me a job," he said wryly. "I turned it down."

* * *

Here is what actually happened, pieced together afterward from Ansel, from Sefa, from a guard-captain named Ivo who drinks like a man paying penance and tells the truth like one too:

When the dragon-fire guttered out and the prison thinned to gauze, the old gods came through — not as monsters, whatever the panic-pamphlets say, but as pressure, as presence, as the feeling of standing in a doorway with something vast on the other side deciding whether you're friend or meal. And Ansel, dying of honest poison with his sister's soul already flying home to her body, stood in that doorway and told them the truth. All of it. The charms, the fear, the sister sold by a queen who priced her dynasty in other people's children.

And the gods — who have not been lied to by a mortal in a hundred years, and had forgotten what it sounded like — found it *delicious.*

So they claimed him. First honest servant in a century. Which meant taking him apart down to the rivets and offering to rebuild him as something new: a god-speaker with actual gods on the line, a voice with real weight behind it, power enough to reshape the city. All he had to do was say yes.

He said, "No, thank you. I'd like to go back to my kitchen."

Sefa swears the silence afterward lasted a full minute, and that somewhere in it, something ancient laughed — not cruelly, but the way family laughs when a child says something outrageous and true.

They gave him back. Mortal. Powerless. Immune to nothing now — every poison at court could kill him tomorrow, and everyone at court knows it, which is its own kind of exile. But they left the door unlatched. That's the part that frightens the queen's people and thrills everyone else: the gods didn't close the account. They just moved it to long-term.

When I asked Ansel why he refused them — power, purpose, a seat at the table of actual divinity — he picked at his blanket and wouldn't look at me.

"Because I spent seven years earning belonging," he said finally. "Tasting, smiling, keeping my head down, selling little charms to people who needed them more than I needed safety. And it never once felt like mine. It always felt like rent." He glanced up. "Then a laundress hid me from the watch, and a guard opened a road he shouldn't have, and a friend followed me into the dark to drag me home instead of reporting me, and none of them had to. None of it was owed. That's when I understood the difference. Belonging you earn can be taken back. Belonging that's chosen—" he shrugged, embarrassed, "—you have to give that to each other. Nobody can revoke it."

"You learned that dying?" I said.

"I learned it in the kitchens," he said. "Dying just made it official."

* * *

We buried the old life on a grey morning two weeks later, which is to say we held a party, because Highmoor has never met an occasion it couldn't turn into a street festival once magic came back and gave everyone an excuse.

Maren cooked enough for three times our number, because grief and joy both make him bake. Ivo came off the wall at sundown with his daughter on his shoulders — a solemn girl of six who took one look at Ansel and appointed herself his personal physician, issuing orders about broth and blankets that he pretended to resent and visibly treasured. Sefa climbed out of the wall itself, blinking in daylight like a cat evicted from its favorite chair, and stood in the square outside the kitchen door and said, gruffly, "You were the best student I ever ruined," and Ansel hugged her before she could escape.

I watched all of it from a bench with a cup of mulled wine I was, for the first time in seven years, actually tasting. Somewhere across the square, a licensed charm-seller was doing brisk business; somewhere above, the mountain kept its sealed door and its secrets and its queen, who had not abdicated, not confessed, not fallen — merely gone quiet, the way dangerous things go quiet. The pamphlets called it a new age. The priests of the revived shrines called it a reconciliation. Sefa called it "the calm part before the interesting part," and she'd know.

Ansel lowered himself onto the bench beside me, moving like a man twice his age, and stole my wine.

"How does it feel," I asked, "being mortal and powerless and famous?"

"Like being a taster again," he said. "Everything's a risk, nothing's certain, and somehow I keep getting fed anyway." He nodded across the square, toward the kitchen door where Maren was arguing with Ivo about whether a guard captain could be trusted alone near the honey cakes. "Look at them. None of them had any reason to stay, after. Ivo's cleared of the blackmail — well, mostly. Maren could have washed his hands of the whole scandal. Sefa's got her name back and a dozen temples begging her to lead them. And they're all still here, fighting over dessert."

"Maybe they like you."

"Don't be disgusting." But he was smiling, that tired crooked smile I'd missed through seven years of glass and gold.

It was Ivo's daughter who noticed it first — children notice first, they haven't been trained out of it. She stopped mid-tantrum over the honey cakes, pointed one small finger past the rooftops toward the mountain, and said, clearly, in the ringing voice children reserve for awkward truths:

"The smoke's coming out of the seal-door again."

Every head in the square turned. Above the palace, above the terraces, above the sealed door the masons had triple-barred after the court named the queen's crime — a thin thread of smoke rose straight up into the windless sky. Not dragon-smoke. Dragon-smoke smells of sulfur and hot iron. This smelled, even from across the square, even to a nose as untrained as mine, of bread baking. Of offerings left out and accepted. Of a door standing politely, patiently, insistently *unlatched.*

Ansel set down the wine. He looked at the smoke for a long moment, and then at me, and then at the strange, chosen, impossible family bickering over dessert behind him.

"Well," he said, standing, and holding out his hand. "They did say the account was still open."
