## 1. The Ledger of Small Debts

The candle had burned down to its last knuckle by the time Petra Dunmore found the error.

She sat in the counting room beneath the Gilded Ledger, where the air smelled of tallow and old vellum and the faint copper tang that clung to everything traded in impossible things. Around her rose the shelves: hundreds of ledgers bound in leather the color of dried blood, each one holding debts no ordinary moneylender would recognize. A mother's grief for a drowned son, sold twice. A king's last honest thought. A soldier's courage, pawned on the eve of battle and never redeemed.

Petra balanced them all.

Her pen moved across the columns with the steady rhythm of a woman who had learned, long ago, that precision was the safest kind of armor. *One memory of a first snowfall, withdrawn against collateral of three years' happiness — balance outstanding.* *One bottled regret, decanted at client's request, vessel returned empty — fee collected in full.* The guild's founder had built her fortune on the principle that everything has a price, and Petra had built her life on the principle that she would never again be the thing being priced.

Outside, Highmoor's rain came down in long grey sheets, drumming on the slate roof like impatient fingers. Somewhere above, the free city went about its evening — fishwives arguing over the day's catch, harbor bells tolling the fog in, usurper's criers reading proclamations at the Salt Gate that no one stayed to hear. None of it touched the counting room. That was the point of the counting room.

Petra turned the page and paused.

There was a mark on her wrist where her sleeve had slipped — a small grey sigil, like a coin pressed into ash. She tugged the cuff down over it without thinking. Twenty years of practice. In her mercenary years, men had died for less than glimpsing that mark; in her ledger years, she had simply made sure no one looked. Sable knew, of course. Sable knew everything and said nothing, which was why Sable was handler and Petra was merely excellent.

*Veyl,* she thought, the way one touches an old scar with the tongue. The silent god. The god who did not answer. She had sworn her oath to him on a night of blood and rain, kneeling in a ditch outside Varnholt with her company dead around her, and he had accepted — and then he had never spoken again. Not once in twenty years. The priests called it the Long Silence. The faithful called it a test. Petra, who had stopped being faithful somewhere between the ditch and the guild's door, called it what it was: abandonment dressed up as mystery.

And still she wore his mark. Still, some stubborn animal part of her kept the oath, the way a dog keeps faith with a master who has moved away.

She bent back to the column. The error was small — a trade recorded twice, a memory of a childhood summer entered under two different clients. Easy enough to correct. But as she struck through the duplicate line, her pen hesitated, because the handwriting in the second entry was not the broker's.

It was hers.

Not today's hand — not the careful, compressed script she used for balancing. This was older, looser, the letters leaning right as if hurrying somewhere. She knew that handwriting intimately. She saw it every time she signed a receipt, every time she initialed a correction. It was her own hand from years ago, before she had trained herself into tidiness.

But she had never written this entry. She would have remembered. She remembered everything about the ledgers; that was the whole architecture of her invisibility. You could burn her face from every portrait in the city and she would survive on the strength of her bookkeeping alone.

Petra set down the pen. Pressed her palms flat against the desk until the wood's grain printed itself into her skin. Rain on the roof. Candle guttering. Her own heartbeat, slow and deliberate, the heartbeat of a woman refusing to be afraid.

*Someone copied my hand,* she told herself. *Brokers forge signatures the way forgers mint coins.*

Except the ink was old. Older than her employment. The vellum around it had yellowed evenly, without shadow, which meant the entry had been written when the page was fresh — decades ago, perhaps more. She checked the folio's spine: *Ledger of Small Debts, Vol. IX*. Volume nine. The guild was on volume thirty-one.

She read the entry properly for the first time.

*A memory of tomorrow, sold by an unnamed client, purchased by the house. Content withheld per founder's seal. To be opened on the date inscribed within.*

And beneath it, in her own younger handwriting, a date.

Tonight's date.

Petra sat very still. The rain filled the silence the way water fills a boot — gradually, then all at once. She was aware of the candle, of the shelves pressing close with their centuries of other people's losses, of the sigil on her wrist gone cold as river stone.

A memory of tomorrow. Sold to the house. Sealed until tonight.

She should have fetched Sable. That was procedure. Unusual entries went to the handler; the handler decided. Petra had followed procedure for eleven years with the devotion of a convert, because procedure was the wall she had built between herself and the person she used to be — the woman with the sword and the dead company and the god who wouldn't speak.

Instead, she reached for the entry again and touched the date with one fingertip, the way you touch a wound to confirm it hurts.

Tonight. The date was tonight.

* * *

Sable found her there an hour later, or perhaps Sable had been standing in the doorway the whole time; with Sable it was impossible to say. The handler was a narrow woman of indeterminate age, dressed in dove-grey, with eyes like the flat of a blade. She carried a lantern she did not need — the counting room's candles were lit — but she carried it anyway, because Sable believed light was something you brought with you rather than waited for.

"You've found it," Sable said. Not a question.

"The entry." Petra did not look up. "It's in my handwriting."

"It is."

"Volume nine is older than me."

"It is."

"Sable." Petra finally raised her head. "I have never seen this ledger before tonight. I have never written in it. So either someone forged my hand forty years before I held a pen, or—" She stopped, because the alternative was a door she did not want to open.

"Or," Sable said, setting the lantern on the desk with a soft click, "the founder wrote it, and the founder wrote it in your hand, because the founder wrote many things in many hands, and most of them were not hers." She lowered herself into the chair opposite, folding her hands. "You balance our accounts beautifully, Petra. Do you know why I hired you? Not your arithmetic. Half the orphans in this city can add. I hired you because you are a woman who understands that every debt comes due, and that the wise debtor chooses the hour."

"I don't understand what you're telling me."

"No," Sable agreed. "But you will. The question is whether you go looking for the understanding, or wait for it to arrive. Understanding that arrives uninvited tends to break the door down."

Petra looked at the open ledger between them. The date glowed on the page like an ember that refused to die. "What happens at midnight?"

"That depends entirely on whether anyone opens the sealed entry." Sable's voice was mild, almost kind. "The house purchased a memory of tomorrow, Petra. Tomorrow, in this case, being tonight. Someone sold us a glimpse of what hasn't happened yet, and the founder — may she rest in whatever peace she bought — decreed it stay shut until the day it described. That day is now. Which means, technically, the house owes itself a delivery."

"And if we don't deliver?"

Sable smiled, and it was the smile that had unnerved Petra for eleven years — warm as a hearth and exactly as safe. "Then the debt remains outstanding. And you, better than anyone under this roof, know what the house does about outstanding debts."

The candle guttered. Outside, the rain thickened, and beneath it — so faint Petra might have imagined it — the harbor bell tolled nine.

She looked down at her wrist, at the cuff covering the silent god's mark, and felt the first hairline crack run through the careful invisible life she had built, brick by patient brick, over twenty years.

"Where is the memory kept?" she asked.

Sable rose, took up her lantern, and paused at the door. "In the vault," she said. "Where the founder kept everything she didn't want found until it was too late."
