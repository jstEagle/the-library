## 2. A Century of Tomorrows

The vault beneath the Gilded Ledger had no door, only a threshold that tasted you.

Petra felt it now as she always did—a cold pass over her tongue, a question asked in a language older than the guild—and she answered it the way she answered everything: by being exactly what she appeared to be. A clerk. A keeper of accounts. A woman whose hands smelled of ink and lamp oil and nothing else. The threshold let her through with the small sigh it gave to all its own.

Beyond lay the memory of wealth. Not gold—the guild traded in rarer stock—but the architecture of it: shelves of black iron rising into dark, each drawer labeled in the founder's angular hand, each one holding something someone had once owned and could no longer bear to. Bottled griefs ranked like soldiers. Second chances slept in lead coffers, ticking faintly, like hearts told to wait their turn. Somewhere in the deep stacks a stolen summer was kept at a precise temperature, and somewhere else, a name that had been sold three times and was beginning to fray.

Petra walked the aisles with her ledger under her arm and her lantern held high, and she did not look at the drawer she always did not look at.

It was the third night since the client with the gray eyes and the trembling hands. The third night since she'd balanced an entry for *one tomorrow, remembered in advance*, and found the date written in her own hand—her exact slant, her exact pressure, down to the little hitch in the numeral where her pen always caught on the seventh stroke. She had checked the ledger against every sample of her script in the office. She had checked it against the handwriting of every scribe the guild employed, living or dead. There were forty-one of them in the reference folio. None matched.

Hers matched perfectly.

Which meant either someone had learned to forge her so well that even she could not tell the difference—which was its own kind of horror—or the entry was hers, written by her hand, in a year not yet arrived.

Tonight she meant to settle which.

* * *

The prophecy folios lived in the oldest section of the vault, past the griefs and the grudges, past the shelf where the guild kept wars that had been purchased before they happened and never used. The air changed there. Petra had noticed it on her first week, eleven years ago, when Sable had walked her through on induction: a stillness, a hush, as though the vault itself lowered its voice out of respect or fear. She'd thought then it was the cold. She knew better now. Cold didn't make your oath-mark ache.

She touched the brand beneath her collar without meaning to. The mark of Veyl—the closed eye, the unspoken word—had been pressed into her skin the night she swore her mercenary's oath, back when she was a different woman with a different name and a sword instead of a pen. It had never hurt before this week. Now it throbbed with a low, patient heat, like a coal remembering fire.

*You're tired,* she told herself. *You've been reading entries that shouldn't exist until your eyes bleed. Of course your skin is complaining.*

The folio cabinet stood at the heart of the old section: a single drawer of pale wood in a wall of iron, unlabeled, because everyone who needed to know what it held already knew, and everyone else was never meant to open it. Access required a founder's key. Petra had none.

But she had spent eleven years learning the vault's habits, and the vault, like any old creature, had habits. The lock was blood-bound—not to the founder's line, that line having ended, but to the *office* of First Keeper, and Petra had signed herself into that office every morning for a decade with the same ring of guild-ink on her thumb. The lock did not care about titles. It cared about the ledger's continuity. And Petra *was* the ledger's continuity, insofar as anything mortal could be.

She pressed her inked thumb to the pale wood.

For a long moment, nothing. Then the wood warmed, sighed, and split along a seam she'd never seen, and the smell that came out was not dust but something older—rain on hot stone, myrrh, the inside of a sealed room where someone had once made a terrible decision and then shut the door on it.

Inside lay a book.

It was not large. Bound in gray leather gone soft as cloth, its spine cracked in four places from a century of consultations, it bore no title on its cover—only a small stamped device that stopped Petra's breath: a closed eye.

Veyl's sigil. The god's silence, stamped in blind-tooling on a book hidden in the deepest shelf of a guild that officially kept no religion at all.

Her oath-mark flared white-hot. She bit down on the sound and carried the book to the reading table in the center of the aisle, where the light was best, and she stood over it for a full minute before she dared touch it again.

*Don't,* said the careful part of her, the part that had kept her alive through nine years of contracts and two years of ledgers. *Close the drawer. Walk away. Tell Sable the lock failed and let the guild decide.*

But the guild deciding was exactly what frightened her. Because if the entry in last night's ledger was real—if a client had truly sold a memory of tomorrow, and tomorrow had a date in Petra's handwriting—then the guild's most sacred rule had already been broken, quietly, by someone who knew how. And the only person she trusted less than a rule-breaker was a rule-breaker with access to her hands.

She opened the book.

* * *

The first pages were mundane, almost disappointingly so: inventories. The founder's own hand, tight and furious, cataloguing the guild's first trades. *One widow's grief, decanted. One soldier's courage, returned at cost. One king's mercy, sold twice.* Petra read them the way she read all ledgers, half-conscious, her mind sorting figures—and then the dates began to run forward.

Past the founder's death, according to every history Petra had ever been taught. Past the guild's second century. Past last winter. Past *last week*.

The entries continued. Same hand. Same furious, beautiful slant. Dates marching on into years that had not happened yet, each one annotated in that same ink, in that same script, describing trades that had not yet been made:

*Tonight: one tomorrow, remembered in advance. Buyer pays in sleep. Seller unknown to self.*

Petra's lantern flame bent, though there was no draft.

That was last night's entry. Word for word. Her own annotation style—she abbreviated *unknown to self* the same way, with the same cramped asterisk.

She turned the page with fingers that had gone distant and strange, as though they belonged to the clerk and not to her.

The next date was tonight.

And beside tonight's date, in the margin, in the founder's century-old hand, was a note. Not an inventory. Something more personal, more urgent, the letters pressed deep enough to emboss the page behind:

*The vessel comes to the vault tonight. Let her find what she will find. The seal holds better when the keeper knows the shape of her cage.*

Below it, a name.

Not a description. Not a title. A name, written plainly, the way one writes the name of someone already known:

**Petra Dunmore.**

The lantern went out.

Not blown—there was no wind—it simply died, and in the sudden dark the vault breathed around her, ten thousand bottled sorrows exhaling in the black, and Petra stood very still with her hand flat on the page over her own name and listened to her heart try to climb out through her ribs.

A century ago, a dead woman had written her name beside tonight's date, and had known—*known*—that she would stand here, in this aisle, at this hour, holding this light.

*The vessel comes to the vault tonight.*

*Vessel.*

The word slid into her alongside the ache in her oath-mark and clicked into place with a sound she felt rather than heard, and suddenly eleven years of small wrongnesses reorganized themselves: why the silent god's priests had sought her out in a border town; why her mercenary company's chaplain had wept when he blessed her and refused to say why; why the guild had hired her—an ex-soldier with a forged history and a brand of a god nobody worshipped—to keep its books, when a hundred honest clerks would have done. Why the threshold tasted her every night like it was checking.

Checking on what? Checking that she was still—

Still what. Still sealed? Still carrying?

Still *full*?

"No," Petra said aloud, and the vault took the word and gave it back to her smaller than she'd said it. "No. I'm a clerk. I balance accounts. That's all I am."

The dark did not answer, which was somehow worse than an answer.

She relit the lantern with hands that shook only a little, which she counted as a victory, and she looked down at the page again. Below her name, the founder had written one more line, smaller, almost gentle:

*If you are reading this, you were never invisible. Forgive me. I needed you to survive long enough to choose.*

*Choose what?*

The page after tonight's entry was blank. Whatever choice the founder had foreseen, she had not written its outcome. Either she hadn't known—or she had known and left it deliberately empty, the way one leaves a signature line, waiting.

Petra closed the book. The sound it made was soft and final, a coin dropping into a slot.

* * *

"You're not supposed to be able to open that."

Sable's voice came out of the aisle behind her, level as a plumb line. Petra turned slowly. Her handler stood in the gloom with his arms crossed, silver-haired, unhurried, wearing the expression he wore when a trade had gone sideways and someone was about to lose money. He'd been her handler for eleven years. In all that time she had never once seen him surprised.

He wasn't surprised now. That was the worst thing in the room.

"How long have you known?" Petra asked.

"Known what, specifically? There's a great deal on file." He came closer, boots silent on the stone, and glanced at the book in her hands without touching it. "I know you pulled a founder's folio with a thumb-print, which should be impossible. I know you've been sleeping four hours a night since the Gray-Eyed Man's trade. I know your mark has been hurting, because you've been touching your collar eleven times a day instead of your usual six." He looked up. His eyes were kind, which from Sable was more frightening than cruelty. "I count, Petra. It's my job."

"Then count this." She held the book up, open to the page, to her name. "A hundred years ago. Her handwriting. My name. Tonight's date. Explain it."

Sable read it the way he read everything—thoroughly, without hurry—and when he finished, he did something she had never seen him do. He closed his eyes.

"Burn it," he said quietly.

"What?"

"The folio. Burn it, log the loss as water damage, and never—" he opened his eyes, and for the first time in eleven years, Petra saw fear in Sable's face, real fear, worn like a borrowed coat that didn't fit, "—never come down here alone again. There are things in the deep stacks that are only dangerous when they're *read*. That book has been waiting a century for someone with your thumb. Do not give it another night."

"It already had its night. It had mine. My name is *in it*, Sable." Her voice cracked upward and she hated it. "She wrote me before I was born. She wrote *tonight*. If burning it were safe, if it changed anything, don't you think—"

"If you burn it, you might change everything," Sable snapped, and then caught himself, lowering his voice to that grave-level murmur brokers used near the grief-shelves. "Prophecy isn't a record, girl. It's a *debt*. Every word in that book is a lien against someone's future, and the interest compounds. You read it, you owe it. You answer it, you're collateral." He stepped close enough that she could see the sweat at his temple despite the cold. "I have watched this guild destroy people for lesser trades than the one you're holding. Burn the book. Forget the name. Come upstairs and balance the evening's accounts, and in twenty years you'll be an old woman with clean hands and no idea how lucky you were."

"And if I'm the vessel?"

The words left her before she could stop them, and the moment they were spoken, the vault *heard* them. Petra felt it in her teeth—in the mark, in the deep stacks, in the pale wooden drawer that had opened for her thumb. Somewhere in the dark, ten thousand bottled sorrows leaned toward her like grass toward wind.

Sable went very still.

"That," he said finally, "is precisely why you must burn it."

They stood facing each other across the founder's book, handler and keeper, and between them the lantern flame stood perfectly straight, as if listening.

Petra looked down at the gray cover, at the blind-stamped closed eye, and felt the defiance arrive—not hot, but cold and certain, the way resolve had come to her on battlefields years ago, right before the charge. The founder had needed her to survive long enough to choose. Someone had sold a memory of tomorrow using her hand. Her god's silence sat branded on her skin like a debt unpaid.

She had spent eleven years being invisible. The book said she never had been.

"No," Petra said, and tucked the folio inside her coat, against her heart, where the mark burned hottest. "I'm keeping it."

Sable exhaled slowly through his nose. "Then may whatever gods are left forgive us both," he said, "because there's a broker missing from the Saltmarket office, and I suspect you'll want to see what they found in his mouth at dawn."

He turned and walked toward the threshold, and after a moment, Petra followed—out of the hush of the deep stacks, past the griefs and the grudges and the wars never used, carrying a century-old debt against her ribs, while behind them in the dark the pale drawer stood open, empty, and waiting.
