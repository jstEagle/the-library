## 3. The Usurper's Coin

The broker's name was Halloway Fen, and he had owed the guild eleven years of service and three favors uncollected when they pulled him out of the Gray Canal.

Petra stood on the towpath with the morning fog beading in her hair and watched the watchmen lever the body onto the stones. The canal had kept him two days. Water had done what water does—softened him, simplified him—but it had not touched his mouth. Someone had wedged it open before he went into the water, or after; the coin sat between his teeth like a communion offering, gold catching the gray light.

She should not have been there. Handlers kept their ledgers behind glass and their brokers at arm's length, and Sable had taught her that grief was a currency you never spent where anyone could see it. But Halloway Fen had taught her to hold a quill properly, eleven years ago, back when she was still a mercenary pretending to be a clerk, and so she stood on the towpath with her hood up and let the crowd press against her like a tide.

"Usurper's mint," one of the watchmen said, turning the coin with a pencil-thin knife. He didn't touch it with his fingers. Nobody touched usurper's coin with bare fingers; everyone knew the story about the tax collector in Vell who'd handled a chest of it and woke up owning nothing, not even his name. "Look at the face on it."

Petra looked.

The face was new—the Usurper's own, young and hard, struck in profile the way emperors liked themselves. But below the face, at the coin's base, was the mint-mark, and Petra's breath went out of her slowly, the way air leaves a room through a door someone forgot to close.

A circle, broken by a single vertical line that did not quite touch either rim.

She knew that mark. She wore that mark, inked small beneath her left ear where her hair would cover it, where she'd worn it since the night she swore her oath to Veyl and the god had gone silent in the same breath, as if her vow had been a door closing from the inside. Every text she'd ever smuggled for the guild said the same thing: the Silent God has no sigil. His silence *is* his sigil. And yet here it was, stamped in gold by a mint that had no business knowing it existed, pressed between a dead man's teeth.

*Someone remembers,* she thought. *Someone remembers what everyone else forgot.*

The watchman dropped the coin into an oilcloth bag and knotted it. The crowd murmured its murmur and began to dissolve, because crowds are tides and tides do not linger over one drowned man when there is bread to buy. Petra stayed until the last of them went, then crouched by the body as if adjusting her bootlace.

Halloway's hands were wrong. She saw it in three seconds, the old mercenary arithmetic running under the grief like a second pulse. Drowned men don't have bruised knuckles. Drowned men don't have ligature marks half-hidden under their collars, thin wire-burns that the canal had swollen shut but not erased. He hadn't fallen in. He'd been held under, or strangled first and sunk after, and whoever did it had wanted the city to find him with that coin in his mouth.

A message. Not to the guild—to *her*, though she couldn't have said how she knew. Messages have addresses. This one had hers written all over it in a hand she almost recognized.

She straightened. Her hands were steady. She made them stay steady all the way back to the Gilded Ledger, up the servants' stair, past the memory-vaults with their cold iron doors, into the counting room where the day's accounts waited in their neat columns like soldiers awaiting inspection.

She opened the ledger of small debts and found Halloway's page. Eleven years of trades, each one initialed: a stolen lullaby sold twice, a widow's grief decanted and resold as vintage sorrow, a soldier's courage purchased back from him at a fair price when the war broke him. In the margin of the last entry, dated four days ago, Halloway had written a note in his cramped, careful hand:

*Client paid in gold. Refused script. Mint-mark wrong. Checking provenance.*

Four days ago. Two days before he died.

Petra read the line six times. Then she closed the ledger, and sat very still in the green lamplight, and understood that she had read this entry two days ago and thought nothing of it. A broker checking provenance was a broker doing his job. She had balanced the column and moved on, and somewhere across the city men with wire and a bucket of canal water had been waiting for him to finish checking.

*I could have warned him.* The thought arrived without permission and settled in her chest like a swallowed stone. If she had gone to him, if she had asked one question—one—about a client who paid in raw gold and refused guild script, Halloway might have walked into the Gray Canal a living man. Instead she had done what she always did: kept the books, kept her head down, kept herself invisible, because invisible things survive.

Invisible things also let other people die.

She took out her pen. Her hand did not shake. That was the worst part—that it didn't shake, that eleven years of ledger discipline held even now, and she wrote *deceased — account closed — balance forfeit to the guild* in the same clear hand she used for everything, and initialed it, and blotted it, and felt nothing move in her face at all.

Under the desk, hidden by the wood, her other hand found the mark beneath her ear and pressed it, hard, the way you press a bruise to prove it's real.

*Someone remembers your god,* she thought. *And they are killing people to tell you so.*

* * *

Corvane ate breakfast the way he did everything: precisely, and alone, and while reading other people's mail.

The spymaster's quarters above the Salt Gate barracks had once belonged to a harbor pilot, and Corvane had kept the man's charts on the walls because they pleased him—a city rendered as depth and current, every channel marked, every shoal named. The free cities were much the same. You learned where the water ran shallow, and you ran your ships accordingly.

The letter in front of him was from a wool merchant in Highmoor, full of pious complaints about tariffs, and encoded in the complaint about the tariff on indigo dyes was a report: the Gilded Ledger had moved something large out of its vaults two nights ago. Something heavy enough to need four porters and a covered barge. His agent didn't know what it was. Corvane found he didn't much care yet. What interested him was that the Ledger was nervous, and nervous institutions made mistakes, and mistakes were doors.

He buttered his bread, and considered the drowned broker.

Fen had been a good choice. Corvane had not chosen him personally—he left the choosing to his people in the city, the quiet ones, the ones who paid in gold because gold left no ledger entries—but he approved of the choice. A mid-level broker, respected, connected, not important enough to trigger a purge and not expendable enough to be ignored. And the coin in the mouth: that had been his own suggestion, passed down through three intermediaries, because Corvane believed in grammar. A corpse says little. A corpse with a coin says *we know what you are*. A corpse with *that particular* coin—

He smiled at his bread.

The mint-mark had cost him two years to find. It was in a fragmentary manuscript his agents had pulled from a burned monastery in the borderlands, a heresy older than the Usurper's grandfather: an account of the war the free cities preferred to forget, the war fought over a god whose voice had been taken from him, sealed away, silenced—and by whom the manuscript declined to say, because the pages that said were ash. But the sigil survived. A circle, broken. The mark of Veyl, the Silent God, patron of oaths and debts, the god whose silence the free cities had built their tidy commerce of memories upon, trading in the mind's goods as if the mind were just another warehouse.

Corvane's master—the Usurper, who disliked being called a usurper in company—had grasped the implication before Corvane finished explaining it. If the god's voice had been *sealed*, it could be *unsealed*. And whatever held the seal was a thing, and things could be taken, bought, or broken. The free cities' entire order rested on that seal. Crack it, and you didn't conquer the cities—you dissolved them, the way salt dissolves in rain.

So: the coin. Placed in the mouth of a Ledger broker, where the Ledger would find it. Let them chew on the meaning. Let them discover, as Corvane had discovered, that their foundation was a theft, and watch the cracks spread on their own. Fear was cheaper than armies. He had a budget for both, but fear compounded better.

There was only one variable he had not yet priced.

His evening reports lay in a second stack, and the topmost was from the watcher assigned to the Gilded Ledger itself—a dull, patient man who wrote dull, patient sentences, which Corvane trusted more than poetry. Most of it was inventory: deliveries, faces, hours. But near the end:

*One handler keeps unusual hours. Female. Comes and goes by the servants' stair. Was present at the canal this morning when the body was recovered, hooded, alone, stayed after the crowd dispersed. Examined the corpse. Left before the watch bagged the coin.*

Corvane read it twice.

Most mourners lingered. Most spies hurried. This one had done neither—she had looked at the body the way an appraiser looks at a forged signature, and then she had left, and leaving was the interesting part. Either she was exactly what she appeared, a functionary with an inconvenient conscience, or she was the most disciplined creature in the free cities.

He set the report down and looked at the harbor charts, at all those carefully named depths.

"Find out who balances their books," he said to the empty room, because his adjutant had learned not to knock during breakfast. "Quietly. I want to know what she reads, what she writes, and what she prays to—if she prays to anything."

Then he finished his breakfast, because the army was eleven days from the walls and a man should keep his habits while he still had walls to keep them behind.

* * *

Sable was waiting in Petra's office when she came back from the vaults, which meant Sable had a key, which meant Sable had decided the conversation was happening whether Petra consented to it or not.

"You went to the canal." Her handler stood by the window with her back to the light, so her face was all shadow and her voice was all edge. "You stood over a dead broker in front of forty witnesses, and then you came home and entered his account closure in your own hand. Bold. Very bold."

"He was my teacher."

"He was an account." Sable stepped forward, and the light caught her face at last: tired, sharp, and—for one unguarded instant—afraid. "And now he's a message, and messages have recipients. The coin, Petra. Tell me you looked at the coin."

"I looked at the coin."

"And?"

Petra weighed it. Eleven years of hiding, weighing everything, spending nothing. But Halloway was dead in the Gray Canal with a god's name stamped in gold between his teeth, and the scales had shifted.

"It was Veyl's mark," she said. "The sigil that doesn't exist. On usurper's gold."

Sable closed her eyes. When she opened them, the fear had been packed away into something harder and more useful. "Then the Usurper knows about the seal," she said. "Or thinks he does. Either way, the Ledger is no longer a merchant house. We're a target, and you—" she pointed at Petra's chest, at the place where, beneath wool and linen and eleven years of careful invisibility, a silent god slept inside her like a fire banked in a locked room— "you are the vault they'll come for. So we are going to burn every prophecy book in the archive, starting tonight, and you are going to stop asking questions."

"The archive?" Petra said quietly. "You mean the founder's book. You've known."

For a moment Sable said nothing, and in that silence Petra heard the whole weight of the guild settle—every impossible trade, every bottled grief, every second chance sold at retail, all of it resting on a secret old enough to have killed a broker for brushing against its edge.

"There are things in that book about you," Sable said at last, "that I have prayed every night you'd never read. Come to the vault at moonrise. Bring nothing. Trust no one—not Ilse, not me, least of all yourself."

She left. Petra sat alone with her ledgers, and the green lamp, and the neat columns of debts that suddenly looked like what they had always been: a map of everything the guild had ever taken from anyone, including, perhaps, her.

Below the desk, her fingers found the mark beneath her ear again.

And for the first time in eleven years, faint as a heartbeat heard through a wall, she thought she felt it answer.
