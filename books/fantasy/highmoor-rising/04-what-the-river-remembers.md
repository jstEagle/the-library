## 4. What the River Remembers

The river that fed Highmoor had no name anymore. It had traded it away, Maren said, in the guild's first century — sold the sound of itself to a dying king who wanted to hear his mother's voice one last time, and what remained after the sale was water that moved without speaking, gray-green and patient as a debt.

Petra followed its banks for two days out of the city, past the tanneries and the drowned orchards, until the reeds grew taller than her shoulders and the world narrowed to mud, water, and the smell of silt. She told no one where she was going. Not Sable, who would have ordered her back with that flat look he wore like armor. Not Ilse, whose face she could not stop seeing when she closed her eyes — Ilse's hands turning the pages of a memory-bottle like a woman handling something holy, Ilse saying *you can't decode a prophecy you're inside of, Petra, but gods help us, we can try.*

She could not bring this to Ilse. Whatever lived at the bottom of the founder's book, whatever had written *the vessel swears* beside her name, it deserved to be faced alone first. If there was rot in her, she wanted to find it before anyone she loved had to smell it.

Maren's hut stood on stilts over the water, leaning east like an old woman listening. Petra climbed the ladder, boots slipping on wet rungs, and found the door already open.

"You took the long road," Maren said from inside. "Good. The short one is for people who know what they want."

The oracle sat by a window of oiled hide, wrapped in three coats against the damp despite the summer heat. She had been old when Petra was a child; she was old now, in the way of things the ledger did not touch. Her eyes were the pale, washed-out color of the river itself, and they did not track movement the way living eyes should. They looked at Petra the way water looks at a stone dropped into it — accepting, absorbing, already forgetting.

"I need to ask you about a night," Petra said. "Nineteen years ago. The night I swore my oath to Veyl."

Maren smiled without warmth. "You need to ask me why your god went quiet."

The words landed in Petra's chest like a coin dropped down a well. She heard it fall for a long time before it struck bottom.

* * *

They had been eleven of them, that night. A free company called the Nine Spears, because names were cheaper than men and they'd lost two in the marshes at Corvane's Ford and never bothered to update. Petra had been twenty-two, good with a blade and better with numbers, which was why Captain Ashe kept her close: not for her sword arm but because she could tell, at a glance, whether a contract was worth the blood it would cost.

The war had chewed through the borderlands for three years by then. The usurper — not yet crowned, still just a general with a beautiful voice and an army that never seemed to lose — had been buying up every hedge-priest and bone-reader in the provinces. And the Nine Spears had taken a contract that Ashe should have refused: escort a relic-caravan through the Ashen Pass while the usurper's outriders hunted it.

The relic was a man. That was how the caravan master put it, and everyone laughed, and then the curtains of the litter opened and Petra saw that it was true, or true enough. An old priest of Veyl, the Silent God, the god whose temples still stood in every free city though no prayer had been answered from them in living memory. The priest was dying. He said so cheerfully, the way other men announced weather.

"The god has a voice," he told them around the fire that night, while the pass winds screamed above. "It has always had a voice. You mistake silence for absence. Silence is only a voice being held."

"By whom?" Petra had asked. She remembered asking. She remembered the firelight on his ruined hands.

He had looked at her for a long moment — too long, the kind of look that makes a soldier check where her knife is — and said, "By whoever can afford to hold it."

Two days later the ambush came at the narrows, and the Nine Spears died well, all but four, and Petra killed six men in the space of a hundred heartbeats and felt nothing, which frightened her more than dying would have. And when the fighting was done and the dead lay steaming in the snow, the old priest crawled to her across the ice — shot through, drowning in his own blood — and gripped her wrist with fingers strong as a trap.

"He asked for a vessel," the priest gasped. "A thousand years he has asked. And you — you knelt in the snow and you didn't even know you were praying—"

"I wasn't praying."

"You were. Everyone prays when the killing stops. That's the only honest prayer there is." Blood filled his mouth; he spat it pink into the snow. "And something answered. Something that has waited since the founding for a throat empty enough to fill. Swear it, girl. Swear the oath. Give him the vessel he asks for or he will take the whole of you, and everything you are will be a voice shouting in an empty house forever."

She had sworn it. Kneeling in the blood-slush with her company dead around her, she had sworn the oath of Veyl — the old words, the mercenary's words, *my hand, my blade, my silence* — because the alternative was worse, because the priest was dying anyway, because grief does strange arithmetic.

And the moment the last word left her mouth, the world went quiet in a way it had never been quiet before. Not silence. *Heldness.* Like a breath drawn and kept.

The priest died smiling. "Paid in full," he whispered. "At last. At last."

Petra had buried him and walked south with three survivors and a mark on her wrist that hadn't been there before — a small gray sigil like a closed mouth — and she had told herself the shaking in her hands was cold.

* * *

"That's not how it happened," Maren said, when Petra finished. The fire had burned low. Outside, the river moved without sound. "That's how you remember it. There's a difference, and in my trade we live on the difference."

"Then tell me how it happened."

Maren rose with the creak of old joints and crossed to a shelf where dozens of bottles sat in straw — memories, Petra realized, the trade-goods of the guild, each one glowing faintly with the color of the life inside it. Most were dim. One, near the end, was nearly black.

"This is mine," Maren said, touching the dark bottle without picking it up. "What's left. I sold the rest a piece at a time, sixty years ago, to buy my gift. Do you understand what I paid? I don't. That's rather the point. I know I had a sister. I know I loved someone enough to make the trade make sense. I know it the way you know a room you've only seen described." Her washed-out eyes found Petra's. "So believe me when I tell you what the river remembers, because the river forgets nothing. It only agrees to be quiet."

"Tell me."

"The night of your oath, the god's voice did not go silent." Maren sat again, slowly. "The god's voice went *into* you. Those are different things, child. A silenced bell hangs dead on its rope. But a held note—" she pressed one fist to her own sternum, "—a held note is still singing. Somewhere. Under everything. And everything downstream of it changes pitch."

Petra's hands had gone cold. "The temples. The unanswered prayers. Nineteen years of—"

"Were nineteen years of *you*. Every prayer spoken into that held note passed through the vessel first. Through you." Maren tilted her head. "Did you never wonder why you survived things that should have killed you? Six men at the narrows. The fever at Saltmarsh. The fall at Greywater Bridge. Mercenaries die, girl. Constantly. Professionally. And yet here you sit, marked and whole, balancing books for a guild that trades in impossible things — a guild founded, if the river tells it true, by a woman who spent her whole life preparing a vessel she would never live to see born."

The vertigo came then, exactly as Petra had feared it would: the floor of her life tilting, every choice sliding toward an edge she couldn't see. She thought of the prophecy book in the vault, her own handwriting beside a date written a century ago. *The vessel swears.* Not a prediction. A stage direction.

"My survival," Petra said slowly. "It cost someone."

"Everything costs someone. That is the first law of ledgers." Maren's voice gentled, which was somehow worse. "But yes. Ask yourself what the seal requires, child. A vessel must stay empty enough to hold a god. Empty of what?"

Petra knew. She had known since the vault, since Sable's face when he said *burn it*. "Attachment. Love. Everything the founder wrote next."

"And have you noticed," Maren said, "how everyone who gets close to you comes to grief? Captain Ashe. The boy at Corvane's Ford who carried your water. The broker Tallowin, dead with a coin in his mouth not ten days past, who owed you a kindness and paid it with his life." She spread her ruined hands. "I am not saying you killed them. I am saying the seal is not passive. A held note wants resolution. It bends everything near it toward the ending it was promised. And the ending it was promised, according to the river, is a payment. The beloved. First of all payments."

The word *beloved* went through Petra like a spear through wet canvas, and behind it, unbidden, came Ilse's face — Ilse laughing over the decoding table, Ilse's ink-stained fingers, Ilse saying *we can try* — and Petra understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, why she had needed to come alone.

"There's more," Maren said. "There always is, with rivers. The usurper's people came asking the same questions a month ago. A spymaster. Gray-eyed, polite, paid me in gold minted with a closed mouth on it." Her smile returned, thin as ice. "I told him nothing. But he didn't need me to tell him. He was following the same current you are. And he was moving faster, because he isn't afraid of where it goes."

Petra stood. Her legs felt borrowed. "Where does it go?"

"To the source," Maren said. "To the chapel under the guild, where the founder kept her accounts with heaven. And child — the river says one thing more, and you will wish it hadn't." She leaned forward, and for a moment her blank eyes seemed to see Petra entirely, all the way down. "The seal is failing. It has been failing since the day you swore. Every year the note holds, it strains harder against the throat holding it. Soon — months, perhaps less — either the vessel speaks the name and the god pours out, or the vessel breaks and the god spills. There is no third column in that ledger."

Outside, far off across the dark water, a bell began to ring in Highmoor — once, twice, the guild's emergency pattern, the one that meant *come home now*.

Petra was already running for the ladder, and the last thing Maren said to her back was almost lost under the ringing:

"It knows you've learned. Things that are held always feel the hand loosen."
