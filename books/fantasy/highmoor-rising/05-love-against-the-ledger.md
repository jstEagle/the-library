## 5. Love Against the Ledger

The memory-keeper's workshop sat at the bottom of the guild's spiral stair, below even the vaults, where the stone drank candlelight instead of reflecting it. Petra had passed its door a hundred times in her three years with the Gilded Ledger and never once gone through it. Keepers were not for brokers to bother. They were for clients — the grieving, the desperate, the rich — who came down those stairs carrying their worst hours in glass and left lighter by exactly as much as they could pay.

Tonight she knocked anyway.

"Enter," said a voice like warm water over stones.

Ilse Varn was sorting when Petra came in: rows of little vials laid out on black velvet, each one stoppered with wax the color of dried blood, each one labeled in a hand so small it looked like insect tracks. She was younger than Petra had imagined — everyone said *the keeper* the way they said *the founder*, as if age were part of the title — with ink-stained fingers and a scar splitting one eyebrow, which no one had ever mentioned either. Guild gossip kept its silences as carefully as its secrets.

"You're Dunmore." She didn't look up. "The ledger-woman."

"I'm the ledger-woman."

"Then you already know I can't help you. Brokers don't get their memories read. Rule nine."

"I'm not here about rule nine." Petra set her satchel on the workbench, between the vials of bottled grief and something that glowed faintly amber and made her think, without knowing why, of a summer she'd never had. "I'm here because someone has to tell me what this says before Sable burns it."

She unwrapped the oilcloth. The prophecy book lay between them like a corpse at a wake — the founder's own hand, a century old, the pages soft as moth wing. Ilse went still in a way that reminded Petra, uncomfortably, of herself. Of soldiers before an ambush. People who had learned that stillness was cheaper than panic.

"Where did you get this?"

"The vault. Third shelf, behind the founder's death mask, under a false bottom that wasn't supposed to exist."

"That's not what I asked."

"No," Petra agreed. "It isn't."

Ilse studied her for a long moment. Then she pulled a lamp closer, tilted the page into its light, and began to read aloud in a language Petra didn't know — old Highmoor cant, the kind the guild used for contracts so convoluted no client could ever claim they'd been cheated. Halfway down the page her voice slowed. Stopped.

"*When the silent god stirs in the vessel of his sealing,*" she translated finally, finger under the line, "*the beloved shall be weighed first among the payments.*"

Petra felt her stomach drop through the floor. "Beloved. Beloved of whom?"

"That's the problem with prophecies." Ilse closed the book gently, the way you close a door on a sleeping child. "They're never clear enough to ignore and never clear enough to act on. 'Beloved' could mean the god's beloved. Could mean the vessel's. Could mean—" She stopped herself, and Petra watched her decide not to say it, watched the word go back down her throat like a coin swallowed whole.

"Could mean what?"

"Nothing. It could mean nothing. Prophecies are inventory, Dunmore. Stock traded a hundred years ago by a woman who liked the sound of her own dread. Half of them never come due." But her hands, Petra noticed, had begun very slightly to shake, and she reached for a vial of something — plain lavender oil, probably nothing, definitely something — just to have a reason to hold them steady.

* * *

They worked three nights running.

It was not supposed to be what it became. Petra brought the book down after her shifts; Ilse decoded while Petra copied the translations into cipher, so that if Sable came asking, there would be nothing to find but a broker's private notes on market futures. That was the story they told themselves. Two professionals, one project, strictly transactional.

But the workshop at night was a country with different laws than the guild above it. Down there, time moved at the speed of candlewax. Ilse talked while she worked — not about the prophecy, mostly, but about the trade itself, the strange theology of it. How grief kept better in glass than in the body. How people who sold their memories always came back within a year, unable to say why, only that they felt a draft blowing through a room they couldn't find.

"What do you think happens to a memory once it's sold?" Petra asked her, on the third night. "Does it live in the vial? Or does it die?"

"Neither." Ilse held a vial up to the lamp. Inside, something moved — not liquid, not light, but the suggestion of motion, like a fish seen through ice. "It waits. A memory is a debt someone owes to themselves. It doesn't stop being owed because the paper changed hands." She glanced sideways at Petra, and the look lasted a half-second longer than professional courtesy required. "You'd know something about debts."

"I keep other people's accounts."

"Mm." Ilse set the vial down. "And who keeps yours?"

The question hung there. Petra found she couldn't answer it, and that Ilse saw her fail to answer it, and that this — the seeing, the being seen without flinching or filing it away for later leverage — was more dangerous than anything written in the founder's poisoned book.

On the fourth night, Petra arrived to find Ilse had cleared a space on the workbench and set out two cups of chicory, black, the way mercenaries drink it, though Petra had never told her that.

"How did you know—"

"I read a thousand memories a week. You learn what people carry even when they've never carried it past my door." Ilse pushed a cup toward her. "Yours is soldier's coffee. You hold it in both hands like it might be taken from you. You do it with everything. Ledgers. Doors. Conversations." A pause. "People, probably, if you ever let any close enough."

Petra wrapped both hands around the cup and did not argue, because arguing would have been admitting how precisely she'd been read, and how much it unsettled her to be known by someone whose job was knowing things people paid enormous sums to forget.

They decoded until the candles guttered. Fragment by fragment, the founder's century-old voice assembled itself out of the dark: *the seal is blood, the blood is a door, the door must be fed*. Payment schedules. Interest rates. A god's silence, itemized like a shipment of silk. And threaded through all of it, recurring every few pages like a refrain, the same two words in the same careful hand:

*The beloved. The beloved. The beloved.*

"It's a contract term," Ilse said at last, pushing back from the table. Her eyes were red-rimmed; she'd been at the fine print for six hours. "That's all. Every seal has a schedule of payments. Land, gold, lives, love — the founder wrote them all into her bargains somewhere. This one names its first payment category, that's all. It doesn't name a person."

"It doesn't have to." Petra heard her own voice come out flat and cold, the mercenary's register, the one she used for bad news. "I've read enough contracts to know when a clause is waiting for a signature. The founder wrote this a hundred years ago. She wrote *my name* beside tonight's date. If the seal needs feeding — if the vessel is me — then the beloved isn't a category. It's whoever I—" 

She stopped. Because saying the rest meant saying it, and saying it would make it true, and true things in the Gilded Ledger had a way of becoming inventory.

Ilse was quiet for a long moment. Then she said, softly, in the voice of someone setting down something heavy: "Then we should be careful what we become to each other."

And Petra — who had survived eleven years of war by never wanting anything she couldn't walk away from by dawn — heard herself answer, "Too late," and knew, with the sick certainty of a woman reading her own handwriting in a dead prophet's book, that she had just signed something.

* * *

Sable found them at dawn.

Not together — Petra was alone in her cell above the counting floor, sleepless, when the knock came, three slow raps that meant *we need to talk, and you will not enjoy it.* Sable stood in the corridor with her grey coat buttoned to the throat and her face doing what it always did, which was nothing.

"You've been downstairs," she said.

"I've been working."

"You've been working on something that isn't yours, with someone whose discretion is legendary and therefore expensive." Sable let that sit. "I warned you to burn the book. You didn't. So now I'm going to tell you what happens next, and you're going to listen, because unlike you I've been in this guild since before you could shave."

Petra waited.

"There's a reason keepers don't take lovers, Dunmore. It isn't prudishness. It's arithmetic." Sable's voice stayed level, but something underneath it had begun to grind, like a wheel turning over gravel. "A keeper who loves someone starts keeping *their* accounts. Starts noticing what they'd pay, what they'd trade, what they couldn't bear to lose. And a keeper who knows what you couldn't bear to lose is holding the most dangerous inventory in this building." She turned to go, then paused. "The founder knew it too. Read your book again. Ask yourself why every payment schedule begins the same way."

After she left, Petra sat on the edge of her narrow bed with the prophecy open across her knees and went back through it line by line, hating herself for obeying, hating Sable for being right, hating the founder most of all for writing it all down a hundred years before there was anyone alive to break her heart.

Every schedule began the same way. Not with gold. Not with blood.

*First payment: the beloved.*

And beneath tonight's date, in the margin, in ink that had faded to the color of old rust, the founder had added one final note — small, almost careless, the kind of annotation a bookkeeper makes without thinking:

*The vessel will try to refuse. Vessels always do. Charge it to the one she loves.*

Petra closed the book. Somewhere below her, faint through three floors of stone, she could hear the memory-keeper's workshop: a vial being stoppered, a lamp being trimmed, someone humming to herself in the dark, unaware — as yet, still unaware — of what her name was about to cost.

She picked up her pen. There was an entry she had to make, and for the first time in three years of keeping the guild's accounts, her hand was shaking too badly to write it straight.
