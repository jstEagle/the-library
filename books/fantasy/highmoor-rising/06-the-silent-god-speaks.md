## 6. The Silent God Speaks

The entry she could not write was simple, as the worst entries always were. *One memory of tomorrow, received in trade. Provenance: client unknown. Value: to be determined.* Her pen hovered over the line until the ink dried on the nib and she had to wipe it clean with her thumb, leaving a grey smear like a bruise across the page.

Below her, faint through three floors of stone, Ilse was still humming.

Petra set down the pen. She told herself she would go down and say something ordinary — ask after a shipment of bottled grief due from the coast, complain about the damp in the counting room — anything to put a living voice between herself and the founder's handwriting. She told herself that was all it was.

She knew better. The prophecy sat in her mind like a splinter worked under the skin: *charge it to the one she loves.* And beneath it, older and colder, the date in her own hand, written by a woman dead a century before Petra learned to hold a pen.

She was halfway down the first stair when the lamps went out.

Not dimmed — *out*, all of them at once, from the counting room to the vault below, as if the whole guildhouse had been plunged underwater. Petra froze on the stairs, one hand finding the wall, the other going to the short blade at her hip out of three years' habit she'd never managed to shed. In the dark she heard the guild breathe: the tick of the great clock in the atrium, the settling of old stone, and beneath it something else, a low pressure against her eardrums like the moment before a storm breaks.

Then the front doors came off their hinges.

They went inward with a sound like a ship's mast snapping, and torchlight poured through the ruin of them — not the orange of oil but a hard white glare that threw shadows of the doorframe across the marble floor like bars. Men came through the light in mail and black lacquer, moving in the disciplined silence of soldiers who have done this many times. Usurper's men. Corvane's men. Their boots left prints in the dust of the shattered doors, and where they walked, the guild's wards — woven into the thresholds by brokers long dead — guttered and died like snuffed candles.

*They found us,* Petra thought, absurdly, uselessly. *They found the Gilded Ledger.*

Downstairs, the humming stopped.

Petra did not go down to fight. That was the training speaking, the mercenary's cold arithmetic she'd spent three years pretending she'd buried: too many of them, wrong ground, no exit. Instead she pressed herself into the shadow of the stairwell and watched, and hated herself for how easily the watching came.

They moved through the atrium with terrible efficiency. One squad to the counting room. One to the memory-vaults, where a broker named Hesk made his stand behind a desk worth more than the street outside and died for it without slowing them by a breath. A third went deeper, toward the workshops, toward —

Toward Ilse.

Petra's blade was in her hand before she decided anything. Later she would not remember crossing the landing or taking the servants' stair, only that suddenly she was on the workshop floor, crouched behind a rack of stoppered vials that glowed faintly in the dark — griefs, mostly, blue-white and restless — while boots rang on stone somewhere ahead and a voice she knew said, calm as a clerk reading terms:

"You'll want to be careful with those. Some of them are older than your empire."

Sable. Her handler stood in the middle of the archive corridor with his hands visible and empty, silver-haired, unhurried, as though the raid were an audit he'd been expecting. Perhaps he had been. Six men faced him, and behind them, stepping out of the torchlight with the patience of a man entering his own house, came Corvane.

He was smaller than Petra had imagined, and that somehow made him worse. Grey-cloaked, unarmored, with the mild face of a scholar and eyes that moved over the shelves of stolen memories the way a miser's eyes move over coin. He did not look at Sable when he spoke.

"The founder's ledgers," he said. "All of them. And the prophecy book. My master believes it may still exist."

"It doesn't," Sable said pleasantly.

"Everything exists." Corvane drifted along the shelf, trailing one gloved finger above the vials without touching them. "That is rather the point of your guild, isn't it? You deal in what cannot be. So does my master. The difference is that he keeps his accounts current." He stopped. Turned. "You have something I want more than books, actually. A keeper. A woman who balances impossible trades. Where is she?"

Petra's blood went to ice water. *He knows I exist.*

"She retired," Sable said. "Grew attached to someone. We let her go. Guild policy."

"Guild policy," Corvane repeated softly, and smiled, and it was the smile that broke something open in Petra's chest — because she recognized it, or rather she recognized what lay underneath it, a hunger shaped exactly like the hunger she'd felt in the vault, in the founder's book, the sense of being *read*. He turned to his men. "Search the upper floors. Bring me anyone who—"

And then Veyl spoke.

It came up through Petra like water through a cracked well-shaft, like something that had been rising for years and had finally, tonight, found the level of her throat. She felt her jaw open. She felt her lungs fill with air that was not entirely hers. And out of her mouth, into the dark of the workshop, rolled a voice that was hers and utterly not hers — layered, resonant, old as the stones the guild was built on, a voice that made every vial on the racks flare white and every soldier spin toward the sound with steel drawn.

"*THE LEDGER IS NOT YOURS TO COLLECT.*"

The words were not in any language Petra knew, and yet she understood them the way she understood pain. The nearest soldier fired his crossbow on reflex; the bolt crossed the room and stopped, hanging in the air a hand's width from her chest, quivering, its momentum simply *gone*, swallowed. The god's silence, Petra thought distantly, had never been absence. It had been weight. And the weight was hers.

"*WHAT WAS SEALED IN BLOOD CANNOT BE TAKEN BY STEEL. LEAVE, OR BE ENTERED INTO THE ACCOUNT.*"

Corvane had gone very still. He was looking past his men now, past the frozen bolt, directly at the shadowed rack where Petra stood — not searching anymore. *Finding.* His scholarly face showed no fear. It showed recognition, and worse than recognition: satisfaction, the look of a man whose figures have finally balanced.

"There you are," he said quietly. To her. To whatever spoke through her. "A century we've looked for the vessel. And the guild kept you all along, hiding you in plain sight among the clerks." He raised his voice. "Take her alive. Wound nothing. If the seal breaks, everything my master has built breaks with it — do you understand me? *Nothing touches her.*"

The soldiers advanced. Petra tried to move, to run, to do anything, but her body belonged half to the voice still rolling out of it, and the voice was not finished:

"*WHEN THE SALT GATE STANDS OPEN AND THE FALSE CROWN ASKS FOR MY NAME —*"

The sentence stopped. Cut off mid-word, like a ledger slammed shut, and the silence that rushed back in was so total and so familiar that Petra nearly wept — because she knew that silence, had carried it inside her for three years without ever once wondering where it came from. Her knees buckled. She caught herself on the rack; vials chimed around her like small bells.

Through the ringing in her ears she heard shouting, the crash of the search reaching the upper floors, and Sable's voice, sharp now for the first time in all the years she'd known him: "Back corridor! Keeper, *move!*"

She moved. Blind, half-deaf, she let his hand close on her wrist and drag her through the archive's hidden seam — the door behind the shelf of bottled second chances that only senior brokers knew — into the dark of the underpassages beneath the guildhouse. Behind them she heard timber splinter, and Corvane's voice, unhurried even in pursuit:

"Seal the exits. She can't stay underground forever."

* * *

They ran through the dark with lamps lit and shuttered, following passages so old they predated the free cities, and Sable talked as he ran, low and fast, because Sable had never in his life met a silence he didn't try to fill.

"Three years I've carried you through the guild's doors and told myself the founder's protections would hold. Do you know what your employment contract actually says, Petra? Clause nine. *The guild shall provide the keeper with purpose, shelter, and concealment.* Concealment. From whom, I asked, the day I hired you. From everyone, the founder's notes said. *Especially from herself.*" He took a corner; lamplight swung wild across wet stone. "I thought it meant your mercenary past. Gods forgive me, I thought it meant your *past*."

"The voice," Petra managed. Her throat felt flayed, as if she'd swallowed broken glass and it had sung on the way down. "That was — Sable, that came out of *me*."

"Yes."

"That was Veyl."

"Yes." He stopped at last at a rusted gate, fumbled a key from inside his coat, and did not look at her while he worked it. "The night you swore your oath to the silent god, you swore it to yourself. Or the part of yourself that was never only yours. I don't know which way round the founder arranged it, and I've read everything she left." The gate groaned open onto a sloping tunnel that breathed river-damp. "Maren might know. Maren remembers things the rest of us traded away."

"Maren traded away her own past."

"Which makes her the only person alive who can be trusted with yours." He pushed the lamp into her hands. "Go to the river. I'll draw them off — I'm old, Petra, and I'm expensive, and Corvane wants the ledgers more than he wants me. I can be worth trading." He almost smiled. "Consider it a debt transfer."

"Sable—"

"Clause nine," he said, and stepped back into the dark the way they'd come, raising his voice so it would carry to the soldiers behind them. "This way, gentlemen! The archives are *this* way, and I do so hate repeating myself!"

Petra ran the other way, down, toward the river, the lamp shaking in her grip, the god's words still lodged in her skull like a splinter of ice: *when the salt gate stands open and the false crown asks for my name.*

Behind her, muffled through tons of stone, she heard the moment Corvane's men caught her handler — a shout, a crash, and then Sable's voice, perfectly composed even now, saying something she couldn't make out, followed by laughter that was not kind.

She did not stop. But some part of her, the part that kept the ledgers, opened a new page as she ran and entered the debt in letters of fire she would carry the rest of her life:

*One handler, taken in trade for my escape. Owed to: Sable. Payable: everything.*
