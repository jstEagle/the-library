## 7. The Founder's Bargain

Ilse had kept the guild's memories for eleven years, and in all that time she had never once opened the door beneath the stairs.

It was not a hidden door, exactly. That was the thing no one understood about the Gilded Ledger — nothing here was hidden, only unpriced. The door at the back of the memory-vault was plain oak, iron-banded, and it appeared on no inventory because it had never been traded, never been valued, never been entered into any book. It simply *was*, the way a debt you've stopped thinking about is still a debt. Every keeper before her had known it and left it shut. The founder's last instruction, inked in her own hand on the vault's first page: *What lies below is paid for. Do not reopen the account.*

But Sable was in Corvane's chains now, and Petra had spoken with a god's voice in her throat, and Ilse had decided, somewhere between the raid and the dawn, that some accounts must be reopened or everything the guild claimed to protect was a lie they told themselves while counting other people's griefs.

"You don't have to come down," she said.

Petra stood behind her with a lantern held too steady, the way soldiers hold things — no tremor wasted. "You said the chapel answers to blood."

"I said the lock answers to blood. Whether anything down there answers to *you* is another question." Ilse pressed her palm to the oak. Beneath the iron banding, faint as a watermark, she could feel the grooves of letters. She had felt them for eleven years and never read them. Now she let her fingers trace them slowly, and the words surfaced like drowned things: *HERE THE FOUNDER KEPT HER SILENCE.*

The lock drank from the cut on her thumb without ceremony. The door swung inward on stairs that went down farther than the building had depth, which was the first impossible thing, and Ilse thought, absurdly, of all the clients who had sold her their memories of cellars, of childhood houses, of places under floors — as if the whole city had been dreaming of this stairway for a century without knowing whose dream it was.

"Eleven years," Petra said quietly. "You walked past this every day."

"Every day." Ilse took the lantern first. It was easier to be brave when she was moving. "That's what the guild does best, Petra. It teaches you to walk past things."

* * *

The chapel was small, which surprised her. She had expected vastness — a god's prison ought to have vaulted ceilings, ought to make you feel like an insect. Instead it was intimate, almost domestic. A round room of grey stone. Seven alcoves, six of them empty, each holding nothing but a worn kneeler and a candle-niche blackened by old flame. The seventh alcove held a bier of pale wood, and on the bier lay a woman who had been dead so long she had become an argument between dust and marble.

And above her, carved into the curve of the wall in letters deep enough to catch shadow, ran a single line:

*I DID NOT STEAL HIS VOICE. I GAVE IT A THROAT.*

Ilse heard Petra stop breathing.

She knew that breathlessness. She catalogued it daily — clients reliving the moment they learned a parent's true name, a lover's betrayal, a child's death. The body's recognition that the floor has been reclassified as sky. She had never expected to feel it herself, standing in her founder's tomb, reading the confession of a saint.

"There were seven," Petra said. Her voice came out sanded flat. "Seven vessels."

Ilse crossed to the bier because someone had to look at the dead woman's face, and looking was her whole trade. Up close the founder was smaller than her statues, her hands folded over a closed ledger, her expression — Ilse searched for it, professionally, tenderly — not peaceful. Resolved. There was a difference, and the difference was the whole history of the free cities.

"She sealed him into herself," Ilse said. "And then —"

"Then she couldn't hold him alone." Petra had moved to the wall, where smaller text wound around the great line like ivy. Ilse watched her lips move through it, and watched the color leave her face in stages, like a tide going out. "A bloodline. She seeded the silence into her descendants. Each generation a vessel. Each vessel holds the voice a little longer, and then—" Petra's finger stopped on a passage. She read it aloud, and her mercenary steadiness cracked straight down the middle. "*When the vessel fails, the voice passes to the nearest of the blood. When the blood runs out, the voice returns to the world.*"

The lantern hissed. Somewhere above them, muffled by stone, the city bells rang the hour, and neither of them moved until the sound died.

"The blood didn't run out," Ilse said softly. "It was spent. Six vessels, six generations, each one dying a little faster than the last — look at the dates, Petra, look at the margins. And the last of the line—"

"Is me." Petra turned from the wall. In the lantern light her eyes were wet and furious and terribly awake. "She wrote my life before I was born. My oath. My service. My *silence*. I gave myself to Veyl when I was nineteen and he never answered, and I thought — all these years I thought I was unworthy. That the god found me wanting." A laugh came out of her with edges on it. "He wasn't silent because of me. He was silent *inside* me. I've been carrying his tongue in my mouth since the day I swore."

Ilse reached for her and Petra stepped back — one step, instinctive, the retreat of a soldier who has just learned the ground itself was an enemy — and then caught herself, and did not take the second step. Ilse noted that. She filed it away with everything else she kept, the way she kept ten thousand strangers' first kisses and deathbed regrets: carefully, and against her own interest.

"Read the rest," Ilse said gently. "There's always a rest. Debts have terms."

Petra read. Ilse watched her read, and saw the exact moment the fury curdled into something colder, because the founder had been thorough, and thoroughness is its own kind of cruelty.

"*If the vessel speaks the name,*" Petra recited, "*the seal breaks entire. His voice returns to heaven and to war alike. The old pacts wake. The thrones built on his silence shake. And every power that fed on the quiet — every usurper, every empire of borrowed divinity — starves.*" She lowered her hand. "And if I stay silent?"

Ilse already knew. She had read ahead; it was her nature. But she made Petra find it herself, because some ledgers must be balanced in the reader's own hand.

"*But silence erodes,*" Petra said at last, barely above a whisper. "*Each year the seal thins. What leaks through pools in the world below, and the world below sells it upward. The usurper does not seek to break the seal. He seeks to harvest it.*" She looked up. "Corvane isn't trying to free Veyl. He's *farming* him. Every year I keep quiet, the usurper grows stronger off the leak. Every soul the guild has ever bottled — the griefs, the stolen tomorrows —"

"— are runoff," Ilse finished. "From the crack in you."

The word hung there. *You.* Not the seal. Not the vessel. *You.*

Petra sat down on the sixth kneeler, abruptly, like a puppet with a cut string, and put her face in her hands, and Ilse finally crossed the distance and knelt beside her and did not say anything, because there was nothing in her considerable inventory that fit this particular grief. Betrayal was common enough. But betrayal by the dead, by a saint, by the very architecture of your faith — that was rarer stock.

"She used me," Petra said into her hands. "A hundred years ago, a woman I've prayed to my whole life reached forward out of her grave and *used* me. My oath, my scars, my—" She stopped. Ilse felt her go rigid, and knew, with the terrible fluency of a memory-keeper, exactly which word had been swallowed.

*My love.* Because the prophecy named the beloved. Because Ilse had read that fragment weeks ago in Petra's lamplit rooms, and neither of them had said it aloud since.

"Petra." She took the soldier's hands away from the soldier's face, gently, the way she handled fragile stock. "Look at me. She made a bargain with a god to save cities she'd never see, and she paid for it with her children, and her children's children, and you. That's monstrous. I won't tell you it isn't. But she also left us the terms, and knowing the terms is the only weapon anyone has ever given us." Her voice shook and she let it. "I have spent my life keeping what people choose to forget. Don't you dare choose to forget that you get to *choose*."

Petra looked at her — really looked, the way she did before battles, memorizing — and something settled in her face. Not peace. Resolve. The same expression, Ilse realized, that the dead woman wore. It passed down the bloodline like eye color.

"Speaking the name starts a war," Petra said. "Staying silent feeds one. Either way, everyone I love stands in the field." She rose, and pulled Ilse up with her, and her grip was warm and hard and mortal. "Then I want the only option she didn't write down. There's always an unpriced door, Ilse. Your whole guild runs on them. Help me find the one she didn't account for."

Above them, far above, the bells began to ring again — wrong, ragged, alarm-pattern — and through the chapel's ancient stone they both heard it, thin as thread: horns. Many horns. At the Salt Gate.

The usurper's host had arrived a day early.

And in the seventh alcove, though neither woman saw it, the founder's folded hands had shifted — or perhaps the dust had merely settled differently — so that the closed ledger on her breast now pointed, like a compass needle, toward the door.
