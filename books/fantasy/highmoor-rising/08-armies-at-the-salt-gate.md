## 8. Armies at the Salt Gate

Corvane watched the free cities from the ridge above the salt flats, and what he saw pleased him the way a well-balanced ledger pleases a miser.

The host spread below him like spilled ink — forty thousand men, the usurper's iron legions and the levied sons of three conquered provinces, their cookfires blooming in the dusk like a second, crueler dawn. Ahead of them the Salt Gate rose from the marsh, black stone furred with white mineral bloom, and behind it the towers of Highmoor and the lesser cities huddled together like sheep sensing wolves.

He had spent eleven years buying this moment. Not the army — armies were simple, coin and fear and grain. He had bought the *inside*. The guild's brokers had sold him their secrets one ledger-page at a time until half of them didn't know they were working for him at all. That was his craft: not theft, but acquisition. Everything could be acquired. Loyalty, memory, gods.

Especially gods.

"Signal from the gate," said his lieutenant, a scarred woman named Vetch who had once been a guild enforcer before Corvane outbid her oaths. "They've closed it. And there's a light in the high tower that wasn't burning yesterday."

"Which tower?"

"The guildhouse."

Corvane smiled without warmth. "Then she's still there."

*She.* The vessel. The Dunmore woman who carried Veyl's silence in her chest like a stone in a fruit. He had never met her, but he knew her the way he knew all his acquisitions — by her price. Every soul had one. Hers was simply larger than most, and stranger: she could not be bought with gold or threat, because her currency was other people. Everyone she loved was already collateral. All he had to do was name the terms.

"Send the white rider at first light," he said. "Under truce-flag. She'll come to the wall to hear him. They always do."

"And if she refuses?"

"She won't." Corvane turned his horse down toward the camp, where the usurper's engineers were already assembling the great trebuchets from salt-cured timber. "I have something she wants more than her own life. I've had it for six days. It's been very instructive."

* * *

Petra stood on the Salt Gate's wall-walk as the sun came up blood-colored through the marsh fog, and counted the enemy's fires one last time.

Below her the flats were drowned in morning haze, and out of the haze came a single rider under a white banner, unhurried, hands visible, reins looped loose. A herald's posture. Petra had been a mercenary long enough to read heralds the way other people read weather.

"He asks for you by name," said the gate-captain, a gray-bearded freeholder named Ostrek who had held this wall through two sieges and trusted no one born inside it. "Dunmore. Not the guild. You."

"Let him speak from horseback," Petra said. "No closer than fifty paces."

The herald reined in below the wall, and when he lifted his visor Petra saw a face she knew — Tam Wicker, once a broker's runner in Highmoor, a boy who'd carried sealed messages for coppers and dreamed of a broker's sash. Now he wore the usurper's black and silver, and his eyes were careful and afraid.

"Petra Dunmore," he called up. "Lord Corvane offers terms."

"I'm listening."

"He says: the guild is already his. Half your brokers signed with him before your walls were even manned. He can prove it — he has the signatures." Tam's voice wavered on that, and Petra understood why. Signatures meant names. Names meant the purge that would follow. "He says the city falls within the week regardless. He says the only question is whether it falls *whole*."

"And my part?"

Tam reached into his coat and drew out an object wrapped in oilcloth, holding it up so the wall could see. Even at fifty paces, even wrapped, Petra knew its shape. She had catalogued it herself, years ago, in the vault's deep inventory: a memory-jar, leaded glass, sealed with black wax stamped with the guild's mark.

Inside it, if Corvane spoke true, was Ilse.

Not her body. Her body was upstairs in the guildhouse, alive, breathing, asking every hour whether Petra had decoded the founder's chapel records yet. But a keeper's mind lived partly in her jars — Ilse had told her that once, laughing, showing her the shelf where she kept her own oldest memories against the day her living ones failed. Six days ago the vault had been raided in the night while Petra slept off the god's voice in her throat. The raiders had taken one jar only. Ilse's jar. The one holding everything she remembered of Petra.

"You give us the god," Tam called. "You walk out the gate, alone, and kneel. Lord Corvane takes the vessel, the seal breaks clean, and the war ends the way wars end — quickly, for the winners. In exchange: the city stands. The guild survives, purged but standing. And the jar comes back to the woman who loves you."

Petra's hands were steady on the parapet. She noticed that about herself, distantly, the way you notice rain on someone else's window. Below her, Tam Wicker waited, a boy grown into a man wearing another man's colors, and behind him the fog was full of forty thousand fires' worth of smoke.

"Tell Corvane," she said finally, "that I'll answer at dawn tomorrow. Not before."

"That's not—"

"It's not a negotiation. It's a message. Tell him exactly that."

Tam lowered the jar, tucked it away, and rode back into the fog. Petra stayed on the wall long after, watching the haze where he'd vanished, doing the arithmetic she had sworn never to do.

Every path cost someone. Surrender the god, and the seal broke deliberately — the old war unleashed on the usurper's terms, with Veyl's voice torn out of her and given to a man who would wear it like a crown. Refuse, and the city stormed, and Ilse died behind these walls along with ten thousand strangers, and the seal eroded anyway, feeding the usurper's power with every day of silence. Run — desert, flee north with Ilse, let the cities burn behind them — and the god went with her, hunted across the world, and everyone who had ever sheltered her paid the price of the chase.

There was no path where the ledger balanced. There was only the question of which debt she chose to default on.

She went down to tell them.

* * *

The guildhall smelled of lamp-oil and burnt paper.

Half the long table was empty. Broker Vess sat with her arms crossed and her sash already unpinned, folded beside her like a shed skin. Broker Halloway wasn't there at all; his chair bore a note in a neat hand: *Terms accepted. No ill will. May your god forgive what mine cannot.* Three others had gone in the night, slipping out with the tide like rats that had finally read the water correctly.

"They're calling it prudence," Vess said bitterly. "In the market squares they're calling it salvation. Corvane's criers are already on the corners: *open the gate, keep your homes.*" She spat. "He bought half of us for less than the cost of one siege engine. We taught this city that everything has a price, and now we're dying of the lesson."

Sable's chair was also empty. It had been empty for six days, since the raid — since the night Veyl's voice had come up through Petra's own throat and spoken a warning none of them understood, and in the chaos of that night the usurper's men had taken the guild's handler away. No ransom demand. No body. Just absence, shaped exactly like a message.

"We don't abandon our people," Petra said. "That's the whole of the law. Everything else is commentary."

"The law doesn't hold walls," Vess said. "Soldiers do. And half our soldiers—" She stopped, because the door opened.

Ilse came in carrying the chapel records bound in sailcloth, and the room's argument died around her the way noise dies around a struck bell. She set the books down in front of Petra, and her hands were shaking, and Petra understood: Ilse had felt the absence in her own head for six days. A hole where Petra should be. She had described it once, quietly, at three in the morning — *like a tooth gone, my tongue keeps going back to the gap* — and then she had smiled and said the strangest part was how much of her still loved Petra anyway, love persisting past its own evidence, a debt recorded in a ledger that had been stolen.

"Read the marginalia," Ilse said. "Page ninety-one. The founder's hand, the night before she sealed the god."

Petra opened the book. The founder's script was small and ruthless, a bookkeeper's hand to the last:

*The vessel will be offered a bargain. It will always be offered a bargain. This is how the seal is tested — not by force, which the silence resists, but by arithmetic. Count what you love. The enemy will count it too, and cheaper.*

Beneath it, in fresher ink — ink Petra recognized with a lurch as her own, the same hand that had written tomorrow's date in a century-old prophecy — someone had added:

*Cheaper than I will.*

Vess looked from the page to Petra and back. "What does that mean?"

"It means," Petra said, closing the book, "that Corvane thinks he's setting the price. Get me Ostrek and the gate-captains. And find out which of our defectors took ship tonight — I want their departure times."

"Why?"

"Because," Petra said, and heard the god stir beneath her ribs like a current under ice, "a ledger cuts both ways. He's been reading ours for years. Tomorrow he learns I've been reading his."

* * *

She found Corvane's answer waiting at midnight, delivered not by herald but by consequence.

A messenger from the northern quarter, mud to the knees: the warehouse of Broker Halloway, the one who had defected, was burning. Not an accident. Inside, when the watch fought through, they found the man himself — dead, and in his mouth, wedged between his teeth, a single coin.

Usurper's silver. And stamped on its face, worn nearly smooth but unmistakable to anyone who had spent years among the guild's oldest records: the sigil of Veyl. The silent god's mark, minted on the enemy's coin, as if the usurper owned the god already. As if the silence itself were his property, purchased and pending.

Petra stood in the wet street watching the warehouse gutter out, and the clarity she had been circling for weeks finally arrived, cold and complete, like water closing over her head.

Corvane hadn't sent the coin as a threat. He'd sent it as a *receipt*. Proof that he understood exactly what he was buying — proof that he knew the god's mark, knew the seal, knew the vessel's true worth better than her own guild did. He was telling her: *I know what you are. The bargain is real. So is the alternative.*

And somewhere in his camp, in a tent that smelled of ink and iron, Sable was alive — Corvane needed her alive, needed her knowledge of the vaults — and every hour Petra delayed was an hour Sable paid for in whatever currency spymasters preferred.

Ilse found her there, in the smoke and the hissing embers, and did not touch her, because some griefs are load-bearing and cannot be leaned on.

"Tomorrow you answer him," Ilse said. "What will you say?"

Petra looked east, where the horizon over the usurper's camp glowed faintly, forty thousand fires banked but not out. Somewhere beyond that glow lay the old war, sleeping in the salt and the stone, waiting for a voice. Somewhere in it, a name she had never spoken aloud, that lived behind her teeth like a splinter working toward the surface.

"I'll say yes," Petra said quietly. "To everything. To all of it at once."

Ilse's breath caught. "Petra—"

"Not surrender." She turned, and in the ember-light Ilse saw what the god had shown her in the raid's chaos, the thing the warning had meant: that the seal was not a wall but a door, and doors swing both ways, and the founder had built the lock assuming the vessel would always choose silence. "He thinks I'm choosing between the city and the god. Between Ilse and Ilse. He's read every ledger except the one being written right now, tonight, in this street, in my hand."

Above them, on the guildhouse tower, the lamp burned. And beneath the lamp, in the vault, on a shelf in the dark, a century-old book of prophecies lay open to a page that had been blank since the founder died.

By morning, it wasn't.
