## 10. A New Ledger

Ilse counted the dead the way she had once counted memories: by weight.

They lay in rows along the Salt Gate road, wrapped in sailcloth because there was no linen left in the free cities that hadn't been torn for bandages. The usurper's host had broken against the walls at dawn and withdrawn by dusk, not defeated—Corvane's riders still circled the hills like crows waiting for something to die—but stopped. Stopped was a word no one had dared use in months. Stopped meant tomorrow. Tomorrow meant you had to decide what to do with it.

She found Petra at sunset, or what remained of her.

The vessel of Veyl sat on the steps of the guildhouse with a blanket around her shoulders and rain drying in her hair, watching the harbor fires with an expression Ilse had catalogued ten thousand times across ten thousand traded memories: the look of someone standing in an empty room trying to remember what furniture had been there. Sable knelt beside her, feeding her watered wine, and when Ilse came close, Sable looked up with eyes gone red-rimmed and old.

"She keeps asking for the ledgers," Sable said quietly. "Not for anyone. Not for herself. Just the ledgers."

"I know."

"Do you?" Sable's voice cracked on it. "Because I stood on that wall too, Ilse. I saw what she paid. She didn't speak the name and walk away whole. She spoke it and *emptied*—"

"The seal required payment." Ilse kept her voice level, ledger-level, the voice she used for clients weeping over bottled grief. "The prophecy named the beloved as first payment. It did not say whose beloved would be taken from whom." She looked at the woman on the steps—the straight back, the ink-stained fingers, the face turned toward the sea as if it were simply weather. "Petra chose. She chose before any of us understood there was a choice. That has to be enough."

"It isn't."

"No," Ilse said. "It isn't. But it's what we have."

She descended the steps and sat beside Petra, close enough that their shoulders almost touched, far enough that it wouldn't matter if they never did again.

"You're the memory-keeper," Petra said. Her voice was flat and clean, like a freshly ruled page. "They told me. You keep the trades."

"I do."

"Then you'll have my accounts. Everything I owed, everything owed to me. The guild requires—" She stopped, frowning at her own hands. "I don't remember why the guild requires it. Isn't that strange? I know the rule but not the reason."

*The reason is me,* Ilse thought. *I'm the reason. Every rule in that building was written so that one day you could sit here hollow and still have somewhere to go.*

"Then we'll begin again," Ilse said aloud. "From the first entry. That's how all ledgers start."

Petra nodded, satisfied, and drank her wine, and asked nothing else about the woman sitting beside her, and Ilse sat in the rain-smelling dark next to the love of her life and introduced herself as though they had just met, which, for one of them, they had.

* * *

The cities held through autumn.

It was not peace. Corvane's banners stayed on the northern hills, and every week another broker's body turned up in a canal with usurper coin where his tongue had been, and half the Gilded Ledger's chapters beyond the walls went silent, bought or burned or simply afraid. But the Salt Gate stood. The harbors filled. Children who had learned to sleep in cellars learned, slowly, the luxury of beds.

In the guildhouse, work resumed, because impossible things kept being traded even at the edge of the world. A widow sold her grief by the spoonful to feed her daughters. A deserter bought a memory of courage he'd never earned and walked back north with it. A man paid three years of his own future for one hour with his drowned son, and Ilse logged the trade in Petra's careful hand and did not let herself think about what Petra would have said about the exchange rate.

Sable ran the brokers now, hard and suspicious, checking every coin for mint-marks. Maren came up from the river once a month, trading small kindnesses for small secrets, and always asked, in her fog-bound way, whether the vessel was sleeping well. Ilse always answered yes. It was even true, most nights.

And every night, Ilse climbed to the vault with a lamp and read the founder's prophecy book, the one that had started everything, and marked how much of it remained unfulfilled.

*On the last day of the war,* the final line read, in ink a century dry, *the vessel will choose, and the choice will hold until the book is closed.*

The book was not closed. Ilse checked the binding each night the way other people checked locks. And each night she found the pages a fraction heavier, the way a ledger grows heavy as debts accumulate, and she did not know what closing it would cost, or who would pay, or whether the war the founders had postponed for a hundred years was merely waiting for someone to turn the last page.

She kept reading anyway. Someone had to keep the accounts.

* * *

Petra woke before dawn, as she always did, though she no longer knew why dawn mattered to her. Some habits outlive their reasons. She dressed in the dark, bound her hair the way a soldier binds it rather than the way a clerk does, and noted the habit without understanding it, the way she noted the scar on her left forearm (blade, old, defensive) and the way her hand curled at rest as if around a sword hilt that wasn't there.

Her room contained one possession that made no sense: a blank book, bound in grey leather, of no guild issue. It had been in her room since the morning she woke on the wall with rain in her hair and a hole in her chest shaped like everything she'd ever loved. No one claimed it. Sable went pale when Petra showed it to her; the memory-keeper, Ilse, only said, very quietly, "Keep it safe," in a voice like someone setting down something breakable.

So Petra kept it safe. It was, after all, a ledger of sorts. Blank ledgers were debts waiting to happen. She understood that much about herself: she understood ledgers the way other people understood breathing.

She took it to the scriptorium, lit the lamps, and began the day's accounts. Grain levies. Broker commissions. The widow's grief, four spoons remaining. The deserter's borrowed courage, interest accruing. Her pen moved without hesitation. Numbers were honest. Numbers never woke up having forgotten why they mattered.

But this morning, when she finished the last entry, her hand did not stop.

It turned to the first blank page of the grey book instead, and wrote:

*On the last day of the war, a woman who cannot remember why will open a door that should stay shut.*

She stared at it. The words were in her handwriting. They were not words she remembered choosing. Beneath them, unbidden, her pen continued:

*She will be offered the price she already paid once, and she will pay it again, gladly, because she will not remember that gladness has a cost.*

*The keeper will try to stop her. The keeper will fail, because the keeper loves her, and love is the one debt that cannot be balanced.*

Petra set down the pen. Her heart was pounding in a way that had nothing to do with fear and everything to do with recognition—the vertigo of reading your own signature on a document you've never seen. Somewhere under the blankness, something was writing through her. Something patient. Something that had waited a hundred years between entries.

*Who are you?* she thought at it.

The answer arrived not as words but as a sensation: the feeling of a mark beneath her skin, cold and quiet, like a god holding its breath.

*The one who owes you,* it seemed to say. *The one you lent your voice to. We are not finished, you and I. There is a war to finish, and a book to close, and a debt outstanding between us that predates your birth.*

Petra looked at the prophecy taking shape under her hand—her fate, written by her own fingers, foretold by a woman a century dead and dictated by something older than empires—and she felt, beneath the emptiness where her loves had been, a single ember of the thing she'd been before: defiance, banked and patient, waiting for its season.

She dipped the pen.

Below the last line, in the space where a new prophecy begins, she wrote:

*Terms accepted.*

And outside, far to the north, on the hills above the usurper's camp, every banner snapped upright at once in a wind that had no source—and Corvane, spymaster, waking in his tent with the taste of old coins in his mouth, reached for his sword and whispered the first word of a name he had sworn never to say aloud.
