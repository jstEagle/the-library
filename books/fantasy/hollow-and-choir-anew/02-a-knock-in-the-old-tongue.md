## 2. A Knock in the Old Tongue

The storm came up the valley like something with a grudge.

Kestrel felt it before she heard it — a pressure behind her eyes, the same pressure she'd felt on the night everything ended, ten years and one lifetime ago. She was in the ledger room of the Guild of Impossible Things, third sub-basement, where the candles burned blue-green because the air down there had been breathed too many times. Outside, above ground, autumn was doing its ordinary business. Down here there was only ink, paper, and the particular silence of a woman who had spent a decade making sure no one said her name.

She was balancing the October entries when the first thunder hit.

The Guild of Impossible Things occupied a building that had been seven other buildings before it — a granary, a debtors' chapel, a private observatory, three things nobody could prove. Its ledgers were kept in the deepest room because paper remembers, and some of what the guild bought and sold was better remembered far from daylight. Kestrel ran the audit by hand. Machines could be read; hands could be trusted to lie in the right places. That was the arrangement. That had always been the arrangement.

Entry four hundred and eleven: *one dream, secondhand, sold to a widow in Corrow Lane, condition fair.*

Entry four hundred and twelve: *one shadow, unclaimed, held in escrow.*

Ordinary miracles. Ordinary money.

She was reaching for the sealing wax when the knock came.

Not at the guild's front door — that was forty feet of stone and two locked gates above her. The knock came at the ledger room door itself, which had no handle on the outside, which opened for exactly three people, none of whom knocked, because knocking implied the possibility of refusal and Master Sarn did not believe in refusal.

Three knocks. Slow. Spaced like someone counting.

Kestrel put down the wax and listened to her own heartbeat do arithmetic it didn't like.

*No one knocks,* she told herself. *No one can knock.*

The knocks came again, and this time she heard what was wrong with them. They weren't struck with knuckles. They were struck with something smaller, harder — a stone against oak, or a bone. And they fell in a rhythm she knew. Three beats, rest, two beats, rest, one long beat dragging into silence.

A rhythm out of the time before kingdoms. A rhythm from under the mountain.

Her hands were steady. She noticed that with a kind of distant professional approval, the way she'd once noted the barometric readings before a flood. Ten years of practice had built her body into an instrument that did not shake, and she hated it in that moment, hated the calm, because calm meant she'd rehearsed for this, and if she'd rehearsed for this then some part of her had always known it would come.

She opened the door.

The corridor beyond should have been empty. It was never not empty; nothing lived between the ledger room and the stairs except dust and the smell of cold tallow. But the girl standing there fit the corridor the way a key fits a lock it was cut for — small, maybe eight years old, barefoot, caked head to hem in wet clay so red it looked like she'd been birthed from the mountain itself. Her hair was plastered flat. Water ran off her in threads and pooled around her feet, and the pool did not spread. It stayed where it was, a neat dark coin of water, as if the floor had agreed to hold it.

Storm-water. From forty feet up and two locked gates away.

The girl looked up. Her eyes were the color of the ledger room candles, blue-green, breathed-too-many-times, and they went through Kestrel like a stylus through wet clay.

Then the girl spoke.

Not the trade-tongue. Not Low Cant or High Cant or the merchant pidgin of the river towns. She spoke the old language, the hollow language, the one with no word for *buy* and seventeen words for *owe*, the one that predated every kingdom because it had been old when the mountains were young enough to be argued with. Scholars called it pre-kingdom. The court beneath the mountain called it nothing, because it was simply the way things down there spoke, the way water speaks when it has been underground long enough.

*I have walked a long way,* the girl said. *My feet are borrowed and they hurt.*

Every word landed whole in Kestrel's chest. She understood it the way you understand your mother tongue — without translation, without mercy. It had been ten years since she'd heard it aloud. She had made herself forget it. She had been *very thorough* about forgetting it, and here it was anyway, rising in her like water finding its old level.

*You shouldn't be here,* Kestrel answered.

In the old tongue. Before she could stop herself. Before the decade of careful, practiced, load-bearing silence could get a hand up to block the door.

The words hung in the corridor. The girl's face did something complicated — not a smile, nothing so simple — and she said:

*You still speak it. Good. I was afraid I'd climbed all this way for a stranger.*

And then, in the voice of a child reporting the weather, in the language of the dead court beneath the world, she said the name.

Not *Kestrel*. Not Thackeray. Not any of the safe, invented, guild-registered syllables Kestrel had worn for ten years like a coat with the tags left on.

She said the name Kestrel had been born under, the name that had been entered into the court's great ledger before she could walk, the name she had traded away a piece of her soul to keep, the name that existed now in exactly one place in the world — a line of ink in a cathedral of stacked ledgers, a mile of rock overhead.

She said it perfectly. First try. The way you say a thing you've been carrying a long way and are very glad to set down.

The candle flames all leaned toward the girl at once, the way grass leans toward a thing about to fall on it.

Kestrel's steadiness broke. Not visibly — her hands stayed flat on the doorframe, her breathing stayed even, her face stayed the pleasant unremarkable mask she'd spent ten years carving. What broke was underneath, in the place where she kept the sealed room of herself. She heard, quite clearly, a door inside her own chest swing open on rusted hinges, and she heard everything she'd stored there shift, and settle, and begin very quietly to burn.

*Who told you that word?* she asked. Still in the old tongue. God help her, still answering.

*No one told me,* the girl said. *I remembered it. Remembering isn't telling.* She tilted her head, mud cracking along her jaw. *You gave them my name instead of yours. I've come to talk about the accounting.*

The water at her feet finally spread. It crossed the threshold of the ledger room and touched the toe of Kestrel's boot, and it was warm, blood-warm, and it smelled faintly of iron and candle smoke and a specific cellar Kestrel had spent ten years not dreaming about.

Somewhere above them, thunder rolled down the mountain's flank, and under the thunder — or perhaps wearing the thunder like a coat — Kestrel heard something else. A sound from deep in the rock, rhythmic and patient. Three beats. Rest. Two beats. One long beat dragging into silence.

The mountain, keeping time. Or counting.

*Come inside,* Kestrel heard herself say, because the alternative was leaving the child standing in a corridor that had no business admitting her, and because the ledger room had wards, and because — though she would not examine this until much later, and then only briefly — some starved part of her had heard the old language again after ten years and wanted, badly, shamefully, to keep hearing it.

The girl stepped over the threshold. The candles righted themselves. The door swung shut behind them both with the soft finality of a page turning, and Kestrel stood in her own sanctuary with a creature of the court beneath the mountain dripping storm-water onto her audit floor, and thought, with the last coherent corner of her mind:

*Sarn must never see her.*

It was the wrong thought. She would understand how wrong much later. But it was honest, and honesty was rare currency in the Guild of Impossible Things, so she let herself keep it.

*Sit,* she said in the trade-tongue, testing. The girl blinked at her, uncomprehending, and Kestrel's stomach dropped through ten floors of guild and into the bedrock. The child spoke only the old language. Which meant every conversation they would ever have would happen in the tongue of the place Kestrel had fled. Which meant every word between them would be a small trip home to the scene of the crime.

*There,* Kestrel tried, pointing at the stool, in the old tongue.

The girl sat. Obediently. Politely. Like a magistrate accepting a chair.

Up close, in good light, Kestrel could see what the mud had hidden. The girl's hands were wrong. Not damaged — *assembled*. The fingers sat together the way stones sit in a wall that has been rebuilt more than once, each one true in itself but fitted from different sources, and at the wrists, beneath the clay, there were faint seam-lines, fine as cartographer's strokes. As if someone — something — had put her back together from whatever pieces were available, working by feel, in the dark, with more patience than skill.

Kestrel had seen seams like that once before. On a tithe-collector's wrist, the night she bought her life. She had told herself for ten years that she'd imagined it.

*What are you?* she asked, and hated the question even as it left her, because it was the question of a clerk, and the girl deserved better, and also because she was afraid of the answer with her whole reassembled heart.

The girl considered her for a long moment. Rain hammered the world forty feet overhead. The candles burned their blue-green steady burn. And the child said, gently, in the language before kingdoms, in the voice of someone delivering news they've carried a long way and are tired of carrying:

*I'm what you left down there. I'm the part that didn't stay sold.*

Then she reached into the clay at her throat and drew out a small object on a cord — a token, a seal, pressed black wax gone hard as jet — and held it up to the candlelight, and Kestrel saw the mark stamped into it and felt the floor of the world tilt one degree and stop, politely, letting her adjust.

Three lines. A rest. Two lines. A long stroke trailing into nothing.

The seal of collection. The mark the magistrates pressed on a debt when they were ready to come for it.

On the guild door above them — though neither of them knew it yet — the same mark was already beginning to seep up through the oak like frost through a windowpane.

The girl turned the token over in her small, seamed, borrowed fingers and said, with terrible childish courtesy:

*They said to give you three days. I walked faster.*

* * *

Master Ovid Sarn found them ninety minutes later, because of course he did. The man had a nose for irregularities the way sharks had a nose for blood, and an unexplained child in the ledger room was the largest irregularity in the guild's recorded history.

He filled the doorway the way he filled every doorway — broadly, expensively, in a coat cut by a tailor who owed him money. His eyes went from Kestrel, to the girl, to the pool of storm-water that had somehow gotten past two locked gates and forty feet of stone, and Kestrel watched him perform the calculation she'd been dreading. She watched commerce arrive behind his eyes and take its seat.

"Well," Sarn said, in the tone he used for inventory. "And what have we here?"

The girl looked up at him and said nothing at all.

Kestrel stepped sideways, casually, into the space between them, and began — in a voice she kept admirably level — to explain that the child was lost, that she'd found her at the postern during the storm, that she'd brought her in out of the rain, that she'd take full responsibility.

Sarn smiled at her with all the warmth of a sealed envelope.

"Responsibility," he repeated, tasting it. "How unlike you, Thackeray."

Behind him, unnoticed by either of them, the girl raised one small seamed hand and touched the black token at her throat — and somewhere far below, patient as interest accruing, something began to count.
