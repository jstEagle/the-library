## 3. What the Guild Buys

Ovid Sarn had made a career of knowing what things cost before anyone else did.

It was the whole secret of the Guild of Impossible Things, though the guild's charter—inked two centuries back by a founder with more poetry than sense—said otherwise. The charter spoke of wonder. Of cataloguing marvels for posterity. Of preserving the impossible against the erosion of the ordinary. Sarn had read it once, early in his tenure, and then read the ledgers instead, which told the truth: the Guild of Impossible Things was a warehouse, and warehouses lived or died by one question only.

What does it fetch?

He was standing in the entry hall at dawn, watching rain come off the girl in sheets, and asking himself exactly that.

She stood on the wet flagstones like something the storm had coughed up. Mud to the knees, hair plastered flat, a dress that might once have been good wool and was now mostly river. She wasn't shivering. That was the first wrong thing. Any child caught out in weather like this would be blue-lipped and chattering, folded double against the cold. This girl stood straight as a fence post and looked at him—not at Thackeray, who crouched beside her murmuring in some language Sarn didn't recognize, but at *him*—with eyes that were too calm and too old and the color of water over slate.

The second wrong thing was the language. He'd heard Thackeray answer her before he could stop himself, quick and low, and Sarn had been in this trade long enough to know that Kestrel Thackeray did not do anything quickly or without stopping herself first. The woman was a ledger with legs. Every word she'd spoken in ten years had been entered twice and audited once.

"Thackeray," he said. "A moment."

She rose. Wiped her hands on her apron out of habit, though they were dry. "Guildmaster. She came up from the river road. Alone. She's maybe eight."

"I can see how old she is." He crossed the hall, boots ringing on stone, and stopped an arm's length from the child—the distance a man keeps from a dog he hasn't yet decided about. Up close she smelled of loam and cold iron, which was not the smell of any child who'd walked out of a flood. It was the smell of deep places. Of cellars that went down farther than the building above them could account for.

"What's your name?" he asked.

The girl looked at him and said nothing. Her mouth stayed closed, and when it opened slightly he saw that behind her teeth there was nothing—no sound ready, no shape of words waiting. Not stubbornness. Absence. Like a room swept clean before company arrives.

"She doesn't speak common," Thackeray said quietly. "Or if she does, she won't. She speaks—" A pause, precise as a knife finding a seam. "An older tongue. Pre-kingdom. I've only ever seen it written."

"You read it?"

"I recognized it."

Sarn filed that away. Thackeray gave him nothing for free and less than nothing when it mattered, and *recognized* was doing a great deal of work in that sentence. But the child was the business at hand, and Ovid Sarn had never once in his life let a side ledger distract him from the main account.

Because here was the thing. Here was the thing he had learned twenty years ago, as a junior factor, from a dying colleague named Ambrose Fell who had spent his last coherent hour telling Sarn about the trade nobody wrote down.

Souls sold to the court beneath the mountain sometimes walk back up.

Fell had been a tithe-broker in his youth—one of the men who carried offers down the tithe-road on behalf of desperate clients, back when the practice was common enough to have brokers and uncommon enough to hang them. He'd described the mechanics to Sarn in the flat voice of a man reciting inventory. A seller comes to the court owing something it cannot pay. The court accepts a substitute: a name, freely given, belonging to another. The named soul goes down. The debt is marked paid. And the seller walks free, lighter by one person and heavier by everything else.

"But they don't all stay down," Fell had said, gripping Sarn's wrist with a hand like a bundle of sticks. "The court is a ledger, boy, and ledgers have errors. Entries misfiled. Debts discharged twice. Once in a great while—a soul walks back up the tithe-road, wearing whatever body the court could assemble for it, and knocks on a door somewhere looking for what it lost." His eyes had gone bright and terrible. "And whoever opens that door holds merchandise the like of which has not crossed a counter in three hundred years. Because you know what a returned soul is?"

"A tragedy," young Sarn had said.

"A *discrepancy*," Fell had snapped. "The court does not tolerate discrepancies. It will send its magistrates to collect, and the magistrates will pay—oh, they'll pay anything—to close the account quietly. Gold. Charters. Forgiveness of debts that would bankrupt a kingdom. I have seen a man trade a returned soul for a pardon signed in the king's own hand. I have seen a woman trade one for ten years of her life back, given all at once, un-aged." He had slumped then, spent. "Find one, boy. Before someone finds it for you."

Ambrose Fell had died owing money to everyone in the city, and Sarn had inherited his files along with his debts, and in those files—buried under forty years of dust—was a page titled *On Returns*, in Fell's crabbed hand, listing the signs.

Signs he was now looking at, one after another, standing in his own entry hall.

The child who does not shiver. The eyes old past their years. The silence that is absence rather than refusal. The smell of deep earth. And the language—Fell had written this last, letters wobbling, as if the pen had been shaking: *they speak the old tongue, the pre-kingdom speech, because the court predates kingdoms and never learned a newer one.*

Thackeray had answered the girl in it.

Sarn turned the thought over slowly, the way he turned every acquisition, feeling for flaws. Ten years ago, Thackeray had appeared at the guild's door with a portfolio of maps so fine they'd made the senior cartographers weep, and no history whatsoever. No family name anyone could trace. No hometown. She'd bought her way in with skill and paid her rent in invisibility, and in ten years Sarn had learned exactly three things about her: she drew better than anyone alive, she kept the ledgers flawlessly, and she wanted, with a ferocity she mistook for mildness, to never be found.

People who wanted never to be found had usually sold something to stay hidden.

And here, dripping on his flagstones, was the thing they'd bought—or the thing they'd *paid with*, come back up the mountain to knock on the door of the person who'd sold it.

His mouth had gone dry. He covered it by walking, crossing to the window where the rain hammered the glass, giving himself the privacy of a turned back. Fear sat in his chest like swallowed ice. Because that was the other half of Fell's lesson, the half the old man had repeated twice, the half Sarn had never forgotten: *the magistrates follow the merchandise.* Wherever a returned soul surfaced, the court's collectors arrived within days—wearing faces like unfinished sketches, Fell had written, faces that kept changing because the court had never bothered to finish them—and they took the soul back, and they took something else besides, from whoever had harbored it. A memory. A year. A name. The court always collected the handling fee.

Every instinct Sarn owned screamed at him to put the girl back out into the rain and bar the door.

But fear, in Ovid Sarn's experience, was just greed wearing a disguise, and greed deserved to be examined honestly. He made himself stand at the window and count the value the way he counted everything: line by line.

A returned soul, offered back to the magistrates voluntarily, promptly, in good condition. What would that buy? Fell's notes listed pardons, gold, years of life, forgiveness of debts. Sarn owed debts. The guild owed debts—considerable ones, contracted quietly over six lean years, owed to creditors whose patience was not infinite and whose methods were not gentle. Six months ago he'd lain awake doing sums that ended in the guild's dissolution and quite possibly his own ruin. He had been turning over ugly options ever since: selling the map vault's contents piecemeal, mortgaging the building, selling *himself* down the tithe-road the way desperate men did, trading his own name for liquidity.

And now a discrepancy had walked up to his door in the rain and knocked.

He turned around. The girl hadn't moved. Water pooled around her feet in a spreading dark continent. Thackeray hovered near her with a blanket she hadn't yet managed to drape over those small straight shoulders, and there was something in the cartographer's face that Sarn had never seen there in ten years—something raw and lit from underneath, like a banked fire kicked open.

That, too, went into the ledger. Whatever Thackeray's history was, it was attached to this child, and attachments were levers, and levers were useful. He disliked needing that thought. He used it anyway.

"Thackeray," he said. "Take her down to the map vault."

Her head came up sharply. "The vault? She needs food, dry clothes, a physician—"

"She needs containing." He kept his voice level, reasonable, the voice he used for board meetings. "You said yourself she speaks a language that predates kingdoms. You said she came out of a flood with no escort, no belongings, and no explanation. Until I know what she is, she stays somewhere secure. The vault has one door, thick walls, and no windows. It's the safest room in this building—for her as much as anyone. If she's a runaway, we're sheltering her. If she's bait, we haven't taken the hook." He smiled, thin and dry. "Call it safekeeping."

Something moved behind Thackeray's eyes—protest, calculation, and then, oddly, a kind of relief, as if the vault were exactly where she'd feared he'd put the girl and also exactly where she wanted to keep watch herself. "I'll stay with her."

"You will. You'll see to her comfort." He let that sit a beat. "And you'll tell me, Thackeray—at your convenience, not mine—what language a pre-kingdom dead tongue is doing coming out of a living child on my doorstep, and why you know it." He raised a hand before she could draw breath. "Not now. Later. When she's settled. I'm not prying into your history; your history is your own and it's worth nothing to me. But this girl is my business, and I don't buy what I can't appraise."

"We're not selling her," Thackeray said. Flat. Final. The first time in ten years she'd told him anything flatly or finally.

"No one said selling." He spread his hands, all innocence, all commerce. "I said appraising. There's a difference, and the difference is that one of them requires a willing party on both sides of the table." Which was true, and also a lie, and he was comfortable holding both.

He watched Thackeray lead the girl away—down the corridor, toward the stair that spiraled into the vault's cold dark—and he noted that the child walked without being pulled, that she glanced back exactly once, at him, with those slate-water eyes, and that the look contained no fear at all. Only assessment. As if *she* were appraising *him*, and had found the price wanting.

When they were gone he climbed to his office, unlocked the bottom drawer of his desk with the small iron key he wore on a cord under his shirt, and took out Ambrose Fell's file. The paper had gone the color of old teeth. He laid *On Returns* flat on the desk and read it through again, slowly, lips moving, while the rain kept up its drumming on the roof like a creditor's knuckles.

*They fetch fortunes,* Fell had written. *But hear me, boy: the fortunes are real and so is the bill. The magistrates come within three days. Always three. They will ask politely. If you have the soul ready and the paperwork clean, they pay and go. If you haggle, they take. If you lie, they take more.*

Three days.

Sarn sat back and did the arithmetic he'd been avoiding since the entry hall. A returned soul, delivered promptly, could clear the guild's debts twice over. Could buy charters, protections, years. Could make the Guild of Impossible Things solvent for the rest of his natural life and endow it beyond his death. All he had to do was hold the merchandise for three days, keep it quiet, keep it contained, and negotiate from strength when the sketch-faced gentlemen arrived.

All he had to do was lock a child in a cellar and wait for the things that were coming for her.

He found, examining himself carefully the way he examined all assets, that he felt very little about this. A flicker of distaste, perhaps—the same flicker he felt when he sold a distressed property out from under a widow, necessary and unpleasant, the cost of doing business in an expensive world. He was not a monster. Monsters enjoyed it. He merely understood that the world presented invoices whether you approved of them or not, and that a man who flinched at paying them died poor, and a guild that flinched died dissolved, and everyone in it scattered into the rain.

Besides—he told himself this last, the way a man tells himself the last lie of the night, the one that lets him sleep—besides, the girl was going back down regardless. The magistrates would come whether he profited or not. The only question was whether the guild got paid for the trouble of her keeping. He wasn't selling her. He was charging storage.

Down in the vault, behind four feet of stone, the girl who did not shiver would be warm enough. Fed enough. Safe enough, until the buyers came.

He blew out the lamp, and in the dark he caught himself listening—listening past the rain, past the settling timbers, down through the floors to the cold heart of the building—for the sound of a small voice speaking a language older than kingdoms.

He heard nothing. That was somehow worse.

* * *

By evening the storm had spent itself and the city dripped and steamed, and Sarn had drafted, burned, and redrafted the letter he intended to send. To whom, he wasn't sure—that was the trouble with the court beneath the mountain; it had no address, no office, no clerk you could write to. Contact happened on its terms or not at all. So the letter became a memorandum instead, for his own files, laying out the situation in the dispassionate language of inventory:

*One (1) item recovered this date, female, approx. eight years, condition remarkable. Provenance presumed subterranean. Authentication pending. Holding period commenced. Offers solicited.*

He signed it, sealed it, and was reaching for the candle to melt the wax when he heard the knock.

Not at the street door. At the *vault* door—which was four floors down, behind two locked gates, in a corridor where no visitor had any cause to be. Three knocks, evenly spaced, patient as a metronome. Then silence. Then three more.

Sarn sat very still. The candle flame leaned in a draft that had no source.

He told himself it was pipes. Old buildings knocked; the guild was older than most; the frost and thaw of a storm night made stone tick and groan like a ship. He told himself this with the fluency of long practice, because he had been telling himself comforting lies about this building for twenty years, and he was good at it.

Then, faintly, up through the floor, he heard Thackeray's voice—low, urgent, answering someone—and beneath it, thinner than a wire, a child's voice making sounds that were not words in any language Sarn knew. Sounds like roots growing. Like stone remembering it had once been molten.

The magistrates did not come for three days, Fell had written.

They had never specified that they always announced themselves at the front door.

Ovid Sarn snuffed the candle with his fingers, sat in the dark with his unsigned memorandum, and began, for the first time in twenty years, to be afraid of his own inventory.
