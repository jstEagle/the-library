## 4. Ten Years of Blank Pages

Sleep had never been a place Kestrel Thackeray could afford to visit unarmed.

She kept her bed narrow and her room bare, the way you'd keep a boat: nothing loose to catch the wind, nothing to remind her of anywhere else. On the wall above the headboard she'd once pinned a map of the city—her own work, flawless, every street named in her small precise hand—and then she'd taken it down again within the week because looking at it felt like being watched by someone who knew her. Now the wall held nothing. A blank page, she'd told herself. A page nobody would bother to read.

She lay awake that night after the audit, after the entry she hadn't written, and let the memory come the way it always came—not all at once but in pieces, like a map delivered in installments by a cruel cartographer. First the mountain. Then the dark under it. Then Wren's hand in hers, going cold not from death but from something worse: from being *unwritten*.

Ten years ago tonight, give or take a season. The court beneath the mountain did not keep calendars. It kept ledgers.

* * *

They had gone down as children do—because they were told, because the tithe-road opened at the edge of their village every seventh winter and refusing was not among the options anyone had ever thought to invent. The village elders called it an honor. The mothers called it nothing at all, because speaking about it invited the magistrates, and the magistrates wore faces like sketches someone had abandoned halfway through—a jaw suggested, an eye implied—and no one wanted to be the detail they came back to finish.

Kestrel had been eleven. Wren had been ten, and small for it, with a laugh like water finding its way downhill and a habit of naming things the adults had never bothered to name. Not people—things. The crooked fence post was Old Bendy. The puddle outside the smithy was the Sea of Hammerwater. She named everything because, she said, things you named couldn't be taken from you without your knowing exactly what you'd lost.

Kestrel understood later how wrong she'd been. Or rather, how right, in the way that cuts deepest.

The descent took three days. The mountain ate sound first—that was the toll everyone knew about, the one sung in warning songs. By the second day you couldn't hear your own footsteps, and the children learned to talk with their hands, and the silence pressed in like deep water. Then the mountain began on memory. That was the part the songs left vague, because the songs themselves were made of memories and the mountain took its cut at the source.

Kestrel lost her mother's face on the second night. She remembered losing it—the sensation of reaching for it the way you'd reach for a word on the tip of your tongue, and finding smooth stone where the word should have been. She wept without sound, because sound was already gone, and Wren held her hand in the dark and squeezed twice, which in their private language meant *I'm here, I see you, you're still real.*

On the third day they reached the court.

It was a cathedral built of stacked ledgers—she saw that even then, even at eleven, with the eye that would later make her famous in a small professional way. Shelves rose into darkness beyond the reach of any light, and every shelf was crammed with books, and the books breathed. Slowly. In unison. Like a sleeping animal the size of a god.

The magistrates met them at the doors. There were nine of them, or perhaps one of them nine times; their unfinished faces made counting feel like guessing. The tallest carried a quill longer than Kestrel's arm, and when it moved, somewhere in the breathing dark above, a page turned.

*Tithe,* said the magistrate, in the old tongue, the pre-kingdom tongue, the language of roots and before. Every child from the villages spoke it until the age of seven, and then forgot it, the way you forget the womb. *One name per child. Freely rendered.*

That was the cruelty of it, Kestrel would understand much later. Not freely given—freely *rendered*, which is a different thing entirely, the way surrender is different from gift. The court wanted you to sign yourself. It wanted the ink in your own hand. A stolen name was only theft, and theft could be prosecuted, avenged, undone. A sold name was a contract, and contracts were sacred down there in the dark where the books breathed.

The other children went forward one by one. Kestrel watched a boy she'd known all her life open his mouth and speak his own name into the magistrate's quill, and she watched the name leave him—not dramatically, not with light or thunder. He simply became slightly harder to look at. Slightly harder to remember. By the time he stepped back into line, his own mother would have walked past him in a market.

Wren went next. And this was the moment, the hinge, the terrible door swinging on its rusted pin: Wren opened her mouth, and hesitated.

Only a heartbeat. Only long enough to look back at Kestrel and squeeze her hand twice—*I'm here, I see you*—and in that heartbeat the tallest magistrate turned its unfinished face toward Kestrel, and the quill lifted, and a voice like a page turning said:

*This one has two names.*

And it was true. It had been true since they were small enough to share a blanket during the lambing cold. Wren, who named everything so nothing could be lost unnamed, had given Kestrel a second name years ago—a secret one, a root-name, spoken in the old tongue against her ear: *Sorrel*. A name for the person Wren decided Kestrel really was, underneath the frightened girl everyone else saw. And names spoken in love are still names. The court's ledger knew. The court's ledger always knew.

*Two names,* the magistrate repeated, with what might have been, in a finished face, hunger. *One may go up. One name must remain.*

Kestrel did not remember deciding. That was the lie she'd told herself for ten years, the comfortable fiction that there had been a moment, a choice, a villain inside her who acted while the real Kestrel stood frozen. But there was no such moment. There was only the dark, and the breathing books, and Wren's hand going slack in hers, and her own mouth opening, and the old tongue—which she had sworn she'd forgotten—rising out of her like water finding its level:

*Sorrel stays. Take hers instead. Take Wren.*

The quill moved. Somewhere above, a page turned. And Wren looked at her—not with hatred, that was the unbearable part, not even with understanding—with the simple blank surprise of someone watching a door close, and then the blankness spread, and the hand that had squeezed hers twice hung at her side like something belonging to a stranger, and the magistrates parted, and a stair appeared that had not been there before, going up, going toward air and sound and the rest of her life.

She climbed it alone. All the other children climbed out together into the grey morning, blinking, lighter by one name each, and none of them could say why one of their number stayed behind, or who she had been, because there was no longer anyone to remember. The mountain had eaten the memory of Wren from every mind except the mind that had sold her.

Guilt makes its own arrangements. Hers was to become invisible.

* * *

Kestrel surfaced from the memory the way a drowning woman surfaces—too fast, gasping, the room tilting around her. The candle had burned to a thumb's width. Below, through the floorboards, she could hear the guild settling into its nighttime creaks, Ovid Sarn locking the strongroom, the apprentices' snores rising and falling like tide.

Invisible. She had built it brick by brick, year by year, and she knew the architecture better than any building she'd ever mapped.

First she'd buried the old tongue. That had been easy; grief is an excellent teacher of forgetting. Then the face—she'd taught herself to stand at the exact angle where lamplight missed her, to walk at the pace that made eyes slide off, to answer questions with answers so precisely adequate that no follow-up ever came. *Thackeray,* she'd told the guild when she applied, and it was nearly true, a half-sister's surname, borrowed the way you borrow a coat from a corpse. *Kestrel.* A bird name. A name that meant nothing to anyone, chosen because kestrels hover—hang motionless in the wind, watching the ground for small moving things—and then strike.

She'd joined the Guild of Impossible Things because it was the last place anyone would look for a woman trying not to exist. An organization devoted to cataloguing miracles attracted believers, and believers looked *up*. Nobody looks up for a bookkeeper.

But the ledgers—ah, the ledgers. That was the confession she never spoke aloud, the sin beneath the survival. She loved them. Ten years of entries, each one a small act of erasure performed on herself: *K. Thackeray, surveyor, fee received. K. Thackeray, correction to chart 44, no comment. K. Thackeray, absent from feast, reason unspecified.* She had written herself into smaller and smaller type until she was practically a footnote in her own life, and the terrifying truth, the truth she admitted only on nights like this one, staring at the ceiling of her deliberately bare room, was that some part of her had *liked* it. Vanishing was painless. Vanishing was safe. Guilt had calcified into habit, habit into craft, craft into identity, and if you had asked her—who are you?—the honest answer was: whoever is hardest to find.

Until tonight. Until an entry dated ten years ago, in ink she didn't own, in a hand that was almost—but not quite—her own:

**Debt incurred below. One (1) name, sold. Status: PAID IN FULL.**

Paid in full. As if the transaction had finally cleared. As if something sold a decade ago had finished traveling, had arrived, had come *back up*.

She got out of bed, dressed in the dark with the automatic precision of a woman who had practiced not existing until it was a martial art, and went down to the ledger room. The lamp there burned low and green behind its glass shade. She unlocked the cabinet with hands that were almost steady, drew out the master ledger, and turned to the impossible entry.

There it was. Same date as the descent—she checked three times, her finger trembling on the numerals. Same date. Same ink-black depth of letters. And beneath PAID IN FULL, so faint she had to tilt the page to catch it, a watermark that hadn't been visible in the audit room's flat light:

A sketch of a face. Unfinished. A jaw suggested, an eye implied.

The court's seal. The mark of the magistrates.

Paid in full meant collected. Collected meant something had come up the tithe-road, out of the dark, bearing the balance of a ten-year-old account. And there was only one account. There had only ever been one.

Kestrel stood in the green lamplight holding the proof of her crime, and felt the two halves of her life grind against each other like millstones: the woman who had spent a decade becoming no one, and the eleven-year-old who had traded her sister's soul for a staircase, both of them suddenly, horribly, awake in the same body.

Somewhere above her, muffled by floors and distance, the guild's front bell rang—one clear note, absurd at this hour, in this storm.

She closed the ledger. She did not run to the door. Running would mean hoping, and hoping was a debt she'd sworn off years ago.

But she climbed the stairs faster than invisibility allowed.
