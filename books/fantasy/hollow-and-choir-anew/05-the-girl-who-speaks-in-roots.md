## 5. The Girl Who Speaks in Roots

The map vault smelled of cedar oil and old mistakes.

Kestrel had spent ten years learning to be no one, and the first rule of being no one was this: never go where you are not needed. But she went down the spiral stair that night anyway, past the sleeping dormitory, past the ink room with its locked cabinets of guild-grade pigment, down to the iron door at the bottom of everything, where Ovid Sarn had put the girl like a coin into a purse.

She told herself it was an audit. She was the keeper of ledgers; the guild's assets were her responsibility; a child was an asset now, apparently, and someone had to count her.

The lie was thin enough to see through. She walked through it anyway.

* * *

The vault was a round room cut from the mountain's own bone, shelved floor to ceiling with maps of places that had stopped existing. That was the Guild of Impossible Things' real trade—not selling wonders but cataloguing their corpses. A chart of the drowned city of Vell. A survey of the road that used to run between here and Harrow's Ford before the road decided otherwise. Maps were how the guild remembered what the world insisted on forgetting, and Kestrel had always found a cold comfort in that. She was, herself, a place that refused to exist. She understood the genre.

The girl sat on a stool between the shelves, exactly where Sarn's bailiff had left her, hands folded in her lap like a small, patient debt. Someone had given her bread and a blanket. The blanket was guild-issue gray. The bread was untouched.

Kestrel closed the door behind her and stood there like a fool for three full breaths.

"You should eat," she said in Common, and then, because the girl only tilted her head, she said it again in the other language—the one with no kingdom attached to it, the one that came up out of the ground before anyone thought to build walls or write taxes. *You should eat. No one will take anything from you tonight.*

The girl answered in roots and riverwater. *I ate what the mountain gave me. It was enough.*

It was never enough. That was the whole theology of the Hollow Choir—enough, enough, until the tithe came due—and hearing the grammar of it again after ten years was like pressing a tongue to a broken tooth. Kestrel crossed the room, sat on the stool opposite, and set the bread in the girl's hands.

"Eat," she said, in both languages. "Please."

The girl ate. Small bites, deliberate, the way you eat when you have learned that food can be taken away mid-meal. Her eyes never left Kestrel's face, and there was something in them that Kestrel refused, point-blank, to name. Recognition without memory. A door opened onto a room that had been bricked over so long ago even the bricks had forgotten.

"I'm going to teach you words," Kestrel said. "Common speech. Enough to keep you alive up here." She didn't know yet why she'd decided this. She suspected she'd known since the knock. "And you're going to teach me what you are."

*What I am,* the girl repeated, tasting the shape of the question. *I am the last page.*

"We'll start smaller," Kestrel said, and her voice did not shake, which she counted as the night's single victory.

* * *

They worked by lamplight while the storm above them worked the city.

Kestrel had taught before—a decade ago, another life, another name—but never like this, never as penance. She started with objects because objects were honest. *Lamp.* *Shelf.* *Stone.* The girl repeated each word back with an accent like water finding its way downhill, patient, unhurried, wearing the Common tongue the way you wear borrowed clothes: correctly, and without ever pretending they were yours.

*Stone,* the girl said, touching the wall. Then, in the old language: *the mountain's tooth.*

"Stone," Kestrel agreed. "And yes. The mountain's tooth."

*Map,* the girl said next, because the shelves were full of them, and then she said something longer, a full sentence of the pre-kingdom tongue, and Kestrel's hands went still on the lamp.

*These are pictures of dead places. Who keeps a grave-room?*

"A guild," Kestrel said. "We remember things the world wants to forget."

*Why?*

Because we're afraid of becoming one of them. Because remembering is the only weapon the erased are allowed. Because—"Because it pays," Kestrel said, which was true, and safer than the rest.

The girl considered her for a long moment. Then she reached out and touched Kestrel's wrist, two fingers, light as ash settling. And she spoke slowly, deliberately, in the old language, choosing each root-word like a stone for a wall:

*You speak my tongue like someone who left it in a hurry.*

Kestrel pulled her hand back. The lamp guttered. Somewhere above them, thunder rolled off the mountain's shoulder and the whole vault hummed with it, the maps shivering in their tubes like a flock of startled birds.

"My mother's tongue," Kestrel said. "I kept a little."

*Liar,* said the girl, gently, in the way of children and mountains, who do not know they are being cruel. *You speak it like the choir speaks. Like the ones below. You were tithed.*

The word went through her like a nail through vellum.

"No," Kestrel said.

*Yes,* the girl said. *That is why I climbed. Not for the guild. Not for the gray man with his scales. For you. You are the debt I was sent to collect.*

* * *

Kestrel made herself stay. That was the thing she would remember afterward—not the words, though the words were bad enough, but the staying. Every instinct she had honed across ten blank years screamed at her to stand, to leave, to lock the door on her side of it and let Sarn's commerce and the court's patience fight it out over a child she had never met. Running was the one skill she had perfected. She had a whole architecture of escape built into her days: three exits from every room, two names in every city, nothing owned that couldn't be burned.

She stayed on the stool. She picked up the lamp. She said, "Tell me your name."

*I don't have one,* the girl said. *That is why I am here. My name is below. I am what walks when a name is sold and the buyer forgets to burn the rest.*

"Names don't get sold," Kestrel said. "That's a story. That's a story mothers tell children so they'll come home before dark."

*Your mother told you that story too,* the girl said. *Before dark, before dark. And then you went below anyway, and you came home, and someone else didn't.*

The lamp flame stood perfectly still, as if listening.

"What was taken?" Kestrel asked. Her voice came out flat, professional, the voice she used for inventory. Underneath it, something was screaming in a language older than kingdoms. "When you climbed. What did you climb out of?"

*Dark,* the girl said. *Stacked dark. Paper dark. A cathedral of counting. I was folded there like a letter no one reads. And then the letter was read, and the reading said: unpaid balance, one soul, final notice, send it up. So I was sent up. Assembled as I climbed—from scraps, from what the dark didn't swallow. Roots for bones. Riverwater for blood. A little of everyone the court has eaten, stirred together, shaped around the shape of the name I used to be.*

"And what name was that?"

The girl looked at her. And Kestrel saw it coming the way you see a wave coming—plenty of time to move, nowhere to go.

*Wren,* the girl said.

The word landed in the vault and did not echo. Vaults are built to hold sound the way they hold paper: tightly, forever.

"Wren," Kestrel repeated, in Common, because she could not say it in the old language, because in the old language the name was a key and saying it would open something in her chest that she had welded shut at nineteen years old with her own hands, by torchlight, in front of a magistrate whose face was only half-finished, whose half-face had smiled at her with terrible courtesy while she traded—

While she traded.

*You knew her,* the girl said. Not a question. The old tongue didn't really do questions; it did invitations, and refusals were expensive. *Your face is doing what faces do when they meet a name they buried.*

"She was my sister," Kestrel said. "Not by blood. By choice, which is the stronger kind. We grew up in the same hollow. When the collectors came through the valley—we were both taken. Both tithed. And the court lets you buy passage out, if you have something to trade." She heard her own voice going somewhere distant, ledger-flat, reciting. "A name of equal weight. Freely given. Those were the terms. Fair terms. The court is very fair. That's the horror of it—it never cheats you. It just waits for you to cheat yourself."

*And you gave hers.*

"I gave hers." Ten years of silence cracked open all at once, and out of it came the whole confession, fast, ugly, unedited, because if she slowed down she would stop. "I told myself it would be temporary. I told myself I'd earn the money, buy the name back, that names were fungible, that the ledger would balance eventually. I was nineteen and I wanted to live more than I wanted to be good. They wrote her name into the great book and wrote mine out of the debt column, and they let me walk up the tithe-road alone, and every step I told myself I was coming back for her." She laughed, and it was a terrible sound in that room of dead maps. "I mapped my way to a new city instead. I got very good at drawing coastlines. I became no one, because no one can't be collected from. And I called it survival for so long that I almost believed it wasn't cowardice."

The girl—Ash, she was already thinking of her as Ash, ash being what remains, ash being honest—watched her with those assembled eyes.

*You ran well,* she said. *Ten years is a long climb to make in the wrong direction.*

"I know."

*But you stopped running tonight. You came down here. Why?*

Because the audit showed a debt marked PAID IN FULL that she hadn't paid. Because a child had knocked on the guild door speaking a dead language and called her by a name she'd buried deeper than any body. Because the ledger always opens both ways, and hers had been open for a decade, waiting.

"Because I owed you," Kestrel said. "Before you knocked. Before you climbed. I've owed you since the torchlight and the half-faced magistrate and the sound a name makes when it's written out of the world. I owe you a name, Wren. And I intend to pay."

The girl shook her head slowly, and there was something heartbreakingly adult in the motion, something that had been assembled from scraps and therefore understood, better than any child should, how scraps are spent.

*Do not promise the court's arithmetic,* she said. *It always balances. That is its cruelty. Whatever you offer, it will take exactly that, and it will call the taking fair.*

"Then I'll offer carefully."

*No one offers carefully below. The Quill writes faster than caution.* The girl drew her knees up onto the stool, making herself small, making herself a knot of gray blanket and riverwater eyes. *Listen. I did not climb ten years through the dark to watch you sign yourself away. I climbed to deliver a notice. That is all I am—a letter. Read me, and be done with me.*

"You're not a letter."

*I am what's left.*

"You're a child sitting on a stool eating bread in a map vault," Kestrel said, "and you deserve a name that isn't a debt. All of you. Even the scraps. Especially the scraps." She paused, and then, because tenderness and dread had become, somewhere in the last hour, the same sensation in her chest, she added: "I'm going to call you Ash. Until we get yours back. Ash, because it's what survives the fire. Ash, because it's honest."

The girl turned the word over. *Ash,* she said, in Common, in that downhill-water accent. Then in the old tongue: *what remains. What remembers the burning.*

"That's right."

*It is a good name,* the girl said gravely. *It will do for walking.*

* * *

They worked until the storm wore itself out against the mountain—words, mostly, small load-bearing words. *Door. Stair. Cold. Warm. Run. Hide. Trust.* Kestrel drilled her on the ones that mattered for staying alive in a city that had just learned what she was worth. The girl learned fast, faster than any pupil Kestrel had ever taught, and Kestrel tried not to think about why—about what it meant that a soul assembled from scraps could fit language into itself so easily. A vessel built to carry one thing learns quickly. A collection notice arrives complete.

Near the end, Ash touched the nearest shelf, the oldest tube, and asked, in careful Common: *"Dead places. Do you draw living ones?"*

"Sometimes."

*"Can you draw a place that does not want to be drawn?"*

Kestrel looked at her for a long moment. "Why?"

*"Because below," Ash said, "is a place like that. And I think you will need a map. And I think"—her fingers curled, roots for bones, gripping the shelf—"that the map will cost you. The court's roads always cost. But I think you already know that, because you have been paying for ten years, in a currency no one audits."*

Kestrel opened her mouth to say something—she never learned afterward what—and stopped.

Because above them, muffled by four floors of guild and stone, a bell began to ring. Not the storm bell. Not the market bell. The visitor bell, the one that rang only for people who arrived without knocking, and it rang twice, politely, the way you ring when you already know the door will open.

Ash went very still. In the old language, barely a breath: *They are early.*

"Who's early?" Kestrel asked, though the cold climbing her spine had already answered.

*The magistrates,* Ash said. *The collectors. They have come to see whether the notice was delivered—and to measure what else the house owes.*

The lamp flame bent sideways, though there was no draft, and somewhere overhead, boots were crossing the guild's front hall with the unhurried patience of paperwork.
