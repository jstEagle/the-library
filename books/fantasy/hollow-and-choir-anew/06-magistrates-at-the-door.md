## 6. Magistrates at the Door

Ovid Sarn had survived forty years in the trade of impossible things by following one rule above all others: never let them see you count.

Not the guild's creditors. Not the city assessors who came sniffing after the solstice taxes. Not even the quiet men in grey who occasionally appeared in his office to ask, very politely, whether the Guild of Impossible Things had any interest in acquiring certain items of a certain provenance. You let your face go soft and pleasant and slightly bored, the face of a man thinking about lunch, and you did all your arithmetic behind it.

He was doing a great deal of arithmetic this morning, and none of it was coming out clean.

The girl sat in the map vault two floors down, behind a door with three locks and a warding sigil that cost him more per year than his clerk's salary. She had eaten nothing since yesterday. She had said nothing since yesterday — she couldn't, not in any tongue the city recognized — but she watched. That was the trouble with her. Merchandise that watched back was merchandise that got ideas about itself, and souls that got ideas about themselves were notoriously difficult to sell.

*Difficult,* he corrected himself. Not impossible. Nothing was impossible; that was rather the point of the guild. It was only a question of price, and price was only a question of patience, and patience was the one commodity Ovid Sarn possessed in genuinely ruinous quantities.

He was reviewing the girl's provisional entry in the acquisition ledger — *one (1) returned soul, condition: assembled, provenance: undermountain court, valuation: pending* — when the light in the ledger room went wrong.

It didn't dim, exactly. It went *thin*, the way ink goes thin when you add water, as though someone had diluted the morning itself. The lamp on his desk burned with a smaller flame than it had a moment before. The dust motes stopped moving mid-air, hung there like punctuation, and then resumed, slightly out of order.

Sarn put down his pen.

In forty years he had learned to read the guild's moods the way sailors read weather, and the building had just gone quiet in the specific way it always went quiet before something arrived that the walls didn't want to admit but couldn't refuse.

The knock came from the street door. Three strikes, evenly spaced, each one landing not on wood but on some deeper timber of the place, so that Sarn felt it in the floor through the soles of his boots and in his teeth through his jaw.

He checked his reflection in the dark window glass. Soft face. Pleasant. Slightly bored. A man thinking about lunch.

He went down to answer it.

* * *

They stood on the guild's front step in the flat grey light of a morning that seemed to have been arranged around them rather than the other way round.

There were two of them, which was correct — tithe-magistrates always came in pairs, because a debt required a witness and a collector, and neither could be trusted to be both. They wore robes the colour of wet slate, unadorned, without sigil or seal, and they stood with the perfect stillness of things that had never once needed to shift their weight.

Their faces were the problem. Their faces were always the problem.

They looked like unfinished sketches. Not blank — blank would have been a mercy, blank would have been nothing to look at. These faces had the *beginnings* of features: an eye suggested by a single confident line, a mouth indicated but not committed to, a jaw that faded out entirely below the ear as though the artist had been called away. And the lines moved. Slowly, subtly, the way a drawing shifts when the paper breathes. When Sarn looked away and looked back, the magistrates' noses were in slightly different places.

"Guildmaster." The taller one spoke first. Its voice was dry and even, like a page turning. "We are here regarding an item of collection."

"Good morning," Sarn said warmly, because warmth was armor, and stepped aside to admit them. "Come in, come in. Terrible weather for official business."

There was no terrible weather. The day was mild and cloudless. The taller magistrate tilted its half-drawn head a degree, considering this, and then both of them crossed the threshold in perfect step, and the light thinned again around them, and Sarn felt the old cold crawl up the back of his neck — the cold of a man standing next to something the world had only partially finished deciding existed.

He led them to the receiving room, the public one, the one with the comfortable chairs and the guild's charter framed on the wall and the little brass scale on the mantel that weighed nothing in particular and impressed everyone. Never negotiate in your own office. Your office has your ledgers in it, and your ledgers have your whole life in them.

"Tea?" he offered.

"We do not consume," said the shorter one.

"More for me," said Sarn, and poured himself a cup he had no intention of drinking, because a man holding a teacup was a man at leisure, and leisure was the costume wealth wore when it wanted to seem effortless.

The magistrates did not sit. They stood side by side facing him, and their unfinished eyes — those single-line suggestions of eyes — fixed on him with an attention that made his skin feel like a document being read.

"You are aware," the tall one said, "of what has come up your mountain."

"A great many things come up my mountain," Sarn said pleasantly. "Ore, pilgrims, rumors. Rumors mostly."

"On the night of the last storm," the short one continued, as though Sarn hadn't spoken, "a soul returned. Sold beneath, walked back up. It entered this building. It is in this building now."

"Well," said Sarn, and laughed — a good laugh, easy, the laugh of a man mildly amused — "if I had a coin for every soul that wandered in here on a stormy night, I'd have retired years ago. We're a guild of impossible things, magistrates. People bring us their ghosts all the time. Half of them are just grief wearing a coat."

The magistrates regarded him.

The silence went on long enough that Sarn became aware of his own heartbeat, then his own breathing, then the small treacherous sound of the teacup trembling faintly against its saucer. He set the saucer down with a click that sounded, in the thin light, like a lock turning.

"The court," the tall magistrate said at last, "does not grieve."

"No," Sarn agreed. "I imagine it doesn't."

"It counts."

"Yes. Well. Counting I understand." He spread his hands, the gesture of a reasonable man among unreasonable circumstances. "So let's talk numbers, if we must. What does the court believe it's owed?"

"The return is owed," said the short one. "Returned property reverts. The soul will be collected, conveyed beneath, and entered into the permanent record."

"And if the soul happens to be, say, damaged? Assembled from scraps? Souls that climb back up are rarely in saleable condition, as I'm sure the court's appraisers know better than anyone—"

"The court does not resell," the tall one said, and for the first time something moved in its unfinished face — the suggestion of a mouth adjusting, though not into anything so committed as a smile. "The court archives."

Sarn heard himself say, lightly: "Ah. A library, then."

Neither magistrate answered, and the silence that followed had weight and temperature, and Sarn understood that he had just seen, briefly, over the top of the wall he'd spent forty years building, exactly what kind of place he had sold things to for four decades.

He drank his tea anyway. It had gone cold. He drank it like a man at leisure.

* * *

Here is what Ovid Sarn knew about tithe-magistrates, gathered across thirty years of careful, deniable commerce with the court beneath the mountain:

One: they could not enter a building uninvited, but a guild's front door was legally a public threshold during business hours, and they knew the law better than the law knew itself.

Two: they could not take what had not been formally identified. Identification required either the owner's confession or the item's own acknowledgment. This was why the wise seller never named his goods aloud, never pointed at them, never so much as glanced toward wherever they were kept. A soul that had been sold once already knew what acknowledgment meant, and souls were quick learners.

Three: they could be stalled. Not refused — refusal converted a debt into a hunt, and hunts ended with guildmasters being collected alongside the goods. But stalled. Delayed. Given paperwork.

Paperwork was the one language every bureaucracy shared, and the court beneath the mountain was the most bureaucratic thing in creation. It had, in effect, invented the ledger. Somewhere under all that stone was a hand that had been writing since before writing had a name, and its clerks — its magistrates, its bailiffs, its sketch-faced auditors — revered documentation the way the devout revered scripture.

So Sarn gave them documents.

He had Thackeray fetch them — the woman moved quietly and asked no questions, which was why he kept her, which was why he'd kept her for ten years — and for two hours the receiving room filled with paper. Acquisition manifests. Provenance chains. Certificates of intake, each stamped with the guild seal, each attesting in increasingly baroque legalese that the Guild of Impossible Things maintained rigorous and lawful custody protocols for all items of anomalous origin.

Thackeray laid the pages out with steady hands. Her face was its usual unremarkable self — brown hair, brown eyes, a face you forgot while looking at it — but Sarn noticed, because he noticed everything about the people he depended on, that her eyes went to the magistrates exactly once, and that when they did, her breath caught in a way she covered by turning a page.

She knows them, he thought. Or she knows what they are.

Interesting. Filed away. Everything was filed away; that was how he'd built the guild, out of other people's filed-away secrets.

"The item in question," Sarn said, when the table was buried, "is undergoing mandatory intake assessment. Condition verification. Valuation. One cannot simply hand over goods of this nature without proper appraisal — the court itself would be the first to insist. Irregular transfers create irregular records, and irregular records—" he tapped the tallest stack of paper "—are an abomination. Am I right?"

The magistrates considered the mountain of paper. Something in the tall one's unfinished face shifted — the eye-line deepening, perhaps, or the jaw fading further — and Sarn, who had spent his life reading faces far more complete than these, chose to interpret it as satisfaction.

"Assessment," the tall magistrate said, "is acceptable."

"Three days," Sarn said promptly, before they could name a number. "Standard intake period for items of returned provenance. It's all in the charter." It wasn't. He'd write it in tonight.

"Three days," the short one repeated, and the words came out with the finality of a stamp coming down.

Both magistrates turned toward the door in perfect step. At the threshold the tall one paused, half-turned, and extended one hand — fingers drawn in outline only, no knuckles, no nails — and pressed it flat against the guild's front door.

Where the hand touched, the wood blackened. Not burned — *inked*. A seal bloomed out of the grain like frost forming in reverse: three concentric rings, and inside them, in characters older than any kingdom's alphabet, a single line of text that Sarn could not read and did not need to. He knew a due date when he saw one.

"Collection," the magistrate said, "in three days."

Then the light unthinned, the dust motes resumed their ordinary drift, and the step outside was empty, and the morning was just a morning again, except for the black seal on the door, which did not fade, and did not dry, and gleamed faintly, wetly, like something freshly written.

* * *

Sarn stood in front of the seal for a long time after they'd gone.

Three days. He turned the number over the way a miser turns a coin, checking for clipping. Three days was enough time to find a buyer — if the buyer was already looking, if the buyer had deep pockets and shallow scruples, if the buyer could be reached tonight. There were collectors in this city who paid fortunes for returned souls, precisely because the court wanted them back; ownership of a thing the undermountain coveted was the purest form of wealth, a treasure measured not in gold but in the envy of something ancient. Three days was enough, barely, if everything went perfectly.

Everything never went perfectly. That was the first lesson of the trade.

And there was a second problem, a problem with unfinished edges, standing two floors down behind three locks: the girl had watched him through the whole negotiation. He'd seen her at the vault door's observation slit, those dark eyes tracking the magistrates with an expression he had no ledger-category for. Not fear. Recognition, maybe. Or accounting.

Souls that counted back were trouble.

He descended to the vault level, keys in hand, and found Thackeray already there, standing outside the door with her hand flat against the wood, as though listening. She withdrew it quickly when she saw him, her face sliding back into its practiced nothing.

"Guildmaster."

"Thackeray." He studied her a moment longer than was comfortable for either of them. "You handled the paperwork well today."

"Thank you."

"You've seen magistrates before."

A pause. Small, but he was a connoisseur of pauses; he traded in them. "The guild sees all kinds," she said.

"It does." He smiled his soft, pleasant, slightly bored smile. "Three days, Thackeray. Whatever intake assessment requires, I want it done. The girl fed, cleaned, presentable. If she speaks that old tongue of hers, I want to know every word of it. Every word. Do you understand me?"

Something moved behind her unremarkable eyes — fast, buried, gone. "Every word," she repeated.

"Good." He turned to go, then stopped, because the thought had finally finished assembling itself, the thought that had been scratching at him since the light went thin. He didn't look back at her when he said it.

"One more thing. The entry in the acquisition ledger. The one dated ten years ago. Marked paid in full." He heard her breathing stop. "You audited that book for a decade, Thackeray. You never flagged it. Which means either you missed it — unlikely — or you knew whose debt it was."

The silence stretched. Down here, under the maps, the lamplight was small and honest, and it showed him her hands: steady, always steady, but white at the knuckles now.

"I'll prepare the girl," Kestrel Thackeray said, in a voice with nothing left in it at all, "for collection."

And Ovid Sarn, who had survived forty years by never letting them see him count, climbed back upstairs doing arithmetic he didn't like: three days, one soul, one cartographer who knew too much, and a black seal on his door that gleamed like it was still wet — still *writing* — and somewhere beneath all that stone, a vast patient hand waiting to see what he would put on the line.
