## 8. The Thief's Confession

*Dark first. Dark always first.*

I am in the vault under the guild, where the maps sleep in their drawers like moths in coats, and I am learning to think in the new tongue. Kestrel gives me words the way you give bread to a bird—one crumb, then wait, then another crumb. *Window. Wall. Wait.* I hold them in my mouth until they soften.

But my own words come from deeper down. They come from before kingdoms had names for themselves. They come from roots and from water saying its long slow say over stone. So this is how I will tell it: half in her language, which is still strange as a borrowed coat, and half in mine, which is not a language anymore because everyone who spoke it is sold or dead or folded into ledgers.

Kestrel says write it down. She says the court cannot take what is written in a guild vault. She says this with a face that does not believe it, but she says it anyway, and I love her for the anyway.

So. Written down:

* * *

Before, I was Wren.

I remember the name the way you remember warmth from a fire that has gone out—not the fire, only the shape of standing near it. Wren. A small bird-word. A quick word. My mother chose it because I never stopped moving, even asleep, even in the dark of her body, kicking like I was already running from something.

I was running toward something too. I just didn't know what yet.

We lived at the mountain's foot, in the last village before the tithe-road begins. You know the road? No—you don't. Nobody knows it who hasn't walked it, and nobody walks it twice whole. Every seventh year the village gave the mountain a child. That was the old bargain, older than kings, older than the word *bargain*: the court beneath keeps us fed, keeps our wells sweet, keeps our winters short, and we keep its ledger full.

My year was a thin year. The wells had gone bitter twice. The winter came early and stayed like a guest who eats everything. And there were two of us that year who fit the requirement—I was one, and the other was my sister-not-by-blood, the girl I shared a bed with when the cold got mean, the girl whose breath fogged the same window as mine.

She was called something else then. Not Kestrel. That name is a coat she put on later, in a city far away, so no one would know her. Her true name I will not write here, even now, even in the dark of this vault where only moths and maps can read. Some things you keep even from paper. Especially from paper. Paper talks. Paper remembers longer than people want it to.

The night the collectors came, she held my hand behind the barn. Her hand was shaking. Mine wasn't. I want that written clearly: *mine wasn't*. I was twelve and I was not afraid, because I did not understand yet what the mountain took. I thought it took your body. Bodies are nothing. Bodies get cold and get buried and the grass grows back over them by spring.

It does not take your body.

Here is what happened, as much of it as I can carry up from the dark:

They lined us in the square, the two of us, and the magistrate wore a face like a sketch someone had abandoned halfway—a jaw finished, an eye only suggested, a mouth that was more intention than mouth. He carried a book bound in something soft. He asked for a name. One name, freely given, to balance against the other. One goes down; one stays. But a staying must be paid for, and names are the only coin the court honors.

And my sister-not-by-blood—

No. Let me be fair, since fairness is the one thing I have left that they didn't inventory. Let me be exactly fair.

She was fourteen. She was terrified in a way I could see but not feel, the way you can see lightning but not be struck by it. The magistrate told her the terms and the terms were simple and terrible: give me her name, and you walk free. Refuse, and the mountain takes both, and takes them slowly, and your village watches.

She looked at me. I smiled at her. I want that written too: *I smiled at her.* Because I still thought bodies were the price, and I was not afraid of the dark under the mountain, and I wanted her to not be afraid either.

She said my name.

She said it quietly, the way you set down something breakable. She said it to the sketch-faced thing with the soft-bound book, and the book opened itself, and my name lifted out of me like heat off a stone.

You cannot imagine what it is to have your name taken. Do not try. Your imagination will make it too small.

It is not pain. It is subtraction. Everything I had ever been that involved being *Wren* went with it—the way my mother said it across the supper table, the way the other children shouted it when they wanted me to race, the way I whispered it to myself in the dark to prove I was real. All of that, lifted. What remained was a shape with a hole in the middle of it, and the hole made a sound when the wind passed through, and the sound was almost a voice but not quite.

Then they took us both down anyway.

That part she doesn't know. Or maybe she guesses. The bargain bought her passage out, but the court is not generous; the court is *balanced*, and balance is crueler than greed. They walked me down the tithe-road while she walked free, and every step the mountain ate something else. First my mother's face—I had it, and then between one step and the next I had the fact of it without the picture, like a window painted over. Then my language. Then the color of my own eyes, which I had never thought about and missed desperately once it was gone. Then years. Whole years, eaten like plums.

At the bottom there is a court. I will tell you what it is like, though telling flattens it. It is a cathedral built of stacked ledgers, higher than seeing, and every book is a person, and every page is a day somebody lived, and the magistrates walk the aisles reading aloud in voices like dry leaves, and the reading *is* the burning of lamps, and the light of the place is other people's lives being spent.

I was put on a shelf.

Ten years. I was a book on a shelf for ten years, and books do not sleep, and books do not die, and books do not stop noticing. I noticed everything. I noticed the magistrates were all former sellers—people who had done what my sister-not-by-blood did, who had bought their lives with someone else's name, and when their debts came due again, as debts do, they had nothing left to pay with except themselves. The court wears its debtors. That is what those unfinished faces are: people mid-payment.

I noticed something else, on the shelf, in the dark, through ten years of noticing.

I noticed that a soul is not one thing. Everyone thinks it is one thing, seamless, a pearl. It is not. It is scraps. It is a quilt stitched from every moment someone loved you, every time your name was said kindly, every memory anyone kept of you alive in their mouths. My name was gone, but the scraps weren't all gone. Somewhere above me, in the world of weather and bread, people still remembered pieces of me. My mother, if she lived. The village. And one other.

Her.

Every time she remembered me—and she remembered me constantly, I could feel it coming down the mountain like heat through a floor, guilt has a temperature—every time, another scrap of me warmed. Another stitch pulled tight. She thought remembering was her punishment. It was my thread. Her guilt was sewing me back together, stitch by stitch, year by year, whether she wanted to or not.

So I assembled. Slowly, badly, from scraps. A quilt of a girl. Mostly holes, held together by the memory of being loved, which is the strongest thread there is.

And then, in the tenth year, the court needed a collection notice delivered.

That is what I am. Write it plainly: I am a bill. The court found its shelf-book partially reassembled—unusual, unprecedented, *irregular*, the magistrates hissed at each other in their leaf-voices—and it decided irregularity was opportunity. A soul that climbed back together could climb back *up*. Could walk out the front door of the mountain wearing a body scraped together from the same scraps, could go find the woman whose debt was still open, could stand in front of her and be the receipt.

They sent me up the tithe-road. Going up was worse than going down. Down, they take from you. Up, you have to *carry* what's left, and what's left is heavy in the way feathers are heavy—each piece nothing, all of it everything. I climbed for a season. I ate roots that speak if you soak them. I slept inside the sound of water. When I finally came out of the mountain's shadow and saw the city, I had three words left in the old tongue and no words at all in any other, and a hole in my middle shaped exactly like a name.

* * *

Now I sit in the vault, and Kestrel teaches me crumbs, and I learn.

*Window,* she says, and points, and I say *window*, and the word sits in my mouth like a pebble I'm deciding whether to swallow.

*Wall. Lamp. Sorry.*

That last one she taught me by accident. She knocked over the ink, and said *sorry* to me—to *me*, the collection notice, the bill come due—and I learned the word from the shape of her shame. In the old tongue we had a word for sorry that meant *I return what I took*. Their word means less than that. Their word is a bandage over a wound that needs stitches. But I take it. I take all the crumbs she offers.

Here is the thing I have not told her. Here is the thing I will write in this vault where only moths can read, because writing it makes it weigh less and I need it lighter:

I do not blame her.

I have turned it over the way you turn a stone to see what lives underneath. Ten years on a shelf gives you time for turning stones. And underneath, every time, the same thing: a fourteen-year-old girl with a sketch-faced thing's soft book open in front of her, and two choices, and both of them were me. Take my name or take my body. She chose the one that let some of me survive. She chose wrong—but the right choice did not exist. There was no door marked *good*. Only doors marked *less bad*, and she walked through one, and she has been walking ever since, and every step of her walking stitched another piece of me back on.

If she had refused—if she had been brave in the way stories want—there would be no me at all. No scraps. No quilt. No girl in a vault learning the word *window*. The court would have unmade us both completely, and completely is forever.

So I do not blame her.

And this is worse. I know it is worse, because I can see it land on her, every day, the way rain lands on someone who expected lightning. She waits for my anger. She has built her whole life—ten years of blank pages, ten years of being unremarkable, ten years of erasing herself entry by entry—she has built all of it on the foundation of the blow she believes is coming. She thinks I climbed out of the mountain to collect. She thinks the debt is hatred.

But forgiveness is heavier than anger. Anger says *you owe me*. Forgiveness says *you owe me, and I am telling you I will never collect, and now you have to live with that instead*, and living-with-that is a sentence with no end date. I watch her flinch when I am kind to her. I watch her check my face for the accusation that isn't there and finding it absent, supply it herself, out of her own guilt, because she trusts her guilt more than she trusts my mercy.

I could end that. I could say the words: *I forgive you, it was not a choice, you were a child and so was I.* I have the words now, most of them. I could set her down like a set-down burden.

I don't. And I don't fully know why. Partly because I am not sure the forgiveness is finished—quilts are never finished, there is always another seam. Partly because her guilt is the warmest thing in her, and if I take it, what is left might be colder. And partly—this part I barely admit to myself—because watching her carry it is the only power I have ever had over anyone, and I was a shelf-book for ten years, and power is a hunger that survives even when everything else is eaten.

There. I wrote it. Moths, you are witnesses.

* * *

Tonight Kestrel comes to the vault late, smelling of lamp oil and fear she's trying to fold flat. She brings bread and the word *tomorrow*, which she says twice, the second time quieter, like the word might break.

"Tomorrow," I repeat. "Tomorrow."

She smiles her practiced smile, the one she wears like the name Kestrel—correctly, convincingly, and never quite touching her eyes. Then she looks at the map spread on the table between us, the one she's been drawing from memory, the one where every line costs her something—I've watched her pause after certain strokes and reach for a memory and find the shelf empty, watched her not know, briefly, what her mother's laugh sounded like—and she smooths it flat with both hands.

"Ash," she says. My new name. The name I chose from the burnt edge of things, because ash is what's left after and ash is honest. "Tomorrow, whatever happens—"

The lamps gutter. Both of them, at once, though there is no draft.

And through the wall—from the ledger room, where Guildmaster Ovid Sarn keeps his soft-voiced accounts—I hear a sound I know from the bottom of the mountain. The dry-leaf whisper of magistrates' voices, but muffled, hushed, *negotiating*. And beneath it, Sarn's voice, smooth as tallow, saying words I cannot catch but whose shape I know the way I knew the shape of my stolen name.

A shape like a price. A shape like *her*.

Kestrel hasn't heard. She is still talking, still folding her fear flat, still offering me tomorrow like it's a thing she owns.

I press my palm against the wall, against the whispering, and the wall is cold as a shelf, and somewhere under my ribs the quilt of me pulls tight around the oldest scrap of all—the scrap that remembers how it felt, in the square, in another life, to be the thing someone handed over to save herself.

Not her, this time.

Him.
