## 9. Ovid's Bargain

The creditors come at dawn, because cruelty keeps business hours.

There are three of them this time, which is two more than last month, and they do not sit in the guild's receiving room like guests. They sit like weather. Master Corvane of the Salt Ledger spreads his papers across my good table without asking, the way a man spreads a tarp over something he intends to take apart. The other two flank him—a thin woman from the House of Weights whose eyes never move, and a boy of perhaps nineteen who carries nothing but a small brass bell he does not ring. The bell sits on the table between us like a third creditor.

"Guildmaster Sarn," Corvane says. He has the voice of a man reading an obituary he wrote himself. "You'll forgive the hour."

"I'll forgive the hour," I say, "if you'll forgive the tea." I pour it anyway. It has gone bitter in the pot, but bitterness is honest, and today I want to deal only in honest things.

Corvane does not touch his cup. "We've reviewed your position."

"My position is excellent."

"Your position," says the woman from the House of Weights, in a voice like a scale settling, "is a building full of maps of places that don't exist, staffed by people who are very good at not being found, secured against a debt you have refinanced four times in three years." She turns one page. "Five times."

"The fifth was strategic."

"The fifth was desperate," she says, "and desperation has a smell. We can smell it on the whole guild. It comes up through the floorboards."

I keep my hands still around the teapot. This is the first thing Ovid Sarn ever learned about money, back when he was a clerk's clerk in a city that has since forgotten him: that the moment they can smell your fear, the price stops being a number and becomes a hunger. So you feed the hunger slowly. You feed it anything except yourself.

"Gentlemen. Madam." I set down the pot. "The Guild of Impossible Things holds assets no other institution in this city can price. We hold the only verified map of the Drowned Chapel. We hold the lease on a door that opens onto yesterday. We hold—"

"We know what you hold," Corvane says. "That's why we're here instead of the bailiffs. A bailiff takes the furniture. We'd rather take the impossible things." He laces his fingers. "But we've run the numbers, Sarn, and the numbers say your impossible things are worth less every year. Curiosity is a wasting asset. Wonder depreciates." He smiles with all the warmth of a ledger line. "What doesn't depreciate?"

I know what he wants me to say. I have known since the girl came through my door mud-caked and mute and speaking a language older than any contract I've ever signed.

Souls don't depreciate.

* * *

After they go—I give them nothing but tea and promises, and they leave the bell on the table, which is its own kind of message—I stand at the window of the receiving room and watch the morning traffic below and try to remember when I became a man who measures everything in what it will cost him later.

It was not always thus. I want to be clear about that, if only to myself, in the privacy of my own skull where no auditor can reach. There was a time when Ovid Sarn bought impossible things because they were impossible, because a map of a chapel under the water made the world feel larger rather than smaller, because wonder was the whole of the business and the money was just how wonder kept its shoes on. I taught a frightened young cartographer named Thackeray how to make herself unremarkable because unremarkable was safe, and I believed then that safety was a gift you could give a person.

Now I keep a child locked in my map vault because she might be worth more than the building she sleeps in, and I tell myself it is for her own protection, and the telling has stopped convincing even me.

Kestrel finds me at the window. She does that—arrives without arriving, a skill I taught her and have come to quietly resent. She stands in the doorway with ink on her fingers and something new in her face, something I haven't seen in ten years of employing her, and I realize with a small cold shock that I don't know what she wants, and that I have built an entire life on knowing what people want before they do.

"The magistrates left a seal on the door," she says. "Three days."

"I saw the seal."

"Do you know what happens on the third day?"

"I know what happens to guilds that default," I say. "I've audited the ruins."

"That's not what I asked."

She looks at me, and for a moment I see her as the magistrates must see her—not as my best cartographer, my quietest keeper, my most reliable erasure, but as a woman standing between a child and a debt, which is the oldest shape in the world and the one the court beneath the mountain understands best.

"Ash stays," Kestrel says. "Whatever they offer you. Whatever the number is. She stays."

"You're assuming I've been offered a number."

"I'm assuming you will be."

And there it is—the thing I couldn't read in her face a moment ago. Not fear. Certainty. She has already done the arithmetic I am doing. She stood in my guild hall and looked at me and did the sums, and the sums came out the way sums always come out with men like me, and she came here anyway, to ask, to plead in the flat voice of someone who has stopped expecting mercy, and I find that I cannot look at her while she does it.

"She's a ward of the guild," I say. "Guild wards are protected."

"By whom?"

"By me."

"Then protect her," Kestrel says, and leaves, and the door closes so softly behind her that I understand it as an accusation.

* * *

The second visit comes at dusk, and it is not creditors.

They do not knock. The sketch-faced ones never knock; doors are a courtesy between things that agree to be separate, and the magistrates of the tithe do not agree to be separate from anything. One moment my receiving room is empty except for me and the brass bell the creditors left, and the next there are two figures by the hearth, and their faces are wrong in the way a drawing is wrong before the artist commits—an eye suggested, a mouth implied, everything waiting to be decided.

"Guildmaster," says the taller one. Its voice is pleasant. That is somehow worse than if it weren't. "We understand you have an item of interest."

"I have a guild," I say. "Full of items."

"We are not interested in your maps." The shorter one drifts toward the window, and where it passes, the glass frosts and clears again, like breath. "Maps describe. We collect. There is a soul in this building that was sold below and has walked back up. The account is ten years in arrears. The court does not enjoy arrears."

"It's a child," I say.

"All accounts begin as children," says the tall one, and something in the flatness of it makes me certain this magistrate was once a man who said similar things in a similar voice, in a room like this one, to someone like me. "The question, Guildmaster, is not whether the soul returns below. Escaped tithes always return; the mountain is patient and the road runs both ways. The question is who delivers it, and what that delivery is worth."

And here is the moment. I want to be honest about the moment, since honesty is the one luxury I cannot put on a ledger. There is a version of me—the young one, the wonder-drunk one, the one who bought drowned chapels—who says *no*, who says *the child is under my protection*, who spends whatever remains of Ovid Sarn on one clean act.

But the young me is also the me who is ten years deep in refinanced debt, and the bell on the table has begun, very softly, to ring, though no hand is near it, and the sound is the sound the creditors' boy would have made if he'd had the courage, and I understand suddenly that everyone in this city has been paid or promised except me.

"What," I say, "is it worth?"

The magistrates do not smile. Their faces are unfinished; smiling may not be available to them yet. But the tall one inclines its head with the terrible courtesy of the court, and says:

"Everything you owe. Forgiven. Sealed. The Salt Ledger closed. The House of Weights satisfied. Your building, your ledgers, your name—clean, as of the moment of delivery." It pauses. "Delivery by the third day. Early delivery is... appreciated. The court rewards initiative."

"And if I don't?"

"Then on the third day we take the soul, and we take the arrears, and we take—" it tilts its unfinished face, considering—"the guildmaster who sheltered an escaped tithe. Sheltering is a form of theft, Guildmaster. Theft of the court's property. You know what the court does with thieves. You've seen our senior staff."

I think of the magistrates' faces. Unfinished sketches. Men and women who sold what they shouldn't and failed to pay, worn down feature by feature until even their faces were repossessed.

"Yes," I say. "I've seen them."

"So," says the tall magistrate, gently, the way you speak to a man at a graveside. "Shall we discuss terms?"

* * *

We discuss terms.

I will not pretend otherwise, and I will not dress it up. We discuss terms for the better part of an hour, in my receiving room, by my fire, while upstairs a child who speaks in roots sleeps in a vault among maps of places that refuse to exist, and I negotiate—God help me, I *negotiate*—for a delivery clause, a confidentiality clause, a clause guaranteeing that the guild's other holdings remain untouched, as though the thing being delivered were a shipment of saffron and not the last free hours of a little girl who calls herself Ash because a woman with ink-stained hands gave her the name out of kindness.

The magistrates agree to everything. That is the horror of it. They agree to everything immediately, because they know—as I know, as some cold clerk in my chest has known since the girl walked in—that I was always going to say yes, and that a seller who is certain needs no haggling.

At the end, the tall magistrate produces a document. It writes itself as I watch, the ink rising out of the page like something surfacing from deep water, and where a signature should go there is a space shaped exactly like my name.

"One condition," I hear myself say.

The magistrate waits.

"The woman. Thackeray. The cartographer." My mouth is dry. "She believes the child is under guild protection. If she learns the terms of this arrangement before delivery, she will interfere. She's resourceful. She made herself unremarkable once; she could make a child disappear." I swallow. "Ensure she isn't present at the delivery. And ensure she learns of it after. When it can't be undone."

Why do I say it? I could lie to myself and say it is strategy—that a grieving, ignorant Kestrel is a manageable Kestrel—but the truth is simpler and uglier: I say it because I cannot bear to be seen doing this. Because the one person in this building who still believes Ovid Sarn keeps his wards safe is the one person whose belief I cannot survive losing, and so I arrange, in writing, never to see it leave her face.

The magistrate's unfinished face does something that is almost sympathy.

"Wise," it says. "Guilt is such an inefficient emotion. The court abolished it centuries ago." It slides the document across the table. "Sign."

I sign.

My name goes onto the page and the page drinks it, and somewhere beneath the mountain, I have no doubt, a great hand adds a line to a great ledger, and the line reads: *One (1) returned soul, delivered early by seller's agent. Payment: debts discharged, account closed.* And beside it, in smaller script, the kind of script reserved for annotations the court finds amusing: *Agent retains name. For now.*

* * *

I sleep badly, which surprises me. I expected to sleep well. Ten years of debt gone in an evening; the guild saved; the future, for the first time since I can remember, a country I am permitted to enter. I lie in my bed above the dark guildhall and listen to the rain and wait for relief to arrive, and what arrives instead is a memory.

A square, in another life. A crowd. A girl—young, clever, terrified—standing where I now stand in the story, holding out someone else's name because her own was being taken. I was in that crowd. I watched it happen. I remember thinking, with the comfortable cruelty of a bystander, *how could anyone do that? How could a person hand over another person?*

And then, ten years later, I opened my door to a mud-caked child and saw a fortune with a face, and I understood that the answer to the question is: easily. Effortlessly. With excellent terms and a confidentiality clause.

The quilt of me pulls tight around that memory, and I find I cannot warm it.

* * *

Morning. The second day. Two days until collection, one day ahead of schedule, thanks to my initiative.

I rise, dress, and rehearse. This is the part I have prepared least and need most: the ordinary day. The guild must run normally. Kestrel must suspect nothing. I will assign her to the eastern archive—far wing, thick walls, work that genuinely needs doing—and I will check on the vault myself, and at dusk the magistrates will come, and by midnight it will be done and I will be a free man who has purchased his freedom at a price he has arranged never to witness being paid.

I am good at this. I taught the best invisible woman in the city everything she knows. I can manage one more day of being unremarkable myself.

I stop at the ledger room to collect the eastern archive keys, and that is when I hear it.

Voices through the wall. The ledger-room wall is old guild stone, and old guild stone carries whispers the way riverbeds carry water—everything that flows above it eventually passes through. I have heard a hundred private conversations bleed through that wall over the years and always stepped away, always honored the guild's one true law, which is that what people erase deserves to stay erased.

I should step away now.

I don't. Because one of the voices is Kestrel's, and the other is Ash's, and Ash is supposed to be locked in the vault two floors down, and instead she is in the map room, and Kestrel is saying—in a voice stripped of all its careful flatness, a voice I have never heard her use—"*You're not going back down there. Do you hear me? Whatever he's promised them, whoever he's promised you to—I will burn every map in this building first.*"

Silence. Then the girl's voice, small and root-slow, in the common tongue Kestrel has been teaching her, each word placed like a stone across a stream:

"He signed it. I heard the pen. The pen sounded like teeth."

The world goes very quiet and very large.

She heard. Through the walls, through the stone, through whatever strange hearing the court leaves in the things it sends back up—the child heard the pen scratch my name onto the delivery order, heard the terms, heard everything, and told Kestrel everything, and now Kestrel knows.

Kestrel knows that Ovid Sarn—the man who taught her how to vanish, who gave her unremarkability like a coat against the cold, who she just begged to protect her ward—has sold the child. Has sold her with clauses and conditions and a request that Kestrel not be told until it was too late to matter.

I stand in the ledger room with the eastern archive keys in my fist, and through the wall I hear Kestrel's voice again, low and terrible and calm, the voice of a woman doing sums:

"He taught me how to disappear," she says. "Time I returned the favor. Get your shoes, Ash. We're leaving tonight—and we're taking the maps."

Keys in my hand. Door between us. One wall of old stone, thin as paper now, and on the other side of it, the two people in the world I have most betrayed are already planning their escape—and I have until nightfall to decide whether Ovid Sarn is a man who stops them, or a man who, for the first and last time in his miserable accounting, lets the impossible thing go free.
