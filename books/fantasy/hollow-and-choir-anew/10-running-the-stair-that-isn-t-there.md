## 10. Running the Stair That Isn't There

The wall had ears, and one of them was mine.

I'd gone to the ledger room for ink — ordinary ink, the cheap kind that smudged, because the good stuff was locked in Sarn's desk and Sarn was locked in his office with two men whose faces were only *suggestions* of faces. I heard the negotiation through the plaster the way you hear a heartbeat through a pillow: muffled, rhythmic, wrong.

"Three days," the magistrate's voice said, and it sounded like a pen scratching on wet paper. "You offer us three days."

"I offer you *certainty*," Sarn said. "The guild does not deal in haste. Haste is how ledgers get unbalanced."

"The soul walks free in your vault."

"She sleeps in my vault. There is a difference, and the difference is collateral." A pause. Then, softer, the voice of a man who has done arithmetic he's ashamed of: "Unless. Gentlemen. Unless we might discuss an *early settlement*. My own accounts with the court are... complicated. Forgive the debt, and I will deliver the girl to your stair myself. Tonight."

My hand was around the ink pot so hard I heard the glaze crack.

I did not go back for the good ink.

* * *

Ash was awake when I opened the vault door, sitting cross-legged on the floor among the rolled maps, drawing in the dust with one finger. She drew roots. She always drew roots — branching, reaching, gripping. When she saw me she didn't startle. She'd already learned that I came at strange hours now, that something had changed in the way I moved through the guild, quick and low, like water finding a crack.

"We're leaving," I said in her tongue — the old tongue, the root-tongue, the one that lived in the throat like a held breath. "Now. Tonight. Sarn means to sell you before morning."

She tilted her head. In ten days she'd learned enough common speech to ask questions, but this one she asked in the old way, with her whole body: *why?*

"Because men who sell names never stop selling them," I said. "Because I was once merchandise too, and I know exactly what the stair down looks like from the inside." I knelt, took her small muddy hands. "And because I owe you a debt ten years old, and I intend to pay it in the only coin left to me. Your name. Wren's name. It belongs to you, and I'm going to take you somewhere you can claim it."

She studied me with those eyes that were not quite a child's eyes, that had been assembled from scraps in the dark under the mountain. Then she nodded once, solemn as a magistrate, and stood, and took my hand.

We went out through the map vault's second door — the one Sarn didn't know about, because Sarn had never read the guild's oldest ledger, and the oldest ledger contained a floor plan drawn by a cartographer who had hated him.

That cartographer was me. Ten years ago, in my first month, before I understood that invisibility required *not* leaving marks. I'd drawn the guild's hidden ways into the margin of an inventory page: the crawl-space behind the atlas cases, the stair behind the plaster, the corridor that existed only between midnight and dawn because its far wall was a door borrowed from elsewhere. I'd thought no one would ever find it. I'd hoped no one would ever need it.

The crawl-space was exactly where I'd drawn it. The guild, bless its impossible heart, kept faith with its maps even when it forgot them.

* * *

Behind us, the vault door groaned open, and something entered that did not walk so much as *arrive*, repeatedly, in the same place.

Bailiffs. Three of them, sketch-faced, their features half-finished — a jaw suggested but not committed to, eyes that were two confident strokes and nothing behind them. They moved the way unfinished things move: without weight, without doubt. One carried a black seal the size of a dinner plate. Another carried nothing at all, which was worse, because its empty hands flexed like they were remembering what hands were for.

"They're behind us," Ash whispered. She'd learned whispers first. Children always learn whispers first; it's the grammar of fear.

"I know." I unrolled my map as we ran — the half-finished thing I'd been bleeding memories into for six days, the court beneath the mountain rendered in lines that cost me my mother's laugh, my teacher's name, the taste of the bread from home. But the top third of the sheet wasn't the mountain. It was the guild itself, drawn in the frantic shorthand of tonight, corridors I'd mapped from memory an hour ago while Ash slept.

Except the memory-map was wrong now. Or rather, the guild was wrong, and the map knew it before I did.

"That corridor," I breathed. "It should end at the cistern stair. It doesn't anymore."

The ink had moved. While we ran, the line describing our route had rewritten itself — not ahead of us, not predicting, but *trailing*, solidifying behind our heels like frost forming in a footprint. Where we had chosen to turn left, the map now showed only left. The right-hand passage, the one we hadn't taken, was fading to ghost-gray, then to nothing, as if the guild were agreeing, retroactively, that it had never existed.

"It's writing us," Ash said, in the old tongue, awed. "The map is writing us as we run."

"Yes." And there was no time to explain what that meant, what it cost, what it offered, so I just said the true thing: "Then let's give it something worth writing."

* * *

The bailiffs gained in the long gallery of framed surveys. Sketch-faces don't tire; they don't breathe; they simply get *closer*, the way a deadline gets closer. I could hear the lead one — or whatever served it for hearing — dragging along the wainscoting, a sound like charcoal on rough paper.

"Ash. The junction ahead. Left or right?"

She looked at me like I'd lost my mind. "You have the map."

"The map is making itself up as we go. Which means *we're* making it up as we go. Which means—" I grabbed her shoulders, "—for the next hundred yards, nobody knows where we are better than we do. Not them. Not the guild. Not the court. Choose."

Her finger hovered. Left: toward the archive, deep, defensible, dead-ending eventually. Right: toward the old surveyor's stair, the one that officially went nowhere, that the guild had bricked over forty years ago according to every record Sarn kept.

She chose right. Of course she chose right. She was a girl who had climbed out of a grave under a mountain; she had opinions about walls that claimed to be final.

The brick wall was real. Forty years of mortar, solid, insulting. Behind us the gallery filled with the soft arrival-sound of bailiffs.

So I did the thing I'd spent a decade refusing to do. I stopped being careful with myself.

I put my palm flat against the brick and I *remembered at it*. Not the court, not yet — I wasn't strong enough to spend those memories, they were ballast keeping me upright. Cheaper ones. The smell of the harbor the summer I arrived in this city. The name of the woman who taught me lettering. The color of the sky over my childhood, which until this moment I had not known I was still carrying.

The mortar softened. Not crumbled — *softened*, the way wax softens, the way certainty softens. The bricks sighed apart around a doorway that had been waiting behind them all along, patient as everything else in this building that refused to obey the rules of buildings.

"How," Ash said.

"I'm paying tolls," I said, and pulled her through.

* * *

The surveyor's stair went down in tight spirals, stone worn into shallow bowls by feet that had died before either of us was born. No lamps. Ash made light — I don't know how else to describe it; she cupped her hands and something like foxfire gathered in them, dim and greenish, the color of the old tongue itself. Sold souls come back with odd gifts. Hers was small, but it was hers, and watching her use it I felt something I hadn't felt since Wren died in every way except the body: pride without ownership.

The map, clutched in my teeth while I climbed, kept rewriting. Our descent appeared stroke by stroke below our feet. And here was the terrible, wonderful thing: the bailiffs' pursuit was appearing too — gray smudges above us, hesitating at the softened doorway, and where they hesitated, the map showed them *fading*. The guild's unmapped corridors didn't know what to do with creatures that had no substance to write down. We were choices. They were approximations. The map preferred us.

"They can't follow the stair," I panted. "It isn't there. Officially. Unofficially. Anywhere. It only exists while we're standing on it."

Ash glanced back down the spiral, firelight jumping in her eyes. "Like us," she said.

I laughed — actually laughed, breathless and half-terrified, the first real laugh in ten years — because she was right. The stair wasn't there. I wasn't there. Kestrel Thackeray, cartographer, ledger-keeper, unremarkable woman, was a fiction maintained daily at great expense. And here was a child telling me, in four words, that fictions could carry you if you kept moving.

* * *

The stair ended at a door of green bronze, corroded shut, marked with a symbol I recognized from the oldest stratum of the city's history: a circle bisected by a root. The undercrofts. Every city has a skeleton beneath its skin — drains, crypts, foundations of buildings torn down before anyone alive was born — and this city's skeleton was older than its name, which was why the city kept forgetting its name. You cannot remember yourself over something that old. It leans on you.

I paid another toll to open the bronze. This one hurt more: I gave up the memory of my sister-figure's voice — not Wren, I would not spend Wren, not even a scrap of her — but of my actual sister, the one before, the one the court took first, the reason I'd been in the tithe-road queue at all. Her voice saying my true name, the buried one, the one Ash had spoken at the guild door in the storm. I let it go. I felt it leave like a tooth coming out, socket aching with absence.

The door opened onto dark that smelled of rain on old stone and something sweeter underneath — candle tallow, cooking smoke, *people*.

Ash's flame flickered toward the smell like a compass needle.

"There are others down here," she said.

"I know." Mother Vellum had told me, in the days before, in the coded language of the erased: *when you run, run down. The Nameless keep the undercrofts. The court's writ thins underground; ink needs air.* "People the court finished erasing. People with no names left to take."

Ash considered this. Then, with the devastating practicality of children everywhere: "Will they like us?"

"I think," I said, stepping through, "they will recognize us."

* * *

We came up out of the last stairwell into a vaulted chamber the size of a chapel, and it was lit — dozens of stub candles, hundreds, stuck to ledgers nailed to the walls, each page covered in handwriting that spiraled inward like fingerprints. The Nameless. Dozens of them, wrapped in layered coats, faces that were hard to hold in the eye — not blank like the bailiffs, but *sliding*, features drifting a little whenever you looked away and back. People worn smooth by erasure.

They turned as one. And I braced for the question every fugitive dreads — *who are you* — knowing I couldn't answer it honestly, knowing my honest answer was a confession.

They didn't ask. An old man with a drifting jaw stepped forward and said, in accented common speech: "You're the one who sold the name. The one who's been buying it back, line by line, all week. We can smell the tolls on you."

Heat crawled up my neck. Beside me, Ash's small hand tightened on mine — not pulling away. Holding on.

"Yes," I said. "That's me."

The old man nodded slowly, and something in the chamber shifted — not forgiveness, not yet, maybe not ever, but *acknowledgment*, which is rarer. "Good," he said. "The guilty ones run fastest and hide deepest. We'll want you rested anyway. The magistrates will seal the upper doors by morning, and the only way to the mountain road runs through our cellars." He smiled with a face that wouldn't stay smiled. "Welcome to the city that forgot its own name, cartographer. Mind the third step. It remembers falling."

Behind us, far above, through forty feet of stone, I heard it: the soft, repeated *arrival* of bailiffs reaching the top of a stair that had, the moment we stepped off it, stopped existing.

The map in my satchel had gone quiet. Finished, for now. Its last line, drying in the lamplight, described two small figures entering a chamber full of candles — and then, beneath it, in ink I had not spilled and did not control, a single new word was blooming:

*Debtors.*

Ash looked at it, then at me, and asked the question that would carry us into the dark below:

"Whose?"
