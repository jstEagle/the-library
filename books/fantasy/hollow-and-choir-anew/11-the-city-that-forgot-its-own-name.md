## 11. The City That Forgot Its Own Name

The candles had no wicks.

That was the first thing I understood about the undercroft, standing in its doorway with Ash's small hand locked in mine. Hundreds of candles, maybe thousands, ranked along stone shelves that climbed into darkness, and every one of them burned from nothing — flame sitting on bare wax like a bird that had forgotten it needed a branch. No smoke. No smell of tallow. Just light, patient and sourceless, the way the court beneath the mountain made light.

I knew because I had seen it once, ten years ago, by the light of candles exactly like these.

"Kestrel." Ash tugged my hand. In common speech her voice was still new, still stitched together from words she'd gathered like a magpie gathers buttons. "Whose debt?"

"Not ours," I said, which was a lie, and she knew it was a lie, and let me tell it anyway. That was its own kind of wound.

We stepped inside.

The chamber was not empty. People sat among the candle-shelves on stools and crates and folded blankets, and they did not look up when we entered, because looking up is a habit of people who expect to be found. These were people who had arranged their lives around never being found. I recognized the posture. I had spent a decade perfecting it in mirrors.

A woman near the door was mending a coat with thread the color of nothing. A man older than the guild itself was copying something onto a slate, over and over, wiping it clean, writing again. Children — two of them, small as Ash — played a game with river stones, except there were no stones, only the shapes of stones, held in cupped hands.

And every one of them had a face like an unfinished sketch.

Not the bailiffs' faces — those had been wrong in a hungry way, features smudged where a magistrate's thumb had pressed too hard. These faces were simply *incomplete*. A jaw suggested rather than drawn. Eyes that were warm and present but left uninked at the edges, so that if you looked away you could not have sworn to a single detail. The erasure hadn't taken everything. It had taken the parts that get remembered.

I had seen people like this before, once or twice, in ten years of mapping the city's forgotten margins. I had always assumed they were beggars, lepers, the merely unlucky. I had mapped right past them.

They were the sold. The erased. The ones whose names had gone down into the mountain and whose bodies had been left up here like coats nobody claimed.

Ash went still beside me. Then she did something that stopped my heart: she lifted her free hand and pressed it flat against her own chest, and said, in the old tongue, the pre-kingdom speech of the Hollow Choir, "*Kin.*"

Every head in the chamber turned.

* * *

They took us deeper in, because the door we'd come through was already being unmade behind us — I heard the stair above dissolve with a sound like a page being torn very slowly, and I understood that the Nameless had closed it, had folded our escape route shut behind us the way you fold a letter. We were inside now. Whether as guests or exhibits remained to be determined.

The deep chamber was round, and its ceiling was lost in candlelight, and at its center stood a chair that had been built from ledger boards — spines facing outward, so that whoever sat there was enthroned in the covers of a thousand destroyed accounts.

She rose from it when we entered.

She was tall, and gray-robed, and she had no face at all. Not unfinished — *absent*. Smooth as the back of a hand, smooth as an unwritten page, and somehow this was not horrifying but restful, the way an overcast sky is restful. Where her eyes should have been there was only the faintest suggestion of attention, like pressure on the far side of a curtain.

"You're the cartographer," she said. Her voice came from everywhere and nowhere in particular, the way sound behaves in places that have agreed not to echo. "The one who draws what refuses to exist."

"I'm no one," I said, on reflex. Ten years of reflex.

"Yes," she said gently. "You've been saying that for a long time. It doesn't make it truer."

Ash stepped forward — forward, in front of me, my hand suddenly holding air — and looked up at the faceless woman with an expression I couldn't read, because it was in a language I had sold.

Then the woman knelt. A faceless matriarch descending to the height of a child, robes pooling on stone, and she said something back to Ash in the Hollow Choir's tongue, low and rolling, and Ash answered, and they spoke for a long moment while I stood there understanding none of it and all of it — understanding, in the way you understand a song you've forbidden yourself to sing, exactly what was being said.

When Ash turned back to me, her eyes were wet.

"She says you brought me home," Ash said. "She says the others want to know why."

* * *

They asked why in the manner of people who have earned the right to ask.

They fed us first — broth, real broth, with actual salt, cooked somewhere in the warren of chambers beyond the candle hall — and then they sat us down in a ring of stools, and one by one they told me who they were. Or who they had been. The stories arrived like items read into a court record, which, in a sense, they were.

The man with the slate had been a stonemason named Aldery. He'd sold his brother's name to pay a tithe the magistrates invented during the famine year, and the court had accepted the payment, and then — because the court's ledgers balance both ways — it had taken his name too, as a fee he'd never been told about. He wrote his brother's name on the slate forty times a day and wiped it clean each time, because keeping it would be hoarding, and forgetting it would be surrender.

The woman with the thread had been a midwife called Sorrel. She hadn't sold anyone. Her daughter had sold *her* — traded her mother's name to buy passage out of a marriage, and the court had honored the transaction, because the court honors all transactions; that is the whole of its morality. Sorrel mended coats for the living because her hands needed work and her heart needed not to think.

There were eleven of them in the ring. Eleven stories. Every one ended the same way: a name spoken aloud, a signature given or stolen, and the long gray afterward of walking through the world as a shape other people's eyes slid off.

"And you," Mother Vellum said at last — for that was what they called her, though she corrected me later that it wasn't a title, just what remained of a word. "Tell them yours."

So I told it.

All of it. The famine year, the tithe-collectors at our village door, the choice they offered two orphaned girls: one name goes down, or both. How Wren had gripped my hand and whispered *don't*, not *don't do it* — *don't choose me* — and how I had chosen her anyway, because I was faster, because I could write, because the magistrates needed a signature and mine was the one they taught me to give. How I signed her name into their book with a hand that shook so badly the ink smeared, and how the smear had never washed out of my memory no matter how many thousand clean pages I filled after.

How I had walked out of the mountain alone, and spent ten years making myself unremarkable, and called it survival, and known every single day that it was theft wearing survival's clothes.

The ring was silent when I finished. The candles burned without flickering, because they had no wicks to flicker.

I waited for the verdict. I had been waiting ten years; I knew how to wait well.

It was Sorrel who spoke first, the midwife with the half-drawn face. "My daughter sold me," she said. "Do you know what I felt, when I understood it? Not rage. Rage came later, and it comes still, some nights. But first — first I felt relief. Because someone had paid, and the payment meant the family lived, and I would rather be a nameless coat-mender in the dark than know my girl went down into that place." She looked at me with eyes that had no edges. "You were children. The court makes children into instruments and calls it commerce."

"The court didn't hold your pen," said Aldery, the mason, and his voice was not cruel, only exact. "I held mine. I know the difference."

"Yes," I said. "So do I."

"That," said Aldery, "is why we don't condemn you. The ones who can't tell the difference — they sleep fine. You haven't slept in ten years. I can see it in how you sit."

Mother Vellum raised a hand, and the ring went quiet.

"We are not a court," she said. "We tried being a court, in the early years. We kept trials, verdicts, sentences. Do you know what happened? We became a smaller copy of the thing below. So now we keep only accounts." She leaned toward me, and though she had no eyes, I felt seen more thoroughly than I had ever been seen in my life. "Your account is open, cartographer. One soul sold, one soul returned carrying the collection notice. The court will come for her. They will come through us if they must, and we are hard to find, but not impossible — nothing is impossible to them; that is their whole nature. Unless the account closes."

"How does it close?" Ash asked. My brave, terrible girl. Always asking the question pointed at the wound.

"Return what was taken," Mother Vellum said. "Or pay equivalent weight." She paused, and the pause had weight of its own, the kind of weight that lets you know what's coming before it arrives. "There is a third way. There is always a third way with the court, and it is always worse. But the road down passes through the tithe-gates, and the tithe-gates take payment from everyone who walks them. Sound. Memory. Years. Faces, sometimes." Her smooth face tilted. "You will need a guide who has already paid everything there is to pay. Someone the gates cannot bill."

The ring looked at her. She looked back at all of us, faceless, and I understood, with a lurch, what she was offering.

"You," I said.

"I have nothing left for them to take," Mother Vellum said. "I am the cheapest passage down that mountain has ever offered. And I—" here her voice did something I had not heard it do, something almost human, almost breakable— "I have wanted to see the court again for thirty years. From the outside. With witnesses."

Ash's hand found mine. Small, mud-stained at the knuckles still, gripping hard.

"Kestrel goes down," Ash said, in her stitched-together common speech, "and Kestrel comes back up. Both. I saw it in the roots." She looked around the ring of the Nameless, chin up, eight years old and older than the kingdom. "Say it back so it becomes true."

And Mother Vellum — who had no face to smile with, and smiled anyway, in the set of her shoulders, in the stillness of her hands — said:

"She goes down, and she comes back up. Both. Spoken and witnessed." She rose from her throne of broken ledgers. "Rest tonight, cartographer. Tomorrow we open the tithe-road. And tomorrow night—" the blankness of her face seemed, for a moment, almost kind— "you should know what waits at the first gate. It takes language first. It will ask you for the tongue you swore never to speak again."

I looked at Ash. Ash looked at me.

Behind us, in the candle hall, the map in my satchel stirred against the leather like something waking, and I did not need to open it to know that a new line was writing itself, in ink I did not control, describing a road going down — and three travelers on it, and above the third traveler, where a name should be, a small careful blank.

*Debtors,* the map had asked. *Whose?*

By morning, I suspected, it would have its answer.
