## 13. Descent

The tithe-road began where the cobblestones gave up pretending.

One moment we were walking the last street of the undercity, past the shuttered lamp-shrines and the doors the Nameless had chalked with warnings; the next, the road simply became something older—packed earth and black stone, worn smooth in two parallel grooves like a cart had been dragging grief up and down it for a thousand years. Mother Vellum stood at the threshold with us long enough to say what she'd come to say.

"The mountain takes as you go," she told me. "Not all at once. In tolls. You won't get to choose what it takes, but you will feel it leave." Her faceless head tilted toward Ash. "The child will feel nothing. That's not mercy. That's arithmetic."

"And if I refuse to pay?"

"You can't refuse. That's what *toll* means." She pressed something into my hand—a scrap of vellum, blank, soft as skin. "For when you arrive. The court respects paper. It's the only language they never learned to lie in."

Then she was gone, folding back into the undercity the way water folds back into a well, and it was just Ash and me and the road going down.

* * *

The first hour, the mountain ate sound.

I noticed it as an absence at the edges—my bootfalls made no echo. Then they made no sound at all. I said something to Ash, testing, and watched my own mouth move around words that died before they reached the air. She looked at me with wide eyes and touched her throat, and I understood: her voice had been taken from her once already, down there beneath us, and the mountain recognized its own property. It wasn't going to eat hers twice.

Mine, apparently, was fair game.

We fell into a rhythm without speech. She walked half a step ahead, the way she always did now, as if she were leading me somewhere she remembered and I didn't—which, I was beginning to suspect, was exactly true. The road switchbacked down through rock that got darker and stranger, veins of something pale running through it like gristle. Once I could have sworn the pale veins were writing. Once I could have sworn they were legible. I stopped looking after that.

At the second hour, the first toll came due.

I felt it the way you feel a tooth being pulled—not the pain first, but the wrongness of the space it leaves behind. I stopped mid-stride. My hand went to my chest as though I could hold whatever it was in place.

It was my mother's face.

I don't mean I forgot her name, or her voice, or the way she hummed while she worked. Those came later, or maybe they went earlier—I couldn't have told you the order anymore than you can tell the order of things stolen from a house while you sleep. I mean I reached for her face, the way you reach for a thing you've kept your whole life, and found a smooth blank where it should have been. I knew she had one. I knew it had been kind, or stern, or both; I knew I had loved it. But the picture itself—the actual image, the thing every other memory hung from—was gone. Lifted out clean. Paid.

Ash turned back when I stumbled. She took my hand, and her grip was warm and certain, and here is the thing I will spend the rest of this account trying to make you understand: her grip was *warmer and more certain than it had been an hour ago.* As if something had left me and arrived in her. As if the mountain weren't eating at all, only moving food from one mouth to another.

"Kess," she said—in common tongue, rough and new, one of the forty-odd words I'd taught her in the vault. She said it again, firmer, like she was pinning the word to me so the mountain couldn't take it too. "Kess. Here."

"I'm here," I said, and heard my own voice come out thinner than before.

We walked on.

* * *

The tolls kept their own schedule, indifferent to our pace.

My mother's humming went somewhere in the third hour. My father's hands—calloused, good with rope—went in the fourth, and I only noticed because I caught myself trying to remember how he'd taught me to tie a knot and found my fingers performing it perfectly while my mind supplied nothing at all. Muscle keeps what memory loses. I tied the knot three times on the empty air, grieving a man whose face I still had but whose hands had become a rumor.

By the sixth hour I had lost the smell of bread baking. By the seventh, the color of the door of the house I grew up in—I knew it had been blue, or green, or possibly red, and the *possibly* was the worst part, because doubt is just loss with its boots on.

And through all of it, Ash grew.

It wasn't dramatic. She didn't glow, didn't change shape, didn't do any of the things souls do in the stories the guild used to tell to frighten new apprentices into honesty. She simply became more *present*. When she walked, the ground seemed to agree with her footsteps. When she looked at the dark, the dark seemed to look back politely. Words I'd taught her came easier to her now, arriving in whole sentences instead of fragments, and sometimes she said things I hadn't taught her at all—phrases with an old rhythm underneath them, a cadence from before kingdoms—and I understood that Wren's soul was rising in her like water finding its level, filling the vessel as the vessel's other occupant drained away.

That was the design. I saw it clearly somewhere around the eighth hour, sitting on a ledge of black stone sharing the last of the undercity bread. The court hadn't sent Ash up as a collection notice by accident. They'd sent her up tuned to me, resonant, because a soul sold by Kestrel Thackeray could only be collected *through* Kestrel Thackeray. Every toll the mountain took from me made the resonance stronger. Every memory that left me made her more herself. By the time we reached the bottom, there would be very little of me left to argue with, and all of Wren there to be received.

They hadn't built a trap. They'd built a scale.

Ash watched me work this out—watched my face do whatever my face does when I map something terrible—and she put her small hand flat on the stone between us. Then, deliberately, she dragged her finger through the dust and wrote, in careful common letters:

*YOU CAN STOP.*

"I can't."

*TURN AROUND. GO UP.*

"And leave you to them?" I shook my head. "Ash. Wren. Whoever you are right now, whoever you're becoming—you climbed ten years to get back up that mountain. I'm not the reason you came back. But I'm the reason you had to. So I'm going down with you, and I'm going to stand in front of whatever's down there, and I'm going to pay whatever it costs, and then—" My voice did something I didn't authorize. "—and then you get to be whole. That's the whole transaction. That's the entire ledger."

She looked at me for a long moment. Then she wiped out her words with her palm and wrote new ones:

*STUPID. BRAVE. SAME.*

I laughed, and the laugh echoed, which startled us both—the mountain letting me keep that, at least, for now—and we walked on.

* * *

The ninth hour took my native language.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is the last tool I have left. I was born speaking two tongues: the common speech of the city, and the older language, the pre-kingdom tongue, the one the Hollow Choir spoke before anyone thought to call anything a kingdom. My mother sang in it. I dreamed in it. For ten years I had refused to speak it aloud because it was the language of the court, the language I'd bargained in, the language of the worst night of my life—and keeping it locked in my mouth had been both penance and armor.

The mountain took it the way you'd take a coat off someone who isn't looking. One moment the words were folded away inside me, available; the next moment I reached for one—just to test, just to say *hello* to Ash in the old sounds—and found the shapes of them gone. Not forbidden. *Gone.* I tried again and produced only common syllables, clumsy as a child's. Somewhere below us, a magistrate had just added a language to a ledger, and somewhere above us, a girl who no longer existed had sung in it to her daughter, and neither fact would ever again be connected to me.

Ash grabbed my arm. She'd seen it happen—seen something go out of my eyes—and she started talking fast in the old tongue, pouring it at me like she could catch the spill and pour it back. And I understood every word.

That was the horror of it. Comprehension stayed. Only production left. I could understand the language of my childhood perfectly and would never speak it again, the way an exile understands the country she can never return to. Ash said: *You're paying too much, it's taking too much, let me carry some of it, give me some of it to carry* —and I could only answer in the flat common words:

"I can't give you what the mountain takes. It doesn't work through hands."

She stared at me, frustrated, and then—clever, always clever—she picked up a stone and scratched into the dust of the road, in the old script, the pre-kingdom characters that predated every alphabet the guild kept in its vaults:

*I REMEMBER FOR YOU.*

And she began, right there on the tithe-road, in the dark, to recite. My mother's songs—or Wren's memory of my mother's songs, which amounted to the same thing now, since Wren had sat beside me on that hearth-floor and learned them too, before either of us knew what names cost. She sang them in a voice that was eight years old and centuries deep, and I listened, and I wept without noise because the mountain had probably taken crying loudly from me an hour ago and I hadn't noticed, and the songs went into me shallow and wouldn't stay, but for the length of each verse I had them. Borrowed memory. Secondhand childhood.

When she finished, she wrote one more line in the dust:

*THAT'S WHAT I'M FOR NOW.*

"No," I said, kneeling, taking her chalky little hands in mine. "That's not what you're for. You're not a ledger, Ash. You're not anybody's storage." I swallowed. "But thank you. While it lasts. Thank you."

* * *

Somewhere past the twelfth hour, I lost the guild.

Not the building—I lost my feeling for it. Ten years of corridors, of Ovid Sarn's lessons in unremarkability, of ledgers balanced by candlelight, of the specific creak of the third stair in the map vault—all of it thinned to a story someone had told me about a stranger. I tested myself: *What does Sarn's study smell like?* Ink, obviously, and—nothing. *Which wall of the vault has the false panel?* I knew there was one. I could not have found it with both hands. The mountain was working inward now, eating the load-bearing walls, and I could feel the structure of myself shifting, settling, deciding what to collapse first.

Here is what surprised me: how little I fought.

I had spent a decade making myself unremarkable, erasing myself entry by entry, and it turned out all that practice had been training for this. The mountain and I wanted, in our different ways, the same thing. There was a horrible comfort in it, like sinking into a bath too hot to bear but exactly the temperature of your guilt. Some part of me—the part that had signed Wren's name away with a shaking hand and told itself it was survival—some part of me considered this fair pricing.

Ash would not allow it.

Every time I slowed, she pulled. Every time my eyes went distant, she waved her hand in front of my face and demanded, in her growing stock of common words, *LOOK. HERE. ME.* She appointed herself the keeper of my remaining inventory. When I lost the name of my first master cartographer, she made me describe maps until I found things that were still mine—the craft itself, the gift, the way a coastline wants to be drawn. When I lost my own birthday, she invented one on the spot and declared it today, and made me celebrate it on the side of a haunted mountain with the last heel of bread divided unevenly in her favor, because she said birthdays belonged to the younger person and she was eight.

"You're not eight," I said. "You're ten years older than me, probably."

*WHOLE LATER,* she wrote. *EIGHT NOW.*

So we descended, and I dimmed, and she brightened, and the exchange rate never varied, and I loved her with a love that had less and less of a person attached to it and somehow weighed more all the same.

* * *

Near the bottom, the mountain took one thing I fought for.

It came for the memory of the sale itself—the night, the bargain, the exact words I'd spoken when I traded Wren's name for my passage. And I felt it lifting, felt the guilt going soft and blameless in my mind, felt myself becoming a woman who had merely *been present* at some unfortunate historical event—and I dug in. I don't know how. I gripped it the way you grip a rope over a drop, with everything, with nails and teeth and spite, and I said into the dark, in the common tongue because it was all I had left: "No. That one stays. Someone has to remember what I did. If it's not me, it's no one."

The mountain paused. The whole mountain paused—I felt the pause in the stone under my feet, a held breath a mile thick.

Then it took something adjacent instead. Something nearby on the shelf. I never learned what. To this hour I don't know what else I paid that night, what gap I've been living around ever since, what piece of me walks loose somewhere in the dark under the world wearing my name tag. That may be the cruelest arithmetic of all: the mountain doesn't itemize.

Ash found me on my knees afterward, shaking, holding my own head as if it might come apart. She knelt with me. She didn't write anything. She just put her forehead against mine, the way children do when they are comforting someone much larger, and stayed there until I could stand.

*ALMOST THERE,* she wrote finally.

She was right. The road had stopped switchbacking. Ahead, the black stone opened into a light that wasn't light—a vast pale glow coming up from below, the color of paper held against a candle, the color of a page with something being written on its far side. And rising out of that glow, faint but unmistakable, came a sound the mountain had spared because it was the mountain's own heartbeat: the scratch of an enormous nib, writing, always writing.

Ash's hand found mine. Hers burned; mine barely registered as warm.

"Whatever happens down there," I said—and my voice sounded borrowed even to me, thin paper over someone else's fire—"whatever they offer, whatever they promise, whatever I say—remember that I chose this. Write it somewhere. You're the only ledger I trust now."

She squeezed my hand once, hard, and stepped with me over the last threshold, onto ground that was soft as vellum and gave slightly, like skin, like a page waiting to be written on.

Below us, lit by the glow of its own eternal sentence, the court beneath the mountain opened its doors—and the doors were ledgers, standing upright, and they opened outward to receive us, and a voice like a nib touching down said, in perfect courtesy, in the language I could still understand and never speak again:

"*The seller ascends no longer. Descend, Kestrel-of-the-blankening. Your account has been expecting you.*"

It knew. Even with half of me gone—even with the tolls paid and the shelves stripped—it knew my name.

And I realized, with a cold that had nothing to do with the mountain, that it had known the whole way down.
