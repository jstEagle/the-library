## 15. Terms of Return

The court received us the way a bank receives a depositor: politely, and with an eye on the scale.

We came up out of the tithe-road into a hall I had no memory of entering, though I had been here before—ten years ago, in another body, under another name. The cathedral of stacked ledgers rose around us in shelves that went up past seeing, each book fat as a coffin, spines lettered in scripts that hurt to look at directly. Somewhere above, the Presiding Quill wrote on. Its sound was not a sound so much as a pressure, a slow arterial pulse of ink.

Ash's hand was in mine. Her grip had changed since the descent. On the mountain she had been a child dragging me forward; now her fingers were steady, deliberate, almost adult, and when she looked at the shelves she did it the way a person looks at a house they once lived in.

*You know this place,* I said in the old tongue.

*I dreamed it,* she answered. *Or it dreamed me. I don't know which way the dreaming goes down here.*

A magistrate detached itself from between two shelves. It wore a face like an unfinished sketch—the same kind we'd fled at the guild—but this one was further along, more of its lines committed to ink. It inclined its head to me with terrible courtesy.

"Kestrel Thackeray," it said. "Cartographer. Ledger-keeper. Seller." Each word landed like a stamp on wax. "You are expected."

"I'm not here to be collected," I said. "I'm here to file a petition."

Something moved behind the sketch-lines that might have been amusement. "No one files petitions here. They are filed *against* you. But the Quill is curious. It has been writing your name for ten years without ever once getting to write it down. Curiosity is expensive. Come."

* *

They gave us a chamber that was less a room than a margin—a narrow space between two ledger-walls where a table stood, and on the table, a single open book. The pages were blank. As I watched, words surfaced in them like bodies rising slowly through dark water: my petition, already written, already granted in principle, already annotated with terms I hadn't offered.

"The Quill has reviewed your request," the magistrate said. It did not sit; magistrates never sat; sitting implied a body that could tire. "Restoration of the soul designated Wren, sold by Kestrel Thackeray in the year of the last bad harvest, payment rendered to the court beneath the mountain, one soul against one passage. The sale was lawful. The merchandise has, however, walked off the premises."

"It climbed back," Ash said. Her voice in the common tongue was still new, still rough-edged, but she used it deliberately now, making them hear her in their own language. "It climbed for ten years. You can't sell something that refuses to stay sold."

"All sales are final," the magistrate said gently. "That is what makes them sales. However." It turned the open book toward me. "The court is not without mercy. Mercy is simply a line item, like everything else. The soul can be restored to the vessel it currently inhabits. The debt can be discharged. All that is required is equivalent payment."

I looked at the page. The terms were written there in advance, which meant they had always been written there, which meant the whole descent—the tolls, the lost memories, my mother's face dissolving like sugar in rain—had been the court clearing away everything else I might have offered, until only one thing remained on the scale.

"A name," I said. "Of equal weight."

"Freely given," the magistrate agreed. "Not extracted. Not traded under duress. That is the old law, older than kingdoms, older than this court. A name taken is theft. A name *given*—" it spread its unfinished hands, "—is the only currency that appreciates."

"And mine would weigh enough?" I kept my voice flat, cartographer's voice, measuring terrain. "I've spent ten years making myself light. Unremarkable. I barely use the thing."

The magistrate made the sound again, that almost-amusement. "Do you imagine weight is measured by how loudly a name is spoken? Yours has been growing heavier every year you carried it in secret. Guilt is a form of interest. Ten years of compounding." It tapped the page. "Kestrel Thackeray. Daughter of a dyer who drowned. Sister-by-choice to a girl sold in your place. Author of three hundred maps of places that refuse to exist. Yes. It weighs."

Ash grabbed my sleeve. In the old tongue, fast and fierce: *Don't listen. Don't. There's another way—we'll go back up, we'll find Mother Vellum, we'll—*

"There isn't another way," I said. "You know there isn't. You've known since the vault."

*Then take mine,* she said. *Take what's left of Wren's name—it's half mine anyway, it's been living in my mouth—*

"That's not how it works and you know it." I crouched to her level. Her eyes were wrong for a child's face—too old, too patient—and right at the same time, because some part of Wren had always been old even at nine years old, even before I ruined us. "If I take anything from you again, even with permission, even as a gift, then nothing changes. Then I'm just the person who takes from you. Do you understand? This is the only trade left where I'm the one paying."

*You promised,* she whispered. *On the stair that wasn't there. You said we'd both walk out.*

"I lied," I said. "People who keep their names don't have to lie. People selling them do."

* *

They gave us an hour to decide, which was a courtesy, and the courtesy frightened me more than any threat could have. Courts that need to threaten you are uncertain. This court had simply written the ending and was letting me arrive at it at my own pace.

We sat in the margin between the shelves. Above us the Quill wrote on, patient as erosion.

"When I was small," Ash said, in the common tongue now, practicing, "before—I mean after, when I was climbing—I used to make up stories about you. About the person who'd sold me." She picked at a loose thread on her cuff. "I thought you'd be tall. I thought you'd have a knife. I thought if I ever found you I'd say the name at you and you'd fall down dead, because that's what names do in stories."

"And instead?"

"Instead you taught me to say *bread* and *door* and *don't be afraid.*" She looked up. "Instead you drew me a map of yourself with all the worst rooms labeled. Nobody does that, Kess. Nobody shows someone the rooms where they keep the thing they did."

"Kess," I repeated. "Is that what you call me?"

"When the other name feels too heavy to carry, I carry that one instead." Her chin came up, stubborn, eight years old and ten years old and centuries old all at once. "So no. You can't give them Kestrel Thackeray. I'm using it. Finders keepers. That's guild law—you told me."

"Ash—"

"Wren," she corrected, quietly, and my heart turned over. "My name is Wren. And Wren says you don't get to disappear. I didn't climb back up through ten years of dark to watch you turn into a blank page. I climbed up to *find* you. What good is being found if the finder gets erased?"

I took her hands. They were small and warm and they had pulled me up a mountain.

"If I don't pay," I said, "they take you back down. Or worse—they leave you here as you are, half-assembled, a collection notice walking around in shoes. I did that to you once. I will not do it twice. Whatever else I am, whatever name I die under or don't, I am not going to be the person who chose herself over you again. Once was the whole allotment. There isn't a second time in me."

"You could run," she said. "You're good at running. You mapped the running."

"And the map rewrites itself around our choices, remember? I wrote that line myself, in my own blood-ink, on the stair." I smiled, and it cost more than the mountain had. "There's no unmapped corridor out of this one, little wren. I've checked. I've spent ten years checking every exit from myself, and they all lead back to the same room."

She was crying now, silently, the way children cry when they've learned crying doesn't change the verdict. "It's not fair."

"No," I agreed. "It's a fair bargain. Those are different things. Fair bargains are the cruelest traps there are, because you can't even hate the trap. You can only hate that you fit inside it."

Above us, the Quill paused.

I felt the pause the way you feel a held breath in a crowded room. Ten years of endless writing, and it had stopped, waiting for me. Somewhere in those infinite shelves a book lay open with my petition in it, and beneath the petition, a signature line, blank as I had spent a decade teaching myself to be.

"Terms accepted?" the magistrate asked, materializing at the margin's edge with the softness of a bookmark sliding home.

Ash's grip crushed my fingers. *Don't,* she said in the old tongue, the language of roots and before, the first language either of us ever spoke. *Don't don't don't.*

I stood up. My legs worked. That seemed like information worth noting.

"One condition," I said.

The magistrate waited.

"She walks out free. Not restored-and-tithed, not freed-with-interest, not owing the court so much as a footprint. Free the way names are supposed to be free—unpriced. Write it into the record before I sign. If the ink dries on my name before hers is unbound, the deal is void and you may chase us both."

For a long moment the sketch-face regarded me, and I understood that I had surprised it—that somewhere in the court's vast arithmetic, no seller had ever tried to negotiate upward. They had all, every one, signed fast and run.

"The Quill finds that acceptable," it said at last. "Sentiment makes such interesting entries."

"Sentiment," I said, "is the only thing you people have never managed to put a price on. I'm glad to finally deal you some."

The book on the table turned its own pages to the signature line. A pen stood ready in a groove beside it—not a quill, but a pen cut from grey bone, and I knew whose bone, and I decided not to think about that.

Ash threw her arms around my waist and held on the way drowning things hold.

"One hour," she whispered into my coat. "They gave Wren an hour, before. Take yours. Please. Please just—be somebody for one more hour, and let me tell you who you were, so that when it's done, someone remembers. Let me be your ledger, Kess. Let me keep you."

And above us, patient as erosion, the Presiding Quill lifted from its page, and waited for my hand.
