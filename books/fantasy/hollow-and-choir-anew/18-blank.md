## 18. Blank

The first thing I know is cold.

Not the court's cold, which was a cold like being filed away. This is mountain cold, wind cold, the cold of grass pressed flat under my cheek and someone else's breath moving slow beside me. I open my eyes and the sky is doing something I have no word for yet — it is *wide*, it goes on past the edges of everything, and I lie there and let it go on going on until my chest stops trying to climb out of me.

I remember climbing. I remember the dark between the ledgers, the long stair made of other people's discarded years, the way the mountain ate sound so that my own footsteps came back to me as silence. I remember being assembled. That is the part I do not say out loud, even now: that I was not born so much as *bound*, scraps of a girl stitched to scraps of something older, sent up the tithe-road like a letter no one wanted to open.

But I remember more than that now.

I remember a kitchen with a crooked door. I remember a woman singing off-key on purpose because she said songs were better when they were slightly broken. I remember hands smaller than mine holding mine in the dark under the mountain, before the dark got long, before the bargain, before—

Before Kestrel.

The name arrives whole, and with it comes everything attached to it, the way a pulled thread brings the whole hem. Wren. My name was Wren. I had a sister — not by blood, by choice, which the Nameless later tell me is the stronger kind — and she sold me, and she climbed out of the dark carrying her own name like a stolen loaf, and she spent ten years making herself small enough that no one would ever come collecting.

Except I did. Except here we are.

I turn my head. She is lying beside me in the flattened grass, and she is breathing, and she is awake — I can tell because her eyes are open, fixed on the too-wide sky, and they are full of a panic so quiet it looks almost like calm. Her lips move. Nothing comes out. She tries again, shaping a word with great care, the way you'd handle something fragile, and the word does not arrive. Her mouth closes. Opens. Closes.

She has no name.

I knew this would happen. Some scrap-deep part of me understood what the signature meant, what it costs, what the Presiding Quill takes when it takes a name of equal weight. But knowing a thing in the dark of a ledger-cathedral and seeing it lying in the grass beside you, blinking at the sky like a newborn foal, are different knowings entirely.

"Ash," I say.

Her eyes find me. There is nothing behind them looking for the sound. No flicker of recognition, no flinch, no guilt. Just a woman waking up inside a stranger's face, hearing a stranger's voice, understanding — I can see her understand — that the voice is speaking a language she used to know and cannot find anymore.

"It's all right," I say, and then I stop, because it isn't all right, and because those words belong to her, they were hers first, she said them to me in the vault with her hand shaking on my shoulder, and hearing them come out of my mouth in her place is like wearing someone else's coat.

I sit up. The world tilts and steadies. Below us the tithe-road unspools down the mountainside, pale as a scar, and far below that the city sits in its valley with its ten thousand chimneys smoking, all of them forgetting things all day long.

My body feels strange. Stronger. When I flex my fingers, they answer like they've been waiting years for the order. When I breathe, the air goes deep, all the way down, into rooms I didn't know I had. Wren's memories sit inside me not like borrowed furniture but like furniture that was always mine, returned from storage, still wrapped in cloth. I unwrap them one at a time and each one fits.

The kitchen with the crooked door. The off-key song. A name for the song, even: *The Miller's Daughter*, and the woman who sang it wrong on purpose was called Marrow, and she had flour on her knuckles always, always, no matter how often she washed.

I am crying. I notice it the way you notice rain starting — not all at once, but suddenly everywhere. These are not only my tears. Some of them are hers, held in trust for ten years, paid out now with interest.

Beside me, the nameless woman has curled onto her side, knees drawn up, arms around herself, watching me cry with an expression of terrible polite helplessness. She wants to comfort me. I can see her wanting it. She has no idea how.

* * *

We walk down. Or rather, I walk and she follows, one pace behind and half a step to the left, the position of a servant or a shadow, and every time I notice it I stop and wait until she draws level, and every time she drifts back again without seeming to decide to. Ten years of making herself unremarkable don't vanish with a signature. Her body remembers how to be invisible even if her mind has forgotten everything else.

She doesn't speak. I think she can't. Not the words themselves — I catch her mouthing shapes sometimes, testing syllables like loose teeth — but the *saying*. A name is the hook everything hangs from, and hers is gone, and when she reaches for a sentence it slips off the wall of her and clatters somewhere she can't follow.

So I talk enough for both of us.

I tell her about the road. I point out where the mountain ate sounds — there's a bend where you can shout and hear nothing come back, and I demonstrate, and she startles, and then, wonder of wonders, she laughs. It comes out of her sideways, surprised, like a sneeze, and she claps her hand over her mouth as though she's stolen something.

"You're allowed," I tell her. "Laughing is free. It's the only thing left that is."

By late afternoon we reach the treeline, and at the treeline we are found.

They come out of the trees the way weather comes — no single moment of arrival, just a gradual presence, figures in grey-brown rags with faces like unfinished sketches. Bailiff-faces. Magistrate-faces. My whole body clenches around itself before I understand that these are not them, cannot be them, because these figures have *eyes*, warm and human and tired, where the magistrates had only blank paper.

"Nameless," I say, using the word Mother Vellum gave me, and the nearest figure smiles with a mouth full of gaps.

"And who's this, then?" she says, nodding at the woman behind me. "Got a name, love?"

"She did," I say. "She spent it."

The smile goes off the woman's face slowly, like a tide going out. She looks at the nameless one — really looks, the way you look at a wound — and then she does something I will remember for the rest of however long I last. She bows. Deep, from the waist, ragged skirt sweeping pine needles.

"Then welcome," she says, "to the country of the spent. We've room."

* * *

The undercity smells of tallow and wet stone and, underneath everything, faintly, of bread. The Nameless live in the bones of the city above — old cisterns, sealed crypts, a chapel whose saint was chiseled off the wall so long ago only the outline remains, a hollow saint-shaped absence that they've hung a lantern in. They call the chapel the Hollow Choir. When I heard the name I laughed until I cried, and nobody asked why, which is its own kind of grace.

They give us a corner of the chapel, two bedrolls of sacking, a shared blanket that smells of woodsmoke. The nameless woman lies down stiffly, like someone sleeping in a stranger's house, which I suppose she is — the stranger's house being her own body.

I should sleep too. I'm tired in a way that lives in the marrow. But instead I sit cross-legged beside her in the lantern light, and I watch her watch the ceiling, and after a while I realize what the panic in her eyes is asking. It isn't *who am I*. She has stopped asking that; there's no one left inside to ask. It's *what am I for*. A page with nothing written wants, more than anything, to be written on.

So I begin.

"You had a mother," I say. "Her name was Sef. She smelled of ink and river water, and she taught you letters by drawing them in spilled flour, and she died when you were nine, and you kept her pen for eleven years after, even after it stopped writing, because throwing it away felt like letting her die twice."

The nameless woman turns her head. Her eyes have gone very wide and very still.

"You were apprenticed to a mapmaker named Corvus Dill, who was a fraud and a bully and, though he never once said a kind word to you, he was the one who saw your gift before anyone else did, and some mean men are honest in exactly one place." I pause. "You hated him. You kept his compass anyway."

Her hand moves — involuntary, reflexive — toward her belt, where no compass hangs anymore, where nothing hangs anymore, where the guild took everything when she ran. Her fingers close on empty air and stay there, and I watch her understand that the memory I just gave her is real, is *hers*, is verifiable by the shape of her own reaching.

"You sang," I say. "Badly. On purpose. You said songs were better slightly broken."

A tear slides out of the corner of her eye and into her hair, and she doesn't wipe it away, and neither do I. Some tears you have to let land where they land.

"I'll tell you one every night," I say. "That's the arrangement. One story a night, until you've got enough of yourself back to argue with me about the details. And you *will* argue. You argued about everything. You once fought a toll-keeper for twenty minutes over two coppers that turned out to be his."

Something happens to her mouth. It isn't quite a smile. It's the place a smile will be built, ground cleared, foundations laid.

"Wren," she whispers.

It's barely a sound. Half of it is breath. But it's the first word she's managed since the mountainside, and it's not a question and it's not a plea — it's a *correction*. She is telling me: that is not my name, whoever I am. That is yours. Give it back.

"Not yet," I say gently. "When you're you again. If I take it back now, while you're blank, it'll just be a word. It has to be earned back by the right person." I lie down beside her and pull half the blanket over us both. "Besides. I've gotten used to it. Eight years old for ten years — you try having a name that doesn't fit."

In the dark, her breathing slows. Just before sleep takes her, her hand finds mine in the space between the bedrolls and holds on, and her grip is strong, a grown woman's grip, a cartographer's grip, ink-stained and sure, and I hold on back and think: *she's in there. All of her. Filed away but not destroyed.*

The Quill doesn't destroy. That's the horror of it. It only *unwrites*, and anything unwritten can, in principle, be written again.

I fall asleep planning tomorrow's story. I have hundreds. I have ten years of hers plus eight of mine, and I intend to spend every single one of them on her, nightly, like interest on a debt the court will never see.

* * *

Three nights in, the stories start working backwards.

That is, she starts telling them to me. Haltingly, in fragments, with long silences where the words should be — but she tells them. She corrects me. The pen wasn't eleven years, it was nine; she counted. The argument with the toll-keeper lasted forty minutes, not twenty, and she won, and she bought a meat pie with the winnings and didn't share it, and she has regretted the not-sharing ever since, and the regret, she tells me with sudden fierce clarity, is the most solid thing she owns.

"That's how I know it's me," she says, in the dark of the Hollow Chapel, lantern guttering. "The regrets. The Quill took the name and the face of my life and the sound of my mother's voice. But it left the shape of where everything was. Like — " her hands move, sketching in the air, and I recognize the gesture with a jolt, the way she used to draft coastlines, quick sure strokes — "like a map of a burned house. You can't live in it. But you can find the doors."

"You're drawing maps again," I say, awed.

She looks at her own hands as if they belong to a stranger, which they do, which they always did, and then she looks at me, and the panic in her eyes has changed register. It's quieter now. Deeper. The panic of someone who has found the doors of the burned house and walked through them and discovered that the person who lived there did something unforgivable, and the person who lived there is also the only person keeping her alive, and both of these things are true at once.

Because she's remembering now. Not everything. But enough.

Enough to remember the vault, and the bargain, and the stair. Enough to remember a younger self in the dark under the mountain, gripping another girl's hand, choosing. Enough to know exactly what was taken from me and who signed the bill of sale.

She doesn't say any of it. She rolls over and faces the wall, and her shoulders shake once, twice, and then go still, and I pretend to be asleep, and the lantern burns down, and neither of us forgives or accuses or speaks, and somehow that silence is the most honest conversation we've ever had.

* * *

On the sixth night, Mother Vellum comes to the chapel.

She moves through the undercity like a rumor — everyone knows she's coming long before she arrives — and when she ducks through the chapel door the whole room adjusts around her, the way water adjusts around a dropped stone. Her face is smooth where a face should be. It is not horrifying. It is worse than horrifying; it is *inviting*, the way an unwritten page is inviting, and I understand suddenly why the Nameless follow her. She is what they could become if they stopped grieving. She is erasure worn like a crown.

"The magistrates came to the guildhouse," she says, without preamble. "Two nights ago. Sketch-faced, three of them, with a black seal and a list."

My stomach drops through the floor, through the crypt, through whatever is under crypts.

"They're hunting a runaway soul," Vellum continues, "and a woman with no name. The guildmaster sold them the corridor routes. He's bought himself a week, maybe less." Her blank face turns toward the nameless woman, then to me, and though she has no eyes I feel looked through rather than at. "The undercrofts won't hold. Sooner or later the seal finds everyone it's marked."

"Then we run," I say.

"You'd run *down*," Vellum says. "Back along the tithe-road. Toward the court." She lets that sit in the smoky air. "There's a door in the mountain that opens only for the named. Your friend there has no name to open it with. But you—" and the smooth face tilts, considering, "—you carry a name that was returned to you whole. Wren. Sold, reclaimed, restored. The court's own seal is on that restoration. Which means, little soul, that you are the only living person who can knock on that door and be *recognized*."

The nameless woman sits bolt upright. "No," she says — her longest sentence yet, her voice rusty and cracking and absolutely certain. "No. She's not going back down there. She *climbed out*."

"So did you," Vellum says mildly. "And here you both are, hiding in a hole while the ink dries on a hunt that ends with one of you filed and the other erased forever." She straightens. "I can get you to the foot of the mountain. Past the checkpoints, through the drowned quarter, three days' travel. After that, the road eats names, and you'll have to pay as you go, same as anyone."

"Why?" I ask. "Why help us? You don't know us. You owe us nothing."

Mother Vellum's blank face does something I have no word for — a softening, a settling, like snow finding its level.

"Ten years ago," she says, "a girl came up out of the mountain and sold her sister's name to buy her passage, and the Nameless caught her at the city gate, and we put her on trial, and we found her guilty, and we sentenced her to the journey — down and return what was taken, or be unmade." She pauses. "And then she slipped our sentence. Ran. Hid. Made herself small. For ten years we thought the debt was dead."

The chapel is very quiet. Beside me, the nameless woman has gone rigid.

"But debts don't die," Vellum says. "They just wait for the debtor to grow a spine. And now here she is — or what's left of her — and here *you* are, carrying the proof that the debt was finally paid." The blank face inclines toward me, courteous as a magistrate's, and for one horrible second I can't tell the difference. "The Nameless keep their accounts, little soul. We're taking you home."

Outside, far above, through the grate that lets in the city's smoke, something vast and patient begins to write.
