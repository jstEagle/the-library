## 21. Hollow, Choir Anew

The seal on the guild door cracked at dawn.

I heard it from the ledger room—a sound like ice giving way on a winter pond, like a page being torn cleanly down the middle—and I was moving before I finished waking. A year of living in this building had taught me its language: the groan of the stairs that weren't there, the sigh of the map vault breathing out old air, the particular silence the master ledger kept when it had something to say.

This morning it said nothing. It didn't have to. The black wax on the front door had split along three lines, and through those lines came a smell I knew better than my own name—which was saying something, because I'd only owned my name for eleven months.

Mountain air. Ink. The cold of places that refuse to exist.

"They're coming," I said to the empty hall, and then, louder, because the guild had learned to answer: "They're coming!"

* * *

We had practiced for this. That sounds absurd—I know it does—but Ovid Sarn was many terrible things before he became something almost good, and one thing he had always been was thorough about catastrophe. In the months since he'd written me back into the world, since Mother Vellum's hand and Wren's and his own had converged over that blank entry bearing my seal, Sarn had drilled us the way a harbor-master drills for flood.

"Ledgers balanced," he'd say, pacing the map vault with his ruined hands clasped behind him. "Every account current. Every debt either paid or discharged in writing. The court cannot collect what is not owed. Understand me—the magistrates are not strong. They are *arithmetic*. They come for imbalance the way water comes for a crack."

So we balanced. For a year, the Guild of Impossible Things kept books so clean they squeaked. We settled debts no one remembered contracting. We found the heirs of people erased by the court and returned to them whatever fragments we could recover—names half-heard in dreams, faces reconstructed from the memories of strangers. Wren spent her evenings transcribing the pre-kingdom tongue into common script so that the Nameless could read their own histories again. Mother Vellum sent up word from the undercity whenever someone new crawled out of the mountain's tithe-road, and we wrote them in. Not restored—no one could be restored, not exactly—but written forward. Given a next page.

I taught cartography to anyone who walked through the door. Real maps, mostly. Places that existed and stayed put. But once a month, in the back room, I drew the other kind, and each line cost me nothing now, which was its own kind of miracle. The gift had changed when my name did. What I surrendered freely, I could spend freely. The court's arithmetic had never accounted for that.

Now the arithmetic was coming up the hill.

* * *

They arrived at midmorning, when the light through the guild's windows goes the color of weak tea.

There were three of them this time. Last time there had been two, wearing faces like unfinished sketches—features suggested rather than committed to, mouths that were mostly intention. These three were worse. Someone had tried to finish them. Their faces had detail now, but the detail was wrong in the way a forgery is wrong: eyes copied from different people, a jaw that belonged to no living bone structure. The court had been studying us. Studying *me*. And it still didn't understand what a face was for.

The lead magistrate stood at the foot of our steps and consulted a ledger of its own—black, bound in something that flexed like skin—and when it spoke, its voice was the sound a quill makes tearing through cheap paper.

"We come for the escaped soul. Returned property of the Court Beneath. Outstanding balance: one soul, whole, plus interest accrued across one year of unlawful harboring."

Behind me, the guild hall was full. Not just staff—Wren at my right shoulder, Sarn at my left, Mother Vellum's people who'd come up from the undercity for this, a dozen former clients and students and strays. All of them standing very still, the way you stand when you've decided to be a wall.

Sarn stepped forward first. He'd insisted on that. "Guildmaster Ovid Sarn," he said, and his voice only shook a little. "You'll address your claim to the guild, magistrate. We keep the ledgers here."

"The guildmaster who attempted to sell the soul in question." The magistrate's unfinished mouth curved. It had learned that much about faces, at least. "Your signature appears in our records, Ovid Sarn. You are complicit."

"I am," Sarn said. "And I've paid for it. Check your books."

The magistrate checked. I watched its finger—it had fingers now, too many joints apiece—travel down the black ledger's column, and I watched the column disagree with it. Whatever Sarn had done in the months since his creditors turned on him, whatever bargains he'd struck and debts he'd swallowed, the arithmetic showed it. His page was clean. Ugly, but clean.

"The soul," the magistrate said, recovering. "The debt of the seller, Kestrel Thackeray, was discharged by forfeiture of her name. But the *soul* remains court property. Sold goods do not cease to be sold because the seller repents."

Here it was. The argument we'd rehearsed forty times.

Wren stepped past me before I could stop her.

She was taller than she'd been as Ash. Eleven months of full soul will do that—fill a body out, put weight behind the eyes. She wore her hair short now and ink-stained, and she spoke to the magistrate in the pre-kingdom tongue first, deliberately, letting them hear it, letting them understand that she understood everything. Then she translated herself, for the benefit of the hall:

"I am Wren. I was sold. I climbed back. And I am not property, because—" she held up the guild's master ledger, open to a page near the end, "—your own court discharged the sale. Read your numbers, magistrate. When Kestrel signed her name into the great ledger, the transaction reversed. A name of equal weight, freely given. Your Presiding Quill accepted payment. The sale is void. Void sales return the goods to—what did your law call it?—*original custody*."

"And who," the magistrate said softly, "held original custody?"

"You did," Wren said. "Before the sale. Before either of us had names anyone wanted. My soul was mine. It's mine again. Your own ink says so."

The second magistrate made a sound like a page turning against its will.

* * *

They came inside. We let them, because refusing would have been a fight and fighting them was losing; they were made of procedure, and procedure devours resistance and calls it interest. So we gave them a table instead. Sarn set out every ledger the guild owned, arranged in chronological order, tabs of green ribbon marking the relevant entries. Mother Vellum's people stood along the walls with their blank faces and their patient hands, and I saw the magistrates look at them and look away. You cannot audit a person who has already been erased. There was nothing left to take.

For two hours they went through our books.

I will give the court this: it reads carefully. The lead magistrate turned pages with those too-many-jointed fingers and cross-referenced entries against its black ledger, and twice it found things—a misfiled discharge, a debt we'd recorded as forgiven that technically remained open until Wren tracked down the creditor's heir and got a signature—and both times we fixed it on the spot, in front of them, in ink. Sarn's hand shook the first time. Mine didn't. I had been a ledger-keeper for ten years before I was anything else, and it turns out that ten years doesn't wash out. It just changes whose books you're keeping.

Near the end, the lead magistrate stopped on the page that mattered. My page. The one Sarn and Vellum and Wren had written together, the entry that had made me out of a blank.

It read for a long time.

"This name," it said finally. "'Kestrel.' It is not the name that was forfeited."

"No," I said. "It's new. Freely chosen. Written by witnesses."

"The court does not recognize names written by witnesses."

"The court recognized the forfeiture," Wren said. "Freely given. Your own terms. Equivalent payment, equal weight. If the old name was heavy enough to buy a soul whole, then any name given with the same weight is valid currency. You can't have it both ways, magistrate. Either names mean what they weigh, or your entire court is counting with nothing."

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight and temperature. The fire popped in the grate. Somewhere upstairs, a student of ours—young Ferro, who still couldn't draw a coastline without giggling—was practicing scales on a borrowed viol, badly, and the bad music came down through the floorboards like a heartbeat.

The lead magistrate looked at me. Its forged eyes tried to find something in my face—the woman who'd been a blank page, the woman who'd signed herself away, the woman these people had written back. Whatever it was looking for, it didn't find, because it wasn't there anymore. That woman had been spent. What stood in her place was something the court had no category for: a person who owed it nothing, feared it less than she feared losing the people behind her, and kept immaculate books.

"The account," the magistrate said at last, "will require review by the Presiding Quill."

"Then request it," Sarn said. "We'll wait."

* * *

We didn't wait long. We waited exactly as long as it takes a cathedral of stacked ledgers to consult an enormous hand.

The black seal reformed on our table—not on the door this time; they brought it indoors, which I chose to take as a courtesy or a surrender, and I've decided since then it was both. The wax pooled and rose and shaped itself into writing, and the writing moved, and all of us leaned in to read the hand of the Presiding Quill itself.

I had seen that hand before, in the court beneath the mountain, writing eternally across a desk the size of a field. I had felt it acknowledge me with terrible courtesy. Now it wrote small, which somehow frightened me more than the enormous version ever had. Small writing is intimate. Small writing is meant to be kept.

It read:

*ACCOUNT REVIEWED.*

*THE SALE OF THE SOUL CALLED WREN: VOID BY EQUIVALENT PAYMENT, ACCEPTED UNDER SEAL.*

*THE FORFEITED NAME OF THE SELLER: SPENT IN FULL, NONRECOVERABLE.*

*THE NEW NAME "KESTREL": WRITTEN BY WITNESSES, WEIGHT VERIFIED, VALID.*

*OUTSTANDING BALANCE AGAINST THE GUILD OF IMPOSSIBLE THINGS: NONE.*

*OUTSTANDING BALANCE AGAINST THE SOUL CALLED WREN: NONE.*

*OUTSTANDING BALANCE AGAINST ANY PERSON PRESENT: NONE.*

Then the writing paused. The wax shivered. And one more line appeared, slower than the others, as though even an eternal hand hesitates:

*THE COURT BENEATH IS OWED NOTHING. IT DOES NOT KNOW WHAT TO BE, WHEN IT IS OWED NOTHING.*

*ACCOUNT CLOSED. CLOSED ACCOUNTS DO NOT REOPEN. THIS IS NOT MERCY. IT IS ARITHMETIC. LEARN THE DIFFERENCE AND LIVE BY IT.*

*SO ORDERED, THE PRESIDING QUILL*

The seal cooled. Cracked. Fell apart into ordinary black dust on our table, and Mother Vellum's people swept it up before it finished settling, because we had agreed on that too: nothing of the court stays in this house overnight.

The magistrates stood very still. Then, one by one, their forged faces began to run—not melting, exactly. *Unfinishing.* The borrowed eyes slid out of alignment. The impossible jaws softened back toward suggestion. Whatever animating purpose had held them together, the closed account took it away, and what remained was three sketches again, unfinished, unfinishable, drifting toward the door like drafts that had lost their author.

At the threshold, the lead one turned. Its almost-mouth moved.

"Keep your books," it said, in a voice nearly human, nearly sad. "Someone always opens another."

Then it was gone, and the morning came in behind it, ordinary and gold and smelling of rain that hadn't fallen yet.

* * *

We closed the door. Sarn threw the bolt, which was theater—we all knew it, and we all needed it—and then the guild hall did the thing it had never done in its long history of impossible commerce.

It cheered.

Ferro abandoned his viol mid-scale and came clattering down the stairs. Mother Vellum's people didn't cheer—they were past cheering, some of them—but they smiled, which for the faceless is a whole speech. Sarn sat down heavily in the guildmaster's chair and put his ruined hands over his face, and when he took them away his eyes were wet and he dared anyone present to comment. No one did. Out loud.

Wren found me at the window.

"Well," she said.

"Well," I said.

"It's over. You heard it. Closed accounts don't reopen."

"I heard it." I watched the street below, where the city went about its business, vendors shouting, carts rattling, a dog stealing a fish with total impunity. "I believe it. Mostly."

"Mostly?"

"That last line. *Someone always opens another.*" I turned from the glass. "It wasn't a threat, Wren. It was a warning. The court isn't the only thing that buys and sells souls. It's just the only thing that keeps honest books about it. Somewhere out there is a merchant with a ledger and no seal, and no magistrate will ever come knocking on *his* door, because nobody audits the trade that looks like kindness."

Wren was quiet a moment. Then she grinned—that quick, crooked grin I'd first seen on a mud-caked child in a storm, older now, sharper, entirely her own.

"So we audit," she said. "That's the whole guild now, isn't it? We're the ones who check the math. Every bargain in this city that smells like a gift but weighs like a chain—we find it, we price it, we break it."

"That's not a retirement plan."

"We're twenty-two chapters into your life, Kestrel. Retirement was never on the table."

She was right, of course. She usually was, these days, and I'd stopped minding. I went to the master ledger where it lay open on its stand, and I picked up the pen, and beneath the entry that had made me—beneath Sarn's steady hand and Vellum's blind certainty and Wren's furious forgiveness—I wrote a single line in my own:

*Account closed. Vigilance ongoing. The choir sings anew, and this time, we keep the score.*

Below the guild, in the undercity, a bell began to ring—one of Mother Vellum's runners, signaling news traveling down. And far above the city, on the mountain's shoulder, where the tithe-road wound down into dark, I thought I heard—faint, impossible, probably the wind—

a great hand, somewhere, turning to a fresh page.
