## 1. The Lamplighter's Name

The night market of Vellamere opened only when the fog came in off the river, and it opened the way a wound opens—slowly, reluctantly, and with the promise that something underneath would be exposed.

Xanthe had lived in the city for twenty years without once being seen, and she intended to keep it that way. Invisibility was a craft, like any other. You learned to walk at the pace of everyone else, to laugh at the right volume, to buy your bread from three different bakers on rotation so no single one remembered your face. You learned never to look too long at anything, because looking was how wanting began, and wanting was how mistakes were made.

But tonight she had broken her own first rule. Tonight she had agreed to walk beside someone.

Oren lit lamps the way other men prayed. She had watched him for months before she ever spoke to him—from the window of her rooms above the chandler's shop, where she mended candle molds and sold tallow by the pound and kept her hands very, very still whenever anyone looked at them. He worked the Lamplighters' Guild rounds along Ropewalk and Tanner's Row, and there was something in the way he lifted his taper to each wick—not hurried, not bored, but attentive, as though each small flame deserved a moment of his whole attention—that made her chest ache in a way she did not permit herself to examine.

He had asked her to the market three times before she said yes. Each refusal had cost her something she couldn't name. The fourth time, he hadn't asked at all; he'd simply appeared outside her door at dusk, holding two paper cones of roasted chestnuts, and said, "I thought you might say no again, so I brought an argument you could eat."

She had laughed. It startled her, the sound of it, like a bird flushing from cover.

Now they walked the fog-thick lanes between the stalls, and Xanthe performed the ordinary woman she had spent two decades building: a widow of modest means, fond of chestnuts, unremarkable in every particular. Oren walked close enough that their sleeves brushed, and every brush felt like a small act of arson.

"You're doing it again," he said.

"Doing what?"

"Counting the exits." He nodded toward the alley mouths yawning off either side of the market lane. "You do it when we walk anywhere. I've started counting them too, just to understand you better. There are four ways out of this square, if you include the drainage culvert behind the fishmonger."

Heat climbed her neck. "Habit," she said. "The city teaches habits."

"The city teaches bad ones." He offered her the last chestnut from his cone, warm as a held secret. "My mother used to say Vellamere is a place where everyone is hiding something, so the kindest thing you can do is not look too hard. I've always thought that was the wisest thing anyone ever told me."

*If only you knew,* she thought, and ate the chestnut.

The market itself was a lesson in sanctioned magic—the kind the Edict permitted because it made money for the Guilds. Charmwrights sold luck-knots braided from horsehair and red thread. A hedge-apothecary displayed jars of feverfew and boneset under a sign reading *HERBS ONLY, NOTHING ELSE, ASK NO QUESTIONS*. Children darted between the stalls wearing paper masks of foxes and hares, and somewhere a fiddler played a tune that seemed to come from slightly to the left of where he stood.

Xanthe catalogued all of it the way she catalogued everything: which charms were harmless, which were technically illegal but ignored, which might draw a witch-finder's eye. Twenty years of practice. Twenty years since Maren, since the fire, since the scaffold in Corvasser Square where they had burned an old woman while the crowd sang a hymn about mercy.

She did not think about Maren. That was another craft she had perfected.

"Look at that," Oren said softly, and stopped.

They had reached the edge of the square, where the licensed stalls gave way to the unlicensed dark—a ragged fringe of sellers who worked by shuttered lantern-light and vanished if a watchman so much as cleared his throat. Most of what they sold was junk with pretensions: cracked scrying stones, love philtres that were mostly gin, "dragon scales" cut from fish. But one stall, set back beneath the ribs of an old warehouse stair, was different. The seller was an old man with a face like crumpled vellum, and before him, on a cloth of faded blue, lay books.

Not many. Seven or eight, arranged with a care that contradicted the junk around them. Their covers were leather gone soft as cloth, their spines stitched with thread that caught the lantern light strangely—greenish, like light through deep water.

Oren had stopped because one of the books was glowing.

Not blazing. Not flaring. Just a faint, patient luminescence beneath its cover, as if a coal slept inside the binding. As if something in it recognized him.

Xanthe felt the recognition in her own body a half-second later—a cold, fine prickling across her palms, the old telltale her mentor had spent years teaching her to smother. *Magic knows magic,* Maren used to say. *That's the first danger and the last. You can hide from people. You cannot hide from what is like you.*

"Don't touch it," she murmured.

"I wasn't going to." But Oren had gone pale in the lamplight, and his hand hung half-raised anyway, drawn like a moth. "It's—it's warm. Do you feel it? Like standing near an oven door."

The old seller watched them with eyes the color of tarnished coin. "That one's not for sale to most folk," he rasped. "Been waiting a long time for the right buyer. Longer than me, and I'm older than the bridge."

"How much?" Oren asked, which was exactly the wrong question, and Xanthe's stomach dropped as the old man smiled.

"For you? Nothing." He slid the book from the cloth and turned it over in his knotted fingers. "It was paid for a hundred years ago. I'm only the keeper till the owner comes."

"A hundred years—" Oren laughed, uneasy. "Then it can't be mine. I'm twenty-six."

"Are you?" said the old man mildly, and opened the book.

Xanthe should have walked away. Every instinct honed over twenty years screamed it—walk away, pull him away, vanish into the fog and never come down Ropewalk again. But she was looking at the open pages, and the pages were moving.

Ink crawled across the vellum like insects waking, letters assembling themselves into lines that had not been written so much as *waiting*. And down the left margin, in a hand older than the city's current bridges, ran a list of names. Twelve of them struck through with a single clean line each, like birds crossed off a hunter's tally.

The thirteenth name glowed.

**OREN VALE.**

Lamplighter. Ropewalk round. Born under a waning moon in the year the river froze.

Xanthe read it twice before the words finished arriving. Then the page turned itself—or seemed to—and beneath the name, in that same antique hand, the ink was writing now, wet and gleaming, describing:

*A fog-bound market. Chestnuts. A woman who counts the exits.*

Tonight. It was describing tonight.

"What is this?" Oren's voice had gone thin. He touched the page and the glow brightened under his fingertip, eager, and he snatched his hand back as if bitten. "What is—how does it know my name?"

"It doesn't know it," the old man said, closing the book with terrible gentleness. "It *kept* it. There's a difference, lad. This is the Hollow Choir. Written before your grandfather's grandfather drew breath. Thirteen souls promised to the court beneath the mountain, and the book remembers each one until the debt comes due." He wrapped the volume in its blue cloth, and the glow died like a snuffed wick. "Twelve are already collected. Yours is the last. The moon turns her thirteenth turn soon, and then—"

"Then what?" Xanthe heard herself ask. Her voice sounded far away, underwater.

The old man looked at her then, really looked, and something flickered across his ruined face—pity, or fear, or both. His gaze dropped to her hands, folded tight against her skirts, and lingered there a moment too long.

"Then the thirteenth soul goes home to the silver court," he said. "And the thief named in these pages either succeeds or hangs beside the rest of us."

He pushed the book toward her across the cloth.

"It's not mine," she said quickly. "I'm not—I don't—"

"No," the old man agreed. "You're not anything. That's rather the point of you." He tapped the cover once, and beneath the wrapping, faintly, the glow pulsed like a heartbeat. "Read the last page when you're brave enough, missy. Your name's in there too."

The fog chose that moment to shift, and down the lane, where the licensed stalls ended, a lantern flared white—not the amber of commerce but the cold, hard white of official light. Voices. Boots. The distinctive jingle of iron rings on a witch-finder's belt.

The old man swept his cloth and books into a sack with the speed of long practice. "Market's closing," he said. "Go home. Don't run—running is a confession. Walk." He pressed the wrapped book into Oren's stunned hands. "Paid for a century past. Delivery owed. No refunds."

Then he was folding himself into the dark beneath the stairs, and he was gone, and the witch-finders' white lantern swung into the square like a small cold moon.

Oren clutched the book against his chest. It was warm; Xanthe could see the heat of it through the cloth, could feel it from a foot away, humming against her own buried power like a struck bell against a struck bell.

"Xanthe," he whispered. "What's happening? What did he mean, your name—"

"Walk," she said. "Don't run. Take my arm. Smile."

He smiled. It was a terrible, frightened smile, and she loved it, and she hated herself for loving anything, because love was a door and doors let things in.

They walked. Past the charm-stalls, past the fiddler whose tune had gone discordant, past the paper-masked children being herded home by anxious mothers. The white lantern swept its light across the crowd, and Xanthe kept her eyes down and her hands still and counted exits—one, the culvert, two, the warehouse stair, three—

The lantern paused on them. Held.

A witch-finder's voice, bored and lethal: "Evening, citizens. Lovely fog tonight. Fog's good for finding things."

Oren's arm trembled against hers. Inside his coat, the book pulsed, brighter, brighter, a heartbeat quickening, and Xanthe understood with sickening clarity that whatever the Hollow Choir was, it was not finished with them—and that the life she had built out of silence and smallness had just cracked open like ice on the river in spring.

She squeezed Oren's arm and turned them, gently, toward the drainage culvert behind the fishmonger.

"There's something I have to show you," she said. "And after that, we run."
