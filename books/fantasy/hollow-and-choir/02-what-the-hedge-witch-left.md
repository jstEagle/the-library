## 2. What the Hedge-Witch Left

Xanthe did not sleep.

She sat in her narrow room above the chandler's shop on Tallow Row with the shutters closed and the candle unlit—she could not bear flame tonight—and watched the dark shape of the Hollow Choir lie on her table like a wound that had learned to read. The book was warm. It had been warm since the night market, since she had seen Oren's name shining in it like a lamp seen through fog: *Oren Vale, lamplighter, thirteenth of the sold.* A century old, that line. Older than his grandfather's grandfather. And beneath it, in the same patient ink, a second name she had not yet let herself look at twice.

She looked at it now.

*Xanthe Thornbury, thief.*

The letters did not glow for her. They simply waited, the way debts wait.

Outside, Vellamere went about its business of forgetting. Carts rattled over cobblestones. The fishwives cried their morning prices. Somewhere a child was learning to whistle. Twenty years she had built herself into this city's ordinary hours—quiet as dust, useful as salt, invisible as breath—and in one evening at a market stall, all of it had cracked like river ice under a careless foot.

She dressed in the grey wool she wore for grief and left before the sun cleared the rooftops, the book wrapped in oilcloth against her chest, bound there with the same twine she used to tie bundles of dried lavender. If anyone asked, she would say she was going to visit her mother's grave. No one ever asked. That was the whole architecture of her life: no one ever asked.

* * *

The road north out of Vellamere climbed through terraces of vineyard gone feral, then through birch scrub silvered with early frost, and by midmorning she could see it—the black tooth of the chimney still standing above the gorse, the only part of the cottage that had refused to burn completely. She had not come here in twenty years. She had told herself it was because the road was bad, because the witch-finders kept records of who traveled to known sites, because grief was a luxury. All true. None of them the truth.

The truth was simpler and worse: she had been afraid that nothing would be left, and more afraid that something would.

The cottage stood in its ring of char like a held breath. Fireweed had taken the dooryard—tall stalks, purple flowers, blooming out of ash the way forgiveness sometimes does, without being asked. The doorframe leaned where she remembered it standing straight. Inside, the roof had folded in on itself years ago; sky came through in ragged panels, and moss upholstered what remained of the settle by the hearth. Her mother's copper kettle lay in the ruins of the fireplace, green with age, its handle pointing at her like an accusation or a welcome. She could not decide which.

"You're late," said a voice from the ashes.

Xanthe's heart stopped, started again, and stopped better trained than it used to be. She turned slowly.

Maren stood in the doorway—or rather, the shape of Maren, the way heat stands above a road in summer, visible only when you refuse to look directly at her. Grey at the edges, bright at the center, wearing the same patched apron she had died in twenty years ago, the one with the burn hole shaped like a crescent moon. Her face was the face Xanthe had spent two decades carefully not remembering, and now that she saw it, every other face she knew seemed slightly out of focus.

"You're late," Maren said again, gentler now. "Twenty years late."

"You were dead," Xanthe said. "I checked. I was at the trial. I watched them—" Her voice snagged on something sharp. "I watched them hang you, and I ran, and I never came back, and I never lit a candle for you because candles are how they find us, and I told myself that was prudence but it was cowardice, and—"

"Sit down, girl."

"There's nowhere to sit."

"Then stand properly." Maren drifted forward, and where she passed, the fireweed bowed though there was no wind. "You've been hunching around this guilt so long it's grown into your spine. Straighten up. We have work to do, and precious little time to do it in."

Xanthe unwrapped the book and set it on the flat stone that had once been the kitchen table. The oilcloth fell away and the Hollow Choir lay open to the page it had opened itself to at the market, the page with two names on it, and Maren looked down at it the way a physician looks at a wound she has seen too many times.

"So you found it," she said. "Or it found you. With that book, it's always both."

"It's a prophecy," Xanthe said. "It's a hundred years old. It has Oren's name in it. His *name*, Maren. Written before he was born. It says tonight—the night I met him, the exact hour, the lamplight, everything—it says all of it, like someone wrote my life down before I lived it and left it lying in a market stall for me to trip over."

"And what else does it say?"

Xanthe made herself say it. "*Xanthe Thornbury, thief.*"

Maren was quiet a moment. Ghosts are quiet differently than the living—not silence but a listening kind of quiet, as if they hear the underneath of things. Then she laughed, short and dry, with no joy in it at all.

"A century ago, some hedge-witch with second sight sat in this very room—before it burned, before either of us was born—and saw a woman stealing something back from the court beneath the mountain. And she wrote down the name she saw. And the name was yours." Maren's grey hands hovered over the page without touching it. "Prophecy is a poor word for it. It isn't prediction. It's memory running backward. The mountain remembers what it loses, and it leaks, and sometimes the leak reaches a woman who can write."

"The court beneath the mountain." Xanthe had heard the phrase her whole life the way children hear frightening words—in lullabies, in warnings, in the muttered prayers of women who hid their craft. *Keep your spark small, keep it cold, or the court beneath the mountain will come for it.* She had thought it a story to make witches careful. "It's real."

"As real as the gallows," Maren said. "Realer. The gallows only kills you. The court keeps you."

"Keeps you how?"

Maren turned her head, and the light through the broken roof moved across her as if she were made of smoke and water. "You know the old rhyme. *Thirteen souls for the singing stone.* Every hundred years, the court beneath the mountain calls in its debt, and the debt is paid in souls—taken whole, taken singing, hung in the deep halls like bells in a belfry, and rung. The sovereign down there wears a crown of what it collects. It has twelve already, Xanthe. Twelve souls, hung and humming, for ninety-nine years. And on the moon's thirteenth turn, it comes to collect the last."

"Which is—"

"Look at the book."

Xanthe looked. Below Oren's name, in smaller script, in a hand tighter than the rest, the hedge-witch had written what she had seen: *the thirteenth soul, sold before his birth, a lamplighter who lights lamps he cannot remember lighting, hollow at the center, dreaming of silver.* And below that, the price and the payment, the whole terrible arithmetic of it: *the thief must give what cannot be weighed to buy back what cannot be bought.*

"Sold before his birth," Xanthe whispered. "By whom?"

"That I don't know. Debts like that get signed in the dark, by people who don't understand what they're signing, or by people who understand perfectly." Maren's voice roughened. "What I know is what I've heard these twenty years, drifting between here and the places the dead go. There's a boy in Vellamere who has felt a draft coming out of his own chest since he was small. Who dreams of a silver hall and wakes with songs in his mouth he never learned. Who lights the city's lamps and feels each flame go out of him a little. You've seen it. Don't tell me you haven't."

Xanthe had. She had thought it was melancholy. Oren was gentle and quiet and sometimes, holding his hand, she had felt something absent in him the way you feel a missing tooth with your tongue—a hollowness, a coolness, a place where something should have been. She had found it tender. She had wanted to fill it. She had not known she was resting her hand on an empty socket where a soul used to live.

"I love him," she said, and was surprised at how easily it came out here, in the ruin, in front of the dead. Twenty years of hiding magic, and hiding this too, saying it to no one, and now it came out plain as water. "Three months. I've known him three months and I love him, and the book says he has until the moon finishes turning, and the moon is nearly done."

"Nine days," Maren said. "Eight, if the cloud breaks."

"Then we run. We take him east, over the border, past the—"

"Run where?" For the first time, Maren's ghost-form sharpened, gathered, the way a storm gathers. "The court doesn't chase, girl. It doesn't need to. What's sold belongs to it the way a root belongs to the tree. He can stand on the far side of the sea and on the thirteenth turn he'll walk into the nearest dark place and keep walking until he's home. There is no away. There's only *back*—taking him out of its halls before the turn completes. Stealing him back."

"Then I'll steal him back."

"With what army? With what craft? You've spent twenty years shrinking your gift down to a candle-flame behind a shutter. You charm bread so it doesn't stale. You whisper knots loose. You have hidden so well you've half-hidden yourself away." Maren drifted closer, and her nearness was cold and familiar at once, like a winter window. "And even if you had the power—and you do, you have more than you've ever dared use—you'd still be one woman walking into a court that has eaten twelve souls and grown fat on the singing. You'd need hands besides your own. Smugglers. Charm-sellers. People who owe the margins of this city their lives. You'd need a family, Xanthe. The kind you build yourself, because the blood ones are gone."

"I don't know anyone like that."

"You do," Maren said. "You met them last night, at the market, while the witch-finders were burning the stalls. The smuggler with the knife smile who pulled you out of the crowd. The apprentice boy with singed fingers who didn't ask your name before he lied to the finders for you. You think that was chance? The book opened itself to you. Things that open themselves want following."

Xanthe pressed her palms against the cold stone table and bowed her head, and for a long moment she let herself feel all of it at once, which she had not permitted in twenty years: the smoke of this room, the rope, the crowd shouting *witch* while she stood at the back with her hood up and her hands in her pockets and did nothing, said nothing, saved no one. Guilt is a room you furnish yourself, Maren had once told her, and Xanthe had furnished hers lavishly, and lived in it alone.

"Maren," she said. "Why didn't you tell me? Before. About any of this. About the court, the souls, the—"

"Because telling you would have made it yours sooner," Maren said softly. "Because prophecy is a hook, and knowing your own name is on it is how it sets. Because I hoped—I was a fool, and I hoped—that if you never knew, the thread might pass you by. It didn't. Threads like this don't pass. They wait." She paused, and something in her flickered, dimmed, steadied. "And because I was afraid. Dead twenty years and I'm still afraid, girl. Fear doesn't burn off. It just changes what it's afraid of."

"What are you afraid of now?"

"Not of the court. Not anymore. Of you." Maren reached out, and her hand passed through Xanthe's shoulder like light through glass, and yet Xanthe felt it anyway, felt it the way you feel a memory of warmth. "Because the book says the thief pays with what cannot be weighed. Do you understand what that means? Not gold. Not blood. *Memory*. The court trades in what a person is made of. To buy a soul back, it will take the memories that weigh the most—the ones you love hardest. And the heaviest thing you carry, heavier than Oren, heavier than your mother, is me. Twenty years of me. My face. My voice. Every lesson I ever taught you."

Xanthe's breath caught. "If I pay with you—"

"You won't lose me," Maren said. "You'll lose having me. There's a difference, and it's the cruelest difference there is. You'll walk out of that mountain with Oren alive beside you and no idea why your hands know how to knot a warding-charm, no idea whose voice taught you the song you'll need to sing to do it. You'll carry me forward without carrying me. Like a tune you hum without knowing where you learned it." She smiled then, thin and fierce, the smile Xanthe had loved and feared in equal measure across a childhood of stolen lessons. "That's inheritance, girl. Nobody tells you that. Inheritance isn't what you keep. It's what keeps working in you after the keeping is gone."

The wind moved through the broken roof and stirred the fireweed, and Maren's edges frayed with it, thinned, went translucent at the hem.

"I have to teach you fast," she said. "Before the turn. Before you spend me. So listen. There's a smugglers' road under the roots of the mountains—old, older than the city, older than the ban. Your smuggler friend knows its mouth, whether she knows she knows it or not. And there are three riddles the road will ask you, and I know the answers, and I'm going to pour them into you now, the way I poured everything else, while you weren't looking." She bent close, close enough that Xanthe felt winter on her cheekbone. "Say the first verse of the lullaby. The one I sang you when the fever took you at seven. Say it now."

Xanthe opened her mouth, and the song came out of her whole, every note, every word, a thing she had not sung in twenty years and had never once forgotten—

—and as she sang, Maren began, deliberately, gently, to fade. Not leaving. Pouring. Becoming the kind of inheritance that works after the keeping is gone.

And in the doorway of the ruined cottage, where the fireweed bowed without wind, Xanthe saw the second figure waiting—grey, slight, singed at the fingertips, watching her with eyes she almost recognized.

Someone had followed her here.

Someone young, and frightened, and running from the smoke of the burning market, with witch-finders' hounds already crying on the ridge behind him.

* * *

The knock, when it came, was barely a knock at all—just the soft collapse of a boy too exhausted to stay upright, folding against her doorframe like a puppet with cut strings, and behind him, far down the valley, three fires bloomed in the dark where the night market had been, and the hounds were singing.
