## 3. Charms for the Gallows-Bound

The boy weighed nothing. That was Xanthe's first thought, absurd and practical at once, as she hauled him over the threshold and into the dark of the ruined cottage—*he weighs nothing, he's all ribs and fear, someone has been starving him slowly.* Her second thought was less kind: *you have just touched a stranger with your bare hands in the open doorway of the only place left in the world that remembers what you are.*

The hounds were still singing on the ridge, but faintly now, their voices braided with men's shouts, turning away toward the valley floor where three fires ate the night market stall by stall. She could smell it even here—tallow and char and something sweeter underneath, the burnt-honey stink of charms catching flame. Twenty years of careful nothing, of being a woman who sold soap and kept her head down and never, ever looked twice at anything that glowed—and in one night she had read a forbidden book, learned her own name written in prophecy, and now stood holding a runaway apprentice while witch-finders' dogs bayed her name across the hills.

Not her name. Not yet. But names were how it started. Names were always how it started.

She dragged the boy to the cold hearth and got him upright against the stones. Up close he was younger than she'd guessed—fourteen, maybe fifteen, with soot smeared across his cheek like a bad attempt at a beard and a burn blistering the back of one hand. His eyes opened halfway. They were quick eyes, clever even half-shut, and they went around the cottage once—the scorched beams, the fireweed pushing up through the floorboards, the iron pot, the bundle of dried herbs that any finder worth his salt would recognize as more than seasoning.

"Nice place," he croaked. "Very discreet."

"You're bleeding on my floor."

"It's mostly theirs." He tried a grin; it cost him something. "The market. I got two of them out before—" His voice snagged. He swallowed it down. "I'm Pip."

"I didn't ask."

"You were going to." He was already fading, his head lolling against the hearthstone. "Everyone asks eventually. It's the hand, isn't it. People always want to know about the hand."

It was a bad burn, the kind that would fester if left to pride. Xanthe knelt, and heard herself sigh the sigh of a woman stepping off a cliff she'd been standing on the edge of for twenty years, and did the thing she had sworn never to do again in front of witnesses.

She hummed.

Just three notes, low, barely breath—Maren's cooling-charm, the one that lived in the melody the way a seed lives in an apple. The fireweed by the door turned its heads toward her. The heat came out of the boy's hand like water drawn from a well, and the blister sank from angry red to a pale pink shine, and Pip's eyes flew open all the way.

"You're a hedge-witch," he whispered, with the reverence other boys reserved for saints or pirates.

"I'm a soap-seller," Xanthe said. "And you're going to be quiet, because there are hounds on that ridge, and hounds bred by the Office of Finding don't track scent. They track *use*. Every charm I just worked is a bell ringing in the dark, and every bell rings for about an hour. So we have an hour to decide whether you're a burden I'm carrying deeper into trouble, or—" she stopped, because the sentence had nowhere honest to go.

"Or family," Pip said, insufferably gentle for a boy leaking blood onto her floor. "That's usually the third option."

Outside, far off, a hound began to sing again. Closer than before.

* * *

They went out through the fireweed field under a sky scoured clean of stars by smoke, Xanthe carrying a satchel of things she had sworn she'd buried years ago—Maren's mortar, a stoppered vial of moonwater, a twist of grey thread that had once been, briefly, a friend. Pip walked beside her with the strange, light-footed steadiness of the very young when they are running toward something instead of away. He talked. He talked the way cracks let in cold.

"The market's gone. All three rows. They came at moonrise with writs and lantern-glass and those awful white masks, and they started with Old Sefa's stall because everyone knows Sefa wards the barges on the canal, and—" his voice wobbled and hardened, "—and I got Marisol's daughter out over the wall, and then the hounds had my scent because I'd been *working*, I'd been selling little luck-knots like an idiot, like a child, and I ran, and I remembered Maren Almeida's cottage from the stories, because every apprentice knows the story, they burned her twenty years ago and her student vanished like smoke, and I thought—if anywhere—"

"Maren wasn't burned," Xanthe said quietly. "Maren was hanged. Burning is for witches who fight."

Pip was silent for three steps. Then: "You knew her."

"Everyone knew *of* her."

"You said her name like you were swallowing glass."

Ahead of them, down in the black fold of the valley, a lantern moved where no lantern should be—not the sweep of a search party but a single steady point, bobbing, patient, as if waiting to be found. Xanthe stopped. Beside her, Pip went rigid.

"That's not a finder," he breathed. "Finders travel loud. That's—"

"A smuggler," said a voice from the darkness ahead, low and dry as paper, "who is currently losing money by the minute waiting for two idiots to stop discussing me in the open."

The lantern rose. Its bearer resolved out of the night in pieces: a tall woman in a coat the colour of wet slate, silver rings stacked four deep on every finger, a scar splitting one eyebrow, and eyes the flat amber of a hawk deciding. She wore no mask. In Vellamere, only the witch-finders wore masks, and only the guilty went bare-faced after dark.

"Sable," she said, as if the word were a receipt. "You'll be the hedge-witch. Word travels. A woman asked questions at the market tonight about the Hollow Choir, bought nothing, touched everything, and left a residue on the prophecy-seller's stock that my charms could taste from across the canal. Then the finders came. Coincidence is a currency I don't trade in." Her gaze flicked to Pip, dismissed him, returned. "So. Either you're the most careless witch in Vellamere, or you're looking for something under the mountain. Which is it?"

Xanthe's mouth was dry. "Who says I'm looking for anything?"

"Your hands," Sable said. "Witches who've stopped practicing hold them like closed purses. Yours keep making small motions. Warding. Testing the air. You've been starving your craft for years and it's eating you alive, and people in that condition either die quietly or do something spectacularly stupid." She smiled without warmth. "I deal in outlawed charms, which makes me spectacularly stupid professionally. I can get you to the mountain road. I cannot get you back. Decide walking."

Another hound-voice rose behind them, nearer, joined by a second. And beneath the baying, faintly, the sound Xanthe had dreaded all her life: men's boots, many, keeping easy time. Not searching anymore. *Following.*

"They've got your thread," Sable said, unbothered, already turning. "The grey one in your bag. Sentimental objects sing loudest. We have perhaps ten minutes before this valley becomes a net. So—decide."

Xanthe thought of Oren lighting his lamps along the canal, unaware, hollow, dreaming of a silver court. Thirteen days until the moon's thirteenth turn. She thought of Maren's ghost in the burned cottage saying *the prophecy names you, thief,* and of twenty years spent folding herself small enough to fit inside an ordinary life, and how the life had cracked anyway, the way eggs crack, from the inside.

"Thief," she said aloud, tasting the word. "All right. I'm looking for something under the mountain. Someone sold it there, and I intend to steal it back."

Something flickered in Sable's hawk-eyes that might, in a better light, have been respect. "Then walk fast and talk never. And give me the grey thread—it goes in the lead-lined box or it sings us all onto the gallows."

* * *

They went down through the valley by ways Xanthe hadn't known existed, though she'd lived her whole life within sight of these hills—Sable moving with the certainty of someone reading a book written in hedgerows and drainage ditches, through a gap in a drystone wall, along a culvert, down a stair cut into the earth so old its steps had melted into waves. Twice they froze as hound-song swept overhead, and twice the song passed them by, baffled, because Sable had rubbed something bitter and metallic on their collars that made their scent go *wrong*, made them smell like stone, like rain, like nothing.

Pip pressed close to Xanthe in the second freezing. In the dark his whisper was barely air: "Is it true? What they say about the court? That the souls down there still sing?"

"I don't know," Xanthe said. And then, because lying to him felt suddenly heavier than truth: "I think one of them might be someone I love."

Pip was quiet a moment. "Marisol's daughter is seven," he said finally. "When they came for our row, she didn't cry. She held my hand and counted the finders' boots going past the wall. Forty-one boots. She counted them like it was a game because I told her it was." His jaw set, too old for his face. "If there's a way to steal people back from under a mountain, I want to learn it. Whatever it costs."

Above them, on the ridge, a white mask caught the moonlight—a finder standing sentinel, scanning the valley, close enough that Xanthe could see the lantern-glass glinting at his belt, the instruments for measuring use, for weighing guilt. He turned his head slowly across the dark like a lighthouse of judgment.

Sable's hand closed on Xanthe's shoulder and drew them both down into the culvert's shadow. Her whisper was silk over steel:

"There's a door under the roots of the mountain that hasn't opened in a hundred years. My mother swore it opens for riddles. Your dead mentor left three of them in a ledger I've been trying to read for a decade." The amber eyes fixed on Xanthe, weighing, wagering. "So here's my price, hedge-witch. Not coin. When we get inside—you tell me what the Choir sounds like. No one who's heard it has ever come back to say."

Below them, the earth shuddered—once, softly, like a throat clearing—and somewhere far beneath their feet, faint as memory, something began to sing.
