## 4. Thirteen Turns

*Xanthe*

The moon had begun to thin.

Xanthe noticed it the way she noticed all dangerous things—sideways, with her body stilling before her mind caught up. She was standing at Oren's window in his narrow room above the chandler's shop, a cup of chicory cooling forgotten in her hands, and beyond the rooftops of Vellamere the moon hung like a coin rubbed almost smooth. Twelve nights of its turning had passed. Thirteen remained.

*Thirteen turns,* Maren's ghost had said, the words still lodged in Xanthe's chest like a fishhook. *On the thirteenth, the debt comes due. The court beneath the mountain will have all of him.*

Behind her, Oren was trimming a wick with the small silver scissors he carried everywhere, the way other men carried knives or prayer-beads. He did it absently, the way he did everything—carefully, tenderly, as though the world were made of things that deserved gentleness. She had thought, for one delirious evening at the night market, that this was simply his nature. Now she knew better. Now she knew that gentleness could be a wound's way of healing itself, and that the wound in Oren was a century old.

"You're staring," he said, without looking up.

"You're interesting to stare at."

He smiled, but the smile didn't reach whatever lived behind his eyes, that place she was beginning to understand was not empty but *guarded*—a door with something scratching on the other side of it. He set the scissors down. "You've been different since the market," he said. "Since you saw the book. You look at me like I'm a letter you're afraid to open."

Xanthe set down the chicory. She had rehearsed a dozen lies on the walk over—she was good at lies, had been lying with her whole body for twenty years—but standing in the amber light of his lamps, watching him wait for her with that terrible patience, she found she had no appetite for them.

"Oren." Her voice came out rougher than she intended. "What do you dream about?"

He went very still. It was the stillness of a man who has been asked the one question he has spent his life not answering.

"Why?"

"Because I think it matters. Because I think—" She stopped, started again. "I think you already know something is wrong with you, and you've known it so long you've stopped calling it wrong. You just call it *quiet*."

The lamp between them guttered, though there was no draft. Oren looked at the flame, then at her, and something in his face opened—not all the way, but a crack, like ice on a river in early spring.

"Come with me," he said. "Tomorrow night. I'll show you."

* * *

*Oren*

Oren had never told anyone about the dream. It was not the sort of thing one told. In Vellamere, a man confessed to drunkenness, to debt, to fathering a child out of wedlock, and was met with rough sympathy. But a man who confessed to dreaming, every night of his life, of a court of silver where his own soul sat on a shelf like a stolen bell—that man was met with silence, and then with witch-finders, and then with rope.

So he had carried it alone for thirty-one years, the way he carried everything: quietly, carefully, with the small silver scissors of his gentleness always at hand to trim the wick before it flared.

But Xanthe had seen the book. He had watched her face in the market lantern-light when she'd found his name glowing in that forbidden page—*the thirteenth soul*—and he had watched wonder curdle into dread, and he had understood, with a relief so profound it frightened him, that he would never have to pretend again.

He took her down through the city at dusk, past the tanneries and the tallow-works, out along the river road to the lamplighters' field where the spare lamps were stored for winter. Hundreds of them, racked in long rows under the open sky, glass panes catching the last of the light. The witch-finders did not come here. There was nothing here to burn but oil.

"Every night since I was a boy," Oren said, "I dream the same dream. I'm standing in a hall made of silver. Not silver-colored—*made* of it, walls and floor and ceiling, and it sings. Everything sings there. The walls hum when you walk on them. And in the middle of the hall there's a shelf, a long one, and on the shelf there are flames. Little flames, like candle-flames, but they're *singing*, Xanthe. Each one a different song. And I walk down the row of them, and I count them, and I always stop at twelve, because I know—" His voice caught. He pressed on. "I know the thirteenth is mine. I know the shelf is waiting for me. And in the dream I'm not afraid. That's the worst part. In the dream I *want* to lie down on that shelf and sing."

Xanthe had gone pale in the blue dusk. Her hands, he noticed, had curled into fists at her sides, and her fists were trembling.

"Tell me the songs," she said. "The flames. What do they sing?"

"One of them sings a lullaby. I don't know it. I've never heard it awake. But I know every note of it, the way you know your own heartbeat." He looked at her. "Why are you crying?"

"Because I know it too," Xanthe whispered. "Oren, I know that lullaby. Someone taught it to me when I was a girl. Someone who was hanged for it."

* * *

*Xanthe*

They sat that night in the lamplighters' field among the racked lamps, and Xanthe told him everything she could bear to tell: Maren, the cottage, the hanging, twenty years of folding her magic small enough to hide in the creases of an ordinary life. She told him about the ghost in the burned foundation, about the Hollow Choir, about the thirteenth turn. She did not tell him everything—her hands still shook too badly for everything—but she told him more than she had told any living soul, and the strange thing, the thing she would remember afterward, was how *light* it made her. As if the secrets had been ballast and she was finally rising.

Oren listened the way he trimmed wicks: completely, without flinching, as though she were a flame worth protecting.

"So it's already decided," he said at last. "A hundred years ago, somebody wrote down that I'd be sold. And now the court is coming to collect." He laughed, a small hollow sound. "I always wondered why I felt like this. Like a house where the furniture's been repossessed. I thought everyone felt that way and was polite enough not to mention it."

"It isn't decided."

"Xanthe—"

"It isn't." She took his hands. They were lamp-oil and callus and warmth, and she held them the way you hold something you have decided, privately and irrevocably, to keep. "Listen to me. Prophecies are not orders. They're *gossip*. They're what someone saw from a long way off, and seeing a thing from a long way off is not the same as it being true. Maren taught me that. Maren taught me—" She stopped. The lullaby rose in her throat, unbidden, and she swallowed it down. "Maren taught me that the future is a door, not a wall. Doors can be locked. Doors can be barred. Doors can be *burned*."

Oren searched her face. "You're talking about fighting a court that's been collecting souls for a century. With what army?"

"With a smuggler who owes me a favor and a runaway who owes me nothing and wants to help anyway. With a ghost. With—" She laughed despite herself, wet-eyed. "With a lamplighter, apparently. The thirteenth soul himself. Oren, the prophecy says I steal you back. It doesn't say I succeed. But it doesn't say I fail, either, and that gap in the middle? That's where we live. That's where we *choose*."

He was quiet a long time. Above them the thinned moon moved through a lace of cloud, and somewhere in the dark a nightjar called its one cracked note.

"I've spent my whole life being gentle," he said finally, "because gentleness was the only thing that felt like mine. Everything else felt borrowed. Or *owed*." His grip tightened on her hands. "But this—wanting to fight for my own soul, wanting to *keep* you—that feels mine too. That feels more mine than anything."

"Then it's decided," Xanthe said. "We don't let the prophecy happen to us. We happen to *it*."

* * *

*Oren*

He woke before dawn with the dream still ringing in his skull, and for the first time in thirty-one years, the silver hall was different.

The flames on the shelf had stopped singing their separate songs. They were singing together now—all twelve, in unison, a rising chord—and the hall's silver walls had begun, very faintly, to *crack*.

And the thirteenth place on the shelf was not empty.

It was occupied by a small flame that did not sing at all. It simply burned, steady and stubborn, and around its base, like a signature, like a brand, was the unmistakable shape of a thorn.

Oren lay in the dark of his narrow room, heart hammering, and understood that something had changed—not out there in the silver court, but in here, in the hollow of himself. The door with the scratching behind it had swung open, and what stood on the other side was not the court's claim on him.

It was his own.

He got up, dressed, and went down into the blue pre-dawn streets to find Xanthe, because there was no one else in all the world he could tell. And as he ran, he did not notice the figure peeling away from the shadow of the chandler's sign—a watcher with a witch-finder's badge hidden under a lamplighter's coat, who had heard enough through the chandler's thin floorboards, and who was already hurrying toward the Bastion with a name on his lips.

Not Oren's name.

*Xanthe's.*
