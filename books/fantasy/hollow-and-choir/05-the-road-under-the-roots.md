## 5. The Road Under the Roots

Sable had run this road eleven times in her life, and every one of those times she had been alone.

That was the first thing wrong with tonight. The second was the company: a witch with a bounty on her head worth more than Sable's boat, a lamplighter who flinched at his own shadow and apologized to it afterward, and a boy of maybe fifteen who kept asking questions the way children ask—endlessly, sincerely, as if the world owed him answers. The third thing wrong was the ghost. Maren's voice came out of a copper locket Xanthe wore on a cord around her neck, thin as smoke scraped over tin, and it spoke in riddles because apparently even dead hedge-witches couldn't give a straight answer to save their lives.

Which was, Sable supposed grimly, exactly what they were trying to do.

*"Where the mountain drinks the sky,"* the locket murmured as they stood at the mouth of the old road, a seam of blackness behind a curtain of ivy gone feral over two hundred years, *"the door is where the water lies."*

"Water lies," Pip repeated. "What's that mean? Water doesn't lie. Water's honest. Water's the most honest thing there is."

"It means," Sable said, crouching at the threshold and pressing her palm flat against the stone, "that we don't go through the front. Fronts are for people who want to be found." She could feel it under her hand—the faint cold breath of the passage, the way the mountain exhaled through its teeth. Every smuggler between Vellamere and the coast knew the Hollow Road existed. Knowing where its mouths opened was another matter; knowing which mouths still opened at all was worth more than most houses. "The road forks ahead. One branch goes up toward the old quarry, dry as a sermon. The other drops down along an aquifer. We take the wet one."

"Why?" Oren asked. He had a lantern lit, because of course he did, because lamplighters could no more walk in the dark unlit than fish could walk on land, and Sable had already given up telling him that light made them visible to anything with eyes.

"Because the witch-finders are up top with dogs and divining rods," she said, shouldering her pack, "and divining rods love dry stone. Water scrambles them. Also," she added, before anyone could mistake her for a generous person, "because I said so, and until we're out from under this mountain, my word is the only law down here."

Xanthe gave her a look—the kind of look that suggested she found Sable's manner exhausting but had decided, provisionally, to survive anyway—and touched two fingers to the locket. "Maren?"

The locket hummed. *"The wet branch remembers my feet. Tell the smuggler she is right, and tell her not to let it go to her head."*

"I hate her," Sable announced to no one, and went in.

* * *

The Hollow Road did not feel dug so much as swallowed. The walls were slick with a moss that glowed a sickly green when their light passed over it, dimming again behind them like something embarrassed to be seen. Roots had come down through the ceiling in places—thick pale roots, root-white as drowned fingers, hanging to knee height so that walking became a constant ducking, brushing, flinching. Pip named them all. That one was Gerald. That one was definitely called Bartholomew. Sable did not have the energy to stop him.

For the first hour she kept her distance from the others out of habit. Eleven runs alone will do that to a person; solitude gets into the bones like damp, and you stop noticing it's there until someone else's warmth makes the ache obvious. She walked point with her knife drawn low, listening to the drip-count of the aquifer, and told herself the tightness in her chest was just the mountain pressing close.

It wasn't the mountain.

It was the sound of Oren counting his steps under his breath. It was Xanthe humming—very quietly, badly, like someone who'd been taught a song by a woman now dead and hadn't sung it since—to keep the dark from feeling like a mouth. It was Pip, twenty paces back, whispering *please don't be a trap, please don't be a trap* to a charm he'd bought off Sable herself at the night market, a luck-knot she'd sold him at triple price because he'd looked desperate and she'd looked away from herself long enough to charge him for it.

She cared whether they lived. She noticed it the way you notice a splinter—not at first, only when it catches.

"Smuggler." Xanthe's voice, low. "Maren wants a word."

Sable stopped. Ahead, the passage split around a fallen slab, and water ran black and quick over both paths, too shallow to see depth. The locket's glow strengthened, and Maren's voice came with it, thinner here, as if the mountain were leaning in to listen.

*"Two roads, both wet, both wrong unless you know the difference. Ask the smuggler: what does the river keep?"*

Sable stared at the water. Cold crawled up her arms. This was the part of Maren's legacy she resented most—not the execution, not the burning, but the habit of teaching by ordeal, as if grief were a curriculum and everyone left alive a student who hadn't asked to enroll.

"What does the river keep," she repeated flatly. "Rivers don't keep anything. Rivers are the least sentimental—"

But even as she said it, she was looking. Really looking, the way she hadn't let herself look at anything in years except profit margins and escape routes. The water ran faster over the right-hand path. Faster meant shallower meant the bed rose. And where the bed rose—

"Gravel," she said slowly. "The river keeps gravel. It drops what it can't carry." She knelt, dipped two fingers, tasted. Mineral, iron-adjacent, and beneath it something sweeter, faintly rotten-sweet. "Left path's fed from deeper down. There's ore water in it. Old workings flooded somewhere upstream. Right path's surface runoff—it'll rise in rain, but rain's not falling." She stood, wiped her hand on her coat. "Right. We go right, and if anyone asks how I knew, tell them a dead woman insulted me until I figured it out."

*"Adequate,"* said the locket, and Sable could have sworn the dead woman sounded pleased, which was somehow worse than smug.

They went right. The passage narrowed, then opened without warning into a gallery where the roots had broken through in earnest—a whole chandelier of them, pale and dripping, and among the roots, wedged in the stone like coins pushed into a crack for luck, were small objects. A clay whistle. A child's shoe, rotted soft. A ring of braided hair gone to dust-brown. Offerings, or leavings. People had come through here fleeing something, once, and had paid the road whatever they could bear to lose.

Pip reached for the whistle. Sable caught his wrist without thinking.

"Road keeps what it's given," she said. "You don't take from a road. You especially don't take from a road while you're asking it to hide you." She let him go. Her heart was going harder than the catch warranted. "Leave it. Come on."

He left it. But as they moved on, she heard him say, very quietly, to Xanthe: "She didn't even hesitate. She just—knew to grab me. Like she'd done it before."

And Xanthe said, equally quiet: "Give her time. She'll deny it eventually."

Sable walked faster, and pretended the heat in her ears was the mountain air.

* * *

They heard the witch-finders at moonrise—or what would have been moonrise, if a mountain had a sky. Sound travels strange underground; it arrived as a vibration in the soles of the feet first, then a ringing in the fillings of the teeth, and only last as actual noise: boots, many, and the thin high whine of divining rods singing against dry stone somewhere far above.

"They're in the quarry branch," Sable breathed, killing Oren's lantern with a snap of the shutter before he could protest. Darkness dropped on them like a lid. In the green murk of the moss-light she saw Oren's face go grey, saw Xanthe's hand find his sleeve. "They've got the entrance. They're sweeping it rod by rod. Which means someone talked, and which means—" she did the arithmetic she hated doing, the arithmetic of hours and distances and the speed of men who get paid by the capture, "—they'll reach the fork within the hour. Then they'll taste the water and follow us."

"Can we outrun them?" Pip whispered.

"Underground? No. Nobody outruns anybody underground. You out-think them or you out-last them, and I'm not sure we can do either." She was already moving, testing walls, hunting for the side-passage she half-remembered from run number seven—a crawl-space behind a curtain of roots, wide enough for the boy, a squeeze for the rest. "There's a bolt-hole. It lets out above the tree line on the north face. It's ugly and it's slow and if any of you are claustrophobic, now is the time to develop courage."

The locket flared. *"No,"* Maren said, sharp as a struck match. *"Not the bolt-hole. The bolt-hole breathes. They will smell your fear on the wind of it. Take them down, smuggler. Down to the Door of Teeth. The court's own threshold. The finders' rods will not sing past it—the silver deafens them."*

Sable stopped dead. "Absolutely not. The Door of Teeth isn't a route, it's a rumor. Smugglers use it to frighten apprentices. Nobody's come back through it in—"

*"I have,"* said the dead woman simply, and the silence after that had weight, the weight of everything Xanthe never talked about and Sable had never asked.

Xanthe's voice came out of the dark, steady in the way that cost her something. "She went in to bargain for a soul once. Hers wasn't enough. She came back out with nothing but me." A pause. "She knows the way because she memorized it on the worst night of her life. If she says the rods won't follow, the rods won't follow."

Oren spoke for the first time in an hour, and his voice was small but it didn't shake. "Then we go down. Whatever's down there—I've been dreaming about it my whole life anyway. Might as well arrive awake."

Sable looked at the four of them—the witch, the hollow man, the boy, the dead woman in her cage of copper—and felt the splinter catch deep. Eleven runs alone. Twelve, now, with witnesses.

"Fine," she said. "Down. But if the Door of Teeth turns out to actually have teeth, I'm feeding it the boy first. He's the lightest."

"Gerald and Bartholomew will remember you said that," Pip said solemnly, patting a root as he passed.

* * *

The descent took them past the last of the moss-light into stone that drank their glow outright, and the road began, unmistakably, to change. The rough-cut walls smoothed. Then they gleamed. Veins of something bright ran through the rock like frost through a windowpane, and where their hands brushed the stone, the brightness answered—a ripple, a shiver, almost a pulse. The air grew warm. The drips grew musical. Somewhere below, faint and patient, something was singing.

Pip heard it first. Then Oren, whose face went strange and yearning, like a man hearing his own name called from a house he'd never lived in. Then Xanthe, whose hand went to the locket and stayed there.

Sable heard it last, because she was trying hardest not to. But it got under her guard the way water gets under a door—through the seams, through the places she'd caulked years ago and never checked. Voices. Many voices. Some of them, God help her, sounded like people she had sold charms to and never seen again. People who'd climbed into the mountains or been taken, and whose families had asked her, once, if she knew anything, and to whom she had said *no* because saying yes was dangerous and she had a living to make.

The song knew. Songs always know.

"Smuggler." Maren's voice, gentler now than it had been the whole road, which frightened Sable more than any threat. "You've gone pale as the walls. Say something cruel, quickly, before it takes hold of you."

"I'm fine," Sable said, which was a lie, and then, because the dead woman deserved better than a lie after dragging her this far: "I care whether they live. Is that what you wanted to hear? I care. It's inconvenient and I resent it and if any of them die down here I will burn this whole mountain's reputation to the ground so no one ever writes a ballad about it. Now leave me alone."

*"Good girl,"* said Maren, insufferably warm, and the locket dimmed.

Ahead, the passage ended at a wall of silver shot through with darkness, and set into the wall—low, at the height of a kneeling person—was a doorway. Its frame was carved in rows upon rows of teeth, human and otherwise, worn smooth by centuries of hands passing over them in hope or terror. From beyond it, the singing swelled, and beneath the singing, unmistakably now, a second sound: a heartbeat, vast and slow, keeping time for the whole mountain.

Oren stepped forward. His reflection moved in the silver a half-second before he did.

"That's not possible," Xanthe whispered.

"No," Sable agreed, drawing her knife out of pure superstition, "and yet."
