## 6. The Court Beneath

The road ended where the roots ended, and the roots ended where the mountain began to sing.

Xanthe felt it first through the soles of her boots—a vibration too low to be called sound, more like a heartbeat made of stone. Then it rose through her knees, her ribs, the hollow of her throat, until she could not tell whether she was hearing the court or whether the court was hearing her.

"Stop," Sable said, holding up one scarred hand. The smuggler had gone quiet in a way Xanthe had never seen from her—not the silence of caution, but the silence of someone standing at the edge of something they did not have words for. "Listen."

They all listened. Pip pressed his ear against the tunnel wall like a child listening for rain. Oren's lamplighter's pole, which he had carried the whole way down as if it were the last honest thing in his life, trembled faintly in his grip.

Beneath them, layered under the stone like sediment under sediment, was singing. Not one voice—hundreds. Thousands. Voices braided so tightly together that they had ceased to be voices at all and become weather, become tide, become the sound a cathedral makes when no one is inside it but God's absence.

"That's them," Maren's ghost said. She stood apart from the others, translucent as breath on glass, her edges fraying into the dark. Twenty years dead and still she spoke with the crisp authority of a woman who had been burned for knowing too much. "The choir of the sold. Twelve souls, kept singing since before you were born. They are not prisoners here, child. They are *instruments*. The court does not lock them away. It plays them."

Xanthe's stomach turned over slowly, like a stone rolling downhill. "Played how?"

"You'll see." Maren's smile was thin as paper held to a flame. "And when you see it, remember: I taught you the lullaby for a reason. I told you once it wasn't a lullaby at all. I hope you were listening better than I was."

She had said that weeks ago, in the burned-out shell of the cottage, and Xanthe had thought it grief talking. Now, standing on the threshold of the silver court with the choir's song already crawling up through her bones, she understood it had been a warning.

* * *

The passage opened without ceremony. One moment they were squeezing through a seam of root-split rock; the next, the floor dropped away beneath their feet into a gallery of stairs carved from pale stone, descending in wide, shallow sweeps toward a light that came from nowhere and everywhere.

It was not firelight. It was not moonlight. It was silver—the color of the moon's thirteenth turn, the color of the prophecy book's glowing ink, the color of Oren's name written a century before he drew breath. It pooled in the stairwells like water finding its level, and it moved. Slowly. Patiently. With the unhurried confidence of something that has never once been refused.

Pip went first, because Pip always went first now; the boy who had flinched from witch-finders' shadows in the night market had grown strange and fearless underground, as though proximity to the thing he feared most had burned the fear out of him like impurities out of ore. Sable followed, her charms clicking softly against each other at her belt—every outlawed charm she'd ever smuggled, worn openly now, because what was left to hide? Oren walked beside Xanthe, close enough that their shoulders touched, and every few steps he would stop and look back up the stairs toward the daylight none of them could see anymore, as though checking that the world above still existed.

"It's real," Xanthe told him each time. "It's still there. We'll find our way back to it."

He would nod, and walk on, and look back again ten steps later. Xanthe began to understand that this was not fear. This was a man trying to hold onto a self he had never fully owned.

The stairs opened at last into the hall.

There were no words Xanthe knew that could hold it, but she tried anyway, silently, the way she catalogued everything: *vast, vaulted, silver, singing.* The ceiling arched higher than any cathedral in Vellamere, ribbed with veins of luminous metal that pulsed in slow rhythm—the rhythm of the song below, she realized. The song was not coming from somewhere. The song was the architecture. The hall itself breathed music the way lungs breathe air.

And hanging in that breath, suspended in tiers that spiraled down into depths the silver light could not reach, were the souls.

Twelve of them. Xanthe counted twice, because her mind refused the number the first time. Twelve shapes, each no larger than a lantern flame, each burning with a different quality of light—one gold, one white, one the blue of deep ice, one green as foxfire—and each enclosed in a casing of wrought silver shaped like an instrument. A bell. A harp strung with hair-thin wires of light. A pipe organ whose keys were slivers of bone. A drum whose skin was stretched membrane, translucent, veined.

They were beautiful. That was the horror of it. They were *beautiful*, and they were being played.

Xanthe watched a soul shaped like a bell swing gently in a current of silver air, and as it swung, it sang—a single clear note that braided itself into the hall's great chord. She watched the harp-soul's strings shiver, each vibration drawing off a thread of light that fed upward into the ceiling's veins. She watched the drum-soul pulse, and felt the pulse land in her own chest a half-beat later, and understood with cold clarity that her heart was keeping time to a stolen life.

None of them looked distressed. That was the worst part. The bell-soul glowed steady and serene, its light even, its note pure. Whatever agony had attended their selling—a century ago, two centuries, however long the court had been collecting—it had long since been polished into something smooth and shining. Suffering, refined, becomes indistinguishable from beauty. Xanthe had seen it before, in Vellamere's gallows-yard, where the crowds came for the spectacle and left humming the condemned woman's last words like a popular tune.

"They don't know," Oren whispered beside her. His voice cracked on the word *know*. "Do they? They can't know what they—"

"They know," Maren said. Her ghost-form drifted closer to the tiered cages, and the nearest souls leaned toward her like flowers toward sun, recognizing something kin. "That is the cruelest economy of this place. They know exactly what they are. They sing because they remember being people, and the singing is the only shape their remembering can take. Every note is a memory. Every chord is a life, played by hands that never asked permission to play it."

Sable had gone very still. The smuggler who joked at gallows, who haggled with witch-finders, who had looked death in the face so many times it had become a business acquaintance—all her sharpness had drained out through her boots. "I've moved cargo my whole life," she said finally, hoarsely. "Crates, casks, charms, people once or twice, when the price was right and I didn't ask questions. I never once wondered what it sounds like, being cargo." She looked up at the swinging bell. "Now I know. It sounds like that."

"We're not cargo," Pip said fiercely. He was gripping his satchel of charms with both arms, the way children hold things they cannot bear to lose. "We're not. We came to take one back."

"Then take him," Maren said, "and be quick about it. The sovereign knows you've entered. The silver light is its attention, and it is turning toward us even now—you can feel it, can't you, the way the light has begun to pool around your feet like rising water?" She turned to Xanthe. "Find him. Thirteenth place is empty. It has been waiting a hundred years, and it will not wait past the turn of the moon."

* * *

Xanthe left the others at the edge of the great hall and went down alone into the tiers.

The silver light thickened as she descended, until walking through it felt like wading upstream through shallow water. Each step took effort, as though the court were politely, persistently asking her to stop. And the song grew louder—or rather, clearer—as she went, the great braided chord resolving into strands she could almost separate, almost hear as individual lives. A woman's voice, singing a weaving song. A man's voice, singing a planting song. A child's voice, singing something wordless and high that might have been joy once, before.

Her hands were shaking. She noticed it the way you notice rain starting—not all at once, but as a fine cold prickle along the skin. Because she knew these songs. Not these exact songs, perhaps, but their *shape*. Maren had sung while she worked, always, and Xanthe had spent her childhood learning magic set to melody, spellcraft braided into verse, every charm a small song and every song a small door. The court had done the same thing. It had taken lives and turned them into music. It had done to twelve strangers what Maren had done, lovingly, freely, with her apprentice at her knee.

*Her past and her present were the same song,* she thought, and the thought arrived with the weight of a verdict.

The thirteenth place was easy to find, because it was the only empty one.

It hung at the bottom of the spiral, below the deepest tier, in a niche of polished silver shaped precisely like the twelve casings above—but vacant. Waiting. Its hooks gleamed. Its fittings gleamed. Everything about it gleamed with the terrible patience of a cradle prepared long before the child, kept ready through decades, dusted nightly by hands unseen. A century of readiness. The court had built Oren's cage before Oren was born, and it had kept it clean.

Xanthe stood before the empty cradle and felt something in her chest crack sideways.

*They knew his name before he had one.* The prophecy book in the night market, the glowing ink, tonight exactly—the court had not foreseen Oren. The court had *ordered* him. Somewhere above, in the world of lamplight and rent due and small kindnesses, a man had been assembled to specification, delivered to the edge of a hole in the mountain, and told he was free.

She was still standing there, hating the empty cradle with a hatred so pure it tasted like copper, when she heard the humming.

It came from behind a curtain of hanging silver chains, deeper in the niche, where the light bent strangely. A small sound. A private sound. The kind of sound a person makes when they think no one is listening—half-remembered, meandering, tender.

Xanthe knew it immediately. Her body knew it before her mind did; her breath changed to match it, her heartbeat settled into its rhythm, and gooseflesh rose along her arms in the pattern of an old, old memory.

It was the lullaby.

*Maren's lullaby.* The one the hedge-witch had sung to her on bad nights, in the cottage, before the fire took it and the witch-finders took Maren and the city took everything else. The one Maren had sworn, laughing, was just a song she'd made up to soothe a frightened child—*there's nothing in it, Xanthe, it's only notes, only comfort, go to sleep.*

Except it wasn't only notes. Maren had known it wasn't. She had said so, at the end, in the burned cottage, with twenty years of regret in her ghost-mouth: *I taught you the lullaby for a reason. I hope you were listening better than I was.*

And now, beneath the mountain, in the silver court, in the dark behind the chains, something was humming it.

Xanthe parted the chains with both hands, quietly, reverently, the way you part a bead-curtain in a sickroom.

Behind them, in a casing of wrought silver shaped like a small round lantern, hung a flame no larger than a cupped hand. It burned low and warm, gold shot through with threads of ordinary human color—the brown of eyes, the amber of lamplight, the particular hue of a kind man's patience. It swayed gently in the silver air. And as it swayed, it hummed.

Oren's soul was humming Maren's lullaby.

Not the court's grand chord. Not the polished, played, performed music of the twelve above. This was smaller, softer, unfinished—the humming of something that remembered being a person and was holding onto the memory the only way it could. Holding onto it through a song. Through *her* song. Through the song that had passed from Maren's mouth to Xanthe's ear to—who knew where—to here, to this flame, in this cage, a hundred feet beneath the world.

Xanthe pressed her palm flat against the lantern-casing, and the flame leaned toward her warmth the way the twelve had leaned toward Maren's ghost, and the humming gathered itself, and steadied, and became—for three impossible seconds—words.

*"Sleep, little spark, the dark is kind..."*

She had sung those words a thousand times. To herself, in the years after the burning, when sleep would not come. To Pip, that first terrified night in Sable's smuggler's den, when the boy woke screaming from dreams of witch-finders. She had thought she was passing on comfort. She had been passing on a key.

The lullaby was not a lullaby. It was a map. It was a thread, spun by a hedge-witch who had known, somehow, that her student would need to find her way through the dark—that her student's love would end up sold, and caged, and singing in the deep, and that the only rope long enough to reach him would be a song learned at a fireside and carried, unknowing, for twenty years.

Maren had known. Maren had known and had not been able to say it plainly, because saying it plainly would have made it a spell, and spells spoken aloud can be heard, and heard spells can be unraveled. So she had hidden it in a lullaby. She had hidden the rescue inside the comfort. She had given her student a gift wrapped so thoroughly in kindness that neither of them recognized it until the night it was needed.

Xanthe's vision blurred. Not from the silver light. From tears she hadn't permitted herself in twenty years, arriving all at once, in the worst possible place, at the worst possible moment.

Because recognition was not relief. Recognition was horror. The flame humming in the cage was Oren—the gentle man who lit lamps, who walked her home, who asked about her day and meant it. And the song he was humming was Maren's—the executed witch, the burned mentor, the mother-shaped hole in Xanthe's heart. Two loves, separated by death and decades, meeting here in a silver cage, in the same seven notes.

Her past and present were the same song. And the song was a cage.

She knelt before the lantern-casing, on stone that had been polished by a century of waiting, and put her face as close to the silver mesh as the light allowed, and sang back.

Softly. Under the court's great chord, under the threshold of what the sovereign's attention could parse. Just the first line, just the way Maren used to sing it when the nights were bad—slightly off-key, slightly too slow, imperfect in the specific way that meant *a person is singing this, not an instrument.*

The flame heard her.

It brightened—not much, not dramatically, nothing the watching light above would notice. But it turned toward her voice the way a cold hand turns toward a window's sunbeam, and its humming shifted, aligning, note by note, with hers, until for a moment there were two singers in the dark instead of one, and the song between them was no longer a cage but a conversation.

Then the silver light pooling around Xanthe's feet rose an inch, deliberate as a tide, and she felt the court's attention settle onto her like a hand laid flat on the crown of her head.

Somewhere far above, in the vaulted heart of the hall, a voice that was not a voice began to speak.
