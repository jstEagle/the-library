## 7. A Bargain in Silver

The voice that was not a voice came from everywhere and nowhere, the way cold comes through a wall. It did not enter Xanthe's ears. It arrived already inside her skull, resonant as a struck bell held against her teeth.

*Thief.*

The word was not an accusation. That was the worst of it. It was spoken the way one reads a label on a jar—*thief*, *flame-keeper*, *Maren's girl*—an inventory of what she was, catalogued without malice. The court beneath the mountain had been counting souls for a hundred years. One more name in its ledger meant nothing to it at all.

Xanthe kept her knees on the silvered stone and her hands flat at her sides, though every instinct screamed at her to run, to shield, to burn. Running was for creatures the court considered real. She had understood, in the long walk down from the smugglers' road, that here she was something smaller: a discrepancy. A note out of place in a song a century old.

"I've come for the thirteenth soul," she said aloud. Her voice sounded thin, human, absurdly warm in all that chill radiance. "Oren Vale. Lamplighter. Third district, Vellamere."

*We know what he is.* The light above her shifted, and she saw then that it was not light at all but a presence wearing light the way a spider wears dust—a vast slow turning of silver, patient as sediment. *We know what you are. We have known since the hedge-witch's ghost crossed our threshold with your name folded into her silence.*

"Maren." Her throat closed around it.

*She bargained poorly,* said the sovereign. *Grief makes poor coin. But you—you come with something we have not held in a hundred years. You come with a memory still warm.*

The flame before her flickered. Oren's soul—the small captive fire humming Maren's lullaby—leaned toward her voice again, and Xanthe felt the pull of it like a hook behind her sternum. *Come here,* the hum said. *Come home.*

"What do you want," she said, "for him."

The silver turned. And turned. And when the answer came, it came gently, which was somehow more terrible than any demand could have been.

*A trade. As all things beneath the mountain are traded. A memory of equal weight for a soul of equal weight. Bring us your heaviest remembering, thief, and carry your lamplighter into the morning.*

* * *

They gave her a place to sit, because even monsters observe forms. A shelf of silvered stone, worn smooth by centuries of other sitters—she did not let herself think about who else had sat where she sat, weighing what they would pay. Sable's voice in her head: *Never sit down in a negotiation, Thornbury. Sitting is surrender with better posture.* But this was not a negotiation. Negotiations ended with both parties walking away. This was arithmetic.

The flame hovered at her knee now, brought close by the court's own courtesy, close enough that its warmth touched her like a small animal seeking shelter. Up close she could see what she had only sensed from across the choir-hall: shapes within the fire, the way faces live in coals. Not Oren's face—not yet, not quite—but the suggestion of him, the way he tilted his head when he listened, the particular gentleness of his attention. A soul, she realized, was not a thing but a leaning. His leaned toward her.

"Tell me how it works," she said. "Exactly. I want no surprises."

*You will choose a memory. You will hold it in your mind and give it a weight—all memories have weights; yours are unusually heavy, thief—and we will take it. Whole. Root and branch. You will not remember that you remembered. The shape it leaves will ache, as any absence aches, but the ache will be anonymous. You will not know what you lost. Only that you are lighter.*

"And if the memory isn't heavy enough?"

*Then the flame dims,* said the sovereign, without cruelty, without anything at all, *and we try again. We are patient. We have a hundred years of practice waiting for people to finish paying.*

Xanthe looked down at the little fire. It hummed the lullaby—*sleep, my sparrow, the hedge grows deep*—and her chest cracked open along a seam twenty years old.

Because she knew that song. Knew it before the words, before the melody, in her hands: the way Maren's fingers had moved when she sang it, kneading bread or crushing feverfew, the way the kitchen of the burned cottage had smelled on the nights Maren sang it—woodsmoke and honey and the green bite of herbs hung drying from the rafters. The lullaby was not a separate memory. It was threaded through everything. Through the first charm Maren ever taught her (a warming cantrip, sung, not spoken). Through the night the witch-finders came and Maren pushed her out the back door and stood in the yard with her arms open like a scarecrow and burned. Through twenty years of singing it under her breath in rented rooms, in alleys, over graves, so that some part of her teacher would keep breathing in the world.

There was no such thing, she understood now, as one memory of Maren. There was only Maren, distributed through her like salt through seawater. To pay for Oren soul by soul, memory by memory, she would have to unmake herself in increments—and each increment would feel, afterward, like nothing at all. That was the trap's true teeth. Grief you could refuse. Absence you couldn't even find.

"Start with the smallest," she told the sovereign, and hated how steady her voice came out.

*Choose.*

She chose the day Maren taught her to whistle through a grass blade. A nothing-day. A summer afternoon, cicadas, Maren laughing until she wheezed because Xanthe could only produce a dying goose sound. Small. Light. Surely light enough.

She held it up in her mind, offered it—and felt the court take hold of it the way a tide takes hold of a sandcastle: not violently, just completely. For one white instant she saw it whole—grass between her lips, Maren's laugh, the heat shimmering off the lane—and then it was gone, and there was a smooth blank where it had been, and the blank did not hurt at all, and that was the horror: it didn't hurt. She reached for the afternoon and found polished emptiness, and some distant part of her screamed that something had been taken, while the rest of her stood in the quiet, lighter, fine, perfectly fine.

The flame brightened. Just slightly. Oren's soul burned a shade steadier, and somewhere in Vellamere, she supposed, a lamplighter dreamed a little less of silver.

"One," she said. Her voice shook. "Again."

* * *

She paid in afternoons.

That was how she thought of them, in the stretches between payments when she sat on the silvered shelf with the flame at her knee and tried to inventory what remained. Afternoons. Maren teaching her to read weather in the behavior of swallows. Maren's terrible handwriting. The time the goat got into the drying shed and Maren chased it swearing words Xanthe hadn't known a woman of sixty possessed. Each one lifted out whole, root and branch, and each time the space it left was seamless, painless, clean—and each time the flame grew brighter, and each time she loved Oren more desperately for reasons she could no longer count.

By the tenth payment she had stopped crying. Not from courage. She had simply forgotten, in the way of water forgetting rain, what she was losing. She knew she had loved someone named Maren the way you know a country exists that you've never visited. The name was in the prophecy book. The name was in Pip's frightened questions. The name was hers, apparently, once. But the kitchen was gone. The smell of woodsmoke and honey was gone. The back door, the yard, the burning—gone, gone, gone, and in their place a smooth and shining hollowness that the court found very nourishing indeed.

Only two things resisted.

The lullaby remained. She tested it after every payment, singing it under her breath—*sleep, my sparrow, the hedge grows deep*—and it stayed, stubbornly, though she no longer knew who had taught it to her or why her hands made kneading motions when she sang. The court circled it sometimes, the great silver turning slowing, considering, the way a diner considers the last sweet on the plate. But it did not take it. She did not ask why. Asking felt like pointing a lantern at the last dry corner of her soul.

And the flame—Oren's flame—had begun to sing back.

It started during the eleventh payment. She had given up a winter—Maren's last winter, the coughing, the feverfew running out, the two of them sitting hip to hip against the stove because it was warmer than grief—and when she surfaced from the taking, hollow and lighter and fine, perfectly fine, the little fire had pressed itself against her hand, and its hum had changed. It was still the lullaby. But it was carrying the harmony. Her harmony. The part she must have sung, once, without knowing she'd learned it.

It was singing her part back to her.

"You don't have to do that," she whispered, and her voice broke on the second word, and she discovered she was crying after all, though she could not have said for whom.

The flame only hummed louder.

* * *

The twelfth payment was the yard. She knew it was the yard even as she chose it, knew it the way you know a wound through cloth, because it was the heaviest thing left that wasn't the lullaby: Maren standing in the doorway saying *run, sparrow, run*, the orange bloom of it, the smell of her own hair scorching as she crouched in the ditch and watched and did not go back, did not go back, did not—

The court took it. The flame flared gold-white, nearly whole now, and Xanthe sat in the ringing silence of herself and counted what remained. A name with no face. A song with no source. Hands that knew charms her mind could not explain. And love—vast, directionless love, pooled in her chest with nowhere left to land, so it landed on the flame, all of it, everything, the whole inheritance poured into a creature the size of a cupped palm.

"One more," said the sovereign, and for the first time its not-voice carried something almost like hunger. *One more, thief, and he walks out of our mountain whole. You know the one we want. You have been keeping it behind your teeth since you sat down.*

Xanthe looked at the flame. It looked back—it leaned back—with everything Oren was, everything gentle and lamplit and worth a hundred afternoons, a dozen winters, a burning yard.

Her lips parted around the lullaby's first note.

And the mountain shuddered.
