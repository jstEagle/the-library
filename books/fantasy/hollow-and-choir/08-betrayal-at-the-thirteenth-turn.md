## 8. Betrayal at the Thirteenth Turn

Pip had never seen a moon finish anything before. In Vellamere the moon was a coin tossed over rooftops, half-swallowed by chimney smoke, always interrupted. Down here beneath the mountain, in the long silver dark of the court's antechamber, it hung whole and patient in a shaft that opened onto the sky through a crack no wider than a coffin, and it was filling itself like a cup.

The thirteenth turn. Maren's riddles had said it plain enough: *when the moon has counted to thirteen, the debt comes due.* Pip counted along with it now, the way he'd once counted the seconds between his master's footsteps on the stairs of the charm-shop, the way he counted everything, because counting was a kind of prayer and he had never learned any others.

Thirteen days they had walked under the roots of the world. Thirteen songs the choir had sung through the stone, each one a little more familiar, until Pip could hum them in his sleep and sometimes did, waking Sable, who swore at him in three languages.

And thirteen people stood in the antechamber now, which was one too many, and Pip knew it, and had known it since the road under the roots, and had said nothing.

He crouched at the edge of the silver pool where Oren's soul burned—a small flame, stubborn as a candle-end, humming the lullaby. Xanthe knelt beside it with her sleeves pushed back and her hands in the light, and even from across the chamber Pip could see how thin she had gotten, how her wrists looked like the stems of cut flowers. The trade was nearly done. She had fed the sovereign memory after memory, and each one went out of her like a lamp going out in a window, and the flame in the pool grew brighter for it, and Oren—Oren slept against the far wall, breathing slow and deep, his face for the first time in his life not hollow at all.

*It's working,* everyone kept saying. *She's winning.*

Pip pressed his thumb against the scar on his palm, where the witch-finder's brand had been turned into a charm-scar by Sable's needlework, and thought about the letter sewn into his coat lining.

* * *

His name wasn't Pip. That was the first lie, and the easiest. His name was Aldous Fenwick, apprentice third-class to the Office of Finding, and he had been eleven years old when they put the ink under his skin: a sigil that woke when he lied to his handlers and slept when he obeyed. A leash made of letters. They told him it was an honor. They were the only adults who had ever told him anything at all.

For six years he had reported on charmsellers, hedge-witches, a woman who sold love-potions that were only gin. Small sins. Bearable sins. He had told himself the word *bearable* so many times it wore smooth as a river stone.

Then came the night market raid, and the girl with the burned-out eyes who moved like someone twice her age, and Sable hauling him over a stall by the collar of his coat, swearing at him with such inventive fury that he had laughed—actually laughed—for the first time since his mother died. And then the road under the roots, where Sable gave him the watch shift nearest her own without ever saying why, and Xanthe taught him a mending-charm using Maren's cadences, and Oren sat up with him when the dreams got bad and said, simply, *I know what it is to be owned,* because of course he did, he was the thirteenth soul, he had been sold before he was born.

The letter in his coat lining was written in his own hand, dictated down a witch-finder relay-stone three nights ago while the others slept. It named their position. It named the pool. It named the hour of the thirteenth turn.

In exchange: the ink under his skin would be cut out, and he would be given papers, and a horse, and a new name in a city where no one sang.

He had dictated it with the sigil burning cold in his arm, and he had hated every word, and he had sent it anyway, because the alternative was the finding-wardens peeling the truth out of him like fruit, and then sending it anyway regardless, only bloodier.

That was what he told himself. That was the smooth worn stone he carried.

* * *

The moon touched the top of its arc, and the choir stopped singing.

Not faded. Stopped. Thirteen voices cut off between one note and the next, and the silence that followed had weight, had texture, pressed against the ears like deep water. The flame in the pool stood up straight, taller than Xanthe, and its lullaby changed key.

Xanthe lifted her head. "No," she said softly. "No, we're not finished—"

The silver walls began to move.

Pip saw it first from the corner of his eye: the filigree patterns in the antechamber's walls unweaving themselves, straightening into lines, into script. Words older than Vellamere crawling up out of the metal like insects after rain. And beneath the floor, a sound like a great bell being struck underwater—the sovereign waking, the debt presenting itself, the court remembering that it had been promised a soul entire.

Oren gasped awake. Sat up. Looked at his own hands as if they belonged to the room.

"The turn," Sable snarled, already moving, knives out of her boots. "Something's wrong. Girl! What did you give it?"

"Everything I—" Xanthe's voice cracked. She was looking at her hands too, turning them over, and Pip understood with a sick drop of his stomach: she couldn't remember what she'd paid. The memories were gone; the flame was bright; but the court didn't want brightness. The court wanted *all of him*, and the moon had finished counting, and somewhere above them, boots were coming down a smugglers' road that only one person alive had mapped for the enemy.

Sable's head snapped around. She looked at Pip. Just looked. She had known, he realized—had maybe always known, had read the letter weeks ago with those black-market eyes that missed nothing—and she had waited to see what he would do.

The sigil under his skin woke like ice water.

*Report,* it said, in the handler's dry voice. *Confirm the trap is sprung. Confirm the thief is contained.*

"No," Pip said aloud.

The walls answered instead. Doors he hadn't known were doors slid open along the antechamber's curve, and through them came the wardens—not men, not yet, but the court's own instruments, figures of quicksilver wearing the shapes of the sold, faces Pip recognized from the choir's song. And behind them, faint but coming closer, human boots on root-hewn stone. Witch-finders. His witch-finders. Right on schedule, because he had scheduled them.

"Pip." Xanthe was on her feet, flame-light on her face. "Pip, what's happening?"

He had perhaps ten seconds. Maybe eight. He spent two of them looking at all of them—Sable with her knives, Xanthe with her emptied self, Oren standing between sleep and claim, and the little flame in the pool humming Maren's lullaby, the song that had been sung to Pip exactly once, by a woman in a charm-shop doorway, the day before the finders took him.

Found family proven in blood, Maren's ghost might have said. Pip had never met her ghost. But he had memorized her riddles on the road, all thirteen, and the last one came back to him now with terrible clarity:

*What does the thief steal when there is nothing left to take? The moment. The moment is always the thief's.*

"Xanthe," he said. "Sing. Now. Whatever you have left—sing it at the sovereign, don't stop, don't listen to anything it says back. Sable—get Oren to the pool, get his hand in the flame, the soul will know him. Go."

"And you?" Sable said.

"I'm going to be late for work."

He ran toward the doors.

* * *

The trap, properly sprung, worked like this: the wardens held the company, the finders took custody, the court claimed its soul, and the informant stood aside and signed the receipt. Pip had watched the drill a hundred times in the training yards below the Office of Finding. He knew every beat of it.

So he broke every beat.

The first warden reached him at the threshold and he gave it the charm Sable had sold him in the night market, back when they were strangers: a sliver of amber called *the honest debt*, which repaid whatever violence was offered at double weight. It should have killed him. It was designed to kill him; Sable had said so cheerfully; *never use this unless dying is cheaper than the alternative.* The warden's blow took him off his feet and the amber took the warden apart into a rain of mercury beads, and Pip lay on the cold silver floor with three ribs gone and blood in his mouth and laughed again, that same wild laugh from the night market, because dying was indeed cheaper than the alternative, and he had finally found something worth the price.

The second warden he tripped into the pool. The flame rose up and *sang at it*, and the thing unraveled, because the choir's song was a song of the stolen and nothing stolen can bear to hear itself named.

The third one he didn't fight. He wrapped his arms around it, both of them, the way you hold a door shut, and he dug his branded arm into the space between its quicksilver ribs, and he let the sigil burn. Let it wake all the way. Let it call home to every handler, every relay-stone, every listening ear in the Office of Finding, and through the sigil he poured everything the court had taught him in thirteen days underground: the shape of the sovereign's attention, the frequency of the choir, the exact pitch at which silver remembers it was once ore and wants, desperately, to be ore again.

A signal, sent backward up the leash. Not a report. A scream.

Above him, distantly, the witch-finders' boots stopped. Somewhere in the dark of the road under the roots, relay-stones burst one after another like grapes in a fist.

The warden in his arms shuddered and went still, and its borrowed face—that of a girl sold two hundred years ago, whose song Pip had hummed in his sleep—softened into something almost grateful before it ran off the floor in threads.

Pip rolled onto his back. The moon hung in its coffin-crack of sky, full past full, beginning—finally, mercifully—to wane. One turn ending. Another beginning.

Across the chamber, Xanthe was singing. Her voice was wrecked and small and utterly fearless, and the flame in the pool had become a pillar, and Oren's hand was buried in it to the wrist, and the walls of the court were shaking loose their script like a book shaken by its spine.

Sable dropped to her knees beside him, hands already moving over his ruined ribs, muttering charms in three languages, most of them anatomically impossible.

"You knew," Pip managed.

"Course I knew." Her voice was rougher than the road. "You're a terrible liar, boy. You count when you're guilty."

"Did you—were you going to—"

"Sell you to the finders myself, if you sprang it clean." She pressed a charm against his chest that burned like a struck match. "But you didn't. You broke your own trap. So now I've got to keep you alive, and I hate sewing, and this is going to hurt."

"It already hurts."

"Good. Means you'll live." She glanced up, once, at the singing, at the pillar of fire, at the mountain beginning to groan around them like a ship in weather. "Whatever happens next, Aldous Fenwick—you stay down, and you breathe, and you let her finish it."

Above them, the moon tipped past its turning, and the sovereign of the silver court spoke its first word in a hundred years, and the word was Xanthe's name.
