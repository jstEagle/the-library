## 9. Hollow and Choir

Xanthe could no longer remember the color of Maren's eyes.

She knew she had once known. The knowledge itself remained, a smooth hollow in her, like the socket of a pulled tooth, and she found herself pressing her tongue against the absence the way she had pressed it against that gap at nine years old—half grief, half fascination. The memory of the cottage garden had gone next, then the smell of the tinctures steeping on the back shelf, then the sound of Maren laughing at something Xanthe had said when she was twelve and clever and not yet afraid of her own hands.

The sovereign had been pleased with each offering. That was the worst of it. The silver court did not take; it *received*, graciously, the way a host receives gifts, and each time Xanthe laid a memory on the scale it chimed—a small, sweet note—and somewhere in the dark above the throne, Oren's flame burned a little brighter.

"Enough," Sable said. She had been saying it for what felt like hours, her voice worn down to a rasp. "Girl. Enough. You've given it half your life already."

"Half," Xanthe repeated. The word tasted strange. She was trying to work out whether it was true.

The court stretched around them like the inside of a bell: vaulted halls of silver-veined stone, pillars carved with the faces of the sold, and everywhere, everywhere, the singing. The choir hung in cages of light along the walls—thirteen flames, twelve still caged and one straining toward freedom, each humming the song that had been theirs in life. Oren's flame hummed a lullaby. She could not remember where she had learned that lullaby. She only knew that when it reached her, something in her chest answered before her mind could, the way a body flinches from a blow.

The sovereign sat upon its throne of woven moonlight and watched her with an expression she could not read, because it had no face she could hold onto. It was beautiful the way frost on a window is beautiful, and just as indifferent to what lay beneath.

"One more," it said. Its voice was the sound of a coin dropped in a well. "You have one more of weight. Give it, and the thirteenth soul goes free, and the bargain is honored, and you may walk out of my mountain whole. Well—" a pause, delicate as a knife, "—as whole as you have left yourself."

Xanthe reached into the hollow where her memories of Maren had been and found the last one waiting.

It was small. That was the cruelty of it. Not the cottage, not the execution, not the night Maren had found a terrified child hiding witchcraft in her cupped hands and had not reported her. It was a morning. An ordinary morning. Maren at the table, grinding something in a mortar, saying: *Listen, little bird. A song isn't yours because you wrote it. It's yours because you'd die before you'd let it go unsung.*

She could not remember Maren's face, but she remembered that. It was the last stone in the wall of her, and the sovereign wanted it, and if she gave it, she would walk out of this mountain with Oren's soul cupped in her hands and no idea who had taught her how to hold anything at all.

"Xanthe." Pip's voice, thin and cracking. He had a burn along his jaw from the trap, from the charm that should have killed him, and he was looking at her the way she imagined she had once looked at Maren. "Don't. Please. There has to be another way."

"There isn't," she said, and was surprised at how steady she sounded. "There never was. The prophecy didn't say *steal*, Pip. It said *sing it back*. I just—" she searched the hollows inside herself, the empty rooms where her teacher used to live, "—I think I misunderstood the instrument."

* * *

She stepped forward, alone, to the center of the court, where the floor was polished to a mirror and showed her a woman she half-recognized: hollow-cheeked, hollow-eyed, a lantern with the candle burned down to the wick.

"Sovereign," she said. "I'll give you the last of her. But I give it my way. A memory given freely must be *sung*—that's the old law, older than your mountain. Even you are bound by it."

The sovereign's stillness sharpened. Somewhere in the vaults of the hall, the twelve caged flames stirred, their humming faltering, listening.

"Sing, then," it said.

So Xanthe closed her eyes, and opened her mouth, and began to sing the court's own song back at it.

She did not know where it came from. That was the truth of it—she had no memory left of learning it, and so it rose out of her the way water rises out of a spring, from somewhere deeper than recollection. It was the lullaby Oren's flame hummed. It was the song the choir sang. It was the song Maren had taught a nine-year-old girl in a kitchen that no longer existed anywhere but in this note, this one note, held and passed forward like a flame from candle to candle down twenty dark years.

Her voice cracked on the second verse. She had almost nothing left to sing with. But the song did not need her to be whole. That was the secret at the center of it, the thing Maren had known and the sovereign had forgotten across its long silver centuries: a song is not diminished by being carried. It is *made* of being carried.

The caged flames began to answer.

One voice first—a woman's, high and fierce, singing a harvest song that had not been sung above ground in a hundred years. Then another, a child's, wavering. Then the rest, all of them, twelve stolen souls finding their own melodies inside her melody, harmonies braiding out of their cages of light, and the hall began to tremble the way a struck bell trembles before the sound.

"Stop." The sovereign rose from its throne, and the moonlight of it curdled. "Those souls are *mine*. Bought. Paid. Sealed in silver—"

"Paid for," Xanthe sang, "is not the same as *owned*," and the words were not hers, they were Maren's, they were the law Maren had died rather than break, and the choir took them up and threw them back in a hundred voices until the pillars themselves seemed to say it.

The cages of light did not shatter. They *unlocked*—as if they had only ever been latched from the inside, as if the souls had stayed because no one had ever sung them the way home sounds. The first flame slipped free and rose, and then the rest, a slow bright snowfall in reverse, and the sovereign screamed a sound like a mountain cracking, and the silver veins in the stone began to go dark, vein by vein, like a body losing warmth.

Oren's flame came to her last.

It hovered before her, humming, and she cupped her hands around it the way she had once cupped a spark for Maren to see, and the flame settled into her palms like a bird coming home to a nest it remembered. Warm. Lighter than it should have been. Singing.

*Run,* said a voice inside her own chest—not her voice, older, wry, unbearably fond—and Xanthe, who could not remember her teacher's face, knew her teacher's voice as surely as she knew her own heartbeat.

She ran.

* * *

The mountain was coming down around them, and it came down *quietly*, which was the most terrible thing about it. No roar. The silver court had been held together by the sovereign's claim, and with the claim shattered the halls were simply forgetting how to stand, folding inward with a sound like snow sliding off a roof, like a held breath released after a hundred years.

Sable caught her at the first stair, hauled her upright with a smuggler's grip. "The flame—"

"Here." Xanthe could not feel her hands. She could not feel much of anything. "Don't let me drop it."

"I won't." And then, gruffer, because Sable would have chewed off her own tongue before saying anything softer: "You stupid, brave, impossible girl."

Pip ran ahead with a charm flaring green to light the way, and behind them the throne room folded into dust and the twelve freed souls streamed upward through cracks in the mountain like light through a shuttered window, going home, going home, going wherever souls go when they are finally sung free.

Xanthe did not look back. She was afraid of what she would feel—or worse, what she would not feel. She had given away the cottage and the garden and the laugh and the face and the last ordinary morning, and what remained was a woman running up a dark road with a stranger's soul in her hands and no memory of why any of it had mattered.

Except.

Except her feet knew the way. Her hands knew how to shield the flame from the falling dust without smothering it. Her voice, when Pip stumbled and began to cry, knew exactly what to hum—a scrap of lullaby, four notes, worn smooth as a river stone—to steady him.

She had emptied herself of Maren. But Maren's craft lived in her hands regardless, the way a song lives in the throat of someone who has forgotten the words.

They came out of the mountain's mouth into a night so violently ordinary that Xanthe nearly laughed. Real stars. Cold wind smelling of pine and rain. Below them, far off, the lamps of Vellamere, and one lamp—she knew it without knowing how she knew—burning at the end of a street called Chandler's Row.

In her hands, Oren's flame pulsed once, twice, like a heart remembering its rhythm.

"Is he—" Pip whispered. "Is he going to be all right?"

Xanthe looked down at the small fire cupped against her breastbone, and felt the hollow places inside herself echo, and did not know the answer. She did not know if a soul could be given back to a body after a century of theft. She did not know what would remain of Oren, or of her. She only knew that the flame was humming her lullaby now—the one she had sung in the court, the one that was Maren's and hers and now, somehow, everyone's—and that somewhere down in the city, a lamplighter was lying cold and still, waiting for his song to come home.

"Get him to Chandler's Row before moonset," she said. Her voice came out scraped raw, a stranger's voice. "And Sable—when we get there, someone will have to tell me who he is. Because I gave away almost everything I had in that mountain." She looked at the flame, and the flame looked back, warm and patient and trusting. "But not the part that matters. Whatever's left of me knows how to carry this."

The moon was sliding toward the thirteenth turn, and the last of the night was running out like sand.
