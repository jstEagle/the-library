## 10. What We Choose to Carry

They came out of the mountain through the smugglers' road, and the dawn was waiting for them like a debt.

Xanthe remembered the road in pieces—Sable's lantern swinging ahead, Pip's small hand gripping hers, the taste of iron and old rain in the dark—but she did not remember why her arms ached, or why she kept her cupped hands pressed to her chest as though holding a bird that might startle. She knew only that whatever lived there was warm, and that it hummed when she breathed, and that she had paid something enormous for it. The shape of the payment was gone. Only the receipt remained: this hollowness behind her ribs, clean-edged, like a room swept bare.

"Almost there," Sable said, for the fourth time. Her voice had lost its merchant's polish somewhere under the roots. It was just a voice now, tired and human. "Chandler's Row. Oren's lodging-house. He told me once he liked the smell of the tallow works." A pause. "He tells people things. That's his whole trick."

"I don't remember him," Xanthe said.

The words came out flat, and she watched them land on Sable's back like stones. Sable didn't turn around. "You carried him out of a collapsing mountain," she said. "That's a kind of remembering."

* * *

Chandler's Row smelled of beeswax and morning. They laid him on his own narrow bed, the flame still cupped in Xanthe's hands, and then everyone stood around looking at one another because none of them knew what came next. The prophecy book was ash in Pip's satchel—or half of it was; the thirteenth page had burned itself blank the moment the mountain began to fall, and Pip kept touching the empty paper like a wound that wouldn't close.

"What do I do?" Xanthe asked the room. She hated how small her voice sounded. "Someone has to tell me what to do."

"No one tells you," said Maren's hands.

She looked down at them. They were moving without her—her fingers opening, closing, tracing a pattern in the air above the flame that she did not know and knew perfectly. A circle drawn backward. A breath taken in and given away. Somewhere in the muscle and bone of her, in some place deeper than memory, a woman with a voice like woodsmoke had spent years teaching these hands what to do, and the teaching had not been stored where the silver court could reach it. It had been worked into her, the way a song is worked into a body: not remembered but *kept*, the way the heart keeps beating.

Her hands cupped over the flame. She leaned down, and though she could not have said whose mouth she was borrowing, she sang.

It was a lullaby. She didn't know its name. She didn't know who had first sung it to her, or in what kitchen, or how many nights of fever and fear it had seen her through. But the melody rose in her throat whole and unbroken, and the flame rose to meet it, trembling, brightening, stretching up toward her lips like a child toward a window at dawn.

Oren woke the way light wakes—not all at once, but by degrees, the gray going gold at the edges. His eyes opened. They found hers.

"You're crying," he said. His voice was rough with sleep and something older than sleep.

"Am I?" She touched her cheek, surprised. "I don't know what for."

"That's all right." He sat up slowly, wincing, alive, impossibly alive, and took her hands in both of his. His palms were warm from the flame. "I'll cry enough for both of us. I've been practicing my whole life." And he laughed—a wet, cracked, astonished laugh—and pulled her into an embrace that smelled of lamp oil and beeswax and morning, and Xanthe held on to him the way you hold on to a rope over a long drop, and let herself be held.

Behind them, Pip made a sound that was trying very hard not to be a sob. Sable put a hand on the boy's shoulder and did not pretend she wasn't crying either.

* * *

Vellamere changed slowly, the way ice changes before it breaks.

At first there was only talk. Talk in the markets, in the tallow houses, in the queues outside the bakeries: whispers about lights in the mountain, about a singing heard across three districts at moonset, about witch-finders who had gone up the slopes in force and come down looking like men who had seen their own funerals. The city had spent two decades pretending magic was a rumor it had agreed to stop repeating. Now the rumor was loose again, and it had witnesses, and the witnesses would not shut up.

A fishwife swore she'd seen a soul fly out of the broken halls like a spark off a grindstone. Two lamplighters claimed the streetlamps along Cordwainer Street had lit themselves at dusk, no oil, no taper, just a soft gold blooming in the glass. The witch-finders' headquarters on Bellows Square stood shuttered, and someone had chalked thirteen marks on its door—one for every soul sold beneath the mountain, though only twelve had ever been recovered, and the city counted them aloud each night like a rosary with a missing bead.

"They're frightened," Sable said, three weeks later, in the back room of a charmseller's shop that had until recently been a smuggler's cache. "Frightened people want stories. So give them one they can live inside." She spread a broadsheet across the table: crude woodcut, bold letters. *THE THIRTEENTH SOUL RETURNS.* Beneath it, smaller: *Magic is not the crime. What was done to children is.* "We print a hundred more tonight. Pip's got the block carved."

Pip looked up from the press, ink to his elbows, and grinned at her with the gap-toothed grin of a boy who had chosen courage once and found it fit better than fear. "The type's crooked."

"The type's always crooked," Sable said. "Crooked type prints true anyway."

Xanthe stood by the window, watching the street. Below, a woman was teaching her daughter to skip rope, chanting a rhyme Xanthe almost recognized—the lullaby, bent into a skipping song already, mutating as songs do, becoming common property, becoming the city's. She felt something tighten and release in her chest. Whatever Maren had been—and Xanthe could no longer see her face, could no longer summon her voice or her laugh or the particular way she'd held a teacup like a bird she meant to free—she was in that rhyme now. In Xanthe's hands. In the twelve souls who had walked out of the mountain and scattered into Vellamere like seeds on wind, humming.

Inheritance, she thought, was not a chest you opened. It was a song you chose to keep singing.

* * *

On the first warm evening of spring, Oren took her walking.

He was a lamplighter again—he had insisted, saying the city needed its lamps and he needed his ladder—and they went together down streets he named for her, since she had lost the names along with everything else. Not the city itself; her geography survived, her craft survived, the deep strata of who she was. But the last year was pocked with gaps. Their first meeting. The night market. The moment she'd seen his name burning in a dead woman's book. He gave these back to her gently, one story at a time, like a man returning borrowed things.

"So," he said, lighting a lamp on Tanner's Corner, the flame leaping obediently to his taper. "The book said you'd steal me back. Did it say anything about what happens after?"

"I can't read it anymore. It burned." She smiled. "But I don't think prophecies work the way we thought. I think they're just... someone's guess. Sung forward. And a guess isn't a chain."

"No," he agreed. "A guess is an invitation." He closed the little glass door on the lamp and turned to her, and the light caught the side of his face, and she thought—not for the first time—that she did not need the missing memories to know him. Knowing wasn't storage. Knowing was what you did next.

Below them, the city was lighting itself. Not everywhere—not yet—but here and there, windows glowing without tapers, a charm-seller's stall open boldly at dusk, a child drawing a circle in chalk on the cobbles while her mother watched and did not call the finders. The ban was cracking like river ice, quietly, from underneath, and nobody could say anymore exactly when it had started to give.

Somewhere down the row, someone was singing. The lullaby again—it was everywhere now—and Xanthe found her lips moving with it, harmonizing without deciding to, and Oren laughed and joined in low, and their two voices braided together over the lamplit street.

There was a gap in her where a teacher used to be. She felt it some evenings, a coolness like an unlit lamp. But she had learned that a gap is also a doorway, and that love does not require the memory of its object—only the practice of it. Every time her hands moved in the old patterns, every time she taught Pip a warding-sign or hummed the melody to a frightened stranger, she was choosing her again. Choosing to carry her forward. That, it turned out, was all inheritance had ever been: not what we are given, but what we refuse to put down.

"Come on," Oren said, taking her hand. "Forty lamps between here and the river. The city's waiting to be lit."

And they walked down into the gathering glow together, singing.
