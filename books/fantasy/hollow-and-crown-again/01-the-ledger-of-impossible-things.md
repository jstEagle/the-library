## 1. The Ledger of Impossible Things

The Guild of Unmapped Realms kept its accounts the way it kept everything else: badly, but with great conviction.

Lior Oyelaran sat at the long oak table in the guild hall's back room, sleeves rolled to the elbow, a pot of cold tea at his right hand and the ledger open before him like a patient wound. Outside, the autumn rain did its best impression of a border storm, drumming on the shingles while Sefa hammered something in the smithy that was either a ward-anchor or a very ambitious horseshoe. The rhythm had become, over eleven years, the sound of home.

"Item forty-one," he read aloud to himself, because the ledger preferred to be spoken to. "One bottled echo, medium quality, sold to the Widow Marsh of Lower Fenwick. Payment received: three jars of quince preserves and one promise never to sing at dawn again."

He dipped his pen. The ink took the entry without complaint.

That was the thing about the ledger — the guild's great black book, older than any of them, bound in leather that might have been cow or might have been something with opinions. It recorded what was true. Not what was written down; what was *true*. If you tried to enter a lie, the page stayed blank until you corrected yourself, which had made for some memorable arguments between Odell and the tax assessor from Vess City. The assessor had insisted the guild owed duties on "intangible goods." The ledger had insisted otherwise, loudly, by refusing to acknowledge that the guild existed at all for six weeks. Odell had won. The assessor had retired to the coast and taken up fishing, which everyone agreed was safer for him.

Lior turned the page. Borrowed shadows, current inventory: four. One belonged to a merchant who'd lent his out during a bad marriage and now couldn't quite decide if he wanted it back. Two were unclaimed, left behind by travelers who'd crossed the border into the Hollow and never returned — those were kept in a cedar box, folded like handkerchiefs, and Lior checked on them every month because someone should. The fourth shadow technically belonged to Bram, who had pawned it twice and stolen it back both times, which meant its provenance was, in ledger terms, *contested*.

"Contested," Lior wrote, underlining it twice. "Again."

The door banged open and Odell came in shaking rain off her coat like a large affable dog. She was sixty, built like a barrel that had decided to become a person, and she carried a sack of turnips as though it were an insult she intended to return to sender.

"The farmer up the hill paid his warding fee," she announced. "In turnips."

"We're a guild of record, not a greengrocer."

"Tell that to Farmer Hobb. Go on. He'll cry, and then I'll feel bad, and then we'll have turnips anyway." She dumped the sack on the table beside the ledger, which considered the turnips for a moment and then, mercifully, accepted them as payment-in-kind. Lior made a note. The ink went down slightly grudgingly, like a cat being picked up.

"How is the great balancing going?" Odell asked, peering over his shoulder with no regard whatsoever for the sanctity of an accountant's workspace.

"There's a debt outstanding in years," Lior said. "The Hollow envoy who stayed with us in the spring — Lady Irun's clerk — borrowed nine days from the guild's time-charter and has not returned them. Interest accrues."

"You can't collect interest on days, Lior."

"You can when the days are *guild property*. They're stored in the cellar next to the bottled echoes. Nine days, sitting in a jar, gathering dust." He tapped the entry. "And they're getting *stale*, Odell. Days go stale. By winter they'll be good for nothing but composting."

Odell laughed, the big rolling laugh that had raised half the guild's apprentices and terrified all of them equally. "You're the only man alive who can make arithmetic sound like a grievance."

"I contain multitudes. Most of them are grievances."

She squeezed his shoulder on her way to the kitchen — a gesture so familiar he barely registered it anymore, the way you stop noticing your own heartbeat — and began the clattering process of turning turnips into dinner. Lior settled back into the accounts.

This was the shape of his life, and he knew its shape the way he knew the lines on his own palm: morning tea, the ledger, Sefa's hammering, Bram arriving late with some excuse involving a locked door and a moral principle, dinner argued over rather than eaten quietly, sleep. He was thirty-one years old. He had been left on the guild hall's doorstep at some unknowable hour in some unknowable year, wrapped in a blanket embroidered with a pattern no one could identify, and the Guild of Unmapped Realms — whose entire purpose was to chart the places maps refused to go — had taken in the one child who came with no map at all.

It had never troubled him. That was the honest truth of it, the truth he'd have entered in the ledger himself: he did not wonder about where he came from, because the guild was where he came from. Everything else was inventory.

He was halfway through reconciling the candle budget — the guild bought candles from three separate suppliers because Odell distrusted consensus — when the knock came.

Three raps, precise, unhurried. Client hours were technically over, but clients rarely respected technicalities, and the guild's charter said the door stayed open to anyone carrying payment or trouble, preferably both.

Lior opened the door onto a thin man in a gray traveling cloak, hood beaded with rain. Middle-aged, forgettable in the way of people who had practiced being forgettable. He held a leather satchel against his chest.

"Guild of Unmapped Realms?" His voice was flat, careful. "I need a border crossing charted. The Hollow side. Off the record."

"Nothing here is off the record," Lior said pleasantly. "The record is sort of our entire thing. Come in, though. You're dripping on the threshold, and the threshold has feelings."

The man hesitated — a beat too long, Lior thought later, though at the time he filed it away nowhere at all — and stepped inside. He sat where he was pointed, at the far end of the long table, and laid out his request: a route through the borderlands, avoiding the main crossings, timed to a date three weeks hence. Standard work. Odd timing. The Hollow's borders were warded and stable and had been for decades; nobody needed smuggler's charts through a wall that held.

"And payment?" Lior asked, opening the ledger to a fresh line. "We take coin, trade, favors, memories in fair condition, or years, though I'll warn you the years are currently stale."

The man reached into his cloak and set a coin on the table.

It was gold. Old gold, soft-edged with handling, stamped with a seal Lior didn't recognize — a crown, but wrong somehow, drawn as if by someone describing a crown from memory to someone who had never seen one. And beneath the crown, a face in profile.

Lior looked at the face.

The face looked back at him, aged forty years. The same long nose. The same stubborn jaw. A scar through the eyebrow that Lior did not yet have but had always suspected he'd earn eventually, given his line of work. The coin showed a man of seventy-odd, weathered and grave, and it was — there was no other word for it, and Lior was a professional of words — it was *him*.

"That's counterfeit," he said, before his mind caught up with his mouth. "I mean — obviously. It's me. It's me when I'm old. Coins don't do that."

The client said nothing. Rain ticked against the windows. From the kitchen came the smell of turnips and, distantly, Odell humming something off-key.

Lior picked up the coin. It was warm, the way coins get warm, the way hands get warm. He turned it over. The reverse bore a single word in a script he almost recognized — a script shaped like the embroidery on the blanket he'd been found in, the pattern no one could identify, the pattern he'd slept under for eleven years without once asking about, because asking would have meant wondering, and wondering would have meant the map had edges, and Lior's whole life was built on the comfortable conviction that it didn't.

His heart was doing something irregular. He noted this with detached professional interest, the way he'd note a discrepancy in a column.

"Where," he said slowly, "did you get this?"

"It was given to me to give to you," the man said. "Payment for the chart. Nothing more."

"That's not how payment works. Payment comes *from* somewhere. Someone minted this. Someone put my face—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "Someone put my face on a coin and sent it across a warded border to a guild hall in the middle of nowhere, addressed, effectively, to *me*. Who?"

"I'm paid to carry, not to know."

"Then you're paid very little, because knowing is most of the value." Lior set the coin down on the open ledger, on the fresh blank line awaiting the transaction, and reached for his pen. "Fine. We'll do this properly. One border chart, Hollow side, off the main crossings. Paid in gold, one piece, provenance... pending."

He wrote: *Received, one gold piece.*

The ledger refused it.

He felt it happen more than saw it — a small resistance in the nib, the ink drying pale and ghostly on the page, and then fading entirely, the way breath fades from a window. He wrote it again. Again the words sank away like stones into deep water. The black book lay open and silent beneath his hand, and for the first time in eleven years of keeping its accounts, it declined to tell him what was true.

Because the truth, apparently, was not recordable.

Lior sat back. Across the table, the gray man watched him with an expression that was not smug and not sorry — just watchful, the way you watch a lock you've already picked.

"You see," the man said softly. "You understand what the book is telling you."

"I understand the book is having a tantrum."

"The book doesn't have tantrums. I'm told it has convictions." The man rose, drew his hood up. "Keep the coin. Consider it a retainer. There will be others coming, guildsman, and soon, and they will not be as polite as I am. When the moon rises full over the border — three nights from now — you may want to be awake."

"That's a dramatic thing to say to your accountant."

"I've found accountants make the best audience for warnings. You're the only ones who check the numbers." At the door he paused, half-turned, rain-light silvering his shoulders. "For what it's worth, the face suits you. Older. It looks like a face that chose something."

Then he was gone into the rain, and the door closed, and the guild hall went on being warm and smelling of turnips as though the world had not just developed a crack straight down the middle of it.

Lior looked at the ledger. Blank line. He looked at the coin. His own face, patient and aged, waiting for him to catch up.

"Lior?" Odell called from the kitchen. "Dinner! And tell Bram if he's late again I'm eating his portion and his portion of dessert!"

"In a moment," he called back, and his voice came out steady, which he counted as a small professional triumph.

He wrapped the coin in a scrap of cloth and put it in his pocket, where it lay against his hip like a second heartbeat. Then he closed the ledger — gently, the way you close a door on a sleeping thing — and sat alone in the lamplight, listening to the rain, doing the one thing he had spent thirty-one years not doing.

Wondering.

Outside, above the wet dark hills, the first stars were coming out. Three nights, the man had said. Lior counted them without meaning to, the way he counted everything, and found that the sky suddenly seemed less like a certainty and more like an account that hadn't been balanced in a very long time.

Three nights. He hoped, in the vague and rising unease of a man whose books have just lied to him, that whatever was coming would at least have the decency to arrive during business hours.

* * *

It did not.
