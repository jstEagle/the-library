## 2. Night Without Stars

The coin with Lior's face on it sat in the ledger's lockbox all day, refusing to be filed, and by evening Lior had decided the sensible thing was to stop thinking about it entirely. He was very good at not thinking about things. It was practically a guild skill. The Guild of Unmapped Realms dealt in what other people couldn't account for — bottled echoes, borrowed shadows, debts owed in years — and its bookkeeper had long ago learned that some entries were best left as smudges.

So he balanced accounts instead. He counted the echo inventory twice (forty-one bottles, one of which had started humming a lullaby in a language no one at the guild spoke, which Odell said was fine as long as it stayed in key). He noted that Bram still owed the guild eleven years of his life, per the contract he'd signed drunk at nineteen, and that interest had compounded to the point where Bram would technically die owing himself money. He wrote *paid in full* beside the widow Ansette's account and felt the small warm satisfaction that was the closest thing to wealth Lior Oyelaran had ever wanted.

Outside, the moon rose over Vess like a struck bell.

He noticed it first as a wrongness in the light. The guild hall's windows faced east, toward the warded border hills, and the moonlight coming through them had gone thin and gray, the color of old dishwater. Then the shadows in the room began to lean — all of them, at once, toward the border, like iron filings finding a magnet.

Lior set down his pen.

The pen rolled off the desk. Not fell — *rolled*, uphill, against the tilt of the world, and stopped pointing east.

"Oh," said Lior, to the empty accounting room. "Oh, that's not good."

The wards went at moonrise exactly. He didn't hear it so much as feel it, a sensation like a great held breath finally released after a hundred years — and every star above Vess went out.

Not dimmed. *Out.* All of them, in the same instant, as if someone had snuffed the sky between one heartbeat and the next. Lior stood in the dark of his own office, heart slamming, and heard from below him the sound of the guild waking up: Odell's voice booming orders, Sefa's boots on the stairs three at a time, the cook Marta swearing in four languages with impressive fluency.

He grabbed the ledger out of habit, which would later strike him as either very funny or very sad, and ran down into the dark.

* * *

Sefa had been asleep on the ward-room cot when her sigils screamed.

That was the only word for it. She'd strung monitoring lines through the whole guild hall — cheap copper wire and borrowed starlight, the ward-smith's equivalent of leaving a kettle on — and when the border wards failed, every line in the building sang at once, a high thin note that went through her teeth and out the back of her skull. She came awake already moving, hands finding her kit in the dark the way other people found their slippers.

By the time she reached the roof, she knew what she'd see, and seeing it anyway was worse.

The sky was empty.

Not cloudy. Clouds had edges, texture, the promise of something behind them. This was nothing, top to bottom, horizon to horizon — a ceiling of black felt stretched over the world. The moon hung in it like a hole cut in cloth, giving light but no comfort, and where the constellations should have been there was only absence, vast and total and *wrong*. Ward-smiths learned the sky the way farmers learned weather. Sefa had navigated by those stars since she was six years old. They were gone like they'd never been.

"Tell me you're seeing this," she said, when Odell heaved up onto the roof behind her, breathing hard.

"I'm seeing it," the guildmaster said. "I hate it."

"The wards didn't break." Sefa was already kneeling at the roof's edge where her boundary-sigils were etched into the stone, and what she found there made her stomach drop. "Odell. Look."

The sigils weren't scorched or shattered. They were *open* — each one unsealed cleanly, deliberately, the way a door unlocks rather than the way a wall comes down. She pressed her thumb to the nearest glyph and felt the residue: old magic, royal-old, blood-cut, patient.

"Somebody unlocked them," she said quietly. "With a key."

"A key to the border of the world."

"A key to the *covenant*." She looked up at the starless dark. "And whatever the covenant was holding up, we're standing under it now."

Below them, the town bells of Vess began to ring — not alarm-bells, just all of them at once, every tower, every temple, the whole city ringing out of pure animal panic. And beyond the eastern hills, where the border wards had stood for a century, the night lit up with a soft, sourceless glow, like dawn arriving from the wrong direction.

Something was coming through.

* * *

They came all night.

Lior worked the front doors of the guild hall, because someone had to, and because Odell had looked at him across the chaos and said, "You count things. Count them in," which was the most Odell way of saying *be brave* that existed.

First came the traders — Hollow-born, mostly, faces Lior knew from ledger entries, their carts piled with goods that had been mid-transaction when the world's hinges gave way. A moss-wife named Hurna arrived carrying her entire stall on her back and her two grandchildren under her arms. A shadow-broker Lior had done business with for a decade walked in without his shadow, which trailed him through the door a full minute later, looking embarrassed.

Then came the creatures. That was the part no ledger could hold. Things that had lived their whole lives on the border-marches, in the overlap places where the wards blurred the rules — a fox with too many tails, all of them trembling; a river that had gotten up and walked, pooling patiently in the guild's cellar while Marta yelled about the damp; a family of hearth-spirits who took one look at the guild's fire and moved in, uninvited, warming every room they passed through. The children among the refugees thought this was wonderful. The adults let themselves think so too, eventually, because the alternative was screaming.

"Entry forty-four," Lior murmured, writing by candlelight in the great hall, surrounded by sleeping bodies. "One fox, nine tails, displaced. No fee. No fee," he repeated firmly, because the fox was watching him with an expression of profound dignity. "Guild of Unmapped Realms does not charge refugees. Someone remind me to put that in the charter."

"You're talking to a fox," said Bram, dropping down beside him with an armful of blankets he had absolutely stolen from somewhere.

"I'm maintaining professional standards."

"You're holding the ledger like it's a teddy bear."

Lior looked down. He was, in fact, holding the ledger against his chest. He put it on the floor with dignity. Bram grinned and handed him a blanket anyway, and for a moment they just sat together, watching the great hall breathe — sixty strangers and a walking river, packed shoulder to shoulder, a hearth-spirit drifting between them leaving little trails of warmth like a friendly ghost.

"They keep thanking us," Bram said, quieter. "People who've got nothing. They keep saying thank you."

"That's what happens when you open the door," Lior said. "Eventually somebody walks through it."

Bram snorted. "Listen to you. One crisis and you're a philosopher." He lay back, arms behind his head, staring up at the rafters because the sky was no longer an option. "Can't sleep under stars I can't see. Feels like the dark is lying to me."

Lior knew what he meant. He'd spent his whole life under those stars without thinking about them, the way you don't think about your own heartbeat, and now that they were gone he felt the absence like a pulled tooth — a gap his tongue kept returning to. Somewhere past the border hills, a whole other realm was sitting under the same black nothing, and both worlds were holding their breath.

Above them, faintly, the lullaby-bottle in the storeroom changed key.

* * *

Sefa didn't sleep. Ward-smiths didn't, during collapses; it was practically doctrine. She spent the night doing triage on the guild's own protections, reinforcing doors, re-keying locks, and trying very hard not to think about the clean, deliberate unlock she'd read in the ruined sigils. Broken wards were weather. Unlocked wards were *intent*, and intent had a name attached, and the name had been redacted so thoroughly that even the guild's oldest records showed only a smear of ink where it used to be.

Near dawn — real dawn, gray and starless and somehow comforting anyway — she found Lior in the kitchen, making tea for what looked like half of Vess's eastern districts. He moved through the crowded room with the easy competence of a man who had spent twenty years keeping accounts of everything except himself, handing cups to hollow-eyed strangers, remembering how Hurna took hers, knowing without asking which of the hearth-spirits liked honey.

Marta had commandeered the stove and was feeding people with the focused fury of a woman who could not fix the sky but could, by gods, fix breakfast. Odell stood in the doorway being large and calm. Bram was teaching a refugee child to pick pockets, using his own, which Sefa chose to interpret as educational.

It should have been a disaster. Sixty strangers, impossible creatures, a guild built for maybe fifteen. Instead the hall had rearranged itself overnight into something warm — blankets shared before introductions, names exchanged over bread, a Hollow trader showing a Vessi farmer's kid how to fold a paper bird that actually flew. Strangers who'd fled the same darkness had become neighbors by morning, the way survivors always did, kinship stamped into them faster than any ceremony could manage.

Sefa stood in the doorway next to Odell and watched Lior pour tea, and felt the dread in her chest shift shape.

Because she'd spent the night reading the residue on those sigils, and the more she read, the more one fact kept surfacing, patient as water: the key that opened the covenant had been cut from royal blood. Blood from the Hollow's lost line. And the records said that line ended — except records lied, redactions happened, infants got smuggled across borders and erased from maps, and guild bookkeepers did not have childhood memories before the age of four, which everyone had always assumed was grief or trauma or simply the ordinary fog of an orphaned boy, but which Sefa now, watching Lior hand a cup to a fox with nine tails, could not stop re-examining.

"Lior," she said.

He looked up, teapot in hand, face tired and open and utterly trusting. "There's tea. Also porridge, if you negotiate with Marta first. She's taken hostages."

"Lior." Her voice came out steadier than she expected. "When the sun's fully up, I need you to come look at something with me. The sigils. There's a name in them."

His brow creased. "Whose name?"

Sefa looked at him — at the man the guild had raised, argued with, fed, forgiven, loved, the man currently pouring tea for a shadow that had arrived late — and said, "I think it might be yours."

The teapot did not fall. Lior Oyelaran, keeper of impossible accounts, set it down very carefully on the counter, the way a man sets down something he suddenly isn't sure he can trust his own hands with.

"Well," he said. "I suppose we'd better balance that entry."

Behind him, unnoticed by anyone, the last bottle on the top shelf of the pantry — the one holding a bottled echo nobody had ever claimed — began, very softly, to sing his name.
