## 3. What the Wards Remember

Sefa had rebuilt a hundred wards in her life and taken apart twice as many, and she could count on one hand the number of times the work had frightened her. The night the stars went out made it a number she needed both hands for.

She worked by lamplight now — real lamplight, oil and wick, because every light-glass in the guild hall had gone dark at moonrise along with everything else that drew its glow from the sky. The great reading room of the Guild of Unmapped Realms had been converted overnight into something between a triage station and a barn. Hollow-folk slept in nests of rolled carpets. A marsh-hag from the border fens snored gently in the corner where the atlas cabinets used to be, her lantern-jaw glowing a faint apologetic green. Someone had hung a bedsheet over the map of Vess so the refugees wouldn't have to look at the blank space where their world was supposed to be.

Sefa sat at the long table under three lamps, surrounded by the wreckage of the border wards.

That was how she thought of it: wreckage. When a ward broke, it broke like a window. Shattered sigils, splintered anchor-lines, fragments scattered across the residual plane in patterns any ward-smith could read like a crash site. She'd spent six hours collecting those fragments — pressing sheets of treated vellum against the air where the wards had stood, letting the dying sigils print themselves like leaves pressed between pages.

The sheets were spread out before her now, forty of them, and they refused to make sense.

"More tea?" asked Odell, materializing at her elbow the way guildmasters did when they wanted an excuse not to sleep.

"If it's the good stuff."

"It's the stuff Bram didn't manage to steal yet. Same thing." He poured, then stood over her shoulder, which was a habit she'd stopped mending him of years ago. "Well?"

"Well," Sefa said, "I've been doing this thirty years, and I would like to formally state, for the record, that this is impossible."

Odell grunted. "Put that on the ledger. Lior will find a column for it."

She almost smiled. Almost. She tapped the first sheet. "When a ward breaks, the sigils shatter. Fracture lines radiate out from the point of failure. You get this—" she traced a jagged starburst, "—a wound. Every broken ward I've ever read looks like a wound."

"And these don't."

"These look like *doors*." She laid out four more sheets in a row. The sigils on them weren't fractured at all. They were folded back, neat as shutters, each glyph tucked against its neighbor in perfect order. "Look. No fracture lines. No scorching. The lattice isn't damaged anywhere. It's been *opened*. Deliberately. From the inside of the working."

Odell set down his cup very slowly. "Unlocked."

"Unlocked," Sefa said. "Which is worse. A saboteur who breaks a ward is a vandal. A saboteur who can open a border-ward like a garden gate — that's someone with the key. And there's only ever been one kind of key that works on a covenant ward."

"Royal blood."

"Royal blood," she agreed. "Cut fresh, pressed to the anchor-stone, spoken over with the true name of the bloodline. That's the old rite. Nobody's performed it in living memory because nobody's supposed to *have* it. The whole point of the covenant was that no single crown could walk through the border whenever it liked."

Odell rubbed his face. "So either some royal survived that we never knew about, or someone's been keeping royal blood in a jar for fifty years."

"Or," said Sefa, and here her hands went still on the table, "someone inherited it without knowing what they were carrying."

The lamps hissed. Out in the hall, the marsh-hag rolled over and muttered something in a dialect of frog.

"I need the archive," Sefa said. "The deep archive. If a key was cut, there'll be a record somewhere — the wards log every touch of royal blood, even when they're being polite about it."

"The deep archive's been sealed since the Harrow years."

"I know. I'm asking anyway."

Odell looked at her for a long moment. Thirty years he'd known her, and he still hadn't learned to argue with her when she got quiet, which was the only time she got quiet. "Take Lior," he said finally. "He knows the filing system better than the clerks do."

* * *

The deep archive lived under the guild hall, behind a door that Odell unlocked with three keys and a fourth key that wasn't a key but a very patient lie told to the lock. Lior came down the stairs with a lamp in one hand and a biscuit in the other, because Lior faced all catastrophes with a biscuit; Sefa had long ago decided it was less a coping mechanism than a philosophy.

"You look terrible," he said cheerfully.

"You look like a man eating a biscuit in a cellar at dawn while the stars are missing."

"That too." He set the lamp on the archive table and looked around at the shelves — rows and rows of ledgers going back two centuries, the guild's memory in ink. "Where do we start?"

"Covenant-era records. Anything touching the border rites." Sefa pulled the first ledger toward her and opened it, and frowned, and opened it wider, as though the problem might be a matter of lighting.

It was not a matter of lighting.

"Lior."

"Hm?"

"What does this page say?"

He leaned over. The page said nothing. It was not blank — blank pages happened, clerks were human — but this was worse than blank. There was a shape to the emptiness, the ghost of margins and line-rules, and where words should have been there was a smooth grey absence, like frost on the inside of a window. She turned the page. Frost again. Turned back. Frost.

"Redacted," Lior said slowly. "But not with ink. Ink you can scrape off. This is..." He touched the page and pulled his fingers back as if it were warm. "This is a ward. A little one. Somebody warded the *words*."

They worked down the shelf in silence after that, and the silence got heavier with every book. Ledger after ledger, the same grey frost wherever the subject brushed against whatever it was — always the same span of years, always the same corner of the record. Births registered at the border stations: frosted. Trade manifests from the Hollow crossing: frosted. One entire volume of the guild's correspondence, its spine labeled *COVENANT AFFAIRS, REGENCY PERIOD*, frosted so thoroughly it might as well have been a brick.

"This isn't hiding a crime," Sefa said at last. Her voice came out strange. "Crimes are messy. This is tidy. This is somebody sitting down with a decade of paperwork and cleaning one person out of it. Carefully. Completely. Like erasing a stain."

"Or erasing a person," Lior said quietly.

She looked up at him. In the lamplight he seemed suddenly younger and older at once, the way he sometimes did when he forgot to perform being ordinary. He was staring at the frosted pages with an expression she couldn't name — recognition without content, homesickness for a place he'd never been.

"There's a story we tell apprentices," she said, mostly to keep her hands moving. "About why the guild exists. About a child carried across the border the night the last true king of the Hollow died. Smuggled out under the wards' noses, because the wards would part for royal blood and couldn't help themselves. Hidden somewhere the regents could never reach." She laughed, and it came out wrong. "We tell it as a ghost story. 'Mind the borders, or the lost prince will trade your shadow.' Nonsense to frighten children into doing their homework."

Lior had gone very still.

"Sefa," he said. "My earliest memory. I've told you about it. The gate. The cold on one side and warm on the other, and someone singing — I've always assumed it was my mother, except I don't remember a mother, I remember *hands*, and then Odell, and then nothing until the guild hall kitchen. I've mapped half of Vess. I can draw the border passes from memory. But ask me anything from before I was five and there's just—" he spread his hands, "—fog. I always thought everyone's beginning was foggy."

"Foggy isn't frosted," Sefa whispered.

Because that was the thing, the thing that had been crawling up her spine since the first ledger, the thing she had been refusing to look at directly the way you refuse to look at the sun. The redactions weren't random. They weren't scattered. They clustered — around certain dates, certain crossings, certain *years* — and if you knew the guild's history the way she did, if you had spent thirty years among its paper ghosts, the cluster had a shape.

The shape of a child's childhood.

Every record that touched the years between infancy and boyhood: frosted. Every mention of the foundling Odell had brought home one winter, no papers, no name anyone would write down: frosted. Not Lior's records specifically — the guild kept almost none, another oddity she'd never questioned — but the *world's* records of him, scrubbed clean by a ward so old and patient it had probably been woven into the vellum itself.

Someone had erased a crown prince from every map in existence.

And the wards hadn't been broken last night. They'd been unlocked. With a key cut from royal blood.

"Oh," Sefa said. It was a small word for the size of the thing falling on her.

Lior was watching her face. "Say it," he said. "Whatever it is. You know I hate it when you do the thing where you go quiet."

She loved him. That was the whole of it, the simple unbearable fact underneath everything else — not romantically, or not only, but the fierce practical love of a woman who had taught a skinny orphan boy to solder sigil-wire without burning his fingers, who had watched him grow into the person who brought her tea when she worked late and argued with her about cartography for the pleasure of it. Family, the kind you chose and got chosen by.

And every instinct she had as a ward-smith was telling her that the boy she loved was the key.

"The wards remember everything," she said. "That's the first law. Even when people forget, the wards remember. So we stop reading the ledgers." She stood, knocking her stool back, already reaching for her kit. "We go to the border itself. The anchor-stones are still standing. If a royal key was cut — whosever blood it was — the stones will have kept the impression. Blood remembers too."

Lior gathered the lamp. At the foot of the stairs he paused.

"Sefa. If it's me — if I'm the reason the stars went out—"

"Then we fix it," she said. "That's what we do. We fix things. It's the family business."

He smiled, and went up into the starless morning, and Sefa followed him with dread sitting in her chest like a swallowed stone — because she had seen the frost on those pages, and she had counted the dates, and she was almost certain now, terribly certain, that the answer to everything was sleeping one floor above her, dreaming of a gate and a song, wearing a face stamped on a coin he'd never minted.
