## 4. The Cartographer's Blind Spot

Lior Oyelaran had drawn the Hollow eleven times in his life, and every one of those maps had been a lie.

Not a malicious lie — a warded one. The Guild of Unmapped Realms kept its charts of the neighboring world behind three layers of sigil-work, and what came through was less geography than gossip: the Ashvale Mountains softened into rumor, the Antler Court rendered as a smudge with a caption reading *here be regents*. Cartography by candlelight through smoked glass. He had always assumed that was simply how the Hollow was — distant, dim, politely out of focus.

Now he unrolled fresh vellum in the guild hall's map room, dipped his pen, and discovered the truth: the Hollow had never been blurry. It had been *blindfolded*.

Without the wards, the land came through his instruments like water through a burst dam. His divining compass spun, settled, spun again, and began to write. Rivers he had only ever sketched from secondhand accounts resolved into braided silver threads. The Ashvales stood up off the page with their true teeth. Even the paper seemed to drink the ink faster, eager, as though the Hollow had been waiting decades for someone to look at it properly.

"Careful," Sefa said from the doorway, two mugs of chicory in her hands. "You're smiling at it. Maps can tell when you're sentimental."

"I'm not sentimental. I'm professionally delighted." He took the mug anyway. "Sefa, look at this. The Vess-side surveys always showed the Thornmere crossing as a single ford. There are six. Six crossings, all seasonally shifting. Whoever drew the old charts either never went there or—"

"Or wasn't allowed to." She set her mug down and studied the page over his shoulder, ward-smith's eyes narrowing at the edges of the ink. "The wards didn't blur the Hollow, Lior. They blurred *you*. Everything routed through the border keys. If your sight was filtered, so was everything you made with it."

He didn't answer right away. Because she was right, and because being right about the wards meant being right about something else, something he'd been circling since the night the stars went out and the coin with his own aged face had refused to enter the ledger.

He turned to the shelf where his earliest work lived — the apprentice maps, the ones Odell kept out of sentiment and refused to let anyone rebind. He pulled the first one down. A survey of the borderlands, done when he was nineteen, ink still brown with age.

There was a blank space in it.

Not a smudge. Not faded ink. A clean, deliberate absence, roughly the size of a valley, sitting exactly where his field notes said he'd spent three weeks sketching a village called Emberhold. He remembered the village. He remembered the innkeeper's terrible stew and the ferryman who sang off-key. He did not remember drawing this map, or at least — he reached for the memory and found his fingers closing on nothing, the way a tongue finds the socket of a missing tooth.

"Sefa," he said quietly. "Did I ever finish my Emberhold survey?"

She came closer. Looked. Went pale in the particular way she did when a ward failed inspection — not frightened, exactly. *Offended.*

"That's not blank paper," she said. "That's a redaction. Look at the edge — there's a seam. Someone cut the memory out of the chart the way you'd cut a figure out of a photograph." She touched it and hissed, snatching her hand back. "It bit me. That's guild-grade erasure work. That's *our* work."

"Ours?"

"The Guild of Unmapped Realms doesn't just map things, Lior. Sometimes we *unmap* them. It's supposed to require a vote of the full council and a written justification." Her voice dropped. "I've never seen an erasure this clean outside the restricted archive."

He pulled down the next map. Another gap. And the next. By the fourth he had stopped counting and started measuring, laying the blanks side by side across the table until they formed a shape — a ragged, negative silhouette of years. His childhood, cut away in clean rectangles.

The map room felt suddenly very large and very cold.

"I have a hole in me," he said. It sounded absurd spoken aloud, so he tried again, more precisely, the way he'd phrase a survey finding. "There is territory here—" he tapped his own sternum "—that I have never charted. And someone used guild methods to make sure I wouldn't."

Sefa put her hand over his on the table. Hers was warm and calloused and smelled faintly of copper and chicory. "Then we chart it," she said. "That's what we do. That's what you do better than anyone I've ever met. You find the blank spaces and you fill them in honestly." A pause. "Even when the honest filling-in is horrible."

He laughed — a small, cracked sound, but real. "You're very bad at comforting people."

"I'm excellent at comforting people. I'm just honest first. It's a sequencing problem."

* * *

Lady Irun arrived at dusk, which Lior would later decide was a deliberate choice, because dusk was when the guild hall looked least like a fortress and most like a home — lamplight in the windows, Bram's terrible lute-scratching drifting from the kitchen, Cook shouting something about onions that could be heard in three counties.

The envoy came alone up the lane, which somehow made her more alarming than an army would have. She was tall and grey-robed, with antler-shed buttons on her cloak — real ones, Hollow-made — and she walked past the guild's outer wards without triggering so much as a hiccup, which made Sefa swear creatively from the watchtower.

Odell met her in the courtyard, guildmaster's chain gleaming, flanked by Bram (leaning on a post, projecting insouciance badly) and Cook (holding a cleaver, projecting it excellently).

"Lady Irun of the Antler Court," Odell said. "We don't recall inviting you."

"No," the envoy agreed. Her voice was dry and pleasant, like autumn leaves. "But I invited myself decades ago, in a manner of speaking. I've come to see how he's grown."

Every head turned — following her gaze — to Lior, standing in the map-room doorway with ink on his fingers and a blank-spotted map still under his arm.

"You know," Lady Irun said, and smiled, and it was a kind smile, which was worse somehow, "when they pulled you across the border, you were small enough to fit in a bread basket. You cried the whole way. Your mother cast silence over the basket twice and you cried straight through it. Very stubborn even then."

"I think," Lior said carefully, "you've mistaken me for someone else."

"Have I?" She tilted her head. "Lioran."

The name went through him like a needle finding a nerve he hadn't known he had. Not unfamiliar — *recognized*. Somewhere under the erasure, something sat up.

"Crown-Undrawn," she continued, gently, mercilessly. "Last light of the Antler line. The covenant-king the Hollow has been fading without. Do you want to know the worst part, Lioran? You've been mapping our world your whole life. Every chart you ever drew was a letter home you couldn't read."

Bram moved first — quick, quiet, knife already loose in his sleeve — and Odell's hand came up and stopped him without either of them looking at each other. Twenty years of guild shorthand in one gesture: *not yet. Watch.*

"He's not going anywhere," Odell said, in the voice she used for negotiating tolls and threatening tax collectors. "And neither, I notice, are you. So why don't you come inside, my lady, have some soup that Cook will insist you finish, and explain to all of us — slowly — what exactly you mean by *Crown-Undrawn*."

"It means the crown was never placed on his head," Lady Irun said, stepping over the threshold as if she'd done it a hundred times before, which, Lior thought with a chill, perhaps she had. "It was drawn *out* of him. His name, his claim, his years — unpicked stitch by stitch and sealed into the covenant. That's why the stars died when the wards fell. The covenant isn't failing, guildmaster. It's *calling in its collateral*."

Cook muttered something about collateral and soup not mixing.

Lior stood very still. The vertigo was not dramatic; that was the strange part. It was small and domestic and total — the feeling of reaching for the stair in the dark and finding one step missing. All his life he had been a man with a complete inventory: orphan, cartographer, guildsman, keeper of impossible ledgers. Now one line item had been added, aged forty years, stamped with his own face, and the whole ledger refused to balance.

"I need air," he heard himself say, and walked out into the courtyard, and no one stopped him, because the guild knew — the way family knows — when to follow and when to let you stand under the starless sky alone for a minute.

Sefa gave him thirty seconds. Then she was beside him, arms crossed against the cold, saying nothing, which was its own kind of fluency.

"There's a blank space where my childhood should be," he said. "On the maps and in me. Same shape. Same hand."

"Do you believe her?"

"I believe the maps." He looked up at the dark where the stars should be. "Sefa, if my name is stitched into that covenant — if the sky went out because *I* was taken — then everyone in the Hollow who's fading, every child born shadowless... that's the interest accruing on a debt I didn't know I owed."

"Or that someone else took out in your name," Sefa said. "Which, speaking as the guild's accountant-adjacent friend, is fraud. We know fraud. We commit fraud professionally, several times a week, for clients with excellent reasons." She bumped his shoulder with hers. "Fraud gets reversed, Lior. Debts get renegotiated. That's literally our whole business."

From the kitchen window, Bram's voice floated out, lazily sardonic: "If the bread-basket prince is done having his existential crisis, Cook says the envoy eats like a heron and Odell wants witnesses at this conversation before she talks us all into anything."

Lady Irun was waiting by the fire when they came back in, hands folded, patient as winter. As Lior sat, she studied him with an expression he couldn't place — until he realized he *could*, because he'd seen it in mirrors on bad days. It was guilt wearing patience as a coat.

"One more thing, Crown-Undrawn," she said softly. "Before your family decides what to do with me. You should know that unlocking the wards required royal blood — and that I am not the only one left who carries it. The regent who opened your borders has been very busy while your stars were dying. And she is coming here next, for the only other key that fits the lock."

The fire popped. Somewhere upstairs, a shutter banged in the windless night.

"She wants the crown," Lady Irun said. "And you, Lioran, are the last thing standing between her and both worlds."
