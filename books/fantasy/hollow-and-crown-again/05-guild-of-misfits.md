## 5. Guild of Misfits

Odell Marsh had been guildmaster of the Guild of Unmapped Realms for nineteen years, and in that time he had presided over exactly four formal votes: whether to admit a talking door (yes, provisionally), whether to sell bottled echoes to the Duke of Vessmark (no, on ethical grounds, and also because the Duke was a cheat), whether to raise dues by two coppers (yes, and he'd been finding dead rats in his boots ever since), and whether to let Sefa keep a salamander in the forge (yes, against all reason; the salamander now had seniority).

Tonight's vote would be the fifth. It would be the one that mattered.

He stood at the head of the long table in the guild hall's common room while the last light died outside — real light, candlelight, lamplight, because there were no stars left to argue with. The shutters were closed against the cold that had come down with the dark, but Odell could feel it pressing at the windows like a creditor.

Around the table sat his people. His impossible, unprofitable, beloved people.

Sefa, ward-smith, arms crossed over her scorched apron, jaw set like she was already welding something. Bram, thief, currently picking the lock on the biscuit tin out of pure habit. Marisol, cook, who had laid out supper before the vote because — as she put it — no one decides a man's fate hungry. Quill, scribe, youngest of them, ink-stained to the elbow, with the ledger open before her and her pen hovering over a page that refused, apparently, to be written.

And Lior. Lior Oyelaran, their ledger-keeper, their mapmaker, the man who knew where every copper in the guild's coffers had ever wandered. He sat at the far end with his hands flat on the table, wearing the face of a man trying very hard to look calm about being the subject of the meeting.

The envoy from the Hollow had left an hour ago. Lady Irun, silver-eyed and patient as frost, had made her claim in eleven words: *The Hollow asks for its prince. The Hollow will wait until moonrise.* Then she had stepped out into the starless street and simply — wasn't there anymore, which Odell felt was showing off.

"Right," Odell said. "You all know why we're here. The Hollow wants Lior. They say he's their missing heir, crown-undrawn, whatever that means. They say the stars come back if we hand him over." He rubbed his beard. "I'll say plainly what I've said to each of you already: I don't intend to give up any member of this guild. But it's not my guild alone, and it's not my decision alone. So we vote. And before we vote—" he looked around the table "—I want each of you to say your piece. Not about what he is. About who he is."

Silence, except for the click of Bram's lockpick finding the biscuit tin's tumblers.

"Bram," Odell said. "Put the biscuits down and testify."

"I'm stress-picking," Bram said, but he set the tin down, unopened, which for Bram was roughly equivalent to fasting. He leaned back in his chair. "Fine. Here's mine."

He turned toward Lior.

"Six years ago I got caught lifting a merchant's strongbox in Vessmark. Not by the watch — by *you*. You'd tracked the strongbox through three ledgers before I'd even fenced the goods. You didn't turn me in. You hired me. You said, and I quote, 'A man who can find anything can be taught to find the right things.'" Bram shrugged, and his voice went quieter than Odell had ever heard it. "Nobody had ever assumed I'd want to find the right things. Everyone else just assumed I wouldn't."

He spread his hands.

"So when they ask who Lior Oyelaran is — he's the first person who looked at a thief and saw a tracker. I don't care whose blood is in him. Blood's just something you bleed. Family's something you choose, and I chose him when I stopped running."

He sat down. Marisol reached over and squeezed his shoulder hard enough to make him wince.

"Sefa," Odell said.

The ward-smith stood, because Sefa did nothing sitting down. She planted both fists on the table.

"I rebuilt those wards," she said. "Every sigil, every anchor-stone, after they fell. And I'll tell you what I found, because it matters tonight. Those wards weren't broken. They were *unlocked* — with royal blood, cut key-fine. Someone used Lior's blood to open the border, and every soul that poured through that breach since has been standing in a hole somebody dug with *him*." Her voice cracked, and she hated it, Odell could see her hating it. "So the Hollow says he belongs to them? Fine. Then they owe him. You don't get to erase a child from every map in two worlds and then send an invoice forty years later."

She pointed at Lior.

"You re-inked my master chart when I broke my wrist. You stayed up three nights doing it and never told me. I found your handwriting in my own margins and thought I was going mad." She sat down hard. "That's who he is. That's my testimony. Next."

"Quill," said Odell gently.

The scribe looked up from the ledger. She was twenty-three and had been found on the guild doorstep at six, and she still sat the way she had then — small, ready to apologize for the space she took up. But her voice, when it came, was steady.

"The ledger won't record the coin," she said. "The one with his face on it. I've tried nine times. The ink dries and lifts off the page like it's ashamed." She turned the book around so they could all see the blank line where an entry should be. "And I think I know why. A ledger records debts. And Lior isn't a debt. He's—" she frowned, hunting the word, "—he's the *margin note*. He's the thing written beside everything else that makes the rest make sense. Who fixed the arithmetic when the echo-trades came back wrong? Who caught the year-debt before old Master Corvin sold us thirty years of his life by accident? Who taught me that a number is just a name for something you're responsible for?"

She closed the ledger and put both hands on its cover, like a shield.

"He's not the Hollow's entry," Quill said. "He's ours. I vote we don't surrender our own handwriting."

"Marisol," Odell said.

The cook had been quiet, which was unlike her, and dangerous. She wiped her hands on her apron and did not stand.

"I fed him his first meal," she said. "Forty years ago. Well — not me. My mother. I was a girl at the stove. A woman came through the warded border half-dead, carrying a bundle, and my mother fed them both without asking a single question, because that's the rule of kitchens in troubled times." Her eyes glistened. "The woman left before dawn. Left the boy. Left a note that said *keep him unmapped, keep him unknown, keep him loved.*"

The room went very still.

"My mother kept the note. I keep it still." Marisol drew it from her apron pocket — soft as cloth with age — and laid it on the table between them all. "Three instructions. We managed two out of three for forty years. Tonight the third one comes due." She looked down the table at Lior, and her voice was warm bread and iron. "You have been loved in this house since before you could walk, Lior Oyelaran. Whatever name the Hollow wants to stamp on you — you ate at our table first. First claim beats crown claim. That's kitchen law."

Lior's composure finally cracked. He pressed the heel of his hand against his eyes and said nothing, because there was nothing in either world adequate to say.

Odell cleared his throat. His own eyes were stinging, and he blamed the candle smoke.

"Well," he said gruffly. "Then let's make it official. All in favor of telling the Hollow that the Guild of Unmapped Realms does not surrender family — raise your hand."

Five hands rose. Six, counting Lior's, which rose halfway and then, catching Odell's eye, rose the rest of the way, trembling slightly.

"Carried," Odell said. "Unanimous. Quill, record it."

"With pleasure," Quill said, and wrote, and this time the ink held.

* * *

They sent the answer by lamplight, carried to the border-wreck by Bram, who returned breathless and pale an hour later.

"She took the message," he reported. "Read it twice. Smiled." He shuddered. "Nobody smiles like that with good news."

"What did she do?" Sefa asked.

"She said—" Bram swallowed. "She said, 'Then the guild may keep him. But the covenant keeps its schedule.' And then the dark over the border got *deeper*, Odell. Like a second night under the first one. And she said the regent's army crosses at dawn, and they aren't coming for Lior anymore."

Odell felt the cold at the windows press closer.

"They're coming for everyone who sheltered him," Lior said quietly. He was standing now, looking at the map of the Hollow pinned to the wall — the new one, drawn with his strange new sight, full of places no chart had ever held. His finger rested on a road that ran straight through the dark toward a citadel marked in his own careful hand: *the seat of the crown, undrawn.*

"No," Lior said, turning, and there was something in his face Odell had never seen before — not fear, not duty, but the particular fierce clarity of a man reading a ledger and finding the balance owed. "If they march at dawn, we ride tonight. Before the roads close."

Sefa was already reaching for her forge-coat. Bram was already counting knives. Marisol was, inevitably, packing food.

Odell looked around at his guild — his misfits, his family, his five raised hands — and sighed the sigh of a man whose ledgers had never once balanced and never once mattered less.

"Get the mules," he said.
