## 6. The Road Through the Hollow

Bram had stolen a great many things in his life: candlesticks, reputations, one memorable afternoon belonging to the Duke of Vess's tax collector. But he had never before stolen across a border into a world without stars, and he found the experience did not agree with him.

"Cheer up," Sefa said, walking beside him on the pale road. "You're doing it for free. That's practically charity."

"That's exactly what worries me," Bram said. "Charity is theft with the guilt left in."

Ahead of them, Lior walked like a man following a scent only he could smell. Which, Bram supposed, was roughly accurate. Since the wards had fallen, Lior's eyes had gone strange — not glowing, nothing so useful as that, but *attentive*, the way a cat watches an empty corner. He kept stopping at places where there was plainly nothing and touching two fingers to the air, as if taking its pulse.

"There," Lior said now, turning off the road toward a field of grey grass. "Something's wrong here."

"Everything's wrong here," Bram said. "We are in a country where the sky has been unplugged."

But Lior knelt at the field's edge and pressed his palm flat against the ground, and Bram felt it — a shudder under his boots, faint as a heartbeat heard through a wall. The grass nearest Lior's hand brightened by one miserable shade, from grey to almost-green, then dimmed again when he lifted away.

"It knows me," Lior said quietly.

"Course it does," Sefa said. "You're the covenant-king. The whole realm is your houseplant."

"I'm nobody's houseplant."

"You're everybody's houseplant. That's rather the job description." She crouched beside him, ward-smith's kit already open, her fingers moving over the soil the way they moved over sigils — reading, not touching. "The land's been drawing on reserves. Whatever sealed this place together ran out of coin when the stars went dark, and now everything's spending itself to keep standing. Grass first. Then trees. Then—" She didn't finish. She didn't have to. They'd all seen the refugees pouring through the broken border, carrying children who cast no shadows.

Lior stood, wiped his hands on his coat, and looked down the long road ahead, where the horizon was less a line than a suggestion. "Then we walk faster."

* * *

The village of Emberlow — though it had no embers left, and Bram privately renamed it Dampmatch — sat in a shallow valley where every window glowed with the same weak amber light. Not firelight. Not lamplight. Something older and more desperate, the light of a place burning its own memory for fuel.

The villagers met them at the well in the square, which was the natural meeting place of all villages in all worlds, Bram had learned, because wells were where people went when they wanted to look busy while gossiping.

They were Hollow-folk, and Bram had expected horns, or antlers, or eyes like coins. What he got instead was a woman named Maren with flour on her apron and a toddler on her hip, who took one look at Lior and burst into tears.

"Oh — no — madam, please, I haven't done anything," Lior said, in the voice of a man who had spent thirty years balancing ledgers precisely so that no one would ever cry at him.

"You've come *back*," Maren managed. "That's what my gran used to say would happen. When the light goes, the light comes home." She looked at Lior's face the way people look at weather they'd stopped believing in. "You have his eyes. The old king's. Everyone said the prince was dead, but Gran said no, you can't kill a name, you can only lose it."

"I'm afraid I don't—"

"He doesn't remember," Sefa said gently, stepping half in front of Lior the way she always did, as if warding him. "It's a long story and most of it's still being written. Tell us about the village. Tell us about the children."

Maren's face changed. The hope stayed, but something else arrived underneath it, the way rot arrives under paint.

"The little ones born since the dark," she said. "They come fine. Ten fingers, ten toes, good lungs, all of it. But they don't cast shadows. And they don't dream — or if they do, they don't wake up knowing they did. My sister's boy, Tam. Sweetest thing you'll ever meet. But you hold him and it's like holding a cup with the bottom gone. Everything pours through."

She led them to her sister's house, and there was Tam, sitting in the doorway, playing with a wooden bird. He was a perfectly ordinary child, round-cheeked and serious, and the sunless sky above gave nothing for a shadow anyway, so Bram might never have noticed — except that Lior noticed. Lior went very still, the way he did before writing a number he didn't like.

Tam looked up at Lior. And smiled. And for one moment — Bram would swear to this later, over drinks, defending it against all reasonable objection — the child *did* cast a shadow. A small dark shape on the doorstep, shaped like a boy wearing a crown.

Then it was gone, and Tam went back to his bird, and Lior sat down hard on the step next to him.

"Hello, Tam," Lior said. His voice came out strange.

Tam held up the wooden bird. "Wing's broke," he said. "Fix?"

And Lior — Crown-Undrawn, heir to a throne built on his erasure, the missing keystone of two worlds — took the wooden bird with trembling hands and said, "I'm better with numbers than wings, but let's see."

He fixed the wing. It took him four tries and a splinter, and Tam watched with the gravity of a foreman, and when it was done the child laughed, and the sound was ordinary, and ordinary, Bram realized, was the whole point. This was what was being erased. Not crowns. *This.* Broken toys and flour-dusted aprons and wells to gossip at.

That night, lodging in Maren's barn, Bram found Lior awake, staring at the ceiling beams.

"Can't sleep?" Bram asked, settling onto the hay beside him with the ease of a man who could sleep anywhere, anytime, including mid-sentence.

"Every village we pass is dimmer than the last," Lior said. "I keep thinking about the ledger. How I used to record debts in years. Someone owed the guild six years once; I never asked how you pay a debt in years. Now I know. It looks like this. A whole realm paying itself out, installment by installment, because someone stole from the account." His jaw tightened. "Because someone stole *me* from the account."

"Ah," Bram said. "Guilt. I know guilt. Guilt and I are old business partners."

"I wasn't even *there*, Bram. I was a baby. I didn't ask to be smuggled anywhere."

"No, but here's the thing about guilt — it's not rational, it's just thorough. Like a tax auditor." Bram plucked a straw and chewed it. "But listen. There's guilt and there's guilt. One kind says, 'look what happened, isn't it terrible,' and lies down. The other kind says, 'look what happened, isn't it terrible,' and gets up. You're getting up. I've watched you get up eleven times in four days. I count things. Occupational habit."

Lior huffed something that was almost a laugh. "What do I even do? I can make grass flicker for a second. I fixed a toy bird."

"You made a shadowless child cast a shadow," Bram said. "Don't undersell that. I've seen grown men fail to cast shadows after a night in Vess's dockside taverns, and none of them were healing anything."

From the loft above, Sefa's voice drifted down, drowsy and dry: "If you two are going to have a heart-to-heart, at least do it quieter. Some of us are conserving our light."

* * *

Three days further in, the road climbed through hills where the fading had gone past dimming into something worse. The trees stood grey as ash but hadn't burned. Rivers ran slow and silver and made no sound. And the villages — the villages were the hardest. Whole houses with doors standing open, tables set, chairs pushed back, as if everyone had simply stepped out to watch the sky and forgotten to come back. Some had gone over the border to Vess. Some, Maren had told them, had "gone quiet," which was a phrase Bram intended never to think about directly.

Lior barely spoke anymore. He walked with his strange attentive eyes open and his hands half-curled, and wherever he passed, the road behind him stayed a shade brighter for a hundred paces before the dark closed over it like water.

It was Sefa who spotted the lights ahead — real lights, many of them, arranged in a pattern too regular for any village.

"City," she said. "Big one. And it's *lit*."

"A city with light while the rest of the realm starves for it," Bram murmured. "In my professional experience, that means one thing: somebody's hoarding."

The road bent around a hill of black pines, and the city opened below them — spires and walls and a citadel crowned with antlered towers, all of it glowing with a hoarded, greedy gold. Banners hung from the gates, and even at this distance Bram could read the device on them: a crown, upside down.

Above the citadel, impossibly, immodestly, a single star burned in the starless sky. Just one. Directly over the tallest tower. As if someone had plucked it out of heaven and nailed it there for a signet.

Lior stared at it, and Bram watched the last of the guilt burn off him like morning fog — not because the guilt was wrong, but because it had finally found somewhere to go.

"That's my star," Lior said. Not a question. A reading from a ledger. "Someone is spending my name, and they've lit themselves a lamp with it."

"Well then," Bram said, cracking his knuckles with the relish of a man about to commit charity, "shall we go introduce ourselves?"

Sefa shouldered her kit. "Quietly, preferably."

"Quiety is just loudly with better planning," Bram said, and started down the hill toward the Court of Ash and Antlers, where — though none of them knew it yet — someone was already waiting who had known Lior's face since before he was born, and had plans for it older than his grief.
