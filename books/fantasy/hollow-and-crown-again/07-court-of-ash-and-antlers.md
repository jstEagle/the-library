## 7. Court of Ash and Antlers

The court of the Hollow smelled of cold hearths and old incense, and it was held in a hall that had clearly once been magnificent and had lately given up pretending.

Lior walked its length on a floor of black glass that showed his reflection from below — a strange sensation, being followed by himself. Antlers branched from every pillar, shed and mounted and dusted with ash, some hung with ribbons whose colors had faded to the memory of colors. The courtiers who lined the walls were thin and tall and gray as winter moths, and they watched him pass with eyes like banked coals: not dead, but nearly.

"They're staring," Bram murmured at his shoulder.

"You'd stare too if a legend walked in wearing guild boots."

"I *am* staring. Mostly at your boots. Those laces are a crime."

Lior almost laughed, which he suspected was the point. Bram had been making jokes since the border, steady as a metronome, the way some people hum when the dark gets close. Sefa walked on Lior's other side, her ward-smith's hands twitching toward every sigil they passed, cataloguing, assessing, preparing for a fight she was too honest to hope they'd avoid.

At the hall's end, beneath an antlered throne that stood conspicuously empty, Lady Irun waited.

She looked older than she had in the envoy's tent — or perhaps Lior was simply seeing her more clearly now. Her silver hair was bound in the Hollow's court fashion, coiled like smoke, and her gown was the gray of things kept too long in attics. When she saw him she did something no one in this court had done: she smiled, warm and human and wrong.

"Lioran," she said. "Crown-Undrawn. You came home."

"Don't call me that."

"It is your name."

"It's a name you gave me four days ago." He stopped three paces from her, close enough to see the fine lines around her eyes, the ash caught in the folds of her sleeves. "I've been doing what I do. Balancing accounts. And yours doesn't balance, my lady. It never has. So let's do this properly, the way we would at the guild. You tell me what you actually traded, and I'll tell you what I've worked out, and we'll see who's been lying about the interest."

Something flickered behind her smile — respect, maybe, or its cousin, annoyance.

"Very well," she said. "Ask."

* * *

He laid it out the way Odell had taught him, back when Lior was nine and all elbows: start with what both parties agree on, then walk backward until someone flinches.

"You found me across the border. You raised the alarm that brought the guild to the orphanage at Vess-harbor — the matron swore for years that a 'gray lady' paid her to look after one particular boy. You've appeared at every turning of my life like a bad penny with excellent timing. And now the wards fall, the stars go out, and here you are, ready to escort the lost prince home." He spread his hands. "I used to think you were my fairy godmother. Then I thought you were my rescuer. Now I'm down to two options, and neither of them flatters you."

"And those are?"

"Either you saved me out of love for my mother—" he watched her face carefully— "or you saved me the way a merchant saves stock. Bought low. Kept safe. Waiting for the market to turn."

The hall was very quiet. Somewhere above, ash sifted down through the rafters like patient snow.

Lady Irun did not flinch. That was the worst part. She simply let the smile go out of her face, the way one blows out a candle before bed, and what remained was older and tireder and far more honest.

"Your mother came to me eight months before the wards fell," she said. "She was pregnant, and she had already read the covenant's terms, and she knew what her child would be: a seal. A living lock. The crown of the Hollow passes by name, and the covenant drinks the heir's true name at birth and binds the worlds with it. She wanted to give you a life instead. So we stole you before the naming. We cut your name out of the record, out of the maps, out of every mouth that might have spoken it — and we sent you across the border to grow up as nobody, which is the only freedom this covenant ever allowed anyone."

"That sounds like rescue," Lior said quietly.

"It was. For twenty years, it was." She turned, and her gray gown whispered against the black glass. "But a weapon in storage is still a weapon, Lioran. I told myself I'd never use you. I told myself the covenant would hold forever. And then the regent began asking questions, and I understood that if I didn't control the moment you were revealed, someone else would — someone who wants you not crowned but *used*. So yes. I kept you. I invested in you. I am a creature of the old court, and we do not do anything without expecting return. Call it theft if you like." Her chin lifted. "I call it hope with collateral."

"Theft," said Sefa flatly, from behind him, "is theft."

"My lady," Bram added, "for what it's worth, in my professional opinion, that was theft."

"*Thank you*, Bram."

"You're welcome."

Lior held up a hand. His heart was doing something complicated — grief and fury braided together, because the story was almost kind, almost forgivable, right up until the word *invested*. "One more question. The wards weren't broken. Sefa read the residue. They were unlocked, with royal blood. Whose?"

For the first time, Lady Irun hesitated.

And into that hesitation, from the shadow of the empty throne, another voice spoke — dry as pages turning.

"Mine, of course."

* * *

The woman who stepped from behind the throne wore mourning white, which in a hall full of ash was either irony or armor. She was small, neat, precise, with hands folded like a closed ledger, and Lior knew her instantly, the way you know a typeface or a forged signature. He had spent his life among merchants. He knew a predator in good tailoring.

"Regent Vessane," Lady Irun said, and there was something new in her voice — fear, quickly filed away.

"No need for titles, Irun. Not anymore." The regent smiled at Lior, and it was a bookkeeper's smile, warm as arithmetic. "The boy has been doing sums. Let him finish them. Go on, Crown-Undrawn. You were saying?"

Lior's mouth had gone dry, but Odell's voice was steady in his skull: *finish the sum.* "You unlocked the wards. You needed blood of the line to do it — but the line's blood is *me*, and I was across the border, unfindable. Unless." The pieces clicked, horrible and clean, like a lock accepting its key. "Unless someone kept a sample. A drop of blood from a smuggled infant, preserved for twenty years, just in case. Irun. You kept a vial."

"I kept *insurance*," Lady Irun said sharply. "Not for her—"

"For yourself. For leverage. And it was stolen, or borrowed, or *traded*—" he turned to the regent— "and you used it to unlock the border, so the realms would begin to fail, so the court would panic, so the only solution anyone could imagine would be putting a crown on the stolen child's head. A crown you intend to hold while he sits on the throne as your puppet. Or—" the last piece fell, and it landed like ice— "or you don't need him crowned at all. You need him *named*. The covenant drinks a freely given name. If I speak my true name willingly, the stars come back — and the binding flows through whoever holds the claim. Through you."

The regent applauded. Softly. Two gloved taps.

"Oh, I *like* him," she said to the court. "Twenty years among merchants and he sums faster than my treasurers. Yes, little prince. That is the shape of it. Your mother gave your name away to save you; Irun stole you away to save you *differently*; and I have merely arranged for everyone's generosity to mature at last. Blood inheritance." She spread her neat hands. "It is theft upon theft upon theft, all the way down. The only question is who steals *last*."

"You won't get my name," Lior said. "Freely given means I choose. I don't choose you."

"No," the regent agreed pleasantly. "But names can be *persuaded*. And fortunately, I have found that people give up their names much faster than they give up their friends."

She snapped her fingers.

The doors at the far end of the hall slammed open, and the gray courtiers moved — all at once, all together, moth-winged and silent — and Lior had one heartbeat to understand that this had never been an audience, it had been a trap with very good manners.

"Sefa!" he shouted. "The pillars — the sigils are live, she's warded the whole—"

Sefa was already moving, tearing a strip of chalk from her sleeve, but there were thirty of them and three of the guild, and the black glass floor lit up under their feet with a lattice of pale fire. Lior felt it take hold of him like cold water closing over his head — a binding circle, old and hungry, keyed to royal blood. *His* blood. Of course. The whole hall was a snare, and he had walked to the center of it and asked polite questions.

Bram moved.

Later, Lior would try to reconstruct exactly how the thief did it — how he went from joking about bootlaces to a blur of motion between Lior and the closing ring of courtiers, knives out, laughing, *actually laughing*, shouting "Go, go, the east colonnade, I counted the exits, I *always* count the exits—" How he bought them six seconds, maybe seven, with nothing but nerve and a pickpocket's perfect knowledge of where people keep their weight.

Six seconds was enough for Sefa to grab Lior's collar and haul him sideways through a gap in the lattice, chalk burning in her fist, both of them stumbling into the colonnade's dark.

It was not enough for Bram.

Lior turned in time to see the courtiers fold over his friend like gray water closing over a stone. He saw Bram go down still grinning, still fighting, and he heard the regent's voice carry sweetly across the hall:

"Do come back, Crown-Undrawn. I have your thief. And I have *so* much time, and you have so few stars."

Then the doors boomed shut, and the corridor swallowed them, and Lior ran — Sefa's hand locked around his wrist, his own breath ragged, betrayal ringing in his ears like a struck bell that would not stop.

They made it three turns through the ash-dark halls before the walls began to glow with fresh-cut sigils, and the corridors ahead filled with guards, and Lior understood, with the terrible calm of a ledger finally balanced, that there was nowhere left to run.

Only, somewhere behind the stone, faint as a heartbeat — a sound.

Three knocks. Pause. Two knocks. Pause. One.

The guild's oldest signal. *Hold on. We're coming.*

Sefa's hand found his in the dark and squeezed it hard, and for the first time since the stars went out, Lior felt something other than falling.
