## 8. A Name Is a Debt

Sefa had broken into three fortresses in her life, and she had a theory about them: every citadel is just a lock that got ambitious.

The Court of Ash and Antlers was no exception. It looked like a mountain that had decided to become architecture — black spires, antlered buttresses, windows of smoked glass that caught no light because there was no light to catch. But underneath the drama, it was stone and mortar and, crucially, ward-lines. And Sefa Venn had been reading ward-lines since before she could read words.

She crouched in the shadow of a drainage arch half a mile downslope, wrapped in Bram's borrowed cloak — which smelled of pickpocket's grease and cheap licorice — and watched the supply wagons roll up to the postern gate. Grain. Tallow. Salt. Even dying courts ate.

"Nobody guards the kitchen door," she murmured to herself. "Nobody has ever guarded the kitchen door. Kings can imagine their own deaths in a hundred ways and never once think about soup."

The wagon driver was arguing with the gate sergeant about a delivery of eels. Sefa slipped beneath the axle, pressed her palm flat against the iron rim, and let her fingers do what they did best: listen. The ward-line running through the gatepost hummed with royal blood-signature — the regent's work, fresh and arrogant, keyed to *her* blood and nothing else. Sloppy. A lock with one key is a lock with one point of failure, and Sefa had spent thirty years being everyone's failure point.

She waited for the eel argument to reach its crescendo, rolled under the portcullis between the wheels, and dropped into the dark of the delivery tunnel smelling of river mud and fish.

* * *

They were keeping Lior in the Antler Tower, which told Sefa two things. First, that they considered him valuable enough to guard well. Second, that they did not understand him at all, or they'd have kept him somewhere with fewer walls and more sky. You didn't cage a cartographer and expect him to stay put in his head. Lior Oyelaran had spent his whole life mapping places nobody else could see. Put him in a box and he'd simply start drawing the box from the inside until he found the seam.

Getting to him was the easy part. The tower's lower levels were old Hollow-work, and old Hollow-work remembered its makers — including, apparently, one very junior ward-smith who'd apprenticed here forty years ago on an exchange nobody at the Guild of Unmapped Realms had ever bothered to record. Sefa pressed her hand to the ancient stonework and felt the old lines wake, drowsy and confused, like an old dog hearing a familiar whistle.

*Oh, you,* the stones seemed to say. *You still owe me for the third-year exam.*

*I know,* she thought back. *I'm collecting.*

She climbed through the maintenance shafts — built for servants, ignored by royalty since before the stars went out — counting turns, marking sigils with chalk stubs, building her map as she went. By the second hour she had the tower's whole circulatory system in her head: where the guards changed, where the warden drank, where the kitchen slops went out (down, past level four, past a door with a lock so old it predated the regent's blood-key entirely).

Old locks. Old locks answered to craft, not crown.

At the third hour, she reached the wall behind Lior's cell.

* * *

Lior had stopped counting days. Counting days implied there would be more of them, and the cell offered no evidence either way. No window. A slot in the door through which came bread, water, and occasionally a guard who wouldn't meet his eyes. The walls were hung with old tapestries gone grey, depicting hunts and coronations and, in one corner, a faded figure with a crown being handed something small and glowing — a name, maybe, though the weavers hadn't known what they were weaving when they made it.

He'd tried everything a cartographer tries. He'd mapped the cell by sound: seventeen paces by twelve, ceiling too high to touch, a draft from the northeast corner meaning a shaft, meaning air, meaning eventually freedom if you were patient and thin, and Lior was patient but not thin. He'd talked to the walls, because his mother — whichever mother, the one who raised him or the one whose face lived now in Lady Irun's smug recollections — had taught him that walls keep secrets better than people.

The walls had never once talked back.

Until tonight.

It started as a rhythm in the stone. Three taps. Pause. Two taps. Pause. One long scrape, like a fingernail drawn slow across slate. Lior sat up on the cot, heart hammering, because he knew that rhythm. Every guild member knew it. It was the first thing Odell taught the apprentices, drilled into them over supper with a spoon against the table:

*Tap-tap-tap. Tap-tap. Scraaape.*

*Who goes there. Who sent you. Answer true.*

Lior pressed his palm flat against the coldest stone and tapped back, clumsy with hope: *Lior Oyelaran. Nobody sent me. I'm a prisoner.*

A pause. Then, faint but unmistakable, transmitted through solid rock like a joke through a family dinner: *I know who you are, you great useless ledger-keeper. I've been crawling through your prison's plumbing for three hours. It's Sefa.*

Lior laughed — actually laughed, a cracked, disbelieving sound that echoed off seventeen-by-twelve feet of despair — and had to sit down again. "You're—" he said aloud, uselessly, then remembered the code. He tapped: *How?*

*Trade secret,* the wall tapped back. Then, after a moment, slower, almost gently: *Are you whole?*

*Bruised. Confused. Questioning several fundamental assumptions.* He hesitated, then added: *They told me things. About my name. About why the stars went out.*

*Tell me.*

And so, tapping against a wall in a language designed for passing salt at dinner, Lior told her what the regent's interrogators had let slip — because villains always talk, another thing Odell taught over supper. The original covenant between Vess and the Hollow, the pact that lit both skies, had not been sealed in blood or gold or treaty-ink. It had been sealed with a name. Freely given. His mother had carried him to the border-stone and spoken his true name into the covenant itself — *Lioran*, whole and unhidden — and the stars had burned on that gift like lamps on oil. A name given freely binds truer than any chain.

Which meant that when someone stole that name — erased it from every map, every record, every memory — they hadn't just stolen a boy. They'd stolen the covenant's keystone. The stars hadn't gone out. They'd been *unpaid*.

*Irun,* Lior tapped. *She hid me. She said it was mercy.*

*Odell says mercy that comes with a receipt isn't mercy,* Sefa tapped back. *It's inventory.*

Lior stared at the grey tapestry, at the little glowing thing in the crowned figure's hands. "So the fix," he said quietly, to himself, to the wall, to whatever gods ran the Hollow's failing ledgers, "is my name. They want me to say it again. Give it back to the covenant. Become the crown prince, take the throne, be the keystone." His hands curled. "Be the thing they erased me *into*."

The wall was quiet a long moment. When Sefa's answer came, it came slow, each tap deliberate, the way she spoke when she was choosing words like a smith chooses hammer-strokes.

*Lior. Listen. A name freely given is the strongest seal there is. That's true. But you're thinking about it wrong. Everyone is. The regent thinks a name is property — hers to steal, yours to reclaim, a title to inherit. Irun thinks it's a weapon. Even you're sitting there thinking the only choice is: give the name back, or refuse to.*

*What else is there?* Lior tapped.

*Names aren't only given once,* the wall said. *Ask anyone in this guild. Odell was born Odella of the Marches and gave that name away at twenty and took a new one off a tavern sign because she liked how it sounded. Bram's had four names and owes money under all of them. I chose mine at fifteen, in a ward-smith's shop, out of a book of dead languages, and my mother cried and then got used to it.* A pause. *A name isn't a debt you're born owing, Lior. It's a debt you're allowed to choose. Who you pay it to. What it buys. That's the whole trick. That's why theirs broke and yours doesn't have to.*

Lior sat with that. Around him the cell stayed exactly as dark and exactly as small as before, and yet something in it had shifted, the way a map shifts when someone finally draws the coastline correctly. He had spent weeks believing he stood at a fork: throne or refusal, crown or guild, the name they stole or the self he'd built. But forks were for people whose maps had only two roads.

*The covenant needs a name freely given,* he tapped slowly, testing the thought as it formed. *It doesn't say whose.*

*Now you're doing cartography,* the wall tapped back, and he could hear the grin in it.

*But the regent will try to force it. Her blood, her claim, her sealing. If she gets to the border-stone first—*

*Then we'd better not let her,* Sefa tapped. *Bram's loose somewhere in this rockpile — don't ask, he stole a guard's uniform and looks insulting in it. Odell's holding the guild hall with half the refugees and all of the cook's temper. And I have a map of this tower in my head and a lock on your door that predates the regent by two hundred years.*

Another pause. Then, softer: *We're coming, Lior. Not for the heir. For you. There's a difference, and the difference is the whole point.*

Lior pressed his forehead against the cold stone, right where her knuckles had knocked, and for the first time since the stars went out, the dark felt less like a verdict and more like a held breath.

*Give me an hour,* the wall said. *Learn the shape of your own name. All of it. Because tomorrow, at the border-stone, somebody's going to speak a covenant into being — and it is absolutely not going to be her.*

Far above, through smoke-glass windows that caught nothing, the first faint tremor ran through the citadel — the sound, distant and wrong, of the regent's forces beginning to move toward the border.

And in the walls, quick and light as a heartbeat, Sefa started counting locks.
