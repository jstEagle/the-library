## 9. The Reforging

The border did not look broken anymore. That was the first thing Lior noticed, and the thing that frightened him most.

Where the wards had shattered — where he had stood three nights ago and watched the sky go out like a lamp pinched between wet fingers — there was now a road. A wide, smooth road of pale stone, running straight through the gap in the world, lined with antlered lantern-posts that burned with a cold white flame. It had not been built. It had *arrived*, the way rot arrives in fruit: quietly, from the inside.

"She's been busy," Bram said at his shoulder. The thief's usual grin was gone; what remained was something leaner and more honest underneath it. "Give Vessa Regent Ashvael three days and an apocalypse, and she'll have infrastructure."

"Regents do love a good crisis," Sefa said. She was checking the straps on her ward-smith's kit for the fourth time, which meant she was terrified, which meant Lior felt better, because Sefa terrified was still the most competent person in any hemisphere. "It's the only time anyone lets them near the tools."

They stood at the edge of the guild's camp — a sprawl of tents and cookfires that had grown up around the broken border like moss around a wound. Behind them: two thousand refugees, human and Hollow, sharing soup and blankets and increasingly heated arguments about whose grandmother's bread recipe was correct. Ahead of them: the road, and at its end, half a mile into no-man's-land, the regent's pavilion of black silk, and above it, the dark.

The stars were still gone. Both skies. All of them.

Lior could feel the absence the way you feel a missing tooth — with his tongue, constantly, against the empty socket of the world. His new sight, the sight Lady Irun's blood had woken in him, showed him the covenant as it truly was: a great seam of light stitched through everything, and every stitch but one pulled loose. One thread remained, frayed to nothing, anchored at the border-stone beneath his feet. Anchored, he now understood, to a name.

His name. The one his mother had given freely, and someone had stolen, and everyone had spent forty years pretending had never existed.

"You don't have to do this," Sefa said quietly. "We could run. I know six ways through the wastes that even the Hollow doesn't map. Seven, if we take the goat."

"The goat is not invited," Bram said.

"I'm just saying the goat has *qualities*—"

"I have to do this," Lior said. And then, because they deserved better than a hero's flat declaration: "And also I want to. Which is worse, probably. Odell would say wanting things is how you get hurt."

"Odell says that about second helpings," Sefa said. "She said it about the entire cheese wheel last winter."

"And she was right," Lior said. "I ate too much cheese and then I found out I was a lost prince. Correlation."

Bram snorted. Sefa smiled despite herself. And that — the small, stupid warmth of it — was the last thing Lior carried with him when he stepped onto the pale road alone.

* * *

The regent had prepared a ceremony. Of course she had.

Vessa Ashvael stood at the center of a ring of standing stones that had not been there yesterday either, wearing robes the color of dried blood, her silver hair bound in a crown-braid that was not yet a crown but was auditioning for the role. Around her, kneeling in ranks, were the Hollow courtiers who had survived the court of ash and antlers, and behind *them* — Lior's stomach dropped — were soldiers, and among the soldiers, wrists bound, one eye swollen shut, sat Bram.

No. Not Bram. Bram was behind him. Lior looked again and understood: it was a glamour, a puppet of shadow and borrowed skin, placed there precisely so that Lior would see it. Beside it knelt another puppet-Sefa, and another puppet-Lior, aged forty years, wearing the face from the coin.

"That's a nice touch," Lior called out, walking forward. "Very theatrical. Did you do the aging yourself or did you have people?"

"Lioran." The regent's voice carried across the stones like frost across glass. "Crown-Undrawn. You came."

"Lior," he said. "Just Lior. It's shorter. Saves ink."

He stopped ten paces from her. Up close, he could see what the ceremony was costing her: the fine tremor in her hands, the blood already streaking her forearms where she'd opened old scars and new ones. The covenant-thread hung before her, visible only to him, and she had tied her own life to its frayed end like a woman mooring a boat to a drowning man.

"You came to stop me," she said. "Or to claim it yourself. Either serves. The seal requires royal blood freely offered, and yours—" her eyes went to the puppet, hungry and contemptuous at once "—is the truest in either realm. Mine is merely... adjacent. Enough to hold the door while I wait for you."

"You unlocked the wards," Lior said. "You cut the key from my blood years ago. Irun told me. She thought it would frighten me into your arms. She was half right — it frightened me. But here's what she didn't understand, and neither do you." He took a breath. "You keep talking about my blood like it's a vault. Like being born is the same as belonging."

"It *is* belonging," the regent snapped, and for one moment the mask slipped and he saw her plainly: an old woman who had spent decades serving a crown that starved her realm, who had decided that if the system was dying anyway, she might as well be the one holding the knife. "Blood is the only inheritance that cannot be forged or flattered. Blood is *certain*. I have watched certainty die in this world, Lioran. I am offering it back."

"You're offering a throne to a man who balances ledgers for bottled echoes," Lior said. "You clearly haven't read my references."

Something flickered at the corner of her mouth. He pressed it.

"Here's what I learned in the guild," he said. "Every impossible thing that crosses our books — every borrowed shadow, every debt paid in years — has one rule. Nothing given under compulsion holds. Nothing taken stays taken. Your seal will fail, Vessa. Not because your blood isn't royal enough. Because you're offering it to a *door* instead of to *people*."

"The covenant is not people. The covenant is law."

"The covenant," Lior said, "is two worlds agreeing to share a sky. And you've been so busy arguing over who owns the sky that nobody checked whether the sky wanted either of you."

He turned from her — deliberately, insolently, the way Bram had taught him to turn away from mark and magistrate alike — and faced the gathered crowd. Courtiers. Soldiers. Refugees who had followed the road from both directions, drawn by rumor and hope. Beyond them, faintly, the cookfires of the guild camp, and beyond *those*, the whole dark world.

"My mother gave her name freely," he said, and his voice cracked on *freely*, and he let it. "That's why the theft unmade the stars. Not because names are magic. Because *giving* is. So I'm not going to offer you my blood, Vessa. And I'm not going to take the throne. I'm going to do something much more embarrassing."

He reached into his coat and drew out the ledger.

It was battered, soup-stained, missing its back cover since the crossing. Every name the guild had sheltered since the night the stars went out was written in it — Lior had stayed up three nights recording them, because that was the job, that had always been the job: write down what is real so it can't be erased. Two thousand names. Human and Hollow. Cook and thief and smith and scribe and goat-adjacent.

"A covenant sealed with one name can be stolen," he said. "A covenant sealed with two thousand has to be *agreed to*. Everyone who wants their sky back — say your name. That's all. That's the whole ritual. No blood. No thrones. Just tell the dark who you are."

Silence. The regent stared at him as though he had begun speaking in fish.

Then, from the back of the crowd, a voice — cracked, elderly, unmistakably the voice of a woman who had once argued with Lior about bread recipes across a border that officially did not exist:

"Marta. Marta Osei. And my sister Enid, who's deaf, so I'm saying hers too."

Another voice. "Tomasin Vale." Another. "Sefekh of the Ashline forges — Sefa, call me Sefa." Another. "Bram. Just Bram. It's shorter. Saves ink." Laughter rippled through the crowd, thin and startled, like ice cracking on a pond in spring.

The regent lunged for the covenant-thread, dragging her blade across her palm. "The seal is *mine* to—"

"No," Lior said, and the word came out of him with all the weight of a name freely given, and the thread *moved*.

* * *

What happened next, no ledger could properly record, though Lior would spend the rest of his life trying.

The names rose off the page like heat-shimmer, like pollen, like sparks off a forge — two thousand small bright truths, each one ordinary, each one irreplaceable. They wove themselves into the frayed thread of the covenant, not replacing the stolen name but *surrounding* it, a wall of witnesses around one small stolen child, until the theft had nowhere left to hide. Lior felt his own name — Lioran, Crown-Undrawn, the erased prince — dissolve into the chorus, not destroyed but *shared*, diluted past the reach of any single thief, including himself.

Vessa Ashvael fell to her knees. Her blood-seal guttered and went out, and she did not seem devastated. She seemed, for the first time in perhaps forty years, *relieved*.

Above them, the sky remembered itself.

One constellation first — the Cartographer's Ladle, low on the northern horizon, seven stars blinking awake like eyes opening one at a time. Then the Antler Crown, then the Weaver, then the Long River pouring itself back across the dark, star by star by star, and the crowd made a sound Lior had never heard a crowd make: two thousand people exhaling at once, and then beginning, raggedly, to sing.

Sefa arrived at his elbow at some point, having punched her way through the glamour-soldiers ("they punch back, but softly, it's very considerate"), and stood beside him watching the stars come home.

"You realize," she said, "that you just abolished a monarchy with a soup-stained notebook."

"Somebody had to. The paperwork on thrones is terrible."

Above them, the last constellation ignited — a small, crooked pattern of five stars that appeared on no chart Lior had ever seen, glowing brighter than all the rest, right above the guild camp.

"What's that one?" Sefa asked.

Lior looked at it for a long moment. It looked, he thought, rather like a window seat.

"I think," he said slowly, "we're going to need a bigger ledger."
