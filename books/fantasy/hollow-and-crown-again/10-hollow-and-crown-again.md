## 10. Hollow and Crown Again

The ledger had never been this heavy.

Lior Oyelaran sat at the guild's long table with the book open before him, and it seemed to weigh more than it ever had — not because of the paper, but because of what he was about to write in it. Forty years of entries, and every single one a trade. *Bottled echo, one vial, for three measures of river-salt. Borrowed shadow, returned slightly singed, against future favors. One year of Odell's life, held in escrow, pending appeal.* Debts and payments, columns and balances. The Guild of Unmapped Realms did not give things away. Giving away implied you knew what you had, and the whole point of the guild was that nobody knew anything for certain.

But tonight, the last page waited, and Lior had decided what went on it.

He dipped his pen. Around him, the guild hall hummed with the particular chaos of a building that had been an improvised shelter for two months and was now, grudgingly, becoming a home again. Someone had hung laundry across the map room. The cook — Marta, who had fed four hundred refugees on soup and sheer stubbornness — was arguing with a Hollow tinker about whether her stove counted as "warded territory" or "sovereign soil." The tinker was losing, but cheerfully.

Bram was asleep in a chair with his boots up, one arm draped over his eyes, still bruised from the citadel. Every so often he muttered something that sounded like bargaining. Even unconscious, the thief negotiated.

Sefa came down the stairs with a lantern she no longer needed — the stars were back, all of them, and the windows were full of their light — but she carried it anyway, out of habit, or maybe just because she liked the way it made her hands look gold.

"You're doing it," she said. Not a question. She set the lantern down and leaned over his shoulder to read the blank last line.

"I'm doing it."

"Odell's going to cry."

"Odell is *always* crying," Lior said. "She cries at inventory. She cried when the new ink arrived."

"She cried when the new ink arrived because you'd written 'ink' in the ledger as 'darkness, bottled,' and she thought it was poetry." Sefa pulled out the chair beside him and sat, close enough that her shoulder pressed his. "Write it properly, then. No poetry. You're terrible at poetry. You once described a haunted bridge as 'structurally ambiguous.'"

"It *was* structurally ambiguous. It was also screaming."

"Write the entry, Lioran."

He smiled at the name. Two months ago it had felt like someone else's coat, too big in the shoulders, smelling of a country he didn't remember. Now it fit fine alongside *Lior*, the way a person can have two names the way they can have two homes — not divided between them, but doubled by them. *Crown-Undrawn,* Lady Irun had called him. The title had dissolved along with the crown itself, which was, he'd been told by the new covenant-council, exactly as intended. There was no throne now. There was a table, and chairs around it, and nine elected members of the first council either realm had seen in living memory, and a cartographer's office with a window that looked out on both worlds at once.

The border stood open now. Not broken — *open*, warded gently, like a door with a latch instead of a lock. People crossed both ways to buy bread, argue about tariffs, attend weddings. Last week a Vessish farmer had married a Hollow weaver under a sky that belonged to neither realm and both, and the stars had come out early just to watch, or so Bram claimed, and nobody argued with Bram about stars anymore. He'd earned it.

Lior wrote:

*Final entry. Not a trade.*

His pen hovered. Sefa read over his shoulder, breath held, which was unlike her; Sefa breathed through everything, including ward-collapse and open-heart negotiations with regents.

*Item: one covenant, reforged. Given freely by: everyone. Received by: everyone. Terms: none outstanding. Balance: even.*

Then, after a moment, beneath it, smaller:

*Note: names used as seal — see attached list, 1,204 signatures, human and Hollow alike. Ledger closed. Ledger reopened as gift registry. Management reserves the right to be sentimental.*

"There it is," Sefa said softly. "Sentimental clause."

"Somebody has to institutionalize it or it'll get lost."

He signed it. Then, because it seemed right, he passed the pen to Sefa, and she signed, and she woke Bram just enough to make him sign — "I'm not awake," he protested, signing anyway, "this is legally binding on my sleeping half only" — and Marta signed with floury fingers, and Odell signed and, as predicted, cried, and by midnight the last page held forty-one names, and the ledger weighed nothing at all.

* * *

The council's first official act, three days later, was to elect Lior to a position that did not exist until they invented it for him: Cartographer-General of the Open Border. His duties included mapping both realms honestly, maintaining the boundary wards jointly with a Hollow counterpart (Sefa volunteered before the sentence was finished), and — this was written into the charter at Bram's insistence — "refusing, in perpetuity, all crowns, thrones, scepters, circlets, hats of state, and ceremonial headwear of any kind, including but not limited to birthday crowns."

"He's just bitter about the birthday crown incident," Lior told the council.

"The birthday crown incident," Bram said gravely, "involved a mind-altering enchantment and my complete humiliation. I stand by the clause."

The second official act was dinner.

They held it in the guild hall, because the council chamber felt too grand and the guild hall felt like theirs. Marta cooked everything at once, having spent two months unable to source ingredients and apparently determined to never feel that way again. The long table groaned under roast things and stewed things and a Hollow dish made of cloud-fruit that tasted like the smell before rain. Refugees who had become regulars sat beside guild members who had become refugees of a sort themselves — displaced from their old certainties, resettled somewhere better.

It was during the third course that the argument began.

"—all I'm saying," Bram said, gesturing with a fork, "is that I found the window seat. Historically. Through applied skill."

"You *stole* the window seat," said Pip, the guild's youngest scribe, who had arrived as a refugee and refused to leave. "From a sick child."

"The child was faking. I checked. Professionally."

"You cannot professionally check whether a child is faking while also stealing their seat."

"Watch me."

"The seat," Sefa said, in the tone of someone laying out ward-sigils, "belongs to whoever has been on border patrol longest that week. That's the rule. That's always been the rule."

"That rule was invented by you," said Bram, "during a week you were on patrol."

"And yet it holds."

Lior let the argument wash over him. He sat at the middle of the table, where he could see everyone — Odell laughing until she wheezed at something Marta said, Pip standing on her chair for emphasis, two Hollow council members trying to follow the dispute with the expression of people watching a foreign sport. The window in question framed a view of the border road, and beyond it, faint in the evening light, the hills of the Hollow, green again, lit again, real again.

Once, those hills had been blank space on his charts. Once, his own childhood had been blank space on the map of himself. He had spent a lifetime believing he was fully mapped — accounts balanced, borders known, self accounted for — and then learned that the most important territory had been erased before he could walk. But erasure, he understood now, was not the end of mapping. It was an invitation. You could draw yourself again. You could let other people hold the pen.

Pip had climbed fully onto the table now. Bram was citing precedent from a burglary in 871. Sefa was winning, as usual, by simply refusing to raise her voice.

"Settle it fairly," Odell called out, wiping her eyes. "Guild rules. Dispute goes to arbitration."

Everyone looked at Lior.

He should have expected it. He was, after all, the arbiter of record now; it came with the charter, right below the hat clause. He looked down the table at his family — loud, flour-dusted, bruised, elected, stolen-from and stolen-for, given and giving — and he picked up his glass.

"I rule," he said, "that the window seat belongs to whoever needs it most tonight. And since none of us will admit to needing anything—" he raised the glass higher, and the starlight through the disputed window caught it, and every face turned toward him, waiting, patient, home— "we'll share it. All of us. In shifts. Effective immediately, and forever, amen."

A roar of protest. A roar of laughter, underneath the protest, holding it up. Pip demanded the ruling be entered into the ledger. Bram demanded an appeals process. Marta said if anyone fought at her table they'd fight outside, in the cold, like civilized people.

And Lior laughed, and reached for the ledger out of pure habit, and stopped — because the ledger was closed now, finished, a gift with no column left to write in.

So he memorized it instead. The noise, the light, the argument, the window full of two skies at once. Some things, he was learning, were not meant for the books.

Some things you just keep.
