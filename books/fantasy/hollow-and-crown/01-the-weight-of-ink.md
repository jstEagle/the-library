## 1. The Weight of Ink

The ink was the first thing Emrys Oyelaran learned to fear.

Not the guildmasters, not the debt-collectors with their soft voices and softer ledgers, not even the extraction room on the third floor whose door never quite closed — but the ink itself, the guild's proprietary black, ground from something they refused to name and mixed with water from a well that had been sealed since before anyone now living was hired. It drank light. It sat in the well of a pen like a small held breath, and when it touched paper it did not so much dry as *settle*, as though the words were sinking into some depth beneath the page and would go on sinking long after everyone who read them was dead.

Emrys had been copying for eleven years. They had learned to hold their wrist at an angle that spared the tendons, to breathe in slow counts of four so the lines came out even, to think of nothing at all while their hand did the work — because thinking, in the Ledger Guild of Vhalen, was a kind of theft. Your thoughts belonged to the hours you'd sold. The guild had purchased them at a rate of four coppers per hour, and it did not appreciate unlicensed use.

"Entry forty-one," said Master Quillon from the front of the scriptorium, without looking up. He was a narrow man folded into a wide chair, and his voice had the texture of paper left too long in damp. "Bottled laughter, one measure. Provenance: the estate of Lady Casserly, deceased. Condition: still warm."

Emrys dipped the pen. The ink took the light and kept it.

*Bottled laughter, one measure.* They wrote it the way they wrote everything — clean, small, exact — and tried not to imagine what laughter looked like in a bottle. Amber, probably. Or maybe colorless, like water, so that you would only know what you had bought when you uncorked it and your own mouth betrayed you. There was a story among the copyists that a clerk in the old days had opened a vial of a stranger's grief by accident and wept for six days, mourning a wife he had never had, children he had never fathered, a house he could not have described if his life depended on it. The guild had docked his wages for the lost hours.

Emrys did not know if the story was true. They only knew that no one laughed in the scriptorium, and that the bottled things on the inventory lists were always, always described in terms of condition. Still warm. Still sharp. Faded. Leaking.

*Leaking* was the worst word. Emrys had copied three entries this month marked *leaking*, and each time their chest had gone tight in a way they had no ledger line to account for.

Outside the scriptorium's high windows, Vhalen was going about its morning — cart wheels in the slush, the fish-bells of the river market, a knife-grinder crying his trade along the canal. The sound came through the glass thinned and distant, like music played in another house. Emrys let it wash over them and kept writing.

*Entry forty-two. A key to nowhere, iron, cold to all touch. Opens nothing tested. Retained for study.*

They paused over that one. Not long — a half-breath, no more; Quillon missed nothing, or seemed to, which amounted to the same thing — but long enough for the ink to gather at the nib and threaten a blot. A key to nowhere. Someone had carried it once. Someone had cut it to fit a lock, had stood before a door with their heart going hard, had turned it — and then? Then nothing, apparently. Nothing tested. The key sat in a drawer in the vaults below, iron and patient, waiting for a door that the world had misplaced.

Emrys knew the feeling intimately. They had been carrying one of their own for eleven years.

Their debt stood at two thousand, one hundred and forty-six marks, nine coppers. They knew the figure the way other people knew their own heartbeat. It was read aloud to them every quarter-day in a small room with one lamp, by a man named Ferren whose voice never changed, as though he were reciting the weather. Two thousand, one hundred and forty-six marks, nine coppers, incurred at birth — because that was how the guild worked, that was its great kindness, its famous generosity: it paid your mother's lying-in costs, your childhood fevers, your bread and shoes and schooling, and it wrote every coin into a book against your name, and when you turned fourteen it handed you a pen and said, *now, child, work it off.*

Most people died working it off. Most people's children inherited the remainder. Emrys had seen entries in the old ledgers — debts passed down through four generations like a cursed heirloom, shrinking by a mark here and there across a century of copying and hauling and dying, until some last descendant paid off the final copper of a grandmother's fever and walked out of the guild gates free, at seventy, into a world that had no place prepared for them.

Eleven years of copying, and Emrys had brought their sum down by thirty-one marks. At that rate — they had done the arithmetic once, late, by candlelight, hating themselves for doing it — they would be free at sixty-eight.

If nothing else went wrong. Something always went wrong. Last winter it had been a cough that cost them nine marks in physician's fees, added cheerfully to the total. The year before, a miscounted crate in the vaults, blamed on them, twelve marks. The guild's arithmetic was very kind, everyone said so. Kind the way deep water is kind to a stone.

"Entry forty-three," Quillon said. "A jar of last words. Contents: assorted. Do not shake."

Emrys wrote it. *Do not shake.* Somewhere below them, in the dark of the vaults, a jar sat on a shelf holding the final sentences of people who had died — and someone, somewhere, would pay good money to hear them. A widow buying back her husband's voice. A son paying for the words his mother hadn't managed to say. The guild called this mercy in its prospectuses. *We preserve what death would take.* The prospectus did not mention the price. The prospectus did not mention that the guild had never once sold anything back at cost.

The pen moved. The ink settled. Outside, the morning went on being ordinary, and Emrys copied the impossible into the record of the world, one careful letter at a time, and wanted — quietly, constantly, the way a crack wants to be a break — something they could not have put a name to.

* * *

At midday they were given bread and thin soup in the refectory, and twenty minutes to eat it, and the twenty minutes were deducted from nothing because meals, the guild said, were an investment in the asset. That was what the clerks called the debtors, when they thought no debtor could hear. *Assets.* Emrys had heard a junior factor use the word about a girl of sixteen who had fainted at her desk, and had watched him catch himself, and had watched the girl pretend she hadn't heard, because pretending was cheaper than objecting. Objecting cost marks.

Emrys ate slowly and watched the room.

This was the other thing they had learned in eleven years: watch. Watch who sat where. Watch which factors spoke to which masters. Watch the door to the third floor, and who went through it smiling and came out quiet. The scriptorium held forty copyists and Emrys could have told you, without looking up from a page, which of them had given up, which were still counting coppers toward freedom, and which — like Sella two benches down, who hummed when she thought no one could hear — had found some small smuggled country inside their own head and lived there as much as the guild allowed.

Sella caught them looking and raised her spoon an inch. Emrys raised theirs back. It was the closest thing to conversation either of them could afford.

"You're wanted after bell," said a voice at their shoulder. Ferren. Of course. He moved like dust settling. "Vault inventory, sub-level two. Overtime."

Overtime meant more marks against the debt — a copper per hour, less than a tenth of what the guild charged for the same hour of a person's life. Emrys nodded. Arguing was a luxury item, and they had never once been able to afford it.

Sub-level two was cold enough to see your breath. The lamps down there burned behind glass and made the shelves glow like the inside of some vast amber body, and everything on those shelves glowed back — or didn't, which was worse. Emrys was given a list and a hand-cart and sent down aisle after aisle of the guild's holdings, checking seals, noting condition, copying serial numbers into a second ledger that would be checked against the first by someone whose job it was to distrust everyone else's.

That was the guild entire, Emrys thought, wheeling the cart between the shelves. Distrust, bound in calfskin.

They found the bottled laughter on the fourth aisle, exactly where the inventory swore it lived: a squat green flask, stoppered with wax, sitting in a nest of straw. The label was old. *One measure, still warm,* and beneath it, in a different hand, smaller: *do not open in company.*

Emrys stood looking at it longer than the job allowed.

It would be nothing, they told themselves. A sound. A few seconds of some dead woman's amusement, caught like a moth under glass. People paid for stranger things — the guild's whole fortune rested on that fact. And yet their hand hovered near the wax seal the way a hand hovers near a stove, drawn and warned at once, and what they felt was not curiosity exactly, or not only. It was more like recognition. Somewhere under their ribs, in a place no ledger had ever inventoried, something leaned toward that little green bottle and said: *that could be mine. Some of me is in there somewhere. Doesn't the guild keep pieces of everyone?*

They wrote the serial number. They noted the condition — *still warm* — and did not let themselves wonder how warmth was measured, or whether it faded, or what happened to a laugh when the last person who remembered laughing it finally paid off their debts and died.

On the sixth aisle they found the key to nowhere. It lay in a shallow tray like a specimen, iron gone brown with age, and when Emrys's fingers brushed it — accidentally, they insisted to themselves afterward, entirely accidentally — it was cold. Cold as the ink. Cold as the well-water the guild wouldn't explain. And for half a heartbeat, no longer, Emrys felt something turn over far away inside them, the way a door turns in the wind of a house you've just left — a sensation of a lock, somewhere, considering.

They snatched their hand back. Wrote the number. Moved on.

The jar of last words was on the ninth aisle, and they did not linger near it at all.

* * *

The overtime ran until the evening bells, and by the time Emrys climbed back up into the grey daylight, snow had begun to fall on Vhalen — early snow, fat and windless, the kind that came down as though the sky were emptying itself of something it had held too long. The guild's courtyard was already white. The statue of the Founding Factors wore caps of it, and their bronze faces looked, as always, mildly pleased about whatever it was they had done.

Emrys crossed the yard with their collar up, past the gatehouse, past the porter's lodge where old Hesk warmed his hands at a brazier, and it was Hesk who stopped them — not with words, but with a grunt and a jerk of his chin toward the gate.

"There's a kid been standing out there," he said. "Half an hour, maybe more. Won't come to the bell. Won't go away." He rubbed his hands. "In this weather. Somebody should see to it, and it won't be me, I'm off at the hour."

Emrys looked through the gate bars.

Beyond them, where the guild's steps met the street, a small figure stood in the falling snow. A child, seven or eight perhaps, hooded and coatless as far as Emrys could tell, though the snow seemed almost to avoid them — landing around their feet, on their shoulders, and yet the child did not shiver. Did not stamp. Did not move at all, in fact, except to stand facing the gate with the terrible patience of someone who has come a very long way and intends to finish coming.

And even at that distance, even through the curtain of snow, Emrys felt the strangest thing happen in their chest — a pulling, a tilting, like a shelf in a ship's hold shifting as the hull leans. As if some part of them recognized the child before their eyes had finished reporting what they saw.

"Hesk," Emrys heard themselves say, "has anyone spoken to it?"

"It don't answer. Or—" Hesk frowned, working his jaw. "Or it answers wrong. Tam went out and asked its business, and it said something back at him, and Tam swears on his mother it wasn't any tongue he knew. Old words, he says. Words like off a monument." He shrugged, uneasy. "Guild'll want it logged, I expect. You're the loggers."

Emrys should have gone inside. That was the correct action, the affordable action: report to the night factor, log the incident, deduct nothing, risk nothing. Eleven years of careful living rose up in them and said it plainly — *go inside, Emrys, close the door, this is not yours, nothing is yours, you are an asset with a balance outstanding.*

Instead they set their hand on the gate latch.

Snow gathered on their knuckles. Beyond the bars, the child lifted their face, and Emrys saw that their eyes were the colour of the guild's ink — that same drinking black — and that they were looking directly at Emrys, past the gate, past the eleven years, past, it seemed, the whole careful architecture of a borrowed life, at something underneath it that Emrys had never once been permitted to see.

The child smiled. Snowflakes landed on their lashes and did not melt.

"Sef," the child said — clear, unhurried, in a tongue that no living throat should have known and that Emrys, God help them, understood — "you took your time."

Behind them, up in the warm lit windows of the scriptorium, someone had already begun to write it all down.
