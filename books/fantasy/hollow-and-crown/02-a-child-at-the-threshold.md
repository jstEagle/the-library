## 2. A Child at the Threshold

The snow came to Vhalen the way debts did — slowly, then all at once.

Emrys noticed it first as a change in the light. The tall windows of the Ledger Guild's copying hall had been pouring down a thin gray afternoon, and then, between one line of ink and the next, they went white. Emrys looked up from the inventory of a dead collector's estate — *one jar, sealed, containing the last words of a woman who died forgiving someone; handle with care; do not open in company* — and watched the first flakes drift past the glass like ash falling upward.

By the time the bell rang for end-of-shift, the streets had vanished. The canal bridges were soft white spines. The lamplighters gave up before the third ring of the city and went home to their suppers, and Vhalen became a place of muffled sound and blue shadow, the kind of night that made even the guild's stone halls feel temporary, like something that might be blown away if the wind decided it.

Emrys stayed late, as always. Staying late was cheaper than eating well.

They worked by the last permitted candle in the scriptorium, copying figures into the master ledger with the small, exact hand that had earned them their place here eleven years ago, when they'd been brought in off the debt-rolls at age nine with ink already under their fingernails and no one to claim them. Eleven years of inventories. Eleven years of impossible things logged, weighed, valued, shelved.

*One key, iron, which opens no lock yet found. Provenance unknown. Value: negotiable.*

*One bottle, green glass, containing laughter — provenance disputed; three claimants; sealed pending arbitration.*

Emrys had copied out ten thousand such lines. They had never once been allowed to touch the things themselves. That was the arrangement. The guild held the objects; the guild held the debts; the guild held, if you asked certain of the older clerks in a certain lowered voice, rather more than either.

The candle guttered. Outside, the storm thickened until the window was only a wall of moving white, and Emrys thought about the walk home — three bridges, one tunnel, the long climb up Tanner's Row where the snow would be drifted to the knee — and decided, with the practical cowardice of the perpetually exhausted, not to make it. There was a cot in the records cellar for clerks kept late by urgent work. It smelled of mildew and old vellum. It was warm enough.

They banked the candle, gathered their papers, and were halfway to the stair when they heard the knock.

Three strikes. Small ones. The kind a child makes when they have been taught that loud knocking is rude.

At this hour. In this storm. At a door that had no business being knocked upon, because the Ledger Guild's front entrance was barred at dusk and its side entrance was barred at dusk and the delivery gate was barred at dusk, and every bar was iron, and every bar was locked, and Emrys knew because they had checked them themselves, that very evening, out of habit, out of the same careful watchfulness that had kept them alive through eleven years of institutional childhood.

The knock came again. Three strikes. Patient.

Emrys stood in the dark of the scriptorium with their heart doing something strange and slow, like a clock deciding whether to keep time.

Every instinct said: *go back to your cot, scribe. Whatever knocks on the guild's door at midnight in a killing snow is not knocking for you.*

But their feet carried them down the stairs instead, past the sleeping records, past the extraction wing with its door of black oak and its brass plate worn illegible, through the vestibule where the guild's motto was carved into the lintel — *ALL THINGS ARE HELD; ALL HOLDINGS ARE ACCOUNTED* — and up to the front door itself, where the three-strike knock sounded a fourth time, so softly it might have been snow falling against wood.

Emrys unbarred the door.

The cold hit like a hand. Snow swirled in, hissing across the flagstones, and standing in it — standing *in* it, though the drifts outside reached Emrys's thigh — was a child.

Perhaps eight years old. Perhaps younger; it was hard to tell, wrapped as the child was in a coat that had been fine once, long ago, cut in a style Emrys had never seen: high-collared, silver-braided, fastened with buttons shaped like small bells. The coat was scorched along one hem, as if it had been near a fire. The child's hair was white — not old-person white but true white, snow-white, the white of a thing that had never known color — and their eyes were dark and enormous and utterly calm.

Snow settled on the child's shoulders and did not melt.

"You shouldn't be here," Emrys said, which was stupid, obviously, but the words came out anyway, because the alternative was to stand there saying nothing while the strangeness of the moment pressed against them like deep water.

The child tilted their head. And spoke.

The language was nothing Emrys knew. It was nothing anyone knew — Emrys was certain of that the way one is certain of one's own heartbeat. And yet it was not formless. It had grammar; it had music. It rose and fell in patterns that snagged somewhere behind Emrys's sternum, patterns that felt less like a foreign tongue than like a half-remembered song from a room they'd been carried out of as an infant. The vowels were round as fruit. The consonants fell soft as the snow still drifting past the door. It was beautiful, and it was wrong, and hearing it made Emrys's eyes sting without permission.

"I don't understand," Emrys said. "I'm sorry. I don't—"

The child stopped. Considered Emrys with those vast dark eyes. Then tried again, slower, and this time there were words in it Emrys almost caught — almost, the way one almost catches a name spoken in a dream — and then the child abandoned the effort entirely and said, in flawless, accentless, ordinary Vhaleni:

"*Sef.*"

The word went through Emrys like a needle finding the one thread in all the world meant for it.

Not *clerk*. Not *scribe*. Not *Oyelaran*, the surname assigned to them at intake, derived from no family, belonging to no bloodline, a name issued like a uniform. Not any of the names Emrys had ever been called by any living mouth.

*Sef.*

And the terrible thing — the thing Emrys would lie awake turning over for nights afterward — was that some part of them, some part below thought and below memory, *answered*. Something in their chest turned toward that name the way a plant turns toward light, quick and involuntary and starving.

"I don't know that name," Emrys said aloud, to the child, to the storm, to themselves. "That's not my name."

The child smiled. It was not a comforting smile. It was the smile of someone confirming an arrival after a very long journey.

"You will," the child said, still in that perfect borrowed Vhaleni. "It's yours. I kept it for you." A pause, and then, with the grave precision of a child reciting something learned by rote: "I kept everything. That's what I'm for."

The wind gusted. Snow poured across the threshold in a white breath, and the candle Emrys had left burning in the vestibule bent low, and in its guttering light the child's shadow fell long across the floor — and Emrys, glancing down at it, felt the back of their neck prickle. Because the shadow was not quite the shape of a child. It was the shape of many children, overlapping, a crowd compressed into one small silhouette, dozens of shoulders and heads layered like pages of a book seen edge-on.

Then the candle steadied, and the shadow was only a shadow, and Emrys told themselves they had imagined it, and knew they hadn't.

"You're freezing," Emrys said, because the body insists on its ordinary business even while the mind is drowning. "Come inside. Come *on*."

The child stepped over the threshold without touching the doorframe — Emrys noted that too, filed it away in the growing drawer of wrongness: the child's feet passed the sill cleanly, as if crossing a boundary drawn for larger things — and stood dripping snow that did not melt onto the guild's ancient flags.

Which was when the lamps came on above, and Elder Hask's voice came down the stair like a dropped blade.

"Who's there? Oyelaran! Who is at that door?"

* * *

Elder Hask descended the way weather descends, in layers. First his voice, then his shadow on the stairwell wall, bent and enormous, then the man himself: gaunt, silver-whiskered, wrapped in a dressing gown the color of dried blood, holding a lamp high in one liver-spotted hand. Behind him came Elder Mauve, and behind her two of the night wardens with their iron-tipped staves, and Emrys understood, with a clerk's quick arithmetic, that the guild's elders slept lightly and armed, and that whatever else the Ledger Guild was, it was also a fortress that expected siege.

Hask saw the child and stopped so abruptly that Mauve walked into his back.

For a long moment nobody moved. The lamps hissed. The storm moaned against the door Emrys had not yet rebarred.

"Where did this come from?" Hask said at last, and his voice was not the voice he used for clerks, or debtors, or petitioners. It was flat and careful and afraid, and Emrys had worked for this man for eleven years and had never once heard fear in it.

"The door," Emrys said. "She was knocking. I heard—"

"Did you open it?"

"...Yes, elder."

"Did you *speak* with it?"

"It, elder?" Emrys glanced down at the child, who stood perfectly still, hands folded, watching the elders with an expression of polite, terrible patience — the expression, Emrys realized with a lurch, of someone watching people they had seen before. "She's a child. She was alone in the snow. She'd have died."

"She," repeated Mauve, in the tone of a woman correcting an inventory error. She came forward slowly, lamp extended, and peered at the child the way one peers at a specimen. At the white hair. At the bell-shaped buttons. At the scorched hem of the coat. And Emrys watched the elder's face and saw something extraordinary happen to it: recognition, arriving and then violently refused, like a man slamming a ledger shut on a page he cannot bear to read.

"That style of braid-work," Mauve said quietly. "Hask. Look at the braid-work."

"I see it."

"That's not possible."

"No," Hask agreed. "It isn't." He raised his voice, and now it was the administrative voice again, the voice that reduced the world to entries, and somehow that was worse than the fear. "Wardens. Take it to the holding room on the third level. Do not speak to it. Do not answer anything it says. If it speaks to you in a language you don't know, you will report the fact, the hour, and the exact number of words, and nothing else."

"It's a *child*," Emrys said. "Elder, she's half-frozen, she should be by a fire, she should—"

"Oyelaran." Hask turned, and the lamp-light carved his face into planes and hollows, and what lived in his eyes was not anger. It was the look of a man doing sums he does not like. "You opened a barred door at midnight during a blackstorm, to a thing that knocked like a visitor. You spoke with it. And it spoke *to you* — I heard it from the landing, one word, and I watched your face when it landed." He leaned close. He smelled of camphor and old paper and, beneath both, faintly, of the cold. "What did it call you?"

The silence stretched. The child looked up at Emrys, calm as a closed book.

"A name," Emrys said finally. "One I've never used."

Something moved behind Hask's eyes — a calculation completing itself, a figure carried forward into a column Emrys was not permitted to see. "Take them both," he said to the wardens. "Separate rooms."

"They're together—" one warden began.

"*Separate rooms,*" Hask said, and the wardens moved.

The child did not resist. That was perhaps the worst of it — the utter absence of struggle, of tears, of any of the things a stolen child ought to do. The warden took their shoulder gently enough, and the child went, walking between the iron-tipped staves like a small dignitary between honor guards, and at the foot of the stairs they paused and looked back at Emrys.

They said something then, in that round-fruited, snow-soft language, four syllables, five, and though Emrys could not parse a single word, the meaning arrived whole, the way a blow arrives before the sight of the fist:

*I found you. Don't let them take what I carried.*

Then the wardens led the child up into the dark, and the lamps swung away, and Emrys stood alone in the vestibule with the door still open behind them and the storm breathing in.

"Rebar it," Hask said. He had not left. He stood at the edge of the lamplight, hands folded into his sleeves, studying Emrys the way the guild studied an asset whose value had just changed. "And Oyelaran — at dawn, you'll come to my office. Bring your intake file. All of it."

"My file? Why my—"

"Because," Hask said, turning away, his voice dropping into the dry register of a man reading from a ledger only he can see, "in thirty-one years of service, I have heard that language exactly once before. And the last time I heard it, we burned the records of having heard it, and I swore on the guild's seal that I would never hear it again." He paused at the stair. "Sleep, if you can. Tomorrow we will discover what you are."

*What* I am, Emrys thought, standing in the cold breath of the open door. Not *who*.

Above, somewhere in the stone throat of the guild, faint as a memory, a child began to sing — that impossible language rising through the floors, through the archives, through eleven years of Emrys's careful, watchful, borrowed life — and every note of it felt like a hand reaching into Emrys's chest and turning over things written there in a hand they did not recognize.

Their own handwriting. Their own name.

*Sef.*

Emrys barred the door, and the storm went on without them, and the singing did not stop until dawn.
