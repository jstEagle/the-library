## 3. Entries in a Dead Hand

The Archive of Impossible Things kept its own weather.

Emrys had learned this in their first year below the Ledger Guild — that the deep stacks breathed out cold even in high summer, that the air tasted of iron filings and old paper and something faintly sweet, like fruit going over in a dark room. The senior clerks said it was the preservative salts. Emrys had stopped believing the senior clerks about most things by their third year.

Tonight the cold was worse than usual. It came up through the floor grates in slow exhalations, and the lamps guttered in a rhythm too regular to be draft — almost like breathing. Almost like something asleep down among the crates of bottled laughter, turning over.

Emrys pulled their coat tighter and kept writing.

The task was simple enough, as guild tasks went: recopy the winter inventory of Vault Nine into the new-format ledger, because the old-format ledgers were being retired, pulped, rendered down into fresh paper for fresh lies. Emrys copied in their small, exact hand — the hand that had earned them their indenture in the first place, at nine years old, when a guild assessor had watched them copy a fishmonger's sign and offered their mother a sum she could not refuse. *A gift,* she'd called it, weeping with relief. *They want to train you, little one. A gift.*

Debt was always dressed as a gift in Vhalen. That was the first lesson of the Ledger Guild, though no one taught it aloud.

*Item 4471,* Emrys wrote. *One key, iron, unmarked. Provenance unknown. The key opens nothing tested; when held to the ear, it hums.*

Their pen paused over the last line. They hadn't written that last line. They'd meant to write only what the inventory card said — *iron key, unmarked, provenance unknown* — but the words about the humming had come out of the pen before they'd decided on them, smooth and certain, in their own handwriting.

Emrys sat very still.

Then they checked the card. The card said: *iron key, unmarked, provenance unknown.* Nothing about humming. Nothing about ears.

They looked at the line again. It was their hand. The slight forward lean of the letters, the way the *h* looped a hair too wide, the ink-weight heavier on downstrokes because Emrys pressed hard when thinking. No one else's hand. Their own.

*Tired,* they told themselves. *You've been copying since the second bell. Your hand knows these forms better than your head does. It invented a detail. Hands do that.*

Hands did not do that. In eleven years of guild service, Emrys had never once embellished an entry, never once guessed, never once written a single word that was not verifiably present on its source document. Accuracy was the one currency they owned outright. It was the reason Corvane let them work the deep vaults alone at night — not trust, exactly, but the institutional equivalent: predictability.

Emrys blotted the line carefully, leaving it in place — striking it would require a correction form, and correction forms required explanation, and explanations went into files — and moved on to Item 4472.

*Item 4472. One jar, sealed, wax black. Contents: last words, assorted. Do not open after dusk.*

That one was real. That one was on the card. Emrys copied it and felt the small, familiar horror of it settle into place alongside all the others, the inventory of a guild that had somehow become the world's pawnshop for things that should not be ownable. Bottled laughter from a funeral that had needed lightening. A mirror that showed the room as it would look if you had never been born. A child's tooth that grew warm near liars. And beneath all of it, filed deeper than everything, the entries that never came up for auction, the ones marked with the grey ribbon — *S-class, restricted, provenance: SEREVAN (VOID)*.

Everyone in the guild knew the word Serevan the way children knew the word for the thing under the bed. It appeared in old ledgers as a place of origin, and then, thirty-odd years ago, it simply stopped appearing, and the entries that bore it were stamped VOID and locked away, and no one spoke of it, and eventually no one remembered why, and eventually — this was the part Emrys found truly obscene — no one remembered to wonder.

But tonight, copying, Emrys's pen kept drifting toward the S-class shelf at the back of Vault Nine. Not their feet. Their pen. Twice more it happened: twice more a detail arrived on the page unbidden, in their own unmistakable hand, describing something the source card did not say.

*Item 4480. One bell-pull, braided hair.* The card ended there. Emrys's pen added: *the hair is brown; it belonged to a girl who rang it for supper every evening until the evening there was no supper.* Emrys stared at the sentence. They did not know any girl who had rung a bell for supper. They had grown up above a tannery where supper, when it came, was announced by smell.

*Item 4483. One map fragment, water-damaged.* Card: *coastline, unidentified.* Pen: *this is the southern shore of Lake Ilvane; the ice sings at midwinter; the children slide on it to hear the bells underneath.*

Emrys put down the pen.

The lamp flame stood perfectly still, which lamps in Vault Nine never did. The cold exhaled through the grates. And somewhere behind the grey-ribboned shelf, in the dark where the VOID things slept, Emrys heard — or imagined, or half-heard, or remembered — a sound like a bell rung under water, patient and far away and horribly familiar, the way a tune is familiar when you realize you have been humming it all day without knowing where you learned it.

They got up. They told themselves they were getting up to fetch a fresh nib. They walked to the back of the vault, past the jars of laughter and the drawer of teeth, to the grey ribbon.

The S-class ledger lay where it always lay, chained at the corner, dust on its cover except for one clean swath, as if it had recently been opened. Emrys told themselves the clean swath was a clerk's routine maintenance. They opened it anyway.

The pages were dense, cramped, beautiful — the hand of a master scrivener, each entry ruled and numbered with a precision the guild itself had abandoned decades ago. Emrys read the header of the first visible page:

*Consolidated Register, Holdings of the Erased Nation. Being a true and complete account of all goods, lands, memories, names, and persons recovered from the territory formerly designated SEREVAN, pursuant to Contract of Purchase dated —*

The date was smudged. Deliberately, Emrys thought. Smudged by a thumb worn into the paper by years of readers who wished not to know.

Emrys turned pages. Orchards, itemized by tree. Bells, itemized by note. Rivers. Roads. Then, further in, the entries changed character, and Emrys felt the temperature of the room change with them:

*Lot 881. Memory, extracted: a woman baking bread on the morning of the Festival of First Thaw. Quality: excellent. Retains warmth. Sold to House Duvane, price —*

*Lot 903. Memory, extracted: a father teaching his son to whistle through cupped hands. Quality: fair. Some grief contamination at the edges. Unsold.*

*Lot 917. Name, personal: "Maren." Extracted intact. Note: bearer consented under debt statute. Name sold separately from bearer. Bearer remains functional.*

*Bearers remain functional.* Emrys read that phrase four times. Functional. As in: still alive. Still walking around. Still able to work off debts. Minus a name. Minus, apparently, whatever else the market required — a face here, a childhood there, a memory of bells under ice —

The pen-detail surfaced again, unbidden, in Emrys's mind, in Emrys's own hand: *the children slide on it to hear the bells underneath.*

"Looking for something, scribe?"

Emrys did not jump. Eleven years of guild training had burned the jump reflex out of them; fear in the Ledger Guild was a liability on your ledger, and the clerks learned to keep it off the books. They turned, slowly, and found Under-Clerk Hask standing between the shelves with a lantern he did not need, his face doing the thing guild faces did — pleasant, blank, weighing.

"Inventory," Emrys said. "Vault Nine. I'm behind."

"The S-register is not on your task list."

"No."

Hask's eyes went to the open register, then to Emrys, then back. He had the patience of a man who had never once needed to hurry. "Curious hand, this," he said, stepping closer, tapping the page without touching it. "Master Ondrel's. Best scrivener the guild ever hanged." He smiled at his own joke, or at the memory of hanging. "You'll see echoes of him everywhere down here, if you stare long enough. Ink gets into people. Makes them think they recognize things." He closed the register. The sound of it was soft and final, like a door in a dream. "Copy your inventory, scribe. Sleep. Come back tomorrow and be accurate."

He stood there until Emrys walked away. Emrys did not look back, because looking back was also a liability on your ledger.

* * *

They finished the inventory. They signed the night-log. They climbed the seven staircases from the vaults to the clerks' dormitory, past the duty board, past the great brass scales in the entrance hall that weighed nothing anyone could see, and they told themselves, with each step, the story the guild preferred: *you are tired, you are imaginative, ink gets into people.*

In the dormitory, twenty narrow beds, nineteen sleepers breathing in the dark, Emrys lay awake and did the arithmetic they did every night — years remaining on indenture, wages against interest, interest against penalties, the elegant machine that ensured the number never shrank — and for the first time in eleven years the arithmetic would not hold their attention. It kept sliding sideways into other numbers. Lot numbers. Dates.

Because there had been one more thing in the S-register. One entry Emrys had seen, in the seconds before Hask's voice, that they had not let themselves read fully then and could not stop reading now, replayed behind their eyes in perfect scrivener's clarity:

*Lot 44. Person, juvenile, female, age two. Assets: complete. Includes name ("Sef"), birth-record, and formative memories, pre-consolidation. Sold pursuant to guardian debt statute. Purchaser: Ledger Guild of Vhalen, general holdings. Price: sufficient.*

*Sufficient.* The guild's coldest word. Sufficient to clear the account. Sufficient to close the file. Sufficient, Emrys thought now, lying rigid in the dark while the dormitory breathed around them, for a guardian to sell a two-year-old's entire self — name, history, the color of a kitchen, the sound of a bell — and walk away owing nothing.

Age two. Female. Name "Sef."

Emrys raised their right hand above their face, into the thin moonlight from the frost-furred window, and turned it slowly, studying it as though it belonged to a stranger. Which, perhaps, it did. It was a scribe's hand, fine-boned, ink-stained at the second knuckle. It was the hand that had written three impossible details tonight in a script no one could mistake. It was, possibly, the hand of someone who had lived two years somewhere else, under another name, before being sold — sold so thoroughly that even the seller's memory of the transaction had been tidied away, because the guild did not sell only the assets, the guild sold the *knowledge of the sale*, that was the elegance of it, that was the kind arithmetic, the debtor forgot the crime and therefore could never contest it—

Emrys pressed the heel of their palm against their eyes until sparks bloomed.

*You are inventing this.* But the thought had no weight. It was like trying to believe the floor wasn't there.

Because here was the thing Emrys had never told anyone, the thing they had folded away at nine years old along with their mother's weeping relief: Emrys Oyelaran had no memories from before the tannery. None. Other children had stories — a fever at four, a dog at five, a fall from a wall. Emrys had a flat white space, and then the smell of hides, and their mother saying *a gift, little one, they want to train you*, and nothing behind any of it, no rooms, no faces, no country of origin their mother would name. When asked, she would go quiet in a particular way, a shut-door way, and say only: *we came from the east. Before your time.*

*Before your time.* Everyone said it strangely. Everyone in the city said it strangely, that whole generation, as if the words had been handled so often they'd worn smooth and slippery.

And the entry said: *age two.* Sold at two. Which would mean the flat white space was not poverty or trauma or a bad childhood poorly remembered. It would mean the space was *inventory*. A cleared lot. Goods removed, premises left empty, ready for the guild to furnish with a new life — a life of debt, and ink, and accuracy, and nights in the cold vaults copying lists of stolen things.

Emrys got up, quietly, barefoot on cold boards, and took their working ledger from their satchel. If they could not sleep, they would work; work was the prayer the guild permitted. They opened to a fresh page to begin tomorrow's drafts early, and their hand — steady, professional, obedient — wrote the date.

And then it wrote, beneath the date, without pause, without permission, in Emrys's own small exact hand:

*I was born in Serevan. My name is Sef. I remember the orchard and the bell and my mother's hands in flour, and I remember the men from the guild coming with papers, and I remember that I was sold, and I remember that everyone agreed to forget it, and I am writing this so that someone, someday, will not.*

The pen lifted. The ink dried, quick in the vault-cold air of the dormitory, black and glistening and undeniable.

Emrys looked at the words for a long time. Every letter was theirs. Every letter was true — they could feel the truth of it the way you feel a stair missing in the dark, the lurch, the drop, the whole body insisting on what the eyes denied. And every letter described a life that had ended before Emrys Oyelaran was born, in a country that no longer existed, recorded in a hand that could not possibly — *could not* — have held a pen before the year of Emrys's birth.

Unless the hand had. Unless the hand had been sold and resold and filed and forgotten, passed down like a tool, like a key that hums when held to the ear, until it came to rest in the grip of a debtor who used it nightly and never once asked what else it remembered.

Somewhere below, deep in Vault Nine, faintly, through seven floors of stone and salt and silence, a bell rang once — or the memory of a bell, or the beginning of one — and stopped.

Emrys blew out the candle, sat back down on the narrow bed, and did not sleep at all.

At dawn, when the duty bell sounded and the dormitory groaned awake, Emrys was already dressed, already waiting at the head of the stairs, already rehearsing the only question that mattered, the question they would ask the archivist with a face like blank vellum, the question that would go straight into a file and summon something worse than Under-Clerk Hask:

*Who sold me?*

They never got to ask it. Because when they reached the entrance hall, the great doors were swinging open on a wall of snow, and the porter was shouting for the elders, and there in the threshold, half-buried in whiteness, stood a small figure shaking snow from its coat — a child, impossibly calm, impossibly unfrozen, who looked up at Emrys across the whole length of the marble hall and said, in a language dead thirty years, in a voice like a bell under ice:

"Sef. You came back."
