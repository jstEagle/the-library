## 4. The Guild's Kind Arithmetic

The fire in Guildmaster Corvane's study burned low and blue, the way he preferred it — alchemical coal, imported at ruinous expense from a province that no longer appeared on maps, which was precisely why it could be had so cheaply. He found there was a lesson in that for anyone willing to learn it: everything in the world became affordable the moment you stopped asking where it came from.

He sat with Emrys Oyelaran's file open across his knees and read it the way he read everything — twice, once for the words and once for what the words were trying to hide.

The file was thin. That itself was an entry worth noting. Twenty-two years in the Ledger Guild's employ, and the boy — no, Corvane corrected himself, not a boy anymore; the file said twenty-six, though files said many things — had accumulated barely three fingers' width of paper. A copyist of middling speed and exceptional accuracy. No disciplinary entries. No debts beyond the original one, which was unusual enough to be interesting, because everyone accrued secondary debts eventually. The guild saw to it. Debt was not a condition of employment here; it was the employment.

*Emrys Oyelaran,* the cover page read, in the clerk's careful hand. *Indentured. Original sum: 340 crowns, 12 shillings, 6 pence. Interest schedule: standard. Term of service: life.*

Corvane turned past the arithmetic to the biographical section, which was nearly blank, and that blankness had always struck him as the most eloquent page in the file. Place of birth: unknown. Parentage: unknown. Found: on the steps of the Guildhall, aged four, wrapped in a blanket of a weave no local clothier recognized, with nothing but a name stitched into the hem in thread the color of old honey.

A foundling. The guild collected them the way harbors collected driftwood. They made excellent clerks, foundlings. No family to write to, no village expecting them home at harvest, no loyalties competing with the ledger. And this particular foundling had turned out to have a gift Corvane had spent years pretending not to notice.

He set the file down, rose, and went to the window.

Snow was falling on Vhalen in thick, soundless curtains, erasing the rooftops one by one, softening the city into something almost gentle. Below, in the courtyard, lamplighters moved between the lamps like slow golden thoughts. Somewhere under all that white, the undercity was flooding as it did every winter, and the poor were doing what the poor always did: drowning quietly, out of sight, at rates the guild had long since calculated into its projections.

Corvane watched the snow and thought about the child.

It had been brought in off the steps at dusk, half-frozen, speaking a language that had made the door-warden go pale and fetch three elders before he would even touch it. Corvane had listened from the gallery above the entry hall, unseen, while the child stood dripping in a puddle of snowmelt and spoke — clearly, fluently, without hesitation — in a tongue that had not been spoken by any living mouth in thirty-one years.

Serevani. Pre-Kingdom dialect, if his ear served, and his ear served; he had paid handsomely over the decades to make certain it did.

The elders had reacted exactly as he'd expected them to. Fear first, wonder second, greed third — the guild's liturgy, in proper order. Elder Massech had wanted the child locked in the cold vaults until it could be determined whether it was a vessel, a spy, or a symptom. Elder Ivenna had wanted it drowned quietly and the incident entered into the record as a delivery of salvage. Old habits. Thirty years ago, when the Serevan transaction had still been settling through the world's ledgers like a stone settling through deep water, such things had needed doing, and the men who had done them had risen far.

Corvane wanted neither vault nor river. Corvane wanted a chair, a warm room, a bowl of soup, and time.

Because the child had done something else, something the elders in their fright had failed to properly weigh. It had looked up through the crowd of grey-robed clerks — dozens of faces, all of them strangers, all of them interchangeable in their guild sameness — and it had gone directly, unerringly, to Emrys Oyelaran. And it had called him *Sef.*

Corvane had reviewed the entry logs afterward. No member of the guild, living or dead, had ever recorded the name Sef in connection with the foundling. The child had known something the ledger did not. In Corvane's long experience, that was the single most valuable category of knowledge in existence.

He returned to his desk and opened the lower drawer, the locked one, the one that held the file within the file.

*

Every clerk in the Ledger Guild believed they understood the institution's business. They believed the guild bought and sold debts, futures, liens on harvests not yet planted, widows' pensions, ships not yet built. This was true, in the way that the visible floors of a building are true. But the guild had cellars, and the cellars had cellars, and at the very bottom of all that descending architecture was the Quiet Exchange — the trade in extracted memory.

Corvane had not invented it. He had inherited it, the way one inherits a house with a ghost already in residence. The founding partners had discovered, some ninety years ago, that memory could be drawn from a living mind the way sap is drawn from a tree — slowly, carefully, with the right instruments and the right incisions — and that once drawn, it could be decanted, sealed, sold. A vial of a mother's voice. A phial of a first snowfall. The taste of a specific summer afternoon, bottled for a buyer who had never had a childhood worth keeping.

The market had been modest at first. Curiosities. Novelties for the bored rich.

And then, thirty-one years ago, the founding partners had been approached by a delegation from a country none of their maps had ever bothered to render in much detail — a country of orchards and bells and long amber evenings, tucked into a fold of the world where the mountains doubled back on themselves. The delegation had come with a proposition, and the proposition had been desperate, and desperation, Corvane had learned, was the most reliable currency of all.

They had offered to sell their country's existence.

Not its land — the land remained, technically, though no road led to it now and no map acknowledged it. Not its people — or rather, not their bodies, which had been dispersed across the world like seeds on a wind, each one waking in a foreign place with a hole where their history had been. What they had sold was the *record*. The collective memory of a nation, extracted whole, entry by entry, year by year, and transferred into the guild's deepest vaults in exchange for debts forgiven, enemies appeased, a catastrophe whose nature Corvane had never fully been briefed on averted.

The founding partners had called it the greatest acquisition in commercial history. They had also, Corvane noted, all died badly — but then, great acquisitions tended to collect interest of every kind.

What remained, after the transfer, was this: a country erased, its people scattered and hollowed, walking the world with names they couldn't explain and griefs they couldn't source. And a vault full of Serevan — its orchards, its bells, its kings and kitchens and lullabies — divided into fragments, catalogued, and sold, over thirty years, in pieces so small that no buyer ever held enough of it to understand what they were holding.

That had been the genius of the operation, Corvane thought. Not the extraction. The *portioning.* Grief in bulk is unbearable; grief by the dram is a luxury good.

He unlocked the inner drawer now and took out the fragment catalogue — not the public one, the true one — and turned to the pages marked with the sigil of restricted provenance. There were perhaps four hundred fragments in the deep vaults that had never been released to market. The founding partners' private reserve. Core memories. Load-bearing ones. The kind that, if reassembled, might begin to remember being a country.

And somewhere among those four hundred, Corvane was increasingly certain, was a fragment that contained a face he had seen every working day for two decades.

He had first suspected it eleven years ago, watching Emrys Oyelaran copy an inventory of impossible things with that unnerving fluency of his. The boy transcribed descriptions of Serevani artifacts — a key to nowhere, a jar of last words, bottled laughter with a vintage date — and his hand never hesitated, never stumbled over the unfamiliar spellings, never required the pronunciation glosses that stumped senior clerks. As if the language were not unfamiliar at all. As if it were merely *rusty.*

Then there was the matter of the handwriting.

Corvane pulled a folio from the drawer: facsimiles of ledger entries recovered from the pre-transfer archive, entries made in Serevan's own record-houses before the country was unwritten. He laid one beside a sample of Emrys's current script. Different hands, obviously — twenty-six years apart, different inks, different purposes. But the terminal descenders curved the same way. The numerals leaned at the same angle, five degrees past vertical, like soldiers leaning into wind. Coincidence produced such things occasionally. Corvane had built a career on not believing in coincidence.

And now a child had walked out of a snowstorm speaking the dead language and named him *Sef.*

Corvane closed the folio and sat for a long moment in the blue light of the alchemical fire, listening to the snow press against the windows like something reading over his shoulder.

*

There were, he reflected, only three explanations for the child.

One: it was a survivor's offspring, raised on the old tongue in some hidden household of the scattered — possible, but the dialect was wrong for transmission; it was too archaic, too complete, the speech of someone who had *lived* inside the language rather than inherited shards of it.

Two: it was a rival's instrument. The Nightmarket fences had grown bold in recent years; Vesha and her ilk had been buying Serevani fragments faster than the guild's own brokers, assembling who-knew-what out of the pieces. A child trained as a key, sent to find the lock. Possible. Worth considering.

Three — and Corvane felt the truth of it settle over him with the particular chill of a correct answer — the child was not carrying memories the way a messenger carries letters. The child was carrying them the way a hive carries honey. Compiled. Concentrated. Someone — something — had gathered the lost children's portion of Serevan and given it legs.

Which meant the child was not a curiosity.

The child was a *consolidation event.* Every fragment it carried was a fragment the guild did not. If it fell into the wrong hands — the Hollow King's hands, say, though Corvane set that thought carefully aside, the way one sets aside correspondence that is better left unread — or even merely into the Nightmarket's diffuse and greedy possession, thirty years of careful portioning could begin to reverse itself. Memory wanted, Corvane had learned, to return to its people. It was the one commodity with a homing instinct. Left long enough in proximity to its origin, it crawled home like water finding a cracked vessel.

The child had to be secured. Not harmed — harm degraded the merchandise, and besides, Corvane had never cared for cruelty as policy; it was imprecise, and precision was the whole of his religion. Secured. Fed. Warmed. Made comfortable and dependent and, above all, *legible.* Everything the child remembered belonged, by every right the guild recognized, in a ledger.

And Emrys Oyelaran?

Corvane turned back to the thin file and read it a third time, hunting the thing beneath the words.

Here was the difficulty, and he permitted himself to savor difficulties the way other men savored wine. If the foundling was what the evidence suggested — born inside Serevan before the transfer, sold young, sold *early*, one of the first extractions, which would explain the blankness of the file and the blanket of foreign weave and the name that wasn't the name — then Emrys Oyelaran was not a clerk at all. Emrys was an asset. Possibly the single most valuable asset the guild had ever possessed without knowing it: a native of the erased country, alive, lucid, and already bound in lifelong debt.

Someone had sold the child's past to pay a debt, and the debt had landed, through channels Corvane had never been able to trace to their source, in the guild's own books. The elegant cruelty of it almost moved him. The guild owned a piece of Serevan that walked and talked and copied inventories, and for twenty-two years it had been using that piece of Serevan to make fair copies of shipping manifests.

Waste, Corvane thought. There was no sin he took more seriously than waste.

But assets that discover themselves become liabilities. That was the iron law beneath all the kinder laws. The moment Emrys understood what he was — and the child, damn it, the child had already begun the telling, had already spoken the name *Sef* into the guild's halls — every careful structure Corvane maintained would be at risk. A man who learns he has been robbed does not ask for compensation. He asks for restitution. And restitution, in this case, meant four hundred restricted fragments, a vault, a transaction thirty-one years deep, and the unraveling of everything.

Unless the discovery could be managed. Channeled. Priced.

Corvane smiled faintly at the fire. There was a version of this that worked beautifully. There always was. You did not forbid the man his questions; you *sold* him the answers. You let his debt do what debt did best — you let it grow until the only way out was through. Forgiveness was the guild's most potent instrument, more potent than any enforcer, because forgiveness purchased not obedience but gratitude, and gratitude policed itself.

He would offer Emrys Oyelaran his freedom. All of it. Three hundred and forty crowns, twelve shillings, six pence, and twenty-two years of interest, struck from the book in a single stroke of the master pen.

In exchange: the child's memories, extracted clean and complete, consigned to the Quiet Exchange. And afterward — why, afterward the guild would need someone fluent in the old tongue, someone with native instincts for the material, someone trustworthy, to help catalogue the largest acquisition in its history. A position of real standing. A name in the senior rolls. Perhaps, in time, a seat at the long table.

People imagined that men like Corvane threatened. Men like Corvane rarely needed to. One simply arranged the world so that every road a person might walk led through one's own front hall, and then one waited, hospitably, with the kettle on.

He drew a fresh sheet of paper toward himself, dipped his pen, and wrote the order in his own hand, because some instructions were too important to trust to a clerk's discretion:

*The child recovered from the north steps this date is to be detained in the east infirmary wing. Kindly treated. Warmly fed. No visitors save by my seal. The child is to be entered in the provisional register as: salvage, organic, provenance pending. Assessment of contents to follow.*

He paused, pen hovering, and added:

*Clerk Emrys Oyelaran is to be assigned to the child's care. He has a gentle manner with difficult texts.*

There. Let the foundling believe it was kindness. Kindness was simply leverage that hadn't come due yet.

Corvane blotted the page, sealed it, and rang for the night porter. Through the window, the snow went on falling, patient and total, covering the city the way a ledger covers a life — line by line, entry by entry, until nothing underneath could be seen at all, and the whiteness looked, to anyone foolish enough to look, exactly like innocence.

He was pouring the last of the blue-coal warmth into his evening calculations when the porter scratched at the door and reported, breathless, that there was a complication: the child had refused to sleep, refused to eat, and had asked — in perfect guild-tongue, which it apparently also spoke — a question that had frightened the infirmary attendants out of their wits.

Corvane set down his pen. "And what question," he said mildly, "does a child ask that frightens trained attendants?"

The porter swallowed. "It asked, sir, how much the guild would charge to sell a person their own name back."

For a moment the only sound was the fire ticking through its blue combustion, and the snow against the glass, and somewhere far below, in the vaults, the faint and endless murmur of a country remembering itself in the dark.

Corvane folded his hands.

"Double the watch on the infirmary," he said. "And send for Clerk Oyelaran at first light."
