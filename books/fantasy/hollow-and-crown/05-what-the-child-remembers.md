## 5. What the Child Remembers

The guild slept badly that night, and so did Emrys.

Snow kept coming down past the high windows in slow, deliberate spirals, as though the sky were copying out its own inventory, line by line. Emrys lay in the narrow cot of the junior scribes' dormitory and listened to the building settle around them: the tick of cooling pipes, the groan of the archive stairs under some insomniac clerk's feet, the far-off clank of the vault doors being checked and rechecked. The child was somewhere below them, three floors down, behind a door with a new lock on it. Emrys had watched the locksmith install it himself. They had watched the elders stand around the door afterward like men around a well they suspected was poisoned.

*No living tongue should know it,* Elder Massek had said, in the voice he used for figures that refused to balance.

But Emrys knew it. That was the thing they had not said aloud to anyone, could barely say aloud to themselves. When the child had spoken — that soft, ringing language full of open vowels and endings that fell away like leaves letting go of branches — something in Emrys's chest had turned over, the way a key turns in a lock that has been painted shut for years. Not understanding. Understanding would have been too clean. It had been closer to *recognition*, the way you recognize a smell from a house you cannot remember ever entering.

And then the name. *Sef.* Spoken in that impossible tongue, aimed at Emrys across the entry hall like an arrow finding the one gap in armor.

Emrys had gone by Emrys Oyelaran since the guild took them in at nine years old, ink-stained and debt-bound, their parents' obligations transferred neatly into their name like a lien on a field. Before that there had been a foundling registry, a matron with cold hands, a number instead of a birthday. There had never been a *Sef*. And yet when the child said it, Emrys's body had answered before their mind could object — a half-step forward, a breath drawn to reply — and Elder Massek had looked at them with an expression Emrys spent the rest of the day trying not to think about.

It had not been surprise. It had been confirmation.

Emrys rose, dressed in the dark, and went down.

* * *

The corridor outside the guest room — it was not called a cell; nothing in the guild was ever called what it was — smelled of lamp oil and old snow. Two enforcers were posted at the stairhead, which was two more than any foundling child warranted, and both of them looked through Emrys as though a scribe were furniture. That was the small mercy of being a Ledger man: you became invisible by becoming ordinary. Emrys carried a sheaf of blank folios and a stoppered inkhorn, the uniform of someone with tedious business, and no one stopped tedious business anywhere in Vhalen.

The lock was newer than the door. Emrys stood before it a long moment, telling themselves they were only doing their job — the elders had assigned no such job, but the guild ran on the fiction that everything anyone did was their job — and then, because lying to themselves had never been among their talents, they admitted it: they wanted to hear the language again. They wanted it the way a starving man wants bread he has never tasted.

They knocked softly. "Ledger work," they said. "Inventory of the foundling's effects."

Silence. Then, from within, in accented but fluent trade-tongue: "I don't have effects."

Emrys opened the door.

The room was a guest room in the way a cage is a room. A bed, a basin, a window too high to see out of, a single lamp turned low. The child sat cross-legged on the bed with their back straight, watching the door the way small animals watch gaps in hedges: ready to be through it. Up close, in lamplight, the child resolved into particulars — perhaps eight years old, perhaps ten; hair dark and snow-damp still at the tips; a face that was ordinary in every feature and somehow entirely wrong for this city, this century, this world. Their coat was stitched in a style Emrys had never seen, close-fitted, dyed a deep orchard-red, mended many times with thread that didn't quite match.

"You're the one I called," the child said. Not a question. An accusation, almost.

"I'm the one who copies inventories," Emrys said. "That's all I am here for."

"That's not true." The child said it without heat, the way you'd note that a ledger didn't balance. "You came back. You've been standing outside my door twice tonight already. First time you only breathed. This time you knocked." They patted the foot of the bed. "Sit. I'm Iri. I know you're tired. You walk like someone carrying water in both hands."

Emrys did not sit. Then they sat.

"How do you know our tongue?" they asked. "No one speaks it. Scholars have tried. There are fragments in the deep archive, glossaries of a dead language, and even those disagree—"

"Because it isn't dead," Iri said. "It's just very far away. Like a bell after the hill takes it. You can't hear it anymore, but the bell is still ringing." They tilted their head. "You understood me before. In the hall. Your mouth knew the shape of the words before your mind caught up. I saw it."

"I don't know what you're talking about."

"Yes you do." No cruelty in it. Only patience, terrible in someone so small. "You want me to tell you about the orchards?"

Emrys should have left then. They knew the shape of the trap the way they knew the shape of every ledger — the moment where a debt begins accruing interest. Instead they heard themselves say, "There are no orchards. Not here. Not anywhere near here. The valley's all slate and pine."

"Not here," Iri agreed. "There."

And they told it.

* * *

They spoke first in trade-tongue, and then, as the night deepened and the lamp burned lower, they slipped into the other language without seeming to notice, and Emrys — who should not have understood a word — understood every word, and felt the floor of their life tilt beneath them like a deck in weather.

"There were terraces cut into the south face of the mountain," Iri said, hands moving as they spoke, shaping the slopes in the air. "Apple above, plum below, and between them the bee-houses, white boxes with blue doors, because blue keeps the wasps honest. In harvest month everyone went up. Everyone. The judges closed the courts early. The bells rang the *working peal* — not the mourning peal, not the feast peal, the working one, seven strokes and a rest, seven strokes and a rest, so you could keep count of your rows by ear if your hands were full." Iri's eyes had gone somewhere else entirely. "The apples came off the branch with a sound like a sigh. And at the top of the last terrace there was a tree older than the kingdom, so old nobody claimed it, and its fruit belonged to whoever was hungry. That was law. Older than law. The hungry-tree law."

"Bells," Emrys said. Their voice came out strange. "You said bells."

"Nine of them in the capital tower. Each one named. The smallest was called *Mercy* and the biggest was called *Answer*, and there was one in between — cracked, they never fixed it because the crack gave it a voice like a grandmother laughing — that one was called *Remembrance*." Iri frowned suddenly, the first shadow crossing their face. "I shouldn't remember the names. I wasn't born yet when—" They stopped. Started again, carefully, like someone walking on river ice. "I remember things I wasn't there for. That's how I know you were there. Because some of what I remember is *you*."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere above them, the guild's pipes knocked, once, like a fist on a door.

"I was never anywhere," Emrys said. "I was born in the foundling ward on Candle Street. I have the registry mark to prove it. Ink doesn't lie."

"Ink lies constantly," Iri said. "Ink is how they killed my country."

Emrys's breath stopped. "What did you say?"

But Iri had turned on the bed to face them fully, knees drawn up, arms wrapped around them, and their expression was no longer a child describing fruit. It was older than the body wearing it.

"Your name was Sef," they said. "Sef Oyelaran. You lived on Bellwether Row, third house past the tannery, the one with the crooked chimney. You had a mother who sang off-key on purpose because it made your father laugh, and a father who pressed flowers in the big dictionary because he said important words needed company. You broke your arm falling out of the plum terrace when you were six, and the bone-setter scolded you, and your mother cried, and you were prouder of the scar than anything until the day you died." Iri's voice dropped. "Until the day everyone died, or almost. Until the day the country went quiet."

"I don't—" Emrys's hands had gone cold. "I don't remember any of that."

"No," Iri said gently. "You wouldn't. It was taken. Sold. I can always tell, with people like you. There's a smooth place inside, where something used to be. Like a riverbed after the water's been diverted. The stones are still there. The shape of the water is still there. Just no water." They reached out and touched Emrys's wrist, two fingers, light as a moth landing. "You're full of smooth places, Sef. More than anyone I've ever met. Someone paid a great deal for you."

The word *paid* landed in the room and stayed there, ringing like the cracked bell, like *Remembrance*.

"The guild," Emrys said slowly. "The guild deals in... unusual acquisitions. Impossible things. Bottled laughter. Keys to nowhere. I copy the entries. I've copied ten thousand entries and never once asked where things come from, because asking is how debts get longer." They laughed, and it came out wrong. "You're saying I'm an entry."

"I'm saying you're a *receipt*," Iri said. "Someone got something for you. And whatever they got, the guild holds the paper."

Outside, the storm had thickened; snow pressed against the high window like a face trying to look in. Emrys thought of the ledgers in the deep archive, the ones in the locked cases, the ones written in a hand that had made their stomach drop the day before — a hand identical to their own, dating entries sixty years old. They had told themselves it was coincidence. Clerks' hands converge; ink teaches everyone the same discipline. They had told themselves many things.

"If I was there," Emrys heard themselves ask, and hated the hunger in their own voice, and could not stop it, "what was it like? Before. Tell me what it was like."

Iri smiled, and for a moment they looked exactly their age, lit from inside by something the lamp couldn't account for.

"Warm," they said. "Not hot-warm. Warm like bread. Warm like a hand. The whole country was warm, even in winter, because we heated our houses with the ovens and the ovens were always going, because feeding people was the first work and the last work of every day. The streets smelled of woodsmoke and plums and wet wool. In the evenings the bells rang the homecoming peal, and everyone on the street would pause — just a beat, mid-stride, mid-sentence — to let the sound finish, and then go on. My—" the frown again, the careful walking-on-ice look, "—someone's grandmother used to say the country didn't have walls, it had bells. Anything inside the sound was home."

"And the erasure? What happened?"

Iri's smile went out like a pinched wick.

"They didn't send soldiers," they said. "Soldiers leave ruins, and ruins can be rebuilt, and names carved on stones can be read. They sent clerks." The word fell between them, and Emrys felt it land in their own chest like a stone dropped down a well. "Entry by entry. Deed by deed. Birth record, tax roll, boundary stone, grave register. Every piece of paper that said Serevan existed, gathered up and struck through, until the country had no receipts left, and a thing with no receipts..." Iri shrugged, small and terrible. "...was never there at all. The world forgot because the paperwork told it to. My people didn't die, Sef. They were *filed*."

Emrys sat very still. Ten thousand entries moved behind their eyes: bottled laughter, jarred last words, keys to nowhere, a vial labeled *the smell of rain on a foreign flag*. All of it catalogued, appraised, shelved. All of it, they understood now, *inventory*. The impossible things were not impossible. They were pieces of a country, broken up for sale like a wrecked ship sold plank by plank, and Emrys had spent eleven years lovingly dusting the wreckage.

"I need to look at something," they said, standing, and their legs nearly failed them, and Iri watched them with those too-old eyes and did not laugh.

"The deep archive," Iri said. Not a question.

"How do you know—"

"Because that's where they keep the dead hand," Iri said. "I've been listening to this building since I got here. Buildings talk, if they're old enough. Yours is very old, and very guilty." They lay back on the bed, pulled the thin blanket up, and regarded Emrys from under the edge of it. "Go and look. But come back. Whatever you find down there — come back and tell me. I've been alone in the wrong country for a long time, and you're the only thing in it that smells like mine."

Emrys paused at the door, hand on the latch, and did not trust themselves to speak. Behind them, quietly, in the language that no living tongue should know, Iri said something short and soft — four syllables, rising, falling — and Emrys's mouth shaped the translation without their permission, the way a scar aches before rain:

*Come home safe.*

They fled down the corridor, past the enforcers who did not see them, up the stairs, into the stacks of the deep archive where the air was cold as a held breath — and stopped dead at the reference desk, because the case that held the oldest ledgers stood open, its lock hanging loose, and the ledgers themselves were gone.

In their place, centered on the shelf with the neatness of a man who believed the world was a filing system, was a single card in Guildmaster Corvane's handwriting:

*Sef — Some questions are best asked of me directly. My office. Morning. — C.*

Below it, in smaller script, almost kind:

*Sleep well. You'll want your rest.*
