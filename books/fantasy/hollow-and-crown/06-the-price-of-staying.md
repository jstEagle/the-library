## 6. The Price of Staying

The summons came at the hour when the guild's lamps were trimmed low and the ink in every well had gone thick as tar — the hour Emrys liked best, when the copying halls emptied and the only sound was the settling of paper, a soft continuous sigh, like a building breathing out.

A junior clerk, one of the pale twins who never spoke above a whisper, stood at the end of Emrys's row with a folded slip of grey vellum.

"Guildmaster Corvane requests your presence," the clerk murmured. "The upper study. Now."

Emrys looked down at the page they'd been copying — an inventory of forty-one jars of *last words*, salvaged from somewhere the entry did not name — and felt the familiar cold slide through their stomach, the feeling that arrived whenever the guild noticed them too closely. Debtors were meant to be invisible. Invisible debtors were safe debtors. But no one was summoned to the upper study for anything good.

"Tell him I'm finishing a line," Emrys said.

"He said *now*."

Of course he had. Emrys capped the ink, blotted the pen, and set the page square with the desk's edge — small rituals, small controls — and followed the twin up the spiral stair.

* * *

Corvane's study occupied the tower's crown, and it was nothing like the rest of the guild. Where the copying halls were cold and grey as a winter river, the study was warm — a fire behind a brass screen, a carpet deep enough to swallow footsteps, shelves of ledgers bound in leather the color of dried blood. The windows were diamond-paned and frosted at the corners, and beyond them Vhalen's rooftops descended in white terraces toward the frozen harbor. Snow fell past the glass in long slow spirals, unhurried, indifferent.

Corvane sat behind a desk of black oak, writing. He did not look up when Emrys entered. He let the silence stretch — Emrys knew this technique, had seen it used on debtors in the assessment rooms: make them wait, let them fill the quiet with confessions. Emrys folded their hands and waited instead, watching the firelight move on the brass screen like something swimming.

At last Corvane set down his pen. He was a broad man gone soft at the edges, with a face built for kindness that had never quite committed to it — heavy brows, a mouth that curved easily but without warmth, eyes the pale grey of ledger-paper. He wore no guild colors. He didn't need to. Everything in the room already belonged to him.

"Sef Oyelaran," he said.

Emrys's breath caught — barely, but Corvane caught it too; his mouth curved a fraction deeper.

"That surprises you. It shouldn't. I read files." He tapped a folder on the desk, thin, dog-eared. "Yours is one of the thinner ones. That's not an insult. Thin files mean careful people. You've been with us eleven years, you've never missed a payment, never petitioned for reduction, never once asked what happens to the things you copy after they leave your desk. Do you know how rare that is? Most debtors ask. They ask constantly. Asking is how they convince themselves they're more than what they owe." He leaned back. "You've never asked me anything."

"You've never answered anyone," Emrys said, before they could stop themselves.

The silence that followed had teeth. Then Corvane laughed — a short, genuine-sounding bark, which was somehow worse than anger.

"There it is. There's the spine. Eleven years of *yes, Guildmaster* and *no, Guildmaster*, and underneath it all, a tongue." He rose and crossed to the window, hands clasped behind his back, a man admiring his own view. "Do you know why I keep careful people, Sef? Because careless people steal. Careless people talk. Careless people look at a jar of last words and wonder whose they are, and wondering is the first step toward taking. But careful people—" he glanced back over his shoulder— "careful people understand value. And I have something I need valued."

"I'm a copyist," Emrys said. "Third rank. I copy inventories."

"You're a scribe born inside a country that no longer exists, who just watched a child walk out of a snowstorm speaking its dead language and call you by a name that isn't in any file I own." Corvane turned fully now, and the firelight made his shadow enormous against the shelves. "Sit down, Sef. Let's talk about what you are worth."

* * *

Emrys sat. The chair was upholstered — actual padding, actual fabric — and the luxury of it was its own kind of threat. People like Emrys did not sit in padded chairs. Padded chairs were where decisions got made about people like Emrys.

"The child," Corvane began, "is currently housed in the east annex. Warm room, meals, a cot. We are not monsters, whatever you may have heard." He returned to his desk but did not sit; he stood over it, palms flat on the black oak, and Emrys understood suddenly that everything in this room was arranged for standing over — the desk, the window, the height of the tower itself. "The child speaks pre-consolidation Serevani. Fluent. Native. Which should be impossible, because Serevan has been gone for thirty-one years, and everyone who could have taught that language to a child has been gone with it."

"People remember," Emrys said carefully. "There are fragments. The guild sells them."

"We sell *inventories* of them," Corvane corrected mildly. "Fragments degrade. A memory traded five times is a rumor of a rumor. But a native speaker — a living, breathing, self-generating source —" His hands pressed flatter against the wood. "Do you understand what walked into our lobby three nights ago, Sef? Not a child. A *mint*. Fresh coin, struck daily, in a currency we thought had stopped circulating."

"She's six years old," Emrys said.

"She's an asset approximately six years old." Corvane waved this away as if the distinction were pedantry. "And here is where you come in." He opened the thin folder and turned it so Emrys could see. Their own name stared back at them in the guild's cramped clerical hand — *Oyelaran, S.* — followed by figures Emrys knew by heart because they haunted every hour of their life. Principal. Interest. Accrued penalties. A number so large it had stopped meaning anything years ago and become simply weather, simply climate, the air they breathed.

"Your debt stands at four hundred and twelve marks, nine silver, and change," Corvane said. "At your current wage and the current penalty schedule, you will die owing us. That is not cruelty; that is arithmetic. Most debtors die owing us. But I am prepared to offer you a different arithmetic." He slid a single sheet across the desk. It was a standard remission form, the kind Emrys had copied a hundred times for other people, other lives, and had never once imagined holding. The debtor line was blank. The consideration line was blank. Both waited for ink.

"Help us work with the child," Corvane said. "You speak some Serevani — don't look surprised, your file notes your aptitude for dead orthographies, and the child responded to you before she responded to any of my staff. She trusts you. Trust accelerates extraction. Sit with her. Talk with her. Let her remember out loud while our assessors listen and record. Six months of sessions, and this—" he tapped the remission form— "is signed, sealed, and burned. You walk out of this guild owing nothing to anyone. You start again at zero, with a reference letter from the Guildmaster of Vhalen himself."

The fire ticked. Snow spiraled past the windows. Somewhere below, faint as a pulse, Emrys could hear the night shift beginning — pens scratching, pages turning, the great machine of the guild digesting the world into entries.

And beneath all of it, quieter than any of it, Emrys heard the child's voice from three nights ago, thin and clear through the storm:

*Sef. Sef, you came back.*

* * *

They had been given no time to prepare for the offer, and Emrys understood that too — Corvane wanted the answer while the chair was still warm under them, while the number four hundred and twelve marks, nine silver, and change was still ringing in their ears like a struck bell.

Because gods, the bell rang.

Eleven years. Eleven years of rising before dawn to be at a desk by the sixth bell, of copying inventories of impossible things until their wrist ached and their eyes swam, of eating the guild's thin soup and sleeping in the debtors' dormitory where thirty bodies breathed in the dark and nobody dreamed aloud because dreams might count as assets someday. Eleven years of watching other debtors break — a woman two rows over who had wept openly for a week after her mother died because she couldn't afford the fee to leave the guild for the funeral, and then had stopped weeping all at once, mid-sob, because grief cost energy and energy cost wages. Eleven years of being *owed for*. Of being, in the guild's truest language, an entry.

Zero. The word was a door left open in a wall Emrys had believed solid all their life.

Walk out owing nothing. Walk out with the frost off their name. Never again feel the ledger's weight settle on their shoulders each morning like wet wool. Never again copy someone else's bottled laughter and wonder if laughter could be sold twice.

And all it would cost was sitting with a six-year-old girl — a girl who had already, impossibly, chosen them — while men with vials listened at the door and took her apart one remembered orchard at a time.

Emrys looked at the remission form. The blank lines seemed to breathe.

"You said six months of sessions," they said. Their voice came out steadier than they felt. "What do the sessions involve, precisely?"

"Conversation," Corvane said smoothly. "Recollection. The child talks; we transcribe. Memory is most stable when it's spoken — you'd know that if you'd ever wondered what happens to the things you copy. Spoken memories can be drawn whole. Written ones fragment." He smiled. "You'd be doing her a service, really. Unburdening her. A mind that size, in a body that small — it must ache. All those orchards and bells crammed into a skull built for games and porridge. We'd be relieving her."

"And after the six months? When she's been... relieved?"

Corvane's smile didn't move, but something behind it repositioned itself, the way ice shifts on a river before it breaks.

"After," he said, "the guild retains custody per standard foundling statutes. Her future disposition will be assessed on her remaining value." He saw Emrys's face and raised a hand, almost paternal. "Sef. Look at me. I am not asking you to hurt her. I am asking you to help her be useful. There is no gentler fate in this world than being useful. Useless things get discarded. Useful things get *kept*." He tapped the remission form again. "Take it. Read it tonight. Give me your answer by tomorrow's sixth bell."

He turned back to the window then, dismissal complete, a man already thinking about other entries. Emrys took the form — what else could they do, refuse to touch it? — and walked to the door on legs that felt borrowed.

"One question," Emrys said, hand on the latch.

Corvane didn't turn. "Yes?"

"The name she called me. Sef. It isn't in your files, you said. Whose file is it in?"

For the first time since Emrys had entered the study, Guildmaster Corvane looked genuinely interested — a flicker of appetite, quick and cold, like a fish turning under ice.

"Now *that*," he said, "is exactly the kind of question that makes you valuable. Goodnight, Sef Oyelaran. Sleep on zero. It's a very comfortable number."

* * *

Emrys did not go back to the copying hall. They went down instead, down the spiral stair and along the gallery above the great lobby, where the storm still hurled itself against the tall doors, and stopped at the rail to look at the place where the child had appeared.

The floor there had been scrubbed. The puddle of meltwater was gone, the scatter of snow, the small wet footprints. Someone had even polished away the scuff-marks where the enforcers had dragged her — gently, the records would say later, *gently* — into custody. The marble gleamed, innocent as an unwritten page.

That was the guild's deepest magic, Emrys thought. Not the jars, not the keys, not the bottled laughter. This. The ability to clean a floor until it looked like nothing had ever happened on it. To enter a thing into a ledger and thereby make it orderly, owned, *managed* — and orderliness was only one remove from erasure. Emrys had spent eleven years copying inventories of impossible things and had never once asked where the impossibilities went when the guild finished with them. Now they knew. They went here, to a scrubbed floor in a warm tower, into a thin folder in a soft-handed man's keeping, into *remaining value*.

They went nowhere. That was the trick. Nothing the guild held ever went anywhere again.

Emrys's hands were shaking. They pressed them flat against the cold rail and made themselves breathe, in through the nose, out slow, the way you calmed yourself before a difficult entry — because this was a difficult entry, wasn't it. Four hundred and twelve marks, nine silver, and change, weighed against a child's orchards. A life weighed against a life. The guild's favorite arithmetic, and here it was running itself in Emrys's own chest, cold and patient, tallying.

*Take it,* said the part of them that had eaten eleven years of thin soup. *Six months. Talk to her. What does talking cost? You can steer it — give them the bells, the weather, the songs, the harmless things. Stall the rest. Sign the form, buy your freedom, and then find a way to buy hers.*

*You'll sign the form,* said another voice, older and less comfortable, *and then you'll watch them take the bells anyway, and then the orchards, and then whatever's under the orchards, and you'll tell yourself each time that you're steering, right up until there's nothing left to steer and she looks at you with a stranger's eyes and says* Sef? *in a language only you both remember, and doesn't know why.*

Emrys had seen what extraction left behind. Everyone in the guild had, if they paid attention — the debtors who came back from the assessment rooms smooth-faced and placid, missing pieces they couldn't name, smiling at their own children with polite blankness, saying *I know I love you* the way you'd say *I know the capital's population*, information rather than blood. The guild called it *lightening the load*. The debtors' dormitory called those beds the empty ones, because whoever slept in them afterward was never quite the person who'd lain down.

And Iri — the name had come to Emrys unbidden sometime in the last three days, and fit the child so naturally it frightened them — Iri was *made* of what those rooms took. She was what remained when a country was lightened of its load. She was the residue, walking around in a small coat, calling a stranger by a name out of a burned country.

If Emrys helped them draw her further, what would be left at the end of six months? A smooth-faced little girl who spoke no Serevani at all, who looked at Emrys with polite blankness?

Who looked at Emrys and saw no one at all?

"No," Emrys said aloud, to the empty lobby, to the scrubbed floor, to the storm hammering the doors. It echoed strangely in the vaulted dark. Then, softer, honestly: "I don't know."

Because that was the truth, and the truth deserved saying even alone, even in the dark. They didn't know. The bell of *zero* was still ringing in them, sweet and terrible, and protectiveness was easy at midnight in an empty hall with no form in hand. Tomorrow, with Corvane's pale eyes waiting and the remission form on the desk between them, blank lines breathing — tomorrow would be the real test.

Emrys pushed off the rail and went down to the dormitory, past the rows of sleeping debtors, to their narrow bunk, and lay awake holding the folded form against their chest like a stone.

* * *

Sleep came eventually, shallow and full of water — dreams of a lake under ice, though Emrys had never seen such a lake, and bells ringing somewhere far beneath the surface, though Emrys had never heard such bells. In the dream they stood on the ice trying to read a ledger page that kept melting through their fingers, the ink swimming off the page and down through the white into the dark water, and beneath the ice something vast turned over slowly, attentive, patient, waiting to see what they would write.

They woke before the sixth bell, which never happened, and dressed in the dark, and did not go to the copying hall.

They went to the east annex.

The corridor outside Iri's room was supposed to be watched — Corvane would not house a mint unguarded — but the watcher was a young enforcer named Pell, third-rank, who owed Emrys for six months of forged attendance entries during his own debt-days, and who looked at Emrys's face now and correctly decided not to know it.

"She's asleep," Pell said. "Assessment team comes at nine."

"I know," Emrys said. "I need ten minutes."

Pell studied the ceiling, the wall, a fascinating crack in the plaster. "Ten minutes," he said to no one, and unlocked the door.

The room inside was warm and bare: cot, blanket, basin, a single lamp turned low. A tray with an untouched supper. And in the corner farthest from the door, knees drawn up, back to the wall, sat the child — awake, of course she was awake, her eyes catching the lamplight like a cat's — and when she saw who had come in, her whole small body changed, the way a clenched fist changes when it's allowed to open.

"*Sef*," she whispered, in that language that should have been dust for thirty years, and scrambled up, and hit Emrys at waist-height hard enough to stagger them, arms locked around their ribs, face buried in their shirt. "You came back. You always come back. You promised."

Emrys knelt, which put them nearly at her eye level, and held her shoulders, and looked at her — at the fine bones of her face, the snow-pale skin, the eyes that were old in a way no six-year-old's eyes should be permitted.

"Iri," Emrys said, and her breath hitched at her own name. "Listen to me. Men are coming at nine o'clock. Men with instruments. They're going to ask you to remember things, and they're going to write the remembering down, and what they write down, they will *take*. Do you understand me?"

She nodded slowly. Then, with the terrible directness of children: "Will you be there?"

And there it was — the whole offer, the whole trap, the whole price of staying, compressed into a single question small enough to fit in a child's mouth. Say yes and Emrys bought their freedom with her orchards. Say no and Emrys stayed bound, marked, watched, a debtor who had refused the Guildmaster, with eleven years left to serve and enforcers newly curious about their name.

Say yes, and be free, and hollow.

"I'll be there," Emrys heard themselves say, "every minute they'll allow. And Iri — when they ask you to remember — remember *slowly*. Remember the boring parts. Remember rain. Remember what the roads were made of. Make them wait for the bells." They touched her cheek. "Can you do that? Can you be slow?"

Something moved behind her old-young eyes — understanding, or its childhood equivalent, a sense of game and rule and hiding-place. She nodded.

Outside the door, boots sounded in the corridor. More than one pair. Early.

Emrys rose, heart climbing, and through the door's small window saw them coming down the annex hall: not the assessment team, not yet, not at nine — these wore the grey-and-black of the Guildmaster's personal retinue, and they were carrying a portable extraction case, brass fittings glinting under the lamps, and they were coming *now*.
