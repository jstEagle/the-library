## 7. The Extraction Room

The guild does not call it a room where things are taken from people. The guild calls it the Reconciliation Suite.

Iri learns this because they learn everything by listening, and listening is the one thing no one has thought to forbid them. They are small enough to be furniture. They are strange enough that the clerks step around them the way one steps around a spilled inkwell — with irritation, and without looking down. So Iri sits in the corridor outside the Reconciliation Suite on the morning of the third bell, knees drawn up against the cold of the marble, and waits for Emrys the way they have waited every morning since the snowstorm brought them through the guild's door: patiently, and with their whole heart aimed at one person like an arrow at a target it cannot yet reach.

But this morning there is another debtor ahead of Emrys in the queue.

Iri knows his name before anyone says it aloud, because names in the Ledger Guild are spoken the way coins are counted — quietly, and only when necessary. Aldous Merrin. Third-floor copyist. Debt outstanding: eleven years, four months. He is a broad man gone soft at the middle, with ink stains up both forearms like old bruises, and he keeps wiping his palms on his coat as though the sweat itself were evidence of something.

"Reconciliation is painless," the clerk at the door tells him. She says it the way one recites a prayer too many times — smoothly, with nothing left inside the words. "You will feel a coolness. Some clients describe it as pleasant."

Aldous Merrin nods. His nod is wrong. It is the nod of a man agreeing to something he has already decided not to survive intact.

Iri knows what comes next. Not because anyone told them. Because they can *smell* it — the faint sweetness leaking under the door of the Reconciliation Suite, like orchard fruit rotting gently in a cellar. Memory, drawn out of a head and into glass, has a smell. Most people cannot detect it. Iri was made of it once; perhaps still is. To Iri, the whole corridor smells of harvest.

They should leave. They know they should leave. Instead they press themselves smaller behind the potted fern that some clerk's widow donated to soften the architecture, and they watch the door open, and they watch Aldous Merrin go in.

* * *

There is a gap beneath the door. Old building, worn threshold, a finger's width of dark air between marble and oak. Iri lies flat on the cold floor and puts their ear to it, and this is how they witness what happens in the Reconciliation Suite — not with their eyes but with their ears and their skin and the strange, terrible instrument of their remembering.

Inside: three chairs, one table, one machine.

The machine is called a Still, though no one says so aloud either. It looks like nothing so much as a loom crossed with a well — a brass ring on a stand, hung with fine silver threads that end in little cups, and beneath the ring, a shelf of vials racked like wine bottles in a cellar. Each vial is stoppered with wax stamped with the guild's seal: a closed eye above an open ledger. When the light moves across the rack, the vials glow faintly, each one a different color, the way a stained-glass window glows differently depending on what stands outside it.

Aldous Merrin sits in the center chair. A technician — Iri hears her breathing, unhurried, the breathing of someone who has done this two hundred times — fits the silver threads to his temples with small adhesive pads that smell of pine resin.

"Comfortable?" she asks.

"Yes," says Aldous Merrin, in the voice of a man who has just lied to his own mother.

"We'll begin with the standard reconciliation. Eleven years of assessed debt, drawn against non-essential memory at a rate of one year per vial. You'll retain language, skills, procedural knowledge, and all memories older than your daughter's fourth birthday." A pause. Paper rustles. "Your file indicates you've elected to protect the early childhood material. That's wise. It fetches a better price intact, and you'll want it later, if you ever clear the balance."

"I want to keep her," Aldous Merrin says. "All of her. Every age. I signed for the protective rider."

"You did," the technician agrees, warm as a hearth. "The rider protects her memories from *sale*. Extraction for assessment is separate, and required. We have to look at what we're lending against. You understand. It's in clause nine."

Clause nine. Iri has heard clause nine invoked the way other institutions invoke God — as the answer to every question, the justification for every ache.

Something hums. The sound comes up through the floor and into Iri's teeth, a low sweet note like a wet finger circling the rim of a glass. And then Iri feels it begin — feels it *in their own chest*, which should not be possible, which no ordinary person would feel at all — the slow tug of memory leaving a man. It pulls at Iri like a tide pulling at seaweed. Because Iri is made of the same substance. Because somewhere under their skin, in the vaulted dark where a child ought to keep a self, there are forty thousand memories of Serevan packed like winter stores, and the Still's song calls to all of them indiscriminately, the way a bell calls to everyone within earshot regardless of grief or joy.

*Come out,* sings the machine. *Come out, come out, come into the glass.*

Iri bites the inside of their cheek until they taste copper, and holds on.

* * *

The extraction takes eleven minutes. Iri counts them by the technician's breathing.

For the first four minutes, Aldous Merrin talks. This is apparently normal — the technician encourages it, murmurs little prompts: *tell me about the house, tell me about the garden, tell me about the day she was born.* Talking loosens the memory, she explains, the way warming honey loosens it from the spoon. And so Aldous Merrin talks about his daughter while she is being drawn out of him, and this is the part Iri will carry longest, longer than the smell, longer than the hum:

He talks about her face.

"She has — she has my sister's chin, everyone says so, and hair like — like dark water, you know, the way water goes dark over stones—" His voice is easy, fond, a man showing off photographs. "She lost a tooth last spring, the front one, and she wouldn't smile for weeks, kept her hand over her mouth like a little—"

He stops.

The hum continues. The technician's pen scratches. Somewhere in the room, glass clinks softly against glass, a vial being lifted from its rack and replaced by an empty one, and the sound is so gentle, so domestic, so utterly without malice, that Iri's stomach turns over.

"Aldous," the technician prompts pleasantly. "Her hair. Like what?"

Silence. Then, carefully, the way a man steps onto ice he does not trust: "Like... hair?"

"Dark hair? Fair?"

"I — it's — she has hair." His voice climbs. "Of course she has hair, she's six, she — why can't I —" A chair creaks; he is gripping its arms; Iri can hear the wood complain. "What did you take. What did you *take*, I could see her, I was just — I was holding her, she was on my shoulders, we were watching the boats, and now when I reach for it there's just — there's a shape where she was, there's a *hole shaped like her*—"

"The assessment drew on the recent strata," the technician says, and her voice does not change at all, not one degree, and that is the most frightening thing Iri has ever heard. "Recent memories are the most liquid. It's temporary in most cases. If you'd like to recover the balance sooner, we can discuss a buyback schedule—"

"*Give it back.*" The chair slams backward. There is a crash — the rack? No; the technician's voice stays level, so probably just the table. "Give her back, give me her *face*, I carried her on my shoulders, I — I know I did, I know the weight of her, I can still feel her heels against my chest, why can I feel her and not *see* her—"

"Mr. Merrin. Sit down, please. Clause twelve prohibits disruption of active reconciliation."

"I don't care about your clauses—"

"Security," the technician says, not loudly, into a speaking-tube in the wall.

And that is how it ends, bureaucratically, the way everything in the Ledger Guild ends: with boots in the corridor, with two enforcers in grey going in through the door Iri has flattened themselves away from, with the sounds of a grown man being folded to the floor by people trained in folding, and then a long quiet, and then the technician again, calm as milk:

"Now. Where were we. Her birthday — shall we continue? We're nearly done. Only eight more years to assess."

Eight more years. Eight more vials. Iri lies on the cold marble with their ear to the door and listens to a man remember his daughter in fragments, haltingly, like a beggar counting coins, and understands — with a clarity that feels less like understanding than like a blade sliding home — exactly what the guild intends for them.

Not the child. The cargo.

* * *

Afterward, the corridor smells of orchards.

That is the cruelty of it, Iri thinks, sitting up slowly, pressing their back against the wainscoting. The extracted memory leaks its sweetness into the hall, and the sweetness is Serevan's sweetness — apple blossom and river-mud and woodsmoke — because all memory, everywhere, in every head, is made of the same country. People simply don't know it. They walk through the Ledger Guild breathing the ghost-orchards of a nation that was unwritten, and they smell nothing, and they sign their clauses, and they go home lighter by years.

Two clerks pass carrying the rack of vials toward the Vault, cradling it like infants. In the foremost vial something turns slowly, gold-brown, like tea steeping. Like dark water over stones.

Like hair.

Iri's hands are shaking. They look at them — small hands, a child's hands, hands built to be underestimated — and make themselves stop shaking by an act of pure will, because there is no time. Emrys is due at the Reconciliation Suite next. Emrys, whose debt is written in figures Iri has glimpsed upside-down on ledgers left open on desks: a sum so large it has its own page. Emrys, who laughs at the right moments and watches doors the way soldiers watch doors, and who does not know — cannot know — what the room at the end of this corridor is for, or maybe suspects, the way one suspects a illness in oneself, refusing the diagnosis.

The door of the Reconciliation Suite opens. The technician leans out, consults a slate, frowns.

"Scribe Oyelaran's appointment is moved to tomorrow," she tells the corridor at large. "Guildmaster's orders. Priority reassessment." Her eyes find Iri, curled by the fern, and linger a half-second too long. "And the foundling shouldn't be here. Foundlings aren't permitted on this floor."

Iri stands. Walks. Small steps, a child's amble, harmless as furniture — past the open door, past the technician, past the rack waiting on its trolley for tomorrow's appointments, where fresh empty vials gleam in ranks.

One of the vials is labeled already. Prepared in advance, wax unbroken, the guild's closed eye staring up from the seal. The label reads, in a neat clerk's hand:

*OYELARAN, E. — RECONCILIATION, FULL STRATA — BY ORDER OF GUILDMASTER CORVANE.*

Full strata. Not eleven years. Not a decade. *Everything.*

Iri walks on, unhurried, heart hammering like a fist on a locked door, and begins — even before they round the corner, even before they find the stair — to plan how they will get Emrys out of this building before tomorrow's bell.
