## 8. Running on Borrowed Names

The Ledger Guild slept in the way that great institutions sleep: not at all, but pretending to. Lamps burned low in the corridors on their long rotation. A clerk somewhere on the third floor coughed in his sleep and was answered by the tick of a clock no one had wound in years. And in the Hall of Records, where the debt-bound were permitted to work past midnight to shave hours off what they owed, Emrys Oyelaran sat alone with a stolen lamp and did not copy anything.

They had been sitting there for two hours, listening.

Listening for footsteps, yes — but also listening to themselves, which was worse. Since the night Iri had spoken Serevani through the storm-door and called them *Sef*, Emrys had become a stranger wearing their own skin. Every gesture now came with a question attached. *Did you learn to hold your pen like that in Vhalen? Or somewhere else? Somewhere that doesn't exist?* The old entries haunted them — pages in their own hand, dated forty years before their birth, ink browned to the color of dried blood, describing orchards they had never walked and bells they had never heard, written in a script that was almost, but not quite, the guild's standard hand. Almost as if someone had taught them to write by holding their wrist.

*You were there,* Iri had said, in the dark of the linen closet where Emrys had hidden them among the winter sheets. *You carried me out. You promised you'd come back.*

Emrys had no memory of any promise. But promises, they had learned this week, could be sold like anything else. Could be extracted, decanted, filed under an account number, and marked *PAID IN FULL*.

So: the ledger page.

Iri's page. The child's entire existence in the guild's eyes was a single folio in the Intake Registry — name, condition, provenance, valuation. Corvane had it locked in the Iron Cabinet in the Records Annex, because a living speaker of pre-kingdom Serevani was worth more than the building she stood in, and the guild protected its assets the way misers protect gold: obsessively, and with an eye toward spending.

Emrys had spent three nights learning the cabinet's rhythms. Three nights of copying inventories with half a mind while the other half counted footsteps, shift changes, the particular creak of the third stair that meant Old Hessel doing his rounds with his bad hip and his lantern turned down so he wouldn't have to see anyone he'd have to report. Three nights of memorizing which of the annex's four keys hung on which hook behind Corvane's desk, and which hook was empty on nights when Corvane took the small key home in his waistcoat pocket, close to his heart, like a locket.

Tonight the small key was gone from its hook.

Which meant tonight was the night, or never. Because tomorrow, Emrys knew, there would be no choosing anymore. Tomorrow Corvane would make his offer formal — forgive the debt, all nine years of it, every hour Emrys had ground away since they were sixteen — in exchange for one small procedure. One extraction. One vial of whatever lived inside a child who remembered orchards.

And the terrible thing, the thing Emrys could not stop examining like a stone turned over and over, was that some part of them had wanted to say yes. Nine years. Freedom. All they had to do was stand in a white room and hold a frightened girl's hands while the guild drank her childhood through her eyes.

That part of them disgusted Emrys more than Corvane ever could.

They rose. The chair did not creak; they had oiled it yesterday, a small sabotage that had felt absurd at the time and felt like survival now. They took the lamp down from its shelf and left it burning on the desk, open, beside a half-finished inventory of impossible things — *one jar, sealed, containing the last words of a drowned man; provenance disputed* — so that anyone glancing in would see a scribe merely stepped away. Then they went into the dark of the guild with nothing but their own heartbeat and the memory of a key's shape, studied through a keyhole across three nights, hanging in their mind like a picture of a saint.

* * *

The Records Annex smelled of cold iron and older paper, a smell like the inside of a locked thought. Emrys moved along the wall where the shadows pooled deepest, past the glass cases of high-value fragments — a bottled laugh here, a lock of hair that remembered its owner's name there — each tagged and priced, each one a person reduced to inventory. They had catalogued these things for nine years without once wondering whose they were. That was the guild's true magic, Emrys thought bitterly. Not the extraction. The making-normal. You could get a scribe to write down a stolen soul in neat columns, date it, initial it, and go to supper afterward feeling honest.

Corvane's office door was unlocked. Of course it was. What thief would dare?

The hooks behind the desk held three keys. Emrys lifted the iron one — heavy, toothed like something that ate — and crossed to the cabinet at the room's heart.

Their hands were steady until they weren't. The key slid in, turned with a sound like a knuckle cracking, and the cabinet exhaled a breath of air that hadn't moved in weeks, dry and faintly sweet, the smell of preserved things.

Inside, on velvet-lined shelves, sat the guild's crown jewels. Vials in ranked rows, each labeled in Corvane's immaculate hand. *Serevan: harvest bell, dawn ring. Serevan: mother's lullaby (fragmentary). Serevan: the smell of rain on the archive roof.* An entire country, shelved. And there, in a plain folio folder at eye level, tagged with a red string: **INTAKE 4471 — CHILD, FEMALE, APPARENT AGE 8. SPEAKER. PROVENANCE: FOUND AT THRESHOLD DURING STORM. VALUATION: PENDING FULL SURVEY.**

Emrys took the folder. Their fingers wanted to shake and they forbade it. Inside was a single page, and beneath the page — Emrys's breath caught — a second document, folded small, that they had not expected: a work order, dated three days hence, authorizing *full cognitive survey of subject 4471, extraction of primary memory-sequences, preparatory to market.* Signed, in advance, by Guildmaster Corvane. He had signed it before offering Emrys the bargain. Before asking. The mercy he intended to dangle was already paperwork.

Something in Emrys went very quiet and very cold, the way water goes just before it decides to be ice.

They took both documents. They closed the cabinet. They returned the key to its hook, adjusted it to the precise angle they'd memorized, and walked out of the annex at the pace of a tired scribe going to bed, which is the fastest safe speed in any institution.

It wasn't until they reached the end of the corridor, until the lamplight of the main hall touched their face, that the shaking started — and with it, unbidden, a wild bright fizzing joy, entirely inappropriate, entirely alive. *I did it. It's done. She's mine to protect now.* They pressed the folder flat against their chest, under their coat, and let the joy burn for exactly three steps before they smothered it, because joy makes noise, and noise in the guild at night was a debt collected immediately.

From somewhere above, a floorboard spoke. Then another. Not Hessel's uneven tread. Something lighter, faster, moving with purpose.

Emrys did not look up. Looking up was confession. They turned instead toward the stairwell that led down — down past the counting floors, past the vaults, down to where the guild's foundations met the flooded undercity of old Vhalen, the drowned streets the city had been built on top of rather than admit existed. Every guild had a back door it pretended not to have. The Ledger Guild's opened onto black water.

Behind them, above, a voice — young, clipped, the enforcer Tallow, whose boots Emrys knew by sound: "Annex check. Now."

Not yet alarmed. Routine. Emrys took the stairs.

* * *

The undercity took them the way deep water takes a dropped coin.

Down five flights of service stairs, past the boiler rooms where debtor-laborers slept in shifts, to the last door — a door Emrys had scrubbed clean a hundred times as punishment detail, and had examined, even then, even in those gray obedient years, with the idle hunger of someone who watches windows. It was oak banded in iron, and it groaned when it opened, and beyond it the dark breathed out cold and river-smell and the distant, rhythmic drip of a city drowning slowly underground.

Old Vhalen. The first city, sunk. Streets running with black canal-water knee-deep and deeper, roofs converted to ceilings, shopfronts fanged with rotted shutters. The guild used it for storage of things too dangerous or too shameful for daylight — failed extractions, memory-vials that had spoiled, the ledgers of debts written off as uncollectable, meaning the debtors were dead. Emrys had delivered boxes down here for years. They knew the causeways, the plank-walks the clerks had laid over the worst floods, the places where the water hid open cellars that would swallow a person whole.

They also knew the enforcers hated the undercity. That was the whole of the plan, such as it was: get down here, get wet, get lost, and trust that greed made men cautious.

Emrys waded in.

The cold climbed their legs like a living thing, an animal made of winter finding its grip. The folder they had wrapped in oilcloth and bound against their ribs with a borrowed belt, and they checked it now by touch — still there, still dry, still *hers* — and then they moved, fast and low, along the causeway beneath a ceiling of ancient brick furred white with mineral bloom. Their lamp they had left behind. Moonlight didn't reach here. They navigated by memory and by the guild's own markers — little brass plates screwed to the walls, aisle numbers for the storage racks, and Emrys followed them backward, 12, 11, 10, reading the numbers like a countdown to a world.

The exhilaration came back and this time they let it. It was a reckless luxury and they took it anyway, grinning into the dark like a madwoman, splashing now, not caring, nine years of careful watchful resignation cracking open to show the person underneath — someone who ran, someone who stole, someone who had apparently once promised a child she'd come back. The water pulled at their knees and they laughed at it. Somewhere ahead, in a flooded chapel or counting-house, drips fell from the vaulted dark in slow applause.

Then, far behind and above, muffled by stone and water: a bell. Not the guild's hour-bell. The alarm.

The joy curdled instantly, the way milk curdles, all at once and irreversibly. Emrys stood chest-deep in a drowned street with a stolen child in oilcloth against their ribs and understood, with total clarity, what they had done. Not theft — the guild could survive theft; theft was just an entry waiting to be corrected. They had committed *narrative*. They had taken a story the guild had already written the ending to, and run off with the middle. Corvane did not punish thieves. Corvane corrected accounts. And Emrys Oyelaran's account, they suddenly understood, had been allowed to grow so large precisely so that it could be called in whenever calling it became useful. Debt was not a burden the guild suffered. Debt was a leash, and leashes were only ever bought for dogs the owner intended to walk somewhere.

They ran.

The causeway ended at aisle 9 where the flood had eaten the planks years ago; Emrys swung up onto a fallen rack, balanced along its rusted spine with the water sucking at their ankles, and dropped into the next street. Their shin struck something submerged — a cart, a coffin, a god knew what — and pain flared white, but fear outran pain, and they ran on, limping, splashing, breathing in gasps that echoed off the low ceiling and came back sounding like pursuit.

Because it was pursuit. They could hear it now, or believed they could — boots on the causeway behind, more than one pair, moving fast, and a light sweeping the far intersection, pale and wrong, not lantern-yellow but the cold blue-white of a guildlamp, the kind the enforcers carried that showed heat and guilt alike. Emrys pressed into a doorway, into the mouth of a dead shop, and made themselves small among the shelves of a store that had been selling, when it drowned, God only knew what.

The light passed. The boots passed. Close enough that Emrys heard Tallow's voice, low: "Split at aisle six. Water's deep past the chapel — she'll go for the ferry-stair."

*She'll go for the ferry-stair.* So they knew Emrys knew the ways. Of course they did. Nine years of deliveries; the guild kept records of everything, including the habits of its property. Emrys's own file probably contained a map of their mind neater than any map of the undercity.

So don't go for the ferry-stair.

There was another way. Everyone said there wasn't. Everyone said the Weepers' Passage had collapsed thirty years ago, flooding half the second district, sealing the undercity's western reaches — but everyone also said Serevan had never existed, and Emrys had lately lost faith in what everyone said. More to the point, Emrys had spent one long bored winter, years ago, cross-referencing the guild's oldest surveys against its newest, and had noticed that the Weepers' Passage appeared on the old maps with a depth notation, and on the new maps with none at all, as if it had been deleted rather than destroyed. Institutions did not delete geography by accident. Someone had wanted that passage forgotten — which, in Emrys's new and bitter education, meant someone had wanted it *kept*.

They turned west, into the water, into the dark that even the enforcers' lamps avoided.

* * *

The Weepers' Passage announced itself by sound before sight: a hollow moaning where water forced itself through a crack too narrow to see, a sound like a country sighing in its sleep. Emrys found the entrance behind a collapsed shrine — some drowned god of commerce, its merchant-saint's face worn blank — and squeezed sideways through a gap that took the buttons off their coat and the skin off their shoulder, and then they were through, into a corridor of black air and blacker water that rose from knee to thigh to waist with every step.

Here there were no brass markers. Here there was only the oilcloth bundle against their ribs and the thought of Iri — locked tonight in the guest quarters that were not cells except in every way that mattered, trusting a scribe she'd known for five days, believing a promise Emrys couldn't remember making and had decided, on no evidence whatsoever, to keep.

*I'm coming,* they thought at the bundle, at the girl, at the country that didn't exist. *Whoever I was. Whoever I am. I'm coming.*

The passage bent. The water shallowed, then deepened, then — mercifully, impossibly — began to fall away, draining through gaps in the floor into some lower dark, until Emrys was walking on slick stone in ankle-deep rills, under a ceiling that rose, and ahead, faint as a coin at the bottom of a well, a gray suggestion of sky.

An outlet. An actual outlet, into the open drains above the river cliffs east of the guild, where the poor fished and the smugglers landed and no ledger had ever been kept.

Emrys climbed toward the gray light with their whole body shaking now, the adrenaline presenting its bill, legs trembling, shoulder bleeding warm down their arm. At the last bend they stopped, pressed their back to the wet stone, and listened for a full minute. Water. Wind. A dog barking somewhere in the cliff-towns. No boots. No blue-white light.

They climbed out into the night.

The sky over Vhalen was the color of slate and snow-threat, and the wind coming off the river cut through their soaked clothes like the guild's arithmetic — impersonal and total. Below the cliffs the black water moved. Above, the city's ten thousand windows burned their small complacent fires, and among them, somewhere on the hill, the guild's lamps were lighting up window after window after window, a spreading infection of light. Searching. Already searching.

Emrys stood dripping on the river rocks, a thief with no plan beyond *away*, holding a child's existence in oilcloth against their ribs — and felt the fear arrive fully now, not the hot fear of the chase but the cold kind, the kind that settles in for a long stay. They had money for perhaps three days of bread. They had no allies. They had a body the guild owned paper on, and a face known in every district where papers mattered. And somewhere behind them, patient as interest accruing, Corvane would be reading the empty hook, the missing folio, the wet footprints — and smiling, Emrys was somehow certain of it, smiling the way a man smiles at a ledger that has finally begun to balance.

But the child was real. The orchards were real. The name *Sef* sat in Emrys's chest like a coal that refused to cool.

They turned east, away from the city lights, toward the river slums and the one place in Vhalen where stolen things changed hands without questions — a place every guild clerk knew of and no guild clerk admitted to. If the undercity had failed to hide them, then they would hide in the open, among people who traded in exactly the kind of goods Emrys now carried.

The Nightmarket. Vesha. The name surfaced from guild gossip like a fish rising: *the fence on the water stairs who pays for Serevani.*

Emrys pulled the oilcloth bundle tighter, looked back once at the burning hill of the guild, and began to walk.

Behind them, far below in the drowned streets, in a passage that had been deleted from every map, the water went on forcing itself through the crack too narrow to see — and if it sounded, still, like a country sighing in its sleep, no one was left down there to hear it.

No one but the things the guild had stored in aisle twelve.

And one of them, that night, was missing from its shelf.
