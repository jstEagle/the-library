## 10. A Market of Forgotten Things

The Nightmarket did not exist by day.

Emrys had heard that said a dozen times in the undercity's drowned alleys, always in the tone of someone passing along a warning wrapped in a joke — *don't go looking for it before dark, friend, you'll only find a wall* — and they had filed it away with all the other folklore of Vhalen's lower depths, the kind of thing scribes wrote down and did not believe. But now, with Iri's small hand cold in theirs and the guild's hounds surely already on their scent, they walked down a stair that had not been there an hour ago, into a street that smelled of tallow and rain and something older underneath, something like orchards.

The stair was slick with meltwater. It descended farther than the undercity had depth to give. Behind them, the last grey light of evening shrank to a coin, then a thread, then nothing at all, and still they kept walking, because the child had said, in her careful, accented trade-tongue, *"Down is where they keep what fell,"* and Emrys had no better map than that.

Then the dark opened like a mouth, and they were inside it.

Lanterns first — hundreds of them, thousands, strung on wires between buildings that leaned together overhead like conspirators, so that the whole market hung beneath a canopy of light as dense as stars. The lanterns were not all fire. Some glowed a soft gold that warmed the skin without casting shadows; some burned blue and made everything near them look remembered rather than seen; one, far down the main row, pulsed a slow bruised violet, and Emrys found their eyes dragging toward it again and again before they understood why: it was lit, Iri whispered, with thirty years of a lighthouse keeper's vigilance, and it could not help but sweep.

And beneath the lanterns: stalls. Stalls upon stalls upon stalls, crammed into every crevice of the buried street, selling —

Emrys stopped walking.

They had spent nine years copying inventories of impossible things. They knew the catalogue language by heart: *one (1) bottled laughter, provenance uncertain; one (1) key to nowhere, brass, warm to the touch; one (1) jar of last words, do not unstopper.* They had thought those entries strange the way a fish might think water strange — present everywhere, questioned never. But this was the water itself. This was where the ledger came from.

A woman at the nearest stall held up a small glass sphere to the lantern light, turning it, and inside the sphere snow fell upward. "Summer of the great hail," she was saying to a buyer in a fur collar. "Whole afternoon of it. You'll feel the cold on your neck, honest as your own skin. You'll remember being somewhere it hailed, even if you never were."

"Whose summer?" the buyer asked.

The woman smiled with professional warmth. "Does it matter? It fits anyone."

Iri's grip tightened on Emrys's hand. The child had gone very still, the way she went still at bells, at certain words, at the smell of bread — stillness as a form of listening. Her eyes moved across the stalls the way a drowning person's eyes move across the surface of water.

"They're selling it," she said. Not loudly. Emrys had learned that Iri's quietest voice was her most dangerous one, the voice that came just before tears or just before truth. "All of it. Piece by piece."

"It's what markets do," Emrys said, and hated how much their voice sounded like the guild's.

* * *

They walked. There was nothing else to do; standing still in the Nightmarket marked you as either buyer or goods, and Emrys intended to be neither longer than necessary. They pulled Iri close and let the crowd carry them, keeping their hood up, keeping their face angled down, doing the thing they had done for nine years in the guild's corridors: being furniture.

It was harder here. The market looked back.

Every third stall seemed to deal in sight of some kind. A man sold mirrors that showed the room behind you instead of the room before you. A pair of twins sold, jointly, a single memory of a mother's hands kneading dough, split between two vials so that no one person could ever have all of it — "that way it stays true," one twin explained to a customer, "a memory one person owns alone starts lying within the year." A child hardly older than Iri sat on a counter swinging her legs above a tray of small brown bottles labeled in a neat clerk's hand: *first snow. first lie. first time unafraid.*

And everywhere, threaded through it all like a color Emrys hadn't known how to see until now: Serevan.

They recognized it the way you recognize your own name spoken across a crowded room. A stall of dried fruit whose sweetness reached them from ten paces and made Iri make a small wounded sound. A rack of ribbons in a green Emrys had no word for except *home*, though they had never owned such a ribbon, had never seen it, had no right to the word at all. An old man playing a stringed instrument with a sound like rain on a tin roof while beside him, in a dish, his memories of learning the song lay gleaming for sale at three coppers each, and he played from what remained, which apparently was enough.

"Where did it all come from?" Emrys asked the air, and a nearby vendor — a broad woman gutting eels that were, Emrys chose not to look closely, glowing faintly — answered without looking up.

"Came off a country that got foreclosed, love. Thirty years back. Whole nation, defaulted on existence. Everything that couldn't run came out in lots." She flicked her knife. "You want orchard or winter? Orchard's sweeter. Winter lasts longer."

"You say that like it's weather."

She did look up then, and her eyes were the flat grey of a ledger page. "Everything's weather if you wait long enough."

* * *

They found the corner they needed because Iri found it — stopped dead in the crowd's current and turned her head like a compass needle, and there, wedged between a fortune-teller's tent and a wall of stacked crates, hung a sign painted on slate:

**VESHA — FAIR PRICES FOR HONEST GHOSTS**

Beneath the sign, a stall narrower than most, and more crowded than any: shelves rising to the canvas roof, every shelf ranked with vials, jars, locket-cases, stoppered bottles of colored glass, and things that defied container entirely — a memory folded flat like a letter; one coiled in a birdcage; one, enormous, occupying a locked chest that hummed when Emrys passed, low and continuous, like a hive.

Behind the counter sat a woman perhaps fifty, sharp-faced, silver-streaked hair bound back with wire, wearing fingerless gloves and an apron with too many pockets. She was appraising a ring — turning it in the lantern light, holding it to her ear as though listening to it — and she did not look up when Emrys and Iri stopped at her counter.

"If you're selling," she said, "I buy quiet things. If you're buying, I sell anything but regret. Regret I keep. Bad for resale, good for company."

"We're not—" Emrys began.

"You're both." Vesha set the ring down and looked at them at last, and Emrys felt the look go through their hood, their lowered eyes, their careful furniture-stillness, all the way to the bone. "Everyone who comes down here is both. It's the house style." Her gaze slid to Iri and snagged there, and something moved behind her face — quick, suppressed, like a fish under ice. "Well," she said, softer. "Well, now."

"She's my sister," Emrys said. The lie came smoothly, worn smooth by nine years of practice. "We're looking for passage out of the city. South road, before the thaw."

"Mm." Vesha picked up the ring again, put it down again. "South road's closed. Has been since the guild started posting watchers at the gates with little silver instruments that taste the air for stolen inventory." She let that sit. "You're carrying stolen inventory."

"I don't know what you—"

"Boy. Girl. Whoever you are." Vesha leaned forward on her elbows, and the lantern light caught the fine scars on her knuckles, old ones, deliberate-looking, in patterns almost like script. "I've been fencing ghost-goods since before you had debts. I can price a memory by the weight of the silence around the person carrying it. And the two of you are carrying enough silence to sink a barge." She glanced at Iri once more, and this time she didn't hide what crossed her face — grief, old and well-kept, like a tool maintained against need. "That child is speaking Serevani in her sleep. Don't ask how I know. Half this market dreams in it and doesn't know why."

Emrys's hand found Iri's shoulder and stayed there.

"What do you want?" they asked.

"Same as everyone. To die owing nothing." Vesha shrugged, brisk again, the moment folded away. "But tonight, specifically: I want to know who's hunting you, because whoever it is has been bad for business for three days. Guild enforcers came through the market yesterday asking after a runaway debtor and a child. And something else came through asking after the same, and the enforcers *left*, quickly, and one of them left his shadow behind, still standing at the mouth of Tanner's Row, not attached to anybody." She said it flatly, a merchant reporting a supply disruption. "So. You're going to tell me what you took, and from whom, and then we're going to discuss whether helping you is worth the trouble, or whether selling you is."

"And if we refuse?"

Vesha smiled. It was not an unkind smile, which somehow made it worse. "Then I'll wish you luck with real feeling, and the market will eat you by morning, and I'll think about you sometimes. That's the fair version, and I do advertise fair prices."

* * *

What Emrys told her was almost the truth, which was the most currency they had left. A debtor of the Ledger Guild. A child who should not exist. A ledger page stolen from the vaults — Iri's page, the record of her extraction, the paper proof of what she was. They did not mention the Hollow King; they did not know yet how to say his name aloud without the air changing temperature. They did not mention the word *Serevan* except once, accidentally, when Iri murmured it at the dried-fruit stall's memory, and Emrys watched Vesha hear it the way a wall hears a knock.

Vesha listened with her chin propped on her fist, and when Emrys finished she was quiet long enough that the market's noise seemed to lean in around them.

"A ledger page," she said finally. "Of all the things in that vault — laughter, keys, last words, thirty years of my country in little glass coffins — you stole the *paperwork*." She laughed, short and disbelieving. "Do you know what that makes you?"

"Tired," Emrys said.

"It makes you the first thief in thirty years to steal something the guild actually needs." Vesha stood, and began moving along her shelves with sudden purpose, pulling vials, checking seals, sliding a locked strongbox out from beneath the counter. "Enforcers can be bought. Shadows can be outrun. But Corvane doesn't send men after paper unless the paper can open something." She paused, her back to them, her hands still. "And there's only one thing left in this world that needs opening."

"The Last Door," Iri said.

Both adults went still. The market roared on around their small frozen island — the upward-falling snow, the split memory of the mother's hands, the old man playing rain on a tin roof — and Iri stood in the middle of it, small and certain, her breath fogging in air that had grown cold without explanation.

"That's where the page goes," she said. "That's where *I* go. I'm supposed to carry it home."

Vesha turned around slowly. She looked at Iri for a long moment, and then at Emrys, and whatever she saw in Emrys's face — the vertigo, the refusal-to-hope, the hunger they'd spent nine years burying under resignation — made her exhale through her nose like someone accepting a bet she already knew was foolish.

"Right," she said. "Here's what happens now. You sleep in the back room tonight, behind a door I've warded twice and once in blood. Tomorrow, before the market wakes, I take you to someone who knows the ways under the lake. And in exchange—" she held up a finger before Emrys could speak, "—in exchange, when this is over, if any of us are alive, you come back and tell me everything. Every entry. Every name. Because I have been selling my country off shelf by shelf for thirty years to keep other people's ghosts fed, and I would like, just once, before I die, to know if any of it can be *bought back*."

Her voice cracked on the last word, barely, a fault-line in stone, and then sealed over.

Emrys looked at her — at the scarred knuckles, the shelves of small glass coffins, the sign promising fair prices for honest ghosts — and understood that they were looking at their own future in one direction and their own past in the other, and that neither offered any comfort, and that they were going to trust her anyway, because the alternative was the market, and the market ate people by morning.

"All right," Emrys said. "We have a bargain."

"No," Vesha said, already turning to unhook a lantern from its nail, already moving, brisk as arithmetic. "You have a debt. Bargains are for people with collateral." She glanced back at them over her shoulder, and for half a heartbeat her eyes lingered on Emrys's face with an expression Emrys couldn't read — recognition reaching for something just out of its grasp, a word on the tip of the tongue.

"Get some sleep," Vesha said. "Tomorrow you'll want your memories about you. All of them. Trust me — you'll miss them sooner than you think."

She hung the lantern by the back-room door, and its blue light made them both look remembered rather than seen, and somewhere far above, muffled through thirty feet of earth, the bells of Vhalen rang midnight — and beneath the ringing, fainter, patient, from a direction that had nothing to do with up or down, something else answered.
