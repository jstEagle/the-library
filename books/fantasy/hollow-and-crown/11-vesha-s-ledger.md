## 11. Vesha's Ledger

The Nightmarket opened at dusk the way a wound opens: slowly, and with a smell you never quite forgot.

Vesha had run her stall beneath the aqueduct arches for eleven years, long enough to know that the market breathed. It inhaled at moonrise when the lamps of green glass were lit and the first buyers came shuffling down the flooded stair, and it exhaled near dawn when the last vials were wrapped in oilcloth and the beggars who slept among the crates woke with other people's dreams still caught between their teeth. She had learned to read the breath of it. A good night tasted like copper and cold air. A bad night tasted like fear, which was the one commodity that never lost its value.

Tonight smelled like snowmelt and something older underneath — river water, wet stone, and faintly, unmistakably, orchards.

She noticed it before she saw them. That was the trouble with dealing in memory: your own nose became a ledger.

"Two drops of the ferryman's," said her first customer, a widow with a face like a folded letter. "The crossing one. Not the drowning."

"The crossing is three silver. The drowning I'll pay *you* to take."

The widow almost smiled. Almost. Grief had made her frugal with everything, including joy. Vesha drew the vial from its rack — third row, behind the false bottom, because the guild's inspectors came through on quarter-days and the ferryman's fragments were technically contraband, being extracted without license from a man who had sold his crossings twice over — and held it up to the green lamp. The liquid inside moved wrong, the way memories always did, swirling against the glass as if looking for a way out.

"Two drops under the tongue," Vesha said. "You'll smell rope and river tar. You'll feel the pull of the current through his arms. You will not remember doing this tomorrow, but you'll be kinder to strangers for a week. That's the standard effect. No refunds."

The widow paid. The widow left. And then the crowd at the mouth of the arch parted around two figures coming through it — a tall scribe in a soaked guild coat moving like someone trying not to be seen, which was itself a way of being seen, and beside them a child in clothes cut from no cloth Vesha recognized, walking with the particular gravity of someone carrying more than a body should hold.

Vesha's hands went still on the counter.

She knew that face.

Not the child's. The scribe's. She knew it the way you know a face seen once in a fire — lit from below, turned toward something terrible, mouth open around a word that never finished. She had watched that face for six seconds, maybe seven, in a fragment she kept locked in the iron box under her floor because she could not bring herself to sell it and could not bring herself to destroy it either.

It was a Serevani fragment. One of the old ones, pre-kingdom, salvaged from a burning house the night the country was unwritten. In it, a young person stood in an archive doorway while the shelves went dark shelf by shelf, like candles pinched out by an invisible hand, and they were shouting — no sound, fragments rarely carried sound, but their whole body was shaped around a shout — and their face was *this* face, younger, unafraid in the way only people who have never yet been betrayed can be unafraid.

The scribe stopped three stalls away, haggling badly with Old Tetch over a map case, and Vesha looked at them and felt the guilt arrive on schedule, punctual as rent.

Her name was Vesha Kell, and she was a thief of the dead. Everyone in the Nightmarket was, in one way or another; that was what the market was *for*. But there were thieves and there were thieves. Some stole from the living, who could grieve and recover. Vesha had built her fortune stealing from the erased, who could do neither — from a country that had been sold out from under its own feet, entry by entry, until even its name was a debt instrument. Every vial on her racks was a splinter of Serevan. The laughter of a woman whose laugh no longer existed anywhere in the world. The taste of a bread baked by hands that had been unwritten mid-knead. A lullaby in a language that survived now only inside glass, sung to children who had grown old outside their own homeland or not grown at all.

She told herself the same story she'd told herself for eleven years: that she was a keeper, not a thief. That things preserved were things not lost. That someday, somehow, she would give it all back.

She had never once believed the story. But she told it well, and telling it well was most of what a life was.

* * *

They came to her stall last, which told her they'd been working the row and finding nothing. Good. The other fences would have taken one look at the child and sent word to the guild within the hour; there were bounties posted, quiet ones, the kind paid in forgiven debts rather than coin. Vesha herself had collected two such bounties in her time. She was not proud of it. She was also not poor because of it, and she had learned long ago that pride and poverty made a bitter loaf.

"You're looking for something specific," she said, before the scribe could speak. "I can tell because you've insulted three of my colleagues and bought nothing."

The scribe — Emrys Oyelaran, according to the guild seal half-hidden under their collar; Vesha missed very little — stiffened. Up close the resemblance to the fragment was worse. Same jaw. Same way of holding the head slightly forward, as if leaning into a wind only they could feel.

"We need information," Emrys said. "About Serevan."

The word dropped into the stall like a stone into still water. Around them the market went on — a buyer sniffing at a vial of a soldier's homecoming, a pickpocket drifting past with professional innocence — but in the small radius of Vesha's counter, everything stopped.

"Serevan," Vesha repeated.

"You deal in fragments. We've heard you're the one who deals in *old* fragments." Emrys glanced at the child — Iri, the scribe had murmured, and the name had landed on Vesha strangely too, though she couldn't have said why — and back. "Pre-kingdom. We want to know where the Door is."

And there it was. The question that got fences killed. Vesha had heard it asked exactly four times in eleven years, and three of the askers had ended up in the guild's extraction rooms, and the fourth had simply vanished, which was worse, because vanishing meant the Hollow King's Silent Court had gotten there first, and the Court didn't extract. They *kept.*

"I don't know what you're talking about," Vesha said pleasantly, "and if you say that word again in my stall, I'm going to call the watch, and the watch here is me, so it'll be very confusing for everyone."

Emrys's eyes narrowed. The child tugged the scribe's sleeve and said something in a language that made the hairs on Vesha's arms stand up — soft, bell-toned, old as orchard roots. Pre-kingdom Serevani. Spoken fluently. By a child who couldn't have been more than ten.

Eleven years in the trade, and Vesha had heard that tongue exactly once outside a vial: from the lips of a dying smuggler who'd carried fragments raw, unprotected, letting them soak into him because he couldn't afford glass. He had died speaking it, smiling, calling for a mother who had never existed in this world at all.

"Come around the back," Vesha heard herself say. Her voice sounded far away, like it belonged to someone younger and stupider. "Both of you. Now, before Tetch remembers how to count."

* * *

The back room was a coffin of brick and dripping mortar, lit by one lamp with a hooded flame. Vesha poured three cups of chicory that had gone bitter years ago and set them down like an apology she wasn't ready to make.

"Rules," she said. "One: you don't touch anything on the racks. Two: you don't tell anyone you were here. Three—" she looked at the child, and the child looked back with eyes that were far too calm, far too full, "—three, nobody asks me why I'm helping. Because I don't have an answer I like."

"Why are you helping?" Iri asked.

Vesha laughed despite herself. It came out cracked. "That was fast."

"I'm told I do that."

She sat down across from them and studied the scribe's face in the lamplight, and the guilt and the greed sat down with her, one on each shoulder, as they always did, whispering their old argument. Greed said: *this is leverage. A debtor born of Serevan, hunted by the guild, needed by the Hollow King — there are three buyers for that and all of them pay in fortunes.* Guilt said nothing. Guilt just showed her the fragment again, the archive doorway, the unfinished shout, the shelves going dark.

"I'm going to show you something," Vesha said. "And then we're going to talk about price. And you're going to hate the price, and I'm going to pretend to care about that, and then we're going to do business anyway, because you have nowhere else to go and I have something you need. That's how honesty works in the Nightmarket."

She knelt, pried up the floorboard, and lifted out the iron box. Inside, nested in wool, lay a single vial — older than the others, its glass clouded, its contents the color of embers sinking.

"This is called *the Burning of House Vael,*" she said. "Salvage classification: witnessed. Which means whoever held this memory was standing there when it happened. When *it* happened. The erasure." She turned the vial in the light. "I've owned it nine years. I've sold pieces of it twice — a buyer wanted the color of the fire, another wanted the sound of the roof beams. Both times I told myself it was just commerce." Her thumb moved over the glass. "But I've never shown it to anyone. Because of the face in it."

Emrys had gone very still. "What face?"

"A face standing in the doorway of the Serevani archive, screaming at the dark like screaming could stop a country from being unwritten." Vesha looked up. "Your face."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere above them, the market roared on, oblivious, trading in the crumbs of a dead nation.

"That's not possible," Emrys said quietly. "I've never been to Serevan. I was born twenty years after—"

"So was everyone," Vesha said. "That's rather the point of erasure." She set the vial on the table between them, and her hand was not quite steady, and she hated that it wasn't. "Nine years I've wondered about you. Whether you were real. Whether the fragment was corrupted, or forged, or whether somewhere out there a person walked around wearing the face of the last witness. And now you walk into my market asking after the Door, with a child who speaks the old tongue, and I have to decide what you are."

"And if I'm real?"

"Then you're the most dangerous thing in Vhalen," Vesha said, "and every power in this city wants a piece of you, and I am about to make the second-worst decision of my life by siding with you anyway."

"What was the worst?"

"Selling the first piece of this." She tapped the vial. "There was a boy in it. In the memory. Standing behind the witness, holding a book. I sold his face to a collector who liked tragic children. He pays well, that collector. He hangs them on walls." She met Emrys's eyes. "That's what I am. Now you know. Decide accordingly."

For a long moment the scribe said nothing. Then: "What's your price?"

Vesha smiled, and it was the smile she used on buyers, and it fit her like a coat that no longer buttoned. "Three things. First: passage. When you find whatever you're looking for — Door, country, miracle, I don't care — I come with you, or I get out ahead of the guild, either way, my choice, no argument. Second: any fragment of Serevan you recover passes through my hands first. I keep copies. Copies only — the originals stay with you, I swear it on the market itself."

"And third?"

Vesha looked at the vial. At the ember-colored memory of a house on fire, of a country going out like a lamp.

"Third," she said, "when this is over — however it ends — you help me give it all back. Every vial. Nine years of inventory. I want to pour every last drop of stolen Serevan back into the world, and I want witnesses, and you're going to be one of them." She leaned back. "That's the price. Not coin. Coin I have."

Emrys blinked. "That's — not what I expected."

"No one ever expects the fence to want restitution. It ruins our reputation." Vesha stood, because sitting still had become unbearable, because the guilt had climbed off her shoulder and was pacing the room like a caged thing. "Understand me, scribe. I have spent eleven years telling myself I'm a keeper. Tonight a child walked in speaking a dead language and I realized I'm running out of years to keep lying. So yes. I'll help you find the Door. I'll hide you, feed you, point you at the fragments you need — and I know where three of them are, including one that's going to hurt you badly, and you'll thank me for it later or you won't."

"The one that hurts," Emrys said slowly. "Which one is that?"

Vesha reached into the iron box again and brought out a second vial, smaller, its glass etched with a serial number in the guild's old hand — a salvage tag, meaning it had passed through official channels once, meaning it had been *bought*, meaning somewhere in the Ledger Guild's deep vaults there was a matching entry that said what it contained and who it had been taken from.

She set it down in front of Emrys.

"It's catalogued as *Archive Interior, Final Hour,*" she said. "Witness-class. First-person." She paused, and for once in her life did not dress the words in merchant's silk. "Whoever holds this memory was inside the Serevan archive when the erasure began. The same room as your face in the burning. If you touch it, you won't be watching someone else remember. You'll be remembering."

The child — Iri — made a small sound. When Vesha glanced over, the girl was staring at the vial with an expression that was not fear and not longing but some terrible arithmetic between them, the look of someone counting and arriving at a number they already knew.

"She shouldn't be here for this part," Iri said, in trade-tongue, nodding at the vial. "When you open it. She's seen enough burning."

Vesha looked at the child. At the scribe. At the ember-colored past sitting on her scarred counter between them, patient as a debt.

"Back room," she said. "Through the curtain. There's a cot." And then, softer, almost against her will: "Whatever's in that vial, scribe — it waited thirty years to find you. It can wait one more hour. Eat something first. Memory goes down easier on a full stomach."

Outside, above the arches, the wind changed, and the market's thousand green lamps guttered all at once, and in the sudden dark Vesha heard — or thought she heard, or perhaps remembered hearing, the line had worn thin years ago — the far-off ringing of bells that belonged to no tower in Vhalen.

Winter was coming down off the lake country. She could feel it in her teeth.

She picked up the vial, and held it out, and let the past take her customer's hand.
