## 12. The Fragment That Bites

Vesha's back room smelled of cedar oil and cold iron, the way all her storerooms smelled — a scent Emrys would come to associate, in the years after, with the particular dread of holding something that did not belong to them.

The fragments lay in shallow drawers lined with felt, each one wrapped in grey silk like a thing in mourning for itself. Vesha handled them with tongs. Not silver tongs, not ritual tongs — kitchen tongs, chipped and blackened, which somehow made it worse. This was not reverence. This was inventory.

"Rule one," she said, without looking up. "You don't touch them bare-handed."

"I thought they were memories," Emrys said. "I thought you had to touch them to read them."

"You have to *want* to touch them." She set a drawer down on the scarred worktable between them. "Wanting is what opens them. That's why the guild keeps its extractors numb. Poppy milk, half a dose, every morning of their working lives. A man who wants nothing can handle anything." Her eyes came up then, sharp and amber in the lamplight. "You want plenty, scribe. I can smell it on you. So we do this my way."

Outside, the Nightmarket went about its midnight business. Through the single high window Emrys could hear the murmur of it — the haggling, the small bells vendors hung over their stalls to warn off thieves, the wet slap of the canal against pilings. Somewhere out there Iri was sitting cross-legged beside Vesha's sleeping cat, eating honeyed bread with the solemn concentration of a child who had learned, recently and hard, that food could be taken away. Emrys kept one ear turned toward that small sound the way you keep a hand on a rope in wind.

"Which one," Emrys said.

Vesha's mouth thinned. "You understand what you're asking. These aren't stories. They're *pieces of people*. When you open one, you don't watch it. You're in it. And when it's done, it leaves sediment. Everyone who handles Serevani goods comes away carrying grit that isn't theirs. Ask any fence in this market why they drink."

"I'm asking anyway."

"Because most of them are cowards," she said, "and you're something worse. You're homesick for a place that never existed." She drew a long breath through her nose, and for a moment — just a moment — the merchant's mask slipped, and underneath was something older and wearier, a woman who had been doing this too long and knew precisely what the merchandise cost. "Fine. But we start small. Bread baking. A wedding. Something with no teeth."

She lifted fragments from the drawer one by one, holding each to the lamp. They were beautiful, Emrys had to admit that. Small panes of something like glass but warmer, like ice that had been breathed on, like the skin of milk. Light moved inside them slowly, the way light moves under water. Each carried a faint color of its own — gold, green, a bruised violet — and a faint sound, if you held it close to your ear. Not words. More the *shape* of words, the way a seashell holds the shape of the sea.

"Bread," Vesha said, setting one aside. "Wedding. Rain on a roof — everyone likes rain on a roof. Harvest festival, partial, cracked at one edge, still good." Her hands paused over the next fragment, and Emrys saw her hesitate — actually hesitate, this woman who haggled over grief like fishmongers haggle over herring.

"What is that one?"

"Archive," she said shortly. "Provenance unknown. Came in with a lot from a guild clerk who owed me money. It's been in that drawer eleven years because nobody will buy it and I won't melt it down." She set it apart from the others, slightly farther, the way you'd set apart a knife you'd once cut yourself on. "It bites."

"Bites how?"

"It doesn't let go until it's done with you. And it isn't done quickly." She looked at Emrys for a long moment. "You're going to ask for it. I can see you deciding already. That's the wanting I told you about."

Emrys said nothing, which was answer enough.

* * *

They started small, as promised.

The bread fragment was nothing and everything. One moment Emrys stood in Vesha's back room with lamplight and cedar; the next they were standing in a kitchen that smelled of yeast and woodsmoke, watching enormous brown hands fold dough on a floury board, feeling — not seeing, *feeling* — the satisfaction of a morning's work rising warm in the chest. There was a window. There was a child's voice somewhere upstairs, singing badly. The whole thing lasted perhaps forty heartbeats, and when it ended Emrys came back to themselves with tears running freely down their face and no idea what they were crying about.

"There it is," Vesha said quietly, offering a cloth. "Sediment."

"It wasn't even sad."

"No. It was *happy*. That's worse, first time. Sadness you've got room for. Happiness you lost before you ever had it — that fills up every crack you have." She took the spent fragment, now dull and grey as river glass, and dropped it into a jar of sand. "Again?"

"Again."

The rain on the roof. The wedding — a bride with ink-stained fingers laughing so hard she couldn't say her vows, and Emrys laughing with her, helpless, married to the joy of a stranger for thirty seconds. The cracked harvest festival, which tasted of apples and ended mid-hymn, the last note hanging unfinished in Emrys's skull like a bell struck and never allowed to fade.

Each time, coming back was harder. Each time, Vesha's back room seemed smaller, dimmer, more temporary — a shed built on the edge of a country Emrys had never seen and was beginning, horribly, to grieve.

And through it all, on the far side of the table, the archive fragment sat in its silk like a closed eye.

* * *

"Iri asked me today what bells sound like," Emrys said.

They had stopped. Vesha was rubbing her hands with salt and oil, an old handler's habit, and Emrys realized their own voice had gone strange — flat, careful, the voice of someone walking on ice.

"The child remembers orchards," Emrys went on. "Bells. A grandmother who sang while she shelled beans. But when I asked about bells — actual bells, the sound — there was nothing. Like a page torn out. Whatever took Serevan took the sound of its bells first, or last, or most thoroughly. And Iri looks at me when they ask these questions like I'm supposed to know. Like I'm the book they were sent to read." Emrys turned the archive fragment over in its silk, not touching it, barely breathing on it. "What if I am?"

"That's not how it works."

"Isn't it? You recognized my face, Vesha. From a fragment. From *the burning*. Eleven years you've kept that one too, haven't you? You didn't sell it because—" Emrys stopped. Because selling it meant admitting she'd watched it. Because some things a fence keeps the way a drunk keeps the bottle he swears he'll pour out tomorrow.

Vesha's jaw worked. "Say your piece."

"A child walked out of a snowstorm speaking a dead language and called me by a name I've never used. I found ledger entries in my own hand dated before my birth. I was born *inside* Serevan, whatever the guild's arithmetic says, and someone sold the proof along with everything else." Emrys's hands were steady. That surprised them. Inside, something was screaming in a register too high to hear. "So yes. I want the one that bites. I want to know what I'm grieving before it eats me alive wondering."

For a long moment the only sound was the canal slapping its pilings, and Iri's low voice beyond the wall, telling the cat something important about orchards.

Vesha swore softly, fluently, in three languages, two of them probably extinct. Then she pushed the silk-wrapped fragment across the table.

"One condition. I stay in the room. If you go under longer than a count of five hundred, I break contact however I have to. Broken arm, broken fragment, broken rule — I don't care. Agreed?"

"Agreed."

"And scribe." She caught Emrys's wrist as they reached for it. Her grip was stronger than her frame suggested, calloused exactly where a lifetime of tongs would put calluses. "Whatever you see in there — it happened to someone. Maybe to you. But it's finished. Finished things only have the power you give them by wanting." Her eyes flicked to the wall, toward the sound of Iri's voice. "Remember what you're carrying it home *for*."

Emrys unwrapped the silk.

* * *

The fragment was warm.

That was the first wrongness — every other fragment had been cool, river-cool, ice-cool. This one was warm as a hand, warm as a cheek, and it pulsed very faintly against Emrys's palm, quick and light, like something asleep that could be woken.

Then it opened, and there was no more Vesha, no more lamplight, no more Nightmarket.

There was dust motes turning in tall windows.

Emrys stood in a hall of shelves that rose past sight into shadow — not the cramped dark of the Vhalen Ledger Guild's stacks but something *cathedral*, vaulted and pale, every shelf carved from wood the color of cream and honey. Air moved through the hall the way air moves through mountains, slow and mineral-cold. And everywhere, everywhere, were books — no. Not books. Ledgers. Tens of thousands of ledgers, spines lettered in a script Emrys could not read and yet understood completely, the way you understand your own heartbeat: *here is a name, here is a birth, here is a debt paid, here is a life accounted.*

The Archive of Serevan. Emrys knew it the way you know the inside of your own mouth. Knew which stair creaked. Knew that the third window from the north end stuck in summer. Knew—

Knew the weight of the ledger under their own arm. Because they were carrying one. Their hands — younger hands, unscarred, a scribe's calluses still soft — held a ledger bound in blue-grey leather, and their sleeves were rolled, and there was ink on the side of their right palm in the exact place Emrys had worn ink their whole remembered life.

*I worked here,* Emrys thought, and the thought had no floor beneath it.

Around them, the archive was dying politely.

That was the only way to describe it. Clerks moved along the aisles with the unhurried calm of people following procedure, and the procedure was horror. At the far end of the hall, a shelf stood empty — not cleared, *empty*, the wood gone grey and featureless as driftwood, as though the shelf itself had been unwritten. A woman in archive-grey stood before the next shelf, reading aloud in a low steady voice, and as she read, the spines faded. Letter by letter. Name by name. She wasn't destroying them. She was *deeding* them — transferring title — and her face was wet and her voice never shook, because the alternative to procedure was screaming, and she had chosen.

"They started with the outer provinces at dawn."

The voice came from beside Emrys — a man, old, white-bearded, wearing the deep blue sash of a Keeper of Records. He was looking at Emrys with an expression Emrys had received a thousand times in the guild: the look of a superior assessing whether a subordinate will hold. Except this man's eyes were red-rimmed, and his hands trembled around the seal-stamp he carried.

"Master Oyelaran," he said. "Are the western annexes done?"

*Oyelaran.* The name landed in Emrys's chest like a stone dropped down a well — falling, falling, no bottom yet.

"Yes," their own mouth said, in a voice higher and younger than theirs. "All of them. Master Keeper, the clerks are asking—"

"I know what they're asking." The old man's laugh was terrible, dry as a moth. "They're asking if it can be stopped. Tell them no. Tell them it was stopped thirty years ago, the day the first signature was bought. We are not being invaded, boy. We are being *foreclosed*." He pressed the seal-stamp into Emrys's hands. "Take this to the deep vault. And take the children's registry with it — the original, not the copy. Whatever survives tonight, that must survive. Do you understand me? Names are the last thing to go. As long as the names exist somewhere outside their reach, the country isn't dead. It's only *closed*."

Emrys's younger self took the stamp. Took the registry. And in the moment of taking, looked up — and through the tall windows, past the dust motes, saw the sky.

It was the wrong color. Not storm-dark, not dusk. It was the color of a page when ink is poured across it and begins to soak — a creeping grey-gold stain spreading from the horizon inward, and where it spread, the mountains were going smooth. Featureless. Unwritten. Somewhere beyond those hills a city was forgetting its own streets, and the sky was simply the record of it, kept in the only ink left.

"We should run," Emrys's young mouth said.

"Where?" the Keeper asked, almost gently. "Child, there is nowhere *outside* anymore. That's what erasure means. We aren't going anywhere. We are going *not*." He straightened his sash with two trembling tugs, an old man tidying himself for death the way you tidy a bed. "Go. The vault. Quickly. And boy —" he caught Emrys's sleeve, and his grip was fierce, desperate, the grip of a man passing a torch through fire — "when they come for your name, and they will come for yours especially, remember: a sold thing can be bought back. A given thing can be returned. Nothing that was written is ever entirely unwritten. Ink remembers. Ink *always* remembers."

He turned back to his shelves and began, in a low steady voice, to read names aloud into the spreading grey.

Emrys ran. Down stairs that creaked in the places they had always creaked, through halls of fading spines, clutching the children's registry to their chest like a child of their own — and behind them the great doors of the archive boomed shut, and the sound they made was not the sound of doors closing but of a word being finished, a period set down at the end of a sentence forty thousand years long—

* * *

"—four hundred ninety-eight, four hundred ninety-nine—"

Emrys came back to Vesha counting, to lamplight, to cedar and iron, to their own body racked sideways across the chair with their hand still fused to the fragment. Vesha had a knife in her fist and was measuring the distance to Emrys's forearm with a professional eye.

"Don't," Emrys rasped. Their throat was raw. Had they been screaming? The silence afterward had the shape of screaming.

"How long?"

"Longer than five hundred. I lost count around the bells." Emrys pried their fingers loose one at a time. The fragment fell to the felt, spent now, grey as ash — and as it cooled, the last image rose off its surface like breath off winter glass: a tall window, dust motes, a sky the color of soaking ink.

Vesha looked at it. Looked at Emrys. All the merchant had gone out of her face; what remained was older and much less comfortable.

"You were there," she said. It wasn't a question.

"I worked in the archive." Emrys heard their own voice as if from a distance. "I carried the children's registry to the deep vault. There was a Keeper who gave me his seal and told me—" The words snagged. Emrys pressed the heels of both hands against their eyes, and the grief arrived then, all of it at once, unearned and undeniable and decades late: grief for cream-colored shelves, for a creaking stair, for an old man reading names aloud while his country soaked into the sky. Grief with no right to exist, in a body that had never stood in that hall — except it had, it *had*, someone had sold the standing-there and left the ache behind like a coat left behind at a house that burned.

"Sediment," Vesha said softly. "I warned you."

"This isn't sediment." Emrys lowered their hands. "This is a *map*. Vesha — the deep vault. The registry. If the names survived, if ink remembers, then somewhere under that lake or behind that Door or wherever Serevan went, there's a book with my name in it. My real name. Proof of what I was before the guild made me a line item." Emrys stood, and the chair went over backward, and neither of them reached for it. "Corvane offered to forgive my debt. I finally understand what he was really buying."

From beyond the wall, the small voice stopped mid-sentence about orchards.

A beat of silence. Then Iri's footsteps, quick and light, and the door opening, and the child standing in the gap with the lamplight behind them — and above the Nightmarket's murmur, above the canal-slap and the vendor-bells, a new sound was threading into the world outside. Faint. Rhythmic. Many feet, moving in perfect unison, making no other sound at all.

Vesha went white. She blew out the lamp.

"Silent Court," she whispered. "They followed the fragment. Get the child. *Now.*"
