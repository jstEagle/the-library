## 13. Silent Court

The Nightmarket had a sound it made when it was happy — a low hum of haggling and lamplight and the wet slap of fish-sellers' knives on old boards — and at some hour past midnight that sound stopped.

Not faded. Stopped. The way a heart stops: all at once, with everything after it only echo.

Emrys heard it from the back room of Vesha's shop, where they had been cataloguing fragments by candlelight, Iri asleep on a pallet of sacking with her cheek pressed to a coil of rope as if it were a pillow she'd chosen in another life. One moment there was the market's murmur through the shuttered window; the next, nothing, and the nothing was so complete that Emrys could hear the candle flame eating its own wick.

They set down the fragment they were holding — a small glass lozenge that sloshed faintly when tilted, labeled in Vesha's cramped hand *rain on a school roof, Serevani dialect* — and stood very still, the way one does when a floorboard creaks in an empty house.

"Vesha," they whispered, though they knew she was out front, sleeping in the chair behind her counter because she never trusted anyone else to guard her stock. "Vesha."

No answer. That was wrong too. Vesha answered everything — insults, questions, the market bells, the weather. She answered silence itself, usually with profanity.

The candle guttered. Not from wind. From cold.

It came under the door like water finding the low places, a cold that had nothing to do with winter — a cold with intention in it, patient and old. Frost feathered up the inside of the window shutters in patterns Emrys almost recognized: not ferns, not flowers, but script. Rows and rows of fine, looping characters, written in frost across every wooden surface, climbing the walls like ivy.

Iri sat up without waking fully, the way children do when danger has a smell. Her eyes opened halfway. "Sef," she said, using the name again, the name that fit Emrys's mouth like a key fits a lock they'd never seen. "Don't let them take my page."

"I won't." Emrys tucked the stolen ledger leaf inside their shirt, against their skin, where it felt less like paper than like a second heartbeat. "Stay behind me."

*Behind me.* As if that would help. As if Emrys Oyelaran, debt-bound copyist, a person whose entire martial experience consisted of fending off aggressive ink-stains, could stand between a child and whatever walked outside making markets go silent.

The front door did not open. It simply ceased to be closed — one moment solid oak with three locks Vesha had shown off proudly, the next standing wide and dark, and the darkness beyond it was deeper than the street should have allowed, deeper than the night, a darkness with figures standing in it.

There were five of them. Or perhaps seven; the eye kept sliding off them the way a thumb slides off wet glass. They wore grey — not cloth-grey but the grey of things forgotten, the color of a word on the tip of the tongue, of a face you can't place at a funeral. Their heads were bare and their faces were smooth as river stones, features worn down not by age but by disuse, as if no one had looked at them in so long that looking had finally given up.

And they did not walk so much as *arrive*, each step placing them somewhere new without troubling itself over the distance between.

The Silent Court. Emrys had heard the name whispered in the Nightmarket's corners, always quickly, always with a glance toward the doors. The Hollow King's remnant. His mourners. His collectors.

One of them raised a hand — or something hand-shaped — and pointed past Emrys entirely.

At Iri.

"No," Emrys said, and was surprised by how steady their voice came out. Fear, it turned out, burned clean if you fed it enough. "She's not his. She's not anyone's. She's—"

The nearest figure tilted its smooth head. And though it had no mouth Emrys could see, no eyes, nothing that should have carried speech, a voice arrived anyway — arriving the way the cold had, seeping in through the low places, and it was a voice Emrys recognized with a lurch that went through them like a blade finding the gap between ribs.

It was the voice from the fragment. The burning archive. The voice that had said *hold the line, hold the line, we are still here* while the world came apart around it.

"You carry what is owed," the figure said. All five spoke together, or rather one voice used five absence-of-mouths. "The King grieves. The King requires."

"He can grieve on his own time." It was Vesha's voice, hoarse and furious, from the front room — and Emrys's heart lurched worse than it had at the cold, because she was supposed to be gone, supposed to have slipped out the back hours ago as any sensible fence would have done. Instead she came through the doorway with a lantern in one hand and a long skinning knife in the other, silver hair loose, nightshirt under a man's coat, sixty years old and utterly unimpressed by the impossible. "You want the child, you'll be wanting a receipt first. Everything in this market goes through proper channels. Even grief."

"*Vesha,*" Emrys breathed. "*Run.*"

"Run? In my own shop?" She spat, which for Vesha constituted a prayer. "I've been robbed by guild enforcers, taxed by two governments, and once held hostage by a man who thought he was a swan. You think I'll run from a pack of faceless debt-collectors in my nightclothes?"

The Silent Court moved.

Later, Emrys would try to remember exactly what happened, and the memory would come back in pieces, the way broken glass comes back: a shape, a flash, a sound. Vesha's lantern shattering and the oil catching on the floorboards, firelight suddenly everywhere, throwing the Court's shadows huge and wrong against the walls. Vesha's knife actually connecting with one of them — Emrys saw it bite home, saw grey stuff part like fog — and the figure not falling, not bleeding, only becoming slightly more difficult to see. Iri screaming and grabbing Emrys's sleeve. The frost-script on the walls flaring white-hot wherever the fire touched it, as if the writing itself objected to being warm.

And underneath it all, that voice, calm as a clerk reading terms:

"The King is patient. The King is kind. What is owed will be collected. Interest accrues."

"There's the problem with you people," Vesha snarled, backing toward the counter, knife up, lantern oil spreading flame around her feet like a moat she was building too late. "You talk like the ledger's already balanced. Nothing's ever owed until someone decides it is, and I decide—" 

The two figures nearest her moved at once, and this time Emrys saw how it worked: they didn't grab. They *surrounded*. They placed themselves at the points of a shape around her, a shape Emrys's eye refused to hold — five-sided, seven-sided, a geometry that hurt to look at — and where the shape closed, Vesha was simply elsewhere. Not gone. Elsewhere. Standing among them, knife arm pinned, her face turned toward Emrys and Iri with an expression Emrys had never seen on her before and hoped never to see again.

Not fear. *Instruction.*

"Back room," she snapped. "Under the floor. Trapdoor behind the shelving, third board, iron ring. The catacombs run under half the city and twice under the guild, and whatever you do—" her voice cracked, just once, just barely, "—whatever you do, don't come back for me. I'm worth more to them talking than dead. Probably. Don't check."

"Vesha—"

"*Go*, you sentimental little debtor! You think I saved your life at the market to watch you spend it on my account?" Firelight made her grin ferocious. "Besides — you still owe me for the fragment. Dead men don't pay. Now *move*."

The Court tightened its shape around her. And Emrys — who had spent thirty years learning that the wise move and the survivable move were usually the same move, who had built a whole careful gray life on knowing when to keep their head down and their hand flat on the table — grabbed Iri's wrist and ran.

* * *

The trapdoor was where Vesha said, behind the shelving, under the third board, the iron ring cold enough to burn. Emrys hauled it open onto black air that smelled of stone and deep water and something older — dust that had never been disturbed, or dust that had been waiting. A ladder of iron rungs set into the wall went down farther than the candlelight reached.

Iri went first, quick as a squirrel, no fear of the dark at all — which told Emrys something, and filed it away in the growing drawer of things about this child that didn't add up. Emrys followed, dragging the trapdoor shut above them, and had the presence of mind to drag the shelving unit over it too, though they both knew it would stop nothing. It wasn't meant to stop anything. It was meant to buy minutes, and minutes were the only currency they had left.

They descended into the catacombs.

The dark down there was total, and then it wasn't: Iri produced light. That was the only way Emrys could describe it. She cupped her hands and something glowed between them, soft and gold-green, like sunlight remembered rather than seen — orchard light, Emrys thought absurdly, the light of a country that no longer existed leaking out of a child's palms.

"How—"

"I don't know," Iri said, in the tone of someone who had decided some time ago not to ask. "It happens when I'm scared. It's like — like remembering hard enough that it spills."

The tunnel stretched away in both directions, hewn from stone older than the city above, older maybe than the idea of cities. And the walls —

Emrys stopped walking.

The walls were covered in names.

Carved, chiseled, scratched, burned — names in their tens of thousands, names in columns and spirals and great deliberate processions, some in the common script of Vhalen and some in the flowing, rooted lettering Emrys now recognized from frost and fragment alike: Serevani. Names upon names upon names, rising out of reach overhead and vanishing into the dark below, covering every surface like moss covers a forest floor. Some were fresh-edged. Some were worn nearly smooth. And here and there, beside certain names, someone had carved a second mark: a small square, like a door seen from the front.

"They knew," Iri said quietly. Her light trembled. "Before it happened. Some of them knew, and they came down here and carved everyone's names so that — so that even if the country was taken, the names would still be somewhere. Written down. You said — you told me once, in the guild, that a thing written down can't be fully lost."

"I said that?" Emrys touched the stone. Under their fingertips, grooves worn shallow by decades of dark. *Maren, daughter of Odile, baker of the Low Orchard district.* The name sat in Emrys's chest like a swallowed coal. They had never read it before. They were certain they had never read it before. And yet their finger had found it without searching, the way a tongue finds a chipped tooth.

"Sef?" Iri's light swung toward them. "Your face went strange."

"My face is often strange lately." But Emrys kept their hand on the name a moment longer, and could not have said why, and hated that they could not say why, and then made themselves let go.

Above them, muffled by stone and earth, the shop was burning. Emrys could hear it — or imagined they could, which amounted to the same thing: the soft roar of flame, the crack of beams. Vesha was up there, in the hands of the Hollow King's court, and she had bought their escape with a ferocity Emrys hadn't earned, and the weight of that settled onto Emrys's shoulders with a specificity that debt never quite managed. Debt was abstract. This was not. This was a woman's grin in firelight saying *don't come back for me*, and Emrys already knowing, with sick certainty, that they were going to spend every remaining hour trying to find a way to break that instruction without getting Iri killed.

Survivor's shame, Vesha would have called it, if she were here to sneer at them. *The worst kind of guilt — the kind that comes free.*

"We keep moving," Emrys said. "Which way?"

Iri considered the branching tunnel ahead — three ways, all identical, all carved with names. Then she closed her eyes, and her light pulsed once, gently, like a heartbeat deciding.

"That way," she said, pointing left. "It smells like rain on a school roof."

Emrys stared at her. "That's a fragment. That's literally a fragment Vesha had in her inventory. *Rain on a school roof, Serevani dialect.*"

"I know." Iri opened her eyes, and in the orchard-light they were ancient and young at once. "That's how I know it's the right way. The country remembers itself, Sef. Little pieces of it are scattered everywhere, in vials and ledgers and people's heads, and sometimes the pieces pull toward each other. Like iron filings. Like birds going home." She hesitated. "Like me."

They walked. The tunnel bent and branched and bent again, and always the names went with them, and Emrys found themselves reading as they went — couldn't stop reading — *Aldous, ferryman. Petra, midwife. The twins Sefa and Sefan, aged six, who shared one pair of boots between them* — and each name landed somewhere in Emrys's body and stayed, accumulating, a cargo they had no space for. By the tenth branch their throat ached from swallowing. By the twentieth they had stopped trying to explain to themselves why a ferryman's name should feel like grief.

Because it wasn't theirs. It couldn't be theirs. They had been born in Vhalen, in the guild's shadow, entered into the Ledger at birth with a debt assigned before they could walk. They had never seen an orchard. They had never heard these bells Iri spoke of. Whatever the fragments showed them, whatever the child insisted, it was sympathy, resonance, the ordinary magic of a story told well—

Their foot caught on something and they pitched forward, hands slapping stone, and found themselves nose to nose with a carving at ankle height, small and crude, done by someone kneeling.

Two names, side by side, in Serevani script. And beneath them, in Vhalen common, in a different, later hand, a single translated line:

*Sefa and Sefan. We could only carve one of you twice. Forgive us.*

Emrys knelt there a long time.

"Sef." Iri's hand, small and warm, on their shoulder. Above them, far away, faint through fathoms of stone, a bell began to ring — one toll, deep and slow, that neither of them could hear so much as feel, rising through the rock into their bones. Iri's light flared bright and her breath went ragged. "Do you feel that? It's the bells. It's *her* bells, the big one, the one from the tower in the capital — she only rang for coronations and funerals and—"

"And beginnings," Emrys heard themselves say. In a voice not quite their own. In an accent they had never learned. "Endings. And beginnings."

The bell tolled again. Somewhere in the dark ahead, water dripped in rhythm with it, as if the catacombs themselves were keeping time. And Emrys understood, with the terrible clarity that had been gathering in them all night like frost, that the Silent Court had not come for Iri alone.

They had come because the King had felt it too. Something moving under the ice of the world. A Door, remembering it was a door.

Iri tugged their sleeve, pointing down the left-hand passage, where the names ran thickest and the dark ran deepest. "Sef," she whispered. "I think the way out of here is also the way in."

Emrys rose, knees cracking, and took the child's hand.

Behind them, far above, the last of Vesha's shop fell in, and the bell tolled a third time, and the names on the walls seemed — Emrys would swear this later, and no one would believe them — to lean forward, listening.
