## 14. Catacombs of the Unwritten

The dark down here was not like other dark.

Iri had known darknesses before — the dark behind closed eyelids, the dark inside a memory that refused to surface, the long dark between one name and the next. But this dark had weight. It pressed against her skin like water, like wool, like hands. It smelled of stone and old smoke and something sweeter underneath, something like apples left too long in a cellar.

"Emrys," she whispered, and her voice came back to her wrong, flattened and multiplied, as if a hundred small girls were whispering it back from every direction at once.

"I'm here." Emrys's hand found hers in the blackness. Their palm was cold and ink-stained and shaking, and Iri held on to it the way she imagined a drowning person holds to driftwood — not because it would save them, but because letting go was unthinkable. "Don't let go. Whatever you hear, don't let go."

They had come down through a grate behind Vesha's burning shop, down a ladder slick with condensation, into passages that no map of Vhalen admitted to owning. Behind them, muffled by stone and distance, they could still hear the Silent Court moving through the upper city — not footsteps exactly, for they made none, but a kind of pressure, a hush that traveled, the sound of a room when everyone in it stops breathing at once.

Iri knew what hunted them. She carried the knowing the way she carried everything: in the crowded, echoing country of herself. The Silent Court were the Hollow King's grief given legs and sent out walking. They did not want to hurt her. That was the terrible part. They wanted to *bring her home* — home being a word that meant, in their mouths, a king's chest cavity, where she would be kept forever, safe and sealed and never again allowed to be anything but remembered.

She shivered. Emrys felt it and squeezed her hand.

"We should keep moving," Emrys murmured. "There has to be an outlet. The undercity floods every spring; there must be channels, drains—"

"There isn't." Iri said it quietly, and then, because Emrys deserved more than quiet, she said the rest. "Not here. Not in this part. Emrys — do you know where we are?"

"The catacombs. Under the old quarter. Everyone knows Vhalen has catacombs."

"No." Her voice caught. She swallowed and tried again. "Everyone knows Vhalen *thinks* it has catacombs. Old bones, old nobles, that's what the guidebooks say. But these aren't Vhalen's dead, Emrys. These are ours."

The silence that followed was so complete that Iri could hear Emrys stop breathing.

"Ours?"

"Serevan's." She turned slowly in the dark, and even though she could see nothing, she raised her free hand anyway, gesturing at the walls she knew were there. "We're standing inside Serevan's unburied dead."

* * *

It had begun, she told Emrys, three years before the end.

Iri had not been alive then — or rather, the girl-shaped thing she now was had not yet been assembled — but she contained people who had been. That was the strange arithmetic of her existence: she remembered things that happened before she was made, because the ones who remembered them lived inside her, shelved like books, like jars, like seeds in a granary waiting for spring. When she spoke of those years, she was never sure whose mouth the words came from. Sometimes all of them at once.

Three years before the erasure, the ledgers had started coming to Serevan.

That was how it worked, she explained, walking with Emrys by touch along a wall that hummed faintly under her fingertips, a vibration too low to be sound, more like a held breath. The Ledger Guild had not arrived with soldiers. It had arrived with clerks. Polite men and women in grey coats who set up tables in the market squares and offered to buy.

"They bought memories first," Iri said. "Little ones, at first. A woman sold the taste of her mother's bread for enough coin to fix her roof. A man sold his grief over a drowned brother — he said he'd wanted rid of it for years anyway. And the guild paid well, Emrys. That's what nobody outside ever understood. They paid *beautifully*. People lined up. People fought each other for places in line."

"And then?" Emrys's voice was very controlled. Iri had learned to read the scribe's control as a kind of fear wearing its best clothes.

"And then the prices went up, and the goods got bigger. A childhood. A marriage. A language. There was a man in our town who sold the whole of his twenties and woke up the next morning not recognizing his wife, and the guild clerk wrote it all down so gently, so kindly, asked him if he was sure, gave him tea." Iri's throat tightened around the memory — someone's memory, Maren's maybe, or the cooper's wife's, she could never be sure which shelf it sat on. "And when there was almost nothing left to sell, when the whole country had been picked over like a carcass, the guild made its final offer. To the Crown itself. They offered to buy Serevan's *existence*. Every record, every map, every mention. To take the country out of the world's memory the way they'd taken bread-taste out of a baker's mouth. And the last king — the real one, the living one, not the thing he became —"

She stopped. In the dark, her hand found a groove in the stone, deep and deliberate, and she went still.

"What is it?" Emrys whispered.

"It's a name." Iri traced it with one finger. The carving was old but sure, cut by someone who had knelt here with a chisel and all the time in the world and no intention of hurrying. Letters in pre-kingdom Serevani, the old script that curved like river-water. "Emrys. Come here. Put your hand where mine is."

She guided them. She felt Emrys's fingers settle into the grooves beside hers, felt the scribe go rigid as the shapes resolved under their touch — a scribe, after all, who read with their hands as much as their eyes, who knew letters the way farmers know weather.

"That says *Yeva, daughter of Osten, who planted the orchard above Harrow Bell*," Iri said softly. "She died two years before the erasure. Her family couldn't afford a grave — the guild had bought up the burial grounds by then, converted them to storage vaults for the fragments. So her son brought her down here instead. And he didn't bury her, because there was nothing left of her to bury except the body, and the body wasn't the point. He carved her name instead. Because a name, written somewhere the guild hadn't thought to look, was the only grave that couldn't be repossessed."

* * *

They walked, and the names kept coming.

Iri counted them as they passed, the way she counted everything, because counting was how the children inside her stayed calm — one of them, a boy named Pell who had been frightened of thunder, used to count fence posts until the storms passed, and the habit had soaked through all of them like dye through cloth. One name. Two. Seven. Forty. Some were single lines, quick and desperate, cut by torchlight by people who feared the guild's auditors would find this place any day. Some were whole panels, generations of a family arranged in careful columns, with little symbols beside the names — a bell for the bell-ringers, a tree for the orchard-keepers, a small bird that Iri's fingers knew intimately because it stood for the children, always the children, *the children who will carry us*.

"Do you understand what this place is?" she asked, and her voice came out strange, layered, several ages speaking at once. She didn't try to flatten it. Down here, among the names, it felt dishonest to speak with only one voice. "It isn't a tomb. Nobody's buried here. It's a *ledger*, Emrys. The only ledger the guild never got to audit. When they erased Serevan, they took the records, the maps, the histories, the gravestones — oh, especially the gravestones, they melted the cemetery gates down for scrap and ground the stones into gravel for guild-house foundations. But they didn't know about this. The people who foresaw it — and some did foresee it, Emrys, some always see — they came down here with chisels and lamps and they wrote us into the rock. Where no entry can be voided. Where no debt can be called."

Emrys had stopped walking. Iri felt the stillness of them the way you feel a door close in another room.

"You said the catacombs were the unburied dead," Emrys said at last. "But there are no bodies."

"No."

"So the names aren't memorials. They're..." Iri heard the scribe's breath catch, heard the moment understanding landed like a dropped weight. "They're the people. All that's left of them. We've been walking through —"

"A census," Iri said. "Of everyone they couldn't otherwise prove existed."

The dark seemed to deepen around the words. Somewhere far off, water dripped with the patience of centuries, each drop landing in a pool with a soft glassy note, and Iri thought: *even the water remembers the shape of falling.* Everything down here remembered. The stone had been made to remember, deliberately, lovingly, by people who knew they were about to be unwritten and chose, with their final strength, to leave a mark that erasure could not reach.

She put both palms flat against the wall.

It was warm.

Not warm the way stone holds the day's heat — warm the way skin is warm. Faintly, stubbornly, impossibly alive. And beneath her hands, so faint she might have invented it, she felt the humming again, and this time she understood it. It was not one vibration. It was thousands. Millions of letters, side by side, name pressed against name against name, and the sum of all that writing made a sound the way a thousand bees make a sound — individually silent, collectively singing.

"They're not gone," she breathed. "Emrys. They're not *gone*. As long as these names exist, Serevan exists. Erasure can't touch this place because erasure works on records, on minds, on paper — and this was never paper. This is the country's own bone. This is what I'm carrying. This is why I am." She laughed, and the laugh cracked in the middle, because awe and grief had always lived too close together in her chest to be told apart. "I thought I was a vessel. I thought I was a bag of leftovers. But I'm a copy of *this*. Of this wall. Someone looked at all these names and thought: the stone might be found and broken. So we'll write them somewhere that walks."

* * *

They rested there, in the dark, with their backs against the singing stone.

Iri told Emrys more. She couldn't help it — the wall had opened something, or perhaps it was simply that fear, which had driven her since the night she'd appeared at the guild's door, finally loosened its grip enough to let the older, larger feelings through. She told them about the bells of Harrow Bell, which had rung in patterns rather than melodies, so that the whole valley could read the news in bronze. She told them about the orchards on the terraces above the lake, apples that kept until midsummer, cider that the old people swore could return a sold memory for one evening — one evening only, and worth every coin. She told them about the dusk, how Serevan's evenings lasted longer than anywhere else's, how the whole country had arranged its life around that long amber hour, how lovers complained and poets built careers on it.

And Emrys listened. Listened the way scribes listen, wholly, hungrily, and after a while Iri realized the scribe was crying without any sound at all, tears running steadily down a face that remained perfectly composed, and she understood — with the certainty of the many children inside her, who had all, in their different lives, watched adults grieve politely — that these were not her memories touching Emrys.

These were Emrys's own, knocking to be let out.

"You were there," she said gently. "I keep telling you, and you keep not believing me, and I don't blame you. But listen to yourself listening, Emrys. You're not hearing stories. You're hearing *names being read off a wall you already know*."

"Don't." Emrys's voice was barely there. "Please. If I start — if I let myself start believing it, I'll have to grieve thirty years all at once, and I can't, Iri, I have to keep us moving, I have to get us out—"

"You can't get us out." Iri said it kindly, and hated saying it. "That's what I've been trying to tell you. There's no exit from this section. The catacombs under the old quarter don't connect to the drains. They connect to—" She hesitated. Inside her, dozens of voices counseled silence, and dozens more insisted on truth, and the truth won, as it usually did down here among honest stones. "They connect to the Door, Emrys. Or near it. Why do you think the guild never mapped this place? Why do you think the Hollow King's Court moves like they know every passage? This is the road to Serevan. It always was. The unburied dead weren't hiding from the world. They were *pointing at it*."

Silence. Then, distantly, horribly, a change in the air pressure — the hush-that-travels, the stopped-breathing sound of the Silent Court, no longer streets away. Closer. Moving with the patient certainty of things that cannot be outrun, only outlasted or out-thought.

And beneath Iri's palms, the wall changed too. The humming shifted key — rose, brightened, quickened — and though she could see nothing, she suddenly *felt* the names around her the way you feel sunlight through a window: Yeva daughter of Osten, Pell who feared thunder, Maren the baker, hundreds upon hundreds, all of them pressing close, all of them awake, all of them saying with one stone-slow voice:

*This way. Child who carries us. This way home.*

Iri stood up in the absolute dark and took Emrys's hand.

"They're showing us the path," she whispered. "Come on. Before the Court finds the entrance we came down. And Emrys — whatever happens next — remember that we're not lost anymore. For the first time since I walked out of the snow, we're exactly where we're supposed to be."

Far ahead, where no lamp burned and no light had any right to be, a thin seam of gold opened in the blackness — dusk-colored, amber, the exact color of a Serevan evening — and began, slowly, to widen.
