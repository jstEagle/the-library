## 17. Bells Under the Ice

The map had stopped lying to them two days ago, which was how Emrys knew they were close.

It still breathed — the cartographer's ink swelling and shrinking like the ribs of a sleeping animal — but it no longer redrew itself each night. The road north held its shape: a gray thread through gray country, past villages whose names had been scraped off signposts so long ago that the wood had gone silver and smooth as bone. The world up here was a country of subtraction. No birds. No smoke from chimneys. Even the wind seemed to arrive already tired, as if it had crossed something vast and empty to reach them and had nothing left to say.

Iri walked ahead, small boots punching holes in the crusted snow, and did not complain. Emrys had learned not to offer rest. The child would only look at them with those too-old eyes and say, *We can stop when we're home,* in that language that should have died with everyone who spoke it, and Emrys's chest would do the thing it did — that aching, unmoored thing, grief arriving for a place they had never been.

*Home.* Iri said it about a country that did not exist. And the terrible thing, the thing Emrys could not put down, was that lately — when the wind came off the north with a certain smell under it, cold stone and blossom-rot and old honey — some part of Emrys agreed.

They crested the last ridge at what might have been noon, though the sky gave nothing away. It was the color of pewter, low and close, and below it lay the lake.

Emrys stopped walking.

It was not the size of it, though the lake was large enough that its far shore was a rumor rather than a fact. It was not even the ice, though the ice was wrong in a way Emrys felt in their teeth before they understood it with their eyes: too clear, too deep, glass laid over darkness instead of white over water, so that looking down was like looking down a well that went somewhere else entirely.

No. What stopped Emrys was the silence.

The whole journey north, the silence around them had been an absence — the hush of a world holding its breath. This was different. This silence was *full*. It pressed against the ears like water. It was the silence of a cathedral between hymns, of a room where someone has just finished speaking and everyone is waiting to see if anyone will answer.

"Iri," Emrys said quietly. "Wait."

But the child was already running.

* * *

Down the slope, snow giving way underfoot, Emrys followed, half-falling, one hand out for balance, the stolen guild ledger thumping against their ribs inside its oilcloth wrap. They caught Iri at the shoreline, catching the child's shoulder, turning them around — and found Iri laughing.

Not the careful, adult laugh Iri sometimes produced, the one that sat oddly on a child's face. This was real laughter, helpless and bright, and tears were freezing on the child's cheeks, and Emrys did not understand any of it until they heard it too.

Bells.

Under the ice. Under the impossible clear ice, far down in the dark, bells were ringing.

They were muffled, the way sound is muffled through a wall or through years, but they were unmistakably bells — great bronze voices swinging slow, one after another after another, a peal that rose and fell and rose again without ever quite resolving. Not a pattern Emrys knew. Not a call to any service. It sounded less like ringing than like remembering: each bell tolling a note it had been holding for thirty years, finally allowed to let it go.

"That's—" Emrys's voice came out cracked. They swallowed and tried again. "That's not possible. Sound doesn't carry through ice like that. Water freezes silent, it—"

"It's not the water." Iri had gone still under Emrys's hand, listening with their whole body, head tipped back, eyes closed. "It's the bells themselves. They never stopped. Nobody told them to stop."

"Someone erased an entire country," Emrys said. "I think somebody told everything to stop."

Iri opened their eyes. In the flat light they were the color of thawing ponds, green shot through with brown, and for a moment they looked at Emrys with such pity that Emrys's breath caught.

"You keep saying it like the erasure was loud," Iri said. "Like it was a war, or a fire, and everything broke. It wasn't. It was quiet. That's the whole point of it. You don't break a country, Sef. You *unwrite* it. And things that are unwritten don't know they've stopped." The child turned back toward the lake. "The bells kept ringing because nobody read them their entry. So they rang. Down there. In the dark. For thirty years."

*Don't call me Sef.* Emrys had meant to say it again, the way they'd said it every time before, and every time before the protest had died somewhere between their heart and their mouth, because the name fit like a key fits a lock they'd forgotten they carried. Instead they said nothing at all, and together they stepped onto the ice.

* * *

The first steps were the worst. Emrys expected the crack, the give, the plunge into black water — expected it so completely that when it didn't come, they felt almost cheated, standing on a surface hard and singing and solid as the floor of the world. But the ice made sounds anyway. Little reports, sharp as knuckles cracking, that ran away from their boots in silver threads. Whines and groans from deeper down, long tectonic sighs, as if the lake were talking in its sleep.

And beneath all of it, the bells.

Closer now, or maybe just clearer. Emrys could begin to distinguish them — three tones, then four, then more, layered like voices in a round. One bell rang high and quick, nervous, insistent. Another answered low and patient, over and over, the same grave note, like a parent calling a child home at dusk. And underneath everything, so deep Emrys felt it in the soles of their feet more than heard it, a single enormous bell tolled once every minute or so, and each time it did, the whole lake shivered.

"The Great Bell of Vessarin," Iri whispered. "It only rang for coronations. And for funerals of kings." A pause. "It's been ringing both at once, I think. All this time. It couldn't decide."

Emrys crouched, slowly, weight forward, and put their bare hand against the ice.

Cold bit through the skin instantly, a white flare of pain up the wrist — but beneath the pain, faint as a pulse, they felt the bells. Felt them travel up through the frozen water into their palm, into the bones of their arm, humming in the marrow. And something else came with the hum. Not memory exactly. Something older than memory, pre-verbal, the way you know a lullaby in your body before you know its words. The smell of beeswax and hot bronze. The particular ache in the shoulders of a bell-ringer at the end of a long peal. The feeling of standing in a tower while the whole country rings below you, every village answering every other, sound rolling across orchards and rooftops and river-meadows like weather—

Emrys snatched their hand back and sat down hard on the ice.

"Sef?" Iri was beside them at once, small hands hovering, afraid to touch.

"I saw—" Emrys pressed the stinging hand against their chest. Their heart was going like a trapped bird. "There was a tower. There were *orchards*, Iri, I could smell them, there was a woman pulling apples off a tree with her sleeves rolled up and she turned around and she looked right at me and she said—"

"What did she say?"

Emrys opened their mouth. Closed it. The words had been there — they had *been* there, warm and specific, a sentence with a face attached — and now they were gone, melted like frost on glass, leaving only the shape of where they'd been.

"I don't know," Emrys said, and hated how small it sounded. "I had it. I had her whole face. She had flour on her cheekbone, Iri, I could tell you which cheekbone, and now it's just — gone. Like the guild took it out of my hand."

Iri knelt down next to them. For a long moment the child said nothing, and the bells rang on beneath them, patient, patient, the low one calling home at dusk.

"She'll say it again," Iri said finally. "When the Door opens. Everything anyone ever said is still in there. Nothing got destroyed, Sef. That's what nobody understands about erasure. It doesn't kill anything. It just puts it somewhere and locks the room." The child looked down through the ice, down into the dark where the light of the pewter sky gave out. "She's still standing in her orchard. Still turning around. Still about to speak. She's been about to speak for thirty years."

Emrys looked down too.

And this time, because they knew to look, they saw it.

Far below — impossibly far, farther than any lake had a right to be deep — there was light. Not the scattered gray of daylight through ice. Light with color in it: gold, mostly, and a green-gold like sun through leaves, and here and there a point of red or blue that might have been a lantern, a window, a festival ribbon. Shapes moved in it, or seemed to. A suggestion of streets. A suggestion of towers, one of them crowned with something vast and dark that swung, very slowly, back and forth.

Serevan. Suspended under the ice like a coin at the bottom of a well. Whole. Waiting.

"Oh," Emrys said. It was not a word so much as a wound. They gripped the ice with both hands as if the lake might tip and pour them down into it. "Oh, it's *there*. It's really there. It's been *here* the whole time, under everything, under the whole world—"

"Yes," Iri said softly. "You're standing on it. You've probably stood on it before. Half the roads in the north cross it. People picnic on it in summer." The child smiled, and it was the saddest smile Emrys had ever seen. "Nobody looks down. Why would they? There's nothing down there. There's never been anything down there. Ask anyone."

*Ask anyone.* Emrys thought of the guild's inventories, the neat columns of impossible things — bottled laughter, a key to nowhere, a jar of last words — each item priced and shelved and utterly unremarked upon by a world that had agreed, entry by entry, to forget why any of it mattered. The erasure hadn't been an event. It had been an accounting. And accountings could be contested.

Emrys had spent eleven years learning exactly how entries could be contested.

"We're close to the Door," they said. Their voice had changed; they could hear it, steadier, harder, the scribe's voice, the ledger voice. "The map says the shore bends east past that point—" they nodded at a black promontory far along the near shore, "—and then there's a cove, and the Door is at the bottom of the cove. Under the ice?"

"Under the ice," Iri confirmed. "In the deepest part. Where the Great Bell's shadow falls."

"Then we walk."

* * *

They walked.

It took hours. The far side of the lake did not seem to get closer so much as to be revealed gradually, the way a truth is revealed, reluctantly, detail by detail. The ice sang under them the whole way — cracks shooting off in bright threads, the deep groans rising and falling with the bells until Emrys could no longer tell which sounds were the lake and which were the country, whether the ice was groaning under their weight or the bells were ringing through the ice or both, whether the boundary between the two worlds ran along the surface of the frozen water or somewhere else entirely, somewhere thinner.

Because that was the thing Emrys kept coming back to, the thing that made their palms sweat inside their gloves even as hope climbed higher in their chest: the ice was thin here. Not thin like danger. Thin like a membrane. Twice, crossing long pale seams where the freezing had been imperfect, Emrys had looked straight down without any dark water intervening at all — had looked directly into Serevan, close enough to see individual rooftops, a bridge, a market square where hundreds of people stood motionless mid-stride, mid-laughter, mid-reach toward one another's hands — and each time, the bells had come through *unmuffled*, full-throated, deafening, and Emrys had felt the country looking back.

Not empty. Not asleep, exactly. *Paused.* The difference mattered. Emrys had watched enough debtors come out of the extraction room to know the difference between a person who was gone and a person who was being held.

These people were being held.

At the third hour, the light began to fail — or the sky did; the light under the ice never wavered, that suspended golden dusk burning steady as a lamp — and the wind came around out of the north with teeth in it. Emrys called a halt in the lee of a pressure ridge, a heaved-up wall of blue ice taller than they were, and got a fire going from the last of their fuel, cupping the flame small and mean against the wind. Iri sat close, closer than usual, and Emrys did not comment on it. The child had been getting quieter as they went north, and Emrys had been telling themselves it was fatigue, or fear of the Hollow King's Silent Court, or the cold.

But watching Iri now, in the shrunken firelight, Emrys admitted what they had been refusing to admit for days: the child was fading. Not weakening — *fading*. The edges of Iri had gone slightly soft, slightly uncertain, the way ink goes when paper gets damp. When the child moved, sometimes the movement lagged a half-beat behind the intention. Once, turning toward the fire, Iri's face had been briefly, horribly indistinct — a child's face, yes, but any child's, a sketch of a child, before it firmed again into the face Emrys knew.

"Iri." Emrys kept their voice level. "Look at me."

The child looked. And smiled, and the smile was an apology, and Emrys's stomach dropped through the ice of the world.

"How much farther?" Emrys asked, though that wasn't the question.

"Not far. Tomorrow. Maybe the day after." Iri held their hands out to the flame. The firelight went through them a little. Just at the fingertips. Just barely. "The Door will know us. It always knows its own."

"And after the Door? After we're through?" Emrys leaned forward. "What happens to you, Iri? Tell me the truth. You owe me that much. I've lied to enough people for both of us."

For a moment Emrys thought the child would cry. Then, instead, Iri laughed — that bright, helpless laugh from the shoreline, but quieter now, worn down to its thread.

"You always ask that," Iri said. "Every time. Right before the ridge, right before the market, right before the catacombs. You always ask me what I am, and I always find a new way not to answer, and you always let me." The child looked into the fire. "You let me because you're afraid of the answer. And you're right to be. But we're almost there now, Sef, and the ice is thin, and I'm — I'm lighter than I was. I can feel pieces of me wanting to go home. So tonight, while the fire's warm and the bells are loud enough to cover us — tonight I'll tell you. I'll tell you what I am, and who put me together, and why they chose your face to follow through the dark."

Above them the sky went fully dark, and the stars came out, and beneath the stars the lake rang on — the high nervous bell, the low patient one, and under everything the Great Bell of Vessarin, tolling its long unresolved chord, coronation and funeral in a single note, while the fire burned down and Iri drew breath to speak.

Emrys realized, distantly, that they were gripping the stolen ledger so hard the oilcloth had torn.

Whatever the child said next, Emrys knew, there would be no walking back from it. Some doors only open inward.

"Tell me," Emrys said.
