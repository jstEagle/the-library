## 18. What Iri Is

The bells would not stop.

Iri had thought that once they reached the lake — once the ice lay flat and silver beneath their boots and the wind came down off the mountains carrying nothing but cold — the ringing would fade. That it belonged to the journey, to the walking, to the long dark of the catacombs where every carved name had hummed against their ribs like a struck string. But here, above the ice, the bells rang on. Not with their ears. With everything else.

*Under the ice,* Emrys had said an hour ago, kneeling, gloved palm pressed flat to the frozen surface as if greeting a sleeping animal. *Do you hear them? They're real. They're still real.*

Iri had heard them. Of course Iri had heard them. The bells were woven into them the way thread is woven into cloth — the noon bell of the Archive district, the cracked bell of Lowmarsh that rang a half-tone flat because no one had ever climbed the tower to fix it, the little handbell Maren Oyelaran used to call her daughter in from the orchard at dusk. Iri carried all of them. Iri carried everything.

That was the problem. That had always been the problem.

They made camp in the lee of a stone outcropping where the pines grew bent and old, their branches combed sideways by decades of wind off the water. Emrys built a small fire, feeding it with care, cupping the flame like something borrowed. The firelight moved across Emrys's face and made the hollows under their eyes look deeper. They had not slept properly since the Nightmarket. Since Vesha's door came apart in splinters and the Silent Court poured through it like smoke given intention. Since Vesha had turned to face them and said, in her dry market voice, *Go. Don't be stupid about it,* and then the dark had closed over her and she was gone.

Iri sat with their knees drawn up and watched Emrys watch the fire, and felt the weight of what they were about to do settle onto them like snowfall — soft, patient, total.

There was a version of this night where they said nothing. Where they walked to the edge of the ice tomorrow morning and put their bare feet on it and did what needed doing without ever explaining, because explaining was a kind of asking, and asking was a kind of risking, and Iri had spent their whole short life being told — by the ones who made them, in the wordless way vessels are told things — that they must never ask anyone to carry what they carry. It was too much. It had always been too much. That was the entire point of them.

But Emrys had bled for them. Emrys had traded ten years of their own remembered life — ten years gone now, scooped out like pulp from a fruit, sold to buy passage across the ice — and Iri had woken the next morning to a person who looked at them with polite, careful blankness, who did not know their name, who had nonetheless stepped between them and the enforcer's blade without hesitation, on instinct alone, the way a body flinches from fire.

You could not owe someone that and then lie to them by omission. Even a vessel knew that much. Especially a vessel.

"Emrys." Their voice came out smaller than they intended. "Can I tell you something? You won't remember it wrong, because you don't have anything to remember it against. But I need you to hear all of it before you say anything."

Emrys looked up. In the firelight their eyes were the color of strong tea, and tired, and kind in the specific way of people who have decided to be kind as an act of will rather than temperament. "All right," they said. "I'm listening."

Iri took a breath. And then another. And then, because there was no gentler way into it, they began.

* * *

"You know how the guild sells memories," Iri said. "Vials. Fragments. A man's last sight of his mother's face for three crowns. You've copied the inventories. You've written the labels."

"I've written a great many labels," Emrys said quietly. There was something raw underneath the words. Iri understood why. Somewhere in the guild's vaults, in some drawer, sat vials holding ten years of Emrys's life, each one neatly labeled in Emrys's own handwriting — because the extractionists always made the debtor label their own memories, Iri had learned that much watching the world, *it saves disputes later.*

"The erasure wasn't just Serevan disappearing," Iri said. "It was Serevan being *taken apart*. Do you understand the difference? A thing that disappears leaves a hole. A thing taken apart leaves pieces. And pieces can be sold."

The fire popped. Out on the lake, faint and terrible and beautiful, the bells rang on beneath the ice.

"When it happened — thirty years ago, the winter the maps went wrong — there were people who saw it coming. Archivists. Bell-keepers. One woman in the orchard country whose name I—" Iri stopped. Adjusted. Started again, the way you step over a gap in a floor. "There were people who understood that the Ledger Guild wasn't buying land or gold. It was buying *existence*, entry by entry. And they understood that when the last entry was written, everyone inside Serevan wouldn't die. Dying leaves something behind. They would simply stop having ever been. No graves, because no lives. No mourning, because nothing to mourn."

"So they did the only thing left," Emrys said. Iri glanced up sharply, but Emrys's face held no recognition — only the logic of it, arrived at fresh, the way a clever mind walks toward a horror on its own legs. "If a country can be unwritten, its memory can be rewritten somewhere else. They saved what they could."

"They saved the children," Iri said.

And there it was. The center of it. Iri felt it turn over inside them, the vast slow weight of it, all those small bright stored lives shifting like water in a jar.

"Not their bodies. Bodies couldn't leave — nothing inside the border could leave once the Door began to close. But memory isn't flesh. Memory is *record*. And records can be moved." Iri pulled up their sleeve, and in the firelight showed Emrys the inside of their forearm, where the skin was pale and fine-grained and marked — faintly, like watermarking in good paper — with lines of script too small to read. "Every child in Serevan whose parents could reach the archive in time. Every birthday. Every scraped knee. Every name called across an orchard at dusk. The archivists compiled them. All of them. Thousands of childhoods, compressed, bound together, and then—"

Iri stopped again. This was the part. This had always been the part. In the making of them, in the warm dark before they had ever opened their eyes, they had been told the purpose, and the purpose had been simple, and simple purposes are the cruelest ones.

"And then they gave the record legs," Iri said. "So someone could carry it home."

* * *

The silence that followed was not empty. It was full of the fire eating, and the wind combing the pines, and the bells below the ice ringing their patient, drowned peal. Emrys had gone very still. Stillness, Iri had learned, was what Emrys did instead of screaming.

"How old are you?" Emrys asked at last. It was not the question Iri had expected. It was better than the question Iri had expected.

"I don't know," Iri said, and found, saying it aloud, that it was true in a way that finally didn't frighten them. "I was made thirty years ago, in the archive, in the last hour. But I woke up eight years ago, in a ditch outside Vhalen, with a head full of other people's dawns. So maybe I'm thirty. Maybe I'm eight. Maybe I'm four thousand mornings of somebody's orchards all pressed together." They hugged their knees. "The vessel grows. That was part of the design. A child can walk into places and past eyes that a cart or a soldier couldn't. A child can be pitied. A child can be overlooked. Whoever built me built me to be *overlooked*. It's the most useful thing about me and I hate it."

"You're not overlooked," Emrys said. "Not by me."

"No," Iri agreed softly. "That's the strange part, isn't it? Everyone who's supposed to overlook me keeps failing."

Emrys fed a branch to the fire. Their hands were steady but their jaw was tight, and Iri watched them assemble the next question carefully, the way they assembled everything — a scribe's hands even now, even memoryless, ordering the world into entries.

"The memories," Emrys said. "The children's. Are they — are you aware of them? All the time?"

"All the time," Iri said. "Like wearing every coat you own in summer. I know the smell of Serevan bread. I've never eaten any. I know how to whistle through a grass stem because a boy named Tam learned it the spring he was seven, and his grandmother scolded him for wasting breath, and he taught it to his sister anyway. I know a lullaby with a false note in the third line because the woman who sang it couldn't sing, and her daughter loved the false note best, and asked for it specifically, *sing it wrong, Mama.*" Iri's voice caught on that one. It always caught on that one. "I know ten thousand things like that. I know them the way you know your own hands. And none of them are mine. That's what nobody understands about me. I am not a girl who remembers a country. I am a country's remembering, wearing a girl."

Emrys made a sound — small, involuntary, wounded — and Iri hurried on, because if they stopped now they might never finish.

"That's why the Hollow King wants me. Why his Silent Court crossed half the world chasing a child. He was king when it happened. He held the Door open as long as he could — longer than anyone believed possible — and when it shut he was outside it, and everything he was king *of* went inside. He has spent thirty years gathering fragments of his country. Buying them, stealing them, hoarding them. Grief-mad, Vesha called him, and she's right, but it's worse than madness. He thinks if he gathers enough pieces he can rebuild Serevan as a shrine. Frozen. Preserved. A kingdom of grief kept exactly as it was on the worst day, forever, with him on the throne of it." Iri looked at the fire. "And I am the largest piece that exists. I am the whole of Serevan's children in one small body. To him I'm not a messenger. I'm a reliquary. He doesn't want to open me and let them out. He wants to keep me shut, on a shelf, in a dark room, perfect and complete and *his*."

"And you?" Emrys asked. "What do you want?"

It was such a plain question. Such an ordinary, human question. Nobody had ever asked it — not the archivists who made Iri, who had given them purpose the way you give a mule a load; not the Hollow King, who wanted the vessel and not the soul inside it; not the guild, which wanted to crack them open like a geode and sell the glitter. Only Emrys, sitting by a small fire with ten years of their own life missing, asking a vessel what it *wanted*.

Iri found they were crying. It surprised them. They hadn't known the vessel could do that — or rather, they had known, because somewhere inside them a girl named Sefa had cried exactly this way at exactly this age, homesick without knowing the word, and the crying had been archived along with everything else, and now it lived in Iri's throat like a seed waiting for weather.

"I want to give them back," Iri said. "That's the whole of it. That's what I'm *for*. The children in me aren't cargo, Emrys. They're people. Half-finished people, people who only got a few years each before the erasure took the rest — but people. And they're not meant to sit in me forever. They're meant to go home. Into the country, into the ones who waited, into whatever's left of Serevan behind the Last Door. I walk in, I open, I pour out, and then—" Iri shrugged, and tried to make the shrug light, and failed. "Then I'm empty. An emptied vessel. Whatever's left of me afterward, I don't know. Maybe nothing. Maybe a little. The archivists weren't specific. I don't think they could bear to be."

The fire burned. The bells rang. Somewhere far off across the ice, so faint it might have been wind, something howled — or sang — or both.

* * *

Emrys did not speak for a long time. Iri let them. This, too, was part of what they had learned about people: that the truth needs room, and that rushing someone through grief is a form of theft.

When Emrys finally spoke, their voice was rough. "Eight years ago. When you woke in the ditch. How did you survive?"

"A bell-keeper found me." Iri smiled despite everything. "Of course it was a bell-keeper. She'd come out from Vhalen to look at the stars, because she said the stars over Vhalen were the same stars and it helped. She heard me crying and she picked me up and I grabbed her wrist and said — in Serevani, in the old tongue, the tongue that shouldn't exist anymore — I said, *I have to get home.*" Iri laughed, wet and unsteady. "She didn't understand a word. She kept me anyway. Two years, until the guild's collectors found me. They tested my blood against the old ledgers — the guild keeps bloodlines, did you know that? They keep everything — and when they realized what I was, they didn't sell me to the King. They put me in a ledger. *Asset, unvalued, pending.* They didn't know what I was worth yet. So they waited. And I waited. And then the storm came, and I saw the guild doors open, and I saw you."

"Why me?" Emrys asked. "Why did you come to me? Out of everyone in that building."

And here was the last of it. The deepest shelf. Iri had been carrying this one since the first night, since the doorway and the snow and the word that had fallen out of their mouth like a coin from a torn pocket — *Sef* — and watched a stranger flinch as though struck.

"Because I know you," Iri said. "Not the way I know the bells. Not secondhand. You were born in Serevan, Emrys. Before the erasure. Your parents sold your first years to the guild to pay a debt — sold them *before* you could remember them, which is why you don't remember, which is why the sale worked so cleanly. But the archivists knew. The archivists kept lists of every child born inside the border in the last generation, because those children were the erasure's loose threads — people the world forgot, walking around in it, carrying nothing. And when they built me, they wrote you into me. Your name. Your face, from the birth-records. Your mother's name. Maren Oyelaran, the baker of the orchard country, who called her daughter home at dusk with a little handbell." Iri wiped their face with their sleeve. "You're in here, Emrys. You've been in here since before I had eyes. When I saw you in that doorway, I wasn't recognizing you. I was *delivering* you. That's what *Sef* is. It's your name. Your first one. The one that was sold along with everything else. Sef Oyelaran. Born under the orchard bells. And I have been carrying your name for thirty years, waiting to give it back."

Emrys stared into the fire. Their face did something complicated — grief and vertigo and a fierce, rising wonder all at once, weather systems colliding. Ten years of their life sat in vials in a guild vault. A name sat in a child's chest beside ten thousand stolen dawns. Nothing about their existence had ever belonged to them, and yet here, absurdly, impossibly, they had been *claimed* — by a country, by a mother, by a small made girl with bells in her bones.

"Then we're the same," Emrys said at last, and their voice broke on it, and they let it break. "We're both ledgers someone else wrote."

"No," Iri said, and reached across the fire, and took their hand. The vessel's hand was small and warm and it shook, and it did not let go. "That's the thing I finally understand, Emrys. Ledgers are just paper until someone chooses what goes in them. The guild chose to erase. The King chose to hoard. We get to choose to *return*. That's the only entry that matters. That's the one I want written down, somewhere, in ink that lasts: *they chose to give it all back.*"

Out on the lake, the bells changed.

Iri felt it before they heard it — a shiver through the ground, through the ice, through the thousand small lives packed inside them like seeds in a pod. The ringing shifted key. Slowed. Deepened, note by note, the way a voice deepens when a stranger enters the room.

Emrys felt it too. They turned toward the water, and the firelight caught their face, and Iri saw the color drain out of it.

"What is that?" Emrys whispered. "What's happening to the bells?"

Iri stood. The cold rolled off the lake in waves now, wrong cold, deliberate cold, cold with intent behind it, and far out across the silver expanse — at the exact center, above the place where the Last Door slept beneath the ice — the aurora that had hung green and quiet over the mountains all evening began to move. To gather. To bend downward like a head inclining toward them.

"He knows we're here," Iri said. Their voice came out steady, which surprised them; everything inside them was ringing. "He's felt me since the Nightmarket. Every mile closer, the bells ring louder — they ring for him, Emrys. They always rang for him. He was their king." Iri swallowed. "And he's done waiting."

Down beneath the ice, in the drowned dark where a country's heart still beat, the bells tolled once — enormous, mournful, final — like a door beginning to open.

Or beginning to break.
