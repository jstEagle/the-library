## 19. The Debt Comes Due

The ice sang before the enforcers came.

Emrys had heard it all their life without knowing what it was — that long, low moan the lake made on cold nights, the sound of water shifting beneath its lid of glass. But now, standing at the center of a frozen world with Iri's small hand in theirs, Emrys understood. The ice was not singing. It was *counting*. Every crack and groan a number entered into some vast ledger beneath them, every settling of frost another line item, and somewhere below — below the bells, below the Door, below everything — an account was being kept.

"Listen," Iri whispered.

"I hear it."

"No. Listen." The child's grip tightened. "It's changed. It's counting *faster*."

Emrys turned. Behind them, far across the white expanse, lanterns had appeared — six points of gold light strung out in a line, moving with the unhurried precision of men who had never once in their lives needed to hurry. The guild did not chase. The guild *arrived*.

"They found us," Iri said.

"The map led us here. They have maps too." Emrys crouched, pulling the child close, breath fogging between them. "Iri. Look at me. Whatever happens next, you keep walking toward the sound of the bells. You don't stop. You don't look back. Do you understand?"

"You said we'd stay together."

"And I meant it. But promises are like debts," Emrys said, and heard the bitterness in their own voice, the guild's arithmetic living in their mouth even now. "They get renegotiated."

The lanterns were closer. Emrys could make out figures now — six of them, dark against the snow, and above them something larger, a shape being carried on a litter or a sledge, draped in canvas that snapped in the wind. Not Corvane. Corvane never left his office; he sent his arithmetic instead. This would be the arithmetic itself. This would be the guild's hands.

* * *

They had perhaps two hundred paces of ice between themselves and the enforcers, and behind them only the frozen lake stretching to a shore of black pines, and beneath it all the faint, impossible ringing of Serevan's bells, coming up through the ice like voices through a floor.

Emrys had spent eleven years learning one lesson: that everything has a price, and that the price is always written down somewhere, and that whoever holds the ledger holds you. They had copied ten thousand entries in the guild's scriptorium — bottled laughter, keys to nowhere, jars of last words — and in all that time they had believed, with the quiet resignation of the truly indebted, that they themselves were simply another entry waiting to be collected.

But entries could be contested. Entries could be *renegotiated*. And Emrys Oyelaran had spent eleven years reading the guild's own contracts, and knew better than anyone alive how they were written — and therefore how they might be unwritten.

"Stay here," they told Iri, and walked out onto the ice to meet the lanterns.

* * *

The enforcers stopped thirty paces away. They wore the grey oilcloth of the Ledger Guild's collection division, hoods back, faces bare — the guild liked its collectors' faces visible, so debtors could see that they were only men. In the center, carried on a sledge by two of them, sat a brass instrument like a great astrolabe crossed with a loom, its needles already trembling, hungry, pointing toward Emrys like compass needles pointing north.

An extraction engine. They had brought it *onto the ice*.

The lead enforcer was a woman with a face like a closed ledger — flat, ruled, nothing wasted. Emrys knew her. Everyone in the guild knew her. Vessik. She had once extracted a man's memory of his mother's voice while he begged, politely, professionally, and afterward she had asked him if he required a receipt.

"Scribe Oyelaran." Her voice carried flat across the ice. "You are in breach of contract, theft of guild property, and flight from adjudicated debt. The sum outstanding is four thousand, two hundred marks, plus penalties accrued since your departure. The guild offers you one final settlement."

"I'm listening."

"You walk back to the city. The child is remanded to the extraction room. Your debt is converted to a service term of twenty years, forgiven upon completion." She paused, and added, with what might have been mercy in a kinder institution: "The alternative is that we take the memories now. All of them. The engine is rated for field work."

Behind Emrys, small boots scraped ice. Iri had not stayed put. Of course not.

"There's a third option," Emrys said.

Vessik waited. She was good at waiting. Waiting was how collections worked — you let the silence do the arithmetic for you.

"Collateral," Emrys said. "You want passage to the Door. That's why you're here, isn't it? Not for me. Corvane needs someone born inside Serevan to open it. I'm not cargo to you. I'm a *key*. And keys negotiate."

Something moved behind Vessik's flat eyes. Not surprise. Recalculation.

"Speak."

"My debt is voided. Fully. Written off, tonight, in front of witnesses. The child goes untouched — no extraction, no detention, no entry in any ledger, ever. In exchange, I pledge collateral against my future conduct." Emrys lifted their chin. "My memories. Ten years of them, held in escrow by your engine. If I open the Door and cooperate with the guild's harvest, I get them back. If I run—" they shrugged, "—you draw them out on the spot, and I spend the rest of my life not knowing what I lost."

"That is not standard practice."

"No," Emrys agreed. "It's worse than standard practice. Which is why you'll take it. Because Corvane didn't send you to arrest me. He sent you to *acquire* me, and acquisition means I have to sign. And I will only sign if the terms include the child."

Silence, except the wind and the counting of the ice and, far below, the bells.

Vessik studied Emrys for a long moment. Then she reached into her coat and produced, of all things, a portable writing desk — a slim lacquered case that unfolded into a writing surface, ink already warming against the cold. Even here, even now. The guild's answer to everything was paperwork.

"Name the decade," she said.

And there it was. The trap inside the bargain. Because Emrys would have to choose which ten years to surrender — and whatever they chose, they would choose blind, not knowing which years held what, not knowing what they were feeding into the needles of that brass machine.

Emrys looked past Vessik, past the enforcers, to Iri standing small and stubborn on the ice, watching with those old, old eyes. Ten years. Any decade. And whatever was in it would be gone — drawn out into vials, catalogued, sold, traded through the Nightmarket until some stranger owned the color of Emrys's mornings.

"The last ten years," Emrys said quietly. "Everything from my twenty-first year forward. Take it as escrow."

Vessik wrote. The pen scratched like a beetle in a wall.

"And the terms of return?"

"I open the Door. I cooperate with the harvest crews for a period not exceeding one season. Then full restoration, debt voided, the child released from all claims."

"Agreed." She signed, blotted, turned the case around. "Sign."

Emrys took the pen. Their hand did not shake, which surprised them. Eleven years of copying other people's losses had taught their fingers a terrible steadiness.

*Don't read the whole contract,* they told themselves. *You know what happens to people who read the whole contract. They find the clause that kills them.*

So they didn't read it. They signed — *Emrys Oyelaran*, in the careful guildhand they'd been trained in since childhood, each letter a small surrender — and watched Vessik countersign, and watched the two enforcers wheel the extraction engine forward across the ice.

"Sit," Vessik said, indicating a folding stool one of the men had set up, absurdly, in the middle of a frozen lake. "This will feel like cold water rising."

* * *

The machine hummed as the needles found their positions around Emrys's skull, and the first thing drawn out was the smell of the scriptorium — lamp-oil and dust and the particular sweetness of fresh ink — and Emrys felt it leave like a ribbon pulled gently from a spool.

Then the taste of the undercity bread. Then the sound of rain on the guild roof. Then a face — whose face? — laughing at something across a crowded refectory, gone before Emrys could hold onto the name.

They had expected pain. There was none. That was the horror of it. It felt like being *tidied*. Like a room being cleaned while you sat in it, objects lifted away one by one, each departure leaving a clean bright absence that did not ache because there was nothing left in it to ache *with*.

Iri was screaming. Emrys could hear the child distantly, held back by two enforcers who were gentle about it, professional about it, the way the guild was gentle about everything it destroyed.

"It's all right," Emrys said, and was astonished to find their voice still steady. "Iri. Look at me. It's all right."

"You're going gray," Iri sobbed. "Your eyes are going gray, like ash, like—"

"Like someone who just bought something very expensive." Emrys smiled. It cost more than the smile knew. "Hey. Little one. Come here."

They broke free — Vessik allowed it, Emrys noted dimly, *allowed* it — and Iri crashed into them, and Emrys held the child with arms that suddenly felt borrowed, and pressed their lips to the top of that wild head, and said the only true thing left in them:

"Whatever happens at that Door — remember for both of us. Remember *me*, even when I can't. Promise me that."

"I promise," Iri whispered into their coat. "I promise, I promise—"

The needles withdrew. Vessik folded her desk. Somewhere in the brass heart of the engine, ten years of Emrys Oyelaran sat in vials, labeled, receipted, filed.

"The Door," Vessik said, nodding toward the distant shore where the bells rang up through the ice. "Lead the way, scribe. Your cooperation begins now."

Emrys stood. The world felt strangely light. Strangely *vacant*. There were rooms in them, they realized, that they could no longer enter — whole corridors of self sealed off, doors shut on decades of living, and they could not even grieve properly because grief requires knowing what was lost.

They turned toward the bells. Iri took their hand without asking, and Emrys let them, though they no longer entirely knew why this child clung to them so fiercely, only that letting go felt like a word they'd forgotten how to pronounce.

Above them the sky was turning the color of old pewter, and the wind had begun to carry flakes — not snow, Emrys thought. Too slow. Too deliberate. Each flake falling straight down, unhurried, like pages.

And far off across the ice, where no lanterns burned, something vast was walking toward them through the storm, patient as winter, wearing a crown that no longer had a country underneath it.

The Hollow King had come to collect too.
