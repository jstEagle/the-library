## 20. Halfway to No One

The enforcers came at dusk, when the ice had gone the color of an old bruise and the bells beneath it had fallen quiet, as if even they were holding their breath.

There were six of them, and they did not walk so much as arrive, unfolding out of the white like figures from a bad dream Emrys had once copied into a ledger — *six men in grey, no footprints, carrying a table.* That was the detail that undid her. Not the crossbows slung across their backs, not the guild sigils stitched in silver thread over their hearts. The table. A folding clerk's table, walnut-veneer, with brass corners and a felt writing surface, exactly like the one that had sat in the assessment hall of the Ledger Guild for as long as anyone could remember. They planted it in the middle of the frozen lake as though they were setting up to take inventory of a shipment of wool.

One of them set a lantern on the felt. Another unfolded two chairs.

"Debtor Oyelaran," said the one in front. He was not large. He was not frightening, in any way Emrys could have named to a magistrate. He had the face of a man who approved mortgages, soft around the jaw, patient around the eyes, and his voice was the voice of every clerk who had ever explained to her why the numbers were simply the numbers. "You'll forgive the hour. Terms don't keep."

Behind her, Iri made a small sound. Not a word. Just a sound, the kind a kettle makes just before it sings, and Emrys reached back without looking and found the child's hand and held it, and thought: *I have done this before. I have stood between this child and something terrible before. I will do it again, however many times the ice asks.*

"The passage is agreed," she said. Her breath came out in a long grey ribbon. "You said you'd honor it."

"We honor everything." The clerk smiled. It was the worst thing she'd seen all winter, and she had seen Vesha taken, and the Silent Court's faces, and the Hollow King's crown of black glass. "That's rather the point of us. But agreements require consideration, Debtor Oyelaran. Passage across guild-held ice, escort through contested territory, non-interference from our colleagues—" he gestured vaguely southward, toward Vhalen, toward the towers where Corvane was even now reading maps that ate themselves, "—these are goods. Goods have prices. You have assets."

"I have nothing," Emrys said. "You took my ledger page. You've taken my wages for eleven years. You've taken—"

"You have yourself." He said it gently, almost kindly, the way a physician says *the arm must come off*. "The guild has long recognized the person as the ultimate collateral. Sit down, Debtor Oyelaran. Let's discuss terms."

* * *

They had brought a scale.

Of course they had. It was brass and glass, small enough to fit in a coat pocket, and it sat on the felt beside the lantern like a birdcage for something invisible. The clerk — his name was Halloway, he told her, twice, in the tone of a man who believed names were a courtesy debtors appreciated — explained the procedure while his colleagues staked the perimeter with little silver flags, each one humming faintly, marking out a circle of guild jurisdiction on the open ice.

"It doesn't hurt," Halloway said. "I want to be very clear about that, because there's a great deal of folklore on the subject and none of it is accurate. Extraction is painless. Extraction is *bureaucratic*. You'll sign a schedule of assets. We draw against the schedule. You retain full legal personhood, all future earnings, and your name." He paused, delicately. "Your current name."

"And what do you draw?" Emrys asked. Her voice sounded far away to her own ears, thin over the ice. Beneath her boots, so deep down that she felt it more than heard it, a bell tolled once, slow, like a heart deciding whether to bother.

Halloway opened a leather folio. Inside were pages of dense guild script, and Emrys's stomach dropped before her mind caught up, because she recognized the hand. She knew every quirk of it — the way the descenders hooked left, the way the ink pooled slightly at the start of each entry. It was her handwriting. It had been her handwriting since she was nine years old and first pressed a stylus to wax in the Guild's foundling school.

It was not a page she had ever written.

"Standard assessment," Halloway said, tapping the folio. "Conducted at intake, when you entered guild service. Every debtor receives one. Most never ask about it." He smoothed the page flat against the felt. "Yours is quite rich, if I may say. Unusual provenance. Serevani-born subjects carry at a premium — the elders call it *deep stock* — but we're not here to strip the whole account. We're reasonable people, Debtor Oyelaran. We want passage paid, not ruin."

"How much," Emrys said, "for how far?"

Halloway turned the folio toward her and slid a blank schedule across, along with a pen. The pen was goose-quill, silver-nibbed, warm to the touch in a way metal should not be. "Ten years," he said. "A decade of drawn memory, selected by the guild from the assessed account, surrendered irrevocably upon signature. In exchange: safe conduct to the Door, non-interference, and closure of all outstanding obligations. You would walk away free, Debtor Oyelaran. No debt. No file. No claim against you or yours." His eyes flicked, briefly, to Iri. "*Or yours.*"

*Free.* The word landed in Emrys like a coin dropped down a well. She listened for it to hit bottom and heard nothing.

She thought of the assessment hall. She thought of the vial she had watched them fill in the extraction room — no, she hadn't watched; Iri had watched, and told her after, in the dark of the catacombs, in a voice too old for its body: *he forgot his daughter mid-sentence. He was talking about her and then he was talking about a girl he'd never met.* She thought of the man's face afterward, polite and empty, thanking the clerks.

And she thought of the Door under the lake, and the bells, and the country that no longer existed except in fragments and in the small stubborn body of a child who called her *Sef*.

"Selected by the guild," Emrys repeated. "Meaning I don't choose which ten years."

"The schedule is randomized against bias," Halloway said. "It's fairer that way. Debtors always try to sell the years they value least, and the market disagrees. Grief holds its price better than joy, oddly enough. Regret is very stable currency." He smiled again. "You'd be surprised what people are worth."

Emrys looked at the pen. She looked at the scale, its little glass bell empty and waiting. She looked past the silver flags, north, to where the ice stretched white and endless toward a shore she could not see, and beneath that ice, somewhere, a Door.

Ten years. She was twenty-six years old. Ten years was most of her life that mattered. Somewhere in that decade was the first time she'd stolen a peach from the guild kitchens and eaten it on the roof, watching the snow come down over Vhalen, feeling for one whole hour like a person rather than a line item. Somewhere in it was the night she'd taught herself to read the old marginalia in the archive, the dead hand, the entries dated before her birth. Somewhere in it—

Somewhere in it was a snowstorm, and a knock at the guild door, and a child speaking a language no living tongue should know, saying a name Emrys had never used.

But she didn't know that was in there. That was the mercy of it, or the cruelty — she could not see the shape of what she carried. Nobody can. Memory isn't inventory. It has no labels, no weights, no neat columns. It is a country entire, and you live in it, and you cannot stand outside it to appraise it.

So when she signed, she signed blind.

* * *

They made her sit in one of the chairs. The other chair stayed empty, facing her, and Halloway explained that it was for the assessor, and the assessor was already present, and Emrys looked at the empty chair for a long moment before she understood that she was meant to watch herself being taken, that the guild considered this part of the service — *transparency*, Halloway called it. The debtor witnesses the drawing. It prevents disputes later.

The scale's glass bell began to hum.

It was not painful. Halloway had been right about that, and she hated him for being right, because pain would have been easier to hate than this. What it was, was *light*. A warmth behind the eyes, like standing in a doorway between a lit room and a dark one. And then things began to leave.

She felt them go the way you feel words go when you try to hold a sentence in your mind while someone talks over it. First the small change went — the taste of the guild's cheap tea, the exact color of the wall outside her cell, the name of the boy who'd shared her dormitory at nine. Then larger denominations. A summer. A winter. Her mother's hands — no, wait, those were supposed to be gone already, sold before her birth, weren't they? She reached for the memory of reaching and found the reach itself dissolving, a corridor closing behind her as she walked it.

She gripped the arms of the chair. The ice sang beneath her, one low note, rising.

"Please remain still," Halloway said pleasantly. "Movement can cause fragmentation, and fragmented stock draws at half rate, which means we'd need to take *more*—"

"Don't," Emrys said, and her voice cracked down the middle like the lake was starting to crack, "talk to me like I'm a shipment."

He had the grace to look mildly apologetic. The scale hummed on. The glass bell filled with something that was not light and not smoke and not water but moved like all three, turning slowly, opalescent, and Emrys understood with a lurch that she was looking at ten years of herself, suspended, appraised, *priced* — and that some junior clerk in Vhalen would pour it into vials and sell it in the Nightmarket, and someone would buy a winter of her life and wear it for an evening and forget it by morning.

The bells under the ice were ringing faster now. Or maybe that was her heart.

Then, near the end, something strange happened. The warmth behind her eyes gathered at one particular seam, and Emrys felt — distinctly, unmistakably — a year peeling loose. A recent year. A year with snow in it, and a door, and a small voice calling a name she couldn't quite hear anymore, the way you can't hear a word someone said just as you fall asleep. She felt it lift. She felt it weigh almost nothing at all, and cost almost everything.

"No," she said. She didn't know what she was refusing. She refused it anyway. "Not that one. Not— whatever that is. Take another. Take the roof, take the peach, take—"

"Assets are drawn per schedule," Halloway said, not unkindly. "The schedule is sealed. Even I don't know what's on it, Debtor Oyelaran. That's the fairness of it."

The bell filled. The humming stopped. The world went very quiet, and very white, and Emrys sat in the folding chair on the frozen lake and felt — fine. That was the horror of it. She felt *fine*. Lighter, even. Tidy. Like a room after the movers have been, when the walls show pale rectangles where pictures used to hang and you can't remember what hung there, only that the room is better this way, cleaner, emptier, *improved*.

"There," Halloway said, capping the glass bell with a stopper of black wax and wrapping it in grey cloth with the tenderness of a man handling an infant. "All settled. Your obligations are closed, Debtor Oyelaran. You are, as of this moment, a free woman of Vhalen." He tucked the bundle inside his coat, over his heart, which struck Emrys as obscene. "The escort will take you as far as the shore. After that, guild jurisdiction ends."

He extended his hand. She looked at it. She did not take it.

He withdrew it without offense, folded the table, and turned to go, and his five colleagues folded up with him like the dream ending, and then it was just Emrys and the ice and the child.

* * *

Iri was standing where she'd left her, at the edge of the silver flags, and the wind had pulled the girl's hood back and filled her hair with frost so that she seemed rimmed in light, and she was looking at Emrys the way people look at a house they grew up in that someone else lives in now.

"Sef," Iri said.

The name arrived and landed on nothing. It slid off her like rain off oiled cloth. Emrys frowned — a flicker of irritation, faint and unreasonable, the way one is irritated by a stranger using a familiar tone. *Why is this child calling me that? Why does she look at me like that?*

"My name is Emrys," she heard herself say. Her own voice, but flatter. Newer. "Emrys Oyelaran. Who are you? How do you know me?"

Something happened in the child's face then that Emrys had no shelf in her mind to put on. It was not surprise. Surprise requires expectation. It was the look of someone watching a second, smaller erasure happen in real time — a country going down inside a face — and knowing better than to cry out, because crying out wouldn't raise anything.

"I'm traveling with you," Iri said carefully. Each word placed like a foot on rotten ice. "To the Door. You promised."

"Did I." Emrys looked north. The ice ran white to the edge of seeing, and beneath it the bells had begun again, patient, enormous, ringing for a country that waited under everything. She felt no debt to this child. She felt no history. She felt only a strange, unaccountable pull in her chest, like a hook set deep in muscle — an instinct with no story attached, telling her: *this one. Whatever else is true, this one you keep.*

She didn't understand it. There was nothing there to understand *with*. The reasons had been poured into a glass bell and wrapped in grey cloth and were riding east over the ice in a clerk's coat, priced and sold and gone.

But the hook held.

"All right," Emrys said, because the hook said so, and because the ice was singing, and because somewhere ahead a Door was waiting that she had given ten years of her life to reach and could not remember why. "Stay close. It's a long way to the shore."

Iri took her hand. Emrys let her, though she could not have said why that too felt less like permission than like remembering.

They walked north together, a stranger and a child, halfway to no one, while under their feet the drowned bells rang on — ringing, though neither of them knew it yet, for everything Emrys had just sold, and everything she was about to find.
