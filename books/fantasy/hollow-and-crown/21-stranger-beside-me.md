## 21. Stranger Beside Me

Iri woke to the smell of woodsmoke and the sound of someone breathing wrong.

Not badly — not hurt — but carefully, the way a person breathes when they are trying to be quiet in a room where they do not know what else is sleeping. Iri knew that breathing. Had known it through three nights of flooded tunnels and one night under Vesha's crooked eaves and half a night on the ice before the ice stopped being safe. Emrys breathed like a person who had spent their whole life being watched, and even asleep could not quite believe they had escaped it.

Except.

Iri opened their eyes to a low ceiling of grey stone, a fire burned down to orange coals, and a stranger sitting against the far wall with a knife across their knees.

The stranger was Emrys. Of course it was. The same narrow shoulders hunched like a drawn bow, the same ink-stained fingers wrapped around the knife's grip, the same face Iri had crossed a country-that-was-not-a-country to find — the face from the orchard, from the bells, from the last bright afternoon before the dusk came down and never lifted. But the eyes were wrong. Not cold. Worse than cold: *unfurnished*. Eyes with nothing behind them but the room itself. When Emrys looked at Iri, there was no recognition in it at all. There was assessment. There was arithmetic. There was the flat, careful attention of a scribe taking inventory of an impossible thing.

Iri's chest did something terrible and small.

*They paid for you,* Iri thought. *They paid ten years of themselves so we could walk off this lake alive, and they don't know it. They don't know me. They don't know why my hand is shaking.*

"Stay where you are," the stranger said. Their voice was Emrys's voice, low and roughened by smoke, but it held none of the warmth Iri had been carrying like a coal cupped in both hands. It was a voice for reading out debts. "You were unconscious when I found you. I dragged you here because leaving a child on the ice felt—" A pause. The knife did not move. "It felt wrong. That's all I can tell you about it."

"You dragged me," Iri said. Their own voice came out younger than they wanted. It always did; that was part of what they were, part of the cargo, a hundred children's voices braided into one throat. "Off the lake."

"There was a hole in the world," the stranger said slowly, as if describing weather they didn't trust. "Or a door. Men in grey coats were shooting at us. Or at me. Or at the door." They frowned, and Iri watched them reach for the memory of it and find the shelf empty, watch the frown deepen as they reached again. "There was a bargain. I remember making a bargain. I don't remember what I paid." The knife-hand tightened. "That frightens me more than the men in grey coats did. And I would like very much to know who you are, and why every part of me that still has an opinion says I should put this knife down and get you somewhere warm."

The fire popped. Somewhere above them, through the stone, wind moved over the lake with a sound like a vast page turning.

"I'm Iri," Iri said.

"Iri." The stranger tried the name the way one tries a coin between the teeth. "And how do I know you? Because I am certain that I do. Certain the way you're certain of a word you've forgotten — it's on the tip of everything." They touched their own sternum, briefly, as if the certainty lived there and ached. "You called me something. In your sleep. You said *Sef*."

The name went through Iri like a bell struck underwater.

"Yes," Iri said. "That's you. That's your real name. Sef Oyelaran. Emrys is the name the guild gave you when they bought your life and sold your past, and Sef is the name you had before, in Serevan, in the house with the blue door on Tallowmaker's Row, and you used to sing while you copied things out because you couldn't help it, and your mother said you'd ruin a perfectly good hand with all that wiggling—"

"Stop." The word cracked. The stranger had gone white around the mouth. "Stop. How do you know that? I have never told anyone that. I don't *know* that. I've never heard of Tallowmaker's Row." But their hand was still pressed to their sternum, hard now, as if holding a door shut from the inside. "I don't know any of that."

"No," Iri said gently. "But your body does. Your body kept everything. They only took the ledger copy."

* * *

The stranger — Sef, Emrys, both, neither — did not put the knife down. But after a while they stopped pointing it anywhere in particular, and after a longer while they set it beside them on the stone, within reach, which Iri understood was the closest thing to trust a guild-raised scribe could perform. They fed the fire. They produced from their coat a heel of bread, two apples gone soft at one end, and a stoppered flask that smelled of plum brandy, and they divided the food without being asked, giving Iri the larger share and the unbruised apple, and seemed genuinely puzzled by their own hands while doing it.

"Habit," they said, catching Iri watching. "Apparently I feed children. Apparently I give them the good apple. I have no idea where I learned that." They looked at their own fingers as though they belonged to a borrowed glove. "Do you understand what that's like? To catch yourself being kind and have no idea why?"

"Yes," Iri said. "Actually. I think I might understand it better than anyone alive."

They ate. Outside, the wind worried at whatever passed for a roof — Iri thought they must be in some shepherd's bothy or ice-fisher's hut built into the lakeshore, one room, one window glazed with frost. Through the frost the light came down the colour of pewter. Dusk light. Serevan light. Iri's chest ached looking at it, and they saw the stranger keep glancing at the window too, frowning, drawn and repelled.

"What happened to me?" the stranger asked finally. It came out quiet, stripped of the scribe's careful diction. "On the lake. Tell me plainly. I've read enough ledgers to know when someone's editing."

So Iri told them. The enforcers coming out onto the ice in their grey coats, boots crunching, lanterns swinging. The Hollow King arriving behind them like winter given a crown, his Silent Court spilling across the frozen lake without disturbing the snow. The Door standing open in the middle of everything, humming, its frame carved with names. And Sef — Emrys — kneeling in the slush with a guild clerk's contract case open on their knee, trading away a decade of their own remembered life, clause by clause, to buy passage for a child the guild wanted opened like a strongbox.

"You bargained," Iri said. "You sat there on the ice with the King's people closing in and the guild's people closing in, and you bargained like it was just another day at the Ledger Guild. Ten years. Every memory from your twentieth year to your thirtieth. That's what bought us off the ice. The enforcers let us go because they got paid. The King let us go because—" Iri hesitated. "Because he wanted you to come to him whole, later, and he thought hunger would bring you. He offered to give it all back, you know. Everything. If you opened the Door for him alone."

"And I refused."

"You refused. You said—" Iri closed their eyes. The words were carved in them deeper than any of the catacomb names. "*You want a kingdom of grief because grief is the only thing left that obeys you. I won't build your tomb and call it Serevan.*"

The stranger sat very still. The firelight moved on their face and they let it, unblinking, and Iri watched them try to fit those words to themselves and fail, and then — this was the part Iri would carry for the rest of their long strange life — watched them decide to believe them anyway.

"I don't remember saying that," they said. "I don't remember refusing a king. I don't remember you." Their voice caught on the last word, snagged like cloth on a nail. "And yet when you said it just now, something in me stood up. Like hearing your own voice on a recording. *That's true. That's mine.* I have no evidence. I have no memory. I have a child quoting me back to myself on a mountainside, and every instinct I possess says: believe her."

"Her," Iri repeated, startled.

"You said 'her' in your sleep too. Several times. Also 'the bells,' also 'don't drop her,' also—" the stranger's ears went faintly pink, and they looked into the fire rather than at Iri, "—also 'I've got you.' Repeatedly. Whoever *her* is, you were holding on to her pretty tightly, and she was saying she'd got *you*." They rubbed their jaw. "Is she someone I should be jealous of? Because I'll be honest, I feel like I've lost a fight I don't remember having."

Iri laughed. It burst out of them sideways, wet and helpless, the way laughter comes after crying has already done most of the work. "She's me," Iri said. "I'm her. I'm the her. You carried me off the lake. You wouldn't put me down even after the bargain was sealed, even after you looked straight at me with those exact same unfurnished eyes and didn't know me. The clerk said the transaction was complete and you picked me up anyway. You said—" Iri had to stop, press the heel of their hand against their eyes. "You said, *I don't know who you are, but nobody leaves a child on a frozen lake.* And then you walked a mile across the ice with me on your back, and you kept talking to me the whole way, telling me what you were seeing, because you said I sounded scared and it seemed like the thing to do."

Silence. The fire settled. When Iri lowered their hand, the stranger was staring at them with an expression Iri recognized from the inside of a hundred borrowed memories — the look of a person standing in the doorway of a home they'd forgotten they owned.

"I don't remember any of it," the stranger said softly. "And I would do it again. Right now, tonight, with no reasons and no memories, if you stood up and walked back out onto that ice, I would go after you. Isn't that the strangest thing? They took the record and left the—" they searched, a scribe searching for the precise term, and found it, and their mouth twisted, "—left the marginalia. All the notes in the margins of me point at you."

"That's love," Iri said. Simply. Because it was simply true, and because a hundred children in Iri's keeping had taught a hundred ways of saying plain things bravely. "That's what it is when the evidence burns and the thing survives. You don't have to take my word for it. You're sitting in it."

* * *

They talked until the fire needed feeding twice more. The stranger asked questions the way scribes ask — precise, patient, in order — and Iri answered, and somewhere in the answering the room changed shape. It stopped being a stranger interrogating a mystery and became something older and more tired and more tender: two survivors dividing up what remained.

The stranger learned about Serevan, the orchards and the bells and the thirty-year dusk. Learned about the erasure, entry by entry, the guild buying a country out of existence. Learned about Iri — and here Iri braced, because this was the confession that had broken everyone who heard it, Vesha included — that Iri was not exactly a child at all, but a vessel, the compiled memories of Serevan's children given legs and a voice so that someone could carry them home. That the cargo was lighter now, spent waking Maren and the others, but not empty. That someday, when it was spent, Iri didn't know what would be left.

The stranger listened to all of it without flinching, which was its own kind of miracle, and at the end they were quiet for a long time, turning an apple core over in their ink-stained fingers.

"So the guild will come for you," they said at last. "For what's left in you. And the King will come for the Door. And I am apparently the only person alive who's been inside it and come back out." They looked up. The unfurnished eyes had furniture in them now — new furniture, hastily arranged, but real: purpose, fear, and underneath both, glowing like a banked coal, that stubborn thing that had carried a child a mile across the ice. "I don't remember choosing you, Iri. But I seem to have chosen you with the best part of myself, the part they couldn't price. So here's what happens next. We stay ahead of them. We get you somewhere the guild's ledgers can't follow. And I write everything down — everything you've told me tonight, everything you remember, every name — because I'm a scribe, that much survived intact, and if they erased a whole country once, then somebody ought to be writing it back."

From inside their coat the stranger drew a small notebook, cheap paper, guild-issue, and a stub of pencil, and opened it to the first clean page. And Iri, watching, felt something loosen in their chest that had been clenched since the lake.

Because the stranger wrote, at the top of the page, before anything else, before the plan or the names or the warnings:

*Sef Oyelaran. That is my name. Someone who loved me paid a great deal to tell me so.*

Then, beneath it, smaller:

*Iri. Friend. Protect.*

"I'm putting it in the front," the stranger said, a little defensively, catching Iri's stare. "In case it happens again. In case they take more. Whoever finds me next should know the important entries first." They glanced up, and there it was — the ghost of the old dry humor, surfacing through the loss like a bubble through dark water. "Consider it a ledger with a conscience."

"It's the most romantic thing I've ever seen," Iri said, "and I contain the memories of four hundred courtships."

"*Four hundred*—" The stranger laughed, short and startled, and then pressed their lips together as if laughing were evidence of a person they weren't sure they still were. But their eyes stayed warm. "Get some sleep. I'll take first watch. And Iri—" They hesitated, pencil hovering, and asked the question quietly, the way one asks about a wound. "The years they took. The decade. Is it gone forever? Is there any way back?"

Iri thought of the Nightmarket. Of Vesha's locked case. Of fragments floating loose in the world like ash after a fire, some of them surely bearing a scribe's careful handwriting, a woman's laugh, a blue door on Tallowmaker's Row.

"Nothing's ever entirely gone," Iri said. "Not in this world. Not anymore. Some of it's scattered. Some of it's *behind the Door.*" They met the stranger's eyes. "We could go back in, Sef. The Door doesn't only lead out."

The pencil stopped moving.

Outside, far across the lake, so faint it might have been wind — but wasn't, and both of them knew it wasn't — a bell began to ring beneath the ice.
