## 25. Through the Last Door

The Door did not open so much as admit them, the way a throat admits a swallowed thing.

Emrys had imagined — in the frantic hours on the ice, in the space between the enforcers' lanterns and the Hollow King's silent court spreading across the frozen lake like spilled ink — that crossing would feel like something. A tearing. A falling. The cold snap of a page turning.

Instead there was a moment of perfect stillness, as if the whole world had paused to read them, and then the ice was gone from beneath their boots and they were falling through air that smelled of woodsmoke and ripening apples and rain that had not yet fallen.

They landed in grass.

Not snow. Grass — deep, soft, silvered with dew, bending beneath Emrys's palms when they caught themselves, wet and real and impossibly green for a world that should have been winter all the way down. Iri landed beside them with a small sound, half gasp, half laugh, and Emrys's hand found the child's shoulder before their mind caught up to the act, pulling Iri close, checking, counting — one head, two arms, breathing, breathing.

Behind them, the Door hung in the air like a wound in a blue sky.

That was the first wrongness Emrys noticed, once the grass had stopped spinning beneath them: the sky was *blue*. Not the bruised grey of Vhalen's undercity, not the white blindness of the lake, but a deep evening blue shot through with gold at the horizon, the exact color of the hour when the lamplighters made their rounds and the bakeries breathed out their last warmth of the day. Dusk. It was dusk here, and it had the quality of a held breath, of a note sustained past all reason.

And it did not move.

Emrys watched the sun — low, swollen, gilded at its rim — and waited for it to sink. It did not sink. They counted to a hundred in the old ledger-fashion, the way they'd learned to time ink drying, and the light lay across the land exactly as it had before, unchanging, patient, like a painting of an hour rather than the hour itself.

"Iri," they said. Their voice came out strange, flattened, as if the air here were thicker, or as if sound itself were unsure it was welcome. "Iri, look at me."

The child turned. In the golden light, Iri's face had changed — or perhaps Emrys was only finally seeing it clearly. There was something older beneath the young skin, something layered, the way a bell holds every note it has ever rung. Iri's eyes were wide and wet and full of a recognition so total it frightened Emrys more than the enforcers had, more than the King's cold heralds striding across the ice.

"We're home," Iri whispered. "Sef. We're home."

*Don't call me that,* Emrys almost said, out of habit — but the name sat differently here, in this place where no guild ledgers could reach, and what came out instead was: "Where is everyone?"

Iri didn't answer. The child was looking past Emrys, up the gentle slope of the hill they'd landed on, toward a road — a real road, cobbled, worn smooth by generations of cart wheels — that ran along a stone wall toward a village below. And along that road, mid-stride, stood a woman with a basket on her hip.

She was not moving.

* * *

Emrys walked down to her because someone had to, because the alternative was standing on the hill forever waiting for the sun to finish setting, and because some stubborn clerk's instinct in their chest insisted that all mysteries were, at bottom, entries awaiting explanation.

Up close, the woman was utterly ordinary and utterly unbearable. She wore a shawl the color of dried lavender, frayed at one edge. Her basket held pears — five of them, one with a bruise going soft near the stem. A strand of hair had escaped her braid and curled against her cheek. She was perhaps forty, with laugh lines and a chipped front tooth visible between parted lips, and she had been walking, clearly, briskly, purposefully, one foot forward, basket swinging slightly with the momentum of a step she had begun

and never finished.

"Hello?" Emrys said. Their voice sounded obscene here, too loud, too alive. "Madam? Can you hear me?"

Nothing. Not the nothing of sleep — sleep breathes, sleep shifts, sleep has a texture of continued life. This was the nothing of a page. Of ink dried into its final shape. Emrys waved a hand before the woman's face and the hand passed through air that offered no resistance and no response, and the woman went on not-falling-forward with such perfect poise that Emrys's stomach turned over.

A fly — there were flies here, that was somehow worse — a fly crossed the woman's cheek and settled on her nose and stayed there, and she did not blink.

"Sef." Iri had come down the hill after them, small feet quiet in the grass. The child did not look surprised. That was the worst part, Emrys realized. Iri looked like someone returning to a house they'd left that morning. "Her name is Talya. She sells pears on market days. On the day it happened she was late because her son had hidden her good shawl, and she was hurrying, and—" The child's voice caught. "And she never got to be angry at him. She never got to find the shawl."

"How do you know that?" Emrys asked, though they already knew, though the knowledge was rising in them like floodwater through floorboards. "Iri. How do you know her name?"

"Because I'm made of the ones who remember her." Iri touched the woman's frozen sleeve, gently, the way one touches a sleeping person one does not wish to wake. "Because she's one of mine."

The dusk light lay gold and motionless across the road. Somewhere beyond the village, very faintly, Emrys thought they heard a bell — one clear stroke, unfinished, hanging in the air like the last syllable of a word the speaker forgot.

* * *

They walked into the village because there was nowhere else to go, and because Emrys had learned long ago that horror avoided is horror owed, with interest.

It was called Serevan, the village — or part of something called Serevan; Iri said the country spread further than any of the children's memories could hold, orchards and cities and a coast none of them had seen. The sign at the crossroads said *VELLIMORE*, in carved letters softened by moss, and beneath it, smaller: *MARKET THURSDAYS.*

The market square was full.

That was how it struck Emrys first — not the stillness but the fullness, the abundance of people, dozens of them, hundreds, frozen in the middle of a Thursday market as if a god had pressed his thumb down on the hour and kept it. A man haggled over fish, mouth open mid-objection, coin held aloft between finger and thumb. Two boys chased each other around a well, one laughing, one reaching, neither ever catching nor being caught. An old woman argued with a fruit-seller over the price of plums with the particular ferocity of someone who has done it every week for twenty years and will do it every week forever, except that she wouldn't, because forever had come for her in the shape of a single Thursday afternoon, and it had come without her noticing, and it had kept her exactly as she was.

Emrys walked through them and could not stop shaking.

It wasn't fear, precisely. It was the vertigo of the archive — Emrys knew that feeling intimately, the sensation of standing among records that vastly outnumber you, of understanding that every shelf is a life compressed, that the dead are filed alphabetically. But this was worse than the archive, because here the lives were not compressed. Here they were *preserved*, whole, at full size, wearing their own clothes and their own expressions, and they were not dead, they could not be dead, they were simply — suspended, like flies in amber, like words in a ledger entry that no one would ever come back to amend.

A girl of maybe six stood at the fountain with her cup halfway to her lips. Water arced from the spout and hung there, a glass ribbon, catching the unmoving sunlight.

"They can't see us," Iri said softly, walking beside Emrys, threading between the frozen bodies with the ease of long familiarity. "Or — they're not not-seeing us. There's just no *them* to do the seeing. It's like..." The child frowned, searching. "Like the words are still on the page but nobody's reading."

"And you? Can you wake them? You said — on the ice you said you were carrying them home—"

"I don't know." For the first time since the door, Iri sounded small. Genuinely small, a child's smallness, and Emrys hated how much relief and grief arrived together in the same breath. "I know I'm supposed to. I can feel them inside me, all of them, pressing, wanting — but I don't know the way in. I thought... I thought if we got home, home would know what to do."

The bell rang again — that same single unfinished stroke — and this time Emrys turned toward it, and saw the church at the end of the square, and beside the church, on a post, a notice board with papers pinned to it, and one paper flapping.

Flapping.

Emrys stared. Everything else in Vellimore was still as painted glass — the water, the smoke above the chimneys (frozen, gray ribbons), the leaves on the plane trees (suspended mid-tremble) — but this one corner of paper lifted and fell, lifted and fell, in a wind Emrys could not feel.

They crossed the square. The notice was written in a fine, careful hand, in Serevani — and Emrys read it, understood it, *read* it, which should have been impossible, which sent a cold thread down their spine even as comprehension bloomed, effortless, native, as if the language had been waiting behind their eyes all along for a door of its own.

*By order of the Crown Archive,* it began, *all citizens are reminded that in the event of national emergency, the Protocol of Continuance shall be observed. Do not be afraid. Your memories are your country, and your country keeps what is hers. Report to your district registrar for sealing.*

Below it, in fresher ink, a second notice:

*THE DOOR WILL BE OPENED FROM THE OUTSIDE. IF ANYONE IS LEFT TO READ THIS: WE ARE SORRY. WE BOUGHT YOU TIME. WE DID NOT BUY ENOUGH.*

And beneath that, signed in a hurried scrawl that Emrys recognized — recognized with a lurch that dropped through them like a stone through thin ice — with their own handwriting:

*Archivist Second Class S. Oyelaran.*

* * *

They sat for a while afterward on the church steps, because Emrys's legs declined to do anything else.

The dusk held. It would always hold, Emrys understood now, or believed they understood — this country existed in the last hour of its last day, replayed eternally, a nation preserved at the moment of its erasure like a pear in a jar of last words. Somewhere in the guild's vaults in Vhalen, Emrys had catalogued fragments of this: bottled laughter sourced from an unknown festival, a key stamped with a crest no heraldry book contained, a jar labeled *last words, mixed, 312 units.* All of it inventory. All of it *this* — chopped from a living country and sold by the vial, while the country itself stood here in its golden afternoon, holding its breath, waiting for someone to come back and finish the sentence.

"I was born here," Emrys said. It was not a question, and Iri did not treat it as one. "Before. Before the erasure. And then someone sold me out of it. Sold my — my first years, my name, my—" They stopped. Their hands were trembling again. "There's a version of me that grew up here. That chased those boys around that well. That drank from that fountain. And I've spent my whole life in a guild copying inventories of everything they took from this place, and I never once wondered why the work felt like mourning."

Iri leaned against Emrys's side, warm and solid and heavy with other people's childhoods. "You wrote the notices," the child said. "The Archivist Second Class. That's you. That's who you were before they took you."

"Then why can't I remember writing them?"

"Because remembering costs," Iri said simply. "Everything here runs on the same rule. The guild taught you that better than anyone."

Above them, the bell tolled its single unfinished note again, and again, patient as tide. Emrys looked out over the frozen market — at Talya and her pears somewhere up the road, at the girl with her cup, at the old woman arguing eternally over plums — and felt something crystallize in their chest, hard and bright and furious. Not the numb resignation of the ledger hall. Something with edges.

Thirty years. Thirty years these people had stood in their golden hour while merchants in Vhalen decanted their laughter and their griefs into glass and priced them by the dram. And the guild had done it with entries and signatures and the clean arithmetic of debt, and somewhere in that arithmetic was a line item with Emrys's own name in it, sold before they could walk.

"We have to wake them," Emrys said.

"I know."

"Not someday. Not when it's safe. Corvane will follow us through — he'll strip this place to the shelves. Every hour we sit here is an hour he's loading carts." Emrys stood, and their legs held, and the fury carried them upright the way despair never could. "You said the children's memories are inside you. Then we start there. We start with whatever you can give back, however small. One person. One pear-seller. One boy at the well. We wake this country one soul at a time if we have to, and then—"

Then the bell changed.

It did not toll its single note. It rang — fully, wildly, all its bronze voice at once, the way bells ring for fire, for invasion, for the death of kings — and the sound rolled out over Vellimore and broke against the frozen houses and came back changed, and in the market square below, the water ribbon at the fountain *trembled*.

Just once. Just a shimmer, like a reflection disturbed.

But Emrys saw it. And Iri saw it. And far away, at the edge of the dusk, where the road left the village and climbed toward hills that had no right to exist under a sun that never set, the light bent — folded — and a figure stepped through, tall and robed in colors that hurt to look at, followed by another, and another, lanterns kindling in their hands though no wind stirred and no dark had fallen.

The harvest crews had found the Door.

And behind them, at the crown of the hill, vast and patient as winter itself, something wearing the shape of a king stood watching the village, watching the church steps, watching Emrys — and raised one hand, slowly, in greeting, or in claim.
