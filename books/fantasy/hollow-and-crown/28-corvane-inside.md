## 28. Corvane Inside

The Door did not open for Corvane. It *admitted* him, which was different, and he noted the difference the way he noted all discrepancies: in a small hand, in a margin, to be dealt with later.

He had expected resistance. He had prepared for it — three notarized writs of salvage, two instruments of debt-assignment sealed with the Guild's double ring, and a letter of intent written in iron-gall ink so old its recipe had died with its maker. What he had not prepared for was dusk. The light on the other side of the Last Door was the color of a candle guttering in a room no one had left for thirty years, and it fell on everything equally, without shadow, as though the country itself were too tired to distinguish between what mattered and what did not.

"Master," said Brannoc at his shoulder. The enforcer's voice came out flattened, reverent, wrong. "Is this — are we—"

"We are inside an asset," Corvane said.

He stepped through, and his boots found cobbles. Real ones, worn into shallow bowls by generations of feet that had walked them believing they would go on walking them forever. Somewhere ahead, water ran in a fountain — he could hear it, a thin silver thread of sound — but when they rounded the corner of a plastered house with blue shutters, the fountain stood still. Not frozen. Still. A sheet of water hung in the basin like a pane of glass, and above it, mid-arc, hung the splash itself: a crown of droplets suspended in the amber air, each one holding a small inverted image of the sky.

Brannoc reached out to touch one. Corvane caught his wrist.

"You don't know what your hand does here," he said quietly. "Nothing moves until we understand the terms."

*Terms.* That was the word that had brought him across, more than ambition, more even than the arithmetic that had ruled his life since he was a boy counting another man's grain. The Guild had spent sixty years buying pieces of Serevan — laughter in bottles, keys to nowhere, jars of last words, the grief of a king decanted drop by drop — and every purchase had been made against a collateral no one could produce. You cannot repossess a country. But you can enter one. And entry, Corvane had argued before the Council of Ledgers until his voice went hoarse and his argument went down in the minutes like a knife going into soft bread, entry is a form of collection.

He had won the vote by four hands. He thought of those four hands now, standing in the held breath of a dead nation, and permitted himself something he almost never permitted: satisfaction, unaccompanied by a figure.

The street climbed gently toward the center of the city. Houses leaned together the way old friends lean at funerals. Laundry hung on lines, dry and stiff and thirty years past needing rain. In a doorway, a woman stood with a broom half-raised, her face turned toward something down the lane — a dog, perhaps, or a child late for supper — her mouth open around a word she had been saying since before Emrys Oyelaran was born. Dust had settled on her shoulders like a second skin. On her broom. On the step beneath her feet, where a cat lay curled, its fur furred over with the fine grey velvet of decades.

"She's alive," Brannoc whispered. "Look — the chest. It rises."

"It doesn't rise. Your eye wants it to." But Corvane looked, and saw the same thing Brannoc saw: an almost-motion, a tremor at the edge of perception, the way a star trembles when you look directly at it and goes still when you look away. He took out his ledger — the personal one, vellum, clasp of Vhaleni brass — and made his first entry on Serevani soil.

*Population, initial survey, one street: eleven persons, animate-paused. Condition: preserved. Cause: pending.*

*Pending.* He underlined it twice. Everything here was pending. That was the horror of the place, if horror it was, and also — he pressed his thumb flat against the page to blot the ink — its value beyond any calculation yet attempted. The Guild had spent sixty years trading fragments of these people the way slavers once traded fingers cut from statues: proof of beauty, stripped of the thing itself. But here was the statue entire. Here was every finger, every face, every unfinished sentence, waiting in the amber of a bought-and-paid-for dusk for someone with the right instruments to come and *harvest*.

"Set the crews in order," he said. "Survey teams first. Nothing is touched until it is entered. Nothing is entered until it is appraised. Nothing is appraised—" he looked up the street, at the bell tower rising against the colorless sky, its bells hanging silent in their arches like fruit no one had picked, "—until I have seen it myself."

"And the locals?" Brannoc asked. His eyes kept going back to the woman with the broom. There was a look on his face Corvane had seen before, on other men, in other rooms where the work got done — the look of a man discovering that his stomach has opinions his salary cannot overrule.

"The locals," Corvane said, "are inventory."

* * *

They made camp that first night in a caravanserai off the market square, because it had walls and a well, and because Corvane did not yet trust the houses. A house, he reasoned, is a record of its people, and he was not ready to read so much at once. The caravanserai was neutral ground, a place built for strangers, and its strangeness was already spent.

The crews lit lamps, and the light behaved strangely — it pooled instead of spreading, sat on the flagstones like spilled honey, refused to reach the corners. Corvane sat apart with his ledgers and worked by his own candle, entering the day. Around him the harvest crews ate and spoke in the low voices of men in a cathedral, and every few minutes one of them would stop speaking altogether and stare at nothing, at the doorway, at his own hands, and Corvane marked each occurrence in a separate column. By midnight the column held forty-one marks. He titled it, after some consideration, *Awe, unbudgeted.*

It was not awe he felt. He had spent his whole life training awe out of himself the way a farrier trains a horse out of shying, and he was proud of the result. What he felt was closer to recognition — the cold, clean click of a lock accepting the key cut for it. All his life the Guild had taught him that everything could be entered: every debt, every asset, every grief, every name. Men had laughed at the older clerks who claimed some things resisted the ledger. Corvane had never laughed. He had simply waited, patient as interest, for the world to prove him right.

And here it was. A whole country, proven.

Near dawn — if dawn it was; the sky held its amber like a breath held too long — he walked the market square alone. Stalls stood shuttered or open, goods laid out under cloths gone grey with dust: bread gone to stone, fish gone to leather, bolts of cloth whose colors had not faded because color, apparently, required time, and time here had been paid up in advance and then cancelled. At one stall a merchant sat behind his scales, both pans empty, his hand resting on the counter as though the next customer were always about to arrive. Corvane stopped before him. Up close he could see the man's eyes were open, fixed on a point slightly to the left of forever, and that his lips were parted around the beginning of a greeting.

Corvane found himself, unaccountably, returning it. "Good morning."

The silence answered. It was the fullest silence he had ever heard — not absence of sound but presence of all the sounds ever suspended here, packed into the amber air like seeds in a seedcake: a thousand greetings, a thousand bargains, the haggling of thirty years of mornings, none of them finished, all of them owed.

He stood there a long while. Then he opened his ledger and wrote, in the small hand that had unsettled four guildmasters before him:

*Note: the country is not empty. The country is owed. Every person here is owed a morning they did not get. This must be entered as liability, not asset.*

He looked at what he had written. It was true, and truth in a ledger is only a line item; but his hand had shaken on the last word, *asset*, and he blotted it carefully and told himself the shake was the cold.

There was no cold here. That was the other thing. The air was warm as a sickroom, warm as the inside of a closed fist, and it smelled of dust and ripeness and, underneath, faintly, unbearably, of orchards.

* * *

By the third day the harvest crews had learned the shape of the work, and the work had begun to learn them back.

The procedure was Corvane's own design, refined from the extraction rooms of Vhalen: a memory-vial of black glass, a focusing lens of ground Serevani crystal — the Guild had bought enough of it over the years, fragment by fragment, to equip a dozen teams — and a clerk with steady hands and a steady heart, though the second quality proved harder to staff than the first. The clerk touched lens to the paused air above a sleeper's brow — for that was how it worked best, on the sleepers, the ones caught in beds and doorways and fields, the ones whose last act had been rest — and turned it as one turns a key, and the memory rose out of the stillness like cream rising, a slow bright thread, and coiled into the vial, and the vial filled, and was corked, and was labeled in the clerk's best hand.

*Orchard, autumn, unnamed woman, age indeterminate. Contents: picking apples; a song, half-remembered; the smell of rain coming. Appraised at 40 crowns. Reserve: higher.*

That was Tessaly's entry, from the first full day. She was the best of the junior clerks, quick and unsentimental, and by the end of the second week she had stopped singing to herself while she worked, which Corvane noted in his private ledger without comment.

The trouble — and there was trouble, there is always trouble, trouble is only profit wearing its work clothes — began with the crews themselves. Brannoc's team pulled memories from a row of sleeping soldiers in a barracks near the east gate, and that night three of the six men assigned to it did not eat. The following day a crew working the granary district came back short one man, who was found at dusk sitting on a doorstep beside a paused woman, holding her hand, having pried it loose from whatever it had been doing, his face wet, saying over and over a name that appeared in none of the Guild's records.

Corvane handled it as he handled everything: procedurally. Rotations were shortened. Pay was doubled. A rule was posted — *no crewman shall address the inventory* — and when a young harvester named Pell broke it, sitting an entire shift beside a paused boy of perhaps ten, whispering to him, Corvane did not have him flogged. He had him transferred to the appraisal tables, where the work was numbers and the numbers did not look back at you, and he entered the transfer in the ledger under *personnel, compassionate*, and slept that night better than he had any right to expect.

But it was on the ninth day, in the archive district, that the work showed him its true face.

The central archive of Serevan rose above the city like a held note — a long building of pale stone, its windows dark, its doors open. Open. Of everything in the country, only the doors of the archive stood open, as though the archivists had been the last to understand what was happening and had flung wide the doors either in welcome or in accusation, and Corvane, standing before them, could not decide which, and the not-deciding sat in his chest like a swallowed stone.

Inside, the shelves ran away into the dusk further than lamplight cared to follow. And along the shelves, at the desks, in the aisles — archivists. Hundreds of them. Standing. Sitting. One man halfway up a rolling ladder with a book in his hands, frozen in the act of reshelving, his finger keeping his place on the shelf-edge. A woman at a reading desk with her pen lifted, a drop of ink hanging from the nib like a black pearl, thirty years from falling.

"They knew," said Tessaly, at his elbow. Her voice had gone thin. "Look — the doors open, the books out, the pens inked. They were working *up to the end*. Or—" she stopped.

"Or they meant to be found," Corvane said. "Read. Remembered. An archive exists to be opened by someone who comes later. They arranged themselves like a letter."

"To whom?"

Corvane did not answer. He walked the aisles slowly, reading spines, and the titles went through him like cold water through cloth: *Births and Names, District Nine. Orchard Leases in Perpetuity. Correspondence of the Bellfounders' Guild. A Concordance of Lullabies.* Whole lives, filed. A whole country, catalogued by its own people with a love so thorough it had become indistinguishable from prayer.

At the end of the third aisle he stopped. On a lectern, open, lay a ledger — a true ledger, vellum, brass-clasped, in the Serevani style — and its final entry was dated the day of the erasure, and it read, in a firm and beautiful hand:

*Entry concluded. Balance outstanding: everything. To whoever keeps accounts after us: we were here. We were here. We were here.*

Three times. As if once were not sufficient witness. As if the writer had known that repetition is the poor man's seal.

Corvane stood before the open book for a long time. Behind him he heard the crews moving, the soft glass music of vials being uncorked, Tessaly's voice giving the day's orders in a tone grown flat and careful, and somewhere deeper in the archive a harvester laughed at something, and the laugh sounded obscene in that place, the way a laugh sounds in a sickroom, and was strangled off at once by the silence, which accepted it and kept it.

He took out his own ledger. He wrote: *Archive survey commenced. Holdings: incalculable.* Then he stopped, and looked at the words *incalculable*, and understood that he had finally met the thing his whole life had been aimed at: a sum that would not resolve. The Guild had bought this country piece by piece for sixty years and had never once asked what remained when the pieces were taken — and the answer stood all around him, patient as the woman with the broom, patient as the ink-drop that would not fall: what remained was everything, and everything could be carried out vial by vial, shelf by shelf, and it would still be everything, and the carrying would take forever, and the forever was the point. Plunder was not greed. He saw that now, standing in the pale stone silence with the archivists' open eyes upon him. Plunder was piety. It was the only worship the Ledger Guild had ever taught him: that the world is real insofar as it can be entered, owned, carried away, and that anything which cannot be carried away —

He closed that thought the way he closed a ledger at day's end, firmly, with both hands.

"Begin the removals," he said. "Start with the lullabies."

Tessaly looked at him. "Sir?"

"The concordance. *A Concordance of Lullabies.* If we are to sell a country, we begin with what mothers sang. That is what the buyers in Vhalen will pay for. Not armies. Not treaties." He heard his own voice go quiet, and did not fight it. "They will pay for the sound of being loved. It is the rarest commodity in the world. We hold the entire supply."

* * *

On the eleventh day, the bells rang.

Not the tower bells — those still hung silent, fruit unpicked. These came from everywhere and nowhere, from under the cobbles, from inside the amber air, a single deep stroke that rolled through the city like a wave through a field of wheat, and every crew dropped what it held, and every vial in every crate rang softly in answer, hundreds of small voices, and Corvane stood in the market square with his ledger open and his pen dry and felt, for the first time in forty years, that he was standing inside someone else's account.

The stroke faded. The dusk settled. And then, far off across the rooftops, at the edge of hearing, came a second sound: footsteps. Many. Unhurried. Coming toward the city through the orchards, with the patience of a debt that has waited thirty years and knows the debtor cannot leave.

Brannoc appeared at his side, sword drawn, which was useless, and they both knew it was useless, and neither said so.

"Master," Brannoc said. "Someone's coming."

"I know," said Corvane, and closed his ledger, and for the first time in his life did not write down what he was feeling, because he suspected it would not fit in the column provided.
