## 29. Waking the Orchard

The first thing I learned about resurrection was that it smelled like apples.

Not fresh ones. Not the bright green bite of fruit pulled warm off a branch. These were old apples — cider gone soft in a cellar, sweetness turning at its edges — and they rose up out of the orchard rows of paused Serevan like breath from sleeping mouths. The trees stood heavy with fruit no one had picked for thirty years, and the fruit had not rotted, because rot was a kind of time and time had never been allowed to finish here. It just hung there, golden and patient, filling the dusk with a smell like someone's grandmother's kitchen, like a festival I had never attended but remembered anyway, because I was made of other people's remembering.

I walked between the rows with my hands open at my sides, palms up, the way Vesha had once shown me beggars held them in the Nightmarket. But I wasn't asking. I was giving.

"Here," I said softly, to a woman frozen mid-stride on the lane between the trees, her basket tipping, an apple rolling through air that hadn't moved since before I was anything at all. "I have something of yours."

She could not hear me yet. Her eyes were open and fixed on nothing, or on everything — on the last moment of a country, held like a fly in amber. Dust motes hung around her shoulders in a constellation that would fall when she moved. I reached out and touched her wrist, and I opened the little door inside myself, the one the archivists had built into me when they folded three hundred children's memories into a body small enough to carry, and I let one memory go home.

It left me the way a bird leaves a hand: a push, a flutter, a sudden lightness where weight used to be.

And the woman breathed.

* * *

Her name was Osla Venn, though I didn't know it then. She came back into herself the way you come up from deep water — badly, gasping, hands clawing at the present tense. The apple fell from her basket and struck the ground with a sound so ordinary it made me cry. Thirty years of stillness ended with an apple hitting dirt.

"What—" she said. "What year. What *year* is it."

"I don't know how to tell you," I said. "I'm sorry."

She looked at me then, really looked, and I watched her do the arithmetic every woken person did: my face too young, my eyes too old, my clothes cut in no style any living tailor knew. She saw the memory settling into her — I could always tell, the way a person's gaze went distant and inward, receiving — and whatever she received made her knees buckle. I caught her. We sat together in the orchard dust, an ancient child holding a middle-aged woman while she wept for a country that had been stolen out from under her mid-step, mid-breath, mid-life.

"The bells," she whispered. "I remember the bells stopping."

"They're going to start again," I told her. "That's why I'm here."

* * *

There were nine thousand people in the city of Serevan proper, according to the census carved into the archive wall. There were more in the villages beyond, in the hill towns, along the coast road — but the Door had opened onto the city, and the city was what I could reach, and so the city was where I began. Nine thousand sleepers. Three hundred memories in my keeping, each one belonging to a specific person, keyed to a specific face, because the archivists had been careful even in their desperation. A memory given to the wrong sleeper would not take root. It would slide off them like rain off glass.

So I worked by lamplight — well, by dusklight, which was all this country had — going house to house, street to street, orchard row to orchard row, matching faces to fragments.

It was slow. Gods, it was slow.

Emrys would have hated it, I thought, and then loved it. Emrys, who copied inventories line by line for years, who understood better than anyone that enormous things are done in small strokes. I tried to work the way Emrys worked: patiently, carefully, checking each name twice against the ledger I kept in my head, because I *was* a ledger now, in a way, and I intended to be a good one.

The second sleeper was a man on a balcony, frozen pouring wine. His fragment was a girl's memory of him teaching her to whistle through cupped hands. When he woke he finished the pour out of pure momentum, wine spilling over the glass rim and down the balustrade, and he stared at his own wet hand as if it belonged to a stranger, and then he said, in pre-kingdom Serevani, "*Liri?*" — calling for a daughter — and I had to stand there and be the vessel that carried his daughter's voice and tell him: she isn't here yet. She might be somewhere in the city. She might be one of the sleepers. Help me wake them and you'll find her.

He came with me. They mostly came with me, after the first grief broke. What else does a resurrected person do? Sit back down in the amber?

* * *

By the fourth day I had a procession.

It grew the way snowballs grow, the way debts grow, the way everything in both worlds seemed to grow except time itself. Osla Venn walked at my front carrying her basket again, gathering apples as we passed the trees — "someone will want to eat when they wake," she said, practical as a hammer — and behind her came the whistling man whose name was Davor, and behind him a cobbler named Pell who had woken screaming and now wouldn't speak at all but worked with his hands, mending the shoes of everyone we woke, because grief needs a task or it turns inward and eats. Behind Pell, a dozen more. Behind them, dozens more. A river of the returned flowing slowly through the streets of their own city, touching doorframes, reading shop signs, standing in front of windows and seeing their own reflections aged thirty years past the selves that stared back out of the glass.

Because that was the terrible arithmetic of it: the sleepers hadn't aged. Their bodies were exactly as the erasure had taken them. But the memories I carried were children's memories — recorded in the years before the end — and so every woken adult met themselves young again through someone else's eyes, and every meeting was a small funeral for the years they'd lost. Thirty years, stolen. Weddings not attended. Children raised elsewhere or not at all. Parents buried by other hands.

I carried their childhoods. I could not carry their missing decades. No one could. Those were simply gone, spent like coin in a guild ledger, and there was no countersuit to file against time.

I stopped counting how many I woke. I counted instead in other measures: how many apples Osla's basket held (forty-one, and climbing). How many times Davor whistled through his cupped hands and some newly woken stranger answered from a window, the old call-and-response of a city remembering itself. How many times Pell knelt in the dust to fix a sleeper's shoe before we woke them, so they'd step into their new life on solid footing. Small strokes. Enormous things.

* * *

On the sixth night — if you could call it night, this place had only ever dusk and deeper dusk — I found the school.

It stood on a hill above the orchard district, and I knew it before I saw the sign, because I had forty memories of it inside me and all forty flared at once like lamps catching flame. The schoolyard gate. The bell rope. The coat pegs with names burned into them. I stood at the bottom of the hill with my procession fanned out behind me, and I couldn't move.

"You don't have to go in alone," Osla said. She'd appointed herself my keeper somewhere along the way, the way strong people do when they find a child doing an impossible labor. "Whatever's in there."

"It's mine," I said. "All of it. That's the whole building. That's what I am." I laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "They put a whole school inside me, Osla. Forty children. I know where every coat peg is. I know which floorboard in the third classroom squeaks. I know a boy named Tam who hid a knife under the west stair because he was scared of the dark, and I have never once been afraid of the dark, because how could I be? I'm full of other people's courage. It's the only thing I have that's actually mine — being the place where theirs is kept."

Osla took my hand. Her palm was rough with thirty years of paused apple-picking. "Then let's go give it back," she said.

We woke the school last of the district, and we did it in order of the coat pegs, alphabetically, because I wanted them to come back to their names first and their bodies second. Each child — no. Not children anymore. Each *adult*, each grown stranger wearing a child's unaged face, woke into a body that hadn't lived a single day past eleven, and each of them looked at their own small hands and screamed or laughed or went silent, and each of them looked at me and knew me instantly, the way you know a house you grew up in. Because I was the house. I was the schoolyard and the bell rope and the squeaking floorboard, and when they touched my face they were touching their own remembered walls.

"We felt you coming," a girl named Sennet told me — a girl with a baker's future waiting somewhere in her, though she didn't know it yet. "For days. Like weather. Like a storm made of us."

"That's what I am," I said. "A storm made of you. And I'm almost empty now."

Almost. Not quite. I could feel the hollows opening inside me where memories had lived, spaces going dark and quiet one by one, and I did not know yet what would fill them. Maybe nothing. Maybe, at the end of all this giving, I would finally be a person instead of a vessel — small and unfinished and entirely my own. The thought terrified me more than the Silent Court ever had.

But that was tomorrow's hunger. Tonight there were still sleepers on the coast road, and lanterns to make, and Osla's basket needed emptying into the hands of the newly woken, because someone will want to eat when they wake.

* * *

We came down toward the bakery district near what passed for dawn, our procession now numbering in the hundreds, a slow bright river of the returned carrying torches through streets that had waited thirty years for footsteps. And that was when I heard it — ahead of us, from a low stone building with flour still dusted across its doorstep, frozen mid-sift — a sound underneath the silence.

Singing.

Someone inside the bakery was already awake. Had been awake, maybe, for hours — a sleeper who needed no fragment, no vessel, no key, who had simply fought her way up through the amber on her own, alone in the dark of a paused world, and was singing to keep herself from shattering until help came.

I knew the song. Of course I knew the song. Every one of my three hundred knew it; it was the lullaby of the whole country, sung in kitchens and schoolyards in the years before the end. My chest went tight around the last few memories I still carried, and one of them stirred — a kitchen, flour in the air, a woman's voice, a small girl on a stool — and I knew, with the certainty of a ledger entry written in my own bones, whose memory I was about to give home.

I stepped through the bakery door, and the singing stopped, and a woman turned toward me with flour on her hands and terror and hope fighting openly on her face.

"Hello," I said, in pre-kingdom Serevani, in the voice of her daughter. "My name is Iri. I think I have something of yours."
