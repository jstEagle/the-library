## 30. The First to Wake

The last thing Maren Voss remembered was the smell of cardamom.

Not the bells — though the bells had been ringing, all of them at once, a sound like the whole city crying out one long note. Not the light — though the light had gone strange, thick as honey, pooling in the doorways of her street as if the evening had decided to stay forever. What she remembered, what she held onto as she fell upward into sleep, was the cardamom: she had been grinding it for tomorrow's loaves, and her daughter had come running through the shop with dirt on her knees and a beetle cupped in both hands, shouting *Mama, look, look what I found*, and Maren had said, *Wash your hands first, little bird,* and the girl had laughed —

And then nothing. Thirty years of nothing.

She woke the way bread rises: slowly, from the center outward, warmth spreading through what had been cold and dense and still. The first thing to return was weight — the weight of her own body, her arms, her chest rising. Then sound: a drip of water somewhere, rhythmic as a metronome. Then smell, and it was cardamom again, faint and impossible, and Maren thought, *I never finished the loaves.*

Her eyes opened.

She was standing in her bakery.

That should have been a comfort, and for one heartbeat it was — the great brick oven against the far wall, the flour-sacked counter, the shelf of copper molds her mother had left her, all of it exactly where thirty years ago had left it. But the light was wrong. It came through the windows without moving, without warming, a dusk-colored amber that did not shift no matter how long she looked at it. Dust hung in the air in perfect suspension, each mote fixed in place like stars in a painted sky. And outside the window, on the street, people stood mid-stride, mid-gesture, mid-word — the lamplighter with his pole raised, a woman with a basket of pears tilted forever toward the cobblestones, two boys frozen in the act of stealing those very pears, their faces caught between mischief and terror.

Maren's hand went to her mouth. She made a sound behind it, small and animal.

*I am dreaming,* she told herself. *I fell asleep by the oven and I am dreaming.* But dreams did not include the ache in her feet, or the precise itch of flour dust dried into the creases of her knuckles, or the terrible clarity of the pear about to hit the ground — she could see every bruise on it, every golden freckle, and she knew with a certainty that lived in her bones rather than her mind that if she caught it, it would be real in her hand.

She caught it.

It was cold. Cold as lake water, cold as a stone kept in shadow, but it filled her palm with its honest weight and its skin was slightly tacky, the way ripe pears are, and Maren stood there in her unmoving bakery holding a pear and shaking.

*Little bird,* she thought. *Where is my little bird?*

Her daughter's name rose in her — *Sela* — and then, horribly, the name came with no face attached. She knew the name the way you know a word in a language you've stopped speaking. She reached for the girl's face and found a smooth blankness where the memory should have been, like a page torn cleanly from a book, and the not-knowing hurt worse than any grief she had ever felt, because grief needs a shape and this had none. She could not even mourn properly. She could only stand in the ruins of her own kitchen and reach, again and again, into the empty space where her daughter had been.

"Hello," said a voice behind her.

Maren turned so fast the pear flew from her hand. It struck the floorboards and rolled, unbruised, and stopped — and lay there, and did not continue rolling, because the floor of this place seemed willing to hold things still when they were frightened too.

A child stood in her doorway. A girl, perhaps nine years old, wearing a coat too big for her and boots crusted with ice-melt, and there was something wrong with her eyes — not wrong the way sickness is wrong, but wrong the way a window is wrong when it shows you a room that isn't yours. They were full of other people. Maren could not have said how she knew that. She simply looked into the child's face and felt a crowd looking back.

"You're awake," the child said. Her voice was careful, the way a person walks on river ice. "You're the first. You're really the first."

"What is this?" Maren's voice came out rusted. She swallowed and tried again. "What did you do? Where is everyone? Where—" and her hand moved without her permission, pressing flat against her own sternum, over the hollow place. "Where is my daughter?"

The child flinched. It was a small flinch, quickly mastered, but Maren had spent twenty years reading the faces of customers who could not afford their bread, and she saw everything in it: guilt, and sorrow, and something older than either.

"I don't know where she is," the child said. "I'm sorry. This city is big, and everyone is... standing. But I know her." The girl stepped forward, and her small fists clenched at her sides as if bracing for a blow. "I have her."

"You—"

"I have her memories," the child said. "All of them. From when she was very small until—" She stopped. Swallowed. "Until the end. They gave them to me. All the children's memories, all together, so someone could carry them home. That's me. I'm the carrying." She lifted her chin, and her strange crowded eyes shone wet in the honeyed light. "I've been giving them back. One at a time. You're the first one who woke up all the way."

Maren backed away until her hip struck the counter. Flour puffed up from the sacking and hung in the air beside her, motionless, a little ghost-cloud that refused to fall. "That's not possible," she whispered. "Memories aren't things you can *carry*. They aren't things at all."

"They are here," the child said simply. "Here, they are. Here, they can be sold." She reached into the pocket of her enormous coat and drew out something that caught the amber light and bent it — a sliver of glass, or crystal, or something that was neither, no bigger than a thumbnail, glowing faintly from within like a coal wrapped in gauze. "This is hers. Do you want it?"

Every instinct Maren owned told her to say no. Strangers did not offer you pieces of your own heart in doorways; the world did not work that way; the world had rules, and the rules had kept her safe her whole life, and the rules said that a child with a crowd behind her eyes offering you a glowing shard was either a trickster or a ghost and both should be refused.

But the hollow place under her hand ached. And somewhere in that hollow, a name echoed — *Sela, Sela* — with no face, no laugh, no weight of a sleeping child on her shoulder, and Maren discovered that thirty years of frozen nothing had burned away every instinct except one.

"Yes," she said.

* * *

The child — Iri, she said her name was, just Iri, as if a single name were enough to hold her, which Maren would later understand it was not — pressed the fragment into Maren's palm and folded her fingers closed around it.

"It will feel like falling," Iri said. "Don't fight it. Fighting makes it take longer."

"What feels like—"

The world dropped out from under her.

Not the bakery — the world. Maren fell through darkness that smelled of rain on warm stone, and then the darkness opened like a curtain, and she was *there*.

She was kneeling in the courtyard behind the bakery, and Sela was four years old, and it was raining, and the girl was stomping in puddles with her boots on the wrong feet because she had dressed herself and would hear no corrections, and she looked up at Maren with her whole face — with that face, that round-cheeked gap-toothed face with the smudge of mud across the nose — and screamed with joy, pure joy, the joy of a child for whom rain exists solely to be jumped in, and Maren heard herself laughing, heard her own young laugh ring off the courtyard walls—

Then Sela was seven, sitting on the flour counter with her legs swinging, reciting the names of breads while Maren shaped them — *brioche, boule, batard* — getting them wrong on purpose to make her mother groan—

Then Sela was eleven, furious, slamming the door because Maren would not let her go to the festival alone, and Maren stood in the kitchen with her hands still dusty and let herself smile where no one could see, because the fury meant the girl was growing, and growing was the whole point—

Then Sela was thirteen, asleep against Maren's shoulder on the bench outside the cathedral, both of them tired from the market, and the bells began, and Sela murmured without waking, *Mama, the bells sound like the sky is full of water*—

Then Sela was fifteen, and there were men in gray coats in the square, and the bells were ringing wrong, all of them at once, and Sela's hand was in hers, gripping hard, and Sela said, *Mama, why is the light doing that,* and Maren said, *It's nothing, little bird, it's nothing, hold my hand*—

Then the light took everything.

Then nothing.

Then Maren was on her knees on the bakery floor, and the pear had rolled to rest against her shin, and she was weeping — not the delicate tears of remembering but the raw, heaving sobs of a woman who has been handed back her entire child in the space of ten seconds and must now survive having received her. Every year. Every scraped knee and stubborn silence and festival dress and fevered night. It was all there. It was all *her*.

"She had a mole," Maren gasped, "on her left shoulder, shaped like a comma — I used to kiss it — I'd forgotten — how could I have forgotten, it was *hers*, it was—"

"The ones who took them didn't care about moles," Iri said quietly. She had knelt down too, an arm's length away, close enough to touch and not touching, the way you approach a wounded animal. "They cared about weight. Memories weigh differently depending on how much love is in them. Hers were heavy. They sold well."

Maren raised her head. Grief was still pouring through her, huge and tidal, but underneath the grief something else was assembling itself, joint by joint, the way a body stands up. Something with teeth.

"*Sold.*"

Iri nodded once.

"By whom?"

"People from outside. From a guild, in another country. They bought our country out from under us, entry by entry, ledger line by ledger line, until there wasn't enough of Serevan left to keep it in the world. And they took the memories and carried them away and sold them." The child's voice did not shake. That was the most frightening thing about it. "The man who runs them is here now. Inside. With crews. He's cutting pieces out of the city while it sleeps. While everyone stands in the streets holding their pears."

Maren looked down at the pear against her leg. She picked it up. It was still perfect, still cold, still waiting thirty years to finish its fall, and she understood suddenly that this was what she had been doing too — suspended, unfinished, a life interrupted mid-arc — and that the child before her had walked through a burning, hollowed world to bring her the missing half of herself.

Something broke open in her chest, and it was not grief. Or it was grief, but grief turned inside out, grief with a spine.

"Get up," Maren said.

Iri blinked. "What?"

"Get *up*." Maren rose, and her knees cracked like green wood, thirty years of stillness protesting, and she did not care. She pulled off her apron — the apron was still there, still dusted with cardamom, still hers — and tied it tight. "How many more are standing? In this street? In this quarter?"

"Hundreds. Thousands. Everyone. But I'm slow, Mama—" The word slipped out of the child before she could catch it, and her eyes went wide, and Maren saw the crowd behind them go utterly still. "I'm sorry. I didn't mean—I carry so many mothers, sometimes the words—"

"Say it again," Maren said.

Iri's mouth trembled. "Mama."

"No." Maren crouched, took the small cold face in her flour-dusted hands, and looked into those crowded eyes until the crowd looked back at her. "You listen to me, little bird. Whatever you are carrying, whoever else lives in there — right now, in this kitchen, you are mine too. Do you understand? My daughter's memories live in you, which means some part of her taught you to say that word, which means I have earned the right to feed you. Have you eaten? Don't answer. Of course you haven't. No one feeds the person carrying the whole country."

"Maren—"

"There's dough proofing under that cloth that was going to be tomorrow's bread thirty years ago, and by some mercy it hasn't died, and I am going to bake it, and you are going to eat, and *then*—" She stood, and pointed through the window at the frozen street, at the lamplighter and the pear-woman and the thieving boys, at the whole suspended congregation of her city. "*Then* you are going to show me how to give them back. Because you've been carrying them alone, child, and that was never the design. A baker knows: no loaf rises alone. It takes a whole oven's worth of heat."

Iri stared at her. And then the crowd behind her eyes shifted — Maren watched it happen, watched thousands of sleeping children turn over at once, as if her words had reached them through the glass — and the girl began to cry, silently, hugely, the way children cry when they have finally been allowed to put down something heavy.

"I'm so tired," Iri whispered. "I've been so tired, and I couldn't stop, because if I stopped—"

"You stopped," Maren said. "Right here. Right now. Sit down. Eat first. Save the world after supper — that's the rule in this house, and it always has been."

* * *

The oven, impossibly, still held its heat.

Maren chose not to question this. There was a limit to how much impossibility a person could metabolize in one morning, and she had decided to spend her ration on the living. She scored the risen loaves — the dough had continued its slow work through three decades of dusk, patient as anything in Serevan — and slid them in, and within minutes the bakery filled with the smell of baking bread, and it was the truest thing that had happened since she woke. Bread did not care that the light stood still. Bread rose anyway. Somewhere in that fact, Maren found a theology.

Iri sat on the flour counter — the same counter, Maren realized, where a seven-year-old had once swung her legs and mangled the names of breads — and ate the first hot loaf in torn-off pieces, chewing with her eyes closed, and with every bite the crowd behind her eyes seemed to settle, to quiet, to make room.

"How does it work?" Maren asked, kneading the second batch out of old habit, her hands remembering what her mind had lost. "Giving them back. The fragments."

"You hold it and you think of them," Iri said. "Really think — their name, their face, something true. The fragment wants to go home. It's been waiting. You're not forcing it, you're... opening the door." She hesitated. "It's easier if you love them. The guild never figured that out. That's why their extractions always leave holes. You can't handle a memory roughly and expect it to arrive intact."

Maren snorted despite herself. "Thirty years frozen and the first lesson is still the same: everything comes down to whether you were gentle."

"That's what Sela says." Iri stopped. Corrected herself, softly: "Said. When she was small. About beetles. *Be gentle, Mama, it's more scared than you are.*"

Maren's hands went still in the dough. For a moment the kitchen blurred, and she let it.

"All right," she said when she could speak. "Show me the next one. And the next after that. We'll work the street corner by corner — the lamplighter first, he's closest, and his poor arm must be tired." She wiped her hands and held them out. "Give me a fragment, little bird. Let's wake a city."

Iri was reaching into her coat when she froze — head cocked, listening to something Maren could not hear, the crowd in her eyes all turning to look in the same direction at once, out the window, toward the river.

"What is it?" Maren said. "What's wrong?"

"Bells," Iri whispered. "Under the ice. Someone's at the Last Door. And—" Her face went pale. "There are men in the archive district. Gray coats. They've started a fire in the Hall of Records — they're burning what they can't carry." She looked up at Maren, and for the first time since waking her, the child looked like a child: scared, and asking. "The man who runs them. His name is Corvane. He's coming this way. He can feel every memory I give back — each one is a lamp being lit in a dark house, and he's been walking toward the lamps."

Maren untied her apron and retied it tighter. Through the window, the frozen lamplighter stood with his pole raised, ready, she thought, to be handed his flame at last.

"Then we'd better burn bright enough to blind him," she said. "Come along, little bird. Bring your whole crowd. Mama's making noise."
