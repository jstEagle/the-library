## 37. What the Guild Keeps

The guild hall smelled of ash and old paper for eleven days after the winter broke.

Vesha walked its corridors with a lamp in one hand and a crowbar in the other, because the doors had sealed themselves when the harvest collapsed — wax poured into the locks by clerks who understood, better than anyone, that a ledger can be closed from either side. She pried them open one at a time, room by room, and in each room she found the same thing: the machinery of memory, stopped mid-motion.

Vials still racked in their cedar cabinets, thousands of them, each labeled in a clerk's careful hand. *Bottled laughter, vintage unknown. A mother's voice, forty seconds. The smell of rain on a specific street in a city that no longer exists.* The extraction chairs sat empty, their leather straps hanging loose like shed skins. In the great counting room, Corvane's abacus lay on its side, beads scattered across the floor in a pattern no one would ever read as arithmetic again.

She did not loot it. That was the first strange thing, and she noticed it being strange, because looting was what Vesha did — had done for thirty years, since she was a girl with quick fingers and no surname worth stealing. She stood in the doorway of the vault with her hands full of other people's lives and felt nothing move in her except the old familiar hunger, and the hunger said *take*, and she said, out loud, to the empty room: "No."

Her voice echoed off the vials. It sounded like someone else's.

* * *

They came to her because there was no one else. That was how power worked in Vhalen's undercity — not granted but defaulted, settling like silt into whatever low place remained. The Nightmarket elders first, then the debtors' kin, then, absurdly, two junior clerks who had survived the collapse of the guild by hiding in the map room and eating the glue off the envelopes. They stood in her stall among the Serevani goods she'd never sold — the copper bell, the folded wedding shawl, the jar of last words she'd kept out of superstition rather than sentiment — and they looked at her the way people look at a lighthouse.

"The guild is yours," said the elder called Ombreth, who had sold Vesha her first stolen fragment back when both of them were young enough to think memory was infinite.

"It's not mine."

"Someone has to hold it. Someone has to decide what happens to what's inside." He gestured at the sealed doors above, the whole drowned weight of the Ledger Guild sitting on the undercity like a stone on a chest. "You know its workings. You dealt in its goods. And you were taken by the Silent Court and came back." His eyes went to her hands, where the frost-scars still showed, white lines across the knuckles like tally marks. "That makes you either the most qualified or the most cursed person in Vhalen. Choose which story we tell."

Vesha looked at the clerks. One of them, a boy of maybe nineteen with ink stains to his elbows, was holding his own ledger against his chest like a shield.

"What do you want me to do with it?" she asked him.

"Burn it," he said immediately, and then, quieter, with the terrible honesty of the young: "But I don't know what's in it yet. I only ever saw my floor. There might be things down there that shouldn't burn."

There were. Vesha knew there were, because she'd spent three decades buying and selling exactly those things, and she knew the difference between a commodity and a relic the way a fence knows the difference between a stolen ring and a dead man's ring. Some fragments you could sell without a flicker. Others went through your hands cold, all the way through, and left you unable to sleep.

"Fine," she said. "I'll hold it. Not keep it. Hold it. There's a difference, and I'll be charging rent on anyone who forgets."

* * *

The work took the whole of the thaw.

She started with the ledgers, because ledgers were the spine of the thing, and if you wanted to understand a body you started at the spine. She carried them up from the archive in armfuls, dusty volumes bound in ledger-calf, and spread them across the counting-room floor, and she read.

She read about herself, first, because vanity is honest. Entry 4,411: *Vesha Ansel, fence, Nightmarket. Debt incurred: protection fees, 12 years' accrual. Assets held: none of value. Note: subject deals in Serevani fragments; recommend surveillance rather than extraction; useful intermediary.* Useful intermediary. Thirty years of her life summarized in three words, and beneath it, in Corvane's hand, a small annotation she would carry to her grave: *Subject retains one fragment of personal origin. Do not touch. Sentimental assets make unreliable intermediaries.*

He had known. All those years, all those deals across her stall, he had known she kept one thing back, and he had let her, because a woman with one soft spot is easier to steer than a woman with none.

She read about Emrys Oyelaran next, or tried to. The file was a ruin — pages voided, pages rewritten, whole sections burned away by some countersuit written in blood-ink, the clerical warfare still visible in the scorch marks along the margins. But here and there a line survived: *born within the boundary, pre-erasure*; *memories extracted at age six per standard sale*; *balance outstanding: everything.* She closed that file gently, the way you close a door on a sleeping child, and put it in the pile marked RETURN.

Because that was the system she built, in the second week, on the counting-room floor: three piles. *Return* — fragments whose owners could be found, or whose owners' children could be found, or whose owners were standing frozen in a country beyond a Door that now stood open, waiting to come home to themselves. *Burn* — the cruelties, the extractions taken from the desperate as interest, memories of grief and shame harvested precisely because grief and shame made people compliant. Those she fed to the counting-room stove herself, one at a time, watching each label curl before she let it go, so that no one could ever accuse her of burning something precious by accident. And *keep* —

The keep pile stayed small. That was the discipline of it. Every fragment that went into the third pile had to earn its place twice: once for usefulness, once because returning it would do harm. A midwife's knowledge of a fever that had died with her city — kept, until the Serevani arrived and she could hand it over in person. A confession, freely given, that named a living man's crime — kept, because justice deserved a witness even when the courts were ash. And hers.

Hers she did not put in any pile. Hers lived in the lining of her coat, where it had lived for thirty years, a fragment no bigger than an apricot pit, warm as a held hand.

* * *

On the nineteenth day, the Serevani began to come.

They came through the undercity gates in ones and twos at first, blinking, wrapped in clothes that had been fashionable thirty years ago, carrying nothing, because you cannot carry anything through an erasure and back. Maren the baker led them — a broad, flour-armed woman with a voice like a dropped pan, who had appointed herself the exodus's quartermaster within a day of waking. Behind her came farmers and fiddlers and a tax assessor who wept openly at the sight of Vhalen's ledgers, and children, always children, though fewer than there should have been, and Vesha learned not to ask why.

They came to the guild because Maren decided they should. "You're holding our country's memories," she said, standing in the gutted counting room with her hands on her hips, surveying the vial-racks like a general surveying terrain. "Then we're coming to collect. You'll want help anyway. Half your clerks couldn't find their own names in an index."

"We don't know who half these belong to," Vesha said. "The labels are clerk-shorthand. Half of them are wrong."

"Then we'll match them the slow way." Maren picked up a vial at random, turned it to the light. *A kitchen in autumn, someone singing badly.* Her face did something complicated. "This is Danya's. This is my neighbor Danya's kitchen, and that's her husband singing, and he sang exactly that badly." She set it down with exaggerated care. "You see? We remember. That's the whole horror of what they did and the whole cure for it. You cannot lose what people refuse to stop knowing."

So it became a kind of market after all — the last market the guild would ever host, and the only honest one. Serevani survivors walking the racks, touching vials, saying *mine, mine, not mine, mine, this one is my brother's, he's in the third wave of arrivals, save it for him.* Vesha ran the counter because running a counter was the only prayer she knew. She took no payment. When people insisted, she took bread, or buttons, or a song sung badly in a language that was suddenly, impossibly, a living tongue again.

And every evening she climbed to the counting room, added to the return pile, thinned the burn pile, and checked the keep pile like a miser checking a lock — and every evening the keep pile's smallest member stayed where it had always been, against her ribs, in her coat lining, warm.

* * *

It was the boy clerk — Tam, his name was, Tam with the ink-stained elbows — who finally asked her about it.

They were cataloguing the deep vault, the one below the waterline where Corvane had kept the fragments too dangerous or too valuable for the open racks. Tam hauled a crate onto the table, and Vesha's coat shifted as she leaned, and the fragment slipped — not out, never out, but sideways, and its warmth pressed against her side like a question.

Tam pretended not to notice, which was its own kind of noticing. He sorted vials in silence for a while. Then:

"My mother sold a memory once," he said. "Before I was born. To pay for the midwife. She never told me which one. I used to lie awake wondering what I didn't know about her — what piece of her I'd never meet." He set a vial down. "When the guild fell, I looked. It's in the return pile already. I matched it last week." He paused. "It was her wedding day. She sold her own wedding day, and then she married my father anyway, and never told him why she cried at the anniversary every year."

Vesha's hands went still over the crate.

"Do you ever wonder," Tam said, in the careful voice of the young approaching a cliff, "what you're keeping?"

"No," said Vesha.

"You just said—"

"I said no, and I meant the kind of wondering that asks permission." She straightened, and her coat settled, and the warmth against her ribs pulsed once like a second heartbeat. "Some things you keep because you're greedy, boy, and some you keep because you're the only one left who can. I've spent thirty years telling myself it was the second kind. Now the guild's mine and nobody's making me keep anything, and I still haven't put it in a pile. So maybe it was the first kind all along. Maybe it's both. That's what imperfect means."

Tam considered this with the gravity of someone taking dictation. "What is it? Yours?"

Vesha looked at the vault around them — the crates, the dust, the dark water seeping somewhere below, patient as arithmetic. And because he had shown her his mother's wound, she reached into her coat lining and drew it out: the fragment, apricot-small, glowing faintly the way Serevani fragments glowed, amber shot through with green.

"This," she said, "is the only hour of my life I have never sold, traded, fenced, or spent. I was nine years old. My mother was Serevani — did you know that? Nobody knows that. She crossed the border before the erasure with nothing but me and a recipe for plum brandy, and she married a Vhaleni cooper, and she never spoke the old tongue in the house, never once, except—" Her voice caught, and she let it, because she was fifty-one years old and entitled to one catch. "Except one evening a year. The night the bells used to ring in Serevan. She'd take me up on the roof, and she'd sing, badly, the way people sing when they're remembering rather than performing. And I asked her once what the song was for, and she said, *for the country that isn't anywhere, so it knows somebody's still counting.*"

Tam said nothing. The vault dripped.

"A year later she was dead. Fever. And the debt collectors came for the cooperage, and I needed coin, and I was nine and quick-fingered and I sold everything she'd ever owned except one thing." Vesha turned the fragment in the lamplight. "I sold the song itself, eventually — there was a buyer, there's always a buyer — but before I did, I paid a hedge-witch to draw this out of me. One hour. The last time she sang it. Just the hour, nothing else, so I'd know it happened. So the country that isn't anywhere would know somebody was still counting."

"And you've kept it thirty years."

"I've kept it thirty years." She laughed, short and unamused. "Do you know what I could get for it now? Now that Serevan's real again, now that there are people who'd weep to hear that song? I could fund the whole return. I could buy back every fragment in the burn pile twice over."

"But you won't."

"I don't know," Vesha said honestly, and the honesty surprised them both. "That's the thing about redemption, boy. Everyone thinks it's a door you walk through. It's not. It's a ledger entry you write every single day, and some days you write it clean and some days you write it crooked, and the crooked days don't undo the clean ones, they just sit next to them in the same book." She held the fragment up between them, amber and green, warm. "I returned four thousand memories this month. I burned six hundred cruelties. And I am going to keep one hour of my mother singing on a roof, because I am not a saint, I am a fence, and a fence keeps one thing back. Always one. It's how you stay human in this trade."

She put it back in the lining, against her ribs, where it settled warm as a held hand.

"But," she added, before Tam could look away, "I'm going to sing it. Once. At the spring festival, when the last of them have come through. Badly. The way she sang it. So it stops being a possession and becomes a thing in the air again." She shrugged, embarrassed. "Maybe that's the redemption. Not giving it away. Letting it go free and finding out it comes back on its own."

* * *

The festival came with the true spring, the first spring in thirty years that Vhalen had spent above ground.

They held it on the lake shore, where the ice had gone soft and blue and finally, in the last week, broken apart into drifting plates that rang against each other in the wind — and underneath, faint but unmistakable, the bells of Serevan still sounded, though the Door now stood open on the far shore and the country's people walked out of it daily, blinking, into a world that had forgotten them and was learning fast.

Vesha sang on the second evening, from a cart bed, badly, while Maren's bread went round and the Serevani children — real children now, ordinary, aging, their cargo emptied into the waking country — danced in the trampled snow-mud. She sang the roof-song, and her voice cracked on the high part exactly the way her mother's had, and in the crowd a dozen Serevani mouths opened in recognition and joined her, and the song went up into the air and stopped belonging to anyone.

Afterward, alone by the water's edge, she took the fragment out one last time.

It was lighter. She had known it would be — the hedge-witch had warned her, thirty years ago, that a memory released into the world loses its weight, that hoarded things grow heavy and shared things grow thin. It sat in her palm like a coal going out, amber fading toward glass, and inside it, faintly, she could still hear the shape of the song, the way you hear a bell after it stops ringing.

"Well," she said to it. "We did it, Mam. Country's back. Somebody counted."

She did not throw it in the lake. She was a fence, not a saint. She put it back in her coat lining, where it would grow thinner and thinner and warmer and warmer until one day it was nothing but warmth, and that was fine. That was more than fine. That was the whole trick of it — you keep the vessel, and let the contents become the world.

Behind her, footsteps in the mud. Maren, come to fetch her back to the fire, and beside Maren someone else — a girl of perhaps twelve, ordinary-faced, ordinary-aged, walking with the deliberate care of someone newly light. And behind the girl, at the edge of the firelight, a figure Vesha recognized from files and scorched margins: the former debtor, the born-of-Serevan, the one whose name had been voided and remade, standing uncertain in the spring air with a fresh ledger under one arm.

Maren smiled the smile of a woman about to make an introduction that mattered.

"There's someone you should meet," she said. "They're starting a new guild, of sorts. A record-keeping. And they need a fence who knows where all the bodies — and all the ledgers — are buried."

Vesha looked at the fragment's fading warmth against her ribs, and at the open Door shining on the far shore, and at the fresh ledger, blank and waiting, and felt the oldest hunger in her rise one final time — and, for the first time in thirty years, point the right direction.

"Well," she said. "Lead on."
