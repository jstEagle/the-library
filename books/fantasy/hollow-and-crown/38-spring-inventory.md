## 38. Spring Inventory

The first thing Sef knew was the smell of bread.

Not the guild's bread — that dense grey loaf issued with ration chits and eaten standing, tasting of nothing so much as obligation — but real bread, dark-crusted, sweet with something like honey and something like smoke, baking somewhere below. The smell came up through the floorboards of a room Sef did not recognize and settled over them like a second blanket.

They lay still for a long time, eyes closed, taking inventory.

This was an old habit, older than memory could account for: when you wake in a strange place, count what is true before you decide what to fear. So they counted. A bed beneath them, narrow but well-made, the mattress stuffed with something that rustled faintly — straw mixed, perhaps, with dried lavender, because there was a sweetness under the wool that was not bread. A window at their left, curtained in undyed linen, through which light came the color of new milk. Their own body, whole, aching only dully, the way a body aches after a long walk it cannot remember taking. Two hands. Ten fingers. Ink stains on the right one, deep in the whorls of the thumb and forefinger, the stains of years.

That much was familiar. That much was theirs.

The rest —

Sef opened their eyes.

The room was small and clean and plainly loved. There were shelves, and on the shelves books — not ledgers, though there were those too, stacked neatly on a low table by the door, their spines unmarked — but actual books, novels, herbals, a fat volume of folktales with a cracked spine and a ribbon bookmark hanging out around page two hundred and some. There was a coat hung on a peg by the window, a good coat, patched at the elbows with careful, even stitches. There was a jar of wildflowers on the sill — crocuses and early windflowers, the first brave stupid blooms of thaw — and beside the jar, a cup of tea gone cold, which meant someone had set it there expecting them to wake, and had waited, and had gone away again without disturbing them.

Sef sat up slowly, and the year moved.

It did not come back. That was the wrong way to describe it, and they would spend many mornings learning all the wrong ways to describe it. It *moved* — a great weight shifting somewhere behind their ribs, the way ice shifts on a lake before it breaks, a groan and a shudder and then nothing, no crack, no flood. Just the sense of enormous mass held in suspension, just below the surface, pressing up against the underside of everything they knew.

They knew their name. Emrys Oyelaran — except that even as they thought it, the name sat strangely, like a coat cut for someone slightly taller. They knew the Ledger Guild of Vhalen, its stairwells and its ink-smell and the particular squeak of the third-floor boards. They knew debt — knew it the way you know a scar's shape with your tongue, intimately, without remembering the wound. They knew they had been a scribe. They did not know why they were here, wherever here was, or why the air smelled of lake water and woodsmoke and spring, or why, when they tried to reach backward along the thread of their own life, the thread went soft and grey somewhere past a certain season — past snow, they thought, past a great cold — and simply stopped, frayed, like rope worn through.

They did not know the child's voice calling up the stairs.

"Sef! Bread's done!"

The name went through them like a bell struck inside the chest. Not Emrys. *Sef.* And the strangest thing — stranger than the strange room, stranger than the missing year — was that some part of them turned toward that name the way a plant turns toward light. Without thinking. Without evidence. As if the name had been theirs far longer than the other one had.

They found trousers folded on the chair, and a sweater that smelled of cedar, and they dressed because the alternative was standing in a stranger's nightshirt having a crisis, and Sef — whoever Sef was — had apparently never been one for crises before breakfast.

* * *

The house stood alone on a rise above a lake, and the lake was breaking up.

Sef stood on the doorstep with the morning wind in their face and watched the ice go. It was happening all across the wide grey water: long reports like distant cannon fire, then the slow groan of sheets tilting, the white fields tilting into black water, the whole frozen skin of the lake shrugging itself off after a winter it had clearly believed would never end. Gulls wheeled and screamed over the leads of open water. Down the slope, a village — small, maybe forty roofs, smoke rising straight from chimneys in the windless cold-bright air — and among the roofs, people moving, carrying things, and here and there figures Sef's eye snagged on without understanding: a woman stacking firewood with a strange ceremonial care, kissing each log almost; a man standing in his doorway letting the sun hit his face with his eyes shut and tears running freely down it, making no move to wipe them away; children everywhere, more children than a village that size should hold, as if the place had taken in the displaced from a dozen towns.

Refugees, Sef thought. Or returnees. The word *returnees* arrived in their mind wearing clothes they didn't recognize, and they let it pass.

Behind them, the kitchen was warm and low-beamed and smelled of the dark bread now cooling on its board. At the table sat a girl of perhaps eleven, brown-skinned, sharp-eyed, with her hair in two braids that had clearly been braided by someone who loved her and not by herself, because they were immaculate. She looked up when Sef came in, and her expression did something complicated — a flicker of hope, quickly banked, smoothed over with the practiced neutrality of a child who has learned not to expect too much.

"You're awake," she said. "You slept three days."

"Did I." Sef's voice came out rough. "I'm sorry — I don't—"

"I know." She said it simply, without bitterness, the way you'd say *it's raining*. "Maren said you wouldn't. She said don't be hurt about it. She said—" and here the girl's voice shifted subtly, taking on the cadence of borrowed wisdom, an elder's words worn smooth by repetition, "—she said, *tell them grief has a twin sister called mercy, and between them they took the year, and neither of them will apologize.*"

"Maren," Sef repeated. The woman with the firewood? The name fit her somehow, or fit the idea of her — bread, hearth, fierce practical love. "And you are?"

Something crossed the girl's face then, quick as a fish under ice. She looked down at her hands, folded on the table, and when she looked up again she had arranged her face into politeness, and it was the politeness that broke Sef's heart, though they could not have said why.

"Iri," she said. "My name is Iri."

*Iri.* The name landed nowhere. It was a stone dropped into Sef with no ripples at all, and that absence — that perfect, polished absence where a ripple should have been — frightened them more than any memory could have. You do not miss what you never had. But some animal part of them, some part below the ledger-line of the self, flinched at that smooth blank water and wanted to cry out that something had been *taken*, that the stillness was wrong, that names should splash—

"Tea?" Iri asked, already pouring, already knowing.

"Yes," Sef said. "Please."

* * *

They ate bread and honey and hard-boiled eggs, and Iri talked, filling the silence the way children do, and every word she said was a small key turning in a lock Sef couldn't find.

She told them the village was called New Vesper, though it wasn't really new; it had been called that before, long ago, by people who were only now coming back to it. She told them the lake was called Lake Serel, and that under the ice — under where the ice had been — there were bells, and in spring, when the water cleared, you could sometimes see them from the rowboats, green with weed, still hanging in their drowned tower. She told them that most of the people in the village had come through "the Door," and she said the word with a capital-D gravity, the way you say a name of someone dead or holy, and that the Door was in the hills to the north and that these days it stood open, and that people came through it every week, blinking, weeping, carrying nothing, remembering everything.

Remembering everything. Sef set down their cup.

"And me?" they asked. "Did I come through the Door?"

Iri considered this with the seriousness of a magistrate. "Sort of," she said at last. "You're written down as coming through. Maren wrote it in the arrivals book. But Vesha says—" and again the shift of register, the borrowed cadence, "—Vesha says your coming-through was of a different kind. Vesha says you paid the toll twice, once going and once staying, and that the second payment was the larger, and that she hopes—" Iri frowned, working to get it exactly right, "—she hopes the interest compounds gently."

"Vesha sounds like a woman who talks in ledger entries."

"She used to keep a shop," Iri said, with the tone of one explaining everything. "In the Nightmarket. She keeps the arrivals book now instead. She says it's the same work, only honest."

Sef laughed — surprised to find laughter still in stock — and Iri smiled, quick and bright, and then the smile faltered, caught on something, and she looked away toward the window where the gulls were crying over the breaking ice.

"What is it?" Sef asked.

"Nothing." Too fast. Then, with the terrible honesty of children deciding to spend their honesty all at once: "You laughed the same way before. That's all. Maren said you might not. She said the person who comes back isn't always the person who left, and I shouldn't watch for him in you. But you laugh the same way." She picked at her bread. "So he's not entirely gone. He's just — quiet."

*Him.* Sef turned the pronoun over. It fit and didn't fit, the way the name Emrys fit and didn't fit. Somewhere in the suspended weight beneath their ribs, something groaned again, ice shifting, holding.

"Iri," Sef said carefully. "Did I know you? Before?"

The girl was quiet for a long moment. Outside, another sheet of lake ice let go with a sound like a sigh the size of a country.

"You knew me best of anyone," she said finally. "But it's all right. Maren explained it. You spent what you knew to buy something important. That's what spending is *for*." She looked up, and her eyes were dry and very old and very young at once. "I'm not allowed to tell you what. She made me promise. She said if you learn it too soon, it'll hurt in the wrong shape, and hurts learned in the wrong shape don't ever sit right. You have to grow into it. Like shoes."

"That seems wise," Sef said, "and also infuriating."

"Maren says most wise things are."

* * *

After breakfast, Iri showed them the house.

It was small — kitchen, this bedroom, one other room — but every inch of it was arranged with intention, and walking through it was like reading a letter from a stranger who knew them perfectly. In the second room there was a desk under the window, facing the lake, and on the desk a writer's tray, pens, a stoppered bottle of good iron-gall ink, and stacks of paper ruled in a hand Sef recognized with a jolt: their own. Whoever they had been, they had been preparing for work. On the wall above the desk hung a single framed page, old and foxed at the corners, written in a flowing formal script that was *not* Sef's hand, headed with a title:

**Inventory of the Country of Serevan, Taken Spring of the Thaw.**

Below the title, in the same hand, a single line:

*Item the first: one country, formerly lost, now found. Condition: waking.*

Serevan. The word went into Sef like a splinter sliding home — painless on entry, and then a slow spreading warmth, an ache, a rightness, as though a bone long out of joint had been eased back with one gentle push. They did not remember Serevan. They had never, as far as their memory ran, heard the word. And yet their hand rose without instruction and touched the frame, the way you touch a doorpost of a house you grew up in.

"It was yours," Iri said from the doorway. "The idea of it. You wrote inventories for everyone who came through. What they brought, what they lost, what they hoped to find again. Hundreds of pages. Maren has them all in a chest downstairs. She says it's the truest history anyone's ever written, because you only ever wrote down what people said mattered." A pause. "You were going to write one for yourself, at the end. That frame was for it. You made the frame first." Another pause, longer. "You never got to write the page."

Sef stood before the empty inventory of their own life and felt the suspended year shift overhead, vast and patient, like a glacier deciding.

"Why not?" they asked.

Iri's answer was barely a breath. "Because you hadn't finished losing things yet."

* * *

In the afternoon, Sef walked down to the village.

They had meant only to walk, to put their body through the paces of the world and see what it remembered — and the body remembered more than the mind, which was its own quiet astonishment. Their feet knew the path down the rise. Their legs knew the rhythm of the shingle beach. When a woman struggling with a barrel of nails glanced up, Sef's hands were already reaching to help before any thought authorized the motion, and the woman said, "Bless you, scribe," with such easy familiarity that Sef understood they had done this a hundred times, in this exact spot, in a life now filed away in some archive they could not enter.

At the center of the village stood a long low building with a new door and an old sign, and above the sign, freshly painted letters: **THE RECORD HOUSE.** Inside, it smelled of paper and lamp oil and, faintly, of bread — Maren, Sef guessed, fed everyone who worked there, possibly everyone in the village, possibly on principle. Rows of tables. Stacks of books. And people — dozens of them, the tearful man from the doorway, the careful wood-stacker, strangers with Serevani vowels in their mouths — sitting at the tables and talking, while at each table a scribe wrote, and the talkers wept and laughed and corrected themselves, and the scribes wrote it all down.

A country being written back into the world, one testimony at a time.

Near the door, a broad-shouldered woman with grey in her braid and flour on her sleeves looked up from a ledger and saw Sef, and her whole face changed — a sunrise happening in miniature, hope and grief and welcome all at once — and Sef braced for the embrace of a friend they could not place.

But Maren Hartwell — the name arrived unbidden and correct — only took Sef's hands in hers, flour and all, and held them, and studied their face with the frankness of a woman who had buried enough to stop pretending.

"There you are," she said. "Or — there you mostly are." Her grip tightened. "Don't apologize. Everyone wants to apologize, and it makes the returning ones feel like broken goods. You're not broken. You're *spent*. There's a difference, and the difference is everything." She released one hand and reached into her apron pocket. "You left this for yourself. Instructions, of a kind. You said — give it to them on the third day, when the fog lifts enough to be insulted by a mystery."

It was a single ledger page, folded twice, soft at the creases from handling. Sef unfolded it.

The handwriting was their own. Not the handwriting they'd woken with — not quite — this hand was surer, rounder, a hand that had written ten thousand pages since whatever hand Sef last remembered using. The ink had faded unevenly, as if the page had been carried a long way, close against the body, through weather.

It read:

*You chose this. Be kind to the child.*

And below that, smaller, pressed hard enough to emboss the paper, as if the writer had needed the words to survive anything:

*If you can't remember why, trust the shape of what remains. You kept her name off every list. Ask yourself what that cost, and then ask yourself what it bought. The bells will tell you the rest. Go down to the water in the evening and listen.*

Sef read it three times. The words did not unlock anything. That was the maddening, merciful truth of them: they opened no doors, restored no year, returned no face. They simply *sat* in Sef's chest, warm as a coal, and refused to explain themselves.

*Her,* Sef thought. *Kept her name off every list.*

They looked up. Through the Record House's open door, down the lane, Iri was waiting by the fence at the top of the beach path — waiting the way you wait when you have waited many times before and expect to wait many times more, patient as a lighthouse, watching Sef with an expression carefully emptied of hope.

The lake boomed behind her. Another field of ice went under. And from somewhere out on the black open water — faint, impossible, unmistakable — came the sound of a bell.

One stroke. Deep and green and drowned and ringing.

Iri heard it too. Sef saw her hear it, saw her whole body turn toward the water like a compass needle finding a north it had been born pointing at. And when she looked back at Sef, the careful emptiness was gone from her face, and what replaced it was so nakedly hopeful that Sef's chest ached with a grief they owned no evidence for.

"Will you come down to the water?" Iri asked. "In the evening? It's better at evening. That's when they ring clearest." She hesitated, then added, in a smaller voice: "You used to say the bells were keeping count. Of everyone who came home. You said they wouldn't finish counting until—" She stopped herself, jaw tightening around the end of the sentence, and held out her hand instead. An offer. An introduction. A beginning.

"My name is Iri," she said again, formally now, as if for the first time — because, Sef understood with a vertiginous tenderness, for this version of them, it was. "Will you walk with me, Sef Oyelaran?"

The name fit differently today. Looser. Almost right. Like a coat being grown into.

Sef took the child's hand.

"Yes," they said, as the bells began, far out under the evening water, to count.
