## 39. The Child Who Waits

The first thing Iri learned about being ordinary was that mornings arrived whether or not you wanted them.

In the country that had waited, there had been no mornings at all — only dusk, patient and amber, holding its breath over the orchards for thirty years. In the vessel-years, before she had legs, she had been a warmth in someone's arms and a murmur of a thousand voices, none of them hers alone. Even in the year of running — the lake, the ice, the Door held open against the weight of winter — she had been *carrying*, and carrying had made her certain. Every step had a destination. Every memory in her chest had a name attached.

Now she was just a girl in a borrowed nightgown, waking in a small house above the old lake because her body had decided it was morning, and nobody had asked her opinion.

She lay still and took inventory.

This was a habit from the ledger days, though she'd never set foot in the Ledger Guild herself; she had absorbed it secondhand, from Sef's careful hands and Vesha's sharp tongue and the general atmosphere of a world that counted everything. Inventory: one body, nine years old by the count of her bones though she had lived — if *lived* was the word — for longer than the town below. One room with a sloped ceiling and a window that fogged at the corners. One quilt, patched, smelling faintly of cedar and woodsmoke. Outside, the sound of meltwater ticking off the eaves like a slow clock, and beneath it, far down, the bells.

The bells under the ice were not under the ice anymore. The lake had opened in spring, all at once, with a groan like a giant turning over in sleep, and the water had risen clear and cold and full of light, and the bells rang now the way bells are meant to ring — above the water, in a crooked wooden tower the Serevani had raised on the shore within the first month, because Maren said a town without bells was just a pile of houses pretending.

Iri listened to them and did her inventory of the other kind.

Her cargo had been forty-one children's worth of memories when she crossed the Door. She knew the number the way you know your own heartbeat. Now, in the third spring, she carried eleven.

It didn't hurt. That was the strange part, the part she could never explain to Maren or to Old Tomas or to any of the woken ones who looked at her with such tender, unbearable gratitude. It didn't hurt to empty. Each time she sat with one of the returned — in Maren's bakery, usually, because Maren's bakery had become the place where things were given back, flour dust hanging in the lamplight like a blessing — each time she took a sleeping child's face in her hands and breathed out, something left her. A warmth. A weight. A voice that had ridden in her chest since before she had a chest. And what remained behind was simply... more Iri. Less vessel. More girl.

She was growing into the space they left, like water filling a hole in sand.

Some nights this frightened her. Some nights she lay awake doing arithmetic she hated: *eleven left, then ten, then none, and then who am I?* If she was the sum of forty-one stolen childhoods, what was the remainder?

But other nights — most nights, lately — she thought about the door of the small house above the lake, and the person who slept behind it, and she thought: *remainder* might be another word for *beginning.*

* * *

Sef Oyelaran was in the garden when Iri came down, which was where Sef was always in the early morning, kneeling in the wet grass with ink-stained fingers in the dirt, wearing the expression of someone negotiating with vegetables.

"You're up early," Sef said, without looking around. Sef always knew. Iri had stopped wondering how. Some things survived the erasure of a decade; some instincts ran deeper than memory, down in the marrow where ledgers couldn't reach.

"The bells woke me."

"They wake everyone." Sef sat back on their heels and wiped their hands on their apron — an apron, Iri noted, that had once been a guild scribe's coat, cut down and re-sewn, the Ledger Guild's gray dye stubbornly refusing to wash fully out of the cuffs. "Maren's baking honey bread. I can smell it from here. She does it on purpose, you know. Weaponized hospitality."

"I know." Iri sat down on the step. The stone was cold through her skirt. "That's why I'm going."

Sef glanced at her then — that quick, sidelong glance, the one that always caught Iri off guard, because it was the glance of a stranger trying to solve a puzzle and a mother checking for a fever at the same time, and neither of those people existed in Sef's own understanding of themselves. "You've been going every morning for two weeks," Sef said carefully. "To the bakery. To sit with people. You come back looking..." Sef's hands made a vague shape in the air, a shape like something both emptied and filled. "Lighter. And heavier. Both at once."

"Do you want to know what I do there?"

Something flickered across Sef's face. Want — plain, naked want, hunger of exactly the kind the old Emrys had starved on in the guild's gray halls. But then the flicker folded itself away, the way it always did, tucked behind the polite architecture of a person who had learned — no, who had *chosen*, though they couldn't remember choosing — not to take what wasn't offered.

"If you want to tell me," Sef said. "I won't ask. Asking feels like taking, with you. I don't know why."

*I do,* Iri thought. *Because you paid a decade of yourself to buy my passage across the ice, and the part of you that knows why is the part you sold.* She had watched it happen. She had been the only witness, standing on the black ice while the enforcers' vials filled and the bargain sealed, watching Sef's eyes go distant and smooth over like a pond freezing top-down, watching ten years of a life drain out through the fingertips — including, though neither of them knew it yet, every hour of the year they had spent together. Every shared meal on the run. Every lesson in Serevani. Every night Iri had cried against Sef's shoulder and been held.

Sef had woken up a stranger beside a stranger, and had still — on instinct, on marrow-deep instinct — put their own coat around the child's shoulders before asking a single question.

Iri had decided, in that moment, that she would wait as long as it took. She was, after all, very good at waiting. Waiting was practically her native language.

"There's a woman in the bakery," Iri said instead, "named Odile. She was eight years old when the country paused. She got her memories back last month. She remembered her dog. She'd forgotten she ever had a dog, and then one morning she remembered, and she cried into the bread dough, and Maren let her, and now Odile is going to be a shepherd again someday, because that was what she wanted to be when she was eight." Iri pulled her knees up. "I gave her that. Not me exactly. I was just... the road it traveled on."

Sef was quiet for a long moment. The meltwater ticked. Down in the town, the bells finished their quarter-hour and the silence afterward felt like held breath.

"That's the most extraordinary thing anyone has ever told me," Sef said finally, "and I include everything in the guild's impossible inventory, and I only remember about half of why I was there." A pause. The ghost of the old dry humor, surfacing without knowing how deep its roots went. "You trust me with it. I don't know why you trust me with it. We met in — what, autumn? You appeared at my door half-frozen and called me by a name I've never used—"

"Sef."

"—and I have known you less than a year, and you tell me things you don't tell Maren, who has actually earned—" Sef stopped. Sef's hand had gone, unconsciously, to the pocket where the ledger page lived. The page Iri had written, in the weeks after the Door, in careful block letters, and slipped into Sef's coat when the forgetting was fresh and raw:

*You chose this. Be kind to the child.*

Sef read it sometimes. Iri had seen them reading it, late at night, by candlelight, lips moving. It answered nothing and explained everything, and Sef had never once demanded more than it said. That restraint was its own kind of memory, Iri thought. The old Emrys would have torn the world apart for answers. This new Sef simply kept the page close, like a compass whose needle pointed somewhere they'd agreed not to look.

"Anyway," Sef said, standing, brushing dirt from their knees. "Honey bread. Go. And Iri—" They hesitated in the doorway, backlit, ink-stained, ordinary, impossible. "Whatever it is you give away, every morning. Whoever you were before. I hope you know that whoever you're becoming seems like someone worth becoming."

Iri's throat went tight.

"Thank you," she managed.

And because beginnings should be walked toward rather than waited for, she stood up, and went down the hill toward the smell of honey bread and the sound of the bells, and did not look back — though she felt Sef watching her go, the way someone watches a letter they wrote but cannot remember sending.

* * *

Maren's bakery smelled the way Iri imagined forgiveness would smell, if forgiveness were baked at dawn: warm crust, dark honey, woodsmoke, the faint mineral tang of lake water carried in on everyone's boots. The woken ones drifted in and out all morning — there were nearly three hundred of them in the valley now, and more coming through the Door settlements every week, blinking thirty-year-old eyes at a sky that moved again — and Maren fed all of them and charged none of them and pretended this was sustainable, and everyone loved her for the pretense.

Odile was there, kneading. Her hands had remembered the work before her mind had; that happened sometimes, the body keeping faith when the self couldn't. She nodded to Iri, solemn as a fellow craftswoman.

"He's here," Maren said quietly, wiping her hands. "The one from yesterday. The ferryman's boy. He came at dawn with his mother."

Iri's chest tightened — the good tightening, the tightening of a door about to open.

The boy was sitting on the bench by the window, swinging legs that didn't reach the floor. He was maybe six. He had been six for thirty years, frozen mid-laugh in an orchard in southern Serevan, until last month, when Iri had sat with him in the settlement house and breathed out, and given him back to himself.

He looked up at her with eyes that were still learning how to focus on the present tense.

"You're the one," he said. His voice was rusty. All their voices were rusty at first. "Mama says you're the one who carried us."

"I carried some," Iri said. "Not all. There were others like me, once. I'm the last one walking."

"What happened to the others?"

"They became people," Iri said. "That's what happens. That's the whole point."

He considered this with the gravity of six years old plus thirty years of pause. Then he slid off the bench, crossed the floor, and hugged her around the middle, hard, the way children hug — total, unembarrassed, a whole-body verdict.

"Thank you," he said into her apron. "For the orchard. I thought I'd lost it."

"You didn't lose anything," Iri said, over the sudden blur in her eyes. "It was waiting. Everything was waiting. That's what our country does best."

When he let go, she felt it leave her — the eleventh. A small warmth, a bell-tone, a child's summer afternoon with the taste of green plums in it, passing out of her chest and settling home where it had always belonged. She swayed slightly. Maren's hand was instantly at her elbow, steadying, no questions asked, because Maren understood that resurrection was labor and laborers needed steadying.

Ten left, Iri thought. Ten.

And underneath the arithmetic, quieter, a different number entirely: *one*. One girl. Growing. Becoming.

* * *

She stayed at the bakery past noon, helping — she was learning to knead, badly, and Maren corrected her hands with the patience of someone who had raised dough and daughters through the end of the world. It was late afternoon when the stranger arrived.

He came in with the wind, tall, travel-worn, his coat crusted with the particular gray dust of the high passes — the passes beyond the lake, where the old world thinned into the territory the maps still refused to hold still. He had the look of the arrivals: Serevani features, but weathered by decades spent elsewhere, in the outer world, in exile they hadn't known was exile. One of the diaspora. The ones who had been outside the country when it was erased, who had spent thirty years feeling a grief they couldn't name, an ache shaped exactly like a homeland.

They came through the Door settlements too, these ones. And they came looking for Iri.

"You're the vessel," he said. Not unkindly. He said it the way people said *you're the lighthouse keeper* or *you're the midwife* — a function, a wonder, a job.

"I'm Iri," she said. "The vessel part is mostly retired."

"My name is Davan Ashkari." He sat heavily on the bench, and his hands shook, and Iri saw that he was holding himself together the way the woken ones did in their first weeks — deliberately, joint by joint. "I was a student in Vhalen when it happened. Thirty-one years old. I woke up one morning and the letters from my family had changed handwriting. My mother's village wasn't on any map. There was a hole in the world shaped like everyone I loved, and everyone told me I was mad, and eventually I believed them." He laughed, a cracked sound. "Then last winter the bells started ringing under a frozen lake in a valley no cartographer could find twice, and I walked four hundred miles, and here's the thing — here's the thing I need you to understand—"

He leaned forward. His eyes were wet and fierce.

"I remember them now. All of them. I woke up three days ago in the settlement and my sister's face was *there*, whole, thirty years older in my dreaming of her but *there*, and I never lost it, I understand that now — it was kept. Someone kept it for me. Someone carried it." His voice broke. "Did you carry mine?"

And Iri, who had done this arithmetic so many times, who knew the cargo by its weights and tones the way a musician knows chords, reached inward and searched — and found, in the tenth remaining warmth, in the deepest chamber, a young man's voice reading poetry aloud in a kitchen, and a girl laughing, and the smell of cardamom—

"Yes," she said. "Yours is still with me. It's one of the last."

Davan Ashkari put his face in his hands and wept in Maren's bakery, and Maren put honey bread in front of him without a word, and Iri sat beside him and let him weep, because she had learned that the giving-back was only half the work; the other half was witnessing what people did with what they got.

"When?" he whispered, when he could speak. "When can I have her back?"

"Soon," Iri said. "There's a place and a way. Maren knows. When it's time, I'll come find you."

Because she had decided — she and Maren and Old Tomas and the council of the woken — that the last ten would not be handed out like rations. They would be *returned*, properly, with ceremony, in the archive hall the Serevani were building on the shore, with the bells ringing and the whole valley gathered. A country that had been unwritten deserved to watch itself being written back.

But tonight, walking home up the hill in the blue dusk with the lamps of the town kindling below her and the bells ringing the hour, Iri let herself feel the other thing. The thing under the joy.

Ten left. After Davan's, nine. Then eight. And the girl she was becoming grew larger with every departure, and the vessel she had been grew smaller, and one day — soon, a season, maybe two — there would be no cargo at all. No voices but her own. No memories in her chest except the ones she'd made herself: honey bread, cold steps, a garden, a door, a person inside it who had chosen her without remembering why.

An ordinary girl. With an ordinary lifetime ahead of her, all of it unwritten.

She stood at the top of the hill and looked at the small house, its window lit gold, smoke rising from its chimney, and she practiced saying it to the evening air, quietly, the way you practice a word in a new language:

"Iri. My name is Iri. Just Iri."

It fit. It was starting to fit.

Inside, Sef had left a lamp burning for her, and — she saw as she came up the path — the front door was propped open with a stone, the way it had been every evening since midwinter. Never locked. Never closed. As if Sef's hands, which could not remember her, had insisted on leaving a light and an open door anyway, every single night, against all evidence and all reason.

Tomorrow, Iri thought, tomorrow she would go into town early, because Sef had taken the record-keeper's post at the settlement hall — the villagers had asked, almost shyly, whether the former guild scribe would write down their stories, their names, their testimonies, so that no one could ever again be unwritten — and tomorrow the first official entries would be made in the new ledger, the red one, the one bound in orchard-tanned leather that Maren had gifted.

And Iri had already decided what she would ask for. The first entry. Her name, written in Sef's careful hand, in a book that belonged to no guild and no king: *Iri. Arrived in this world by way of a country that waited. Resident. Age: nine, and counting.*

Beginnings without endings. That was what the woken ones called this life, when they talked among themselves in the bakery, in the low voices of people still learning to trust permanence. Nothing concluded, nothing closed, everything continuing.

She stepped through the open door, into the light, into the smell of ink and woodsmoke and the ordinary miracle of a home.

Down in the valley, the bells began to ring the evening hour — and beneath their familiar bronze voices, faint but unmistakable, rising from the direction of the lake, Iri heard a second peal answering. Deeper. Older. A bell that had no tower, tolling up through the water and the earth, a sound she knew in her bones, a sound she had heard only once before —

On the night the Door first opened.

The bells of Serevan were ringing from under the lake. And they were ringing a name.
