## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The Hollow woke the way it always did: slowly, and pretending it had never been asleep.

Ansel Fairweather stood at her window with her hands wrapped around a cup of barley tea gone cold, watching the fog lift off the terraced fields in long gray ribbons. Below, her neighbors were already moving through their morning — Tam Broadfoot beating dust from his rugs with the particular violence of a man performing contentment, the Alder sisters sweeping their shared step in unison, old Maren pulling weeds from a garden that had not produced anything worth eating in six years. Ordinary motions. Ordinary people. The valley hummed with the sound of lives being lived deliberately small.

That was the point of the Hollow. Everyone said so, though no one said it plainly. The valley forgot things. It forgot grievances and debts and the names of the dead, gently, like snow melting off a roof. People came here to lay down what they could no longer carry. Ansel had come here carrying more than most.

She set down the tea and went to the chest beneath her bed.

The lock was brass, green with age, and it opened for her the way it always did — reluctantly, with a click that sounded, if she let herself imagine such things, like a held breath released. Inside, wrapped in wool gone soft as ash, lay her mentor's sword.

It had no name. That was the first strange thing about it, the thing that had made her certain, all those years ago, that Brannic had been running from something when he came to the valley. Swords of any quality carried names the way rivers carried water; it was simply what they did. But this blade had been stripped of its name before Ansel ever touched it, scoured down to bare steel and silence, and when she wrapped her hands around the grip she felt nothing but the cold patience of metal.

*Nothing*, she corrected herself. She felt *nothing*. That was the discipline. That was the whole of it.

Because the truth — the truth she kept folded up smaller than the sword itself, buried deeper than the chest, hidden in the one place in the Hollow where forgetting could not reach — was that the sword was not silent at all.

Ansel knelt on the floorboards and drew the blade from its wrappings. Morning light came through the window and ran along the fuller like water finding a channel. It was a beautiful thing even now, even nameless: a hand-and-a-half sword with a grip of dark leather worn to the shape of a hand that had not been hers, that would never be hers again.

Brannic had died four winters past, quietly, in his sleep, the way everyone in the Hollow eventually agreed to die. He had left her the cottage, the garden, the chest, and one instruction, delivered in the flat voice he used only for the most important things:

*"Never listen to it. And never, never let them hear you speak."*

She had obeyed him for eleven years. Eleven years of tending a sword she never drew, oiling steel she never bared, keeping her gift folded away like a garment too fine and too dangerous for daily wear. The neighbors thought her a quiet woman who'd inherited a quiet life from a quiet old man, and they approved of her precisely because there was nothing to remember about her.

Ansel laid her palm flat against the flat of the blade.

Cold. Only cold. She counted her breaths the way Brannic had taught her — *in for the mountain, out for the river, in for the mountain* — until her heartbeat slowed and the valley's gentle numbness settled over her like silt settling in still water. This was the ritual. This was how she proved, every morning, that she was safe. That she was ordinary. That the thing inside her stayed sleeping.

But this morning, for the first time in eleven years, the sword was warm.

Not much. Not enough that anyone else would notice. But under her palm, along the steel, there was a faint heat like a stone pulled from a fire the night before, and Ansel snatched her hand back as if bitten.

"No," she said aloud, to the sword, to herself, to the room. "No."

The word fell into silence. The sword lay gleaming and mute and warm, and outside, Tam Broadfoot's rug-beating had stopped.

* * *

The proclamation came at noon, read from the back of a wagon by a man in crown-gray who had ridden up the only road into the valley with two soldiers behind him and dust on everything.

The whole village gathered in the square, because that was what villages did, and because not gathering would have been its own kind of statement. Ansel stood at the edge of the crowd, half-hidden behind Maren's broad shoulder, and watched the crier unroll a scroll sealed with wax the color of embers.

"Hear the will of the Crown," the man read, in a voice trained to carry without warmth. "By decree of the Regency Council, an accounting shall be made of all persons gifted in the old ways. Such persons are commanded to present themselves for service in the Ember Hunt, that their talents may serve the realm's renewal. Sheltering the gifted is treason. Concealment is treason. Failure to comply is treason."

He paused, and licked his lips, and read the part that mattered.

"The Hunt begins at season's turn. All valleys, all hollows, all places of hiding are hereby opened. There is nowhere the Crown's memory does not reach."

A murmur went through the crowd — not fear, exactly. The Hollow did not do fear well; fear required remembering why you were afraid. It was more a kind of collective blinking, the discomfort of people whose careful numbness had been prodded.

"Gifted," someone repeated. "What gifted? Ain't nobody here but farmers."

"Then there's nothing to worry about," the crier said pleasantly, and rolled up his scroll.

Ansel did not move. She watched the wagon rattle back down the road, watched the dust settle behind it, watched her neighbors shake their heads and drift back to their interrupted lives with the ease of people setting down a weight they were confident they'd never have to pick up again. By evening, half of them would have forgotten the proclamation entirely. By tomorrow, all of them. The valley would see to it.

*There is nowhere the Crown's memory does not reach.*

Her hands were shaking. She looked down at them, at the calluses from the hoe and the wood-splitter, hands that had done nothing remarkable for eleven years, and understood that the shaking was not fear. It was the feeling of a floor shifting underfoot — the floor she had built out of routine and silence and the daily practice of being no one.

They were coming to the Hollow. Of course they were coming to the Hollow. A valley that forgets was not a refuge; it was a larder. A place where the gifted went to be stored, numbed, easy to collect.

And she had spent eleven years making herself easy.

* * *

Night came down over the valley like a lid.

Ansel sat on the floor of her cottage with the chest open and the sword across her knees, and for the first time since Brannic died, she let herself stop counting breaths.

The warmth had not faded. If anything it had grown — a low, banked heat, patient as coals, and now that she stopped fighting it she could feel what lay underneath it, the way you can hear a voice under a door if you press your ear close enough. Not words. Not yet. But a shape of attention, a pressure like someone standing on the other side of a wall, waiting to see if she would knock.

"You're awake," she whispered. "Eleven years, and now you wake up. Why now?"

The heat pulsed once, almost like laughter. Almost like grief.

She thought of Brannic then, really thought of him, for the first time in years — not the smoothed-over version the valley had made of him, the harmless old man who mended fences and grew poor onions, but the real one: the way his eyes went to the horizon whenever riders passed on the far road, the way he'd flinched at the smell of smoke, the scar on his forearm he'd never explained and she'd never asked about. He hadn't come to the Hollow to rest. He'd come to hide her. Or hide himself. Or both.

*Never listen to it.*

"But they're coming anyway," she told him, to the dark, to the sword. "You hid me so well I hid myself. And it didn't matter. They've opened every hollow. There's nowhere—"

Her voice cracked, and she hated it, and she pressed her palm flat against the steel because she needed something solid, and the sword answered.

Warmth flooded up her arm — not burning, never burning, but *filling*, the way dawn fills a valley — and beneath her hand the silence broke. Not into words at first. Into a feeling: iron and rain, a road running north, a woman's laugh cut short, a door made of ash. Then, threading through it all, rough and familiar as an old boot on a stone step:

*Hello, little spark. You took your time.*

Ansel's breath left her in a sob she hadn't authorized. "Brannic?"

*Part of me,* said the voice in the steel, wry and tired and achingly known. *The part I couldn't bury. Listen fast, girl, because the valley's already chewing at this conversation, and I haven't got much—*

Outside, on the road below the cottage, a lantern swung.

Not the crier's wagon. Not a neighbor out late. A rider, alone, moving fast up the valley road in the dead hours, and slung at the saddle's side a cage of black iron with something inside it that glowed — faintly, desperately, like a coal refusing to go out.

The rider swayed. The horse stumbled. And in the silver wash of the moon, Ansel saw the rider's hands — bare against the reins, blistered and blackened at the fingertips, burned as if they had been holding fire for a very long time.

The sword pulsed warm against her knee, urgent now.

*Little spark,* Brannic's ghost-voice said, and there was something new in it, something that sounded terribly like alarm. *Open your door.*
