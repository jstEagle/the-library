## 2. A Knock at the Ashen Gate

The knock came at dusk, when the Hollow was already folding itself into sleep.

Ansel heard it through the floorboards — three soft strikes, more like a bird hitting glass than a fist on wood. She set down the whetstone she had been turning uselessly in her fingers and went still, the way she had learned to go still, the way the whole valley went still whenever something from outside pressed against its skin.

No one knocked in the Hollow. Neighbors called out names across fences. The miller's boy simply let himself in and stole honeycomb. A knock was a stranger's grammar, and strangers did not come to a valley that forgot.

She crossed the room past the hearth, where the coals had burned down to their red bones, and past the shelf where Maren's sword lay wrapped in oiled linen, sleeping the way it had slept for six years. She put her eye to the shutter's gap.

A girl stood on her step, swaying like a reed deciding whether to be a tree. She was small, soot-streaked, her hair cropped short and singed at the ends as though fire had tried to take it and she had argued it down to a truce. Her hands were bandaged in strips of dirty linen gone brown at the palms. And hanging from her shoulder by a strap was a lantern — no ordinary lantern, but one whose cage glowed from within with a light that moved like something breathing, amber and gold and hungry, throwing shadows that leaned the wrong way.

Ansel knew what an ember-lantern was. Every child in Vael had known, once, before Vael stopped being a country and became a rumor with borders. Fire-keepers carried them. Fire-keepers were dead, or should have been, or were hunted — and this girl wore the Hunt's attention like a second shadow, because Ansel could see the riders now, far down the lane where the valley road bent past the alder trees. Three of them. Torchlight. Moving slow, the way hunters move when they are certain.

The girl looked up. Through the gap in the shutter, her eyes found Ansel's — dark eyes, fever-bright, cracked lips shaping words Ansel couldn't hear but understood anyway.

*Please.*

Every rule Ansel had built her life upon said *turn away*. The Hollow kept its people safe by keeping them empty. That was the bargain the valley had struck with the crown a generation ago: we will not remember magic, and magic will not remember us. Maren had taught her that the safest sword is the one never drawn, the safest voice the one never raised. Six years Ansel had tended that silence like a grave.

The girl's knees buckled.

Ansel opened the door before she had decided to.

* * *

The girl collapsed across the threshold like something poured out. Ansel caught her under the arms — she weighed nothing, a bundle of sticks wrapped in a burnt cloak — and dragged her inside, kicking the door shut behind them with her heel. Outside, the torchlight swung along the lane, unhurried, patient.

"Water," the girl rasped, and then, absurdly, "don't let it see the dark."

"The what?"

But the girl had fainted, or folded into something near enough to fainting, her head lolling against Ansel's shoulder. Up close she smelled of woodsmoke and blood and underneath it something sweeter, like a candle just snuffed. Her bandaged hands had seared the linen brown straight through; the burns beneath must be terrible. The lantern sat between them on the floor, glowing, pulsing — and Ansel realized with a cold slide of understanding that it pulsed in time with the girl's breath. It was not a lantern holding fire. It was a lantern being held *by* fire, kept alive by whatever the girl still had left to give it.

And when the girl's breathing stuttered, the light guttered.

Down the lane, a horse snorted. Voices — two men, maybe three, low and bored the way cruelty gets when it has done a thing many times.

"Door's open," one said.

"Valley folk don't lock anything. Nothing here worth taking." A pause. "Nothing here worth *remembering*."

Boots on packed earth. The sound came closer, and Ansel felt the old fear rise in her throat, the fear she had swallowed every day for six years until it went down smooth as water: *they will find out what you are.* Not the girl. Her. Because the Ember Hunt did not only want fire-keepers. The proclamation nailed to the meetinghouse door three weeks ago had been very clear, written in the crown's tidy hand: *all gifted persons* — all who spoke to steel, all who heard what blades remember — *are to be taken for the Hunt.*

They would ask her questions. They would look at her hands, her eyes, the way she stood near the shelf. And if they grew suspicious they would do what hunters always did with the gifted: hold iron to her skin and wait to see if it whispered back.

Her eyes went to the shelf. To the linen-wrapped shape that had not been unwrapped since Maren died.

*Don't,* said six years of silence. *You swore.*

The boots stopped outside the door.

Ansel knelt, tore back the linen, and took up the sword.

It was heavier than memory. Maren's blade — plain steel, honest crossguard, a grip worn to the shape of one woman's hand and then, carefully, over years, to the shape of two. Ansel wrapped both hands around it and did the thing she had sworn never to do again in the Hollow, in Vael, in this life.

She listened.

For a moment there was nothing. Just cold metal and her own hammering heart and the boots on her step, and she thought, *it's gone, I've starved it, I've buried it so deep—*

Then the blade breathed.

Not sound, exactly. Swordspeech was never sound. It rose up through her palms like warmth returning to frozen fingers, like a voice remembered rather than heard, and it filled the hollow places the Hollow had carved in her:

*"Well,"* said Maren. *"Took you long enough, little ember."*

Ansel's vision blurred. Six years, and the voice was exact — dry, warm, sardonic at the edges, the voice that had corrected her grip ten thousand times and told her stories about dragons until she slept. "I'm sorry," she whispered aloud, stupidly, to a sword, to a dead woman, to herself. "I'm sorry, I didn't—"

*"Hush. Company."* And the blade shifted in her hands, subtle as a suggestion, angling itself toward the door. *"Behind you. Low stance. You've gotten rusty, girl, hiding out here in the fog."*

The door burst open.

He filled the frame — a hunter in the crown's grey-and-cinder livery, sword already drawn, face lit orange by his own torch and by the lantern's strange living glow. Behind him, the lane, the horses, the other torches swinging toward the house.

"Ember contraband," he said, eyes finding the lantern. Then, slower, finding Ansel kneeling with a bare sword in her hands, and something flickered across his face — doubt, the first crack of it. "And what's a valley mouse doing with a drawn blade?"

"Cleaning it," Ansel said. Her voice came out steadier than she had any right to expect. "It's my grandmother's."

"Grandmothers in the Hollow don't carry swords."

"This one did."

*"Tell him,"* Maren murmured through the steel, low and amused and deadly, *"that if he takes one more step, he'll learn what else grandmothers did."*

The hunter stepped.

What happened next, Ansel would spend many nights afterward trying to describe, and never quite manage. She did not decide to move; the sword decided with her, the way a word decides with a mouth. Maren's memories poured up her arms — footwork drilled into a younger Ansel at dawn, parries learned beside a river, the particular way Maren had once disarmed a tax-collector's thug outside a market in a country that no longer existed — and Ansel's body simply *remembered*, all of it at once, everything she had spent six years pretending to forget.

She caught his descending wrist with the flat, turned, swept his ankle. He went down hard across her threshold. Before he could draw breath to shout, the point of Maren's sword rested at the hollow of his throat, and Ansel heard herself say, in a voice that was half her own and half someone braver:

"You'll get up. You'll walk back down that lane. And you'll tell your captain there's nothing in this house but an old woman's rusted steel and a sick neighbor. Because if you come back—" she let the blade speak a fraction closer, let him feel the hum in it, the faint wrongness that made his eyes widen, made the superstitious animal in him flinch — "the sword will remember you. And I promise you don't want that."

The hunter stared up at her. Whatever he saw in her face — or whatever he *heard*, rising faint and cold from the steel — drained the certainty out of him. He scrambled backward off the step, sheathed his sword without being told, mounted, and rode, and his companions followed, casting looks at the little house with the open door as though it might grow teeth.

Silence fell back over the Hollow, thick as wool.

Ansel stood shaking in her doorway until the last torchlight vanished past the alders. Then she shut the door, dropped the bar, and slid down against it to the floor, Maren's sword across her knees, laughing once — a broken, astonished sound — before pressing her sleeve against her eyes.

*"There she is,"* the blade said softly. *"There's my girl."*

"I broke my oath."

*"You kept the better part of it. I said guard the gift until it's needed. It was needed."* A pause, and the warmth in the steel turned grave. *"Now listen. Your guest is burning up, and that lantern won't last the night without her, and she won't last the night without help. And Ansel — when she wakes, she'll have questions. So will you. The Hunt doesn't send three riders after a lantern. They sent them after what the lantern means. There's a Door in the old stories, little ember. Ash-colored. And I think that child knows where it sleeps."*

Ansel looked over at the girl crumpled on her hearthstones, the ember-lantern pulsing beside her like a second heartbeat, dimmer now than an hour ago. Faintly, in the depths of its glow, something shifted — a shape, vast and coiled, turning over in its sleep.

The girl stirred. Her cracked lips moved, and this time Ansel caught the words, barely a breath:

"...door of ash... have to reach..."

Her eyes opened — dark, fever-bright, fixed on Ansel with the desperate clarity of someone spending their last coin.

"You spoke to it," the girl whispered. "Your sword. I heard it. From the road, I *heard* it." She tried to push herself up on her ruined hands and failed, and laughed instead, a small wild sound. "Six years I've run, and the first person who ever answered me back is hiding in a valley that forgets."

Outside, far off, a horn sounded — long, low, and patient. Not three riders anymore. The Hunt, gathering.

Maren's voice rose quiet through the steel in Ansel's hands: *"Well, little ember. Best pack light."*
