## 3. What the Blade Remembers

Wren had always thought the Hollow would feel like drowning.

That was what the fire-keepers said, back in the capital that no longer admitted to its own name: the valley where magic went to die. They spoke of it in whispers, the way you speak of plague-grounds or the inside of a tomb. *Nothing follows you in,* they said. *Your gift thins like smoke. You arrive whole and leave hollow.* Wren had crossed the ashen gate with a stolen lantern burning against her chest and her hands still blistered from the last thing she'd ever carried for her order, and she had braced herself for silence — for the great muffled nothing of a place that ate wonder.

It did not feel like drowning. It felt like being *forgotten*, which was worse.

The lantern still burned. That was the first miracle, though she hadn't recognized it as one yet. Ember-fire was not supposed to survive past the valley's rim; it fed on memory the way ordinary flame fed on air, and the Hollow was a country of deliberate amnesia. The neighbors here — she'd seen them only at a distance, faces smooth and pleasant as river stones — had built their whole lives on the gentle lie that nothing had ever happened to them. No wars. No kings. No country called Vael, erased so thoroughly from the maps that even its ghosts had been evicted.

And yet the lantern burned. And yet the woman who had dragged Wren through her door in the dark hours before dawn — Ansel Fairweather, all sharp elbows and sharper silences — kept a sword wrapped in oilcloth above her hearth like a relic, or a wound.

Wren watched her now across the small room, in the gray light of a morning they could not afford. Ansel moved the way people move when they are trying not to wake something: softly, precisely, her eyes flicking again and again to the wrapped blade as if it might answer questions she hadn't asked aloud.

"You're staring," Ansel said, without turning.

"I'm wondering." Wren cradled her bandaged hands against her stomach. The burns had stopped weeping sometime in the night, thanks to a salve that smelled of comfrey and cold iron. "You knew what I was the moment you saw the lantern. You didn't flinch. People flinch at fire-keepers, even here. Even *especially* here."

"Fire gets people killed."

"So does whatever you're not telling me."

Ansel's jaw tightened. Outside, the valley lay green and quiet under a sky the color of unpolished pewter, so beautiful it looked apologetic. Somewhere beyond the ridgeline, Wren knew, the Ember Hunt was moving — crown riders in ash-grey cloaks, sweeping house to house, asking their soft terrible question: *Are you gifted? Have you carried? Have you heard?* She had run three days ahead of them. Three days was not enough. It would never be enough.

"They'll reach this valley by tomorrow," Wren said. "Maybe tonight, if the weather holds for them. I've seen what they do to keepers. They don't kill us, Ansel. That's the horror of it. They *hollow* us. There's a rite — I heard the priests call it the Quiet Mercy. They take the fire out of you and leave the shape of you behind, and you smile, and you thank them, and you spend the rest of your life watering someone else's garden."

Ansel turned then. Her eyes were the brown of turned earth, patient and buried. "You came here because you thought the Hollow would hide you."

"I came here because I had nowhere else." Wren's voice cracked on it, and she hated that. "The Door of Ash is a story. My order is ash itself. I stole the last ember my order kept, because I couldn't stand to watch them feed it to the Hunt's brazier like it was just — fuel. Like warmth is just fuel." She lifted the lantern, and its light pulsed, faintly, like a sleeping heartbeat. "This is Vael's last dragon-fire. Do you understand? Everything my people were, everything this country was before the crown decided it had never existed — it's all in here. And I am the only fool alive who cares."

For a long moment Ansel said nothing. Then she reached up, took down the oilcloth bundle, and laid it on the table between them.

"I'm going to do something I haven't done in six years," she said. "I'd like you not to scream."

* * *

They left through the back of the garden, past beehives humming with drowsy industry, up the sheep-track toward the valley's edge — because Ansel had listened to her sword, and her sword had told her things in a voice that made her go grey around the mouth. Wren walked behind her and watched, and what she witnessed on that track, in the thin gold light of midmorning, rearranged everything she believed about the world.

Because the sword *spoke*.

Not aloud — not at first. But Wren carried ember-fire, and ember-fire sees warmth the way owls see movement, and she could see the thread. It ran from Ansel's cupped hand into the blade like a hairline of dawn-light, and when Ansel walked, the sword walked too, hovering at her shoulder in its wrapping, and it *leaned* toward her. It leaned the way an old dog leans on the leg of the one person who never kicked it.

"What is it saying?" Wren whispered. She couldn't help herself. Her heart was slamming against her ribs like a bird in a shuttered room.

"That the Hunt passed through the low farms at moonset." Ansel's voice was flat, but her knuckles were white around the hilt. "That there were nine riders, and one of them carried a broken sword wrapped in black cloth, and the blade remembers her. Remembers—" She stopped. Swallowed. Started again, quieter. "It remembers her *oath*. She was sworn, once. Bound to a blade the way I'm bound to this one. A hunt-captain who used to be one of us."

The path narrowed along a shelf of rock above a stream gone silver with morning. Below, the valley spread out in its quilted green, chimneys smoking peaceably, and Wren thought: *they have no idea. They've forgotten so well they don't even remember to be afraid.*

"She has a name?" Wren asked.

"Sable." Ansel said the word like it tasted of rust. "My sword knew her sword. Blades talk to each other, apparently, when their bearers sleep. It says Sable's oath-sword broke the day she broke her oath — snapped at the guard, clean as a bone. She carries the pieces wrapped in black. She's led the Hunt for four years." Ansel's laugh had no humor in it at all. "Four years of hunting her own kind, and the blades still grieve her. Isn't that obscene? They *miss* her."

Wren stopped walking.

She had seen many kinds of magic in her short, burning life — the fire-keepers' coaxing of living flame, the priests' cold pageantry, the crown's hollowing rites that she had fled rather than undergo. But she had never seen anything like this: a woman walking through a forgotten valley with a dead mentor's memories whispering at her shoulder, carrying grief like a second shadow. It should have frightened her. Swordspeech was the rarest of the gifts, the one the crown feared most — a voice that could command every blade within earshot, waking the violence sleeping in steel.

Instead, Wren felt something crack open in her chest, warm and terrifying.

"It's beautiful," she said.

Ansel glanced back, wary. "What is?"

"You. What you can do." Wren's eyes stung, and she let them. "They told us swordspeakers were monsters. They told us your gift was just killing at a distance — that you could turn any army's weapons against them, that's why the Hunt wants you worst of all. But that's not what I see. I see you *listening*. Your sword isn't a weapon to you. It's — it's a grave you tend. It's a *person* you visit." She laughed, wet and disbelieving. "Do you know what I would give, to hear my ember speak? To know if it misses them too?"

The lantern at her chest flared — gently, like a sigh — and both of them stared at it.

"It's never done that before," Wren breathed.

"Maybe it's never been near anyone who could listen back." Ansel's mouth did something complicated, halfway between a frown and a smile. "Come on. The ford at Miller's Bend, my sword says. We cross there and cut north through the old orchards. If we're fast, we're over the ridge before—"

Hoofbeats.

Not many. Two, perhaps three, coming up the valley road below at a canter, and Wren felt the lantern shudder against her sternum like a startled animal. Ansel pulled her off the track into the bracken, down behind a fallen rowan furred with moss, and they crouched together in the green smell of crushed fern while the riders drew level with the bend below.

There were three of them, ash-cloaked, and at their head rode a woman whose posture made Wren's breath catch — straight-backed, economical, a rider who had become one piece with her horse. At her saddle hung something wrapped in black cloth, long and broken-shaped, and even from thirty yards Wren felt the lantern recoil from it. Not in fear. In *grief*.

The captain raised her hand. The riders halted. And her voice carried up the slope, clear and cold and tired all the way through:

"Fairweather lands. House to house. The proclamation stands — all gifted persons to be taken for the Ember Hunt. Anyone harboring them shares their sentence." A pause. "We're looking for a fire-keeper. Stolen goods. Singed hands. And—" something flickered across her face, quickly buried, "—reports of a swordspeaker. Unconfirmed."

One of the riders, young, eager: "Captain, if it's true, a swordspeaker in the Hollow after all these years—"

"If it's true," Captain Sable said, and her hand came to rest on the black-wrapped ruin at her saddle, almost without her noticing, the way a tongue returns to a missing tooth, "then she'll hear us coming long before we see her. Move."

They moved. The hoofbeats faded down the valley road, toward the smoke and the beehives and the smooth-faced neighbors who had already begun, Wren understood, to forget they had ever seen a stranger at Ansel's door.

In the bracken, neither of them breathed for a long time.

"She's close," Wren whispered finally. "She's *here*, Ansel. Not tomorrow. Today."

"I know." Ansel's hand was still wrapped around the sword-hilt, and Wren saw now that the dawn-thread between them had brightened — the blade straining toward the riders below, toward the broken thing in black cloth, like a voice calling a name across water. "My sword keeps saying her name. Over and over. *Sable. Sable.* Like it's trying to remind her of something she's made herself forget."

"Then we run."

"We run." Ansel rose, pulling Wren up with her, and for a moment they stood face to face among the ferns, two fugitives in a country that officially did not exist, bound by a stolen flame and a listening blade. Something steadied in Ansel's eyes that hadn't been there yesterday — reluctant, unwilling, but real. "But Wren — when she catches us, and she will catch us, because she was oath-bound once and she knows exactly how we think — I need to know you won't let them take the lantern."

Wren's hands closed over the ember-lantern, over the last warm heart of a murdered country.

"They'll have to burn me to get it," she said.

Below them, far down the valley, faint as memory, a horn sounded once — and the Hunt began to move.
