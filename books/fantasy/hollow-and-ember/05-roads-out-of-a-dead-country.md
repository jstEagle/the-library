## 5. Roads Out of a Dead Country

The heartland of Vael began where the Ashen Road crested the last hill and the valley let go of them all at once.

Ansel had never seen it before. No one in the Hollow had, not in living memory — the valley kept its people the way a cupped hand keeps water, and the world beyond was spoken of only in the flat voice reserved for things that did not matter. But she knew this country anyway. She knew it the way she knew the sword on her back: by its silences.

It should have been full. That was the wrongness of it, the thing that made her chest ache as they rode down through the morning mist. A country this green, this broad, with rivers braiding silver through fields gone soft and unclaimed — it should have been loud with living. Instead the keeps stood empty along the ridgelines, their banners long rotted to threads, their gates standing open like mouths that had finished speaking. Crows owned the watchtowers now. Ivy owned the walls. In one courtyard they passed, a well still stood with its bucket rope intact, as if the whole village had stepped away for a moment and simply never come back.

"They renamed everything," Sable said. She rode at the front, because Sable always rode at the front, her broken oath-sword slung across her back like an accusation she'd grown used to carrying. "Every town. Every river. The crown's cartographers went through with ink and brushes and wrote Vael out of existence. You can buy a map in the capital — a fine one, gilded at the edges — and there's nothing here. Just *the Northern Reaches, formerly unsettled.*" Her mouth twisted around the words. "*Formerly unsettled.* As if no one ever built a hearth here. As if the smoke never rose."

Wren rode between them with the ember-lantern hung from the saddle horn, and the little flame inside it leaned toward Ansel whenever she spoke, the way a child leans toward a familiar voice.

"It rose," Wren said quietly. "I've seen the smoke. In the lantern, sometimes, when I feed it — fire keeps what burned near it. Old hearth-smoke. Bread-smoke. The smell of a forge my grandmother's grandmother worked." She lifted the lantern so the light caught her face, singed hands steady on the handle. "Fire remembers warmth, Ansel. Everyone thinks it only remembers burning. But that's not true. Burning is just the last thing it was asked to do."

Ansel said nothing. She was learning that about Wren — that the girl's silences were not empty but full, packed tight like a banked coal, and that if you waited, warmth came out of them.

* * *

They made camp that night in the shell of a waystation inn, three stories of grey stone with its roof half fallen and its signpost blank where the crown's men had scraped the name away. Someone had loved this place once. Ansel could feel it in the sword at her hip even without speaking to it — the mentor's blade had gone quiet since the ford, quiet in a listening way, like an old man sitting by a window watching weather come.

She built the fire while Wren saw to the horse and Sable walked the perimeter with the grim thoroughness of a woman who expected the past to arrest her. When the flames caught, Wren crouched beside Ansel and opened the lantern, and the stolen ember breathed out toward the campfire like a bird recognizing its flock.

"Watch," Wren said.

Ansel watched. The two fires touched — just touched, flame to flame, a kiss of light — and the campfire changed. It didn't burn hotter. It burned *differently*. The smell shifted from wet wood and pine to something older, sweeter: woodsmoke over a harvest kitchen, apples drying on a line, tallow candles, bread. For one impossible moment the ruined common room around them filled with the ghost of itself — Ansel would have sworn she heard laughter, low and many-voiced, and the clink of mugs, and someone singing badly and being loved for it.

"What is that?" she whispered.

"The inn's fire," Wren said. "Or what it left behind. Every hearth that ever burned here poured a little of itself into the next one, and the next, down all the years, until the last one went cold. Fire passes memory along. It has to. It's the only way it survives being put out." She looked up at Ansel, and the light moved on her face like water. "That's why they hunt us, you know. Not because we're dangerous. Because we're *evidence*."

Ansel's throat tightened. She thought of the Hollow, where forgetting was a virtue and silence a sacrament, where her mentor had taught her to keep the swordspeech folded small and hidden, a letter never sent. She had thought hiding was safety. Sitting here in a dead country's ghost-kitchen, breathing its remembered bread-smoke, she understood for the first time that hiding was also a kind of burning — slow, private, and just as final.

"The blades do that too," she said. "Remember forward. My mentor told me once — every sword holds everyone who ever held it. It's why speaking to them is never quiet. There are so many voices."

"And you carry them alone." Wren said it simply, without pity, which was somehow worse and better at once. "That seems heavy."

"It is," Ansel admitted, and was startled to have said it aloud.

* * *

Sable came back at moonrise and sat across the fire without asking permission, which Ansel had learned was how Sable did everything. She fed the horse. She sharpened a knife she did not need to sharpen. Finally she pulled something from her coat and tossed it into Ansel's lap: a map, folded soft as cloth from much handling.

"I stole it from the Hunt's quartermaster before I defected. Official issue." Her voice was flat as slate. "Look at the heartland."

Ansel unfolded it. The parchment was beautiful, the lettering fine, the borders drawn in confident gold. And across the entire center of the country — the keeps, the rivers, the road they were sleeping in — the cartographer had written, in a clean bureaucratic hand:

*TERRA INCOGNITA. UNSETTLED. NO RECORD.*

"No record," Ansel repeated.

"Vael didn't fall," Sable said. "Vael was *filed away.* There was a war nobody won, and afterward the crown decided it was cheaper to pretend the country had never been than to admit it couldn't hold it. They burned the registries. They renamed the towns. They offered the people who stayed new names and new loyalties, and the ones who refused—" She stopped. Fed the fire instead of finishing.

"The Ember Hunt," Ansel said quietly.

"The Hunt started as a census," Sable said. "I know because I ran it. We were counting the gifted, cataloguing them, and then someone in the capital realized that a country full of people who could hear the old names in old steel might someday *say* those names. Might wake something. So the census became a list, and the list became a hunt, and I—" the knife stopped moving — "I told myself it was mercy. Relocation. Protection. I was very good at telling myself things."

Wren, curled on her bedroll with the lantern against her chest like a doll, said drowsily, "You're telling yourself something different now."

Sable looked at her a long moment. "Yes," she said. "Badly."

* * *

Three more days carried them deeper into the heartland, and the heartland carried them deeper into grief.

They passed villages renamed in fresh paint over old signs — *Hearthlow* become *Northmark Station*, *Emberford* become *Crossing Nine* — the crown's letters already peeling like scabs. They passed a shrine where someone had scratched, in desperate haste, *WE WERE HERE* into the stone, and beneath it, smaller: *remember us*. They passed a battlefield where the grass grew lush and wrong, fed on something underneath, and both horses refused to walk the straight path through it, and none of them suggested why.

And each night, Wren opened the lantern, and each night the fire gave back another piece of the country. A wedding feast. A funeral where the mourners sang until dawn because the dead woman had asked for joy. A midwife's kitchen, a schoolroom, a smithy where an apprentice burned his first blade and his master laughed and called him *sword-brother*. Ansel learned to sit close and listen with her whole body, and slowly, without deciding to, she began doing something she had never done: she spoke back to the fire. Small things. The sound of the Hollow's rain. Her mentor's terrible singing. The taste of the valley's blackberries, which grew nowhere else in the world.

"You're feeding it," Wren said the fourth night, wonder in her voice. "You're giving it memories to keep."

"Somebody should," Ansel said. "This country was filed away. Somebody should keep the file."

Wren smiled — the first full smile Ansel had seen from her, crooked and bright as a struck spark — and reached over and took her hand, singed fingers lacing through hers without ceremony, the way you take a thing that is already yours.

* * *

On the fifth day the road climbed toward a pass marked on Sable's stolen map with nothing at all — white space, gold border, *NO RECORD* — and Sable reined in suddenly, one fist raised.

Smoke ahead. Not hearth-smoke. Too much of it, too black, rising from beyond the ridge in a column that bent and streamed like a banner.

"That's not a village burning," Sable said, eyes narrowing. "Villages don't burn like that anymore. There's nothing left to burn." She turned in the saddle, and Ansel saw something in her face she had never seen there: fear, worn openly, like a wound uncovered. "That's a pyre. That's what the Hunt does with what it takes when the wagons are full and the orders say *dispose.*"

Ansel's hand found the hilt of her mentor's sword, and for the first time in days the blade stirred under her palm — not words yet, but attention, a held breath, the way a dog goes rigid before the storm breaks.

Far off, thin on the wind, came a sound that did not belong to any remembered feast or wedding song.

Drums. Many. Marching.

And beneath them, fainter, threaded through the smoke — a voice, human and terrified, crying out in a language the crown had spent thirty years trying to erase, and which Ansel realized with a jolt she could almost understand.
