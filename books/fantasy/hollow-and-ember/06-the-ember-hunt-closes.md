## 6. The Ember Hunt Closes

Sable had walked through a hundred markets in her years as hunt-captain, and she knew the smell of one built on bones.

It was in the way the ground sat wrong beneath the cobbles — too level, too tidy, the stones fitted by soldiers' hands rather than merchants'. It was in the way no birds landed. Crows circled the market square of Vael's Rest — *Ashford Crossing*, said the crown's new sign, paint still tacky from the renaming — but none of them touched down, wheeling instead like ash caught in an updraft that never ended. And it was in the way the vendors' voices carried: flat, careful, the hush of people who had learned that loud things got noticed and noticed things got taken.

Old battlefields made good foundations. The crown had learned that early. Dead ground held its shape, asked no questions, remembered nothing — or so they believed. Sable had signed the orders herself once, years ago, directing the paving of this very square over the field where the last loyal companies of Vael had broken against the Ember legions. She had stood on the fresh-laid stone and felt nothing, because feeling nothing was the whole of her office then.

Now she walked the same stone with a fire-keeper's stolen lantern hidden under her cloak and two fugitives at her shoulders, and she felt everything.

"Left," she murmured, not turning her head. "The tanner's row. There's a gap behind the drying sheds."

Ansel shifted beside her, close enough that their sleeves brushed. The swordspeaker had gone quiet in the way she went quiet when blades were near — which, here, was always. Every third stall in the market displayed steel: skinning knives, cleavers, old cavalry sabers polished up and sold to farmers who'd never seen cavalry. Sable watched Ansel's eyes track across them the way other people tracked weather. Reading pressure fronts. Listening for thunder.

"They're all whispering," Ansel said under her breath. "The whole square. Like a room full of people trying not to talk about the thing everyone's thinking."

"What are they thinking?"

"That something underneath them is awake." Ansel's hand drifted toward the hilt of her mentor's sword, wrapped in sacking at her hip. "Or dreaming. I can't tell the difference down here."

Wren bounced once on their heels, restless as a struck match. "I need oil," they said. "Lantern oil, wick-cord, and if anyone has resin pitch, I could kiss them. The ember's been eating through fuel since the ford, and if it gutters before we reach the Door—" They didn't finish. None of them liked finishing sentences about the Door. "There. Chandlery, corner stall. Two minutes."

"Wren—"

"Two minutes, Captain." Wren grinned at her, singed fingers already counting coins. "You worry like a woman who expects ambushes."

"I *am* a woman who plans ambushes," Sable said. "It's why I expect them."

But she let Wren go, because the chandlery was thirty paces away and visible the whole time, because Wren moved through crowds like smoke through fingers, and because some traitorous part of her wanted to believe, just once, that caution could rest for the length of a purchase.

She counted the exits instead. North gate: barred at dusk, two sentries. South: open, but funneled past the guardhouse. East: the tannery road, narrow, unwatched — hers, if she needed it. West—

West was where the hunters came from.

They didn't come shouting. That was the first wrongness. Her own training, her own doctrine: a market arrest began with noise, proclamation, panic, so the quarry broke cover and ran into prepared hands. But these hunters entered quietly, six of them in plain wool with swords slung loose and easy, spreading along the square's edges like water finding cracks. And at their center walked a woman in ember-grey with a badge of black iron at her throat.

Lieutenant Corvane. Sable had trained her. Sable had taught her that quiet entries meant the quarry was meant to be taken breathing.

*Meant to be taken breathing.* The words turned over in Sable's chest and came up cold. Not killed. Taken. Because the Ember Hunt had learned there were things you could do with a living fire-keeper, and none of them were merciful, and every one of them had been authorized by the same crown whose oath Sable still technically wore, the oath she'd burned and buried and told herself was ash.

Corvane's eyes swept the square and passed over Sable without recognition — then snapped back.

Of course. Six years, and the woman still knew her captain's stance.

Sable moved before thought finished forming. "Ansel. Tannery road. Now."

Ansel didn't ask. That was the thing Sable was still learning to survive — how fast these two trusted her, how little they weighed her word before obeying it. Ansel caught Wren's sleeve mid-purchase; the oil jar dropped; and the square understood, all at once, what it was holding.

The hunters drew.

And the weapons in the stalls drew with them.

Sable would remember it afterward as sound more than sight: a groan of leather and wood as forty blades strained against their ties, cleavers shivering on hooks, an old saber rattling in its scabbard like a dog hearing its name. Ansel stood in the center of the square with her hand outstretched and her voice pitched low, almost gentle, the way one speaks to frightened animals or grieving friends, and every piece of steel within earshot turned toward her like flowers toward sun.

"*Not yours,*" she said, and the words carried strangely, doubled, as though the blades repeated them in voices of their own. "*None of you belong to these hands. Remember whose you were.*"

A cleaver tore free of its hook and buried itself in a stall post. A hunter's sword wrenched sideways out of his grip and hung in the air, quivering, refusing him. He stared at his empty hand as if it had betrayed him — and perhaps it had; perhaps the betrayal ran deeper than flesh, back through the steel's long memory to whoever had forged it and whatever oaths had been folded into the metal when Vael was still a country with a name.

The crowd did not scream. That was the second wrongness. The crowd *backed away*, silent, practiced, faces carefully empty — people who had survived the Hunt before by being furniture. Only the crows finally landed, settling along the rooflines to watch, and their eyes were old field-eyes, carrion-patient.

"Captain Sable." Corvane's voice cut clean across the square. She hadn't drawn; her sword stayed sheathed, and her hunters, disarmed or nearly so, looked to her rather than to their empty hands. "The warrant names three. It will grieve me to add a fourth."

"It should," Sable said. "I wrote half the doctrines you're following. I know exactly how this ends for you if you take us."

"How does it end for you?"

Honest question. That was worst of all — Corvane asking it honestly, standing in the square Sable had paved over bones, waiting for an answer Sable had been avoiding since the ford, since the night she'd let three fugitives walk past her sword and called it hesitation.

Sable looked for Ansel and found her holding the square's steel at bay with visible effort, sweat cutting lines through the dust on her face, the mentor's sword half-drawn and singing faintly at her hip. She looked for Wren and found—

Nothing. The space where Wren should have been, between the chandlery and the tannery road, was empty, and the lantern-oil jar lay shattered in a spreading dark stain, and Corvane smiled the small smile of a commander whose real plan had never been the front door.

"The fire-keeper," Corvane said. "Came in through the west ten minutes ago. Quiet entry. Your doctrine, Captain. You taught us that the loud arrest is for amateurs." She lifted two fingers. Somewhere beyond the west eaves, boots answered. "The ember-lantern too. The crown is very interested in what burns inside it."

Fury arrived in Sable not as heat but as a terrible clarity, cold as well-water, the clarity she'd spent six years drowning. She saw the whole board at once: the quiet entry, the split force, the square itself chosen because its old battlefield soil muffled Ansel's reach — no, not muffled it, *fed* it, because the dead ground's blades remembered everything and that memory was the only reason Ansel wasn't already in irons. She saw that Corvane had planned around Sable's defection, had counted on it, had built the trap with her former captain's conscience as the load-bearing wall.

And she saw Wren — dragged between two hunters at the west edge, wrists bound with iron wire, head high, mouth still going, probably insulting someone's parentage — and understood that the choice she'd been deferring since the river ford had never been deferred at all. It had been made the moment she let them go. Everything since was just her catching up to herself.

"Sable." Ansel's voice, ragged, the square's steel trembling at the edge of her control. "Don't—"

"Take the east road," Sable said. "Both of you. That's an order."

"You're not my captain."

"No." Sable stepped forward, out from Ansel's cover, out from behind the family that had formed around her like scar tissue around a wound, and pulled her broken oath-sword from her back — the blade she'd snapped across her knee the night she burned her oath, the halves she'd carried ever after like a penance she couldn't finish. "I'm worse. I'm the one who built this."

She walked toward Corvane across the stones she'd laid over the dead, and the crows watched, and the freed blades in the stalls hummed low and attentive, listening, and Sable raised the broken sword so both halves showed plainly in the grey light.

"Lieutenant. You want your prisoners. Here is my offer: me, walking, unarmed, and the full confession of everything I did for the crown — every order I signed, every village I renamed, every grave I paved. In exchange, the fire-keeper walks free."

Corvane's eyes flicked to the broken blade, and something moved behind them — doubt, or its cousin. "That's not how warrants work, Captain."

"No," Sable agreed. "It's how oaths work. You wouldn't know. Yours is still intact."

Behind her, she heard Ansel cry out — heard the square's steel answer, dozens of voices at once, rising — and heard, above it all, Wren's voice from the west edge, clear and furious and utterly unafraid:

"*Sable, don't you dare—*"

Too late. Sable knelt on the bone-ground of Vael's Rest and laid her broken oath at Corvane's feet, and the surrender was the truest thing she'd done in six years, and it was a lie, and she prayed to every forgotten god of the country that officially did not exist that Corvane would not see the shape of the trap closing inside the trap — that no one would notice the freed blades drifting, patient as weather, into positions around the hunters; that no one would mark how Ansel, retreating east with the mentor's sword singing, had stopped retreating; that no one would look at the crows on the rooflines and understand they were not watching a capture.

They were waiting for a signal.

Corvane looked down at the broken sword. Then at Sable. Then, slowly, at the humming square.

"Captain," she said quietly, "what have you done?"

"What you should have done years ago," Sable said, and closed her fist.

* * *

Afterward there was dust and screaming steel and the strange mercy of blades that remembered better than men. Sable fought with half a sword and won anyway, because the square fought with her — because Ansel, from the east edge, spoke and spoke and the dead field beneath them rose in its long-delayed answer. When it was over, four hunters lay bound in their own belts, Corvane stood disarmed with her back to a stall post and fury like a wound across her face, and the west road stood open.

Open, and empty.

Because while the square had risen, two hunters had not waited for the outcome. They had simply done their duty — quiet, professional, Sable's own doctrine executed without sentiment — and carried Wren and the ember-lantern out through the west gate before the first cleaver left its hook.

Sable stood in the wreckage of the market with blood on her knuckles and her broken oath back in her hand, and the fury that came then was not cold at all. It was the oldest fire she knew, the one she'd banked and buried and named ash, and it lit her from the inside like a lamp.

"They'll take the coast road," she said. "Toward the Hunt's main camp. Interrogation by dawn." She was already moving, sheathing the broken blade, checking the bound hunters for keys and maps. "We can outrun them to the volcano pass if we leave now and don't sleep."

Ansel caught her arm. The swordspeaker's face was pale, streaked with sweat, but her eyes had gone somewhere Sable hadn't seen them go before — past fear, past grief, into a country beyond both.

"The blades told me something," Ansel said. "Before the gate. The ones from the old battlefield — they remember the way to the Hunt's camp. Every road the legions ever marched." Her grip tightened. "They're offering to lead us."

Sable looked at her — this woman who had spent a lifetime burying a gift, speaking now with the dead ground of Vael itself at her shoulder — and felt the last of her old loyalty burn away like fog off a morning field.

"Then we ride with ghosts," Sable said. "And by dawn, we take back our own."
