## 7. The Price of Silence

The hedge-witch's cottage sat where the road forgot itself, at the seam between a birch wood and a field gone to seed. Ansel saw it first as a smudge of smoke, then as a shape leaning against the wind like an old woman against a fence post — which, she realized as they drew closer, was exactly what it was. The cottage had been built around a living tree, its beams grown rather than cut, and the whole structure creaked and settled with the sound of someone muttering in their sleep.

Wren spotted it too. "Smoke," they said, hoarse. Their hands were still bandaged from the market — Sable had rewrapped them that morning with strips cut from her own cloak, working in silence, jaw tight. "Where there's smoke there's—"

"Don't say fire," Ansel said.

"I was going to say *soup*."

They had been three days out of the burning market, three days of ditches and hedgerows and Wren's absence like a wound reopened before it had finished closing. Sable walked ahead of them both, the way she always did now, one hand never far from the broken oath-sword at her hip. She hadn't spoken much since the camp. Since she had stood in front of a hundred hunters and told them what she'd done, what she'd been, what she'd burned — and watched the word *traitor* travel down their ranks like a lit fuse.

Now she stopped at the edge of the witch's yard and studied the crooked gate. "Hedge-witch," she said flatly. "Out here. This far into the dead country."

"You know her?"

"No." A pause. "I know the *type*. They sell small mercies to people who can't afford large ones." She looked back at Ansel, and something in her face was careful, the way a hand is careful around broken glass. "We need food. Bandages. Maybe a horse that isn't a rumor. We go in, we buy what we need, we leave."

But the witch was already in the doorway, and she was already looking at Ansel.

She was small and brown and wrinkled as a walnut, with eyes the color of strong tea, and she did not look at Sable's sword or Wren's bandages or the wanted-notices nailed to every tree for fifty miles. She looked at Ansel the way you look at a door you've been waiting behind for a long time.

"Come in," she said. "All of you. The kettle's on, and the road's been listening."

* * *

Inside, the cottage smelled of dried yarrow and woodsmoke and something older underneath, something mineral and cold, like the air off a deep well. Herbs hung from the rafters in bundles gone gray with dust. A kettle sang on a hook over the fire, and the fire itself was wrong somehow — too quiet, too steady, burning without any crackle at all, as though it had been charmed into good manners.

The witch — Maren, she said, just Maren — fed them barley soup with wild garlic and dark bread, and asked no questions, which was its own kind of question. Wren ate like someone who had learned not to trust the next meal. Sable ate the way she did everything: precisely, watching the door.

Ansel ate slowly, and felt the silence of the Hollow settle over her again, that old familiar hush she had carried out of the valley like a stone in her boot. Here it was almost restful. Almost safe.

"You're the swordspeaker," Maren said at last, not looking up from her mending.

Sable's spoon stopped.

"There's no notice on you yet," the witch went on, threading her needle. "Notices take time. But the Hunt has a way of describing its quarry to the hedge-folk. A girl from the Hollow. Hears voices in steel. They pay double for ones who can *speak*, because speaking is catching. You talk to a blade long enough, it starts talking back to its owner." She bit the thread. "Dangerous thing, memory. It spreads."

"We're not staying," Sable said.

"Didn't think you were." Maren set down the mending and looked up, tea-colored eyes settling on Ansel with terrible gentleness. "But I can offer you something better than leaving, child. I can offer you *arriving*. Somewhere the Hunt will never look, because there'll be nothing left of you to find."

The kettle sang. The fire burned its silent, mannered burn.

"What do you mean?" Ansel asked, though some cold part of her already knew.

"Hollowing," Maren said. "It's old craft. Older than the crown, older than the Hunt. Your valley knew it once — why do you think the Hollow forgets? Someone hollowed it, ages back, scooped the magic out like pulp from a fruit. I can do it gentler than that. Just the gift. Just the hearing. One night's work, a little pain going in, none coming out. You'd wake tomorrow and be nobody. Ordinary. Free." She smiled, and it was a kind smile, which made it worse. "The Hunt would ride past your door and see a farm girl. No notices. No hunters. No more running."

* * *

Later — after Wren had fallen asleep by the hearth mid-sentence, hands curled under their chin like a child's; after Sable had taken the doorway watch with her back to the room and her face to the dark — Maren led Ansel to the back of the cottage, where a low bed stood beneath the window, and a basin of water caught the moonlight.

"It doesn't hurt," the witch said. "That's what no one believes. Everyone thinks surrender should cost agony, so they refuse it on principle. But forgetting isn't pain, child. Forgetting is *quiet*. It's the quietest thing there is."

She set the basin down. In it, the water lay perfectly still, and Ansel saw her own reflection — thin, road-worn, a stranger's face lately, all sharp edges and sleeplessness.

"Think about it honestly," Maren said. "What has the gift ever given you? A dead woman's voice in a dead man's sword. Neighbors who'd have turned you in for bread. A life spent flinching every time someone sharpened a knife within a mile of you." Her voice went soft as ash. "You could put it down. Like setting down a weight you forgot you were carrying. You could wake up light."

And oh, the treachery of it — how easily Ansel imagined it. She stood there in the moonlight and let herself want it, really want it, for the first time. A cottage somewhere south, past the border, in a country that existed. A garden. Bread rising. Hands that didn't shake. A name no hunter knew. She would plant beans. She would learn the names of birds. She would never again feel a blade humming at the edge of her hearing like a fly against glass, never again carry other people's deaths in her skull like stones.

She would sleep through the night. She would sleep through *everything*.

"Yes," she heard herself say. Her voice sounded very far away. "Yes. Do it."

Maren nodded, unsurprised, and began unpacking her things: candles, chalk, a bone needle, a small dish of ash. And beside the bed, forgotten since the market, wrapped in oilcloth because Ansel couldn't bear to look at it and couldn't bear to leave it behind — her mentor's sword.

It woke the moment her fingers brushed the wrapping.

*Little wren of mine,* said Ysolde's voice, warm and dry as autumn, *what are you doing?*

Ansel snatched her hand back. "Nothing," she whispered. "Go back to sleep."

*You smell of ash and decision,* the blade said. *Bad combination. Sit down. Let me show you something.*

"I don't want—"

*Sit.*

And because it was Ysolde's voice, because it had been Ysolde's voice since Ansel was nine years old and first pressed her palm to the flat of the blade and heard it hum her name — she sat.

The sword showed her.

Not words this time. Memory is a current, and when a blade chooses to share it, you drown in it. Ansel sank through the steel and came up somewhere that had not happened yet — a morning, bright and ordinary, in a cottage with a garden. Beans climbing their strings. Birds whose names she knew, or would know, or had known. She was happy; she could feel it, a low sunlit contentment, the happiness of a person with nothing chasing them.

A knock at the door. She opened it.

A stranger stood there — young, freckled across the nose, with singed-looking hands and eyes the color of banked coals. The stranger smiled hopefully and said, "Ansel? It's me. It's Wren. From Vael. We crossed the dead country together, remember? The Door of Ash?"

And Ansel looked at this stranger with polite, empty kindness, and heard herself say:

"I'm sorry. I don't know anyone by that name."

The vision folded. Ansel came back to herself kneeling on the floorboards, gasping, the taste of that polite emptiness in her mouth like coin metal. Across the room, Wren slept on by the hearth, one bandaged hand open against the stones, trusting even in sleep, trusting *her* —

*There,* said Ysolde's voice, gentle and merciless. *That's the price. Not pain. That. Every morning of bean-planting and bird-naming, bought with that. You wouldn't even grieve them, little wren. Grief requires remembering what you lost.*

"They'd be safe," Ansel whispered. "If I'm nobody, they'd be—"

*Safe.* Yes. And alone. And hunted anyway, because Wren carries fire in their blood and Sable carries treason in her name, and neither of those can be hollowed out by any witch's needle. You would trade your memory for their capture. That's not safety, child. That's just choosing which of you gets to be lost.*

Ansel pressed her forehead to the floorboards. The moonlight lay in the basin like a blade of its own, silent and cold.

"I'm so tired," she said, to the sword, to the witch, to no one. "I'm so tired of being hunted for what I am."

*I know,* Ysolde said, and for once the voice was only a voice, old and grieving and fond. *But listen to me. The Hunt wants you to believe your gift is the cage. It isn't. The cage is believing you must be small enough to hide. You were never meant to fit inside the Hollow, little wren. You were meant to make it sing.*

Behind her, softly, Maren began gathering up her candles and chalk. "Changed your mind," the witch said. It wasn't quite a question.

Ansel rose. Her legs shook. She unwrapped the sword fully, at last, and buckled it to her hip where it belonged — where it had always belonged, if she was honest, since the day Ysolde died and left her voice behind in the steel.

"Keep your soup recipe," Ansel said. "And thank you for the offer. Truly." She managed something that was almost a smile. "But I'd rather be hunted whole than safe and missing."

Maren studied her for a long moment with those tea-colored eyes. Then she laughed — a dry rustle, like leaves — and pressed a cloth bundle into Ansel's hands: bread, cheese, a jar of yarrow salve. "For the road," she said. "And a warning, free of charge. The Hunt stopped asking travelers about you two days ago."

Ansel went cold. "What do you mean?"

"They stopped asking," Maren repeated, "because they found something better than asking. There's a dragon's roost under the old volcano, girl. Door of Ash, the old stories call it. And your captain's former second-in-command figured out where you're headed." The witch's smile faded into something grim. "They're not hunting you anymore, child. They're waiting for you at the Door."

* * *

Ansel woke Sable first. The hunt-captain listened without moving, and when Ansel finished, Sable looked out at the dark for a long time, and her hand closed slowly around the broken hilt of her oath-sword.

"Cael," she said quietly. "Of course it's Cael. He always was patient enough to wait at the end of the road instead of running down it." She turned, and in the dimness her eyes were ember-bright. "Then we stop running tonight. Get Wren. We ride for the volcano now — we reach the Door before he does, or we don't reach it at all."

Above the cottage, clouds were peeling away from the moon, and far to the east, beyond the black line of the hills, something glowed faintly against the sky — too steady to be fire, too red to be dawn.

The mountain was already awake.
