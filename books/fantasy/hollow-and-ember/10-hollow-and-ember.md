## 10. Hollow and Ember

**Ansel**

The Door of Ash did not open so much as *remember* itself.

One moment it was a slab of black glass set into the volcano's gutted heart, and the next it was a doorway full of morning — not the gray, forgetful light of the Hollow, but a gold that had weight and warmth and a smell like bread and rain on hot stone. Ansel felt it pass through her the way a bell's note passes through the body: Vael, coming back into the world's memory, moving through her ribs like a second heartbeat.

She had spoken the vow with every voice she carried. Her mentor's sword had sung last, its old steel finally quiet in her hands, its memories given freely into the fire rather than hoarded against forgetting. Around her, dozens of blades — the Hunt's own weapons, Sable's broken oath-sword, even the rusted farm-irons Wren had gathered from the battlefield market — had hummed one chord together, and the dragon-fire at the Door's threshold had drunk that shared remembering instead of Ansel alone.

Now the soldiers stood in the ash-field outside, and they were weeping.

Ansel watched them from the threshold. She could hear their swords still, faintly, laid down in rows on the ground like sleeping animals — not commanding anymore, just *conversing*, the way you talk to an old friend across a kitchen table. The blades were telling their bearers what they had burned. A granary in Corren's Ford. A bridge with someone's name carved into it. A village where a woman had once taught children to whistle through cupped hands, and the soldiers remembered her voice now, all of them at once, and could not stop crying.

"They're not dissolving," Wren said softly beside her. The fire-keeper's singed hands were wrapped around the ember-lantern, which burned low and content, its stolen flame finally home. "The Hunt, I mean. They're just... becoming people again."

"People are harder to command," Sable said. She stood a little apart, as she always did, her broken oath-sword cradled in both arms like a wounded bird. But her voice had lost its flint edge. "I know. I tried it for eleven years."

Ansel looked at the two of them — the keeper of fire and the breaker of oaths, both ash-streaked, both exhausted, both *here* — and felt something in her chest unclench that had been clenched since she was nine years old and first heard a blade whisper her name.

"I can still hear them," she said. It came out almost apologetic. "The swords. I thought maybe when the Door opened, when everything was shared, the gift would... go quiet."

"Do you want it to?" Wren asked.

Ansel considered. In the Hollow, silence had been safety. Silence had been her mentor's dying instruction: *never let them hear you speak to steel*. She had spent her whole life pressing her gift down flat, like a hand over a singing mouth.

But this morning, listening to the soldiers' swords murmur grief and relief in the ash-field, she realized the silence had never actually been safe. It had only been small.

"No," she said. "I don't think I do."

* * *

**Wren**

Wren had spent three years carrying an ember that everyone else wanted dead, and had learned to read fire the way farmers read weather. So when the Door finished opening and the last dragon-fire settled into the lantern like a tired bird into a nest, Wren knew what it was saying.

It was saying *enough*. Not dying. Resting. There was a difference, and Wren had nearly forgotten there was a difference, because for three years every night had been about keeping the flame alive and every day about running.

Now there was no more running. The thought made Wren dizzy.

They walked out through the Door together — the three of them, and behind them a straggle of former hunters who no longer wished to be called hunters, who asked in rough voices what Vael had been called before the crown renamed it, who touched the doorframe like pilgrims touching a relic. Beyond the threshold, the world had changed in small, enormous ways. Maps, somewhere far away, were rewriting themselves. Somewhere a cartographer was going to have a very bad week. Wren found this deeply funny and said so, twice.

The valley beyond the volcano was not the Hollow. That was the first thing Wren noticed. The Hollow forgot; this place *remembered*. Wildflowers grew in patterns too deliberate to be accident — old garden-beds gone feral, planted by hands two hundred years dead. A road ran under the grass, straight and sure, going somewhere on purpose. Even the wind sounded different here, carrying voices instead of erasing them.

"Vael," Ansel said, testing the word aloud like a stone she wasn't sure would hold her weight.

"Vael," Wren agreed.

Sable said nothing, but she walked with her shoulders back for the first time since Wren had known her, and that was answer enough.

They made camp that night in the shell of a waystation inn, its roof half-collapsed, its hearth cold for generations. Wren knelt before the hearth and opened the lantern, and the last dragon-fire flowed out onto the old stones and caught — really caught, wood and peat and something deeper, some coal of the building's own memory — and the room filled with light the color of homecoming.

Ansel sat close to the flames, holding her mentor's sword across her knees. Wren watched her lips move, very slightly, and knew she was speaking to it. Not commanding. Just talking. Telling it about the day.

"What does it say back?" Wren asked.

Ansel smiled. It transformed her face; Wren kept being surprised by how young she was underneath all that watchfulness.

"It says my mentor is proud of me. It says—" Ansel laughed, wet-eyed. "It says she always knew I'd talk too much eventually."

* * *

**Sable**

Sable did not sleep. She sat outside the ruined inn under a sky crowded with unfamiliar stars — stars that had names in Vael, she realized, names she had never been taught because the crown had erased them along with everything else — and she sharpened nothing, and broke nothing, and let herself feel the full weight of eleven years.

She had hunted her own kind. She had burned villages whose names she now remembered, thanks to Ansel's borrowed chorus of blades. She had told herself it was penance, staying in the Hunt, taking the worst assignments, keeping other hunters from the worst excesses — and it had been, partly, but mostly it had been cowardice wearing duty's coat.

And these two — a girl who spoke to steel and a boy with burnt hands — had walked into her life and refused, with maddening gentleness, to treat her as a monster. They had fed her. They had argued with her. Wren had once, memorably, thrown a shoe at her head during an argument about navigation, and Sable had caught it and, God help her, *laughed*.

Footsteps behind her. Ansel, wrapped in a blanket, lowering herself onto the waystone beside her without asking permission. Sable found she didn't mind.

"The soldiers voted," Ansel said. "Most of them are heading south, to the villages they burned. To rebuild. Some want forgiveness. Some just want work." A pause. "A few asked if you'd lead them."

Sable exhaled slowly. "I'm the last person—"

"You're the *first* person," Ansel said. "You're the only one who knows both sides of the wall. You burned your oath, Sable. That means you know exactly how much oaths cost. Who else should build the new ones?"

Sable stared out at the dark valley. Somewhere below, a nightbird sang a song she didn't recognize, and she understood that she would spend the rest of her life learning songs.

"I'll think about it," she said, which was, from her, a surrender complete enough that Ansel grinned and bumped her shoulder and went back inside to the fire.

Sable stayed a while longer. When she finally rose, she took her broken oath-sword from where it leaned against the wall, and instead of buckling it on, she carried it inside and laid it on the hearthstone, within reach of the flames and of Ansel, where any sword that still had words left to say could say them among friends.

Then she slept, dreamless, for the first time in eleven years.

* * *

**Ansel**

Spring came to the valley the way news travels in a small country: quickly, by word of mouth, with embellishments.

The waystation became a house again, then a village again, though it was weeks before anyone agreed on what to call it. Wren argued for the old name from the maps. Sable argued for something new, on principle. Ansel settled it by asking the hearth — because the hearthstones remembered too, if you listened right, and Wren had taught her that fire keeps warmth the way steel keeps blood — and the hearth gave back a name none of them expected: *Emberfall*, from before the crown, from before the Hollow, from a time when this valley had been a crossroads and a welcome.

So Emberfall it was. People came. First the former hunters, rebuilding what they'd burned, hauling timber with their swords reforged into hammers and plow-blades — Ansel had spoken each one into its new purpose, and the metal had seemed grateful. Then refugees from the renamed towns, then the simply curious, then families who had forgotten they had Vaeli blood until the Door opened and something in them woke up homesick for a country they'd never seen.

Ansel's gift stopped being a secret sometime in the second month, when a farmer's daughter brought her a rusted kitchen knife that had belonged to the girl's grandmother and begged her to make it speak. Ansel knelt in the mud of the yard, held the little knife, and listened — and told the girl about a woman who had sung while she cooked, badly, on purpose, to make the children laugh. The girl cried and laughed and carried the knife home like treasure, and after that there was always someone at Ansel's door with something metal in their hands and a question about the past.

She never hollowed herself. She never went back to the Hollow. She thought of it sometimes — the numb, safe valley of her childhood — the way you think of a coat you outgrew. It had kept her warm once. It had also kept her small.

On the evening of the year's first frost, the three of them sat around the hearth in what had become, without discussion, their shared house. Wren was teaching the neighbor children to feed the fire properly, palms open, *offering* rather than demanding. Sable was mending a saddle and pretending not to enjoy the children's chaos. And Ansel sat with her mentor's sword across her knees, listening to it hum its low, contented note, and understood at last what her whole buried life had been preparing her for.

Not power. Not safety. This. A room full of noise and warmth, a gift shared instead of hidden, a country breathing again.

"Sable," Wren said suddenly, poking the fire. "There's smoke on the north road. Travelers?"

Sable rose, crossed to the window, and went very still.

"Not travelers," she said quietly. "Banners. And they're flying colors I haven't seen since—" She turned, and her face held something between dread and disbelief. "Since before the Erasure. Someone's coming up the valley under the old royal standard of Vael. And they're carrying a crown."
