## 1. Small Charms

The rain came down on Copperlane like a tax — steady, unavoidable, and collected from everyone equally, which was to say it fell hardest on those who could least afford umbrellas. Ivo Almeida stood beneath the crooked awning of her stall and watched it hammer the cobbles, and thought, not for the first time, that three years ago she would have simply asked the sky to stop.

She did not ask anymore. Asking was how people ended up in the Inquisition's ledgers.

"Charm-seller," said a voice at her elbow, and Ivo's hand moved before her mind did — sliding the little tin box of *real* work beneath the counter's false bottom with the smoothness of long practice. What remained on top was legal merchandise: knotted red thread, dried lavender in muslin bags, copper coins polished bright and stamped with nonsense runes she'd invented herself. Harmless. Decorative. The magical equivalent of a pretty rock.

The charm-inspector who had spoken was young, wet to the shoulders, and bored in the way that only men with small authority and smaller imaginations could be bored. He picked up one of her lavender bags, sniffed it, and put it down as though disappointed it hadn't confessed to something.

"Lovely weather for it," Ivo said.

"Inspecting." He turned over a knot of red thread. "You're Almeida, yes? The one on Tallow Street?"

"I'm the one on Tallow Street."

"Someone reported charms of efficacy sold from this location." He said *efficacy* the way other men said *plague*. "Working charms. You understand that's a different offense entirely."

"I sell souvenirs," Ivo said. "Thread and flowers and shiny things for people who want to feel lucky. If feeling lucky were illegal, Inspector, you'd have to arrest half the city before breakfast and the other half by supper."

Something flickered at the corner of his mouth — not quite a smile, but a cousin of one. He wrote nothing in his little book, which Ivo took as a good sign, and lingered a moment longer than necessary over the copper coins, which she took as a bad one. Then the rain intensified its collection efforts, and he decided, visibly, that standing in a downpour to harass a woman selling lavender was above his pay.

"Mind yourself, Almeida," he said, retreating into the grey. "Reports come in waves. Like weather."

*Like everything,* Ivo thought. She waited until he'd turned the corner before letting out the breath she'd been holding, and then she let out a second one for good measure, because the first had been held since morning.

Three years. Three years since the gods went silent — all of them, everywhere, at once, like a door closing in an empty house. Three years since the country had passed the Edict of Quiet and made prayer a crime and magic a hanging matter, as though silence were the gods' idea rather than whatever it actually was. Three years since Ivo had stopped being a battle-priest of Vessia the Lanternkeeper, sworn shield of the Ninth Company, and started being a woman who sold knotted thread to people who needed luck more than they needed truth.

Her hands remembered the old work anyway. That was the cruelty of it. Her hands remembered how to braid a warding-sign into a soldier's sword-belt, how to press warmth into frostbitten fingers, how to hold a dying man's face and speak him across the dark water. Her hands were traitors. They kept reaching for prayers the way a tongue reaches for a tooth that isn't there anymore.

The rain thinned toward evening, as rain in Copperlane did when it grew tired of bullying the poor and moved on to richer districts. Ivo was beginning to pack up — folding the oilcloth, counting coins that would cover rent and bread and not much else — when the woman came.

Ivo saw her before she heard her: a small figure picking her way down Tallow Street with the particular gait of someone carrying something heavier than arms can hold. A shawl gone thin at the elbows. Shoes that had been mended so many times they were mostly repair. And eyes that went from stall to stall with the desperate arithmetic of a mother pricing hope against rent.

She stopped at Ivo's stall because Ivo's stall was last, and last stalls get the customers no one else wanted.

"You're the charm-woman," the woman said. Not accusing. Hoping.

"I'm the souvenir-woman," Ivo said gently. "Thread and lavender. Nothing that works."

"That's what they all say." The woman leaned close, and her voice dropped to the frequency of secrets. "But Marta down the well-lane bought a knot off you in autumn, and her fever broke that night. And old Fenwick says his cough cleared after he wore your copper piece a week. People talk, charm-woman. People talk even when it's dangerous to."

People talked. That was the problem with doing good work — even small, guilty, half-ashamed good work. Ivo had told herself, three years ago, that she would never work another charm. Then winter came, and a neighbor's child was burning up, and Ivo had lain awake listening to the coughing through the wall until she'd gotten up and done something about it, and told herself *never again*, and meant it, and broken it eleven times since.

Each time she told herself the same lie: *this is the last one.* Each time the lie got thinner.

"My son," the woman said, and here her voice cracked like river ice. "He's seven. There's something wrong with his breathing. The physician wants two crowns just to look at him, and I've got—" she opened her hand, and in it lay four coppers and a button. "I've got this."

Ivo looked at the button. It was brass, worn nearly smooth, shaped like a flower. Someone had loved it once, hard enough to rub the detail away.

"What's your name?" Ivo asked.

"Bree. Bree Halloway."

"And the boy?"

"Tomas." Saying the name seemed to cost her something and give her something back at once. "Tomas. He laughs even when he can't breathe, if you can believe that. Little fool laughs anyway."

Ivo looked at the four coppers. She looked at the button. She looked down Tallow Street both ways, at the emptying stalls and the lamplighter making his rounds and the general absence of inspectors, and she felt the familiar sick lurch of the trap she'd built for herself out of mercy and fear, one plank at a time.

"Give me the button," she said.

Bree's face did something complicated — hope and terror wrestling for the same ground. "It's not worth anything—"

"It's worth exactly what I need it to be worth. That's how charms work. They're not magic, mostly. They're attention. Somebody has to pay attention to a thing, properly, completely, and then the thing remembers it matters." This was, technically, a lie wrapped around a truth wrapped around a lie, which was the only kind of theology permitted in Copperlane these days. "Wait here."

She stepped into the back of the stall — a lean-to of salvaged planks that served as home, workshop, and hiding place — and closed the curtain. In the dark, alone, Ivo let her hands do what her hands wanted.

This was the part she hated. Not the risk — she'd grown strangely comfortable with risk, the way you grow comfortable with a limp. What she hated was the *smallness* of it. Once, her prayers had held shut a breach in the world at Marrow Ford while nine hundred soldiers retreated behind her. Once, Vessia's light had come down her arm like a poured thing, and she had raised it overhead and called it dawn and men had wept. Now she crouched in a shed the size of a coffin, whispering over a button, checking the street twice, flinching at footsteps.

*Vessia,* she thought — the way you think at a grave. *If you can hear me. If there's anything left where you used to be.* Nothing answered. Nothing ever answered. The silence had a texture now; she knew it like a room. She worked inside it the way a deaf woman works inside quiet — carefully, by memory, touching each step twice.

She braided the button into a knot of red thread, three turns widdershins, and spoke over it the old words for breath and opening, the ones the Lanternkeeper's order used for drowning men and asthmatic children alike, because the principle was the same: *make a way where there is no way.* She didn't ask Vessia to power it. She couldn't. She poured in the only currency she had left, which was herself — a sliver of her own warmth, her own luck, her own tomorrow, skimmed off the top like cream. It was why she always looked tired. Everyone assumed it was the hours. Nobody guessed she was quietly spending pieces of her own future to keep strangers' children breathing.

It was a bad bargain. She kept making it.

"There," she said, coming back out, pressing the knotted button into Bree's palm and closing the woman's fingers over it. "Red thread, three turns. He wears it at his throat, under his shirt, and nobody takes it off — not for washing, not for anything. And you don't tell anyone where it came from. Not Marta, not Fenwick, not a priest, not an inspector, not your own reflection. Can you do that?"

"Yes," Bree whispered.

"Bree." Ivo held her eyes. "Say it back to me like you mean it, because if they come asking, I sold you a souvenir and you're too poor to know better."

"I bought a souvenir," Bree said, meaning it so hard it was almost true. "From the souvenir-woman."

"Good girl." Ivo pushed the four coppers back across the counter. "Keep those. You'll need them for the physician's look, even with the charm. The charm buys him nights. The physician buys him years. Use both."

Bree stared at the coins. "Why would you—"

"Because I overcharged you for the button," Ivo said, "and my conscience is a nagging old cow. Go home. Go on. Before I come to my senses and start charging properly."

Bree laughed — a wet, cracked laugh — and gripped Ivo's hand with both of hers, hard, once, and went. Ivo watched her go down Tallow Street, small and quick against the lamplight, carrying a button and a borrowed piece of Ivo's tomorrow, and felt the usual aftermath settle over her: the exhaustion, certainly, but underneath it something worse. Something that had teeth.

Guilt. Not for breaking the law — the law was a coward's invention and she'd stopped respecting it somewhere around the second child. Guilt for the *pretending*. For standing in front of desperate people and saying *nothing works, nothing works* in a voice gone smooth with practice, while her hands itched with everything that worked. Survival had made her a liar in a city that rewarded lying, and the worst of it, the part that woke her at 3 a.m. staring at the ceiling, was that she'd gotten *good* at it. Good at being small. Careful. Dishonest about what she'd been.

There had been a time when Ivo Almeida's name was spoken aloud in barracks and temples, when she'd stood in armor at Vessia's altar and the light had known her like a dog knows its person. Now her name lived in a false-bottomed tin box and a ledger somewhere with her old unit's heresy record, and the only prayers she said were muttered over buttons in the dark, addressed to a god who had stopped answering along with every other god, three years ago, all at once, like a door closing—

Ivo froze, mid-fold of the oilcloth.

Like a door closing. She'd never thought of it that way before. Silence didn't fall. Silence *closed*. And doors, she thought, doors that close can open. Doors swing both ways. Somewhere, maybe, on the far side of that terrible quiet, something was still standing at the hinge.

She was still standing there, lamp guttering, coin-count forgotten, chasing the thought like a spark up a fuse, when the screaming started.

Not nearby. Two streets over, maybe three — a man's voice, raw and rising, and beneath it something that made the hair on Ivo's arms stand up and take notice: a crowd gathering fast, and running feet, and someone shouting a word she hadn't heard shouted in three years.

*"Answered."*

Someone was shouting *answered*. Someone was saying that a prayer had been *answered*. Here. In Copperlane. Tonight.

Ivo snuffed her lamp with two fingers and stood very still in the dark of her stall, heart slamming, listening to the city change shape around her.
