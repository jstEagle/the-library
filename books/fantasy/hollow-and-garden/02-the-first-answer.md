## 2. The First Answer

Ivo told herself she was going out to make sure the crowd wasn't coming for *her*.

It was a good lie. It had the shape of sense. A charm-seller whose stall sat three streets from a miracle had every reason to check which way the wind was blowing — inspectors would be out by morning, and if the Inquisition came to Copperlane they'd come through the market first, kicking over trays, asking who'd sold what to whom. She needed to know. That was all.

She took the back ways anyway, hood up, because a good lie deserves supporting details.

The crowd had gathered in the mouth of Tanner's Row, where the street widened into a ragged little square around a dry fountain that hadn't held water since before the Silence. Torches. Dozens of them, and faces jumping in the light, and above it all a sound Ivo hadn't heard in three years and had never once stopped hearing in her sleep: people praying *out loud*, together, without shame.

At the center, on the fountain's rim, sat a woman holding a child.

Ivo worked her way along the wall of a shuttered dyehouse until she could see properly. The child was small, maybe four, and gray as old tallow — fever, by the slackness of him, the bad kind, the kind that emptied houses in Copperlane every wet season. The mother was saying something over and over. Not a formal prayer. Not one of the old liturgies with their careful architecture. Just a name, and *please*, and the name again.

And the child was breathing.

That was the thing that made Ivo's stomach drop. Not light, not voices, not any theater of divinity — just a small chest rising and falling, easy and even, color coming back into the boy's face like ink soaking into cloth, while his mother laughed and sobbed and couldn't do either one properly.

"He asked me to tell you," the mother kept saying, to no one, to everyone. "He said — he said *tell them I'm still here*. He said—"

Ivo stopped listening.

Because she knew that phrasing. She knew it the way you know a voice through a wall after years apart — not the words, but the *shape* of them, the particular gentleness, the way her god had always spoken as though leaning close, as though there were time, as though nothing in the world were more interesting than the person being spoken to.

Vess. The Quiet Hand. The god of small mercies, of mended fences and found keys and fevers broken at dawn. Her god. The one who had gone silent mid-sentence three years ago, on a cold morning in a pass in the Grayreach mountains, while she knelt in the snow with blood freezing in her boot seams and waited for an answer that never came.

*I'm still here.*

"Get away from the fountain," someone shouted, "the inspectors are coming," and the crowd broke like a dropped plate, and Ivo broke with it, moving with the flow, heart hammering, telling herself the whole way home that it meant nothing. A fever breaking. A hysterical mother. Coincidence wearing a crown, the way coincidence always did when crowds were watching.

But gods didn't say *tell them*. Gods didn't lean close. And Vess had never once, in all the years Ivo had served — the years of oaths and small miracles and the long slow silence afterward — spoken so much as a word to anyone but her.

***

She didn't sleep. She sat in the back of her stall with the shutters drawn and a knife across her knees, and she did arithmetic.

If it was real, it was catastrophe. Three years of law built on the Silence — the Charm Acts, the inspections, the pyres, quiet ones, in the courtyard behind the Hall of Records where they didn't let you watch. Every prayer answered was a crime committed. Every god awake was a country's worth of warrants waiting to be written. If Vess was awake, then Vess was answering prayers in public squares, and the Inquisition would be here within days, and they would pull this district apart thread by thread looking for anyone with the old training, the old marks, the old oaths.

Anyone like her.

And underneath the terror, shameless as a cat at a fishmonger's, sat the other thing. The wanting. Three years of praying into a well and hearing nothing, and now — *now* — when speaking aloud could kill her, he was back? Now, when she had spent the silence learning to be small? Where had he been when she'd knelt in the snow? Where had he been when Dain—

She put that door shut. Firmly. Some doors you don't open at night, alone, with a knife on your knees.

Toward morning, exhausted past caution, she did the stupidest thing she'd done in three years.

There was a beggar who slept in the lee of her stall most nights. Old Tomas, or maybe not Tomas, names changed hands in Copperlane like counterfeit coin. He'd coughed all evening, that deep wet rattle, and tonight he was quiet in the wrong way — the stillness of a man whose body has started closing up shop. Ivo crouched beside him with her lamp turned low and put two fingers against his throat, and what she felt there was thin and skipping, a bird's heartbeat run too fast.

He opened his eyes. Knew her, vaguely, the way you know weather.

"You're the charm woman," he rasped. "Your luck charms don't work."

"No," Ivo said. "They don't." She said it lightly, and it went through her like a blade, because once her charms had worked, once she had been *good*, once Vess had poured through her hands like water through open fingers and she had mended bones in ditches and cooled burns with a touch and never once thought to be grateful for it, because it had simply been true, the way rain is true.

His lips moved. No sound. His hand twitched toward hers.

And Ivo — tired, frightened, three years hollow — heard herself pray.

Not the old liturgy. Not the careful architecture. Just: *Please. You're back, I think, I felt you tonight, I know I felt you — please, he's nobody, he's exactly the kind of nobody you always loved, please, one small mercy, one, I'll ask nothing else, I'll never ask again—*

Her voice cracked on the last of it, and she hated herself for the crack, and she bowed her head over the beggar's hand and waited for the silence the way you wait for a blow.

"*Ivo.*"

The world stopped.

Not figuratively. The lamp flame stood still. The drip from the eaves hung in the air like a bead of glass. Somewhere a dog mid-bark became a statue of itself. And in that held breath of a moment, warm and close and unmistakable, leaning near the way he always had:

"*Oh, my dear one. I am so sorry. I am so sorry it took so long.*"

Then everything lurched forward — the drip fell, the dog finished its bark, the flame swayed — and Tomas breathed. One breath, deep and easy, then another, and the rattle in his chest was gone, and his face, in the low lamplight, had smoothed into ordinary sleeping peace.

Ivo sat down hard on the cobblestones.

Her hands were shaking. She looked at them as though they belonged to a stranger, these hands that had just done — that had just *been used* — and she pressed them flat against the stones to stop the shaking and found she was laughing, or crying, or both, silently, helplessly, in the dark behind her own stall.

Three years. Three years of silence, and the first thing he'd said to her was her name, and the second thing was an apology, and she wanted to scream at him and she wanted to throw her arms around the empty air and neither of those things was possible because he was already gone, withdrawn, the connection closing like water over a stone. But the warmth lingered. It soaked into her like sun into a wall. Like being remembered.

*He's awake,* she thought. *He's awake, he answered me, he answered in front of—*

In front of no one. That was the only mercy in it. A dead-dying beggar and a dark street and her.

Which meant, said the cold practical voice that had kept her alive for three years, that nobody had seen. Nobody had heard. Nobody had written her name anywhere.

Except.

Except that a god does not wake in secret. Whatever bargain or prison or long payment had held the gods silent, it had broken tonight — broken publicly, in Tanner's Row, under forty torches. The Inquisition would come. They always came. And when they came they would not be looking for a feverish child's mother; they would be looking for the *trained*. For anyone who could hear clearly enough to be answered. For anyone whose prayer had the old grammar in it.

For anyone whose name was already in a heresy ledger somewhere, filed under a mountain pass, under a unit that died, under a betrayal Ivo had never stopped tasting.

She got up. She banked her lamp. She looked down at Tomas, sleeping the sleep of the genuinely healed, and she whispered, because she couldn't help it, because some habits are deeper than fear:

"Thank you."

Nothing answered. Of course nothing answered. But the air felt *attended*, somehow, the way a room feels when someone has just left it — and that, more than anything, more than the terror or the joy or the shaking hands, was what finally made Ivo Almeida start packing her goods into crates in the dark, moving fast and quiet, planning routes out of a city she'd sworn she'd die in.

Because somewhere out there, an Inquisitor was going to open a book.

And somewhere in that book, in an old record, in faded ink, was her name.
