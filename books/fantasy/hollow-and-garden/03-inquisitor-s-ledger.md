## 3. Inquisitor's Ledger

The Inquisition's archive smelled of dust, tallow, and the particular despair of clerks who had been promised a new roof for eleven years. Sera Vane liked it. She liked the way the ledgers sat in their shelves like sleeping animals, patient and heavy, holding everything the city preferred to forget.

She also liked that at this hour — the bell before dawn, when even the devout were still rubbing their eyes — she could work without an audience.

"Vane." The archivist, Brother Odo, materialized beside her with the unerring instinct of a man who could smell someone about to make more work for him. He was seventy if he was a day, bald as an egg, and wore his spectacles pushed up on his forehead like a second pair of eyes that had given up on seeing anything worth seeing. "You're early."

"You're later," Sera said. "You're always here before me. It's unsettling to find you absent."

"I'm not absent. I'm strategically delayed." He squinted at the stack of papers under her arm. "And you've brought work. I can tell by the way you're standing. You stand like a woman carrying bad news."

"I carry a request form."

"Worse."

She set it on his desk anyway: a requisition for every record of reported answered prayer in the capital, going back ninety days. Odo read it twice, which for Odo constituted open weeping.

"Ninety days," he said. "Do you know how many prayers get reported in this city in ninety days?"

"None," Sera said. "That's rather the point. Prayers aren't supposed to be *answered* anymore. That's the whole arrangement. So when someone files a report claiming one was —"

"They're usually drunk."

"Usually," she agreed. "Which is why I want all of them. The drunks establish the baseline. If there's one report in the pile that isn't a drunk or a fraud, I'll know it by its shape."

Odo looked at her over his spectacles — the lower pair, the working ones — with something like fondness. He'd known her since she was nineteen and still flinched at loud noises. "You know what your problem is, Vane? You actually believe the rules are real."

"That's my job."

"No. Your job is to enforce them. Believing they're real is extra, and it's the kind of extra that gets people like us buried in paperwork until we die of it." He sighed, hauled himself up, and shuffled toward the deep stacks. "Ninety days. You'll have it by evening. Try not to find anything interesting."

"I never do," Sera said, and both of them knew it was a lie, and both of them let it stand, because that was the kind of friendship theirs was.

* * *

The reports began arriving at midday, carried up from the archive in armloads by two novices who looked as though they'd been told the ledgers might bite. Sera cleared her desk, sent her junior inquisitor — young Pell, earnest as a spaniel — out to walk the Lowmarket circuit, and began.

Most of it was exactly what she expected. A fishwife swore the Saint of Tides had calmed a storm for her personally; investigation revealed the storm had been mild and the fishwife owed money to several people who might benefit from her piety being noticed. A child claimed the Lantern God lit his candle back after it went out; the mother, questioned gently, admitted the child was frightened of the dark and the mother was frightened of something worse, and had told him so. Fraud, grief, wishfulness. The ordinary sediment of a city that had spent three years learning to live without miracles and hadn't quite finished grieving them.

Sera read each one anyway. All of them. She read the fraudulent ones carefully, because a lie told well enough became a panic, and panics killed people — usually the wrong people, usually quickly, and always messily. That was why the law existed. Not because gods were evil. Because people, given a miracle, would do anything at all to keep it, and the last time this country had learned that lesson, the lesson had cost forty thousand lives and a war whose name nobody used anymore.

People thought the Inquisition hated the gods. Sera had heard it said to her face, spat in taverns, whispered behind her back in the cathedral square. Let them think it. The truth was worse and simpler: the gods had been *right*, once, to go quiet. Whatever bargain had been struck — and the upper ranks of the Inquisition knew more than they told her, she was sure of it — the silence had held the country together. Every prayer that went unanswered was a small mercy. Every miracle was a crack in the dam.

So she read, and sorted, and found nothing, and was almost content.

Almost, because there was one report she kept returning to. Filed four days ago by a watch-sergeant on the Saltgate road. A beggar, dying of lung-rot in the mouth of an alley off Cooper's Row. A passerby — unnamed, described only as a woman, tall, weathered, "the sort who looks like she's carried things" — had knelt over him and prayed aloud. And the beggar, who by the sergeant's own medical assessment should have died within the hour, had instead died within the minute: suddenly, cleanly, without pain, sitting up straight with his eyes open and a look on his face the sergeant described, in careful watchman's handwriting, as *"like a man hearing his name called from a house he thought was empty."*

It wasn't a healing. It wasn't a miracle anyone would pay for. But the sergeant had written one more line, and Sera had read it perhaps thirty times:

*"The woman stopped praying mid-word. Like something answered her before she finished asking."*

Prayers weren't supposed to be interrupted by replies. That was the entire architecture of the age. You spoke into the silence; the silence was the point; the silence was the price paid on everyone's behalf, and you honored it by not pretending otherwise.

Sera set the report down, rubbed her eyes, and did the thing she did when she didn't want to do the thing she needed to do: she made tea. There was a small iron kettle in the corner of her office, older than her commission, and she went through the ritual of it slowly — water, leaves, the three minutes she allowed herself to stand at the window and watch the city wake below. Smoke rising from ten thousand chimneys. Bells. Carts. Children already shouting in the lanes. A country that did not know it might be a country with a crack running through it.

Then she sat back down, pulled the old heresy index toward her, and looked up Cooper's Row.

Not the address itself — addresses meant nothing, prayers happened everywhere. But the prayer. The woman had prayed aloud, and the sergeant, bless his thorough heart, had recorded the words as best he'd heard them through a wall of rain. *"Old one. Old friend. If any part of you is left where he can reach it — take him gently. Take him like you took us."*

*"Like you took us."*

Us. A soldier's phrasing. A soldier's god, too — the old hearth-and-march liturgy, the kind mercenary companies kept when no temple would have them. Sera had read enough confiscated field-prayerbooks to know the cadence. She turned to the index of recorded heresies, military section, and ran her finger down the columns.

There were more names than she liked. War made believers of people, and belief made cases, and the years after the Silence had been full of soldiers who couldn't accept that their gods had stopped answering and started looking for reasons, and then for scapegoats, and then, in a few ugly instances, for blood. The Inquisition had handled those years badly in places. Sera had read those files too, all of them, and kept them where she could see them, because a law that forgot its own failures was just a weapon with good manners.

Her finger stopped.

**ALMEIDA, I. — Charmwright (unlicensed). Former oath-sworn, Company of the Verdant Standard. Charged: unsanctioned invocation, possession of consecrated materials, refusal to recant. Sentence commuted. Status: released to surveillance. Cross-ref: Company of the Verdant Standard — see Betrayal Inquiry 7/11.**

Betrayal Inquiry 7/11. Sera knew that file. Everyone in the Inquisition above a certain rank knew that file; it was one of the ones that got read aloud to new inquisitors as a cautionary tale, though the caution in it was never quite clear. A mercenary company, twelve years ago, sold out to a rival banner for gold. Ambushed at a river crossing. Nine dead. One survivor who turned crown's evidence against her own employer — and whose testimony had, in the end, been used mostly to bury the whole affair quietly, because the man she testified against had friends in high places, and justice, like everything else, had a budget.

The survivor's name was in the file. Sera remembered it without looking.

Almeida. Ivo Almeida.

She sat very still for a moment, tea cooling forgotten at her elbow, and felt the shape of the thing assemble itself the way she always felt it — not as suspicion, which was loud, but as pattern, which was quiet. A soldier's prayer, in a soldier's cadence, spoken over a dying stranger with no profit in it. An answer that came *before the asking ended*. A woman with nine dead comrades behind her and three silent years in front of her, who had knelt in the rain and asked an empty house if anyone was home.

And something had said yes.

Sera did not believe in omens. She believed in ledgers. But she wrote the name at the top of a fresh page in her own hand, small and precise — *Ivo Almeida* — and beneath it, the date of the report, and beneath that, the only question that mattered:

*If the gods are answering again, who hears it first?*

The answer, she suspected, was whoever had been listening hardest all along. People who had spent three years talking into the silence didn't stop talking just because the silence didn't reply. They kept praying the way sailors keep watching the horizon. And if the horizon finally moved —

A knock. Pell's head appeared around the door, cheeks pink from the cold, clutching his notebook. "Ma'am? Nothing in the Lowmarket, same as usual. Except—" he hesitated, the way he did when he wasn't sure a thing was worth an inquisitor's time, which was, in Sera's opinion, his single best quality. "Charm-stall on Tanner's Lane got inspected yesterday. Routine. Inspector noted the charmwright there — sells luck-knots, little things, nothing serious — but he wrote that her charms *work*. Consistently. Above chance. He flagged it as probably forgery, skillful hand, recommended a follow-up visit."

Sera looked at the name on her page. Then at Pell.

"Tanner's Lane," she said. "What's the charmwright's name?"

Pell checked his notes. "Doesn't say. Stall's registered to a broker. But the inspector wrote a description — said she was tall, weathered, looked like—"

"—like she'd carried things," Sera finished quietly.

Pell blinked. "Yes, ma'am. How'd you know?"

"Get your coat," Sera said, standing, and reaching for the ledger with the fresh page in it. "We're going to buy a luck-charm."
