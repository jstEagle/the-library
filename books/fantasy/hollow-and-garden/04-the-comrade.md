## 4. The Comrade

The trouble with selling charms in a city that had outlawed charms, Ivo reflected, was that you had to be charming twice — once to the customer and once to the inspector who might be standing behind the customer.

She was mid-way through the second charm when Dain Corvess walked into her stall like a bad memory with good boots.

He hadn't changed much. Three years had put silver at his temples where there used to be only mud, and he'd filled out the way men did when they'd been eating regularly instead of marching, but he still stood the same way: weight on the balls of his feet, hands loose, a man perpetually ready to apologize or swing a fist, whichever came first. He wore a ferryman's coat, unremarkable, well-mended. He'd always been good at looking harmless. It was practically a discipline with him.

Ivo kept her eyes on the charm in her hands — a knotted cord, nothing in it, no magic at all, just knots and the *idea* of luck, which was honestly what most of her stock amounted to these days — and finished tying it off for the widow Marta, whose son was shipping out on a grain barge and whose nerves needed something to hold.

"There," she said. "Hold it when the water's rough. It won't do anything. But neither will worrying, and this is cheaper."

Marta laughed, paid her three coppers, and shuffled off toward the harbor, clutching the cord like it was already working.

Which left Ivo alone in the stall with Dain Corvess and eleven years of unfinished business.

"You look terrible," he said.

"You look *alive*," she said. "Funny how that works out."

* * *

She closed the stall early. Not because of him — she told herself it was because the light was going and the inspectors got twitchy at dusk — but her hands were shaking as she folded the awning, and Dain watched her do it with that awful patient expression he used to wear while waiting for her to admit she was lost.

They ended up in the back room of a tea house that smelled of wet wool and cardamom, because Dain insisted on buying, and Ivo discovered she was petty enough to let him pay for the privilege of being hated.

"Say it," he said, once they were seated. "Whatever you've been saving up. Say it now, so we can get past it."

"Get past it." She turned her cup slowly on its saucer. "Eleven years ago you sold our position to the Inquisition for a pardon and a pension. Six people died in that farmhouse. Brannic died *slowly*, Dain. And you want to get past it over tea."

"I want to talk about the Hollow Gate."

"And there it is." She laughed, and it came out ugly. "You know, I wondered how long the pleasantries would last."

"Ivo—"

"No. You don't get to say my name like we're friends." She leaned forward. "Do you know what I do now? I sell knots to frightened women. Knots, Dain. Because if I sold anything real, the inspectors would burn me in Candler's Square, and my god—" her voice caught, and she hated it, hated him for hearing it, "—my god wouldn't even notice. That's what your pardon bought *me*. A life so small I could fit it in a matchbox. So whatever you're here to beg for, the answer is no, and I want you to hear it in my voice, which is steady, unlike yours is going to be."

Dain took a breath. Then another. She watched him assemble himself the way he used to assemble a shield wall — piece by piece, bracing for impact.

"The gods are awake," he said quietly.

The tea house noise seemed to recede, like water pulling back from a shore.

"You felt it too," he said. "Don't lie to me. I saw your face when the news came through the market — everyone thought it was the fever, but I know you, Ivo. I knew you before you learned to hide."

"I don't know what you're talking about."

"A beggar died on Tanner's Row two nights ago. Half the street heard him stop coughing and start *singing*. The Inquisition burned three charm-sellers yesterday. Not for charms. For *praying wrong*." He leaned in. "And there are rumors — real ones, from people who deal in real things — that the borders are starting to go soft. Roads that loop. Villages missing from maps. The Hollow Gate is the last door out, and it seals within the month, and I have coin, and papers, and a route, and not one soul alive willing to travel it with me."

"Because you're you."

"Because I'm *useful*," he said, "and useful is the only thing anyone's ever wanted from either of us. Don't pretend otherwise. You were the best hedge-priest in the company and you spent it all keeping soldiers alive, and when the gods went quiet, what did the world hand you? A stall. Knots." His voice cracked, just slightly, and she was furious to find it moved her. "We were made for war, Ivo. Both of us. And now the whole country's coming apart and there's one door out, and I am asking — I am *begging* — you to walk through it with me. Not for forgiveness. For survival. Yours and mine."

* * *

She walked. Not away — she wasn't ready to give him the satisfaction — but around. Through the evening market, past the shrine of Saint Ovel where someone had left fresh bread for a god who might finally eat it, down along the canal where the lanterns doubled themselves in the black water.

The arithmetic was the worst part.

She turned it over as she walked, the way she used to turn a whetstone over a blade, and every face of it cut.

Fact: she needed out. The prayer on Tanner's Row had been answered by *her* god — she'd felt it go through her like a bell struck inside her ribs — and the Inquisition would trace it eventually, because they traced everything, and her name was already in an old heresy record gathering dust somewhere, waiting for a clerk with time and a grudge.

Fact: Dain had money, papers, and knowledge of the roads that she lacked. Eleven years of living small meant eleven years of not learning how to flee a country.

Fact: he had betrayed her, and six people were dead, and Brannic had died calling for his mother in a burning farmhouse while Dain rode free.

Fact: none of those dead people could get her through the Hollow Gate.

"That's the arithmetic," she said aloud, to the canal, to the doubled lanterns. "Isn't it. The dead weigh nothing on the scale when you're the one standing on it."

A bargeman gave her a wide berth. Fair enough. She probably looked like exactly what she was: a woman discovering that survival and decency were different currencies, and she was short on one.

Here was the thing she couldn't say to Dain, couldn't even properly say to herself: some small, shameful part of her had missed him. Not the betrayal — never that — but the *before*. The jokes on night watch. The way he'd split his rations without being asked. The way he'd once carried her four miles on his back with an arrow in her thigh, singing badly to keep her conscious, and she'd believed — she had actually believed — that he would die before he let anything happen to any of them.

People were not one thing. That was the cruelest lesson of her whole small life. People were farmhouses burning *and* arrows carried on backs, and you didn't get to keep one and return the other.

* * *

She found him where she'd left him, hunched over his untouched tea like a man waiting for a verdict.

"One condition," she said.

His head came up so fast she almost laughed.

"We don't speak of the farmhouse. Ever. Not on the road, not at the gate, not after. You don't explain, you don't justify, you don't ask me to understand. You carry it. That's your toll, and you pay it every day until one of us dies, and then you pay it alone."

"Ivo—"

"Those are the terms. Take them or leave them."

For a moment he looked like he might argue — she could see the apology assembling itself behind his teeth, the whole speech he'd clearly rehearsed on the road, *I had no choice, they had my sister, I was going to warn you* — and she watched him swallow it. Watched him choose, visibly, to take the weight instead of setting it down.

"Terms accepted," he said hoarsely.

"Good." She sat down across from him and pulled his tea toward herself, because it was cold anyway and she was spiteful. "Now. Papers, routes, coin. All of it, laid out. And Dain?"

He looked up.

"If you betray me again, I won't kill you. I'll leave you. In a country that's coming apart at the seams, with gods waking up angry and Inquisitors burning anyone who remembers how to pray. There are worse things than dying, and you taught me most of them."

Something flickered across his face — hurt, maybe, or relief, or the strange gratitude of a man who has finally been given a punishment he can plan around.

"Understood," he said.

He spread his maps on the table, and his hands were steady, and outside the window the first stars were coming out over a city that didn't yet know it was dying, and Ivo Almeida began — carefully, bitterly, with her eyes open — to plan her escape with the man she despised most in the world.

Neither of them noticed the woman in the grey coat at the corner table, who had stopped pretending to read her book some minutes ago, and who was writing their names in a small black ledger.
