## 6. Road Through Ash

The road out of Corvane was a rumor by morning.

Ivo had walked it a hundred times in her soldiering years — the King's Mile, they'd called it, straight and proud as a drawn blade, running east through orchard country to the river towns. Now she stood at the city's eastern gate at dawn and the road bent. Not curved, the way roads curve around hills. *Bent*, like a finger pushed out of true, angling north where no road had ever gone.

"That's not right," Dain said, stating it with the confidence of a man who had once been paid to notice things being not right.

"No," Ivo agreed. "It isn't."

She touched the milestone at the roadside. It read *Corvane — 0*. The letters were fresh, as if cut yesterday, though the stone itself was old enough to have moss growing over its own inscription. She had passed this stone two days ago coming into the city, and it had said something else entirely. She couldn't remember what. That was the part that made her palms sweat: not that the world was changing, but that she kept *agreeing* with it, retroactively, her memory smoothing each wrongness flat before she could get a good look at it.

"Keep count of things," she told Dain. "Small things. How many fingers. What color the sky is. Whether you've ever been married."

"Have I?"

"I don't know. That's rather my point."

They walked. Behind them, Corvane smoked gently from three separate fires, none of which anyone had started on purpose. Ahead, the orchards were wrong in a quieter way — the trees all leaning slightly east, like an audience straining to hear something. By midday the apples on them were green on one side and ripe on the other, and when Ivo picked one and bit it, it tasted first of summer and then, halfway through the mouthful, of nothing at all.

"Eat fast," she advised. "While it still remembers being food."

Dain laughed — a short, startled bark, as if the laugh had escaped custody. He'd been doing that all morning: laughing, then looking ashamed of it. Three years of guilt sat on him like a pack he'd forgotten how to set down. He was broader than she remembered, grayer, with a new scar through one eyebrow that he clearly wanted asked about and would never ask about himself.

"You could say it," Ivo offered, somewhere past the second mile marker, which read *Corvane — 4* and then, twenty paces later, *Corvane — 9*.

"Say what."

"Whatever you've been chewing on since the temple yard. You keep opening your mouth like a fish at feeding time."

He was quiet for a dozen steps. "I'm sorry," he said. "I know it's worth nothing. I knew where they'd strike and I told them anyway, because they had my sister's name on a list, and I—" His jaw worked. "I did the arithmetic. One unit for one sister. I've done it every day since, and every day it comes out different."

Ivo looked at the road ahead, which had begun, very subtly, to loop — the same crooked fence post appearing on their left for the third time.

"The arithmetic's fine," she said at last. "It's the accountant I don't trust." She tossed him the apple core. "Walk faster. This road is trying to remember where it goes, and I'd rather be off it before it changes its mind."

* * *

Inquisitor Sera Vane read warrants the way other people read scripture: closely, and with an eye for what was missing.

The one on her desk named Ivo Almeida — apostate, unlicensed charm-seller, person of interest in the matter of the Answered Prayer at Tanner's Row. Sera had written it herself, which meant she had also written the parts that weren't there. No charge of consorting with the divine. Not yet. Because the truth was that forty-one prayers had been answered in Corvane in six days, and the Inquisition's own clerks had verified nine of them, and Sera Vane had spent two sleepless nights reading testimonies from weeping mothers and healed beggars and one extremely embarrassed baker whose sourdough had briefly achieved levitation.

The law said gods were dangerous. The law had been written in blood, after the Godswar, after the year the sky over Vellmark caught fire because two rival temples prayed loudly enough. Sera believed the law. She had built her whole self on it, the way a house is built on stone.

But a house notices when the stone starts humming.

"Your warrant lacks a charge of heresy," said Undersecretary Pell, who had come to stand in her office doorway wearing the expression of a man delivering bad news to someone he suspected already knew it.

"It lacks evidence of heresy."

"She spoke with a god. Audibly. In front of witnesses."

"So did the woman on Candle Street whose fever broke mid-prayer. Shall I arrest the whole city?" Sera capped her inkwell. "Pell. Forty-one answered prayers. If the gods are awake, then either the law is wrong, or the world has changed under it. I intend to find out which before I start hanging people."

"And if it's both?"

Sera looked at the warrant, at the name written in her own careful hand. *Almeida.* She'd pulled the old heresy file that morning — the Battle of Redford Bridge, the unit that prayed and fought and won, the sole survivor who'd testified to nothing, confessed to nothing, and vanished into the capital's lower streets for three years. There had been something in the surviving transcript. A fragment of recorded prayer, logged by a field-clerk before the fire took the rest:

*Little lantern, light me home.*

Sera had read it eleven times. On the eleventh reading she had caught herself whispering it back, and had stopped, and had felt — briefly, terribly — that something on the far side of the silence had heard her.

"If it's both," she said quietly, "then I'll want to speak with her before I decide anything. Saddle the horses."

* * *

By the third day, the names were going.

They passed a village whose signpost read *HARROW GATE* in the morning and *HARROW GA—* by noon, the final letters flaking away like ash even as Dain watched. By evening the sign read *HARROW*, and the villagers, when asked, said their village had always been called Harrow, and looked at Ivo with the flat, polite eyes of people who had never been anything else.

"They're not lying," Ivo murmured, walking on. "That's the horror of it. The world edits, and everyone agrees to the edit."

"Can you fix it? You're still — you know. Plugged in."

"I'm a charm-seller with a bad knee and a god who's recently resumed correspondence. Fixing geography is above my station." But she stopped at the signpost anyway, laid her palm against the wood, and whispered the old field-blessing — the one for holding a line, for keeping a wall standing past its hour. Warmth moved up her arm like water finding an old channel. The letters didn't come back. But they stopped fading, and the word *HARROW* settled into the wood as if it had always lived there, and one small piece of the world, at least, had been nailed down.

Dain watched her do it with an expression she couldn't read. "You used to do that before battles," he said. "Bless the ground. Halvard used to tease you about it."

"Halvard's dead."

"Yes. Most of us are, in the ways that matter." He hesitated. "You're different, though. Since the temple. You walk like—"

"Like what?"

"Like someone carrying something heavy who's finally decided it's hers to carry."

Ivo snorted. "Three years of selling luck-charms to desperate mothers, and you sum me up in a sentence. Remind me why I hate you."

"Because I'm useful," Dain said, "and you need me, and needing me reminds you of everything else." He said it without self-pity, which was somehow worse. Then he pointed east. "Also, I think that hill just moved."

The hill had, indeed, moved. Perhaps half a mile closer. It wore a ruined watchtower on its crown that Ivo was nearly certain hadn't been there yesterday, and the tower's silhouette looked less like architecture than like a memory of architecture, edges softening even as they watched.

"We're near the borderlands," Ivo said. "Where the country's thinnest. The priest said the unraveling would start at the edges." She squinted at the tower. "There'll be a shrine at the pass beyond it. The Hollow Gate road runs through shrine country — the old faith built its doors where the walls between things are thinnest." She started walking again, faster. "And we're being followed."

"How do you know?"

"Because the road keeps trying to loop us back toward Corvane, and something keeps *correcting* it. Roads don't have opinions." She glanced over her shoulder at the long brown distance. "Someone behind us wants us going east more than the world wants us going nowhere. Pray it's only one someone."

* * *

Sera found the HARROW signpost at dusk and dismounted, because the horse refused to go near it.

Her clerk, Brother Anselm, a nervous young man with ink-stained cuffs, copied the inscription into his ledger. "It matches the survey records from four years ago," he said. "Except the survey says the village is called Harrow Gate."

"And the villagers?"

"Say Harrow. All of them. Cheerfully." He swallowed. "Inquisitor, the last village back — Marlow Ford — isn't on any map older than yesterday. And there are people there. With memories. With *grandchildren*."

Sera stood a moment in the failing light, looking east along the road, at the country fraying like a hem. Somewhere out there was a woman who had spoken with a god, and a nation coming apart at its seams, and a law that suddenly looked less like stone and more like a fence built on a cliff edge that was no longer where the maps said.

She thought of the prayer fragment. *Little lantern, light me home.* She thought of how, for eleven readings, she had wanted it to be true.

"Anselm," she said. "If it came to a choice between the law and the country — which would you save?"

The young man looked horrified. "The law *is* the country, Inquisitor."

"Yes," Sera said. "That's what worries me." She mounted. "Ride through the night. We're close now — I can feel it. Whatever she's running toward, she's almost there."

Ahead, faint in the dark, the ruined watchtower on its impossible hill lit briefly from within — one window glowing warm gold, like a lantern set in a window for someone expected home.

Neither of them mentioned it. Both of them saw it.

* * *

They reached the pass at dawn, and Ivo understood immediately why the old faith had built here.

The shrine crouched in the throat of the mountains like a held breath — a low stone archway, older than any kingdom, its keystone carved with a door seen from the side. The air around it hummed. Not with magic exactly; with *attention*, the way a room hums when everyone in it is listening.

And sitting in the archway's shadow, mending a sock with the unhurried air of someone who had been waiting a very long time, was a small gray woman with bright black eyes.

"Well," the woman said, not looking up. "Two of you. That's a shame. I only take one toll per soul, and I only have room for so many souls."

Ivo stopped. Beside her, Dain's hand drifted toward a sword he hadn't carried in three years.

"Toll?" Ivo said carefully.

The gatekeeper bit her thread and smiled up at them, kindly and terribly.

"A memory," she said. "One each. Something you can't bear to lose. Those are always the ones that open the door."
