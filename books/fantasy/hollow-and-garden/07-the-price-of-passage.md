## 7. The Price of Passage

The border shrine looked like nothing at all.

That was the first horror of it, Ivo thought. Three years of nightmares about the Hollow Gate — and every road she'd ever imagined leading to it had been lined with fortresses, with armies, with doors of black iron studded with the bones of the unfaithful. Instead there was this: a squat stone hut in a fold of ash-grey hills, its roof furred with moss, a wind chime made of spoons clinking over the door. A vegetable garden out back, weeded with evident care. A pair of boots on the step, drying.

"Is it a trap?" Dain whispered. He'd been whispering for two days, ever since the road behind them had begun to loop — since they'd walked past the same leaning fencepost four times and Ivo had finally thrown a pebble at it and watched it land somewhere else entirely.

"Everything's a trap," Ivo said. "This one just has better manners."

She knocked. It seemed polite. Also, her mother had raised her to knock, and if the world was going to end, it could at least end with manners.

The door opened on a small person — or possibly a large child — wrapped in a coat that had clearly been a tent in a previous life. The gatekeeper's face was unremarkable in the way of people who have decided not to be remembered: brown eyes, brown hair, a smudge of flour on one cheek as though baking were the most important work being done here, which perhaps it was.

"You'll want tea," the gatekeeper said. "Everyone wants tea first. Nobody wants to hear what comes after tea."

* * *

The inside of the shrine was warm and smelled of bread and rosemary, which Ivo found deeply offensive. She had expected incense. She had expected dread. She had not expected a kitchen table with three mismatched chairs and a cat asleep in a basket by the stove, an orange beast with the serene entitlement of all cats everywhere.

They sat. Tea was poured from a pot so old its glaze had gone the color of honey. The gatekeeper watched them drink with the patient attention of someone who had done this many times and would do it many more, until the doing stopped.

"You're going to the Gate," the gatekeeper said. Not a question.

"We're going through it," Dain corrected, and then winced, because even he could hear how much he wanted it.

"Mm." The gatekeeper turned their cup slowly on its saucer. "Then you should know what it costs. Most people arrive having heard rumors. Rumors say blood. Rumors say gold. Rumors say you must leave your sins behind." A small, apologetic smile. "Rumors are kinder than the truth, which is typical of rumors."

Ivo set down her cup. Her hands were steady. She was proud of that, in a grim, exhausted way. "Tell us."

"A memory," the gatekeeper said. "One memory, freely given, per soul. Not chosen by me. Chosen by the Gate. It takes what it needs to hold itself open — a door is only as strong as what's braced against it, and memories are very strong things. Heavier than stone, some of them." They sipped their tea. "You won't get to pick which one. You won't know it's gone. You'll walk through whole and happy and missing a piece you can't name, and you'll never miss it, because that's the mercy of it. That's the *cruelty* of it too, but mostly people only notice the mercy."

The cat yawned. The spoons chimed outside. Somewhere beyond the hills, Ivo knew, the country was quietly coming apart at its seams — roads forgetting where they went, names sliding off signs, the bargain the gods had struck running dry like a well in drought.

"And if we refuse?" Dain asked.

"Then you stay." The gatekeeper said it without judgment, the way one might report the weather. "Plenty do. There's a village two days east full of people who decided their memories were worth more than their lives. Lovely people. Terrible bakers." They paused. "Well. One of them is a good baker. But he gave up his memory of his daughter, so the bread tastes like grief anyway."

Ivo laughed — a short, startled sound that hurt coming out. She couldn't help it. Warmth and horror, sitting at the same table, passing the honey.

"That's it?" she said. "One memory? No oath, no binding, no—"

"No fine print. The Gate isn't a merchant, Almeida." The gatekeeper's eyes flicked up, quick and knowing, and Ivo's stomach dropped — they knew her name, knew her face under the hood, knew the charms sewn into her sleeve. "It's a door. Doors don't cheat. People do."

Dain was quiet for a long moment. Then he said, "I'll pay now."

"Dain." Ivo hadn't meant to say his name. It came out sharp, almost angry, and she supposed she was, a little. "Think. You don't know what it'll take. It could take your mother's face. Your—"

"It doesn't matter what it takes." He said it simply, and that simplicity frightened her more than any desperation would have. He looked tired in a way that had nothing to do with two days of walking. "Ivo. Three years I've carried—" He stopped. Started again. "There are things I need to put down before I go through any door. If the Gate wants one, let it have the heaviest."

The gatekeeper stood, unhurried, and opened a door at the back of the shrine Ivo would have sworn had not been there a moment ago. Beyond it was not a room. It was a pale light, and a hush, and the faint smell of rain on far-off fields.

"One at a time," the gatekeeper said gently. "And when you come out, don't ask yourself what's missing. That way lies madness, and also we're out of tea."

* * *

Dain paid first.

He stood at the threshold for a moment — big Dain, who had once held a shield line at Carrow Ford for six hours with half his arm hanging wrong, who had laughed so loudly at bad jokes that officers came to see what the noise was. He looked back at Ivo, and something moved across his face like weather.

"I'm sorry," he said. "For whatever it is I did. I know there's something. I've known since I found you in Vell's Crossing and you looked at me like I was a grave." His voice cracked, and he laughed at himself, wetly. "Gods, I've been trying to remember *what*, and I can't make myself say it, and maybe that's cowardice, or maybe some part of me already knows I'm about to be forgiven whether I deserve it or not."

"You don't get to be forgiven by forgetting," Ivo said. Her hands had curled into fists without her permission. "That's not how it works. That's not how *any* of it works."

"I know." He wiped his eyes with the heel of his hand, embarrassed, enormous, heartbreaking. "But I'm tired, Ivo. I'm so tired. And if the price of walking out of this dying place is that I stop carrying it — then I suppose I'm the kind of man who pays, and always was, and that's the worst thing about me."

He stepped through.

The light took him gently, the way sleep takes a child mid-sentence. For a heartbeat his silhouette hung in the pale glow — and then it folded, and he was standing on the other side, blinking, looking around with mild curiosity, and then looking at *her*.

"Oh," Dain said pleasantly. "Hello. Are you coming through? It's lovely over here. Feels like spring."

Ivo did not move. She could not move. She stood in the doorway of a kitchen that smelled of bread and watched the largest grief of her life evaporate off a man's face like dew, and understood, finally and completely, what escape cost.

Because she knew. She still knew. She remembered Vell's Crossing — the ambush, the fire, the unit scattered like seed grain, and Dain slipping away in the night to sell their position to the Inquisition for his own skin. She remembered the eleven names. She had carved them into her heart the way other people carved them into stone, and she had carried them every day for three years, and she had told herself the weight was justice.

Now Dain stood on the far side of a doorway, light as a boy, smiling at her with no idea why she was crying.

"That's the trick of it," the gatekeeper said softly, beside her. They had appeared without sound, the way cats and gatekeepers do. "The Gate takes what holds you heaviest. For him, it seems, it was the guilt. So he's free." A pause. "You aren't. You remember everything. Someone has to."

"That's monstrous," Ivo whispered.

"Yes," the gatekeeper agreed, with real sorrow. "Doors are monstrous things, Almeida. Every door in the world stands between someone leaving and someone staying. This one just makes the arithmetic honest." They touched her elbow, feather-light. "Your turn whenever you're ready. Or—" and here their voice did something complicated, something almost hopeful, "—you could look at the anchor-stone first. Before you decide anything. Some travelers like to know what they'd be holding open."

"The what?"

The gatekeeper led her past the impossible door, out the back, past the weeded garden, to the edge of the shrine's yard. And there, half-buried in the hillside like a tooth of the old world, was a stone. Pale, veined with light that pulsed slow as a sleeping heart. And pressed against it, sunk into it to the shoulders, was a figure — a woman, grey with dust, eyes closed, arms spread along the stone's length as though embracing it. As though she had been embracing it for a very long time.

"She chose it," the gatekeeper said. "Forty years ago. The Gate needed an anchor or it would have closed with three hundred souls on the wrong side. She stayed. She's still there. Still hearing everything that passes through." They looked at Ivo sideways. "Still listening. Which is why I mention it. Some people, when they learn what the toll really is — what remembering costs, versus what staying costs — find they'd rather pay differently."

The stone pulsed. Once. Slow. Like something breathing. Like something waiting.

Like something that might, at long last, have a voice.

Ivo stood in the ash-grey light with her dead comrades' names in her chest and her god's silence breaking open around her, and heard herself say:

"Show me how it works."
