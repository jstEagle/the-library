## 8. Hollow Gate

The road stopped pretending to be a road two miles from the Gate.

Ivo felt it first in her boots — the ground going soft the way bread goes soft, all its structure quietly giving up. Then Dain stumbled beside her and swore, and she looked down and saw that the road had become a field of grey grass that had never been planted there, growing in rows too neat for any farmer.

"It's looping again," Dain said. His voice had a new flatness to it since the shrine. Since he'd paid his toll. He said things once and didn't repeat them, because repetition required remembering what you'd already said. "We passed this grass."

"We haven't," Ivo said gently. "You just think we have."

He frowned at her, and there it was again — that terrible blankness where his guilt used to live. She had spent six days hating him with a hatred that had nowhere to sit. Every time she reached for the old fury — *you sold us, you sold Mara and Osk and little Tam with his burned hands* — she found smooth stone instead, because Dain himself no longer knew what he'd done. The gatekeeper had taken the memory as toll, and taken everything wrapped around it: the shame, the sleepless nights, the reason he flinched when anyone said her name. What was left was a big, decent-seeming man who walked beside her and called her *friend* and meant it.

She almost wished he'd betrayed her again. At least then she'd know what to do with her hands.

"Keep moving," she said. "Don't look at anything too long. It takes attention as an invitation."

That was the rule they'd learned on the road out of the capital, learned the hard way when a village they sheltered in lost its name overnight — not forgotten, *gone*, scrubbed off signs and tongues and gravestones alike, so that by morning nobody could say where they were, only that they were somewhere. Names went first. Then edges. The horizon had developed a habit of arriving before you did.

Ahead of them, past the last honest hill, the Hollow Gate stood in its valley like a held breath.

Ivo had imagined it a hundred ways in six days of walking: a fortress door, a cathedral arch, something vast and terrible. It was none of those. It was a doorway standing alone in a meadow — a plain stone frame, tall as three men, with nothing behind it but more meadow, except that the light through the empty frame was wrong. Warmer. Greener. The light of somewhere that still remembered how afternoons were supposed to go.

And around it, people. Hundreds of them. A river of carts and children and old men carrying their grandmothers, all funneling toward the frame in a silence that wasn't peaceful so much as *careful* — the silence of people who had been told not to run, because running attracted the fraying.

"Oh," Dain said softly. "Oh, it's real."

"It's real," Ivo said, and her chest did something complicated, because for six days she had told herself it might not be, that the dying priest's story was a madman's comfort, that she was dragging this hollowed-out man across a dissolving country on the strength of a rumor. "Come on. We're nearly—"

The sky hiccupped.

There was no better word for it. The clouds above the valley skipped, like a needle jumping on a record, and for half a heartbeat the meadow around them was a wheatfield, and then a lakebed, and then the meadow again, and every person in the crowd stood very still until the world finished deciding.

"That one was close," Dain said.

"That one was close," Ivo agreed, and took his arm, and they joined the river of the fleeing.

* * *

The queue moved with the strange courtesy of shared catastrophe. Nobody pushed. A woman ahead of them handed Ivo half a loaf without being asked, saying only, "You look like you've been walking longer than us." A boy of maybe ten was collecting names — actually writing them down in a battered ledger, asking each traveler their name and their mother's name and writing both, "so somebody knows who got through." Ivo gave hers and watched him write *Almeida* and felt the old fear rise — *the Inquisition reads lists* — and then let it go, because the Inquisition's lists were ash now, along with most of the buildings that had held them.

"You'll want to decide what you're keeping," the boy said, businesslike. "When you get up front. The gatekeeper asks."

"I know."

"He asked my mum for her memory of my da." The boy's chin came up, defiant, daring Ivo to make it sad. "She wouldn't pay it. So we're staying till the end, aren't we. Till there's nothing left to walk through to." He shrugged. "Somebody's got to remember Da if Mum won't give him up."

Ivo crouched to his height. "Then you remember him twice as hard," she said. "For both of you."

He considered this, nodded once, and moved on to the next traveler, and Ivo stood with her heart aching in a way that had nothing to do with fear.

Because that was the arithmetic of the Gate, laid bare. The gatekeeper took memories as toll — and the memories people could least afford to lose were the ones they refused to pay. Escape cost you exactly as much as your life had been worth. The woman who'd given her the bread would walk through lighter. The boy's mother would stay and be unmade whole.

Dain touched her elbow. "What are you paying?" he asked. "If they ask?"

"I don't know yet."

"You should decide soon. Look at the front of the line."

She looked, and understood what he meant. The Gate's frame was glowing — faintly, unevenly, like a hearth burning down. And the gatekeeper, a small figure in grey robes who seemed to be several different ages depending on the angle, was turning people away. Not cruelly. Kneeling to speak to them. Pointing back down the valley.

"They're closing it," Dain breathed. "They're already—"

"No." Ivo's stomach dropped through the softening ground. "No, look — they're not turning everyone away. They're slowing it. One at a time now. Slower and slower." She did the arithmetic she'd been refusing to do for six days, the same arithmetic the priest had done with his last breath. *The Gate holds as long as someone holds it. The anchor-stone is not a stone.*

"How long?" Dain asked.

"Not long enough for all of these people."

* * *

They were forty places from the front when Sera Vane found them.

Ivo heard her before she saw her — that particular cadence of authority softened by exhaustion, saying *let her pass, she's with me* — and turned, and there was the Inquisitor, warrant-case under one arm like a shield she'd forgotten to put down, grey-faced, dust-caked, her immaculate coat ruined beyond any tailor's redemption.

Every instinct Ivo owned screamed *run*. There was nowhere to run. There was barely anywhere to stand.

"Inquisitor," she said. "Come to arrest me at the end of the world? You're late. Your colleagues missed the deadline."

Sera looked at her for a long moment. Then she did the last thing Ivo expected: she laughed. It was a small, cracked sound, but it was real.

"My colleagues," Sera said, "are dead, or fled, or standing in that line wondering whether the gods will take a memory of a memory. My office is a hole in the Vellum Quarter. My warrant—" she lifted the case, opened it, and showed Ivo the paper inside: her own name, IVO ALMEIDA, HERETIC, in beautiful red ink, "—is the single most worthless object in this valley. Do you know why?"

"Enlighten me."

"Because there is no law left to enforce it against." Sera closed the case. Her hands, Ivo noticed, were shaking, and she noticed herself noticing, and hated it. "I spent my life believing the law was a wall between people and the gods. That faith unregulated burns villages. That prayer unanswered is safer than prayer answered. I believed it the way you believe stone is solid — right up until the stone stopped being solid." She gestured, and the sky obligingly hiccupped again; somewhere behind them a cart became a boat, sat briefly in water that wasn't there, and became a cart. "I followed you across half a country to arrest you, Almeida. Somewhere around the Ash Road I realized I wasn't following you anymore. I was following the same road."

"And now?"

"Now I'm here because my faith has nowhere left to stand," Sera said simply, "except possibly through that door. And I find I can't make myself get in the line while there are people being turned away from it." Her jaw tightened. "That's not doctrine. That's just — what's left when the doctrine burns."

Dain shifted beside Ivo, uneasy. "You hunted her," he said to Sera, with the blunt honesty of a man with no tact left and no secrets either. "Three years ago. You broke her fingers."

"I did," Sera said, and did not look away from Ivo. "And I'd do it again, if the law still existed to make it right or wrong. That's the truth. I won't insult you with an apology you'd see through."

It should have made Ivo furious. Instead it landed strangely clean — the first honest thing anyone had said to her since the shrine. She flexed the old injury, the two fingers that had never quite straightened, and found that the anger she'd carried like a second knife had gone dull somewhere on the road, worn away by distance and bad bread and the boy with the ledger.

"The law's not gone," Ivo heard herself say. "It's just smaller now. It fits in one person." She nodded at the line, at the gatekeeper turning away another family, kneeling, explaining, pointing back. "Question is what yours says about doors."

Sera followed her gaze. Ivo watched her understand — watched the Inquisitor's face do the same arithmetic Ivo had been doing for an hour, and arrive at the same unbearable sum.

"There aren't enough passages left," Sera said quietly. "Are there."

"No."

"And the Gate stays open only while—"

"Yes."

Sera closed her eyes. When she opened them, something had settled in her, the way sediment settles in shaken water. "Then I'm going to go stand at the back of that line," she said, "and start enforcing a new law. One soul at a time, oldest and smallest first. And you—" she pointed at Ivo, and even now, even ruined, the gesture had iron in it, "—are going to tell me what the gatekeeper told you. Because you've been looking at that door for an hour like a woman doing sums she already hates the answer to."

Ivo looked at the Gate. At the dimming frame. At the hundreds still waiting, and the thousands still coming down the valley road, and the boy with the ledger writing down names so somebody would know who got through.

"The gatekeeper hasn't told me anything," she said. "But my god has. Three years of silence, and on the Ash Road, in the middle of the night, She finally spoke." Ivo swallowed. "One word. She said *hold*."

Behind them, far down the valley, the horizon arrived early — a soft grey tide of not-quite-nothing, eating the road they'd come by, patient as debt.

Dain looked at the tide. Then at Ivo. Then at the Gate, with the blank, open face of a man who couldn't remember why any of this mattered to him, only that it mattered to *her*, which was somehow worse.

"Ivo," he said. "What are you thinking?"

She was thinking that some doors don't open. They're held.
