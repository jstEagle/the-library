## 10. Garden

The valley on the far side had no name yet. That was the first thing Sera Vane noticed about it — a country-shaped absence waiting to be filled — and the second thing she noticed was that nobody seemed frightened of it.

Refugees moved through the new grass like people waking from a long fever. They built shelters out of what they'd carried: wagon canvas, temple doors pried from their hinges and lashed into lean-tos, one entire tavern sign that someone had refused to leave behind. A woman who had once sold fish in the capital's lower market was already selling fish again, from a barrel, at prices Sera considered frankly extortionate, and there was a queue.

Sera stood at the edge of it all in her grey coat, which she had not yet been able to bring herself to stop wearing, and watched a child chase something that might have been a butterfly or might have been a small escaped prayer.

"You're staring," said Odile, the gatekeeper's apprentice, who had appointed herself Sera's shadow sometime during the third week and could not be discouraged.

"I'm assessing."

"You're staring."

Sera let it go. There was no law here yet. That was the trouble and the miracle of it both. She had spent twenty years believing that law was the wall between people and the things that would eat them — gods included, gods especially — and now she stood in a green valley where the wall had fallen down and no one had been eaten, and she did not know what her hands were for.

They still shook sometimes. Less than they had.

"Vane!" bellowed a voice across the camp. It was Marta, the fish woman, waving a whole mackerel at her as though it were a summons. "You settling the dispute about the well or not?"

"There is no dispute about the well. There is one man who wants to charge for water and four hundred people who want him to reconsider his life choices."

"So you'll settle it?"

Sera sighed, tucked her ledger under her arm — she kept a ledger now, but of debts owed and repaid, favors, births, the name of every person who had come through the Gate, because someone had to remember, and apparently that someone was her — and went down into the camp to do justice without a warrant for the first time in her career.

It was, she admitted somewhere around the moment the well-hoarding man burst into tears and confessed he'd only wanted to feel important since his wife died, going reasonably well.

* * *

Ivo Almeida woke when the morning light came through the Gate.

That was how time worked inside: the light arrived on its own schedule, warm and gold and slightly opinionated, and you got up when it told you to. She had stopped fighting it months ago. The garden didn't care about her schedule anyway. The garden cared about watering, and about being spoken to, and — this was the part Ivo had taken longest to accept — about being listened to.

She walked the rows barefoot, because shoes made the soil sulky. On her left grew the memory of a wedding: a hedge of white flowers that smelled like rain on hot stone and a specific somebody's hair. On her right, a stand of trees grown from a soldier's memory of his mother's kitchen, which produced fruit that tasted faintly of cinnamon and made everyone who ate it briefly homesick for a place they'd never been. Further along, the strange ones: a patch of blue grass grown from a child's memory of a story half-remembered; a single tree that bore keys, dozens of them, from an old locksmith's recollection of every lock he'd ever opened and the one he never could.

Every traveler who passed through the Gate paid a toll, and the toll stayed here, and here it became this. Ivo had not planned any of it. She had simply put her hands in the ground on the first day, because her hands needed doing something, and the ground had answered the way old friends answer — immediately, warmly, and with more work for her than she'd expected.

"Morning," she told the wedding hedge. "You're looking smug."

The hedge rustled, pleased with itself.

She paused at the newest plot, near the Gate itself, where the soil was still dark and raw. Dain's memory had gone into the ground there — all of it, everything he'd surrendered at the border shrine, including the part that remembered why he'd ever wronged her. He had walked through the Gate a stranger to his own worst day, and he lived now in the refugee camp on the far side, cheerful and unburdened, and every so often he came to the edge of the garden and looked at Ivo with the expression of a man trying to recall why a stranger's face felt like home.

She had decided, after much bitter argument with herself, to be kind to him. It cost more than hatred would have. She suspected that was the point.

"Grow something good," she told the dark soil. "He wasn't all bad. He just wanted to live. Can't even blame him for that anymore, which is its own kind of hell."

The soil did not answer, but it did seem, very slightly, warmer under her hands.

She worked until the light shifted. She weeded a row of forgotten birthdays. She propped up a trellis sagging under the weight of somebody's grandmother's disapproval, which grew like beans and tasted like iron. She talked the whole time, because the plants liked it, and because — though she would have denied it under torture — she was talking past them, upward, outward, to the silence at the center of the Gate that had held her god for three years and then, on the last day of the old country, had finally spoken.

Not since, though. Not since. And she was tired of pretending that didn't ache.

* * *

In the fourth month, Sera climbed to the Gate.

Everyone else avoided it. The doorway stood alone on a hill of pale stone, tall as a cathedral door and older-looking than stone had any right to be, and beyond its frame was not darkness but depth — a soft gold-green light, like looking down into summer. People came to the threshold, paid their toll to whatever collected it (no one had ever seen the collector; the memories simply left, gently, like birds), and stepped through to the valley. They did not linger. The door made people feel watched in a way that was not unpleasant but was definitely not nothing.

Sera lingered. She had taken to visiting monthly, ostensibly to record arrivals, actually because standing before the Gate was the closest she had ever come to praying, and she was not ready to pray, but she liked practicing.

Today someone was in the garden.

She had heard rumors of the doorholder, of course. The refugees spoke of her the way sailors speak of weather — with respect, gratitude, and total vagueness. *She holds the door open. She stays so the rest of us get through.* Sera had filed the reports with professional skepticism and private awe.

Now she saw a woman kneeling between rows of impossible flowers, sleeves rolled, dirt to the elbows, arguing cheerfully with a hedge.

Sera knew that face. Every Inquisitor knew that face. Ivo Almeida, heretic, oathbreaker, the name that had started a file that had ended a country — except none of that was true, was it, and Sera had known it wasn't true since the road through the ash, since the signs with names missing, since the moment she'd understood that the law she served had been standing guard over a dying bargain and calling it order.

"You're staring," said Ivo Almeida, without turning around.

"That seems to be going around," Sera said.

Ivo looked over her shoulder. Her eyes were the same as they'd been in the wanted notices, except tired in a way that looked earned rather than inflicted. "Inquisitor Vane. Come to arrest me?"

"The Inquisition doesn't exist anymore. Neither does the country. You may have noticed."

"Noticed something. Hard to miss the screaming geography." Ivo stood, wiped her hands on her thighs, and studied Sera with frank curiosity. "So what does the law do when the law runs out?"

Sera considered the question honestly, because the woman had earned honesty if anyone had. "What it always should have done. It goes and helps with the well dispute."

Ivo laughed — a short, surprised sound, like something breaking open in a good way. "Marta told me about that. You made the hoarder cry."

"He needed to. So did I, a little." Sera looked past her, at the rows of growing memory, and felt something in her chest turn over. "This is what they paid. All those tolls. It becomes this."

"It becomes this." Ivo's voice gentled. "Every ugly thing people gave up grows into something worth keeping. I don't know why. I've stopped needing to know why. That took years off my recovery, by the way — the not-needing-to-know."

"I can imagine." Sera hesitated. Then, because the ledger under her arm suddenly felt very heavy and very light at once, she said: "Your name is in my book. First page. I wrote it three years ago, when I opened your file. *Ivo Almeida — heresy.*"

"And now?"

"Now I'm going to cross it out," Sera said, "and write something true instead. I haven't decided what yet. Something with a garden in it."

Ivo looked at her for a long moment. Then she knelt, scooped a handful of dark soil into a small clay pot that had been sitting empty by the path, and held it out.

"For the road," she said. "Plant it wherever you end up. It remembers how to grow. All of it does. That's the whole secret, if you're taking notes."

Sera took the pot. It was warm.

* * *

Alone again, Ivo walked to the center of the garden, where the oldest plants grew — the first tolls ever paid, back when the Gate was young, grown now into trees so vast their canopy was its own weather. She sat down among the roots, the way she did every evening, and she spoke to the silence the way she had spoken to it for three silent years and then one terrible golden day when it had answered.

"I held the door," she said. "Like I said I would. It's harder than it looks, and easier than I feared, and I complain about it constantly, which you probably know, since you know everything, allegedly."

The leaves moved. The light through the canopy went gold, then deeper gold, then a color she had no name for and suspected was older than names.

"You know," she said, quieter, "the funny thing is I'm not angry anymore. Took me long enough. You weren't gone. You were paying. Somebody should have told me. Somebody should have—" Her voice caught, and she let it, because there was no one here but the garden, and gardens keep confidences. "Somebody should have told me it was a bargain and not a betrayal. I wasted three years being small."

The warmth came then — not from the light but from underneath, from the roots, from everywhere at once, the way it had come on the last day of the old country when her prayer had cracked the sky open. Ivo closed her eyes.

And the silence, which was never really silence, which had never once been silence, spoke:

***Ivo.***

Just her name. Just that. It was enough. It was more than enough; it was a harvest; it was every charm she'd ever sold and every oath she'd ever broken and every desperate mother she'd ever cheated with honest magic, all of it gathered up and said back to her in a voice like the first warm day after a long winter.

She laughed, and cried, and pulled a weed, because the garden needed weeding regardless of revelations.

Above her, far away, on the other side of a door she would never walk through, a woman with a ledger planted a handful of remembering soil in a nameless valley, and the first green shoot came up overnight, and in the morning the refugees found it and named the valley after it.

Ivo tended the garden, and the garden grew, and the door stayed open.
