## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The morning the hunters came to Hollowmere, Cyrus Iversen was mending a fence post, and the valley was pretending, as it always did, that the world beyond the boundary stones had never existed.

Fog lay in the hollows like spilled milk. The mill wheel turned with its patient groan. Somewhere up-slope, Wren was swearing at a beehive, and the sound of it—familiar, small, safe—drifted down through the birches. Cyrus drove the post into the earth and thought, as he had thought every morning of his nineteen years, that this was all there was, and that all there was would be enough.

Then his hand began to burn.

He dropped the mallet. The pain came from his left palm, from the mark he'd carried since birth—a rough scar in the shape of an open circle broken by a vertical line, which his mother, before she died, had called nothing, which Master Aldric had called an old burn, which Cyrus had long ago stopped asking about. Now it flared as though a coal had been pressed into it, and when he peeled back his fingers, the sigil glowed.

Not reflected light. Not the dawn. A soft, amber radiance rising out of the scar itself, pulsing like a second heartbeat, and Cyrus stood in the wet grass with his hand open before him and felt the world tilt beneath his boots.

He looked up. That was when he saw the riders.

They came down the pass road in a line of four, and even at that distance they were wrong for Hollowmere. The valley had no visitors. The boundary stones saw to that—not with walls, but with a forgetting, a soft gray blur at the edges of the mind that turned travelers gently around before they knew they'd turned. Yet these four came on, unhurried, their horses dark as wet slate, and above them the king's crown was stitched in gold thread on their surcoats, and beneath the crown, a symbol Cyrus had never seen but somehow knew: a circle, broken, with a line through it.

His mark. Inverted. Hunted.

The glow in his palm guttered and died as they passed the mill, and Cyrus crouched in the grass, heart slamming, and watched them go by. The lead rider turned her head. She was young, or she had been once; her face had the smooth, worn look of a statue left out in rain. Her eyes passed over the fence, over the birches, over the place where Cyrus crouched—and paused there, a half-second too long, the way a hand pauses over a drawer where something has been hidden.

Then she rode on, toward the guildhouse.

* * *

The Guild of the Bound Oath occupied a stone hall above the millpond, and it was, as far as anyone in Hollowmere knew, a school for masons and letter-writers that had fallen on quiet times. Cyrus had grown up sweeping its floors, copying its ledgers, learning geometry and grammar from Master Aldric, a stooped old man who smelled of chalk and lamp oil and who had never once, in nineteen years, said the word *magic*.

Now Cyrus took the back stairs two at a time, his palm wrapped in a rag, and found Aldric in the scriptorium, trying to rise from his chair.

"They're here," the old man said. It was not a question. His voice had gone thin and strange. "I felt them at the stones. Four. How many?"

"Four. They're coming up the road. Master, what's happening to me? My hand—"

"Sit down, boy." Aldric lowered himself back into the chair, and Cyrus saw, with a lurch, how much the old man had failed in a single morning. His hands shook. His eyes, usually the color of a winter sky, were fixed on the middle distance as though reading something written there. "And bring the third ledger. The black one. Quickly."

Cyrus brought it. The ledger was heavy, its cover cracked leather gone soft as cloth, and when he set it on the desk, Aldric opened it to a page near the end and turned it toward him.

The page held a list of names. Dozens of names, in a dozen different hands, some faded to ghosts, some fresh as yesterday. At the top, in Aldric's own cramped script: *The Bound Oath—those who carry it.*

"Read the last three," Aldric said.

Cyrus read. *Wren Calloway. Sera Voss. Cyrus Iversen.* His own name looked back at him, and beneath it, in ink still glossy, a single word he did not know: *Unbound.*

"I don't understand."

"No," Aldric said. "You wouldn't. I was meant to explain when you came of age, and then you came of age, and I found I was a coward, and I told myself another year, and another. That is the particular sin of teachers, Cyrus. We mistake shelter for love." He drew a breath that whistled. "The Guild of the Bound Oath was never a school for masons. It was the last chapter of an order older than the kingdom. We were binders. When something in this world grows too hungry to be killed—when it cannot be slain, only *caged*—it was the Oath that built the cage. And the things we bound, boy, the oldest of them, the worst of them—"

He stopped. His gaze went, involuntarily, upward. Toward the capital. Toward the crown.

"—they eat memories," he said. "Not flesh. Not souls. Memories. Magic first, because magic lives in memory—the first time you shaped fire, the first word of power, the face of the one who taught you. The Tithe takes it all, and when the magic is gone from the world, it will take the rest. Every face. Every grief. Everything that makes a man anything more than breathing meat."

The word *Tithe* landed in Cyrus like a stone dropped down a well. He had never heard it. He knew it the way he knew his own heartbeat.

"And me? What am I?"

"You are the last apprentice of the last guild," Aldric said simply, "and you are not the only one. Wren. Sera. You three carry the Oath in your blood—the sigil, the gift, dormant all these years because the valley's stones kept it sleeping. Magic cannot cross into Hollowmere, Cyrus. Not from outside. Not even the gift inside you could wake while you lived behind them." His eyes flicked to Cyrus's bandaged palm. "But those riders crossed the stones. Which means the king's hunters have found a way to carry his hunger through. And which means, I am afraid, that the boundary is failing."

Outside, hooves crunched on gravel. Voices, low and courteous, at the guildhouse door.

"They'll ask for records," Aldric murmured. "Names. They always start with records. And when they find the ledger—" He pushed it across the desk toward Cyrus with sudden, desperate strength. "The cellar. Behind the wine racks there's a door older than the guildhouse. It opens outward, under the wall, past the stones. Take the ledger. Take Wren and Sera. Go to the capital."

"The *capital*—Master, I can't—"

"You can." Aldric gripped his wrist, and his hand was burning hot, and Cyrus understood with a horror that arrived all at once, like dawn, that the old man had been dying for weeks and had chosen this morning to spend his last hours well. "The king is bound, boy. The thing wearing his crown has been wearing it for thirty years, and the kingdom has forgotten what a true king looked like, and soon it will forget everything else. You carry the Oath. You are the key that was cut for this lock. I would go myself, but—" he smiled, and it was the saddest thing Cyrus had ever seen, "—I am nearly out of memories myself, and I would rather spend the last ones looking at this room."

The door of the scriptorium opened. The huntress stood there, rain-worn and patient, and behind her shoulder Cyrus could see the other riders fanning through the hall with terrible gentleness, touching nothing yet, cataloguing everything.

"Master Aldric," she said. "The Guild of the Bound Oath. We've come about your apprentices."

Aldric rose. He stood straighter than he had in years.

"They're at their studies," he said. "You're welcome to look."

Cyrus took the ledger, and went, as quietly as a man has ever gone, toward the cellar stairs—and the last thing he heard from the room above, before the dark took him, was his master's voice, steady and warm, offering the hunters tea.

* * *

The cellar door was where Aldric said it would be, behind a rack of dusty bottles, and it groaned open on hinges that had not moved in Cyrus's lifetime. Beyond it lay a low stone passage sloping down and out, under the valley wall, toward a gray green light that made his teeth ache.

He found Wren first—dragged her from the beehives with a hand clamped over her mouth before she finished her first curse, shoved the ledger into her arms, and told her in thirty ragged words everything Aldric had told him. To her credit, Wren Calloway asked only one question.

"Sera?"

"Getting her now. Then we run."

"Run *where*?"

Cyrus looked back once, at the guildhouse on its hill, at the smoke just beginning to rise from its chimney where the hunters had asked for tea, at the whole small safe world that he had believed in for nineteen years and that had, in a single morning, revealed itself to be a shell over a wound.

"Away," he said. "And then to the capital. We have a king to unbind."

The passage breathed cold on his face. His palm, wrapped and hidden, pulsed once in the dark—like a thing waking—like a promise, or a warning.

Behind him, far above, he thought he heard the guildhouse door close.
