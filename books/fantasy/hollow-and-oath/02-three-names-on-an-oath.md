## 2. Three Names on an Oath

Cyrus had never run from anything in his life. Hollowmere was a valley that taught you to stand still—to mend what broke, to bury what died, to watch the boundary stones weather season after season without complaint. Running felt like a betrayal of everything the valley stood for.

He ran anyway.

Behind him, smoke rose from the guildhouse in a gray column that bent against the wind like a man bowing at a funeral. Master Aldric's body lay inside it, wrapped in the oath-cloth Cyrus had been too clumsy to fold properly. The crown hunters had given them until dawn. Wren had given them until now.

"Keep moving," she snapped over her shoulder, her braid whipping like a dark rope. "Grief is a luxury. Buy it later."

The third of them—Tam, youngest and quietest, who had carried nothing out of the burning guild but Master Aldric's ledger—stumbled on the sheep path and caught himself against Cyrus's arm. His fingers were shaking. So were Cyrus's.

The boundary stones rose ahead through the morning mist, five great monoliths furred with moss, older than the guild, older than the valley's name. Cyrus had touched them a hundred times as a boy. They had always felt like nothing. Like standing near a wall and feeling only stone.

Now, as he drew close, the sigil in his palm began to burn.

It wasn't pain, exactly—or it was, but a pain so old and familiar he'd stopped noticing it years ago, the way you stop noticing a crooked tooth. Now it woke. Heat crawled up his wrist, his forearm, pooling under his skin like swallowed sunlight. The mark of the Bound Oath—the interlocked ring and key every apprentice bore as a brand of membership—glowed gold through his flesh.

"It knows," Tam whispered. "The stones know you."

"Wonderful," said Wren. "Cross quickly, then."

She went first, one hand flat on the central monolith, and stepped through the space between two stones as casually as stepping through a doorway. Nothing happened to her. She turned, arms crossed, waiting.

Cyrus put his palm against the stone.

The world came apart.

Magic did not enter him. It *detonated* in him. Every dormant thread the valley had smothered for twenty-two years ignited at once, and Cyrus dropped to his knees in the wet grass beyond the stones, screaming, or trying to—he couldn't tell if sound came out. He could feel the lowlands entire: the roots of the alders drinking, the fox in its den three fields away, the slow mineral patience of the hills, and beneath all of it a vast humming web of oath-magic that his guild had once woven across the whole kingdom, frayed now, mostly dead, but *there*, and he could feel every torn strand like a wound in his own chest.

Hands seized him. Wren's voice, distant, sharp: "Breathe, you idiot. Breathe like I taught you—in for four, hold, out for four." Tam pressed the ledger into his hands, told him to read, to anchor himself in ordinary words.

Hours passed, or minutes. When Cyrus finally opened his eyes, the sky had gone the color of pewter, and both his fellow apprentices were crouched over him, mud-smeared and terrified, and something in their faces—two people he barely knew, refusing to leave him—cracked something open in his chest that had nothing to do with magic.

"We don't leave each other," Wren said, as if answering the thought. "That's the whole point of an oath. Say it back."

"We don't leave each other."

"Good. Now get up. They'll have crossed the stones by nightfall, and hunters don't kneel in the grass admiring the view."

* * *

Wren had counted the hunters twice from the ridge: six riders, gray cloaks, the king's stag stamped in black on their shoulders. Six was too many to fight. It was not too many to fool.

"The gift isn't fireballs," she told Cyrus, crouched beside him in the bracken while Tam kept watch below. "Master Aldric always said the Oath-gift is about *binding*. What holds. What connects. You felt the web when you crossed the stones—all those threads. Well." Her mouth curved, sharp as a fishhook. "Threads can be tied. Threads can be cut. And threads can be *moved*."

"I don't know how to move anything. I've never done magic on purpose in my life."

"You lit the guildhouse lamps by snapping your fingers when you were nine. Don't look at me like that—Tam told me. You thought it was drafts." She took his marked hand and turned it palm-up, studying the sigil's fading glow. "You've been doing magic your whole life. You just called it luck. Now we're going to call it on purpose."

Her teaching was merciless. That night, in the hollow of a fallen oak, she made him feel for the threads—*not see, feel, stop squinting like a farrier*—until he found the thin bright line connecting him to Tam sleeping ten feet away. The next night she made him tug it, gently, until Tam woke convinced someone had spoken his name. The night after that, she made him tie a thread between two of the hunters' horses so that when one shied, both bolted, and the riders lost an hour untangling them in a river ford while the three apprentices slipped past along the high bank.

"You're a natural liar," Cyrus told her afterward, breathless, half-laughing for the first time since the guildhouse burned.

"I'm a natural survivor. Lying is just survival with better posture." But she was smiling too, there in the dark, and Cyrus realized he was cataloguing her smiles now, the way he'd once catalogued the turns of phrase in Master Aldric's oldest books. He realized he'd begun, without deciding to, to memorize these people.

On the sixth day, hunters found them anyway.

* * *

They heard the dogs before they saw the riders—a belling cry rolling up the wooded combe where they'd made camp—and then it was all motion, all terror. They ran. The trees thinned. Ahead, the land fell away into a flooded quarry, black water, sheer cut faces, no way around.

"There," Tam gasped, pointing at the old ore-hoarder's hut perched on a shelf of rock above the water. "The hoist. There'll be a chain."

There was a chain—rusted, groaning, anchored to a windlass older than any of them. Wren went first, then Tam, the basket swaying out over the black water while Cyrus cranked the windlass with his whole body, his palms screaming, the sigil flaring so hot it smoked.

Below, the dogs burst from the treeline.

The lead rider raised a crossbow. Cyrus felt the hunter's intention come down the thread before he understood what he was doing—not seeing it, *feeling* it, the taut line of killing intent stretching from the crossbowman to Tam's swinging basket. He grabbed the thread and *pulled it sideways*, the way Wren had taught him, wrenching the aim wide.

The bolt splashed into the quarry water.

But the effort tore through him like a hook through meat. He felt something rip along the web inside him, felt his grip on the windlass fail, and suddenly he was falling—sliding down the crumbling shelf toward the water, fingers finding nothing, the cold coming up to meet him—

A hand closed on his wrist. Wren's. She had thrown herself flat on the rocks, boots braced against a stump, and she held him over the void with a strength no girl her size should have had, her face white, her teeth gritted, saying through them: "Not. Today. Apprentice."

Tam hauled them both back from the edge, and the three of them collapsed together on the quarry shelf, gasping, listening to the frustrated hunters curse on the far rim. Nobody spoke for a long time.

Then Tam said, quietly, "You caught the bolt. For me."

"Don't make it strange," Cyrus managed.

"He caught the bolt," Tam told Wren, "for me."

"I heard. I'm holding his wrist; I'm aware of his heroics intimately." But her voice had gone rough, and when Cyrus looked at her she was blinking hard at the black water, and he understood that whatever family had failed to form for any of them—in Hollowmere, in whatever towns had spat them out—it was forming here, on this rusted shelf above a drowned quarry, sealed in fear and sweat and the specific gravity of almost losing each other.

"My mother used to say blood is just the first draft of family," Wren said at last. "She was usually drunk when she said it. But she wasn't wrong." She stood, brushed off her knees, and offered Cyrus her hand—palm up, the way she'd taken his a hundred times in teaching. "Three names on an oath, then. No master to bind us. We bind ourselves."

Cyrus took her hand. Tam laid his atop theirs. The sigils in all three palms glowed—not gold now, but a color none of them had words for, warm and new.

* * *

They reached the capital road on the ninth evening, travel-worn, half-starved, alive. From the crest of the last hill, the city spread below them like spilled coins, walled and golden in the dying light, and somewhere in its heart stood the palace where a hollow-eyed king repeated the same day forever.

Cyrus felt it even from here—a wrongness in the web, a place where all the threads bent inward toward something that drank them. Cold poured off that spot in his inner sight like winter off a lake.

"That's it," Tam whispered. "That's where he is."

"Yes," Cyrus said. And then, because the feeling sharpened suddenly, horribly, into recognition: "And it knows we're coming. It just—" He swallowed. "It just said my name."

Wren looked at him, and for the first time since he'd known her, she had nothing sharp to say.
