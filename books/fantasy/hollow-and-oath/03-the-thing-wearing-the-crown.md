## 3. The Thing Wearing the Crown

Wren had stolen things before. Rings, bread, a whole afternoon once, from a miller's son who deserved it. But she had never stolen a king.

They went in through the laundry tunnels, because Tam knew them—Tam, who had grown up in the capital's gutters before the guild took him in, who could find his way through stone the way water found its way downhill. The tunnels smelled of lye and old steam, and the walls wept in the dark, and Wren kept her hand on Cyrus's back the whole way because he was walking like a man following a voice only he could hear.

"It's pulling at him," she'd said to Tam an hour ago, low, while Cyrus slept badly against his pack. "The thing in there. It's been pulling since the boundary stones."

"Then we go faster," Tam said, which was Tam's answer to everything, and usually it worked.

Now they came up through a grate into a cellar stacked with casks of lamp oil, and above them, through three floors of palace, sat the throne room where something wore a dead man's crown—or a living man's crown, Wren corrected herself, though from everything the guild records said, *living* was generous.

They moved as servants. That was the beauty of palaces: no one looked at a servant. Wren carried a basket of linens and let her eyes do the work—counting guards, marking doors, learning the rhythm of the place the way you learned the rhythm of any house you meant to rob. And everywhere, threaded through the ordinary noise of the court, ran a wrongness she couldn't see but could feel through Cyrus, whose hand had gone cold as riverbed stone whenever they passed certain corridors.

"He repeats," Tam murmured, when they paused behind a pillar in the great gallery. Ahead, past a screen of gilded wood, they could see him.

The king of Vael sat alone at a breakfast table laid for twelve.

He was not what Wren expected. She'd imagined a monster, or a puppet slumped in its strings. Instead he was neat, gray-bearded, dignified—and empty, the way a house is empty when the family has gone but the furniture remains. A servant poured his tea. He thanked her graciously. She left. He looked at the tea as if seeing it for the first time, thanked the next servant who passed, and asked her to pour his tea.

"She already did," Wren breathed.

"I know," Tam said.

The king lifted the cup, drank, set it down, and began again: "Good morning. Lovely morning." To no one. To the same morning, over and over, a day worn smooth as a pebble from handling. Wren felt something twist in her chest that she refused to name, because naming it would mean admitting she was afraid.

*That's what it does,* she thought. *It doesn't kill you. It just takes the day you're standing in, and then the one before, until all that's left is this.*

Cyrus made a small sound beside her. His palm—the sigil burned into it—was glowing through his glove.

"We should go," Tam said. "Come back at night, when the watch changes."

But Cyrus wasn't listening. Cyrus was looking down the long axis of the hall toward the throne dais, where the light bent wrong, where the shadows pooled like spilled ink, and Wren saw his face do the thing she hated most: the thing it did when he decided to be brave instead of sensible.

"Cyrus," she whispered. "*Cyrus.*"

He stepped out from behind the pillar.

* * *

Later—much later, in the dark under the palace, with blood on her sleeve that turned out to be hers—Wren would try to reconstruct how fast it all came apart. It came apart like a dropped plate. One moment whole; the next, pieces everywhere and no way to pretend otherwise.

Because the Tithe did not wait to be found. It *received* them.

Cyrus walked into the open hall and the great doors at the far end closed on their own, softly, like a host shutting out a draft. The candles guttered green. And from the throne dais a voice spoke—not aloud, Wren realized, but directly into the meat of the skull, intimate as a lover:

**"Cyrus Iversen. Last apprentice of the Bound Oath. You are late."**

The king at his breakfast table didn't even look up. That was somehow the worst part—that the man was *there*, ten feet away, pouring tea into a cup that never emptied, while the thing that ate him spoke over his head.

Wren grabbed Cyrus's arm. "We run. We get Tam, we come back with a plan—" But Tam was already moving, because Tam was always moving, slipping along the wall toward the servants' door to see if it would open, and it opened, and two of the king's own guard stepped through—not men anymore, Wren understood at once, their eyes were too still—and took him by both arms.

"TAM!"

He fought like the gutter-rat he'd been born, all elbows and fury, and it bought him nothing. The guards didn't bruise him. They simply held him, patient as furniture, and the voice said:

**"A hostage is so crude. Call it collateral. Call it a tithe."**

"You don't get to use that word," Cyrus said, and his voice cracked across the hall like a whip. Wren had never heard it sound like that. "You eat memories. You eat *people*. You've eaten him—" he pointed at the king, who smiled vaguely at being noticed— "and now you want the guild's last magic, and you're going to dress it up as a bargain."

**"I want to make you an offer,"** the Tithe said, and the temperature of the room changed, the green candlelight warming to gold, honeyed, kind. **"Your friends have carried you far, but they are anchors, Cyrus. Mortal, breakable, distracting. Walk away from them now—walk away, and let me keep the loud one—and I will give you everything your guild ever hoarded. Every rite. Every binding. Power enough to unmake me *later*, if you wish, once you no longer need these two to hold your hand."**

And here was the thing Wren would hate herself for, afterward: for half a heartbeat, she believed he might take it.

Not because Cyrus was weak. Because the offer was shaped exactly like his grief. *Power enough to save everyone else later.* The Tithe had read him the way she'd learned to read locks, and it had found the mechanism: Cyrus Iversen would burn himself alive for strangers, and call the ash a fair price.

"Cyrus," she said quietly. "Hey. Look at me."

He looked at her. His palm was blazing now, light leaking between his fingers, and his eyes were wet and furious and *his*, still his.

"The guild bound you to us," she said. "Three names on an oath. You remember what you said? At the stones? You said an oath isn't magic. It's just—" 

"—just people deciding," he finished, hoarse. "Deciding, every day, to stay."

**"Sentiment,"** the Tithe said, and the gold light curdled. **"I have eaten the sentiment of kings."**

"Yeah?" Wren bared her teeth at the darkness on the dais. "How's the king working out for you?"

For the first time, the silence that followed had weight—had *offense* in it. And in that sliver of distraction, Tam twisted free of one guard's grip, and Cyrus raised his burning hand, and the sigil flared so bright that the shadows on the throne dais recoiled like a struck animal.

The king looked up.

Just for a moment—one raw, human moment—the hollow eyes focused, and the man behind them surfaced like a drowning swimmer, and he mouthed two words across the ruined hall:

*Help me.*

Then the dark swallowed him again, and the doors burst inward with the whole palace guard behind them, and Cyrus shouted "RUN," and Wren ran—but they were cut off from the tunnels, driven down a stair, cornered in the dark beneath the palace where the walls were older than the kingdom—

—and the Tithe's voice followed them down, softer now, almost gentle, which was worse than any threat:

**"You cannot unbind what I hold without paying what memory owes. Think on what you love most, little oath-keeper. I will accept nothing less."**

In the black, Cyrus's hand found hers. His palm had stopped glowing. When he spoke, his voice was steady in the way that frightened her more than screaming ever could.

"Wren," he said. "I know what it wants. And I think I've known since Hollowmere."

She couldn't see his face. She wished, with her whole sharp heart, that she could.
