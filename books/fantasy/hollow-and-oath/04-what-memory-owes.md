## 4. What Memory Owes

The throne room smelled of cold stone and old incense, the kind that had burned so long it no longer carried any scent at all—only the memory of scent, which Cyrus thought, with a laugh that caught in his throat, was fitting.

He knelt on the flagstones before the dais, chalk circle crumbling at its edges where his shaking hand had drawn it too thin. Behind him, Wren's breathing came fast and shallow. Tam lay where the guards had dropped him, alive but bound, his face turned toward Cyrus with an expression that asked nothing and forgave everything. That was the worst of it. Tam had never once blamed him for any of this.

And before him, on a throne built for a king, sat the thing wearing the king.

It had worn him well, these long years. It knew how to arrange his hands. It knew the weight of the crown, the angle of the jaw, the particular stillness of a man who has learned to be looked at. But the eyes were wrong—hollowed out like rooms after a fire, swept clean down to the ash. The Tithe sat behind them the way a spider sits in the center of its web: patient, economical, utterly without hurry.

"You have come far, little oath-keeper," it said, and the king's voice came out layered, like two speakers sharing one throat. "Farther than the last guild did. They sent armies. You sent three children with chalk."

"We sent an oath," Cyrus said. His voice held steadier than his hands. "That was always the difference."

The Tithe tilted the king's head, considering. Around the throne room, the banners of nine generations hung gray and colorless—not faded, Cyrus realized, but *eaten*. Every thread that remembered dye, every weave that recalled the hands that made it, had been drained to feed the thing on the throne. The kingdom was not dying. It was being *forgotten*, one memory at a time, and no one noticed because the forgetting took the remembering too.

"Then let us speak of oaths." The entity rose, and the crown seemed to sink into its brow rather than rest upon it. "You wish to unbind your king. I will permit it. I am old, little keeper, older than your valley, older than your stones, and I am *tired* of this vessel. A king's memories are rich, but they are few—a life so guarded grows repetitive. I have eaten this same day four thousand times." Something moved behind the hollow eyes, and it might have been hunger or might have been grief; with the Tithe, Cyrus could no longer tell the difference. "But nothing is given. Everything is owed. You know the law of it. Your guild wrote the law."

"Equal weight," Cyrus whispered. "A memory for a memory."

"A memory of equal weight," the Tithe corrected, and now the hunger showed plainly, spreading through the king's face like frost across a window. "Not a trifle. Not a name you barely used. I will weigh what you offer, and if it is light, I will take until it is not."

Cyrus closed his eyes.

He had known, walking up the palace stairs with Wren's hand gripping his sleeve, that it would come to this. He had told himself he was prepared. He had lied, and known he was lying, and walked up the stairs anyway.

There was only one memory in him of true weight. There had only ever been one.

His mother's face.

He was seven years old in the memory. The kitchen of their house in Hollowmere, steam rising off the wash-basin, her sleeves rolled to the elbow. She was laughing at something—he could never afterward remember what—and the lamplight caught her face, and she turned toward him, and she said his name, and her smile—

Her smile was the first thing he had ever loved. Before Wren, before Tam, before Master Aldous and the guild and the oath itself, there had been that smile, and it had taught him what warmth was. When the fever took her that winter, he had kept the memory like a coal cupped in both hands, feeding it secretly through every cold year since. It was why he had survived the guild's harshness. It was why he had survived the flight over the boundary stones, the hunters, the lowlands, the capital's teeth. Somewhere beneath everything, always, was a woman turning in lamplight, saying his name, smiling.

"Cyrus." Wren's voice, low and fierce, from behind him. "Whatever it's asking—you don't have to. We'll find another way. We always find another way."

"There is no other way," the Tithe said gently, and the gentleness was the cruelest thing it had done yet. "There never was. This is what your guild understood, child, and why they bound me instead of killing me: some debts can only be paid in what we love. Choose, little keeper. The king's four thousand days, or your mother's one evening. Which weighs more?"

And Cyrus, kneeling in the ruin of a kingdom, understood suddenly that the question was a lie—not because the memories weighed differently, but because they weighed *the same*. That was the trap. That was always the trap. The Tithe fed on the choosing. It wanted him to stand here forever, weighing love against love, while the world forgot itself around him.

So he stopped weighing.

"I give it freely," Cyrus said, and stood, and spoke the words Master Aldous had drilled into him in a dying man's whisper: "*By oath and by hollow, by what is held and what is owed, I unbind.*"

He opened his hands, and the sigil burned through his palm—the guild mark, the bound circle, the shape of a promise kept—and he reached into himself and found the kitchen, the steam, the lamplight, the woman turning, and he gave it.

He did not fight it as it left. That was the secret Aldous had never lived long enough to teach: you cannot clench a gift. You open your fingers. You let the coal fly free.

The Tithe made a sound then. Not triumph. Something smaller and more terrible—a sound like a lock opening after centuries, like a creature setting down a weight it had forgotten it carried. The memory poured into it, and the entity drank, and drank, and for one suspended moment the hollow eyes filled with lamplight, with steam, with a woman laughing at something no one would ever remember, and the Tithe *wept*, one tear down the king's weathered cheek, and said, in a voice that was suddenly, briefly, only one voice:

"Oh. Oh, I had forgotten what it was to be *given* something."

Then the crown slid from the king's brow and rang against the flagstones like a funeral bell, and the Tithe went out of the world—or into it, or through it, Cyrus would wonder all his life—and King Aldric the Third collapsed forward into Cyrus's arms, breathing, blinking, his eyes filling slowly with color and confusion and the unbearable fresh weight of remembering.

"Where—" the king rasped. "How long?"

"Four thousand days," Cyrus said. "Give or take."

* * *

He woke to birdsong and woodsmoke and someone arguing about porridge.

For a moment he did not know where he was, and then the smell of Hollowmere's valley air settled over him—loam and river-stone and the green breath of the boundary hills—and he knew, and did not know how he had come home, and decided it did not matter.

Wren sat on the edge of his cot, sharpening a knife with more force than the knife required. "Three days," she said, when she saw his eyes open. "Three days you slept like the dead, and Tam talked the whole time, and I pretended to be annoyed, and I wasn't. Don't ever do that again."

"The king?"

"Recovering. Rebuilding. Remembering." She smiled crookedly. "He asked about you specifically. Wanted to know what it cost. Tam told him it cost everything, and I told him to shut up, and the king laughed, which I'm told he hasn't done in twenty years."

Cyrus nodded slowly. Something was missing. He felt it the way a tongue finds the gap where a tooth was—an absence with a shape, a hollow exactly the size of a woman laughing in lamplight. He searched for his mother's face and found a warm blur, a lamp, a feeling like sunlight through a window, and no face at all. Grief rose in him, huge and formless, mourning something he could no longer name.

But he was not alone with it. That was the difference. That was everything.

Tam appeared in the doorway with two bowls of porridge and an expression of profound grievance. "He's awake! Finally. Wren, tell him the guildhall roof is fixed and he owes me a week of chores for every day he slept."

"He owes you nothing," Wren said, "and you know it."

They argued. Cyrus lay between them and listened, and the warm blur where his mother's face had been did not hurt quite so much, because around it, filling every other part of him, was this: Wren's sharp voice, Tam's terrible jokes, the smoke of a fire lit in the old guildhall for the first time in a generation.

That evening they gathered in the guildhall—all three of them, plus half of Hollowmere come to gawk, plus a royal messenger bearing the king's seal and a charter restoring the Guild of the Bound Oath to its ancient purpose. Master Aldous's chair, empty for years, they set by the new fire, and no one sat in it, and everyone somehow did.

Cyrus struck the flint himself. The kindling caught, and the flames climbed, and the sigil in his palm glowed gold in the firelight—whole now, unbroken, a promise instead of a wound.

"To the guild," Tam said, raising his bowl of celebratory porridge, because Tam would raise porridge at a coronation.

"To the guild," Wren echoed.

Cyrus opened his mouth to answer—and stopped. Because in the firelight, in the newest flame, he saw something that made his blood run slow and cold: written in the heart of the fire, in letters of shifting ember, a single word in the old guild-tongue that Master Aldous had made him memorize and never explain.

The word was *returned*.

And beneath the guildhall floor, faint but unmistakable, something knocked—once, twice, patiently—from below.
