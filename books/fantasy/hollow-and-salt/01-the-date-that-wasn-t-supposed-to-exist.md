## 1. The Date That Wasn't Supposed to Exist

The fog came off the moor the way it always did in the last hour before dawn, rising from the black water of the Hollow like breath from a sleeping mouth. Beatrix Thackeray walked the circuit she had walked every night for eleven years — down the keeper's stair, across the shingle of grey stone that clicked under her boots like old teeth, and up to the Gate itself.

The Hollow Gate did not look like much, if you didn't know what you were looking at. A natural arch of granite, perhaps forty feet high, framing a view of nothing: no sea beyond it, no valley, only a wall of pale mist that never lifted and never thickened. The villagers of Thornfell called it the Empty Window. Children dared each other to touch it and ran away laughing, certain there was nothing there at all.

Beatrix knew better. She had been raised on better. Her grandmother had taught her to read the seal the way other folk read weather — by the shimmer along the arch's inner curve, by the faint salt-smell that meant the stone was holding, by the silence of the water in the Hollow below, which stayed flat as poured glass so long as the covenant stood. *As long as you can hear nothing*, her grandmother had said, *you are hearing everything hold.*

Tonight, the water was not silent.

It was very faint — a sound beneath sound, like a voice heard through a wall, or a song remembered rather than sung. Beatrix stopped on the shingle and closed her eyes and listened until her jaw ached with listening. There. Under the wind. Under the drip of the seep-springs. A low, patient thrumming, rhythmic as rowing.

Her hand went to the iron key at her belt — the Thackeray key, older than any lock, warm even in this cold because it had been warm for a hundred years and had never once cooled. She pressed her thumb against its worn bow, the way her grandmother had taught her, and felt the answer come back through the metal: a tremor. Small. But wrong. The key had never trembled in her lifetime.

"Routine," she said aloud, to the fog, to herself. "This is a routine patrol."

The fog did not argue. It was the only thing in Thornfell that ever agreed with her.

* * *

She found the cache an hour later, and she would wonder afterward whether it had wanted to be found.

It was tucked into the split face of the cliff below the north watch-crag — a smuggler's hollow, one of dozens pocking the coast road, lined with oilcloth gone stiff as bark. Smugglers had used these caches for generations; the excise men knew them, the keepers knew them, everyone pretended otherwise. Beatrix checked them on her rounds the way she checked the seal: out of duty, mostly out of habit.

But this cache had been opened recently and resealed badly, and when she pulled the oilcloth aside, the smell hit her first — brine and something sweeter underneath, like flowers drowned in seawater. Saltmere's smell. She had never smelled it. She had no right to know it. And yet her body recognized it the way a dog recognizes thunder.

Inside lay a coil of rope woven from kelp-fiber, three bars of greenish silver stamped with the tide-crown of the drowned kingdom, and a book.

Not a book like Thornfell's books. This one was bound in hide that shifted color when she turned it — grey to pearl to the deep bruised blue of deep water. Its pages were soft as cloth and written in two hands: an older script she could not read, all flowing lines like current-marks, and beneath it, in careful human letters, a translation added later. The ink had faded to the brown of dried kelp.

She should have logged it. She should have carried it back to the keeper's house and entered it in the patrol ledger, as her grandmother's ledger instructed: *foreign articles found within sight of the Gate shall be surrendered to the Council of Thornfell.* Eleven years she had followed every rule carved into that book. Eleven years of patrols, of tallow candles burned down over ledgers, of a life shaped entirely around a door that never opened.

She opened the book instead.

The first pages were history — the war, told from the far side. She read of Saltmere's harbors burning above the waterline, of the exiled king who had begged his own priests for a way to end it, of the covenant sworn between realms: *let there be one door, and let it be shut, and let neither side remember how to long for the other.* She read of the forging of the Gate, and of the woman who had forged it — a human smith named Maren Thackeray, who had given the anchor-stone her own name to hold, so that the seal would know whose blood kept it.

*So that the seal would know whose blood kept it.*

Beatrix sat down on the cold stone of the cache floor without deciding to. Her legs simply stopped holding her.

She turned pages faster now. Prophecies, or near enough — the tide-priests' reckonings, translated by some smuggler's careful hand. Most were dated, most were past, most spoke of tides and kings and famines behind the seal. Then, near the end, the handwriting changed. Tighter. Urgent. As though the translator had known exactly which passage mattered.

*In the hundredth year of the shut door,* she read, *the seal will hunger. The name that holds it will thin. On the night the fog rises and does not fall, the gate will crack, and the crack will widen unless a daughter of the forge descends to the anchor-stone and pays the old price.*

Below it, in the same tight hand, someone had written the date.

Not a year. A night. *The third night of the Drowning Moon, in the year the fog rises and does not fall.*

Beatrix looked up through the cache mouth at the sky. The fog had risen at dusk and had not fallen since. Tonight was the third night of the Drowning Moon. She knew this the way she knew her own heartbeat, because the Drowning Moon was the moon of her grandmother's death, and she counted its nights every year like scars.

And beneath the date, in ink darker than the rest, as if pressed hard enough to tear the page:

*Beatrix.*

* * *

She climbed the north watch-crag with the book strapped inside her coat, against her heart, where she could feel it like a second pulse. From the crag she could see the whole Hollow spread below her, silver-grey in the pre-dawn light, and the Gate standing in it like a window left open in a locked house.

The thrumming was louder here. Not beneath the wind anymore. Beside it. Almost matching it.

She took out the key and held it flat on her palm, and watched it shiver against her skin like a trapped moth.

"No," she said quietly. It was not an argument with the key. It was an argument with a century-old page, with a dead translator's pressing hand, with the whole tidy architecture of her life. "I am not a line in someone else's book."

But even as she said it, she understood — with the cold, settling certainty of a woman who has spent her whole life learning one subject — that the book had not made this happen. The book had only known. The seal was failing because a hundred years was what a seal made of sacrifice could bear, and her inheritance had never been the key, or the house, or the title the Council barely bothered to use anymore.

Her inheritance was a countdown. It had been counting since before she was born. Every patrol, every ledger entry, every silent glass-flat morning of her life had been a number falling toward tonight.

The tremor came at first light.

It was small — a shudder through the crag under her boots, a hiss of gravel — but she felt it pass through her bones and out again, and she felt the key go still on her palm, still in the way of a held breath. Below her, along the inner curve of the great granite arch, a hairline of light appeared. Thin as a scratch on glass. Pale as the underside of a wave.

From somewhere beyond the mist — beyond the door that had been shut for a hundred years — came a single clear note, like a bell rung underwater.

Beatrix stood on the crag with the prophecy warm against her ribs and watched the crack in the world, and felt the dread in her chest do something she did not expect. It hardened. It set like cooling iron, taking an edge.

She did not know yet what the old price was. She would learn it soon enough, and it would be worse than she imagined. But standing there, watching the first light of the Drowning Moon's last night bleed into the Hollow, Beatrix Thackeray made the only vow that had ever truly been hers:

Whatever the anchor-stone demanded, she would go down to it. And she would decide for herself what her name was worth.

She turned south, toward the high passes of Thornfell, where the smugglers' roads began — and where, though she could not have known it yet, a man who spoke the drowned tongue in his sleep was already waiting for her to come looking.
