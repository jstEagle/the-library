## 2. Roads Only Smugglers Know

The smuggler's name was Corren Vale, and he walked through Thornfell as though the mountains had been built around him.

Beatrix had followed him out of the gate's shadow before the tremor's dust had even settled, because the prophecy book had named a guide — *the one who walks both roads* — and because her hands were still shaking too badly to hold a map. He had appeared at the treeline within an hour of the seal splitting, as if he'd been waiting for it, and perhaps he had. His coat was oiled leather gone soft with rain, his boots wrong for the mud, and when she asked how much passage would cost, he looked at her the way one looks at a debt already owed.

"Nothing," he said. "You're carrying something I need delivered."

"I'm not a courier."

"No," he agreed, and smiled without warmth. "You're the last Thackeray."

They climbed for two days, and Beatrix learned quickly that everything she knew about Thornfell was a child's knowledge. She had patrolled its edges all her life — the bramble-choked hollows, the shepherd paths that died into scree — but Corren moved through the fells like a man reading a letter written in his own hand. Where she saw rockfall, he saw a door. Where she saw nothing at all, he pressed his palm against a slab of granite veined white with quartz, murmured something under his breath, and the stone swung inward on hinges of root and old intent.

"The roads were cut by the gate-forgers," he said, when she stared. "Your ancestors. They built the back ways so the covenant could be tended from every side. The smugglers only ever borrowed them."

"My family kept no records of that."

"They kept none on purpose." He ducked beneath a low arch hung with pale lichen. "A secret road survives only if no one remembers it exists."

That was the first thing that troubled her: not that he knew the roads, but that he knew *her family*, knew what they had hidden and why, with the ease of a man reciting his own lineage. The second thing came on the second night, in a hollow beneath a fallen rowan where they made a fire small enough to cup in two hands.

She woke near midnight to the sound of him speaking in his sleep.

It was not any tongue she knew, and yet some buried part of her knew it anyway — the way you know a song heard once in childhood. The syllables moved like water over stone, rolling and low, vowels worn smooth as sea-glass. It was beautiful, and it was wrong, and it raised the hair on her arms, because there was exactly one language in all the world that sounded like drowning made into speech.

Saltmere's drowned tongue.

She said nothing that night. In the morning she watched him, and by midday she had stopped pretending not to notice. He greeted a kestrel in it. He counted their remaining bread in it, absently, the way a man counts in his mother's counting-words. And when the pass narrowed above the tarn country and the wind came down off the heights smelling of deep water — impossibly, of *brine*, miles from any sea — he lifted his face into it and closed his eyes like a man greeting a relative.

"You speak Saltish," she said finally. "Fluently. Natively."

Corren didn't turn around. "Do I?"

"Don't do that. Don't insult me. I've spent my whole life guarding a door against that kingdom. I know its voice when I hear it, even asleep." Her hand had found the knife at her belt without her deciding anything. "Who are you?"

He turned then, and whatever mask he wore — the smuggler's easy slouch, the mercenary flatness — came off all at once, like a coat dropped to the ground. What was underneath was younger than she expected and older than his face. Tired in a way that had nothing to do with walking.

"You want the truth?" he said. "Sit down. It takes longer than a knife-draw."

She did not sit. But she did not draw, either.

"My name isn't Corren Vale. Vale is a smuggler's word — it means *the space between*, and I have been the space between for thirty years." He pulled something from inside his coat: a signet on a cord, tarnished black, stamped with a crest she had only ever seen carved into the gate itself — the wave and the crown, intertwined. "I was born Aldric Maren Vael, third son of Sereth Vael, queen of Saltmere. I am the prince your covenant was written to erase."

The wind moved through the pass. Somewhere below, water dripped in the dark, patient as a clock.

"The night the gate was sealed," he went on, "my mother broke it. Not the great seal — she couldn't, no one could — but the small law underneath it. The covenant demanded total severance. Every crossing closed, every tie cut, every child born of either realm barred from the other. And I—" his voice caught, smoothed itself with visible effort "—I was born half of each. My father was a Thackeray."

The world tilted very slightly, the way a boat tilts.

"A Thackeray," Beatrix repeated.

"One of yours. A keeper who loved my mother during the last years of the war, when love between the realms was already treason. When the sealing came, my mother was given a choice: surrender me to the covenant's erasure, or break her oath and send me across before the gate shut forever. She chose me. She wrapped me in oilcloth, gave me to a smuggler, and paid him with her own crown-jewels, and the covenant marked her for it. Saltmere has starved behind that seal for a hundred years, and my mother has been called *oathbreaker* for every one of them — by her own court, by her own priests, in her own tongue." He looked at the signet, turning it over. "They don't know I lived. Or they've forgotten. Exile is a long memory-shortener."

"And your father—"

"He stayed. He tended the gate until he died tending it. Which means—" and here he paused, and Beatrix felt the sentence arrive in her chest before he finished speaking it, "—which means the blood of the forger runs in both of us. You inherited the duty. I inherited the exile. Same forge, different sparks."

She thought of the prophecy book, its century-old ink naming tonight's date and her own name. *The one who walks both roads.* She had assumed it meant a guide. She had not dared assume it meant kin.

"Why help me?" she asked. "If the anchor-stone can reforge the covenant, why does a smuggler care? Why does a prince walk me through the dark for two days instead of taking the book and doing this himself?"

"Because the anchor doesn't answer to me." His jaw tightened. "I've been to it, Beatrix. Twice, in thirty years. It won't open for my blood alone. The covenant was forged by a Thackeray's hand, and it will only be reforged by one. I can bring you to the door. I cannot knock." He met her eyes at last, fully, and there was no performance left in him at all. "And because my people are drowning slowly behind your gate. Not with water. With forgetting. With hunger. With a hundred years of being told the other side of the wall is empty. I want them to see that it never was."

* * *

They reached the tarn on the third morning, and Beatrix understood why no honest map showed it.

It lay in a bowl of stone beneath the highest fell, black and utterly still, ringed by cliffs that held the wind out and the silence in. No birds crossed it. Even the light seemed to land reluctantly. At its center, just beneath the surface, a vast shape glowed faintly — a pale seam of light like a scar of moonstone, long as a ship, and she knew without being told that it was the anchor-stone, sunk here when the covenant was sealed, drowned on purpose so that no one could reach it casually.

Corren stood at the shore and spoke to the water in Saltish, softly, formally, the way one announces oneself at a door. The glow beneath the surface brightened by a single degree.

"It knows you're here," he said. "It's known since the gate split. That's why the tremor came — it's calling its forger home."

Beatrix waded in before she could think better of it. The cold was total, ancient, indifferent; it climbed her body like something taking inventory. Corren followed, and when the water reached their waists and the stone rose beneath them — vast, smooth, warm despite the tarn's cold, warm like a living thing — she laid her palm flat against it.

The stone answered.

Not in words. In *memory given back*: her father's hands on the gate-bar; her grandmother's voice reciting the covenant's terms; and beneath those, older, stranger things — a wedding on a salt shore, a crown set on wet hair, a woman's scream swallowed by closing water. The covenant's whole ledger, opening.

And then the offer came, clear as a bell struck underwater:

*A Thackeray's hand may reforge the seal. The price is remembrance. Surrender every memory you hold of love, and the gate will close for another hundred years.*

Beside her, Corren went rigid. "Say yes," he said quietly, urgently, and his hand closed over hers on the stone. "Beatrix — say yes. Whatever it shows you, say yes. My kingdom is dying behind that seal. One life's memories against a nation's survival — that is not even a bargain, it's arithmetic."

But the stone was still showing her the ledger, and in the ledger she had just seen something Corren had not: the record of his mother's broken oath, and beside it, in letters of light, the covenant's own confession — *severance breeds hunger, and hunger breeds breach.* The old bargain had failed before. It had failed for a hundred years, precisely as designed.

She looked at Corren — at the prince who had walked her here, whose mother had paid a crown to save him, whose people were starving for the sin of remembering — and she understood that the stone was not offering her a choice between sacrifice and cowardice.

It was offering her the same lie it had always told.

"No," Beatrix said.

The tarn went silent enough to hear her heartbeat.

"The price is wrong," she said, louder, her palm pressing harder against the warm stone. "You ask for memory surrendered. I will give you memory *exchanged*. Reforge the covenant not as a wall but as a bridge — bound to shared remembrance, both realms keeping each other in mind, neither able to forget the other again. That is the term. Take it or find another Thackeray, and there isn't one."

For a long moment nothing moved — not the water, not the light, not the man beside her, who was staring at her as though watching a door open that he had knocked on for thirty years.

Then the anchor-stone began, very slowly, to sing.
