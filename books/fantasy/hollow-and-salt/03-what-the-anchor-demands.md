## 3. What the Anchor Demands

The tarn lay beneath them like a held breath.

Beatrix had known Thornfell's highest lake all her life from maps and her grandmother's warnings — *the water that remembers* — but no map had prepared her for the color of it at dusk: not blue, not grey, but a deep bruised green, the green of things drowned long enough to become legend. The mountains ringed it close, their slopes still wearing the last rags of winter snow, and the wind came down off them carrying the smell of iron and old ice.

Corren stood at the water's edge, and he had stopped pretending.

He had stopped pretending somewhere on the third day of their journey, when she caught him murmuring to a stream in a language like water talking to itself — syllables that rose and fell the way tides do, that seemed to bend the current gently toward him as though greeting an old friend. She had drawn her knife then, and he had turned, and instead of denying it he had simply said, in that same drowned tongue, *I am sorry you had to learn this way.*

The prince of Saltmere. Alive. Walking beside her through smugglers' passes for six days while his kingdom starved behind a seal his own bloodline had forged.

"You could have told me," she said now, watching the light die on the tarn.

"I did tell you. Every time I spoke its name too softly. Every time I flinched when you described the gate." He pulled off his boots, rolled his trousers to the knee. "You would have turned back if you'd known what I was. And you cannot turn back. Not tonight."

"The prophecy says a Thackeray reforges the covenant. It doesn't say anything about a prince leading her by the hand."

"No," he said quietly. "It wouldn't. Prophecies are written by people who only see the shape of a thing, never its cost." He waded into the shallows without waiting for her, and where his feet touched the water, it *glowed* — faintly, phosphorescent, the way the sea does in Saltmere's paintings, the paintings Beatrix had only ever seen in forbidden books. "Come. The anchor is under the center. You have to stand on it. That is how the old rites were done — keeper standing on the stone, the realm pressing up against her from below."

"And what presses down?"

"Everything you love," Corren said. "That's the price, Beatrix. You know that already. You've known since you read the book."

She had. The prophecy volume had been explicit in its cold, careful script: *The gate fails when the moon stands twice-born. A daughter of the forge must kneel upon the anchor and surrender every memory in which love resides — mother, father, friend, first snow, first song — for a covenant built on devotion must be paid in devotion entire.* Her grandmother had died keeping that secret. Perhaps her grandmother had known, in her last years, that the debt would fall to Beatrix, and had said nothing out of mercy.

Beatrix pulled off her own boots. The water was colder than any water had a right to be — cold like a hand closing around her ankle, cold like the deep places of the world reaching up to take inventory. She waded after him, and the glow spread around her legs too, answering something in her blood, the forger's blood, the Thackeray inheritance she had never asked for and never once been allowed to forget.

The bottom shelved away faster than she expected. By ten paces from shore the water was at her chest, and by fifteen she was swimming, or something near it, the tarn pulling her downward with a gentleness that frightened her more than violence would have. Below her, growing out of the dark like the root of the world itself, she saw it.

The anchor-stone.

It was vast — a pillar of black rock wider than her cottage had been, furred with pale weed, carved over every surface with script so old the letters had begun to melt back into the stone. Warmth radiated from it. In a lake fed by snowmelt, in water cold enough to stop a heart, the stone was warm as a sleeping animal, and it *pulled*, and Beatrix let it pull her, down past the light, down into the green-black quiet where the drowned kingdom pressed against the underside of the world.

Her feet found the stone's crown. She stood, and the water fell away from her face as though the lake itself had decided she should breathe, and she stood dry upon the anchor in an airless hollow at the bottom of Thornfell's highest tarn, with Corren treading water above her like a man hovering at the threshold of a house he'd been banished from.

Then the stone spoke.

It did not use words, exactly. It used *memory*. The warmth rose through her feet and became her mother's kitchen, bread and woodsmoke; became her father lifting her onto the wall beside the Hollow Gate, saying *this is yours now, little warden, guard it well*; became Tam Ashlar laughing in the rain the summer they were both seventeen; became her grandmother's hands teaching hers the forge-signs, the sealing-words, the long slow discipline of keeping. Every memory arrived at once, luminous and unbearable, and the stone held them up before her like a merchant displaying wares.

*These,* the stone said, in a voice that was all of them at once. *These are the payment. Give them, and the covenant holds another hundred years. Refuse, and the gate opens unbound, and the war comes again, and the drowning comes again, and everything you love drowns with it.*

"There," Corren said hoarsely, from above. His voice cracked across the hollow. "There it is. Pay it, Beatrix. Please. My people are eating kelp-bread ground with sand. The children are born grey. A hundred more years behind that seal and there will be no Saltmere left to save — pay it, pay it, and I will carry your memory of the price myself, I will remember for both of us—"

"You can't," she said. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her. "That's the cruelty of it, don't you see? If I give up every memory of love, I won't remember why I paid. I'll stand on this stone a stranger to my own sacrifice. Your mother broke the covenant to save you, Corren. Out of love. And the old terms would make me erase the very thing that ever made anyone break a seal for anyone else." She looked down at the script melting across the stone, the letters of the first bargain, worn soft by a century of silence. "The terms are wrong. They were always wrong. A covenant of separation built on forgetting — no wonder it rotted."

"Beatrix—" His voice was raw now. "If you're wrong, the war returns. If you're wrong, my father's fleet burns your coast. If you're wrong—"

"Then I'll be wrong on purpose," she said, "which is more than the stone has ever been."

She drew her knife.

The blade was Thornfell steel, forged above, but her blood was forge-blood, and when she opened her palm along the old sealing-line her grandmother had shown her — the line every Thackeray carried in the skin like a signature waiting to be signed — the stone shuddered beneath her feet.

"Not surrendered," she said, pressing her bleeding hand flat against the warm black rock. "*Exchanged.* Hear me, anchor. Reforge the covenant not as a wall but as a bridge. Bind it to shared remembrance. Let both realms keep each other in memory — humans remembering Saltmere, Saltmere remembering the shore — neither able to forget the other again, neither able to pretend the other doesn't starve or drown or grieve. Memory for memory. Love for love. That is the term. Take it, or find another Thackeray."

"There isn't one," Corren whispered. "There isn't one, and she knows it."

The stone went still. The whole tarn went still enough to hear her heartbeat.

"The price is wrong," she said louder, her palm pressing harder against the warm stone. "You ask for memory surrendered. I will give you memory *exchanged*. Reforge the covenant not as a wall but as a bridge — bound to shared remembrance, both realms keeping each other in mind, neither able to forget the other again. That is the term. Take it or find another Thackeray, and there isn't one."

For a long moment nothing moved — not the water, not the light, not the man beside her, who was staring at her as though watching a door open that he had knocked on for thirty years.

Then the anchor-stone began, very slowly, to sing.

It was not a sound so much as a *remembering* — a chord struck in the roots of the mountains, in the keels of sunken ships, in the bell-towers of a drowned city where children had been born grey and hungry for a hundred years. The script on the stone blazed, and the old letters did not vanish; they *unlocked*, rearranging themselves, the word *separation* dissolving letter by letter into the word *remembrance*, the ancient bargain rewriting itself around her blood as though it had been waiting a century for someone stubborn enough to dictate new terms.

Above her, the water began to rise in a slow spiral, moonlight knifing down through it, and far off — impossibly far, down through rock and root and the floor of the world — Beatrix heard bells.

Saltmere's bells. Ringing for the first time in a hundred years.

And beneath the joy in them, unmistakable, a question: *who is calling? who has opened the door?*

Corren surfaced beside her, weeping openly, salt water and fresh water indistinguishable on his face. "They hear it," he breathed. "Beatrix — they *hear* it. My mother's people. They're coming to the gate."

The stone sang on, and the tarn drained around them in a widening circle of light, and Beatrix understood, with the calm of a woman whose life had just ended and begun in the same heartbeat, that the hardest part was still ahead:

Now both realms would remember each other.

And neither of them would ever be the same.
