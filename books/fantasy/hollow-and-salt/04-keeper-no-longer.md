## 4. Keeper No Longer

The gate stood open.

Beatrix had walked the cliff path to it every morning for three weeks now, and every morning her body expected resistance — the old iron weight of the seal against her palm, the hum of the covenant in her teeth like a held breath. Every morning she found instead a doorway: two pillars of pale stone veined with salt-silver, and between them a shimmer like heat over summer road, through which the drowned kingdom breathed its slow tide-light into the human air.

She did not touch it anymore. There was nothing to hold shut. That was the trouble.

Below the gate, on the human side of the threshold, the joint wardens were already at their morning work. Two of Saltmere's tide-priests knelt at the base of the eastern pillar, their grey-blue robes dark with spray, singing the low rolling notes that kept the passage's edges soft and stable. Opposite them, at the western pillar, stood Warden Ellery of Thornfell — young, earnest, promoted from Beatrix's own patrol roster — checking the anchor-chains with the same ritual gestures Beatrix had performed ten thousand times. He was doing it wrong. Not dangerously wrong; just wrong enough that the chains would chafe by winter. She opened her mouth to call down a correction, caught herself, and closed it again.

Not your post. Not your title. Not your duty.

The words had become a litany, and like all litanies they wore smooth the thing they repeated until she could no longer feel its edges. She turned from the gate and walked back along the cliff, past the keeper's cottage where she had been born, where her mother had died, where every cup and blanket and whetstone still knew her hands — and which now belonged, by the terms of the new accord, to the wardens' rotation. Her name had been struck from the rolls with the same ceremony that had once written it there. Keeper of the Hollow Gate: the line ended with her. It was supposed to feel like freedom.

It felt like standing in an empty room that still remembered the shape of furniture.

* * *

Corren found her at the Salt Cellar, the smugglers' inn below Thornfell village, where she had taken a room because it was the only place in the valley where no one looked at her with either pity or reverence. The smugglers, at least, had the decency to treat her as a woman who drank too much and paid promptly.

He came in wearing his court clothes, which was itself an offense — sea-grey silk shot with pearl, a prince's collar, salt-crusted boots he had never quite learned to replace. Half the room went quiet. Corren Vale, lost prince of Saltmere, returned king-in-waiting, drinking in a smuggler's den among the people who had once ferried his family's contraband. He sat across from her without asking, as he always did, and pushed a plate of smoked eel toward her, as he always did.

"You're watching the wardens again," he said.

"I'm watching Ellery ruin the anchor-chains."

"Then tell him."

"He has wardens for that. I have—" She gestured vaguely at herself, at the table, at the whole unstructured expanse of her days. "I have nothing."

"You have a kingdom's gratitude and a place at my court whenever you want it." He said it lightly, but she heard the weight underneath, the way she always did. "Chancellor of the crossings, if you like titles. Advisor. Anything. My mother asks after you. The tide-priests ask after you. Half of Saltmere thinks you're a saint and the other half thinks you're a story, and both halves would fall over themselves to give you a chair with your name carved on it."

"And what would I do in the chair?"

"Advise. Preside. Be honored." He leaned forward. "Beatrix. You gave up everything to open that gate. You should have something to show for it."

She looked at him for a long moment — this man who had lied to her on the hidden roads, who had spoken the drowned tongue when he thought she slept, who had begged her at the anchor-stone to pay the old price because his kingdom was starving behind the seal. Who had wept, afterward, when she refused and bound the covenant to shared memory instead, because he understood before she did what it meant: that his people would remember the war too, all of it, the griefs flowing both directions across the threshold forever. He had called it madness. Then he had knelt in the tarn water beside her and added his blood to hers, because a prince's oath needed witnessing, and he had never once regretted it aloud.

"I didn't give up everything," she said finally. "That's rather the point. I refused to give up anything. And now everyone keeps trying to hand me things back, and none of them fit."

"What fits, then?"

She turned her cup slowly on the table. Through the Salt Cellar's low window she could see the coast road, and on it — this was still new enough to make her chest ache — a Saltmere fishwife walking openly toward the village market, her basket of glass-kelp glinting, a Thornfell child trotting beside her chattering questions about the drowned city. Trade caravans twice a week now. Pilgrims. Letters crossing the threshold sealed with wax that smelled of brine.

"Roads," Beatrix said. "The hidden ones. The smugglers' passes through Thornfell, the drowned causeways under the tarns, the old paths nobody's mapped since the war sealed them. Corren, do you know how many people want to cross now? Farmers, traders, families with kin on both sides — and half of them are afraid to take the open gate because it's *ceremony*, it's priests and protocols and wardens asking names. But the back ways—" She stopped, hearing herself, and laughed shortly. "I sound like a smuggler."

"You sound like a Thackeray," Corren said quietly. "Your family built the gate. Maybe the last one gets to build what comes after."

* * *

She started with the roads she already knew, drawing by lamplight in the keeper's cottage kitchen — which Ellery had insisted she keep using, whatever the rolls said, because "the maps live here, and so does the stove that doesn't smoke." The old smuggling routes first: Hollowpass and the Kestrel Stair, the fen-causeway, the thirty-two doors in the cliffs that Corren's people had used for a century of quiet traffic. Then the drowned roads, which required Corren, who came out three evenings a week in his ridiculous court silks and waded into tarn water up to his ribs while she sketched from the bank, calling measurements, arguing about scale.

"You've got the Sunken Mile wrong," he said one night, dripping, pointing at her chart. "It bends east after the second marker-stone. It always bent east. We used to race sledges down it."

"Then it's changed since the covenant broke. The water's rearranging the old paths." She made the correction, then sat back and looked at what was accumulating across her table: not one map but a network, a living web of passages threading two realms together, each one annotated in her tight hand — depth, current, season, danger, and, in a column she hadn't planned, *who might use it*: a cart with goods, a family on foot, a pilgrim alone. Roads only smugglers know, becoming roads anyone can walk.

The thought arrived without drama, the way true things do: *this is the work.* Not guarding. Not sealing. Not even opening — the gate was open now, and would stay open whether she lifted a finger or not. The work was the between. The connective tissue. The thousand small paths that let ordinary lives flow where armies and covenants had once forbidden them.

She was still holding that thought when Corren, wringing out his sleeves by the fire, said casually: "There's a road you don't have yet. Far north, past the tarns — my mother's letters mention a crossing the old kings used, a land route, not water. If it exists, it changes everything for the northern villages. No one's walked it since the war."

Beatrix looked up from her charts. Outside, the autumn wind carried the smell of salt off the open gate, three miles distant and closer than it had ever been.

"When do we leave?" she said.
