## 1. The Date That Wasn't There

The sealed archive of the Hollow Guild smelled of dust and tallow and the particular sweetness of old vellum, a smell Lena Vane had loved since she was six years old, when Master Orren first lifted her over the threshold and told her that everything the valley had ever hidden from itself lived in these shelves.

She was twenty-two now, and she knew better than to love anything down here. The archive did not reward affection. It rewarded patience, precision, and the willingness to catalog things that made your hands shake.

Tonight it was the Vellum Codex.

It sat on the iron reading stand like something sleeping—a book bound in hide so pale it might have been skin, its spine cracked into ridges like an old man's knuckles. No guild mark. No accession number. It predated the ledger system entirely, which meant it predated the guild's founding, which meant, by every rule Lena had been taught, it should not have been in the Hollow's keeping at all.

*Older than us,* she thought, turning the first page with a bone spatula, careful as a surgeon. *Older than the bargain.*

The valley of Sigil had been invisible for one hundred and twelve years. Every child learned the number the way children elsewhere learned prayers: the year the founders drew the Veil, the year the world forgot them, the year the wars passed them by like weather around a stone. The guild kept the Veil. The guild kept the silence. The guild kept *them*, safe and small and magicless, because magicless was what safety cost.

Lena had never questioned it. Questioning was not among her duties. Her duties were ink, ledgers, and the slow rescue of crumbling documents from their own decay.

She copied the codex's opening line into her working journal by lamplight:

*What is hidden was bought. What was bought will be called due.*

A nice bit of antique melodrama, she'd have said that morning. She said nothing now. Her quill hovered above the page, and her heart had begun to do something strange and arrhythmic, like a bird trapped in a closed room.

Because beneath the opening line, in a different hand—newer, though still centuries old—someone had written a date.

She read it three times before she let herself understand it.

*The ninth night of the Harvest Dark.*

Tonight.

Not tonight-as-in-a-night-like-tonight. Tonight. This night, this hour, this cold autumn evening with frost feathering the archive's single high window. The ninth of the Harvest Dark fell once a year, yes—but the codex gave a year as well, in the old reckoning, the count-from-the-Veil that only archivists still used. Lena converted it twice to be sure. Once in her head. Once on scrap vellum, her hand unsteady enough that she pressed too hard and tore the corner.

The year was this one.

Her mouth had gone dry. She lit a second lamp, then a third, telling herself it was for the fine work of transcription, knowing perfectly well it was because the shadows between the shelves had begun to feel inhabited.

*Coincidence,* she told herself. *A scribe's error. A prophecy misdated by a century, or misread by me.* Prophecies were the archive's most embarrassing holdings—she'd catalogued dozens, all of them wrong, all of them safely dead. The guild did not believe in prophecy. The guild believed in wards, ledgers, and silence.

But the codex was not a guild book. The codex had come from *before*, from whatever world the founders had fled, and its prophecies had a different texture. They didn't predict. They *scheduled*.

She turned the page.

*On the ninth night of the Harvest Dark, in the hundred-and-twelfth year, the ink shall run wet, and the Veil shall thin, and the hunters shall cross where no hunter has crossed. Let the reader beware: what follows was written before the valley was hollowed, and cannot be unwritten, only answered.*

Lena sat back. The stool creaked. Somewhere overhead, the guildhall settled against the cold with a groan like an old man turning in his sleep.

*The ink shall run wet.*

Absurd. She almost laughed. And then, because she was an archivist, because she could no more leave a text unverified than she could stop her own heartbeat, she leaned close to the date itself and looked.

The letters were brown-black, iron-gall, flaked at the edges exactly as they should be after four hundred years. Except—

Except at the tail of the final numeral, where the ink shone. Just slightly. Just the way fresh ink shines under lamplight in the moment before it sinks.

She fetched the magnifying lens from her kit with hands that felt borrowed. Held it steady. Watched.

For a long time, nothing. Long enough that she began composing the report to Master Orren in her head, embarrassed, relieved: *a trick of the light, Master, a varnish residue, I'll test for resin in the morning—*

And then the ink moved.

Not flowed. Not bled. It *darkened*, the way a bruise darkens, the shine gathering at the numeral's tail and sinking inward, wet going matte, fresh going old, four centuries of drying happening in the space of a single breath—and Lena realized, with a lurch like a floor giving way, that she had caught it backwards. The ink hadn't just dried. It had *finished*. As if the writing had been waiting, suspended mid-stroke for hundreds of years, and tonight—this night, the ninth of the Harvest Dark—it had finally been allowed to complete itself.

As if the book had known she would be here to see.

She stood up so fast the stool went over. The clatter was enormous in the silent vault. She didn't pick it up. She backed away from the stand, one step, two, her heel striking a shelf and rattling a row of ledger spines, and the codex lay open in the lamplight, patient, finished, *satisfied*.

"Master Orren," she said aloud, and her voice came out small. "I need Master Orren."

* * *

He came down within the quarter hour, wrapped in his grey robe, silver hair loose, moving with the unhurried certainty that had governed Lena's entire life. Master Orren had found her begging at the Veil's edge when she was five. He had raised her, schooled her, shaped her. When other children asked what they wanted to be, Lena had never needed to ask; she was the guild's, and the guild was good, and goodness was a thing you served without questioning.

He looked at the open codex for a long moment. Then he looked at her, and something in his face made her stomach drop—not alarm, not wonder, but a kind of weary recognition, the look of a man finding a bill he had long expected.

"You weren't meant to reach the Vellum Codex until next spring," he said quietly.

The words landed strangely. "The rotation put it in my hands tonight."

"Yes." He exhaled through his nose. "Yes, it did."

"Master—the date. You saw it. The ink was wet. It's *tonight*, it names tonight, it says the Veil thins and hunters cross and—" She stopped, because he had raised one hand, gently, the way he did when a child recited a lesson imperfectly.

"Lena." His voice was kind. Kindness from Orren had always frightened her more than anger; anger meant you could still be corrected. "Do you know why we call ourselves the Hollow Guild?"

"Because we keep the valley hollow. Empty of magic. Empty of notice."

"Because we are hollow," he said. "We emptied ourselves so the valley could be filled with quiet. That was the bargain our founders struck, a hundred and twelve years ago, and the price has never changed, and the payment has never been ours to renegotiate." He reached out and closed the codex, both hands flat on the pale cover, as if pressing down a lid on a well. "This book is not a record, child. It is a *term sheet*. And terms do not concern the person who reads them. They concern the parties who signed."

"Then who signed?" Her voice cracked on it. "Who signed for us? For *me*?"

Orren was silent for a moment. In the lamplight his face looked suddenly very old, and very tired, and—for the first time in seventeen years—ashamed.

"That," he said, "is not a question you want answered tonight."

"It's the only question I have."

He straightened. Whatever had softened in him folded away again, neat as a letter being sealed. "Then hear the only answer that matters. The Veil holds because it has always held. Tomorrow it will hold again. What you saw was vellum and old ink and a tired girl alone in a cold room seeing ghosts. You will take the codex to the furnace room, and you will burn it, and you will enter nothing in any ledger, and in the morning you will feel foolish and grateful, which is the natural condition of the young."

"Burn it." She stared at him. "You're ordering me to burn a book older than the guild. You've spent your whole life teaching me that burning is the one unforgivable act—"

"I know what I've taught you." Something flickered behind his eyes—grief, or fear, or both wearing each other's clothes. "That is precisely why I am asking you to trust that I have reasons older than my teachings. Burn it, Lena. Please."

*Please.* Orren did not say please. Orren had never once said please. The word hung in the cold air between them, and Lena understood, with terrible clarity, that it was not a request. It was a test of obedience, and everything she had ever been to him depended on how she answered it.

Her whole life rose up in front of her: the orphan lifted over the threshold, the lessons, the duties, the small warm certainties of a hidden valley where nothing bad had ever happened because nothing was ever allowed to *arrive*. And underneath all of it, for the first time, she heard the other sound—the sound of a plan not hers, drawn before her birth, scheduled to the night, executed with her as its instrument. *You weren't meant to reach the Vellum Codex until next spring.* Someone had arranged even her curiosity. Even her hands.

"No," she said.

The word came out steadier than she felt. Orren closed his eyes briefly, as if she had confirmed something he'd hoped to be wrong about.

"The Veil is already thinning," she went on, and her own voice sounded foreign to her, young and hard and awake. "I watched four-hundred-year-old ink dry itself tonight, Master. If the book is lying, burning it costs us nothing but shame. If it isn't—if the hunters really do cross tomorrow—then I'd rather know what we're facing than die polite." She stepped forward and took the codex out from under his hands. It was lighter than it should have been, warm, like a living thing freshly woken. "I'm entering it in the ledger. Full entry. My hand, my name."

Orren looked at her for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice had gone quiet and distant, the voice of a man speaking across a distance wider than the room.

"Your name," he repeated softly. "Yes. I imagine it was always going to come to that."

He turned and climbed the stairs without another word, and his footsteps faded into the guildhall above, and Lena stood alone in the lamplight with a book that had waited four centuries for her.

Outside the high window, beyond the frost, beyond the dark hills, the night sky over the valley's rim flickered—just once, just faintly—like a ward remembering, somewhere far away, that it had an expiry date.

Lena blew out two of the lamps, tucked the codex beneath her cloak against her heart, and began, very quietly, to pack.
