## 2. What the Valley Cannot Hold

The bells woke her before the wards did.

That was the first wrongness, though Lena Vane would not understand it until later, running through the dark with a book pressed to her chest like a second heartbeat. In Sigil, the wards always spoke first—a low hum through the standing stones at the valley's mouth, a shiver in the well water, the bees going quiet in their hives. The guild had a hundred ways of knowing when something touched the border. The bells were for after. The bells meant the knowing had failed.

She was out of bed and pulling on her boots before the third toll. The prophecy book was already wrapped in oilcloth beneath her floorboard; she had not slept since Corwin's replacement—no, since Master Ellery himself—had stood over her in the archive and ordered the thing burned. She had obeyed, of course. She was guild-born, guild-raised; obedience ran in her like the cold water in the wells. But she had obeyed slowly, and she had obeyed stupidly, feeding the fire a dummy volume from the decoy shelf while the true book lay against her ribs now, its pages still faintly damp where tonight's date had been inked in a hand that had died a century before she was born.

*The wards fail on this night,* the old script said. *And what the valley cannot hold, it must release.*

She had thought it poetry. Prophecy was always poetry until it wasn't.

Outside, the night was the wrong color. Sigil's sky never showed stars—the concealment bent even that light into a soft, starless velvet—but tonight there were stars, hard and white and terrible, more of them than any map in the archive described, as if the valley had been holding its breath for a hundred years and had finally exhaled everything it had been hiding. Lena stood on the doorstep with her cloak half-fastened and felt the world get larger around her, and it did not feel like freedom. It felt like being seen.

Down in Hollowmere, the village proper, lights were blooming window by window. The bell tower kept tolling—not the ward-bell's measured warning but the alarm, fast and arrhythmic, the way it rang only in the oldest stories, the ones the guild taught children were exaggerations.

Lena ran.

* * *

Master Ellery met her at the archive door, and he looked old. He had looked old for years, but tonight the age had come all at once, the way frost comes to a field overnight. His hands shook as he handed her the ward-roster, the ledger of every stone and spring and buried rune-cord that made the valley invisible, and his voice, when he spoke, was the voice of a man reading his own obituary.

"The northern cord is dead," he said. "Has been since midnight. I've walked the line twice. There's nothing to mend because there's nothing left—it didn't break, Lena. It *ended*. Like a debt coming due."

He would not look at her when she asked him the obvious question, the one she had been carrying since the ink stayed wet under her thumb: whether the guild had known. Whether the date in the prophecy book was a warning or an appointment. Whether the fire he'd ordered was meant to destroy a threat or destroy the evidence that they had always known, that the whole dying order had been living inside someone else's countdown, keeping the valley safe not forever but *until*, and no one had told the children of the guild what the *until* meant.

Ellery's silence was answer enough. It was worse than a lie. A lie she could have hated him for.

"Take the west path," he said instead, hoarse. "Down past the mill, over the ford. If anyone asks—" his voice cracked, "—if anyone comes asking after you, you were never cataloguing anything. You found nothing. Do you understand me? Nothing."

It was the first time in her life a master of the Hollow Guild had ever instructed her to have found nothing, and some cold, clear part of her understood that this instruction was the most honest thing he had ever given her: proof that finding things was dangerous, that knowledge in Sigil had always been rationed like bread in a famine, and that the rationing had just failed along with everything else.

Then the inquisitors came through the pass, and there was no more time for understanding anything.

* * *

They came down the northern road in grey cloaks, six of them, walking at the pace of men who had never once been turned away, and the valley let them. That was the horror of it—not violence, not yet, but permission. For a hundred years nothing uninvited had crossed the border of Sigil. The wards had turned back wolves and tax collectors and once, according to legend, a small army. Tonight the stones at the valley's mouth might as well have been garden ornaments. The inquisitors walked between them the way water fills a crack, without hurry, without resistance, and behind them the stars kept burning in a sky that should have hidden them.

Lena saw them from the ridge above the mill, flattened against the wet grass with the oilcloth bundle digging into her sternum. Grey cloaks. Iron masks with narrow eye-slits, the visors worked into the shape of weeping faces—she knew the make, everyone in the free marches knew the make, the usurper's inquisitors wore grief as armor. They carried long hooked staves and lanterns with shuttered flames, and they moved through Hollowmere's outlying farms like men taking inventory, marking doors with chalk sigils she did not recognize, dragging two of the miller's sons out of a barn and binding their wrists with something that glowed dull red in the dark.

One of them stopped at the boundary stone nearest the ford. He put his gloved hand flat against it, the way you'd check a fever, and then he laughed. Lena heard it from forty yards up the slope, a single dry note, and it went through her worse than any scream could have. It was the laugh of a man confirming a rumor. *It's true,* the laugh said. *The invisible valley is visible. It can be taken.*

She should have run west immediately. Every instinct Ellery had drilled into her said run, say nothing, be nothing, let the valley's old magic do what it could no longer do. Instead she lay in the wet grass and watched, because some stubborn piece of her needed to see it with her own eyes: home, being found. Home, which had been safe the way a locked room is safe, which had never once in her nineteen years shown her its locks, now standing open with its insides showing, and the insides were people—her people—the tanner's widow, the beekeeper's twins, old Maren who had taught her to read runes on summer evenings, all of them spilling out of their houses into the lanes with torches, confused, frightened, asking each other questions nobody could answer: *What's happening? Why can they see us? Who let them in?*

Nobody had let them in. That was the thing Lena knew and they didn't, the thing pressing against her ribs through the oilcloth. Nobody had opened the door. The door had simply finished closing, the way a lease finishes, the way a bargain finishes, and whatever the founders of the Hollow Guild had traded a century ago to keep this valley hidden, the payment had come due at midnight, and no one alive had known the terms except perhaps the dead, and the dead had written them down in a book the guild had tried to burn.

*What the valley cannot hold, it must release.*

She got up and ran west, and she did not look back again, because looking back had started to feel like a luxury purchased with other people's blood.

* * *

The ford was swollen with snowmelt and the crossing was bad, and halfway across it became worse than bad, because halfway across she found him.

A man, lying in the shallows among the stones, face-down, half-submerged, his left leg twisted under him at an angle legs don't go. He was bleeding from somewhere high on his thigh, the water around him clouded pink, and he was conscious—she could tell because he was gripping a rock with both hands, white-knuckled, refusing to let the current take him, which was either courage or the purest stubbornness she had ever witnessed.

Later, much later, she would learn his name was Tam and that he had come over the mountains alone, three days ahead of the inquisitors, following a map he claimed had been drawn by a dead king's loyalists. Later still she would learn that almost none of what he told her that night was the whole truth. But kneeling in the freezing ford with the water shoving at her knees and the alarm bell tolling behind her and the oilcloth bundle held above the surface in both hands like an offering, all she knew was this: he was wounded, he was drowning by inches, and he was not from here. No one from Sigil dressed in road-leather that fine or carried a sword with a king's cipher ground off the pommel but not quite erased.

"Help me," he said, or tried to; his mouth was full of river. What actually came out was less than that, a broken syllable, and then his grip on the rock slipped.

Lena made her choice in about a second and a half, which surprised her, because she had spent her whole life being the careful one, the youngest, the one who checked the latch twice. She dropped the book's strap over her shoulder, hauled his arm across her neck, and pulled. He was heavier than he looked—all of them are, the drowning—and the current fought her for every foot of the far bank, and twice she went down to her shoulders, and both times the thought that kept her moving was not heroism but anger, white and specific: *they do not get to have all of us. Not the inquisitors, not the usurper, not the guild, not the prophecy. Someone in this valley gets to decide who is saved, and tonight, for once, it's going to be me.*

She dragged him onto the shingle below the willows and got him propped against a root, and he coughed up half the ford and looked at her with eyes that were too steady for a drowning man, and said, in a voice scraped raw:

"You're guild."

Not a question. He'd seen the knot of her sash, the way she'd tied it—guild-trained hands tied things a certain way, tight and doubled, habits beaten in by masters who believed slovenly knots invited sloppy deaths.

"I'm leaving the guild," she said, which was not the same thing, and was also, she was beginning to understand, exactly the same thing.

"Good." He laughed, wet and awful, and pressed his hand harder against his thigh. "Because I came here to find you. Or—to find *someone* like you. The last of them. The keeper." He fumbled at his collar, dragging out a leather packet soaked nearly through, and from it a scrap of vellum, soft as cloth from handling, marked with a seal she had only ever seen in the archive's oldest plates: the sun-and-thorn, the mark of King Aldric, the king who was not supposed to matter anymore, the king whose name appeared in the prophecy book on the same page as tonight's date, in the same faded brown ink.

"I serve the true king," Tam said, and then his eyes rolled back and he slid down the root, and Lena caught him mostly by accident, mostly because she was closest.

Behind them, upstream, torchlight had reached the ford. Voices. The chalk-scratch of inquisitors' markers on stone. And in her arms a bleeding stranger who knew the word *keeper*, who had crossed a mountain range to find a girl he had never met, in a valley that had just stopped being invisible, on the exact night a century-old book had named.

Lena looked at the vellum seal, and at the darkening water, and at the bundle against her chest that Master Ellery had wanted burned, and understood—with the strange, ringing clarity that arrives only when everything else has broken—that there was no longer any such place as home to return to. There was only forward, and the book, and whatever the words inside it had been waiting a hundred years to make her do.

She got Tam's arm over her shoulder again, and she stood up, and she carried him into the trees.

* * *

They made maybe four miles before dawn, most of them hers. Tam could stagger when she propped him against things and collapse when she didn't, and by the time the sky went grey he had gone from heavy to feverish, muttering in a language she didn't know and then, worse, in one she did—old guild cant, the archivists' shorthand, words like *cordage* and *witness-stone* and *the ninth binding* tumbling out of him in a delirium that made no sense, because a king's soldier should not know the guild's private vocabulary. She filed that away with everything else she was filing away, in the growing room inside her where doubt lived now, permanent as furniture.

They sheltered in a shepherd's hut above the tree line, one room, a hearth she dared not light. Through the gap in the shutters she could see back down into the valley, and she made herself look, because Ellery had taught her that a coward's blindness gets people killed and she refused to be blind on her first morning outside the safety she'd been born inside.

Hollowmere was still standing. That was the mercy. Smoke rose from chimneys; the bell had stopped; life, grotesquely, went on. But the inquisitors' chalk marks glowed pale on the doors, six-pointed and precise, and at the valley's mouth, where the standing stones had guarded the border for a hundred years, men in grey were raising something new—a post, a gallows-frame, tall enough to be seen from the ridges, patient as winter. Not a gallows, she realized, studying it until her eyes ached. A frame for a banner. They were going to fly the usurper's colors over Sigil. They were going to *name* it. Everything the valley had been—hidden, unnamed, outside all maps—was being written into someone else's ledger while she watched.

Tam stirred, and surfaced from the fever enough to speak clearly, and chose his words with the care of a man who knows he has one audience and one chance.

"They'll burn the archive," he said. "Whatever your masters haven't already burned. And then they'll burn the keepers, gently, one at a time, starting with the youngest, because the youngest talk." His eyes found hers and held them, fever-bright. "You have the book. You're the last one holding the terms of the bargain. Which means, little keeper, that you are currently the most dangerous person in this valley, and everything that happens next happens because of what you carry."

He closed his eyes again. His breathing went shallow and ragged, and Lena knelt on the hut's cold floor beside him with the oilcloth bundle open in her lap, turning pages she had already memorized and still could not stop reading, and the morning came up thin and cold over the mountains, and somewhere beyond those mountains, the prophecy promised, waited a disgraced archivist who knew how the bargain had been struck and by whom and why—and whether it could be undone.

She had never left the valley. Not once. Nineteen years inside borders she couldn't see, and now the borders were gone and the world outside was a blank page where her whole family's sins were waiting to be read aloud.

Tam's fever broke toward noon, or broke enough. He woke, drank the meltwater she fed him, and looked at her with the flat honesty of a man with nothing left to lose.

"There's a road east," he said. "There's a man named Corwin Hale at the end of it. And there's a king wearing a crown that eats whoever sits under it—and your guild helped put it on him."

Lena closed the book.

"We leave at dusk," she said.
