## 3. A Crown of Borrowed Breath

Tam had lied to kings, to inquisitors, to the woman who once shared his bed and his cause, but he had never before lied by omission to someone carrying a book that could end him. He told himself this was different. He told himself a great many things on the road east, and most of them were lies.

The girl walked like someone who had never learned what roads were for. She kept to the center of the track as if it were a corridor with walls, flinching at the openness of it—the way the land rolled away on both sides without so much as a hedge to say *this far and no farther*. Twice she stopped dead when birds went up out of the grass, her hand pressed flat against her sternum as though holding something in.

He understood. The first time he'd left a warded place he'd been twenty and armored in certainty, and he had still wept at the size of the sky.

"You can look at it," he said, when she paused a third time at the crest of a hill. "The horizon. It doesn't bite."

"I know how horizons work."

"Do you?" He hadn't meant it cruelly, but it landed that way, and he watched her jaw set and decided not to apologize, because an apology would have made it worse. Some wounds you only salted by acknowledging them.

They walked. The valley of Sigil was behind them now, folded back into its concealment—or what remained of it—and Tam kept checking over his shoulder anyway, the way a man checks a wound that has stopped bleeding only because the blood has run out. The wards had failed. Not cracked, not frayed: *failed*, all at once, like a held breath released after a hundred years. Which meant the prophecy's arithmetic was running true, which meant everything else in that book would come due on schedule too, including the parts Corwin Hale had underlined in a hand that shook.

Including the part about the crown.

* * *

They made camp in the shell of a shepherd's cot, its roof fallen in, its walls good enough to break the wind. Lena built the fire with the brisk competence of someone raised to chores, and Tam sat across from her and watched the light move on her face and tried to decide how much truth a person could drink in one night without drowning.

She saved him the trouble of deciding.

"Tell me about the king," she said. "The real one. You said *wearing*. I want to know what you meant."

So he told her. Or he began to, and found that once the words started they came out of him like water from a breached cistern, years of it, all of it foul.

"The man on the throne is Aldric," he said. "I want to be clear about that, because it matters. It isn't an impostor. It isn't some sorcerer's puppet wearing his face. It's his body—his hands, his voice, his scar from the boar hunt at Carrowmere that everyone used to laugh about. His heart beats. He eats, he sleeps, he rides. If you cut him, he bleeds." He turned his hands toward the fire. "But Aldric isn't in there anymore. What's in there is Vessel."

"Vessel," she repeated. "That's a name?"

"It's a description. The old texts call it a crown-born entity, which is archivists' language for *we don't know what it is, only what it does.* It lives in the iron of the crown. Has lived there for centuries, maybe longer—it passed from crown to crown, kingdom to kingdom, wherever smiths made circlets heavy enough to hold it. And when a king sets it on his head—" He stopped. Started again. "It doesn't possess you the way stories say demons do. There's no struggle, no screaming. That's the horror of it. It *wears* you. It settles into your will the way water settles into ground, slow, filling every low place, and by the time you notice you're drowning, the part of you that could have fought is already gone. Devoured. It eats wills, Lena. That's its food. A king has more will than any man alive—that's practically the job description—so a king is a feast that lasts decades."

The fire popped. Somewhere out in the dark a fox screamed, high and human-sounding, and Lena didn't flinch this time; she'd gone very still instead, the stillness of someone listening to a diagnosis.

"Aldric took the crown eight years ago," Tam said. "He was thirty-one. He was—" His voice caught on something, and he let it. "He was the best of them. Genuinely. He ended the grain tithe in his first year. He sat in the courts himself, listening to farmers' grievances until midnight. When the plague came up the coast he rode into the quarantine towns when his own council wouldn't. People loved him, and not the way crowds love crowns—they loved *him*. And Vessel knew it. Knew exactly what it was getting." He looked at his hands. "Eight years later he burned the granaries at Lowmarsh to starve a rebellion that didn't exist. He had the woman who organized the bread riots flayed in the market square. He signed orders in his own hand, in his own script—I've seen the letters, I used to carry them—and the handwriting never changed. Not a tremor. Because it wasn't possession. It was just Aldric's hand doing what Aldric's will no longer chose."

"And no one knows."

"The court knows something is wrong. Courts always know. But say it aloud and you're a traitor, and besides—" he gave a bitter half-laugh, "—who would believe it? The king is vigorous. The king is decisive. The king remembers every debt owed to him, every slight, every name. Vessel is a perfect king, if what you want is a king. It's only if you want a *man* that you notice anything missing."

Lena fed a stick to the fire. Her face, when she looked up, had the expression he was beginning to dread on her: the archivist's expression, the one that weighed and filed and did not flinch.

"My guild hid a valley for a hundred years," she said. "You're telling me they helped put that thing on a throne."

"I'm telling you Corwin Hale will tell you that. Whether it's true—" He spread his hands. "I'm not the one who should judge my own testimony. Ask him. Hate me afterward if you like."

"Why would I hate you?"

Because of what I haven't told you yet, he thought. Because of the brother-shaped hole in this story. Because when I say *devoured* I know the taste of it from the inside.

"Because I'm about to tell you the worst part," he said aloud. "Vessel doesn't stay satisfied with one will. A king's will feeds it for a generation, but it's always hungry underneath, and it's learned to reach. Through the crown's officers. Through oaths sworn on the throne's authority. Anyone who binds themselves to the wearer in service—" he tapped his own chest, twice, an old gesture, half salute and half ward-sign, "—gives it a thread to pull. Most people, it only tugs. Enough to make a guard captain look away at the right moment. An inquisitor find the wrong evidence convincing. But the closer you stand to the throne, the more of yourself it takes, and the less you notice it going."

Lena was quiet for a long moment. Then, in a voice carefully level: "How close did you stand?"

* * *

He had planned to lie. He had a whole architecture of lies prepared, load-bearing ones, and he watched them collapse in the space between her question and his answer, because she was looking at him the way no one had looked at him in eight years—not with suspicion, not with pity, but with the plain open attention of someone who had spent her life among books and believed that things written down ought to be true.

"I was his squire," Tam said. "Then his shield-bearer. Then, in the last year, his"—he searched for the word and hated each candidate—"his instrument. I carried the orders I told you about. I stood in doorways while things were done. And I told myself the threads were pulling other men, weaker men, that I was still choosing. That's what it lets you believe. That's the last thing it eats—your belief that you're free." He drew breath. "My brother Wren served with me. He started to see it. Started saying it, quietly, to people who mattered. And one morning I woke knowing—with a knowing that felt like my own thought, that felt like conscience—that Wren had become a danger to the realm. To the king. To everything good." His hands had gone white around his knees. "I reported him. I gave the inquisitors his name myself, and I stood at the trial, and I watched, and I felt *relief*, Lena. Relief that the danger was handled. It was three days before the fog lifted enough for me to understand what I'd done, and by then—"

He didn't finish. He didn't need to. The fire filled the silence with small industrious sounds.

"They hanged him," Lena said. Not a question.

"He's buried under the east wall at Carrowmere. Unmarked. I dug the grave myself, afterward, or I told myself I did. I don't remember doing it. I remember arriving at the wall with a shovel and leaving without one, and the earth being fresh." He finally raised his eyes to hers. "So when I say your guild helped bind a king, understand that I am not a clean witness. Understand that everything I've done since—Corwin, the book, finding the valley's border before the inquisitors did—is penance, and penance makes liars of us all. We tell the story that makes our guilt useful."

Lena looked at him across the fire for a long time. Then she reached into her coat, drew out the prophecy book, and set it on the stone between them—not offering it, just placing it, like a card laid face-up.

"Then start the story here," she said, "and I'll decide what's useful."

* * *

They walked at first light, and it was on the second morning, crossing the long moor above the river Dole, that her magic woke.

He saw it before she knew what was happening to her. She stopped mid-stride, and her shadow did something shadows shouldn't—leaned against the wind, then against nothing, then split faintly at the edges like ink in water. She made a sound, small and bitten-off, and went down on one knee in the wet grass with her arms wrapped around herself.

"Lena—"

"It's fine." Through her teeth. "It's fine, it's just—"

It was not fine. He knelt beside her and saw the light moving under her skin, faint as moonfish, tracing lines along her forearms that might have been veins and might have been writing. In Sigil, he understood suddenly, the wards hadn't merely hidden the valley—they had *muffled* whatever slept in its keepers, the way thick walls muffle a bell. Outside, with a hundred years of prophecy coming due, the bell was ringing whether she wanted it or not.

"What do I do?" she whispered. It was the first time she had asked him for anything, and it undid him more than the accusation he'd been bracing for.

"Breathe," he said. "Don't fight it and don't chase it. Let it pass through you like weather." He hesitated, then covered her clenched hand with his own, and where their skin touched the light leapt—bright, startled, recognizing something in him that he had spent eight years pretending wasn't there. "Oh," he said softly. "Oh, that's bad."

Her eyes opened. They were full of pain and, beneath it, unwilling wonder—the wonder of a woman who had catalogued a thousand sealed volumes and never once suspected she was one of them.

"Tam," she said. "What did the guild put inside me?"

And far behind them, on the moor's southern rim, a line of torches crested the ridge where no road ran—too many, too orderly, and moving at the pace of hunters who already knew exactly where their quarry stood.
