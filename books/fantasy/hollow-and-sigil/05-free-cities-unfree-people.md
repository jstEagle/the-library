## 5. Free Cities, Unfree People

Marrowgate announced itself by smell before sight—woodsmoke and river mud and the sour tang of too many people pressed behind walls that had never been built to hold them. Lena smelled it from the crest of the eastern road, a full hour before the city rose out of the haze, and she thought of the valley, where the air tasted only of pine and cold stone and nothing had ever been allowed to rot.

The road itself was the first shock. She had grown up on maps in which the world beyond Sigil's borders was drawn as vague suggestion—mountains without names, rivers like spilled ink. But this road was real in a way that hurt. It was rutted and crowded and lined with the abandoned belongings of people who had walked it before her: a child's shoe gone gray with rain, a wheelbarrow sunk to its axles in mud, a prayer-flag faded past all color still knotted to a fencepost as if letting go would be the final surrender.

And the people. They came toward her in a slow, shuffling river—the whole road flowing one way while she walked against it. Families with everything they owned strapped to their backs. An old man pulling a cart whose wheels had been replaced with logs because there was no wood left for spokes. A woman carrying a goat. Children who did not cry anymore, which was somehow worse than crying.

"Refugees," Tam said quietly beside her. He had not slept well; she could see it in the bruised skin under his eyes, in the way his hand kept drifting to the sword he no longer wore openly. "From the east. Vessel's armies crossed the Sorrel three weeks ago."

"I know what refugees are." She said it more sharply than she meant to. "I've read about them."

Tam glanced at her, and there was something in his face—not pity, exactly, but its near cousin—that made her want to strike him. "Reading about a flood," he said gently, "is not the same as drowning."

She had no answer for that. In the guild's archive, war had been a chapter heading. Famine had been a marginal note in a chronicle, the deaths rendered in tidy columns of figures. She had traced those figures with her finger a hundred times and felt nothing but the pleasant chill of old paper. Now an old man with log-wheels met her eyes as he passed, and looked away, ashamed to be seen like this, and Lena understood that every column of figures was a road like this one, and every road was full of people who were ashamed.

Corwin walked ahead of them, hood up, staff in hand, moving with the peculiar confidence of a man returning to a place he had sworn never to see again. He had barely spoken since they'd left his hiding place in the hills. When Lena had asked what Marrowgate was like, he'd said only: "Loud. Expensive. Full of people who will sell you and call it friendship." Then, after a mile of silence: "It is also the last place in the free lands where the truth can still be bought cheaply, if you know which stalls to visit."

* * *

Inside the walls, the city swallowed them.

Marrowgate had been built in layers over centuries, and the layers showed: ancient black stone at the foundations, then brick, then timber, then the ramshackle upper stories leaning out over streets so narrow the sky appeared only in slices. The lower city was drowned in newcomers. Every doorway held a sleeping family. Every fountain had become a washhouse, a drinking trough, a meeting place for the desperate. Criers moved through the crowds calling out the council's decrees—*ration cards issued at dawn, no camping within the temple district, all persons of eastern origin report for registration*—and each decree was greeted with the flat silence of people who had learned that decrees were weather, something that fell on you.

Lena kept close to Corwin's back. Her magic, which had woken outside the valley like a muscle cramping after long disuse, prickled uneasily under her skin here. There were too many threads of it in the city—wards on gates, charms on shopfronts, the faint sour residue of fear-magic clinging to the refugee quarter like smoke. In Sigil, magic had been a story told about other people. Here it was woven into the mortar.

"The Council of Nine meets tomorrow," Corwin murmured, steering them down a stair cut into the rock beneath a tannery, into streets that smelled of brine and lamp oil. "They'll debate whether to open the granaries to the eastern refugees or fortify against them. And whether to treat with the usurper or with whatever remains of loyalist resistance." He stopped at a door of green-painted iron and knocked a pattern—two, pause, three, pause, one. "There are nine votes. Six are honest. Three are for sale."

"And we're going to buy one?"

"We're going to watch someone else buy one." His mouth twisted. "That, at least, I can still do. Watch. Remember. Write it down."

The door opened onto a room that took Lena a moment to understand. It was an archive—but not like hers. No sealed cases, no velvet ropes of reverence. Books were stacked in towers on the floor, chained to tables, piled in bathtubs. Papers hung from lines strung across the ceiling like laundry. A dozen people worked in the lamplight, copying, comparing, arguing in whispers. On the far wall, someone had painted in letters two feet high:

**THE TRUTH DOES NOT REQUIRE YOUR PERMISSION TO EXIST.**

"This is the Undercroft," Corwin said, and something in his voice softened for the first time in days. "I helped found it, thirty years ago, before—" He stopped. "Before I disgraced myself. The point is: the councils lie in daylight. Down here, we keep the night's ledger."

An old woman with ink-stained fingers to the elbow looked up from a copying desk. "Corwin Hale." She said it the way one names a disease. "You have nerve, coming back."

"Sera Voss. You have nerve, still being alive. Between us we're evenly matched."

Something almost like a smile passed between them and died. Sera Voss turned to Lena, and her gaze was a scalpel. "And this is?"

"Lena Vane. Of the Hollow Guild."

The room went quiet. Not dramatically—a pen simply stopped scratching, a page simply stopped turning—but Lena felt the silence settle on her shoulders like a weight. These people knew the name. Not as legend. As *accusation*.

"The Hollow Guild," Sera repeated. "Keeper of the hidden valley. Sworn to neutrality." She let the word hang until it rotted. "We have records, girl. Payments routed through salt merchants. Guild seals on letters instructing council members how to vote. Your order has been buying the free cities for fifty years, and now you walk in here wearing its colors?"

Lena's hand went to the gray sash at her waist before she could stop it. "The Guild keeps the valley safe. That's all. That's *all it does.*"

"Then you're a fool," Sera said, without heat, turning back to her desk. "Or new. Possibly both."

* * *

They gave her a corner of the Undercroft and a stack of ledgers, and told her to read.

She read. Salt merchant accounts, freight manifests, letters of credit drawn on banks she'd never heard of. And there—in the third ledger, in entries spanning eleven years—was the pattern Sera had promised. Payments to a factor named Ossian Belt. Payments from Ossian Belt to three councilors whose names appeared, always, on the losing side of every vote concerning the usurper's advance. The sums were modest, almost insultingly so. The price of a conscience, itemized.

*The Guild doesn't do this,* she told herself. *Someone is forging the seal.*

But she knew the seal. She had pressed it herself, a hundred times, into wax for routine correspondence. The little hollow ring surrounding a hollow star. She knew the slight irregularity in the star's lower point, the flaw in the die that Master Ellery had complained about for years and never fixed. It was there, in the wax impressions preserved between these pages. Flawed star. Hollow ring.

Her own hands had made marks like these.

She sat very still for a long time while the Undercroft went about its business around her, and she felt something in her chest shift—not breaking, not yet, but settling, the way a house settles when its foundation begins to fail.

* * *

The council session was public—all sessions were, in Marrowgate, though the gallery was small and the acoustics were designed, Lena suspected, to make listening exhausting. She sat in the back beside Tam, in borrowed clothes with her guild sash hidden in her boot, and watched the Council of Nine deliberate the fate of ten thousand refugees.

Three councilors argued for mercy. Three argued for the walls. And Councilor Maren Doss—a heavyset woman with a voice like warm honey—argued, at length and beautifully, for a compromise: aid the refugees, yes, but send envoys to the usurper first. Peace through acknowledgment. Stability through accommodation. It was a good speech. Lena could see it working on the gallery around her, heads nodding, despair briefly dressed up as wisdom.

Then Councilor Doss paused, and glanced—just once, just slightly—toward a box near the chamber's side door.

Two men sat there. Gray cloaks. Traveling clothes of good wool, worn deliberately dull. One of them, the older, had a face Lena knew from a hundred childhood evenings: Master Fenwick, Third Chair of the Hollow Guild, who had taught her her letters and her stars and how to hold a seal above a candle.

He nodded, almost imperceptibly.

Councilor Doss resumed speaking, and her argument bent—not obviously, not dishonestly, but the emphasis shifted, the compromises tilted, and by the end of it she was proposing not envoys but *accreditation*: formal recognition of the usurper's authority over the eastern territories, in exchange for trade guarantees. And the gallery, lulled, murmured approval.

Lena's hands were shaking. Tam's hand closed over hers under the bench. "Breathe," he whispered.

"That's Fenwick. He taught me to write my own name."

"I know."

"You *know?*" She turned. "How could you possibly—"

"Because I've seen him do this before. In two other cities." Tam's voice was flat and tired. "The Guild doesn't defend the valley, Lena. The valley defends the *Guild*. It's their fortress, their treasury, their alibi. Everything they do out here, they do wearing neutrality like a mask." He hesitated. "I'm sorry. I hoped you'd have longer before—"

"Before what?" Her voice cracked. "Before I found out my whole life is a *costume*?"

Across the chamber, Master Fenwick rose, gathered his papers, and left through the side door with the unhurried gait of a man whose work was finished.

* * *

She followed him.

She didn't decide to. Her feet decided, and her heart went along, hammering. Through the side door, down a corridor of flaking frescoes, out into a courtyard where rain had begun to fall, fine and cold. Fenwick stood under an awning speaking with Councilor Doss, and Lena pressed herself into the shadow of a buttress close enough to hear the rain change pitch on stone.

"—the vote holds tomorrow," Doss was saying. "The accreditation passes. But the granary question is another matter. Voss's people have been stirring the gallery. If the mob turns—"

"The mob turns when it's hungry," Fenwick said. His voice was exactly as she remembered it: patient, dry, kind at the edges. The voice that had explained eclipses to her when she was seven. "Feed the western districts and let the eastern camps fend for themselves. Hunger does our work better than gold does. And Councilor—" a rustle of paper, a purse changing hands, the soft clink that Lena had heard a thousand times in the guild counting-room without ever asking what it counted *for* —"the usual arrangement. Half now. Half when the session closes."

"And the Guild's interest in all this?" Doss asked, and there was something almost curious in it. "Forgive me, Master Fenwick, but I've never understood. What does a hidden valley care who sits on thrones?"

A pause. Rain on awning canvas.

"The valley cares about nothing," Fenwick said at last. "The Guild cares about *quiet*. A strong usurper makes noise. A weak king makes noise. We prefer the realm asleep, Councilor. It has been so much easier to manage asleep."

Lena stood in the dark with rain running down her face and understood, finally and completely, that she had never been a daughter of the Guild. She had been a *product* of it. Raised in a hidden valley, taught a dead language of secrecy, fed reverence instead of history—and all of it, every lullaby and lesson, in service of keeping the realm asleep.

They separated. Doss went one way, Fenwick another, alone now, moving toward the tanners' gate with his collar up against the rain.

Lena should have gone back to the Undercroft. She knew that. Instead she followed him again, through streets gone blue with evening, to a respectable inn called the Gilded Scale, where he took a room under a name that wasn't his, and where—she watched through the window of the neighboring stable loft, heart in her throat—he unlocked a traveling case and removed a leather folio, thick as a bible, and set it on the desk while he went down to supper.

The folio was bound in gray leather stamped with the hollow star.

She was inside the room four minutes later. She would never clearly remember picking the lock—only Tam's training, suddenly and strangely useful, and her own heartbeat loud enough to wake the dead. The folio held everything. Letters of instruction in the Guild cipher she had learned at her mother's knee. Ledgers matching Sera Voss's, but complete, extending back sixty years. Names of councilors in nine cities. And on the first page, in Master Fenwick's own careful hand, a heading that made her breath stop:

*Disbursements — Operation Stillwater — approved by unanimous vote of the Inner Chairs, for the continued management of the realm's sleep.*

Operation Stillwater. She had heard the phrase once before—whispered between Master Ellery and the First Chair in the archive corridor, the night after she found the prophecy book, the night they ordered it burned.

Below the folio in the case lay something else: a letter, unsealed, addressed to the Inner Chairs, dated three days ago. She read it by the light of the streetlamp through the glass, and the words went through her like cold iron:

*The Vane girl lives and carries the book. Our agents failed at the border. Recommend we disavow her formally at the next conclave and offer her head to the usurper as proof of good faith. The valley's safety requires it. She was always, after all, expendable—we chose her bloodline for precisely this contingency.*

*Chosen*. Not born. Bred, perhaps. Raised like a candle in a window, lit for a purpose she had never been told.

Downstairs, a floorboard creaked. Voices. Fenwick, finishing his supper, laughing softly with the innkeeper about the weather.

Lena closed the case. She took the folio and the letter both, wrapped them in oilcloth from the stable below, and climbed out into the rain, where Tam waited in the dark with two horses and a face that asked no questions.

"Well?" he said.

She swung up into the saddle, clutching the oilcloth bundle against her chest like a child, like a weapon, like the last piece of a home that had never actually existed.

"Now we go find Corwin," she said. "And then we make them answer for it."
