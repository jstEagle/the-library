## 6. The Price of Names

They took him at the ford.

Tam had known the river would be watched—he had told Lena as much, told her twice—but knowing a trap and avoiding it were different arts, and his legs had been failing him since the pass. The water came up silver in the moonlight, cold enough to bite through his boots, and he was halfway across when the lanterns bloomed on the far bank like poisonous flowers opening all at once.

He could have run. That was the bitter joke of it. He had run from worse than six guild hunters with iron-tipped crossbows; he had run from the thing that wore a king's face, and he had lived. But running meant leading them back to Lena, back to the book, back to the only chance the realm had left of waking up from its long nightmare, and so Tam Vess did the one thing he had sworn never to do again.

He stood still, raised his hands, and let them take him.

The chains were guild-work, old and heavy, stamped with the hollow circle that was their sigil—a ring with nothing at its center, which Tam had always thought was the most honest emblem any order had ever chosen for itself. They marched him through the dark hours to a waystation built into the bones of an old watchtower, one of the border forts the Hollow Guild had maintained for a century without anyone outside the valley ever asking why a hidden valley needed fortifications at all.

They put him in the cellar. They put him in the dark. And then they waited, because they knew what Tam knew: that the girl would come.

* * *

He counted the hours by the drip of water somewhere behind the wall. It was a habit from another life, from years spent in places where counting was the only power a man retained. In those years he had learned that time measured in drips moved differently than time measured in bells—slower, heavier, each drop a small stone laid on the chest.

His brother had taught him the trick. Wren, who had laughed at everything, who had counted aloud even when there was no need, who had died at nineteen with Tam's hand on the door and Tam's feet already moving toward the light.

No. Not like that. He had told the story differently for eleven years, sanded it smooth, worn it into something almost bearable: *the guards were coming, there was no other way, I had no choice.* But sitting in the dark with the drip keeping its slow ledger, Tam found the old shape of the truth pressing up through the floorboards of himself, the way water finds the crack.

There had been a choice. There was always a choice. He had made his, and Wren had paid it, and Tam had walked out of the Spire of the Wearing with his brother's name still warm in his mouth and Vessel's voice still whispering through the walls—*you may go, little traitor, you have given me enough*—and every day since, he had told himself that survival was not the same as surrender.

Some nights he almost believed it.

Footsteps on the stairs. Not the hunters' tread—they moved in pairs, deliberately loud, wanting to be heard. These steps were light, hesitant, and they stopped twice before reaching the bottom, and Tam felt something in his chest turn over, painful and unwelcome, because he knew that hesitation. He knew the particular care of someone trying not to be a sound in the world.

"Lena," he said to the dark. "You shouldn't be here."

A shuttered lantern opened a seam of amber light. She stood at the foot of the stairs with the hood of her traveling cloak back and her face pale as paper, and behind her, filling the doorway, one of the hunters—a broad woman with a guild badge worked in gray thread over her heart.

"They said you'd want to see me," Lena said. Her voice was steady. Her hands were not; he could see them trembling where she gripped the lantern's handle. "They said you asked."

"I asked." He shifted against the wall, and the chains dragged across the flagstones with a sound like a slow exhale. "I wanted to tell you not to give them anything. Whatever they offered—it's a lie dressed in reasonable clothes. The amnesty, the safe conduct home. The moment the book is in their hands, you're a loose thread, and the Hollow Guild has never once in its history failed to cut a loose thread."

"I know," she said quietly. "Corwin told me. Corwin told me a great many things, actually." Something flickered across her face—not anger, exactly. Something more tired. "Did you know? About the binding? Before we ever left the valley?"

Tam closed his eyes. The drip kept its count behind the wall. *One. Two. Three.*

"No," he said. "But I knew the guild wasn't what you believed it was. I knew it the first time I saw the wards fail and watched your elders seal the gates behind you rather than open them for anyone else. I let you walk out of that valley thinking we were fleeing together toward the same truth, when really I was using your grief to move you where I needed you to go." He opened his eyes and made himself hold hers. "So if you're here to hate me, hate me efficiently. We don't have much time."

"That's not—" She stopped. Breathed. The hunter behind her shifted, and lamplight caught the woman's face: patient, faintly amused, a person who had watched this exact scene play out many times and always to the same end. "They want the book by dawn. If I give it to them, they release you and grant me passage back to Sigil. Full amnesty. Elder Maren signed the writ herself."

"Then Maren is afraid," Tam said, "and fear makes the powerful generous for exactly as long as generosity costs them nothing." He leaned forward as far as the chains allowed. "Lena. Listen to me. You've read the prophecy now—all of it, not just the page with tonight's date. You know what happens if the book burns. The unbinding dies with it. Aldric stays buried inside his own body forever, and Vessel wears him until the realm forgets there was ever a man under the crown. A hundred years from now, some other child will find some other date inked wet in some other book, and the whole wheel turns again."

"Then tell me why," she said, and her voice cracked at last, clean down the middle. "Tell me why you know so much about what Vessel does to the people it wears. Tell me why the hunters looked at you when you were captured and one of them crossed herself, like she'd seen a ghost. Tell me why you flinch every time anyone says the word *crown*."

The cellar was very quiet. Even the drip seemed to pause.

"You deserve the truth," Tam said slowly. "All of it. Even the parts that will make you wish you'd taken the amnesty."

"Tam—"

"My name isn't Tam." The lie he had carried for eleven years came out of him easier than he expected, the way a rotted tooth comes out easier than a sound one. "My name is Tammas Vess. Eleven years ago I served in the household of King Aldric. I was a cupbearer. A nobody. Which is precisely why Vessel noticed me—because a nobody can stand beside a king's chair for hours and never be seen. It used us, the servants. It whispered through the walls of the palace like damp comes through stone. It told us things. Small things at first. Where to stand. What to repeat. What to forget."

Lena had gone very still. Behind her, the hunter smiled.

"And your brother?" Lena asked. "Wren?"

"Wren was fourteen when we entered service. Fourteen, and Vessel liked him." Tam heard his own voice go flat, the way it did when feeling pressed too hard against too small a space. "It liked the young ones best. Their wills hadn't set yet. It wore at him for two years, the way water wears at limestone, and by the end he wasn't my brother anymore. He was its hands. It sent him to open the postern gate on the night it finished taking the king—the night the binding was sealed, the night your guild's ancestors stood in that throne room and helped it, because they wanted a king they could rule through rather than a king who ruled."

"The guild did that?" Her whisper barely reached him. "That's what Corwin said, but I thought—I hoped he was lying."

"He wasn't lying. I saw the gray cloaks in the torchlight. I saw the hollow circle on their sleeves." Tam's hands had closed into fists without his noticing; the chain links bit into his palms. "And I ran. That's the part that matters. That's the part I've never said out loud. On the night of the binding, Vessel gave the servants a choice—stay and be devoured with the king, or leave and carry its message to the world. Wren couldn't leave. It had him too deep; his legs wouldn't obey him. And I—" The drip started again, and Tam matched his breathing to it. "I stepped over him. He was on the floor of the corridor, crying, reaching for my ankle, and I stepped over him and walked out into the free air, and I have been walking ever since. Eleven years. Every mile of it away from that door."

The lantern trembled in Lena's grip. For a long moment she didn't speak, and Tam braced himself—for disgust, for pity, worst of all for understanding, which would be unbearable.

"You escaped," she said finally. "By letting him die."

"By letting him die," Tam agreed. "Yes. That's the shape of it. I've tried every other shape for eleven years and none of them fit."

* * *

The hunter left at some point—or perhaps she never fully left; Tam suspected she stood just beyond the stairwell's turn, letting the scene ripen. Guild hunters were patient that way. They understood that guilt did half their work for them, and mercy did the rest.

When the cellar was quiet again, Lena crouched down in front of him, close enough that he could see the exhaustion carved under her eyes, the ink stain still ghosting the side of her right hand from weeks of copying pages by firelight.

"They're going to kill you at dawn either way," she said. "Amnesty or no. Isn't that true?"

"It's true."

"And if I keep the book and run, they'll hunt us both, and the prophecy might die in some ditch between here and the mountains."

"Also true."

She looked at him for a long time. Then she reached up and pulled something from inside her cloak—a thin strip of black iron, no longer than her forearm, etched with lines that caught the lantern light and held it wrong, the way guild-warding always did.

"This is the key to the chains," she said. "I stole it off the hunter while she was gloating about the writ. I've been holding it for ten minutes trying to decide whether you're worth what it's going to cost me." Her mouth twisted—not quite a smile, but near kin to one. "Then I remembered that I'm the one who found the date in the book. My whole life has been someone else's plan, Tam. Someone else's prophecy, someone else's bargain, someone else's guild. I am done living inside other people's designs—including yours."

She fit the key to the lock at his wrist. It turned with a click that sounded, in the silence, like a very small beginning.

"Get up," Lena Vane said. "We have a king to unbind, and I intend to do it loudly."

Above them, faint through the floorboards, came the sound of the waystation bell ringing once—and then, unmistakably, the horn-call of the guild rising from three directions at once.

They had been discovered. Or they had been expected.

Tam got to his feet, rubbing his raw wrists, and found he was smiling for the first time in eleven years.
