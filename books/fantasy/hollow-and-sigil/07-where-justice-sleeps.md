## 7. Where Justice Sleeps

The mountain did not have a door. That was the first thing Lena understood about the Sunken Court: it was not a place you entered so much as a place you were permitted to fall into.

Corwin had led them for two days along goat-tracks that weren't tracks at all, through pine forest gone black with shadow, past cairns stacked by hands long dead. Tam walked with a limp now, the wound in his side from the inquisitors' blades knitting slowly under Corwin's bittergreen salve, and he said little. Corwin said less. Twice Lena caught the old archivist watching her with an expression she could not name—something between apology and appraisal, as if he were measuring her against a standard she hadn't been told existed.

On the third morning they stopped before a cliff face indistinguishable from any other, and Corwin pressed his palm flat against the stone and spoke a word that wasn't a word—a sound like a held breath finally released.

The rock remembered being open.

They descended through a throat of stairs cut so precisely that no chisel-mark survived, down and down, past veins of pale mineral that glowed faintly when Lena's sleeve brushed them, as if the mountain itself were waking to look at what passed through its body. The air grew warm. Then cold again. Then warm. Somewhere below, water dripped in a rhythm too regular to be accidental.

"How far down?" Tam asked.

"Far enough," Corwin said, "that no king has ever bothered digging."

* * *

The Court announced itself not with grandeur but with sound: voices, dozens of them, echoing up the stairwell like water finding its level. When the stairs finally opened out, Lena stopped on the threshold and forgot, for one full breath, how to breathe herself.

A cavern vast as a buried cathedral spread beneath the mountain's ribs. Stalactites hung like the pipes of some impossible organ, and from their tips someone—generations of someones—had suspended lanterns on long chains of woven hair and wire, hundreds of them, each burning with a small blue flame that cast no heat. The light fell in shifting constellations across a floor of polished black stone, and upon that floor stood the Court itself: not a building but a ring, a great circle of benches carved from living rock, tiered like the rings of a felled tree, descending toward a central well of darkness where the accused were made to stand.

People filled the benches. Hundreds. Lena saw merchants in dyed wool and miners still wearing the dust of other mountains, a woman whose robes bore the sigil of a free city's council, a boy no older than twelve sitting cross-legged with a slate on his knees, taking notes. No guards. She looked twice to be sure. There were no guards anywhere—only the crowd, and the crowd's attention, which was heavier than any blade.

"They call it the Sunken Court because it sank," Corwin murmured beside her. "Beneath every law, beneath every crown. When the written law fails—or serves only those who wrote it—the wronged come here. There is no appeal from this place because there is nothing above it."

"And who judges?"

"Whoever comes." He gestured at the ring of benches. "Anyone may accuse. Anyone may speak for or against. The verdict belongs to everyone who stays to hear it out. That is its power and its terror, girl. Here, justice answers to no throne—which means it answers to *everyone*, and everyone is a harder master than any king."

Lena's hands had gone cold inside her gloves. "And if I'm lying? If I'm wrong about the guild?"

"Then you will stand in that well," Corwin said, nodding toward the darkness at the center, "and a hundred strangers will take your name apart syllable by syllable, and if they find rot in it, you will carry the mark of a false accuser for the rest of your life. Every door in the free cities closes to that mark." He turned to face her fully, and in the lantern-light his eyes looked ancient and very tired. "I have carried my own shame for thirty years, Lena Vane. I know what it costs to say a true thing in front of strangers. So ask yourself now, while the stairs are still behind you: do you want safety, or do you want truth? You cannot bring both down these steps."

She thought of the prophecy book wrapped in oilcloth against her chest, its ink still wet after a hundred years. She thought of the archive's sealed shelves, of Master Ellery's face the night he ordered the book burned—not angry, she realized now, but *afraid*, and afraid in the way of a man protecting something, not destroying it. She thought of the valley, green and hidden and safe, and of the refugees on Marrowgate's walls who would never see anything so safe again.

"I didn't climb this far to stop at the bottom," she said.

* * *

They waited four hours for the Court to convene. Corwin used the time badly, pacing; Tam used it sleeping, propped against the wall with his hand resting near his knife even in dreams. Lena spent it memorizing the room—the exits (three stairwells, all narrow), the mood of the crowd (restless, skeptical, hungry for spectacle), the rhythm of the cases heard before hers.

She watched a miller accuse a grain-factor of fixing weights. She watched a widow accuse a mercenary captain of burning her village under contract, and produce the contract, signed and sealed, and watch the crowd turn on the captain like weather turning. The captain did not deny it. He stood in the dark well and said, "It was lawful work," and a voice from the benches called back, "Not here it isn't," and the roar that followed shook dust from the stalactites.

That was the moment Lena understood what she had walked into. This was not a trial. It was a *remembering*. The Court did not punish so much as refuse to forget—and there was no punishment in the world more feared by powerful men than being remembered rightly.

When the herald—an old woman with a voice like gravel poured slow—called for new accusations, Lena stood before her courage could reconsider.

"The Hollow Guild," she said, and her own voice sounded strange to her, thin in that enormous dark. "I accuse the Hollow Guild of the valley of Sigil."

The name moved through the benches like wind through wheat. She saw it reach people: *Hollow Guild*—a rumor, a fireside story, the invisible hand behind a century of quiet arrangements. Some faces sharpened with interest. Others closed like doors.

"Step forward, accuser," the herald said. "Name yourself."

"Lena Vane. Journeyman of the Hollow Guild." A murmur—*one of their own*—and she lifted her chin against it. "Which is why I can tell you what I know."

She descended into the well.

The darkness at its center was not empty. Standing in it, she could see every face in the ring above her, lit by falling lantern-light, and every face could see hers, and there was nowhere to hide, no shadow deep enough, no formula of polite language to soften what she had to say. Her heart hammered so hard she was certain the whole cavern could hear it. *This is what fear feels like when it turns over,* she thought. *This is fear becoming something else.*

"Hundred years ago," she began, "the guild did not hide our valley from the world. We hid the world from a crime."

She told them. All of it, as far as she knew it: the prophecy book with tonight's date written in ink that never dried; the wards failing exactly on schedule; the confession of Corwin Hale, last archivist of the guild, who had found the original accord in the sealed archive—that the guild's founders had helped bind King Aldric the Just, helping the crown-entity Vessel cage the man while wearing him, so that the guild could rule unseen through proxies in every court of the realm. She named the payments her own eyes had seen recorded in Marrowgate: guild silver buying council silence while the usurper's armies advanced. She held up the ledger page she'd stolen, and let the herald's runners pass it hand to hand through the benches, and listened to the crowd read her family's sins aloud in fragments.

"You were raised by these people," someone called down. "Why should we believe a daughter against her mother-house?"

Because, she wanted to say, I loved them. Because love is not evidence. Instead she said: "Because they sent hunters to kill me for knowing. Because they hold a stranger of mine in irons as we speak. And because—" her voice cracked, and she let it crack, let them hear it— "because everything in me wants to be wrong. I have no home but that valley. I am asking you to help me burn down the only house I've ever loved. Do you think I'd do that for a lie?"

Silence, then—long, ringing, total. Even the dripping water seemed to pause.

Then Corwin stepped to the well's edge, and gave his name—Corwin Hale, archivist, oath-breaker, exile—and laid his thirty years of shame on the stone beside her testimony, document by document, and the Court weighed them together.

The deliberation took until the blue lanterns had burned low. Lena stood in the dark and felt herself being taken apart and examined, syllable by syllable, exactly as Corwin had promised, and discovered that she was not afraid anymore. Whatever they decided, she had already done the thing that could not be undone: she had made the guild's secret into the world's knowledge, and knowledge, once spoken before witnesses, could not be re-inked or burned.

At last the herald rose.

"The Court finds the accusation *true*," she said, and the cavern erupted—but she raised her hand, and quieted it. "The Hollow Guild stands accused before all free peoples: of regicide unproven but credibly bound, of usurpation by proxy, of purchase of councils, of concealment of crimes against the realm. Let it be remembered in every city, every mine, every market. Let no honest hand sign its papers. Let its agents be named on sight."

Then the old woman looked down into the well, directly at Lena, and her gravel voice dropped into something almost gentle.

"But hear the Court's finding entire, accuser. Truth spoken is not truth *finished*. The guild's crime lives in a crown on a living man's head, and while it lives, verdicts are only words. The Court cannot march. It cannot break crowns." A pause. "Someone must."

Lena felt every eye in the cavern settle on her, hundreds of them, heavy as the mountain overhead—and understood, with terrible clarity, that she had not come here to ask permission. She had come here to be *sent*.

Behind her, Tam rose stiffly from the wall, hand on his knife, and Corwin closed his eyes as if he had known, all along, exactly where these stairs led.

"The throne road runs east," the herald said. "Three weeks' ride. The Court grants you its memory—every city will know your name and your cause. Whether it grants you anything more depends on what you carry out of this mountain."

Lena climbed out of the well alone, into the falling blue light, and the crowd parted before her—not in fear, she realized, but the way water parts around a prow.

East. Toward the crown. Toward Aldric, and the thing that wore him.

Toward home's last secret, waiting to be broken open.
