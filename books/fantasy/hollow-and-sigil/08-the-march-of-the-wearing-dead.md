## 8. The March of the Wearing Dead

The bells of Marrowgate rang wrong.

Lena had learned their voices in the weeks since the tribunal—dawn bells bright as struck glass, the dusk bell low and patient, the alarm bell a single hoarse note that meant fire in the tanneries or a collapsed pier. What she heard now, from the high window of the safehouse above the Saltmarket, was all of them at once, every tower, every tongue of bronze in the city speaking together, and beneath them a sound she knew only from prophecy books: the drumbeat of an army that did not march so much as *pour*, like water finding the cracks in a wall.

She pressed her palm to the cold pane. Beyond the harbor, beyond the breakwater, the hills were moving. Not the hills themselves—she corrected herself with a scholar's reflex—but the things upon them, rank on rank of soldiers whose armor caught no light, whose banners hung dead in a wind that should have filled them. And here and there among the ranks, taller shapes that walked with a lurching, borrowed gait, as though each body were a coat worn by something that had never learned how shoulders worked.

*The wearing dead.* The archive's oldest texts had used the phrase without explanation, and she had copied it out a hundred times in her neat guild hand without once understanding it.

Now she understood. Now she could see, even at this distance, the way some of the marching figures dragged one leg, or favored an arm, or turned their heads too far—the small wrongnesses of bodies piloted by something else. Vessel did not raise corpses. It simply emptied the living and kept using them.

Below in the street, Marrowgate was doing what Marrowgate always did: arguing. She could hear the council criers, the militia sergeants, the fishwives shouting each other down about whether to man the walls or open the gates, whether the free cities' pact bound them to fight or merely to flee. A city built by merchants and smugglers, a city that had never once agreed on anything, trying to agree on how to die.

Lena took the prophecy book from its oilcloth wrapping and held it against her chest. The verdict was supposed to come first. Corwin had sworn it—the Sunken Court moved slowly because justice moved slowly, but it moved, and the writ naming the Hollow Guild's crimes would reach Marrowgate before Vessel did, and the councils would have no choice but to stand with her.

The bells kept ringing. The hills kept pouring.

"Corwin," she said quietly, to the empty room, "where is our verdict?"

* * *

Tam ran through the Warrens with blood in his mouth and his brother's name in his throat, which was a habit he had thought himself rid of.

He had been on the eastern wall when the vanguard hit the outer villages—not attacking, not even slowing, just *absorbing*. He had watched a farmwife come out of her burning house with a hay fork and put it into the chest of the nearest soldier, and he had watched the soldier look down at the wound with mild interest, the way a man looks at rain, and keep walking. The woman had stood there a moment, waiting for something to happen. Then two more of the empty ones had taken her arms, gently, almost courteously, and she had gone pale in a way Tam recognized from the inside, from the year he had served the thing in the crown, and he had known what she understood in that final second: they were not going to kill her. They were going to *keep* her.

He had jumped from the wall rather than watch.

Now he cut through the dye-works, where the vats steamed and the workers had already fled, leaving the colors to bleed into the gutters—crimson and indigo running together down the channel like a wound being washed. Behind him the eastern gate had gone quiet, which was worse than screaming. Quiet meant it was over there. Quiet meant the army was inside and did not need to make noise, because noise was for enemies and these people had stopped being enemies the moment they were taken.

A child stood in the middle of the lane ahead of him, maybe six years old, holding a wooden boat, staring back toward the sound of the bells.

Tam scooped her up without breaking stride. She didn't cry; she went rigid with the particular silence of children who have already learned that adults cannot be trusted to fix things. "I've got you," he said, and hated how often he'd said those exact words in another life, in another uniform, to other families, right before—

He ran.

At the corner of Rope-Walk he nearly collided with a guild hunter—one of theirs, gray cloak, the hollow sigil stitched at the shoulder—and for one heartbeat they stared at each other across the running crowd, two men serving opposite sides of the same lie. The hunter's eyes went to the child in Tam's arms, then to the smoke rising over the east quarter, and something in his face came apart. He turned and walked away into the crowd, unclasping the sigil from his cloak as he went, letting it fall in the gutter beside the bleeding dyes.

Tam did not stop. But he thought, later, that he should have picked it up.

* * *

Corwin Hale had spent forty years learning where knowledge lived, and in the last hour he had learned where it died.

The Chancery of Records burned. He stood across the canal and watched three centuries of Marrowgate's charters, deeds, judgments, and genealogies go up in a column of black smoke, and he understood with terrible clarity that this was not collateral damage. Vessel did not burn libraries out of spite. Vessel burned libraries the way a counterfeiter burns real coin—because every true record made its own lies harder. Every deed proved someone owned something. Every judgment proved someone had been answerable to something. A realm of the emptied needed no past.

"You're the archivist," said a voice beside him—a militia boy, sixteen at most, soot-striped, clutching a pike taller than himself. "They say you're the archivist who was cast out. Is it true the guild kept books older than the king's line?"

"It's true," Corwin said.

"Then write this down." The boy's chin trembled and his voice did not. "Write down that the Third Company of Marrowgate held the Chancery steps until the end. Write down their names. I'll give them to you."

And Corwin, who had catalogued ten thousand dead men's trifles without weeping, took out the little wax tablet he carried and wrote the names of living men while the building that would have preserved them fell in on itself, and his hand shook so badly the letters came out crooked, and he let them be crooked, because crooked was honest.

When the last name was given, the boy asked, "Will anyone read it?"

"The tribunal will," Corwin said. "If any of us live to carry it."

"And if we don't?"

"Then it will still be true," Corwin said. "Truth doesn't need readers to be true. It only needs witnesses to be *known*." He looked east, toward the safehouse, toward Lena, toward the book that was older than every burning thing in this city. "Go south. Find the refugees. Tell them the verdict is coming."

It was a kindness and a lie in equal measure, and the boy took both and ran.

* * *

By full dark, Marrowgate's western districts were rivers of people.

Lena stood on the quay with the book strapped to her back and watched them come—fisherfolk with their boats burned behind them, dyers still blue to the elbows, a councilman in his robes of office carrying nothing but the ceremonial chain, which he dropped into the harbor when his wife told him, gently, that no one would ever ask him to preside again. The free cities had always been many voices quarreling in one room. Tonight they were one voice, and it said only one word, over and over, in a thousand accents: *away, away, away.*

"They'll rally at Greyford," Tam said beside her. His arm was bandaged; he refused to say how. "The mountain pass. It's defensible. It's—" He stopped.

"It's where the tribunal sits," Lena finished.

The verdict had come at last, an hour after the eastern gate fell. A single rider, mud to the eyebrows, had found her in the Saltmarket and thrust a scroll into her hands with such force it tore. She had read it standing in the street while the city died around her, and she had read it three times because grief kept sliding the letters out of focus.

The Sunken Court had judged. The Hollow Guild was named: *accessory to the binding of King Aldric, author of the valley's concealment, purchaser of councils, keeper of false peace.* Its charter was voided. Its archives were ordered surrendered. And because the original binding had been made in the guild's blood, the rite of unbinding passed by law to the last sworn member of the guild's inner line—to Lena Vane, *heir to the unbinding, until the crown is broken or the heir is dead.*

Too late to save the city. In time to save everything else, if there was an everything else.

"They'll follow you," Tam said. He wasn't looking at her; he was looking at the quay, at the crowds, and Lena followed his gaze and saw what he saw. People were stopping. Small groups at first, then dozens. Refugees who had walked all day with their lives in bundles on their backs were turning to look at the girl with the old book, and word was passing along the waterfront faster than the fire was passing through the east quarter: *the heir, the tribunal's heir, the one the court named.*

An old dyer planted herself in Lena's path, indigo-stained hands knotted together. "Is it true?" the woman demanded. "You can take the crown off him? Off the thing?"

"I can try," Lena said. It was the only honest sentence she owned.

"Try, then." The dyer turned to the crowd and raised her ruined hands. "She tries! Who walks with her?"

For a moment there was only the fire-noise and the bell-noise and the sea. Then a fisherman stepped forward. Then the boy from the Chancery, soot-striped, pike on his shoulder—he had made it after all. Then a dozen, then fifty, then the quay itself seemed to shift and flow toward her, and Lena Vane, who had spent her whole life inside a hidden valley learning to be unseen, stood visible before thousands and felt terror turn over inside her like a plowed field and become something else. Something with furrows in it. Something you could plant.

"I can't promise you Marrowgate back," she called, and her voice cracked on the city's name, and nobody minded. "I can't promise you the dead. I can promise you the truth, spoken where everyone can hear it, and a crown broken on open ground where no guild and no throne can ever hide again!"

The roar that answered was not a cheer. It was rougher than that, uglier, grief-shaped—it was the sound of people who had lost a city deciding what to do with the losing. It rolled off the water and came back off the burning hills, and Tam leaned close and said, quietly, "They'll die following you."

"Some of them," Lena said. Her eyes stung. She did not blink it away; the valley had taught her that concealment, and she was done with everything the valley taught. "So will we, maybe. But we'll die *pointing* somewhere, Tam. That's more than Vessel's soldiers get."

* * *

Far to the north, in the sack-quiet heart of Marrowgate, the thing that wore King Aldric stood in the ruined Chancery and felt, through ten thousand borrowed skins, a thread pull tight.

Its armies had fed well. Its records were ash. And yet somewhere south, in the river of the fleeing, a girl carried a book that predated crowns, and the court of the lawless had named her, and the thread that bound entity to valley to heir had begun, very softly, to sing.

Vessel raised Aldric's stolen hand, and in the reflection of a shattered case, the old king's face smiled without the old king's permission.

"Come, then, little heir," it said, in a voice that had been great men's voices, one after another, for a hundred years. "Bring the book. Bring the rite. I have worn better than you, and I remember how they broke."

Southward, in the dark below Greyford's pass, the fires of the refugees answered the fires of the city, and between the two lights the mountains waited, and under the mountains the Sunken Court opened its doors for the trial of a kingdom.
