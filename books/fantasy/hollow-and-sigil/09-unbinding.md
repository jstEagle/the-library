## 9. Unbinding

The throne room had no doors left to bar. Its great bronze gates hung torn from their hinges, hurled inward by the shockwave that had carried Lena, Tam, and Corwin up the last flight of stairs—and beyond them the hall opened like the ribcage of some enormous dead thing, pillars marching into shadow, banners of a kingdom that no longer existed drooping from corroded rings.

At the far end, beneath a window of colored glass gone gray with grime, sat the throne. And on the throne sat the king.

He did not look like a monster. That was the first cruelty of it. King Aldric—what remained of him—was a broad-shouldered man in his fifties, silver at the temples, dressed in ceremonial mail that no one had polished in years. His hands rested on the arms of the throne with the terrible stillness of a statue. His eyes were open. They were brown, and human, and utterly empty, and they did not track her as she crossed the threshold.

The crown did.

It sat on his brow like a thing grown there rather than forged—a circlet of black iron worked into the shape of thorns, and along its length something moved, a slow shimmer, as if light were being swallowed just beneath the surface. As Lena came closer she felt it begin to *notice* her, a pressure against her mind like a thumb pressed to the center of her forehead.

*So,* said a voice that was not a voice, arriving fully formed inside her skull, courteous and vast. *The little hollow girl comes home to roost.*

Lena stopped three paces from the dais. Tam flanked her right, sword drawn though they both knew steel meant nothing here; Corwin stood behind, clutching the prophecy book to his chest like a shield, his lips already moving through the opening phrases of the rite.

"I'm not here to bargain," she said aloud, because it seemed important to say words with her own mouth, in her own voice, while something else spoke inside her.

*Everyone bargains,* Vessel said. *I have worn kings who swore the same. I wore Aldric's grandfather's advisor for eleven years. I wore a saint once, in the east, until her own faithful pried me loose and burned what was left.* The pressure eased, almost gently. *You misunderstand what is happening here, child. You believe you have come to free him. But look at him. Truly look.*

She looked. And hated herself for looking, because under the emptiness there was *something*—a flicker behind the eyes, faint as a candle seen through a wall, turning toward her the way a drowning man turns toward light.

*Forty years he has been mine,* Vessel continued. *Do you know what remains when I am pulled out? A man who watched his own hands sign death warrants. Who heard his daughter cry for him through his own mouth and could not answer. I am not his prison, Lena Vane. I am his mercy. Break me, and you give him back every memory of every year he spent as my glove.*

"That's not your choice to make for him," Lena said. Her voice shook. She let it shake.

*No,* the entity agreed, and there was something almost like respect in it. *But it may be yours. Hear my offer.*

Corwin's voice rose behind her—"Lena, don't listen, it lies, it has only ever lied"—but the hall seemed to fold around the entity's next words, carrying them to her alone:

*Spare me, and I will spare the valley.*

The word landed in her chest like a stone dropped down a well.

*You think I do not know about Sigil. I have known about Sigil since before your grandmother drew breath. Your guild bought its hiding with my patience—they paid me tribute in silence, in names, in the occasional inconvenient archivist, and I honored the bargain because a hidden valley full of guilty keepers amused me. But the wards are failing. In a season, perhaps two, the world will find your valley on its own, and the world is far less patient than I am. Armies will come. Fires will come. Every soul who ever sang you to sleep will—*

"Stop."

*—die screaming, or worse, be worn. Whereas if you turn around, take Corwin's book, and walk away, I will extend the concealment myself. A thousand years. Your mother grows old in her garden. Your guild's halls stand forever. All it costs is one old man's suffering, which he cannot even feel, and your silence, which no one will ever test—*

"I said *stop*." Her voice cracked across the hall like a whip, and to her astonishment, the pressure receded. Not defeated. Listening.

Because it was true, that was the horror of it. Every word. She could see it all: the valley green and secret, her mother's kitchen, the archive smell of dust and lamp oil, Tam alive beside her instead of bleeding out on some future battlefield. One old man. One crown. One lie, kept the way her whole life had been kept—carefully, by people who loved her, for reasons they never explained.

*There it is,* Vessel murmured. *I can feel how much you love them. It is the sweetest thing I have tasted in a century.*

"It should disgust you," Lena whispered, "how well you understand love."

And then she thought of the ink, still wet, a hundred years after it dried. Of the guild elders burning books in the cellar while telling children they were keepers of memory. Of Corwin in exile, and Tam's brother whose name she'd never learned, and Marrowgate burning on the horizon two nights ago, the sky over it the color of an infected wound.

Her whole life had been someone else's plan. This was the moment the plan expected her to flinch.

"No," Lena said.

She stepped onto the dais.

*Think carefully—*

"You've made one mistake," she said, climbing toward the king, toward the crown, toward the vast cold attention bent upon her. "In a century of wearing men, you never learned what the Hollow Guild actually taught its children. Not the secrets. Not the binding rites. We were taught *endurance*. We were raised in a valley that gave us nothing and asked everything, and we learned to hold grief in our hands without setting it down." She reached the top step. "You're offering me my home. But I've already lost it twice—once when I learned what my family built itself on, and once tonight, when I understood I'd trade the whole world for it if you asked nicely enough. There's nothing left you can threaten me with that I haven't already buried."

She took Aldric's face in her hands. His skin was cool, waxy, a man-shaped absence. Up close his empty eyes were wet.

He was crying. He had probably been crying for forty years.

"Lena!" Corwin's voice, ragged. "The rite—it requires a name! The true name of the bound!"

"I know," she said. "That's why I asked him."

She hadn't—not yet, not aloud—but she leaned close to the king's ear, past the thorned iron, and spoke as if to the flicker behind the eyes, the candle through the wall.

"Aldric of House Merren," she said. "They told me you loved the eastern orchards. That you planted a pear tree the day your daughter was born. That you used to sneak out of council meetings to watch the harvest." The eyes flickered. The wetness spilled over. "Your daughter's name was Sera. She waited for you for nine years before she stopped. She named her son after you. He has your hands—I've seen the portraits. You are not a glove. You are not a mercy. You are a *father*, and you are *in there*, and I am so sorry it took this long for anyone to come."

The candle behind his eyes guttered—and then, impossibly, *flared*.

A sound came out of him, small and ruined, half a word: "*Sera—*"

And in that instant of opening, Lena began the unbinding.

The words of the rite were old, older than the guild, older than the valley—words Corwin had drilled into her on the road until she dreamed them, words that had cost the archivist his tongue's smoothness and nearly his life to carry out of exile. She spoke them now with her hands on the king's face and the crown inches from her fingers, and the moment the first syllable left her lips, Vessel *moved*.

It did not rage. That would have been bearable. Instead it went into her memories like a thief into a house it knew well—the valley first, always the valley, tearing through them with claws of pure negation. Her mother's face blurred. The smell of the archive dissolved even as she grasped for it. The path down to the swimming hole where she'd learned to float on her back and watch hawks—gone, the water gone, the hawks gone, the *name of the valley itself* sliding out of her mind like water through fingers—

*Yield,* Vessel said, gentle as ever, even now. *Say the word and I give them all back. Every memory. I will make the valley greener in your remembering than it ever was.*

Lena held the words in her mouth like stones she refused to swallow, and kept speaking.

Her sixth summer went. The lullaby her mother hummed went. Tam's face wavered—she grabbed at it, held it, felt it tear anyway, and she wept as she spoke the rite because she could no longer remember why she was crying, only that she must not stop, that somewhere behind the fog there had been a person worth crying for—

*Last chance, hollow girl.*

"—and by root and by ruin," she gasped, "by the name he answered to before you, **ALDRIC OF HOUSE MERREN, I UNMAKE YOUR BARGAIN**—"

She seized the crown.

Iron thorns bit her palms. The world went white and then black and then a color that had no name, and inside it she felt Vessel pour itself into her the way floodwater pours into a broken dike—vast, shrieking, offering, begging, promising her the valley, her mother, her *name*, and she held on, held on, held on, anchored by the one memory it could not find because she had hidden it herself, deep, in the place grief keeps things: not a place, not a face, but a single sentence Corwin had said on the road, *the truth is the only home that can't be taken from you*—

And she broke the crown the way you break a spell: by refusing, finally and completely, to believe in it.

The sound was not loud. It was the sound of a held breath released after a hundred years.

The black iron came apart in her hands like frost in sunlight, dissolving upward into motes that winked out one by one, and the presence that had worn kings tore free of the room with a last silent scream that Lena felt in her teeth and then—nothing. Silence. Ordinary, dusty, human silence.

King Aldric slumped forward into her arms, breathing, weeping, *heavy*, a real weight, a man's weight.

"My daughter," he rasped. "Is Sera—"

"She had a son," Lena managed, holding him up, her palms bleeding freely onto his mail. "He has your hands."

The old king looked at her—at her torn hands, her tear-streaked face, the fog behind her eyes where a life used to be—and whatever he saw there made him grip her arms with sudden, desperate strength.

"Child," he said, voice cracking like dry wood, "what did it take from you?"

Lena opened her mouth to answer.

And found she did not know her own name.
