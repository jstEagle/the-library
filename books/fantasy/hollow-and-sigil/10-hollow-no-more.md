## 10. Hollow No More

The crown did not shatter all at once.

It came apart the way a long-held breath comes apart—first a crack, thin as a hair, singing through the throne room like a struck bell; then a thousand cracks, branching, racing; and then Vessel screamed with King Aldric's throat, with a hundred dead kings' throats, with throats it had worn and discarded across three centuries, and the sound broke against the walls of the hall and fell as silence.

Lena stood in the falling gold.

Pieces of the crown drifted around her, unhurried, as though the air had thickened to honey. Each fragment held a sliver of light that was not light—memories, she understood dimly, the stolen years of every wearer, spilling out at last. One piece turned past her cheek and she saw in it a young man laughing in an orchard, and knew it for Aldric before the binding, before the guild had come to him with their careful words and their careful knives of law.

Then the pieces remembered they were metal, and fell.

They rang on the flagstones like the end of something. When the last one stilled, the man on the throne was only a man.

He was old. That was the first cruelty of it. Aldric had been thirty when they had crowned him; the body that sagged now against the armrests had lived every year since, even if the mind inside had been buried under Vessel's weight like a seed under stone. His hair was white and long and unwashed. His hands, when he lifted them, trembled—not with weakness, Lena thought, but with the astonishment of being answered after so long.

"Is it—" His voice was rust and dust. He tried again. "Is it morning?"

Lena knelt. Her knees found the cold stone and she was grateful for it, for something simple to hold her up. "It's over," she said. "You're free."

He looked at her for a long time with eyes the color of rainwater, eyes that had seen everything and been permitted to say nothing. Then he began, very quietly, to weep—and it was not the weeping of grief, or not only that. It was the weeping of a man setting down a weight he had carried so long his spine had grown around it.

Behind her, Tam made a small sound. She did not turn. Some things a person deserved to witness without witnesses watching them witness.

* * *

They brought Aldric down from the dais slowly, an arm over Tam's shoulders, Lena carrying the train of a robe that smelled of centuries. In the corridor outside the throne room, the palace staff stood in clusters, blinking, like people waking from a shared dream. A chambermaid dropped a stack of linens when she saw the king's face—the true face, the old face—and pressed both hands to her mouth.

"He'll need physicians," Tam said. "Real ones. And food. And time."

"And truth," Lena said.

Tam glanced at her. In the gray light through the corridor windows his face was thinner than she'd ever seen it, scored with new lines since Marrowgate, since the chains, since his brother's name had finally left his mouth in front of someone who could hear it without flinching. "That's coming," he said. "Whether anyone's ready or not."

It was already coming. Word ran ahead of them through the city like fire through dry grass: the king is freed, the king is old, the thing in the crown is dead. By the time they reached the great doors of the keep, a crowd had gathered in the square beyond—not cheering, not yet, but standing, thousands of them, in the particular silence of people waiting to learn what world they had woken into.

Aldric stopped at the top of the steps. The wind took his white hair and he let it.

"People of the realm," he began, and then stopped, because his voice failed him, because there were no words left in a throat that had spoken only another's will for a hundred years. He tried once more. What came out was smaller than a speech. "I am sorry," said King Aldric, to his kingdom, in a voice like a cracked bell. "I am here. I am sorry."

The silence held a moment longer. Then somewhere in the crowd a woman began to sing—an old song, a harvest song, the kind sung in valleys and market squares by people who had never cared who sat the throne—and the crowd caught it the way dry grass catches flame, until the whole square was singing and the sound rose up the keep's face and washed over the three of them standing in the doorway like a tide.

Lena felt tears on her own face and did not remember starting them.

* * *

She went home in autumn, when the passes were still open.

Home. The word had changed shape in her mouth over the months of marching and courts and ash. It no longer meant warmth. It meant a place she would have to look at honestly, and that was harder than any road she had walked.

The valley had not hidden itself well enough to survive what she had done. Everyone had known this would be the price; she had paid it anyway; and still, cresting the last ridge on foot—she had sent the horse back at the border, unable to bear the thought of hooves on that ground—she was not prepared.

There was no veil anymore. From the ridge, where for a hundred years travelers had seen nothing but crag and cloud, the whole valley lay open to the sky like a palm unclenched: the river, the terraced fields gone half to weed, the slate roofs of Hollowmere darkened with moss, and above them all the guildhall with its bell tower, its windows catching the low sun. Smoke rose from a few chimneys. Someone was still there. Someone had stayed.

The wards were dead. She felt it as a physical absence, the way an amputee feels weather. All her life the valley had hummed beneath her awareness, a lullaby so constant she had mistaken it for silence. Now the true silence was enormous, and in it she could hear the river plainly, and birdsong, and her own footsteps on the scree.

They had never heard those sounds before. None of them. Not once in a hundred years.

She stood on the ridge until the sun went down, and then she went in.

* * *

Hollowmere was emptier than she had ever known it. Half the houses stood open-doored, abandoned in the panic after the wards failed, when the valley's people had poured out into a world they'd been told was myth. But not all. Old Marta was still there, sweeping her step as though the century hadn't ended. The Tallow brothers were mending a roof, badly. And in the guildhall, when Lena pushed open the great doors that had sealed behind her the night she fled with a stolen prophecy book and a bleeding stranger, she found forty people sitting in the nave among the overturned shelves of the archive, sorting pages by candlelight.

Corwin Hale looked up from a table buried in ledgers. He was older than the mountains some days, but tonight his eyes were clear.

"You came back," he said. Not surprised. Corwin was never surprised; it was his most infuriating virtue.

"It's my mess."

"It's your order." He gestured at the room—at the archivists, the few remaining guild members, the children of Hollowmere who had nowhere else to go and had begun, tentatively, to help. "We've been cataloguing. Everything. The binding rites, the payment records, the names of every proxy the guild installed on every throne from here to the coast. It's all going south to the Sunken Court when the passes close. Every page."

"Even the ones that damn us?"

"Especially those." He set down his pen. For a moment the shame she had first seen in his exile cottage passed over his face again, old and familiar as a scar. "Lena. There is no version of this where we keep anything back. You know that. You built this."

"I know what I built." She walked the length of the nave, touching nothing, looking at everything—the archive she had spent her girlhood dusting, the prophecy book itself lying open on a lectern under glass now, its final page filled at last, the wet ink dried forever. "I want the guild dissolved," she said. "Not reformed. Dissolved. The name dies with the secrets."

Murmurs moved through the candlelit room. Corwin didn't flinch. "And in its place?"

"In its place, nothing with a seal. Nothing with a tower. An order with no walls, no hidden archive, no oath of silence." She turned to face them all. "People who learn things and tell the truth about them, even when the truth is ugly. Even when the ugly truth is ours. Justice that answers to no throne—we said that in the Sunken Court, and I meant it, and I mean it more now that I'm standing in the house that proves why."

An old woman near the back—Marta, of course it was Marta, broom still in hand as if she'd swept her way straight into history—spoke up in her cracked voice. "And who leads this wall-less order, then? You?"

"No one leads it. Not alone." Lena looked at Corwin. "But someone has to keep the ledger honest. Someone who knows exactly how heavy a secret gets." She held out her hand. "First Archivist. Open archives. Your confession, printed and posted in every free city, in your own words. That's the price of the title."

For a long moment Corwin Hale, disgraced keeper of a dying order, looked at her hand. Then he laughed—a rough, disbelieving sound—and took it.

"Dissolve the Hollow Guild," he said to the room. "Long live whatever comes next."

Outside, the valley lay open under the stars, unhidden, unashamed, its lullaby finished at last.

* * *

Winter came early that year, and with it a rider.

Lena was in the new scriptorium—they had taken over the millhouse, because mills had windows and guildhalls did not—when Tam's knock came, and behind him a courier crusted with road-ice, holding out a letter sealed not with any royal sigil but with the plain wax-and-quill mark of the Sunken Court.

She read it twice. The first time her hands shook too much to take in the words.

The tribunal had finished its work. The guild's crimes were entered into the public record, read aloud in every free city, posted on every door. Pardons were issued—hers included, though she found she barely felt it; you cannot pardon someone out of a grief like hers. The proxies were being rooted out, thrones trembling from the coast to the passes. And at the bottom of the page, in the tribunal's spare, unsentimental hand:

*The Court requests the presence of Lena Vane, formerly of the Hollow Guild, at the spring session. There is a matter requiring her judgment. It concerns the valley.*

She looked up. Through the millhouse window, snow was beginning to fall on Hollowmere—on the open fields, the mended roofs, the graves she had helped dig for the ones who hadn't survived the exposure of the world. Somewhere beyond the pass, King Aldric was learning to rule again with a council of commoners at his elbow and a lifetime of borrowed years to answer for. Somewhere south, the Sunken Court waited with its scales and its terrible patience.

*A matter concerning the valley.* No one used that phrase idly anymore. The valley was the wound at the center of the age, the place where a hundred years of lies had finally bled out into the light.

Lena folded the letter, warmed her hands at the stove, and reached for her cloak.

Spring was a long way off. But some journeys, she had learned, were best begun in the snow.
