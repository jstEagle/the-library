## 1. The Night the Stars Went Out

The pass above Kettle Hollow had a name on no map Rafael Okafor ever drew, and that was the first thing she liked about it. She'd christened it herself at nineteen — the Zipper, for the way the trail stitched up the mountainside in a crooked silver line visible only when the moon hit the scree just so — and in six years of running goods through Thornfell's back teeth, she had never once gotten lost on it.

Tonight she was thinking about getting lost. Not because the trail had changed, but because the sky had.

"Raf." Doss's voice came low from behind her, the mule's lead creaking in his grip. "You seeing this?"

"I'm seeing it."

Above the notch of the saddle ahead, where the sky should have been salted with the cold bright scatter of late autumn — the Shepherd, the Loom, the three sisters of the Northern Crown — there was nothing. Not cloud-cover nothing, not fog nothing. The kind of nothing that has edges. She could see it happening: a star near the zenith dimming like a coal breathed on, holding one last orange ember of itself, and then going out. Then another, farther east. Then a whole cluster, snuffed in a breath, as if a hand were passing over them one by one, deliberate, unhurried, thorough.

"That's not weather," Doss said.

"No," Rafael said. "That's not weather."

She stopped walking and did what she always did when the world misbehaved: she took out her maps. The satchel rode high on her hip, waxed canvas stiff with cold, and inside it lay the night's cargo — not the brandy or the bolt-cloth strapped to the mules, but eleven charts, hand-inked, each one a working document of the passes between Vess and the high country. Her own work, mostly. Copies of copies underneath, going back through other hands to older hands, all the way down to paper gone brown and brittle as leaf-litter.

She unrolled the top chart by feel, angling it toward the last light they had, which was a shuttered lantern turned down to a coin-sized glow. The Zipper was there under her own ink, confident and fine-lined, with her marginalia in the margins where marginalia belonged: *scree unstable after rain; watch for ice at the second switchback; good camp in the lee below the horn.*

And then, as she watched, the line of the trail began to move.

Not smudge. Not bleed. It moved the way a worm moves under bark — a slow traveling ripple passing along the ink from the southern edge toward the north, and where it passed, the line it left behind was thinner. Paler. As though someone were drinking the drawing out of the paper.

"Doss," she said quietly. "Put down the lead. Come look at this."

He came. He was a big man, Doss, patient as a plow horse, and he had been running the passes almost as long as she had, and in all that time she had never seen him afraid of anything smaller than an avalanche. He looked at the map for a long moment. The ripple reached the northern edge of the chart and the trail simply — ended. Not faded to nothing. Ended, the way a sentence ends when the speaker forgets what he was saying.

"Maybe the ink's freezing," he offered.

"Ink doesn't freeze in lines, Doss. Ink freezes in blots."

Another star went out. She felt it before she saw it — felt it in her teeth, a faint high pressure like the moment before thunder — and when she looked up, the darkness where it had been seemed deeper than the darkness around it, as if the sky were developing holes instead of lights.

Below them, far down the valley, the beacon-fires of the ward-stations burned in their ancient sequence: Hollow Ford, Grey Saddle, the Watcher's Cleft, each one answering the next in a chain as old as the country itself. Rafael had grown up falling asleep to that rhythm through her bedroom shutters, a slow pulse of orange against the ceiling, the heartbeat of Thornfell keeping time. Every child in the high valleys learned it before they learned their letters. *As long as the fires answer, you are home.*

Hollow Ford flared. Grey Saddle answered.

Then Grey Saddle's light stuttered — flickered like a candle in a doorway — and went dark between one pulse and the next, without ever having finished its turn.

The Watcher's Cleft held its fire alone for the space of ten heartbeats, burning bravely at nothing, and then it too guttered, and the valley floor below them went black from end to end.

And Rafael heard the wards snap.

There was no other word for it. Later she would try a dozen words — *crack, tear, break* — and reject them all, because none of them carried the sound. It was harp strings cut close to the bridge: a chorus of them, hundreds, thousands, a chord sustained so long that no living person remembered it beginning, and then severed all at once. The note didn't stop so much as get *removed*, leaving behind a silence with the shape of sound pressed into it, the way a body leaves a shape in a bed. She felt it pass through her chest and out her back. The mules screamed and hauled against their leads. Somewhere downslope, a rockslide started and stopped, as if even the mountain had flinched.

Doss had his hat off. She hadn't seen him take it off. He was standing bareheaded under the ruined sky with his mouth moving, and after a moment she realized he was reciting the ward-litany, the old children's rhyme, the one every soul in Thornfell knew:

*Fire to fire and stone to stone,*
*the Spire remembers what we've known—*

He couldn't finish it. His voice kept snagging on the third line like a boot on a root, and Rafael understood, with a cold that had nothing to do with the altitude, that he wasn't forgetting the words. The words were forgetting themselves. She tried to catch the third line in her own head and found a hole where it should have been, smooth-sided, deliberate, a gap exactly the size of something that used to live there.

"What was the third line?" she asked him.

"There isn't—" He frowned. "It's two lines. It was always two lines."

"It was four lines. My mother sang all four."

"Your mother sang it wrong, then." But his hands, taking up the mule's lead again, were not steady.

* * *

They made the shelter cave below the horn by dead reckoning and the memory of her feet, which was all either of them had now. The stars were gone — all of them, not dimmed but *gone*, the whole vault of heaven become a single seamless black, so absolute that Rafael kept catching herself reaching up as if to touch it, the way you probe a tooth that isn't there anymore. Without starlight the snowfields gave back nothing. The world ended three feet from the lantern in every direction, and beyond that boundary there was simply more of the same black, above and below and around, until walking began to feel less like travel than like falling very slowly sideways.

In the cave, she built the fire small and got the satchel open across her knees.

All eleven charts were changing.

She laid them out in order, oldest beneath, newest on top, and watched the sickness move through them like frost through a windowpane. The oldest chart — a copy of a copy of a survey done in some great-great-grandmother's day, the vellum soft as cloth — was worst. Its mountain ranges were still there, but the names along them were lifting off the paper, literally lifting, the letters rising a hair's breadth off the vellum and hanging there trembling like midges before settling back down as blankness. *Grey Saddle.* Gone. *The Watcher's Cleft.* Gone. Even *Thornfell* itself, the name of the whole range, the name of the whole country, was thinning as she watched, its hard thorny letters going translucent, going ghostly, going—

She put her palm flat over it, absurdly, as if she could hold the word down.

Under her hand, the ink stilled. Just for a moment. Just while she touched it. When she lifted her fingers, the fading resumed, slower now, grudging, like a fire banked rather than quenched.

"Raf." Doss was at the cave mouth, looking out at nothing. "The border stones."

"What about them?"

"When I took water at the stream back there. The marker stone at the crossing. It's—" He turned around. In the firelight his face looked older by twenty years. "It's not marked anymore. The carving's smooth. Like nobody ever cut it."

Rafael sat back on her heels. Outside, the wind came down the pass carrying the smell of snow and, faintly, underneath it, something else — something burnt and mineral and old, like a forge gone cold, like the inside of a chimney. She had smelled it once before, years ago, standing too close to a ward-station when a keeper was renewing the fire. It was the smell of the wards themselves. And now it was loose in the air, untethered, drifting like ash off a dead blaze.

Her family had never talked much about the old days. That was true of every family in the high valleys — the wars and the bindings and the founding were all folded away into litanies and beacons and things sung to children, and nobody asked questions, because the wards held and the stars shone and what was there to ask? But her mother had one story she told only once, the winter before she died, half-dreaming by the stove: that the Okafors hadn't always been cartographers. That somewhere far back there had been an ancestor who drew something more than roads. Who drew the country itself the way you'd draw a fence around a sleeping animal — carefully, lovingly, knowing that if the line ever broke, everything inside it would wake up and walk away.

Rafael had filed the story under bedtime nonsense, the way you file most of what mothers say by firelight.

Now she looked down at the charts dissolving under the lantern light, at the trails she had walked since childhood erasing themselves stroke by stroke, and thought: *the line is breaking, and everything inside it is starting to walk away, and I am the only person on this mountain holding the pen down.*

"We go to Vess," she said.

Doss looked at her. "Vess is two days' hard walking, and the passes are—" He gestured at the cave mouth, at the starless dark, at everything.

"The passes are the only thing I know better than my own hands. And Vess has record-houses. Chancellery offices. If something's happened to the country, there'll be paper about it. There's always paper." She was already rolling the charts, sliding them back into the satchel with a care that surprised her — handling them less like documents now than like wounded things. "We deliver the brandy, we ask questions, we find out who turned off the sky."

"And if the answers are bad?"

"Then at least they'll be answers." She cinched the satchel. "I can't navigate a question mark, Doss. I need this to be a place with edges again."

He grunted — his version of agreement, of comfort, of prayer — and went to see to the mules.

Rafael stood a moment longer at the fire. Above the cave mouth, where the Shepherd should have been wheeling his slow circle, the black was total and patient. She found herself doing something she hadn't done since she was small: she said the litany in her head, all of it, or what was left of it. Two lines now. Maybe it had always been two lines. Maybe the other two had never existed anywhere except in the minds of people who needed them.

*Fire to fire and stone to stone,* she thought, *the Spire remembers what we've known.*

Somewhere north of her, past the Teeth, past the high white shoulders of the range, the Spire of Thornfell stood in the dark. She had drawn it a hundred times from a hundred angles, its silhouette as familiar as her signature. She had never once wondered what burned inside it.

She wondered now. And beneath the wondering, quiet as a draft under a door, came a thought that did not feel entirely like her own — that felt, in fact, like something very old turning over in its sleep and noticing her:

*The maps know your name, Rafael Okafor. Come and ask them why.*

She snuffed the lantern. In the dark, in the cave, in a country that was forgetting itself one letter at a time, she slept badly, and dreamed of ink.
