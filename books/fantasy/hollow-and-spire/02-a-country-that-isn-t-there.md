## 2. A Country That Isn't There

The road to Vess was the kind of road Rafael Okafor could walk blindfolded, which was fortunate, because for most of the descent she felt blind in every way that mattered.

No stars. Not clouds, not fog, not the pale wash of a moon behind weather — nothing. She had looked up a hundred times on the way down from the passes, and a hundred times the sky had answered her with the flat, patient black of a closed ledger. The mountains at her back should have been visible even by starlight, their snowfields holding a faint gray luminescence like old bone. She kept turning to check. The mountains were there. She could feel them the way you feel a wall in a dark room — by the way the wind bent around them, by the cold that rolled off the high snow and pooled in the valley like water finding its level. But when she looked directly at them, her eyes slid off, unable to find an edge, a peak, a silhouette. The Thornfells had become a rumor at her own back.

She told herself it was exhaustion. She had been awake for two days, and the last night had ended with harp strings snapping somewhere behind her eyes and her maps rewriting themselves in her satchel like living things. Any reasonable person would be seeing things.

Rafael had never been entirely reasonable, but she was precise, and precision was its own kind of sanity. So she did what she always did when the world went strange: she measured it. She counted her paces down the switchbacks. She noted where the pine line began — lower than it should be, by a good fifty yards, as if the forest itself were nervous. She marked the place where the Maren stream crossed the road, and found the stream running thin and confused, braiding and unbraiding like it had forgotten which channel was its own.

The land remembers, her grandmother used to say, rolling out a chart across the kitchen table, flour-dusted fingers on vellum. Land remembers longer than people do. That's why we draw it — not to own it, but to remind it what it is.

Rafael walked faster.

* * *

Vess announced itself the way it always had: by smell. Woodsmoke and tanneries and the river mud of the lowlands, and under it all the brine-and-tar note of the trade road, because Vess sat where the mountain routes spilled their cargo into the river barges. Every smuggler worth her salt knew Vess as the last soft place before the hard country — the town where you sold what you'd carried and bought the lies you'd need to carry it home again.

Rafael came in at dusk, hood up, satchel strapped tight against her spine. The town looked right. Lamplit windows, carts in the square, the customs house with its green copper roof gone black with evening. It looked so right that some knotted thing in her chest began, cautiously, to loosen. Maybe the night had been a fever. Maybe the stars would be out over Vess, and the mountains behind her would have their edges back, and she could laugh about all of it over a plate of river eel and too much bad wine.

Then she reached the square, and stopped.

There was a map on the customs house wall. Public notice boards always carried one — the Chancellery's great survey chart of the provinces, updated every few years, the kind of map so ubiquitous that nobody actually looked at it anymore. Rafael had looked at it a hundred times in her life. She knew the Thornfells sat in its northwestern corner like a clenched fist, the passes threaded through the knuckles, Vess itself a small inked dot at the wrist with the river running out of it south.

The map on the wall showed empty country.

Not blank — that she could have understood, could have explained as damage, as a printing error, as some clerk's oversight. It was worse than blank. The northwestern corner of the chart showed gentle hills fading into open grassland, a few tidy farm symbols, a road that bent where no road had ever bent, and the river — the river was there, but it began in the middle of nowhere, rising out of nameless ground like water from a stone. The cartographer had drawn it with complete confidence. Hachures for the gentle hills, a border post symbol, a scale bar. Everything correct except the truth.

Rafael stood in front of it for a long time. A lamplighter passed behind her and climbed his ladder and did not light the lamp beside the map, because the map was already lit by the one across the street, and she barely registered him. Her breath came out slow and white.

She unslung her satchel, right there in the square, and pulled out one of her own charts with hands that were almost steady. Her working map of the northern passes — the one she'd drawn herself, at nineteen, from three seasons of survey work. She held it up beside the wall chart.

Her map showed mountains. The wall showed none.

One of them was a forgery, said a voice in her head that sounded unpleasantly like a customs officer, and she crushed the thought before it could finish forming, because she knew her own hand, knew every quaver of it, knew the little tick she used for scree slopes and the way her north arrow leaned a half-degree left of true because she'd calibrated it against a lodestone that had its own opinions. That was her map. She had made it. The mountains were there because she had stood on them.

She put the chart away. Around her, Vess went on with its evening. A woman argued cheerfully with a fishmonger. Two boys chased a dog between the carts. Nobody looked at the map. Nobody ever looked at the map.

"Evening, traveler." A man had come up beside her — heavyset, gray-stubbled, with the easy slouch of someone whose authority had never once been questioned. Customs house badge on his coat, green enamel, the river-and-bridge sigil of Vess. His eyes were on her satchel with the flat professional hunger of every customs man in every border town since the invention of borders. "Long way from somewhere, are you?"

"Down from the north," Rafael said. "The passes."

Something happened to his face. It was very small — a flicker, a hesitation, like a man reaching for a stair that isn't where his foot remembers. "The passes," he repeated.

"The mountain routes. Thornfell."

He smiled then, kindly, the way you smile at a drunk or a child or a madwoman. "There's no mountains north of here, friend. Just the downs, and past them the grass country. You've come the long way round, looks like." He nodded at her satchel. "You'll want to declare anything you're carrying. Trade goods, documents, that sort of thing. House is open till the ninth bell."

"The Thornfell passes," Rafael said, and heard her own voice come out too flat, too careful. "The Spire. The ward-keeps. The high road to Callowmoor."

"Never heard of any of it." He said it easily, without malice, and that was the worst part. There was no lie in him to catch. He believed it the way he believed the river flowed south — completely, without thinking, because the world had always been that way. "Names like that turn up in old songs sometimes. Mountains made of glass, and all that. But there's nothing up there now but grass." He patted her shoulder, familiar as an uncle. "Ninth bell. Don't make me come find you."

He walked off toward the customs house, and Rafael stood in the lamplight of a town that had forgotten a country, and felt the ground tilt under her even though her feet told her it was flat.

* * *

She did the only sensible thing. She found a tavern.

The Drowned Bell was the smugglers' house in Vess — had been for as long as there had been smugglers, which was to say, forever. Its keeper, a wide woman named Bess Arda with forearms like hawsers, had fenced Rafael's first contraband at fifteen and never once asked what was in the boxes. If anyone in Vess remembered the north, it would be Bess. Smugglers' memory outlives official memory the way cockroaches outlive empires.

The common room was warm and loud and smelled of spilled ale and roasting onions. Rafael pushed through it to the bar, and Bess looked up from tapping a cask, and Rafael felt her whole body brace like a woman about to catch a falling thing.

"Bess. It's Rafael. Okafor. I've got a run to settle up — the Corvel wool, three weeks back, and I've got more coming down if the roads hold—"

"New face," Bess said pleasantly. "Don't know you, friend, but the ale's good and the rooms are clean. What'll you have?"

The words went into Rafael like a blade between ribs, sliding clean, no resistance at all. She had known this woman eleven years. Bess had taught her how to tell a forged Chancellery seal by the smell of the wax. Bess had held her hair back the night her father's death-letter came and Rafael drank herself under the table in this exact room.

"Bess Arda," Rafael said. "You know me. You've known me since I was—"

"First time in Vess?" Bess's smile didn't waver. There was nothing behind it, no flicker, no guilt, no buried recognition fighting to the surface. Nothing. "You've got an accent from somewhere upriver. Callowmoor way? We get a lot of Callowmoor folk."

Rafael looked around the room. The smugglers were there — she recognized types if not faces now, and that was its own horror, that the faces themselves had gone soft in her memory, as if the country's erasure were eating outward from the maps into her own head. A card game in the corner. A pair of river-boatmen arguing over a ledger. A thin man in the shadows by the fire with the watchful stillness of a professional fence.

"Does anyone here," she said, and her voice cracked and she hated it, "remember the Thornfell mountains? Does anyone — the high passes, the ward-keeps, the Spire with the fire — anyone at all—"

The room had gone quiet in the way rooms do when a stranger starts shouting about things that don't exist. The card players glanced up and away. The boatmen smirked. The thin man by the fire watched her with new, sharpened interest.

"Love," said Bess gently, and there was real kindness in it, which made it worse, "sit down before you fall down. You look like you've walked through a bad night. There's no Thornfell. There's never been a Thornfell." She slid a mug of ale across the bar, unprompted, uncharged. "Drink. You'll feel less like the world's ending."

Rafael took the mug because her hands needed to hold something. The ale was the same ale it had always been, dark and bitter with a taste of the river in it, and that sameness was somehow the most terrifying thing in the room. Everything else had been rewritten. The ale remained.

She drank it standing up, and left coin on the bar, and Bess said, "No charge for the first one, friend," and Rafael walked out of the Drowned Bell into the dark with the taste of home in her mouth and no home left to taste of.

* * *

She should have left. Every instinct she had — the smuggler's instinct, the animal one, the one that kept you alive in the passes — said leave now, tonight, before the town finished noticing you. Walk south to Callowmoor, or east to the river cities, find some place where the night's madness hadn't reached.

But her feet took her to the customs house instead, and she was honest enough with herself, even then, to know why. Because if it was real — if Thornfell was gone from every record, every mind, every chart in Vess — then the only proof left in the world was in her satchel. And she needed someone with authority to look at it and tell her she wasn't mad. Even a customs officer. Even the enemy.

The customs house was nearly empty at the ninth bell. A single lamp burned at the duty desk, behind which sat a woman with a narrow face and spectacles and the patient expression of someone who had spent thirty years telling travelers their paperwork was wrong. A brass plate on the desk read DUTY ASSESSOR M. CORVANE.

"Declare your goods," said Corvane, without looking up.

"I want to file a report," Rafael said. "A discrepancy. In the survey charts."

That got the woman's attention, as she'd known it would. Discrepancy was a word with weight in a customs house. Corvane looked up, took in Rafael's road-worn state, the satchel, the accent. "Discrepancy."

"Your wall chart. The provincial survey. It's missing the entire Thornfell range. The mountains north of Vess. The border wards, the passes, the Spire — the whole country."

Corvane's expression didn't change, but something behind it did — a small settling, like dust finding its level. "There are no mountains north of Vess," she said. "There is grassland and the downs, and the river rises in the hills past Marrow Ford. This is established. This is in every record the Chancellery holds. What exactly are you proposing to report?"

"That your records are wrong."

"Show me what you're basing that on."

And there it was — the door, and Rafael walked through it because she was too tired and too frightened to do anything else. She unslung the satchel and laid her charts out on the duty desk, one by one, with the care of a surgeon laying out instruments. Her survey of the northern passes. The trade-route map with the smuggler markings she'd been born too honest to ever fully erase. Her father's old chart of the ward-keeps, ink gone brown with age. And at the bottom, wrapped in oilcloth because it was the one thing she owned that could not be replaced: the Sela chart. The old one. Vellum gone soft as cloth, drawn in a hand that was unmistakably the ancestor of her own.

"My family are cartographers," Rafael said. "Four generations. We've mapped Thornfell since before the Chancellery existed. This is the northern range. These are the passes. This—" her finger stopped on the Spire, drawn at the heart of the range like a candle flame at the center of a web, "—this is the Spire of Thornfell. You can send a rider north and see it from the downs in a day and a half. It's been burning since before your grandmother was born."

Corvane put on her spectacles. She examined the charts with the same flat competence she'd have given a shipment of untaxed wool, and Rafael watched her face for the flicker, the hesitation, the stair that wasn't where the foot remembered.

It never came. Corvane looked at the mountains and saw paper.

"These are forgeries," she said at last. Not unkindly. That was the horror of it — she sounded almost sorry. "And quite elaborate ones. The vellum's been aged artificially — you can tell by the handling, someone's rubbed soot into the creases. The linework imitates the old pre-Chancellery survey style. Someone's gone to a great deal of trouble." She began, without asking, to square the charts into a stack. "Where did you get them?"

"I drew them," Rafael said. "I drew that one myself." She put her hand flat on her own survey map, the nineteen-year-old's map, the one with the left-leaning north arrow. "I stood on every mountain on this chart. I measured them."

"Madam." Corvane's voice was gentle as a nurse's. "There are no mountains."

The words fell into Rafael like stones into a well, and she listened for them to land, and they didn't. They just kept falling.

"I'm going to have to impound these," Corvane continued, already producing a docket book, a string, a seal. "Forged documents presented in connection with trade — it's procedure, you understand. You can petition for their return through the assessor's office in Callowmoor, if you like, though I'll be honest, the backlog is years. You'll sign here, and here. You're free to go. I'd suggest a meal and a bed. You've had a shock of some kind, I think. The road does that to people, coming out of the grass country alone. It's very empty up there. Very quiet. People's minds fill the silence with... things."

She was writing as she talked, and Rafael watched her own maps disappear into a canvas impound bag — her father's chart, her own surveys, four generations of her family's hands — and heard herself say, "The Sela chart isn't a forgery. It's older than your office. It's older than your Chancellery. It's—"

"It's the best work of the forger's art I've seen in ten years at this desk," Corvane said, and for just one moment — one — her spectacles caught the lamplight and her eyes behind them were not patient at all. They were quick, and hard, and interested. "Whoever made it wanted very much for someone to believe in these mountains. That's the whole art of forgery, isn't it? Belief." She tied the bag. Stamped the docket. Slid it across the desk. "Receipt. Good night, madam. And do see someone about that hand — it's shaking."

Rafael looked down. Her hand was shaking. She took the docket without letting their fingers touch, and walked out of the customs house into the dark of Vess, and the door closed behind her with a sound like a book shutting.

* * *

She made it three streets before she stopped, in the mouth of the tannery alley where the smell covered everything and no one lingered, and put her back against the wall, and let herself shake properly, all of her, like a wet dog.

They had taken the maps. All of them. The proof. Four generations of her family, gone into a canvas bag behind a customs desk, and in the morning they would be gone entirely — burned as forgeries, or filed in some archive where no one would ever look, and the world would go on agreeing that there was nothing north of Vess but grass.

And she had stood there and let it happen. Because what could she do? Every person in the town agreed with the customs officer. The ale agreed with the customs officer. The map on the wall, the road signs, the river rising out of nameless ground — everything agreed. She was one woman with shaking hands insisting that a whole country existed, and the whole world, politely, kindly, without any malice at all, insisting back that it didn't.

That was the thing she couldn't stop shaking about. Not the theft. Not the loss. It was the kindness. If they'd been cruel she could have fought. But the whole town looked at her with the gentle, concerned eyes you'd give a madwoman, and offered her a free ale, and told her to get some rest, and every one of them meant it.

She pressed her palms against her eyes until she saw sparks. Think, she told herself. You're a cartographer. Map the problem.

What she knew: the mountains were still there. She'd felt them all day at her back, the cold rolling off the snow, the wind bending. The land hadn't moved. Whatever had happened last night — the stars going out, the wards snapping, the maps rewriting themselves — it hadn't erased the country. It had erased the country's record. Its name. Its place in the world's memory. The mountains stood, and no one could see them, and no one could say them, and that was a different thing from nothing, and a more terrible thing.

What she didn't know: why. And who. And whether the Chancellery had done this, or something older than the Chancellery, or whether her own family's work — the wards, the fire, whatever it was her ancestors had actually done up there in the high country they'd never spoken of — had finally, after all these years, come due.

And one more thing she didn't know, which arrived in her mind just then, unbidden, in the voice of the thin man by the fire in the Drowned Bell, the one with the watchful stillness, the one who had watched her the way a fence watches a mark or a hunter watches a trail:

Who else in Vess was lying?

Because Corvane's eyes had been quick and hard and interested, for one moment, before the patience closed over them again like water. And Bess had given her a free ale, and Bess never gave anything free. And the town agreed too completely, too smoothly, the way a witness agrees when the testimony has been rehearsed.

Rafael pushed off the wall. Her hands had gone still. Somewhere in the dark of the alley, a rat moved through the tannery refuse, and above the rooftops of Vess the sky was still black and starless and wrong.

The maps were in the customs house. The customs house kept its impound room locked, and every lock in Vess was a lock she'd known since she was fifteen, because Bess Arda had taught her locks along with forged seals, back when they'd both thought the world would hold still.

Rafael pulled her hood up and started walking back toward the square.

She was going to steal her family back.
