## 3. The Ward-Keeper's Daughter

Imre Vashti had been drinking in the Salt Cellar for three hours when the customs clerk came in talking too loudly about a madwoman from the mountains.

He was young, that clerk, with the soft hands and softer certainty of a man who had never been further than the Vess fish market. He set his tankard down on the bar and said, "You should have seen her. Swears up and down there's a whole country north of the passes. Thornfell, she calls it. Drew maps of it. Maps!" He laughed. "Confiscated the lot. Forgeries, obviously. Good ones, though — you could almost believe—"

The word *Thornfell* went through Imre like a hook through the gill.

She did not move. She had learned, in eleven years since the Chancellery struck her name from the ward-rolls, how to be furniture: how to sit at the end of a bar with her cup and her gray coat and let the world flow around her without once catching on her edges. She kept her eyes on her cup. The dregs made a shape like a coastline.

"Mountains," another drinker said. "What mountains?"

"That's the thing." The clerk leaned in, pleased to be the center of something. "Nobody remembers any. But she wouldn't stop. Kept saying we'd all been there. Kept saying it like she knew our names."

Imre paid for her cup with coins counted out slow, so her hand would not shake, and walked out into the rain.

*Thornfell.*

No one had said that name aloud in Vess in nine days. Not because it was forbidden — because it was gone. She had tested it herself, the morning after the sky went black over the north. She'd stood in the market square and asked a fruit-seller, casually, what lay beyond the Teeth, and the woman had looked at her with mild blue incomprehension and said, *Beyond? There's nothing beyond. There's just the edge of the survey charts.* And when Imre had pressed — the passes, the high country, the spire — the woman's face had done something terrible and gentle, the way a face does when someone speaks of a dead child you never had. Pity. Confusion. A flicker of grief for a hole where a memory should be.

Nine days, and the Chancellery's clerks had burned or buried every record, and the country of Thornfell had been amputated from the world so cleanly that even the ache was gone.

But not from everyone. The clerk had said the smuggler woman *wouldn't stop*. And people who wouldn't stop were the only kind worth knowing, now.

Imre walked through the rain to the customs house.

---

She told herself it was professional interest. That was the lie she'd carried for eleven years and it fit as well as ever, which is to say badly but familiarly. She had been a ward-keeper of the third rank, sworn to the border lattice of Thornfell, one of forty-two souls who walked the high stones each season and sang the old maintenance verses into the anchor-points until the wards hummed like plucked wire. Then the Chancellery had come south with its new doctrine — *borders are an expense; erasure is economy* — and the ward-rolls had been struck, and the keepers scattered, and Imre had signed the surrender papers with a hand that shook, and gone to Vess to drink.

That was the guilt she wore under her coat like a second skin: she had signed. Others hadn't. Old Havel had chained himself to the Spire gate. Marta Ondek had burned the evacuation ledgers so no names could be fed to the clerks. Imre Vashti had read the indemnity clause twice, found her pension in it, and signed.

So when she stood across the square from the customs house in the rain and watched the lit windows, she told herself: *professional interest. The lattice was mine. If someone is carrying maps of it, I want to see them before the Chancellery's furnace does.*

It was even true. It just wasn't the whole truth, and the rest of it had a name she couldn't say yet.

---

Breaking into a Chancellery impound room was, in principle, impossible. In practice, Imre had spent eleven years learning exactly how far the Chancellery's competence extended, and the answer was: very far on paper, and precisely as far as its budget in fact. The Vess customs house had one ward-seal on its records door, a standard civic pattern, renewed quarterly by a contractor who billed for a full rite and performed half of one. Imre knew the pattern the way she knew her own heartbeat. She also knew that a half-performed rite left a seam, and a seam could be opened with a copper stylus and four minutes of patience and a verse hummed at the correct pitch, low, under the breath, so the seal thought it was hearing its own renewal song and obligingly unmade itself.

She went in through the coal chute, because dignity was a currency she'd stopped holding years ago.

The impound room smelled of dust and lamp oil and confiscated things: bales of untaxed silk, a crate of unlicensed prayer-flags, someone's smuggled brandy. And on a shelf by the door, tagged with a lead seal and a docket number, a leather satchel.

Her heart was already going before she touched it. She told herself it was the climb.

She cut the tag. Opened the flap. Drew out the topmost sheet by lamplight.

And stopped breathing.

It was a map. Of course it was a map — the clerk had said maps. But it was not a smuggler's chart, not one of those grease-pencil fantasies of goat-trails and stills that smugglers drew to flatter their own cleverness. This was survey work. Fine ink on rag paper, hills shaded with the patience of a saint, watercourses hatched, elevations noted in a small precise hand. It was the upper Vale of Sorrel and the eastern shoulder of the Teeth, and Imre knew every contour of it because she had walked it thirty times singing maintenance verses into stones that this map marked with tiny careful symbols —

Symbols she recognized.

Not road-markers. Ward-anchors. Drawn in the old notation, the pre-Chancellery notation, the notation they taught at the ward-school before the school was closed, and drawn *correctly*, which meant whoever had made this map had stood at each anchor-point and felt the lattice sing.

Imre sat down slowly on a crate of contraband brandy and turned the sheet toward the lamp, and that was when she saw the signature block in the lower margin, the cartographer's mark, the name written in that same small precise hand:

**Sela Okafor, Survey Corps of Thornfell, by commission of the Spire.**

*Okafor.*

The name landed in her like a stone dropped down a well. She sat in the dark listening for it to hit bottom, and what came back up was old — old as her apprenticeship, old as the first time Master Corvin had walked her along the Spire switchbacks and pointed his stick at the foundation-stones and said, *Who laid these, girl? Not kings. Keepers. And above the keepers, the blood that lit them. Say the names.* And the names had been a litany, three families out of legend: Vashti who kept, Solenne who reckoned, Okafor who burned.

Vashti who kept. Her own line, worn down to a disgraced woman in a gray coat drinking in the Salt Cellar.

Okafor who burned. The fire-line. The blood that had bound dragon-fire into the Spire of Thornfell six hundred years ago, the flame that anchored the whole lattice, the flame the Chancellery's clerks had never understood and never needed to, because you don't need to understand a hearth to brick it over.

And here was an Okafor map, seized by customs, in a town where no one remembered the country it depicted — which meant the hand that drew it was either six hundred years dead or standing somewhere in Vess right now, telling clerks about mountains with a voice that wouldn't stop.

Imre heard boots in the corridor above. Two sets, unhurried, the night round beginning.

She should take the map and go. That was the sensible thing. Take the map, take the satchel, vanish into the rain, and spend the rest of her life knowing one true thing in a world of lies.

Instead she counted the boots' rhythm, waited for the turn at the stair, and looked through the open docket ledger for the prisoner intake.

The entry was there in a clerk's neat hand: *Female, itinerant, claims false provenance. Name given: Rafael Okafor. Held overnight, transfer to provincial magistrate at dawn.*

Dawn. Three hours.

Imre put the map back, closed the satchel, and resealed the tag with wax from her pocket and a forgery of the customs seal she'd learned from a smuggler named Bess Arda, may she rot comfortably. Then she climbed back out the coal chute into the rain, and stood in the alley with the water running off her chin, and admitted the truth she'd been hiding from since the Salt Cellar:

She wasn't doing this for the lattice.

She was doing this because a woman with her ancestor's name was locked in a Chancellery cell, and if Imre Vashti let one more thing belonging to Thornfell be erased while she stood by and signed — she would not survive it. Not the Chancellery. Herself.

*Reluctantly,* she thought, wading toward the lockup behind the customs house, *and with considerable resentment, I am going to commit a crime.*

---

The lockup was easier than the records room, which insulted her professionally. One bored gaoler, one jug of cheap wine, one door with a lock older than the Chancellery itself. Imre bought the gaoler's attention with a story about a runaway apprentice seen near the tanneries — specific enough to send him looking, vague enough to waste an hour — and picked the cell block door in ninety seconds with two pieces of bent iron.

Three cells. The first empty. The second held a snoring drunk. In the third, a woman sat on the bench with her back straight and her wrists raw from the irons they'd apparently decided she didn't need after all, and she was watching the door with eyes that had already catalogued everything about Imre: her coat, her hands, the picks, the way she favored her left knee.

"You're not the gaoler," the woman said.

"No."

"Then either you're robbing me or rescuing me, and I don't have anything worth robbing except my boots."

"They gave me your boots?" Imre glanced down. "They took your boots?"

"They took my boots, my satchel, my knife, and my dignity, in roughly that order of enthusiasm." The woman rose. Up close she was younger than her voice — thirty, maybe, weathered by roads, with ink stains worked so deep into her fingers they looked like birthmarks. "Rafael Okafor," she said. "Since apparently you know that already."

"I know the name," Imre said. "I don't know you."

"Most people don't. Most people these days don't know anything." Something bitter moved under the words. "Are you going to tell me why a stranger is picking Chancellery locks for me, or are we saving revelations for a more convenient time?"

"We're saving them for outside. Can you run?"

"I can outrun you."

"Everyone can outrun me. It's my knee. What I'm asking is whether you'll run *toward* something instead of away, when I tell you to."

Rafael Okafor studied her the way she probably studied terrain — measuring fall lines, weighing crossings. "Try me," she said.

* * *

They went out through the yard, along the wall where the watch-lamp's reach ended, and into the tanner's lane, and Imre set a pace that hurt her knee and didn't complain, because complaining was a debt she'd stopped allowing herself. Behind them the customs house stayed dark and undisturbed. Ahead, the rain thinned and the town of Vess slept its starless sleep, and only when they'd crossed the millrace and climbed into the orchard terraces above the town did Imre stop, lean against a pear tree, and let her breath come back.

Rafael waited, arms folded, rain dripping off her hood. Patient as a contour line.

"Sela Okafor," Imre said.

"What about her?"

"She signed the map they took from you. Survey Corps of Thornfell. Commissioned by the Spire."

For a moment nothing in the woman's face moved at all. Then something did — a settling, like snow deciding whether to hold or slide. "My great-great-grandmother," she said. "Maybe more greats. The maps are hers. I inherited them. I've been guiding runs through the passes with them for six years." Her jaw tightened. "Except tonight the stars went out, and the wards snapped — I felt them snap, like harp strings cutting — and my maps started rewriting themselves. Ink moving. Places vanishing off the page. So I came down to Vess to find out what in the hell happened, and nobody—" her voice cracked, caught, steadied, "—nobody has ever heard of Thornfell. Traders look at me like I'm insane. The customs man called Sela's charts forgeries. Six hundred years of survey work, called forgeries by a boy who's never been higher than a haystack."

"The Chancellery erased it," Imre said. "Nine days ago. Records burned, rolls struck, memory smoothed over. It's a new technique. They've been practicing on smaller places for a decade. Thornfell is the largest thing they've ever tried."

"And how," Rafael said quietly, "do you know that?"

Imre looked out past the orchard, north, where the mountains should have been. Where the horizon was wrong — not empty, exactly, but *smoothed*, like a scar grown over too well, a place where the eye slid off and refused to report.

"Because I was a keeper of those wards," she said. "Third rank, Vashti, out of the old ward-school. My family kept the border stones for four hundred years. Your family lit them." She turned and met Rafael's eyes. "Sela Okafor bound the fire of the Spire into the lattice six centuries ago. Dragon-fire, anchored in blood. Every keeper after swore by it. Every verse we sang ran through it. And when the Chancellery came to erase Thornfell, the first thing they had to kill was the fire — because as long as it burns, the country can't be fully erased. Something always resists. A name that surfaces. A dream of mountains. A map that rewrites itself."

Rafael had gone very still. "You're saying my maps are fighting them."

"I'm saying your blood is. Or was. Or—" Imre exhaled. "I don't know what's left. I know the stars went out the same night the wards snapped, and I know that's not coincidence, because Nadya Solenne — another old name, another old family — used to argue the lattice held more than borders. Light. Memory. Things the country couldn't afford to lose. Everyone thought she was mad." She laughed, short and joyless. "Everyone thought *I* was mad, and I was merely disgraced."

"So the Chancellery didn't just erase a country," Rafael said slowly. "They caged everything it was. And the cage is failing."

"And when the cage fails completely, everything inside it goes with it. The land. Whatever's left of the people who didn't get out." Imre pushed off the tree, knee screaming, and faced her squarely. "I broke you out because I couldn't stand by and watch another piece of Thornfell disappear. That's the honest part. Here's the part I'll deny under oath: I need you. An Okafor's blood ran through every anchor-stone I ever tended. If anyone can walk back into a country that officially doesn't exist and make it real again, it isn't a disgraced keeper with a bad knee. It's the heir of the woman who built it."

The rain had stopped. Somewhere below, Vess lay dark under its missing stars, and north, where nothing was, the absence of mountains stood against the sky like a held breath.

Rafael Okafor looked north for a long moment. When she spoke, her voice was flat and steady and more frightening than shouting would have been.

"I was a smuggler," she said. "Six years, running goods through passes that aren't there anymore. My plan, before last night, was to make enough to leave. Go south. Buy a boat. Never see these mountains again." She turned back, and her eyes had a light in them that Imre recognized from old stories, from the litany, from *Okafor who burned*. "And now some clerk has stolen my family's work and called it forgery, and the sky is missing its stars, and you're telling me the fire in my blood is the only thing keeping a whole country from dying."

"Yes," Imre said.

"Then I want my boots back," said Rafael, "and then I want to burn their customs house down."

"No," said Imre, already turning downhill, already limping toward whatever came next, "but I know where they're keeping your maps, and I know a child in the refugee camp by the tannery who's been asking for you by name — a girl who smells of smoke, who says she remembers the fire."

Rafael stopped dead on the path.

"Ash," Imre said quietly. "She won't speak to anyone else. She's been waiting nine days for someone with your surname to come down out of the hills."
