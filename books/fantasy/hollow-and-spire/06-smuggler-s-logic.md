## 6. Smuggler's Logic

The refugee camp at Marrow's Edge had no name on any map, because the camp itself was made of people from places that had no names anymore. Tents of patched sailcloth leaned against the ruins of a tithe-barn that still remembered being real — its stones held their edges, at least, unlike the eastern fields where the mist had begun to eat the hedgerows whole.

Rafael sat cross-legged on the floor of the barn's loft with a sheet of blank paper in front of her and nothing else. No compass. No charts. The Chancellery officer in Vess had taken her satchel, and Imre's rescue had gotten them out with their lives but not her instruments. All she had left was a stub of charcoal she'd charred herself over the cookfire, a scrap of vellum Ash had found somewhere, and thirty years of walking Thornfell's passes in every season the mountains offered.

"You can't be serious," Imre said from the doorway. She had a bowl of barley soup in each hand and the expression of a woman watching someone gamble away their last coin. "You're going to draw it from memory."

"I'm going to draw it from *knowledge*," Rafael said. "There's a difference."

"Your maps are gone. Your instruments are gone. You haven't seen these roads in—"

"Four years." She didn't look up. "And before that, I walked them for eleven. Imre, I could draw the Serpent's Stair blindfolded. I've climbed it in fog so thick I navigated by the smell of the juniper on the south wall. A map is just memory made portable. Right now my memory is the only copy of Thornfell that exists."

Imre set the bowls down without comment and lowered herself onto an upturned crate, which groaned but held. Up close, in the grey light through the slats, the disgraced ward-keeper looked older than she had in Vess — or maybe Rafael was simply running out of energy to see her any other way. There were new lines around her mouth, the kind carved by nights of not sleeping.

"The passes have changed since you left," Imre said. "The Teeth shed rock every winter. The ford at Callowmere shifted after the flood year. You'd be navigating by a country four years dead."

"A country four years dead is still more current than a country that doesn't exist at all." Rafael tapped the blank vellum. "Every map in the lowlands now shows empty grassland where Thornfell should be. My memory is the freshest survey available. That's not sentiment. That's cartography."

Something flickered across Imre's face — the reluctant twitch of a professional recognizing another professional's logic even while hating it.

"And if your memory's wrong?"

"Then we die in a snowfield with excellent views." Rafael began to draw. "But it won't be wrong. Watch."

She started not with the mountains but with the water. Every smuggler knew the first rule: roads move, bridges fall, but rivers lie about themselves less than anything else in the world. Her charcoal whispered across the vellum, and the Vessin took shape — that cold, quicksilver river that rose in the high Teeth and ran down past Vess to the sea. From the river, everything else could be triangulated.

"There." She marked the confluence where the Vessin met the Aldwater. "We crossed there two nights ago, coming down out of the hills. Which means we're here." A dot. "Marrow's Edge sits on the old drover road, half a day north of the crossing. Correct?"

Imre frowned at it. "Correct."

"So." Lines began to branch from the dot like frost spreading across glass. "Three ways back into Thornfell. The King's Road is out — it runs straight through the Chancellery checkpoint at Callowmere Ford, and we are three people with no papers traveling toward a country that officially isn't there. The Drover's Pass is longer but emptier, except—" her charcoal paused, "—except the Drover's crosses the high moor, and I don't know what the wards' collapse has done to the moor. If the ground out there is as uncertain as the fields east of here, we could walk for a week and end up back where we started. Or worse than nowhere."

"And the third way?" Imre asked, though her tone said she already dreaded the answer.

Rafael drew a third line, thin and wandering, switchbacking up into the jagged teeth of the range she'd sketched from memory alone. "The Smuggler's Cut. Old Sela's road, if the maps were telling the truth about that much. It enters the mountains below the Teeth proper, threads between them, comes out above the valley of Hollowmere. No checkpoint, because until three weeks ago nobody believed the road led anywhere worth guarding. And because—" she allowed herself the smallest smile, "—because I am possibly the last living person who knows it exists."

"That's the part that worries me."

"It should worry you less than the alternatives." Rafael sat back and looked at what she'd drawn. It wasn't beautiful — charcoal on scavenged vellum never was — but it was *true*, and looking at it did something to her chest she hadn't felt since the night the stars went out. For three weeks she had been a woman carrying a country no one else could see. Now the country was on the page again. She had made it real, or the closest thing to real that remained.

Her hands weren't shaking. She noticed that with a small, fierce satisfaction. In Vess, when the customs officer had called her life's work forgery, her hands had shaken for a day and a half. Now they were steady. Because this — this was the thing she knew how to do. The world had erased her maps, but it hadn't erased her.

*They can take the satchel,* she thought. *They can't take the surveyor.*

* * *

Ash arrived the way Ash always arrived: silently, and then all at once. One moment the loft was empty except for the two women and the map; the next, the child was sitting cross-legged on the floor beside Rafael, close enough that the faint smell of woodsmoke that always clung to her mixed with the charcoal dust. Nobody had heard the ladder creak. Nobody ever did.

Ash looked at the map for a long time. Her eyes — amber, with pupils that went vertical in certain lights, a detail Rafael had learned not to stare at — tracked the lines of the Smuggler's Cut up and up into the drawn mountains.

"That one," Ash said.

"You can read it?" Rafael asked. The child was seven, maybe eight; her reading of letters was erratic, but maps were another language, and some children spoke it natively.

Ash nodded. Then she reached out one small finger and touched the place where the Cut threaded between two peaks Rafael had labeled, from habit, the Sisters. "It goes under here," Ash said. "Through the mountain. Not over. There's a door in the rock, and inside it's warm, and there used to be a light." Her finger traced onward. "It comes out by a waterfall that falls *up* for a little way before it remembers to fall down. Near Hollowmere."

Rafael went very still.

She had never told anyone about the waterfall. She had barely admitted it to herself, because a waterfall that ran upward for twenty feet before surrendering to sense was not the kind of thing a serious cartographer put on a chart — it was the kind of thing that got your work called forgery. She had seen it once, at fourteen, on her first solo run through the Cut, and she had kept it off every map she'd ever sold, a private truth hoarded like contraband.

"How do you know that?" she asked quietly.

Ash shrugged, a small, terrible shrug, the shrug of a child for whom impossible knowledge was simply weather. "I remember things. From before." She didn't say *before what*. She never did. "The mountain told me. When the fire was strong, everything in Thornfell talked a little. Rocks talk slowest, but they remember longest." She looked up at Rafael, and there was such complete, unguarded trust in that amber gaze that Rafael had to look away first. "You drew it right. Even the part you got wrong."

"Which part did I get wrong?"

Ash tapped a bend in the Cut, halfway up. "It doesn't turn east here. It turns west. But only in winter. The mountain moves a little when it's cold."

Imre made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth. "The mountain *moves*."

"Everything moves," Ash said, with the patience of the very young for the very dense. "You just have to know how it likes to move." She looked at Rafael again. "She knows. She walks like she's listening to the ground."

Rafael picked up the charcoal and moved the bend westward. It felt absurd. It felt exactly like the moment at fourteen when she'd stood under the reversed waterfall and decided, finally, that the world was stranger than her employers wanted it to be, and that she would rather be accurate than respectable.

"West it is," she said.

* * *

They left Marrow's Edge two hours before dawn, in the blue-black hour when even the mist seemed to sleep. Rafael led, Imre walked behind with Ash between them, and the drover road unspooled northward under their boots, pale as a scar in the dark.

By midmorning they had left the last of the tents behind and entered country that grew strange by degrees. First the hedgerows lost their shadows. Then a stream they crossed ran uphill for a dozen paces before thinking better of it — Ash giggled at that, a small smoky sound, and even Imre's mouth twitched. By noon the horizon itself had begun to misbehave: hills in the east appeared and vanished like fish surfacing in a pond, and once Rafael could have sworn she saw a village — rooftops, a church spire, smoke from chimneys — standing calmly in a field where, when she blinked, there was nothing but wet grass and sheep.

"Don't look too long," Imre murmured. "Places that are half-erased notice being noticed. It embarrasses them."

"Is that ward-keeper doctrine or folk wisdom?"

"My mother's. She kept wards on the Vessin marshes for thirty years." Imre's voice went carefully flat. "She died believing her work mattered. I'd like to keep believing that too, if it's all the same to you."

Rafael let it go. They walked.

The Smuggler's Cut announced itself the way it always had: as nothing. Where the drover road bent around a shoulder of hill, a sheep track wandered off to the left and promptly gave up, dissolving into bracken and scree. Any reasonable traveler would conclude the track went nowhere. That was the entire genius of it. Sela Okafor — or whoever had built the Cut, generations deep in the Okafors' past — had designed a road to be invisible to anyone not actively looking for it, and now the Chancellery's erasure had done the final polish, wiping even the suggestion of it from the land's official memory.

But Rafael had spent eleven years looking.

She found the first marker where her memory said it would be: a leaning stone, waist-high, carved with a spiral that most people took for a shepherd's mark. She put her palm flat against it. The stone was warm. Not sun-warm — the day was overcast — but warm the way a hearthstone stays warm hours after the fire dies.

"It remembers," Ash whispered beside her, pressing her own small hand to the spiral. Her eyes had gone wide and bright. "It remembers *her*. The map-woman. She touched this stone a thousand times."

"Sela," Rafael said. The name felt different in her mouth now than it had a month ago. Less like history. More like family.

They climbed. The track, invisible from below, revealed itself step by step to those who already knew its shape — a ledge here, a shelf there, a gully that looked impassable until you knew to traverse left along a seam of red stone. Rafael called the turns from memory, and twice Ash corrected her — *the rocks slid here, take the high line* — and both times the child was right, and both times Rafael amended her mental map with a cartographer's discipline rather than a proud woman's reluctance. The mountain moved when it was cold, Ash had said. Well. The mountain was very cold now.

By late afternoon they could see the problem ahead: the Callowmere road, far below and to the east, winding along the valley floor toward the pass that was the only official way into Thornfell-that-had-been. And on that road, where it passed beneath the shadow of the Sisters, sat the checkpoint.

Even at this distance they could see the lanterns. Six, maybe eight guards. A barricade of sharpened stakes. And something worse — a tall pole bearing a device Rafael recognized from the customs house in Vess: the Chancellery's sigil, a closed eye above crossed keys. Under it, faintly, the air itself seemed to thicken, the way air thickens over a heat haze, as though the checkpoint were not merely watching the road but *editing* it.

"They'll have a seeing-ward," Imre said grimly. She had gone pale. "Standard issue for erasure enforcement. Anyone approaching the erased border gets turned around without knowing it. They'll walk to within fifty yards of the barricade, feel suddenly tired, decide they meant to go somewhere else entirely, and wander home wondering why their boots are muddy. The guards won't even have to raise a hand."

"But us?"

"We're not approaching from the road. We're above them, on a road that doesn't exist, heading for a country that doesn't exist." Imre shook her head slowly. "I genuinely don't know what a seeing-ward does with that. Nothing in my training covered patrolling the inside of a hole in the world."

Rafael studied the terrain, and the old craft woke in her blood like a banked coal breathed upon. Below them, the Smuggler's Cut descended toward the valley through a fold of land — a dry ravine choked with boulder and juniper — that ran parallel to the King's Road for nearly a mile before climbing again toward the high pass. The ravine's rim stood maybe forty feet above the roadway. Close enough to be seen. Far enough to matter.

"If we stay in the ravine, we're hidden by the rim," she said. "Except for one stretch — there's a washout about halfway, where the rim drops. We'd be exposed to the checkpoint for perhaps two hundred paces."

"Two hundred paces in sight of a seeing-ward," Imre repeated. "Wonderful."

"Not necessarily." Rafael chewed the end of the charcoal stick, then grinned despite herself. "Imre. What does a seeing-ward actually do? Mechanically."

"It... makes the unseen unseen. It enforces the Chancellery's version of the landscape. Anything that contradicts the official map becomes difficult to perceive."

"Anything that *contradicts the official map*." Rafael tapped the vellum. "And what does the official map show, right here?"

Imre opened her mouth. Closed it. Rafael watched the understanding arrive in her face like dawn coming up over the ridge.

"Empty hillside," Imre said slowly. "Grass and scree. No road. No travelers. Because travelers need a road, and there is no road on any map the Chancellery recognizes—"

"—and we are not travelers," Rafael finished. "We are *terrain features*. Three rocks with legs. As long as we don't do anything the empty hillside couldn't plausibly do — no waving, no shouting, no sudden movement — the ward has no category for us. It can't see what it has already decided isn't there." She spread her hands. "Smuggler's logic. The best hiding place isn't darkness. It's being boring."

Ash was nodding vigorously. "Like the goat," she said.

"What goat?" Imre asked.

"The goat that lives near the Spire. He stands on the wall and everyone looks straight through him because goats belong on walls. Mama—" Ash stopped. Something crossed her face, brief as a cloud-shadow, and was gone. "Someone told me that once," she finished, quieter.

Rafael's chest tightened. She filed the moment away — another fragment of the child's before, another grief deferred — and instead knelt to pack the vellum away. "We cross the exposed stretch at dusk," she said. "Last light, when the guards' eyes are on the transition and the lanterns aren't lit yet. We walk slowly. We walk *boringly*. No talking. If Ash is right about the mountain moving, the washout may have shifted anyway, and we'll want the light to find the new line."

"And if the ward sees us anyway?" Imre asked.

Rafael looked at her — at the disgraced keeper with the prison still fresh in her posture, at the woman who had broken Rafael out of a cell on nothing but a surname and a debt to the dead.

"Then we run," she said, "and I redraw the route from wherever we end up. That's the job, Imre. The map is never finished. You just keep surveying."

For the first time since Vess, Imre smiled. It was a thin, tired, genuine thing. "Sela Okafor would have liked you," she said.

"I'm beginning to suspect she'd have been insufferable about it."

* * *

Dusk came down purple and cold, and they crossed.

Rafael went first, setting the pace: slow, level, unhurried, a woman taking an evening stroll across an empty hillside, because that was precisely what the world's official record insisted she must be. Behind her came Ash, imitating her stride with uncanny precision, and behind Ash, Imre, breathing deliberately, hands loose at her sides.

Below and to the right, the checkpoint glowed with its first lanterns. A guard paced the barricade. Another leaned on his spear, staring out at — nothing. At grass. At scree. At the official version of the world.

Rafael did not look at them. Looking, she suspected, was a form of insisting, and insisting was the one thing the hillside never did. She fixed her eyes on the middle distance, on the seam of red stone where the ravine resumed, and she walked, and she counted her steps the way she'd counted them on a hundred smuggling runs, and at step ninety the washout appeared exactly where memory said it would, the rim dropping away, the valley opening below her like a held breath.

Step one hundred forty. The guard below turned his head. Rafael felt the motion in her peripheral vision and kept her pace, kept her gaze soft and forward, kept being a rock, a juniper, a featureless slope wearing boots.

The guard looked directly at her.

She felt his gaze like a physical pressure, like a hand pressed flat against her sternum — and then she felt the moment the seeing-ward answered him. She felt it as a kind of sliding, a gentle correction, the world quietly rearranging itself around its own paperwork. His eyes drifted off her. He frowned at the empty hillside, rubbed his neck, and turned back to the fire he'd been building.

They passed the washout. They passed the two hundred paces. They climbed into the ravine's shelter, and only when the rim rose solid above them did Rafael allow herself to exhale, long and shaking, her knees abruptly aware of the day's miles.

Ash threw her arms around Rafael's waist and squeezed hard enough to creak ribs. "You *bored* him," the child said delightedly, muffled against her coat. "You bored him right off us!"

"Cartography's finest weapon," Rafael managed.

Imre caught up, sagged against the ravine wall, and laughed — actually laughed, a short rusty bark of a sound, as though the machinery hadn't been oiled in years. "Eleven years I kept wards," she said, "and I never once thought to defeat one by being dull. The Chancellery trains its people to fear the spectacular. Fire, dragons, blood-magic. Nobody trains them for a woman walking like Sunday afternoon."

"Sunday afternoon is the deadliest force in the lowlands," Rafael agreed.

They made camp in the lee of a boulder, cold rations, no fire — boring to the last. Above them, through the ravine's notch, Rafael could see the sky, and she checked it out of habit, the way you check a wound.

One star. Faint, low in the northeast, trembling at the edge of visibility.

A month ago the sky had held nothing at all. Two weeks ago, Nadya Solenne — locked in her cell in Vess, ranting about stars being *held*, not destroyed — had been a madwoman. But Rafael had memorized the northeastern sky before the Going-Out, professionally, the way she memorized everything, and she knew what constellation belonged in that patch of dark.

The Wain. And there, exactly where the Wain's foremost ox ought to stand, hung a single recovered point of light.

"Imre," she said quietly. "Look."

The ward-keeper followed her gaze and went still. "That wasn't there yesterday."

"No," Rafael said. "It wasn't."

Ash stood between them, small hands clenched, face tipped up to that lone star, and her eyes were shining — with wonder, or with reflected firelight from some fire only she could see. "It's the lattice letting go," she whispered. "Little bits slipping through the cracks. Like embers through a grate." Her voice dropped, and the smoke-smell of her sharpened, and for a moment she sounded far older than eight. "Which means the fire's getting *thinner*. Things slip out when the cage stops holding them. Rafael — we have to hurry. The Spire doesn't have seasons left. It has weeks."

Rafael looked from the child to the star to the dark mass of the Teeth rising against the last violet band of sunset — the mountains she had drawn from memory, the country that existed now only in charcoal and recollection and the stubborn warmth of a leaning stone.

"Then we don't wait for full dark," she said, and shouldered her pack. "We climb tonight."
