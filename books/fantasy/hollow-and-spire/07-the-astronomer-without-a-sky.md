## 7. The Astronomer Without a Sky

They had taken Nadya Solenne's instruments first. That was the cruelty she had not expected — not the cell, not the bread and water, not even the questions asked in that flat Chancellery voice that treated her like a malfunctioning clock rather than a person. It was the brass. Her astrolabe, her quadrant, the little orrery her mother had left her with its nine silver spheres gone green at the seams. Confiscated as "materials of sedition." As if a woman could commit treason against the sky.

So she did what any astronomer does when robbed of her tools.

She built new ones out of nothing.

The cell in Vess's Chancellery annex had one window, high and barred, facing east over the customs yard. Through it, by day, came a wedge of grey light. By night — well. There was no night anymore. That was the whole of it, wasn't it? That was why she was here, in a cell that smelled of damp stone and old ink, writing equations on the wall with a stub of charcoal she'd smuggled from the hearth in her shoe.

*There is no night anymore.*

Nadya had spent thirty-one years studying the heavens from the observatory at Callowmere, and in all that time she had never once doubted that the stars were *there*. Distant, indifferent, eternal — yes. But there. You could set your watch by them. You could sail by them, plant by them, swear oaths by them. The sky was the one map no government could redraw.

Then, on the night the wards of Thornfell snapped, forty-one stars had gone out between one heartbeat and the next. Not dimmed. Not occluded by cloud. *Gone*, along with every constellation that contained them, leaving holes in the sky like missing teeth — and worse, leaving holes in the mathematics, because the stars had not simply vanished. Their absence had *weight*. She had measured it before they took her: the tides off the Callowmere coast ran eleven minutes late. Migrating birds circled in confusion over the marshes. Light itself seemed to bend, very slightly, around the empty places, the way water bends around a stone.

You do not get bending around nothing. Nothing has no mass. Nothing has no gravity. Nothing cannot hold a shape.

Something was holding those spaces open. Something was *there*, where the stars should be, wearing their absence like a mask.

That was the sentence that got her arrested. Spoken aloud, in a public lecture hall, to an audience that included two Chancellery observers: "The stars have not been destroyed. They are being held."

Held by whom? Held *where*? The Chancellery's interrogator — a dry man named Pellwith who smelled of vinegar and bureaucracy — had asked her these questions for six days running, and for six days she had answered honestly, which was her great failing as a prisoner and her pride as a scientist.

"I don't know where," she told him again on the seventh morning, sitting cross-legged on the cot with her charcoal and her wall. "But I know *how many*. Forty-one stars, massing approximately—"

"Madam Solenne." Pellwith sighed, setting down his ledger. He was tired of her. Good. Tired men made mistakes. "The Crown's position is that the phenomenon is a natural astral event. A stellar conjunction of unusual character. The disappearance of certain navigational references is regrettable but temporary."

"Temporary," Nadya repeated. "It's been nineteen days."

"The Crown's position—"

"Your Crown's position is that Thornfell doesn't exist either," she said. "I've read the customs manifests. Mountains don't stop existing because you file them incorrectly. And stars—" she jabbed the charcoal at the wall, at the great spiraling proof that covered it floor to ceiling, layer upon layer of arithmetic climbing toward a conclusion she both dreaded and adored — "stars don't get *filed*, Master Pellwith. They get *kept*. And whoever is keeping them is losing their grip."

Pellwith looked at the wall. She watched him try not to look at the wall, and fail. Even a bureaucrat's eyes would follow those curves eventually; they were beautiful curves, if she said so herself, elegant as a dancer's spine. The lattice equations. She had derived them from tide-tables and bird-flight and the residual wobble in Callowmere's pendulum clocks, and when she'd finished, she had sat back and felt something she hadn't felt since childhood: awe with teeth in it.

Because the lattice was not natural. Natural things did not arrange themselves into perfect dodecahedral harmonics anchored at seven points across a mountain range that officially did not exist.

Natural things did not *hold stars*.

"You understand," Pellwith said quietly, "that I am required to report continued insistence on this theory."

"I understand that you're required to do a great many things," Nadya said. "I've read your job description. It's written on your face. It says *I would rather be anywhere else*."

For one unguarded instant, something flickered behind his eyes that might, in a better world, have been a smile. Then he gathered his ledger, rapped twice on the door, and left her alone with her mathematics and her wedge of grey light.

Nadya looked at the wall for a long time.

Here was the thing she had not told him. Here was the thing she had told no one, because telling it would make it real, and real things could be confiscated too.

The lattice was failing. She could see it in the numbers — in the way the harmonic constants drifted a little further each day, in the way the held stars' gravitational ghosts grew fainter, in the eleven-minute tides becoming twelve, then thirteen. Whatever engine kept the sky's stolen pieces suspended was winding down. And when it stopped —

She pressed her palm flat against the coldest part of the wall.

When it stopped, the stars wouldn't come back. Stars in freefall didn't return to their stations. They fell. All of them, at once, out of whatever cage had been holding them, and down into a world that had forgotten how to bear their weight.

Unless someone relit the fire first.

She didn't know yet what fire. She only knew that every equation pointed, ultimately, to a single terminus — a point in the northern mountains where all seven anchor-lines converged, a point her charts labeled with a surveyor's mark so old it predated the Chancellery itself:

*Spire of Thornfell.*

A country that wasn't there. A tower that held up the sky. And somewhere in the lowlands, if the rumors in the prison yard were worth anything, a cartographer who carried maps that rewrote themselves.

Nadya picked up her charcoal and began, carefully, to calculate how long they had.

The answer made her hand shake.

* * *

The lock on her door had been failing for three days. She knew this the way she knew everything now — by inference, by the patient accumulation of small evidence. The guard's keys had begun sticking. The mechanism ground on its second rotation. And tonight, the evening patrol had passed her door at the wrong interval, eleven minutes late, which meant the duty roster had changed, which meant the shift change gap would fall at half past the second bell instead of the third.

Prisoners of the Chancellery were not permitted pencils. But nobody had thought to confiscate her memory of orbital mechanics, and a woman who could predict the position of Neptune to within a hair's breadth could certainly predict the position of a bored guard.

So when the lock finally surrendered — to a tension-wire fashioned from a bedspring and about four minutes of swearing — Nadya was already standing, already dressed, already holding her charcoal stub like a dagger she had no intention of using.

The corridor beyond was dark. The window at its end showed a sky with holes in it, and she caught herself looking up at one of those holes — the place where Sarnath's Eye used to burn, the red star sailors had steered by for a thousand years — and feeling the old grief rise like bile.

"Hold on," she whispered to it. "I'm working on it."

Footsteps. Two sets, moving fast, wrong for the patrol schedule. Nadya flattened against the wall, mind racing through contingencies — and then the footsteps arrived, and a lamp swung around the corner, and she was looking at a tall woman with a cartographer's ink-stained fingers and a face like a closed book someone had started to open, and behind her a grey-haired ward-keeper with a soldier's stance and a child wrapped in a blanket riding her shoulders, a small girl whose eyes caught the lamplight the way embers catch wind.

"You're Solenne," the tall woman said. It wasn't a question. "The astronomer. Imre says you're the one who wrote the paper about the stars being held."

"I wrote several papers. I'm told they're seditious."

"We need everything you know." The woman — Okafor, the name surfaced from the prison-yard gossip, Rafael Okafor, the map-thief, the ghost of Thornfell — glanced back down the corridor. "Quickly. We have maybe ninety seconds before the night wardens finish their rounds."

Nadya looked at these three improbable people — the smuggler-cartographer, the disgraced keeper, the ember-eyed child — and felt something in her chest turn over, slow and enormous, like a telescope slewing toward a new star.

"Ninety seconds," she repeated. "Amusingly, that's almost exactly how much time I need. Come see my wall."

They came. She watched Okafor's eyes travel the lattice equations top to bottom, watched the cartographer's lips move slightly, reading not the words but the *shape* of them — and then Rafael Okafor went very still, and pulled a battered chart from her satchel, and spread it against the wall beside Nadya's charcoal work.

The lines matched.

Not approximately. *Exactly.* Seven anchor-points, converging northward, drawn in faded surveyor's ink decades before Nadya had ever derived them from tides and pendulums.

"That's impossible," Nadya breathed.

"It's Sela Okafor's," Rafael said. "My ancestor. I think she built the thing you've been calculating."

Nadya stared at the convergence point on the old chart, then at her own equations, then at the child on the ward-keeper's shoulders, who was looking at the lattice-wall with an expression of terrible recognition, small hands curled against Imre's hair like claws remembering fire.

"Well," Nadya said, and heard her own voice crack with something that was terror and joy in equal measure, indistinguishable at this magnification. "Then we haven't much time at all. My figures give us — at current decay rates — eleven days. Perhaps twelve. After that, the lattice fails, and everything it's holding comes down at once."

"Everything it's holding," Imre said slowly. "Which is?"

Nadya looked up through the barred window, at the wounded sky, at the hole where Sarnath's Eye had been.

"Forty-one stars," she said. "And I would very much like to go and get them back."

* * *

They went out through the laundry yard, under a sky like a torn curtain, and Nadya walked between them with her confiscated life reduced to a charcoal stub in her pocket and thirty years of starlight folded up inside her head.

The child — Ash — walked beside her most of the way, silent, watching the holes in heaven. Once, without preamble, she reached up and took Nadya's hand. Her palm was warm. Warmer than a child's palm had any right to be, warm as a stone left too near a hearth.

"They're scared," Ash said quietly, nodding at the sky. "The ones in the cage. I can feel them going quiet."

Nadya's throat tightened. Every instinct she had said this was impossible — stars did not have feelings, stars did not whisper to half-dragon children in refugee camps — but every instinct she had also said the sky could not be emptied by paperwork, and here they all were.

"Then we'll be loud enough for all of us," she said.

Above the eastern hills, in the black socket where Sarnath's Eye had burned for a thousand years, something flickered. Just once. Faint as a dying coal disturbed by breath.

Ash squeezed her hand. Nadya quickened her pace, doing the arithmetic again, faster now, hoping she was wrong, knowing she wasn't.

Eleven days. And the stars were starting to go out from the inside.
