## 8. First Pass: The Teeth

The Teeth did not look like teeth anymore. They looked like nothing at all.

Rafael stood at the mouth of the pass with the wind tearing at her hood and a world of white pressing in from every direction — white sky, white ground, white air so thick with snow that up and down became suggestions rather than facts. Somewhere ahead, if her memory served, the pass narrowed between two granite walls and climbed three switchbacks before spilling into the high valley beyond. But there was no seeing it. There was only the whiteout, and the small circle of their breath, and the rope.

"Tell me again why we're doing this," Imre said behind her. She had the rope coiled over one shoulder and her ward-keeper's iron ring — useless now, she'd said, more than once — hanging at her belt like an accusation.

"Because it's the only way in." Rafael pulled her mittens off, flexed her bare fingers against the cold, and held them palm-up as if she could read the wind the way she once read contour lines. "Because every map south of here is blank now, and blank maps don't get us to the Spire."

"And your memory does?"

"My memory is the map." She said it flatly, because saying it any other way would have let the fear show through, and they couldn't afford fear right now. Not with Ash standing at her elbow, small and silent, smelling faintly of woodsmoke even in this frozen air.

Ash tugged Rafael's sleeve. Her eyes were that unsettling amber, slit-pupiled, and they didn't squint against the wind the way human eyes should have. *"I can feel the mountain,"* she signed, then spoke aloud anyway, her voice thin and strange under the howl. "It's angry. Or scared. I can't tell which."

"Mountains don't get scared," Imre muttered.

"This one does," Ash said simply, and no one argued, because Ash was half dragon and had been born inside these borders when the borders still meant something, and lately none of them doubted anything she said about what Thornfell felt.

Rafael knelt and checked the knots on the rope herself, though Imre had tied them and Imre's knots were better than hers. It gave her hands something to do besides shake. Four of them: herself on point, Ash roped directly behind her where Rafael could hear her breathing, Nadya third, Imre last with the anchor irons. Nadya stood apart, face tilted up toward the featureless sky, lips moving through some calculation Rafael didn't want explained.

"Nadya," she called. "Sky report."

"There is no sky," Nadya said, with the particular cheer she reserved for catastrophic data. "Which is itself informative. The snow isn't falling *from* anywhere. Watch." She caught a flake on her sleeve, held it out. It sat there, six-pointed, perfect, unmoving, until the wind ripped it away. "No tumbling. No melt. These flakes are being *remembered* into existence, not falling through it. The country is running out of substance to be made of, and it's using its memories as filler."

"So the storm is Thornfell forgetting," Rafael said.

"The storm is Thornfell *dreaming*," Nadya corrected. "Which is worse. Dreams collapse without warning."

"Wonderful." Imre spat into the wind. "Lead on, cartographer. If we die, I want it noted that I objected."

"Noted," Rafael said, and stepped into the white.

* * *

She navigated by childhood.

That was the truth of it, the thing she'd never told any of them outright: she wasn't reading the mountain. She was reading herself. Every fifty paces she stopped, closed her eyes, and went looking for a girl of nine or ten with ink-stained fingers and a father who dragged her up these passes every summer, teaching her the surveyor's catechism — *the land lies, the map doesn't; count everything twice; trust the shape under the snow.*

There was a boulder here. A split one, like a broken tooth, where Papa had made her eat lunch in the rain because shelter was shelter. She walked forty paces, sixty, eighty — and her boot struck stone, and she knelt, and swept the snow aside with both hands, and there it was: granite, split, big enough for two people and a cold meat pie.

"It's here," she breathed, and heard the others' relief travel down the rope like a current.

"You're certain it's the same rock?" Imre asked.

"I ate a terrible lunch beside it in 1187. I'm certain."

They moved from memory to memory the way other climbers moved from cairn to cairn. The scree field where she'd twisted her ankle at twelve — she found its edge by counting her own old limping steps. The frozen stream she'd fallen into at fourteen, which she located by the sudden wrongness of the ground underfoot, the hollow sound of ice over water, and stood back while Ash cracked it with one small fist to let them drink. The place where the pass bent east around a shoulder of black stone — she knew it not by sight but by smell, by the mineral stink of that particular rock that had soaked into her memory along with her father's voice saying *smell it, Rafa, a good cartographer knows a mountain by its smell.*

The whiteout never lifted. Hours passed, or what felt like hours; time went soft and unreliable in all that white. Rafael stopped trusting her eyes entirely and trusted instead the strange interior archive of herself, walking blind through a country drawn from the inside out. Once, she glanced back and saw the others as pale ghosts trailing her, and understood suddenly why the Chancellery had needed to erase Thornfell rather than conquer it. You didn't need armies to kill a country. You just needed everyone to forget the way through.

"Left here," she said, at a bend that existed only in her. "Then up. The switchbacks start after the next rise, and after that we're exposed, so we move fast and we don't stop."

"Why exposed?" Nadya asked.

"Because the slope above the second switchback is a loaded gun." She heard her own voice go quiet. "Papa wouldn't take the caravan through in heavy snow years. He said the whole face hangs on a shelf of old ice, and the mountain keeps it the way you keep a secret — barely, and only until someone gives it a reason not to."

Ash's hand found hers in the white. Small, warm — impossibly warm, furnace-warm, the girl was always warm — and gripping hard.

*"You know the way,"* Ash said. Not a question. A statement of faith so absolute it frightened Rafael more than the avalanche talk had.

"I knew it," Rafael said. "When I was a child. When it mattered less."

"It matters now," Ash said, "and you still know," and squeezed her hand, and Rafael discovered there was no answer to that except to keep walking.

* * *

The switchbacks were worse than she remembered, because she remembered them from below, from summer, from a child's legs that didn't know yet what exhaustion cost. Now each climb came through knee-deep snow that drifted and re-drifted, filling their tracks behind them as fast as they made them, so that Rafael had the constant vertiginous sense of being the first person ever to cross this pass — of walking off the edge of every map at once, including her own.

The gun above the second switchback went off an hour into the climb.

There was no warning she could have used. Later she would replay it a thousand times — the small deep *whump* somewhere upslope, the way the white world shivered like a struck drumhead, the half-second in which her body understood before her mind did — and every time she would conclude the same thing: there had been nothing to know. The mountain had simply decided.

"*Down!* she screamed, and threw herself flat, and grabbed for the rope.

The world came downhill.

It didn't look like the avalanches in ballads. It looked like the whiteout deciding to become solid — the whole slope above them peeling loose and pouring down in a silence so vast it seemed the sound had been erased along with everything else, and then the sound arrived, a roar like the country screaming through a closed throat, and Rafael was tumbling, weightless, buried, clawing, the rope burning through her grip —

— gone.

Darkness. Pressure. Cold that wasn't cold anymore but a kind of absence, a nothing pressing on her from all sides. Snow packed in her mouth, her hood, down her collar. She couldn't tell which way was up. Her hands were somewhere near her face; she dug, wildly, and broke through into a pocket of blue-grey light, gasping, and the first thing she did — before freeing her legs, before checking her ribs — was count.

"One," she said aloud, her own name. She hauled the rope hand over hand, following it downslope through the debris, digging with her forearms. "Two — Nadya —"

Nadya surfaced thirty feet along, coughing, one boot missing, alive. "Three?" Rafael shouted, and got no answer, and dug faster.

Imre broke out on her own, twenty yards wide, bleeding from the eyebrow, already moving. "Where's the girl?"

The question hit Rafael like a physical blow. She turned a slow circle in the settling white, listening past the ringing in her ears, past the wind, past her own heartbeat.

Nothing. No small voice. No amber eyes.

"Ash!" Her voice cracked on the name. "*Ash!*"

Silence. Then — faint, muffled, coming from beneath six feet of snow somewhere to the left — a sound that was not a cry for help. It was a low, grinding growl, animal and furious, and Rafael's blood went cold, because she'd heard that sound before, the night Ash talked in her sleep about fire.

"She's under," Rafael said. "She's under and she's *panicking.*" And panicking dragons, even half-grown ones, even children — she didn't finish the thought. She dropped to her knees and started digging.

* * *

Later, she would not remember most of it. That was mercy, probably. What stayed was fragments:

Her mittens lost somewhere in the first minute. Her bare hands in snow so cold it burned, flinging it left and right in fistfuls, the skin splitting across her knuckles, blood freezing bright on the white. Imre beside her, digging too, both of them wordless, the rope guiding them down and down. Nadya shouting measurements — *ten feet left, the rope bends down, she's deeper than you think* — in the flat voice of a woman using mathematics to keep from screaming.

And underneath it all, growing louder as they dug, the growl. Not fading. *Growing.* As if the thing buried under the snow were not suffocating but smoldering, as if the panic were feeding something, and Rafael dug with a new terror — not that Ash would die under there, but what might happen if she woke up fully, down in the dark, with fire and nowhere to put it.

"Ash!" she screamed into the hole. "Ash, listen to me! It's Rafael! It's Rafael, I'm coming, you hold on, you do *not* burn, you hear me? You hold it, sweetheart, you hold it —"

The growl stuttered.

"— I'm coming, I'm right here, I'm coming —"

Her hand punched through into a hollow, and something seized it — small fingers, crushing, furnace-hot, strong enough to grind bone — and Rafael didn't pull away. She let it crush her. She let it hurt.

"I've got you," she said. "I've got you. Dig, Imre!"

They widened the hole together, and then Ash came up out of the snow like something surfacing from deep water — snow-crusted, wild-eyed, pupils blown to slits, a thread of smoke rising from between her clenched teeth, her whole small body shaking with the effort of holding back whatever was trying to get out.

Rafael took her face in her ruined, bleeding hands.

"Look at me," she said. "Just me. Nothing else exists. Look at me."

Amber eyes fixed on hers. The shaking slowed. The smoke thinned.

"Breathe," Rafael said. "In. Out. Like the campfire game. In — out. That's it. You're not under. You're out. You're with me. Nobody's leaving anybody. Not today."

The growl died by degrees, like a fire settling into coals. Ash's grip on Rafael's wrist eased from bone-breaking to merely desperate. And then the child folded forward into her arms, and Rafael held on, kneeling in the avalanche debris with her shredded hands pressed against a small warm back, rocking her, saying nothing now, because there was nothing left to say that her arms weren't already saying.

Over Ash's head, she met Imre's eyes. The ward-keeper looked stricken in a way Rafael had never seen — guilt and awe tangled together, the expression of a woman watching something she'd spent years insisting she no longer believed in happen anyway.

"She held it," Imre said quietly. "Six feet of snow and terror, and she *held* it. Do you understand what that control is worth? At her age? Okafor, the ward-schools would have —" She stopped. Swallowed. "We keep her safe. Whatever else happens at the Spire. We keep her safe. I'll swear it on the wards themselves."

"You don't have wards to swear on," Rafael said, but her voice had no edge in it.

"I have myself," Imre said. "It'll do."

* * *

They made camp that night in the lee of the split-tooth boulder — Rafael's boulder, her terrible-lunch boulder — because it was the only shelter either memory or mountain offered. Nadya built a fire from fuel she produced out of her pack with explanations nobody asked for. Imre bound Rafael's hands in strips of shirt, working silently, competently, refusing help.

"They'll scar," Imre said.

"Maps have scars too," Rafael said. "Creases. Where they've been folded and carried and opened a thousand times. My master used to say a crease proved the map was loved."

Imre snorted, but her eyes stayed soft. "Your master sounds like he'd have liked us."

"He'd have liked Ash," Rafael said, glancing to where the girl sat closest to the fire, closer than anyone else dared sit, feeding sticks into the flames with the tenderness of someone greeting an old friend. "He always said fire was just heat that had learned where it belonged."

Ash looked up. "Is that true?"

"No," Rafael said. "But it should be."

Ash smiled — the rare full smile, the one that showed the slightly-too-many teeth — and came and sat against Rafael's side, fitting herself into the space under Rafael's arm as if it had been built for her, and Rafael, who had spent thirty-one years keeping everyone at the length of a handshake, found she did not want to move.

"We nearly lost you today," she said, into the girl's hair.

"But you didn't."

"No. But we nearly did." She was quiet a moment. "Ash. If it happens again — if you're buried, if you're scared — I want you to try something before the fire. Say my name. Just my name. Can you do that?"

"Why?"

"Because I'll hear it," Rafael said. "However deep you are. I'll hear it, and I'll come. That's a promise, and I don't make those often, so I expect you to spend it carefully."

Ash considered this with the gravity she gave everything. Then she nodded once, solemn as a treaty, and burrowed closer, and within minutes was asleep — warm as a hearthstone, breathing smoke-scented breath, utterly trusting.

Across the fire, Nadya was sketching star-charts by firelight, charts of stars that weren't there, muttering figures. Imre sat sharpening her anchor irons with a whetstone, rhythmic and steady, a sound like patience itself.

Rafael looked at the three of them — the disgraced keeper, the mad astronomer, the sleeping dragon-child — and felt something rearrange itself in her chest, quiet and irreversible, like a border being redrawn.

*Family,* she thought, testing the word the way she'd test unfamiliar terrain. *So that's what this is.*

She had crossed the Teeth by memory, and the memory had held. Tomorrow the pass crested, and beyond it lay the high valley, and beyond that the Spire, and whatever waited there — Sela's inheritance, the guttering flame, the choice she was already afraid to name.

She slept badly, and woke before dawn to Ash sitting bolt upright, staring north through the dying dark, her amber eyes reflecting a glow that had no business existing on a horizon where no town stood.

"What is it?" Rafael whispered. "What do you see?"

Ash's voice came out very small.

"The Spire's going out," she said. "I can feel it. We have to hurry, Rafael. We're almost out of *real*.
