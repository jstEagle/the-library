## 9. Ghosts of the Survey Corps

The survey station appeared out of the snow like a shipwreck.

Rafael saw it first — a dark tooth of stone jutting from the ridge at the head of the valley, its roofline canted where decades of wind had leaned on it. She had mapped this ridge when she was nineteen, on her second solo commission, and the station had been there then too: a squat granite block with an iron door and a weathervane shaped like a compass needle, built by the old Survey Corps back when Thornfell's borders were worth measuring.

"It's real," Ash said beside her, wonder cracking through the child's usual wariness. "You said it would be real."

"I said I *hoped*," Rafael said. But she was smiling, and her hands had stopped shaking somewhere in the last hour of climbing, and that was something.

They had been in Thornfell proper for two days now, moving up the eastern passes along routes that existed nowhere but in Rafael's memory and in the fading ink of Sela Okafor's charts. The country around them was wrong in ways that were getting harder to ignore. Rivers ran pale as skimmed milk. The pines on the lower slopes had gone translucent at their tips, so that sunlight passed through them like through smoked glass. Twice they had crossed roads that simply ended — not washed out, not blocked, but *ended*, the packed earth dissolving into a gray nothing that made Imre's jaw tighten and Nadya take notes until her fingers went numb.

And always, overhead, the empty sky. No stars even at night. Just a smooth black ceiling, close enough some evenings that Rafael felt she could reach up and touch it.

"The fire's dimmer," Ash said quietly, looking north toward the Spire, invisible beyond three more ridges. The child said it the way other people reported weather. "I can feel it in my teeth."

"Then we make camp," Imre said. "And tomorrow we move faster." The ward-keeper was gray-faced with exhaustion, her left hand still wrapped from the frostbite she'd taken in the Teeth, but her voice carried the flat authority of someone who had spent thirty years being obeyed and hadn't yet learned to stop. "Survey stations have cellars. Cellars have stores. And if the Corps built it right, it has a ward-anchor in the foundation, which means walls that will actually hold warmth."

"You can still read ward-anchors?" Nadya asked. There was no malice in it — the astronomer seemed constitutionally incapable of malice — but Imre flinched anyway, the way she flinched at every reminder of her disgrace.

"I can still read everything," Imre said. "That was never the problem."

* * *

The door was swollen shut with ice, and it took Rafael twenty minutes with her ice axe to persuade it otherwise. Inside, the station was a single long room beneath a vaulted ceiling, furnished with the ghosts of purpose: iron bunk frames rusted to lace, a slate table scarred by decades of drafting pins, shelves of instruments corroded into unrecognizable metal sculptures. Snow had gotten in through a cracked skylight and drifted against the far wall in a pale dune. Everything wore the same skin of dust, fine and gray, like ash.

But the cellar was dry, and Imre found the ward-anchor exactly where she'd predicted — a fist-sized knot of black iron set into the foundation stone, still holding a thread of warmth after all these years. When she laid her good hand on it and spoke three syllables that made Rafael's fillings ache, heat bloomed up through the floorboards, and the room became, for the first time in what might have been fifty years, a place where people could live.

Nadya claimed the slate table immediately and spread her star-charts across it, muttering calculations. Ash curled up on one of the bunks with her coat over her head — the child slept like a creature in a burrow, always, nose tucked under something — and within minutes her breathing went slow and deep.

Imre stood at the window, watching the light die over the valley. "I'll take first watch," she said, which was what she said every night, and every night Rafael pretended not to notice that Imre never woke her for the second.

Which left Rafael alone with the desk.

It stood in the corner behind the drafting table, smaller, private, the kind of furniture a station commander would keep. Its drawers were locked, but the locks were older than the frostbite in Imre's hand, and Rafael had been breaking into official furniture since she was twelve. A knife blade, two minutes of patience, and the top drawer slid open with a groan.

Ledgers. Ration tallies. A roster of names, the Survey Corps' eastern division, forty-one men and women listed with their specialties — altimetry, hydrography, ward-surveying — and a column at the end marked *status* that had been filled in all at once, in the same hand, in ink gone brown: *transferred. transferred. transferred.* Every line. Forty-one transfers, dated the same week, sixty years ago. As if the whole corps had been ordered out of the mountains in a single breath and no one had ever come back.

Rafael's chest did something complicated. She knew this story. Everyone in the lowlands knew it, or had known it once, before the knowing got erased: the Survey Corps had been disbanded, its records sealed, its members scattered to postings in Vess and beyond. An administrative footnote. She'd never wondered why. Footnotes didn't need reasons.

The bottom drawer stuck. She worked it loose, and inside, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff as bark, was a book.

She knew before she touched it whose it was. She knew by the weight of it, by the way her hands wanted to shake, by the small pressed sprig of mountain sage tucked between the pages that matched the sprig in the locket Rafael had carried since she was nine years old — the only thing her mother had left her besides the maps and the name.

*Property of S. Okafor, Chief Cartographer, Thornfell Survey,* said the flyleaf, in handwriting that Rafael recognized with a lurch, because it was the handwriting on every chart in her satchel. Her own hand, older. Or her own hand's grandmother, or great-great-grandmother — however many generations back the blood ran. She had spent her whole life being told she wrote like a boy, cramped and slanted, letters leaning forward as if hurrying somewhere. Now she knew where the hurry came from.

She carried the journal to the hearth-side, wrapped herself in her blanket, and began to read.

* * *

*Day 3,401 of the survey. The Chancellor's men came again today. Third time this season. They want the lattice charts "for review in the capital," and I have told them, as I told them in spring, that the working copies are distributed among my surveyors precisely so that no single copy can be removed without the network's maintenance suffering. This is true. It is also a lie, because the true master is under the third floorboard of my quarters at the Spire, and I am the only living soul who knows it.*

*Rafael — if you are reading this, then I am dead, and the board under the floor is yours.*

*Yes, you. Whoever you are. Whoever you turned out to be. I am writing this to you specifically because there is no one else it can be.*

Rafael stopped reading. Her heart was going hard enough that she checked, absurdly, whether Imre could hear it across the room.

The journal sat in her lap, sixty years old, addressed to her.

*Let me tell you how the wards work, since no one else will, and since the Chancellery has spent a decade making sure the knowledge dies with me. A border is not a wall. A border is an agreement — between a land and itself, between a land and the sky above it, between the name of a thing and the thing named. Thornfell agreed to be Thornfell, long ago, and the agreement was written in dragon-fire, and dragon-fire does not gutter unless something feeds on it.*

*Something is feeding on it.*

*I have traced it for eleven years. The Chancellery calls it "consolidation of provincial administration." What it is, is a slow theft. Every clerk who files Thornfell's taxes under a neighboring province, every cartographer ordered to leave our valleys blank, every schoolbook that drops our name — each one takes a mouthful of the agreement. Erasure is not an act. It is a diet.*

*And the fire that holds the agreement? Fire must be tended by a keeper. And a keeper must be bound to the flame by more than duty, because duty runs out. So I bound it to blood. Mine. I stood at the Spire on the longest night of the year and I gave the lattice my name and my line, so that as long as one Okafor lived and remembered, Thornfell would hold.*

*I thought I was being clever. I thought I was building a lock that would outlast any siege.*

*I did not understand that I was building a candle that burns down.*

Rafael read that passage three times. Outside, the wind worried the eaves; inside, the ward-anchor hummed its low warm note; and Rafael sat very still with the terrible arithmetic unfolding in her chest.

Each keeper's death dims the fire. Not metaphor. Mechanism. Sela had wired the survival of an entire country into the continuity of a single family — a family that had, generation by generation, been dwindling. Rafael's mother, who had died when Rafael was nine, in a fall in the very passes Rafael now guided smugglers through. Her grandfather, lost before that. Aunts, cousins, scattered and thinned like the population of any mountain country, except that in this family every death was a draft blown against a flame.

How many Okafors were left?

Rafael already knew the answer. She had known it since the night the stars went out, though she'd refused to say it even to herself.

One.

* * *

She read until the candle burned down to a puddle, and then she lit another from Imre's store, and kept reading. Sleep was not going to come anyway.

*Entry 3,612. My daughter asks why we cannot simply leave. She is six and she is smarter than the entire Chancellery. I told her the truth: that if we leave, the fire dims faster, because distance matters, because memory matters, because the lattice holds best near its anchor. She asked me if that meant we were prisoners. I said no. I lied. I am recording the lie here so that someday, when you read it, you will know that I knew it was a lie when I said it.*

*We are prisoners, and I built the prison, and I would build it again, because the alternative was watching ten thousand people become unpersons. But you deserve to know that your keeper chose this for you before you existed, and that I am sorry, and that I am not sorry. Both things are true. That is what inheritance is.*

Rafael's eyes stung. She wiped them impatiently with the heel of her hand and glared at the page as if it had done something to her, which, she supposed, it had.

Grief came strangely. Sela Okafor had been dead for decades; Rafael had never heard her voice, never seen her face, knew her only as a name in a ledger and a style of handwriting. And yet here she was, alive on the page — funny, furious, tired, honest in a way no one in Rafael's life had ever quite managed to be. Grieving her felt like grieving a stranger and like grieving herself, both at once, because every entry was also a mirror: the same restlessness, the same love of the high empty country, the same habit of drawing maps when she couldn't sleep.

And underneath the grief, rising slow and hot like water through a fault line, anger.

Because Sela had chosen. Sela had stood at the Spire and volunteered, weighed the cost, accepted it, written her apology and her refusal-to-apologize in the same paragraph. But Rafael had never been offered anything. She had inherited a debt the way you inherit eye color — invisibly, totally, before you had any say in the matter. Every smuggler's run she'd ever guided, every map she'd ever drawn, every year she'd spent saving coin for a boat passage south and a new life in a city with a real sky — all of it had been spent inside a cage whose bars she couldn't see, tending a fire she didn't know she was keeping alive just by existing.

Her mother had died carrying this. Had died *of* it, maybe — the journal hinted that keepers who strayed too far from the Spire sickened, faded, fell. Rafael thought of her mother's face, pale in the last months, and had to put the book down for a while.

When she picked it up again, Imre was watching her from the window.

"You've been crying," the ward-keeper said. Not unkindly. Nothing about Imre was ever unkind, exactly; it was just that kindness, in her, came pre-packaged with bluntness, like medicine in bitter coating.

"I found something," Rafael said. Her voice came out rough. She cleared her throat. "From the Survey Corps. From—" She stopped. Started again. "From my family."

Imre crossed the room and sat on the bunk opposite, elbows on knees, waiting. She was good at waiting. It was one of the few things Rafael genuinely liked about her.

"She bound the wards to our blood," Rafael said. "Sela Okafor. Deliberately. On purpose. She tied the whole country to one bloodline and let it thin out over sixty years like — like a fuse burning down." The anger was fully awake now, and she let it speak. "Nobody asked me. Nobody asked my mother. We were born into a contract we couldn't read, in a language nobody taught us, and the penalty clause is that when the last of us stops existing, so does everyone else. Ten thousand people. Tied to *me*. To whether I decide to stay or go."

"Do you want to go?" Imre asked quietly.

"That's not the point—"

"It's entirely the point." Imre's eyes were steady. "But it can wait until morning. Right now, show me the book."

* * *

They read together for the rest of the night, taking turns, Rafael translating the technical passages while Imre supplied the ward-theory the journal assumed its reader already knew. Nadya joined them around midnight, drawn by the lamplight, and filled three pages of notes on Sela's descriptions of the lattice — "she measured the stars' positions *inside* the wards," the astronomer kept saying, half to herself, "sixty years ago, she documented them, she *knew*—" — until Imre sent her to bed with the firmness of a woman used to managing obsessive subordinates.

Only Ash stayed asleep, burrowed and breathing, dreaming whatever half-dragon children dream.

Near dawn, Rafael reached the final entries.

*Entry 3,890. The Chancellery has ordered the evacuation of the Spire garrison. Officially, "administrative restructuring." Unofficially, they believe that if the keepers are removed, the country will forget itself, and forgetting can be made permanent. They are not entirely wrong. That is the worst part. They are not entirely wrong.*

*I have perhaps a year. The fire gutters when I sleep now. I can feel the edges of the valleys going soft, like wet paper. So I am doing the last thing a cartographer can do: I am drawing the way out. There is a door, Rafael. There has always been a door. Every erasure leaves a seam, and a seam can be opened. I am binding it to the Hollow Road, so that when the end comes — mine, or the country's — there will be one road left that leads out of the dream. Call it mercy. Call it cowardice. I have called it both.*

*But hear me, whoever you are, blood of my blood: the Door is the easy thing. The Door is what I would choose, and I was a coward at the end, tired and alone, and I do not trust my own judgment anymore. So I leave you both paths, and no instruction, because instructions are just another kind of prison and I have built enough of those.*

*The fire or the Door. Stay or go. I am sorry. I am not sorry.*

*Both things are true.*

*— Sela Okafor, last Keeper of the Spire, Chief Cartographer of a country that deserves better than what I gave it*

The candle had burned out. Gray light stood in the windows. Somewhere below them, muffled by stone and snow, Ash was screaming.

Rafael was moving before she was fully awake, blanket tangling, boots half-on, taking the cellar stairs in two strides. The child was sitting bolt upright on her bunk, coat fallen away, both hands pressed flat against her sternum as if holding a wound closed, and her eyes were open and blazing amber and full of tears.

"Ash—"

"*It's going out,*" the child gasped. "*I can feel it going out, it's cold, it's cold where the fire should be—*" She looked at Rafael, and there was such naked terror in her face, such absolute certainty, that every argument Rafael had been rehearsing all night — *the Door, the escape, the boat south, the life owed to her* — burned away like fog in sun.

"How long?" Rafael heard herself ask. "Ash. Look at me. How long do we have?"

Ash swallowed. The amber in her eyes banked slowly back to brown. "Days," she whispered. "Not weeks. Days."

Behind Rafael, Imre was already pulling on her gear. Nadya was gathering her charts with shaking hands. And Rafael stood in the gray dawn light of her ancestor's station, Sela's journal heavy in her pocket like a second heartbeat, and understood that the choice she had been circling all night had just been taken out of her hands.

There was no time left to be angry about the prison.

There was only time to decide what to do inside it.

"We walk for the Spire," she said. "Now. While there's still light enough to find the switchbacks." She swung her satchel onto her shoulder, and her hand brushed the journal, and she added, quieter, to no one — or to one person, sixty years dead:

"Hold on. We're coming."
