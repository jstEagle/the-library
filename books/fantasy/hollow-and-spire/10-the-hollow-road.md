## 10. The Hollow Road

Ash smelled the road before anyone else did.

It was not a smell like smoke, though everything about Ash was smoke-adjacent — the faint char under her skin, the way her breath came warm even in mountain cold. This smell was older. It was like the inside of a banked fire after everyone has gone to bed: ash and iron and something sweet gone to cinders. It was the smell of *between*, and Ash had been born half in it.

She stopped on the trail and lifted her chin, nostrils flaring, and behind her Rafael stopped too, because Rafael always watched for what Ash noticed now. Imre came up last, favoring the leg she'd twisted in the Teeth, and Nadya clutched her star-charts against her chest as if the wind might steal them.

"What is it?" Rafael asked. Her voice had changed since the avalanche — softer at its edges, but with a core of iron that hadn't been there before. Ash liked listening to it. It sounded like a door being held open.

"The road," Ash said. "The real one. The one that isn't a road."

She didn't have the words the grown-ups used. She was nine, or she had been nine when the Spire's fire began to gutter, and time moved strangely inside her anyway, the dragon-half of her counting years the way a hearth counts them — by fuel, not by suns. But she knew what she knew. She knew the fire was dying. She knew the country was forgetting itself. And she knew there was a road underneath all roads, the one her mother had walked once, carrying Ash inside her, when the Chancellery's erasure first began to chew at the edges of Thornfell and things that were lost needed somewhere to go.

"It's down there," Ash said, pointing past the trail's edge, past the scree and the pines gone gray at their tips, into a fold of the mountainside that looked like nothing at all. "Where the shadow is too smooth."

Rafael crouched beside her and looked. Ash watched her do the cartographer thing — eyes moving in little jumps, measuring, comparing, finding the lie in the landscape. Rafael was good at lies in landscapes. It was one of the reasons Ash had chosen her, back in the refugee camp, out of all the hollow-eyed people who smelled of nowhere. The others had looked through Ash. Rafael had looked *at* her, and then had knelt down so their eyes were level, which no one ever did, and had asked her name as if names still mattered.

"Ash," she'd said. "Like a fire."

"Like what a fire leaves," Ash had answered, and Rafael's face had done something complicated and sad, and Ash had known: *this one*.

Now Rafael traced the fold of the mountain with one finger, not touching it, just tracing the air above it. "There's no path here," she said slowly. "But there's no *rock* here either. Not really. It's like..." She frowned. "Like a page where something was erased so hard it took the paper's grain with it."

"Yes," Ash said, relieved. Grown-ups needed things explained in their own language before they could believe them. "That's how you get in. You walk on the erasing."

Imre made a sound low in her throat. She was doing her ward-keeper face, the one that meant she understood exactly enough to be frightened. "The Hollow Road," she said. "My grandmother told stories. She said the old keepers used it to move between the anchor-stones, back when the lattice was whole. She said you don't walk it. You *fall* between, politely."

"Politely," Nadya repeated. The astronomer had been quiet for two days — quietly recalculating, quietly staring at the sky that wasn't there, quietly grieving stars the way other people grieved people. Now her head came up and her eyes had that hungry shine Ash had learned meant hope. "A space between moments. If the lattice stored the held stars somewhere, somewhere *outside* the ordinary flow of—"

"You'll get your sums later," Imre said, but gently, for her. "First we have to not die."

Ash tugged Rafael's sleeve. "I lead," she said. "You can't find it without me. My mother showed me. And—" she hesitated, because the next part was the part that made grown-ups go pale, and she was tired of making Rafael pale, but she owed her the truth; that was the rule between them, the rule they'd never spoken aloud but both kept. "And you'll see things in there. Things that got erased. Don't touch them. Some of them are still trying to be real, and if you hold on to one, it holds on back."

Rafael looked at her for a long moment. Then she nodded, once, and shouldered her satchel — the satchel with Sela Okafor's charts in it, the maps that remembered what the country had forgotten — and said, "Lead on, then. I'll follow your nose."

Ash turned toward the too-smooth shadow, breathed in the smell of banked fires and ended things, and stepped off the edge of the world.

* * *

Falling between, politely, felt like this:

It felt like the moment when you blow out a candle and the dark hasn't finished arriving yet. It felt like the pause between heartbeats, stretched until it creaked. Ash walked forward and the mountain was suddenly not around her, and also it had never left, and also she was walking through the memory of a mountain that some vast patient hand had rubbed away — and under her feet, where stone should have been, there was a road.

It was pale. That was the first thing, every time. Pale as bone, pale as the moon that wasn't in the sky anymore, and it stretched ahead of them through a grayness that had no distance in it. No walls. No ceiling. No horizon. Just the road, and the sediment drifting on either side of it.

That was the word Ash's mother had used, once, when Ash was small enough to be carried and curious enough to ask. *Sediment.* The lost things of an erased country, drifting slow as silt in deep water. And here they were.

There was a farmhouse, or the idea of one, hanging thirty feet off the road's edge and turning over very slowly, its chimney trailing smoke that had forgotten which way smoke goes. There was a wedding dress, folded neatly around nothing, drifting like a jellyfish. There was a flock of sheep — Ash counted eleven — moving across a meadow that existed only beneath their hooves, and when the last sheep passed, the meadow went with it. There was a man sitting on a chair that floated, whittling a stick that was already gone, his hands moving and moving, and he did not look up as they passed, and Ash was glad, because some of the lost things had faces and the faces were the worst.

Behind her, she heard the others' footsteps change — the hitch and stumble of people whose bodies kept expecting ground rules that didn't apply.

"Oh," Nadya breathed. "Oh, it's beautiful. It's terrible. It's—"

"Don't name anything," Ash said, without turning. "Names are handles. In here, handles work both ways."

They walked. The road unspooled. Time did something soft and strange around them; Ash felt it in her teeth, the way she felt the Spire's fire, a dim far-off pulse that stuttered now instead of beating. Somewhere ahead — or maybe everywhere at once — the fire was guttering, and this place, the between-place, was where everything Thornfell had lost came to rest, and lately the country had been losing so much.

A village drifted past on their left, whole and silent and mid-morning forever: laundry on lines, bread smell that reached them from nowhere, a dog asleep on a step. A woman stood at a well, drawing water, drawing water, drawing water. As they drew level with her, she paused. Turned her head. Her face was pleasant and unfinished, like a sketch someone had abandoned.

"You're going toward the Spire," the woman said. It wasn't quite a question.

"We are," Ash said, because her mother had taught her that too: be polite to the sediment. They were nobody's enemies. They were only sad.

"Take the bread," the woman said, holding out a loaf that steamed. "Someone should eat it. Someone should remember there was a bakery on Coil Street, and Marta baked on Thursdays, and her husband played the fiddle badly and everyone loved him." Her pleasant unfinished face crumpled, just slightly, at one corner. "I've been drawing water for a hundred years and no one remembers Thursdays."

Ash took the bread. It was warm and real and heavy, and the woman smiled with her almost-face and returned to her well, and the village drifted on behind them, and Ash heard Rafael make a small broken sound and did not look back at her, because looking would make it worse for both of them.

They ate the bread as they walked. It tasted like Thursdays. Nobody spoke for a long time.

* * *

The futures started after the second rest.

Ash didn't know why the Hollow Road showed them — whether the between-place was generous, or cruel, or simply so empty that it filled itself with whatever walked through it. Her mother had warned her: *the road will offer you mirrors. Don't buy any.* But she couldn't stop it from happening, and she couldn't stop watching Rafael's face when it happened to her, and maybe she didn't want to. Maybe she wanted to know what Rafael saw. Maybe that was selfish. Ash was learning that most big feelings were a little bit selfish, and that this was allowed.

It began as a shimmer at the road's edge, like heat-haze in winter. Then another. Then dozens — pale panes standing in the grayness, each one showing something moving.

Nadya reached toward the nearest and Imre caught her wrist. "Don't," Imre said. "Whatever it is, don't."

But Rafael had already stopped in front of one.

Ash stood beside her and looked too, because it was Rafael's mirror and Ash trusted her more than she feared anything.

In the glass: a harbor city, all noise and salt and rigging-creak. Rafael stood on a dock with a travel-worn satchel and no one beside her, and she was laughing at something a sailor said, and she was signing papers — ship's charts, Ash realized, sea charts — and she looked *light*. Unburdened. She looked like a woman who had put down something heavy a long way behind her and never once looked back. The mirror-Rafael turned, as if sensing them, and her eyes swept right past the glass, past Ash, past everything, and there was nothing in her face of the campfire warmth or the fierce digging hands or the way she said *I've got you* when Ash woke screaming. She was happy. She was happy and she was a stranger.

"That's not me," Rafael said quietly. "That's who I was going to be. Before."

"There's another one," Ash said.

And there was: further down the road, a pane showing a small stone house in the foothills, and Rafael older, gray coming in at her temples, teaching apprentices at a long table covered in maps, and through the window behind her the Spire stood on its mountain with fire crowning it like a second dawn. In that mirror, Rafael turned and looked directly out — straight at Ash — and smiled, and mouthed something the glass wouldn't carry.

Rafael's breath caught. "That one's not possible," she whispered. "The fire's nearly out. That's—"

"The road doesn't show what is," Ash said. "It shows what's *owed*. What could still be paid." Her mother had said that too, or something like it, in the last days before the camp, before the coughing, before the small grave that no longer officially existed. "Some of the mirrors are lies. You have to smell them. The true ones smell like the fire."

Rafael leaned close to the second pane and breathed in, and Ash watched her shoulders change — watched something settle in her, some argument ending.

"And this one?" Imre's voice, rough. She was standing before a third mirror, and she wasn't letting anyone else look at it, standing square in front of it with her arms slightly out, the way ward-keepers stand. Whatever it showed, her jaw was set like masonry. "This one's mine, and none of you need see it." But her voice cracked on the last word, and Ash caught a sliver of the image past her elbow: a cell, a candle, a table, a man with kind cold eyes leaning forward — and then Imre shifted and it was gone.

Nadya stood apart, before a mirror that showed no city, no house, no faces. It showed a sky. Full of stars — hundreds, thousands, wheeling in patterns no living astronomer had ever seen, because those constellations belonged to a sky that had been taken apart and shelved. Nadya had both hands pressed flat against the glass as if she could warm it, and tears ran freely down her face, and she was smiling, and it was the most frightening smile Ash had ever seen.

"They're alive in there," Nadya whispered. "All this time, they've been *alive* in the lattice. Look how they've arranged themselves — they're not stored, they're *huddled*. Like people in a cellar during a bombardment." She laughed, wet and wondering. "We can let them out. We can actually—"

"Later," Ash said softly, and touched the astronomer's hand, and Nadya came back to herself with a shudder.

They walked on. The mirrors thinned. But Ash noticed that Rafael kept glancing sideways at her own hands, flexing them, as if checking whether they already belonged to one future or the other. And Ash noticed something else, something that pricked at her dragon-side like a burr in a paw: the road was getting darker ahead. Not shadow-dark. *Absence*-dark. The pale bone-color of the way was fading, patch by patch, like coals going gray.

The between-place was running out of between. Because the between was fed by the fire, and the fire was dying, and when it died there would be no Hollow Road at all — no resting place for lost things, no mirrors, no mercy of memory. Everything erased would simply stop being anywhere, including in here.

Including Ash, maybe. Half of her had come from this side of things. She had never let herself think that thought all the way to the bottom before. She thought it now, all the way, walking, holding Rafael's sleeve so she'd know where the real world ended.

Rafael looked down at her. "What is it?"

"The road's dying faster than we walk," Ash said. "We have to reach the Spire before the last coal. After that there's no way in at all. Not for anyone. Not ever."

"How long?"

Ash closed her eyes and listened to the stutter in her blood, the far-off heartbeat of the Spire. Counted, the way a hearth counts.

"Days," she said. "Maybe four. Maybe fewer."

Nobody said anything. But all three of them — the mapmaker, the keeper, the star-counter — lengthened their stride, and Ash trotted to keep up at the center of them, where the warmth was, and the Hollow Road carried them onward through the sediment of a country that was forgetting itself, toward the one small stubborn flame that remembered everything.

* * *

They came out of the between at dusk, three days' hard walk south of the Spire, onto a hillside that smelled blessedly of pine and dirt and ordinary wind. Behind them the too-smooth fold of rock sealed itself like water closing over a stone.

Ahead, in the valley below, the river Vess cut silver through the gloaming, and where it shallowed into a wide ford there were lights. Campfires. Too many, and wrong — laid out in a grid, neat as a ledger. Banners Ash didn't recognize hung limp in the still air.

Chancellery blue-and-silver.

Imre went very still. "That's a forward post," she said. "That's not a patrol. That's an *occupation*. They're staging at the ford — they're preparing to move on the Spire." Her voice dropped to something bitter and quiet. "Someone told them our route. Someone knew we'd come down out of the high passes, and when."

Ash felt the cold arrive before she understood it. She looked at Rafael, and Rafael was looking at the ford, and her face had gone the color of the Hollow Road.

"The guide," Rafael said. "Dorin. The one we hired below the Teeth." She swallowed. "He knew the passes. He knew where we'd cross. He knew *everything*."

Down at the ford, a lantern swung, and figures began to move between the fires — unhurried, organized, certain. Waiting for travelers who would, within a day, come walking down out of the hills straight into their hands.

Ash's hand found Rafael's without either of them deciding it. Above the occupied valley, in the starless dark, the distant Spire pulsed once — faint, faltering, furious — like a heart refusing, for a few days yet, to stop.
