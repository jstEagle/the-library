## 12. Interrogation by Candlelight

They took her boots first.

Imre Vashti had expected that. Boots were the first thing taken from ward-keepers too, back in the old days — not out of cruelty but out of doctrine. A keeper's boots touched threshold-stones. They carried residue. The Chancellery had not changed its manuals since the Purges; it had only changed which side of the door its officers stood on. So when the woman with the ledger-voice ordered a guard to remove Imre's boots and burn them in the yard, Imre felt something almost like nostalgia.

The cell was stone, dry, and cold in the particular way of buildings warded against listening. She could feel it the moment they shut her in: a hum beneath the floor, faint as a toothache, running up through her bare soles into the bones of her ankles. A suppression lattice. Crude, but competent. Whoever had laid this prison's wards had known their trade once, and then been made to forget it, the way the Chancellery made everyone forget everything.

Imre sat on the cot, folded her hands, and began to count.

Not sheep. Nodes.

*One,* at the door lintel — she'd felt it spark against her scalp as they ducked her through, a little net of compliance-wards meant to dull resistance. *Two,* under the floor, the suppression hum. *Three,* somewhere above, in the ceiling plaster, where the air tasted faintly of copper — a scrying anchor, probably, feeding someone's viewing-glass an image of her sitting docile on her cot. *Four…*

She was on eleven when the candle came.

It arrived before the interrogator did, carried by a guard who set it in a wall sconce with the exaggerated care of a man handling something consecrated. A single tallow candle, unremarkable except for one thing: it burned without flickering. Perfectly still. A flame like a painted flame, like a memory of fire rather than fire itself.

Imre looked at it for a long moment and felt the first genuine fear she'd experienced since the ford.

Containment-flame. She'd read about them in Sela Okafor's marginalia, in texts the Chancellery had burned everywhere except inside Rafael Okafor's satchel. A containment-flame didn't give light so much as *define* it — it drew a boundary around a room and everything said within that boundary stayed within it, pressed flat between the walls like flowers in a book. You could scream under a containment-flame and no god, no ghost, no sympathetic ear three kingdoms away would hear you.

Which meant the Chancellery intended to say things tonight that even their own archives must never record.

The door opened. The man who entered was not what she'd expected, though she couldn't have said what she had expected. Torturers came in all shapes; she'd imagined someone broad and scarred, someone whose body did his arguing for him. Instead he was slight, gray at the temples, with ink stains on his fingers and the tired eyes of a man who read reports past midnight. He carried a second chair, set it down across from her, and sat with the sigh of someone settling into a long evening's work.

"Imre Vashti," he said. "Formerly Senior Warden of the Third Circle, Thornfell District."

"Formerly," Imre agreed. "You have me at a disadvantage. You know my whole history and I don't even know your name."

"Halloran." He unfolded a sheet of paper. "Chancellery Office of Boundary Integrity."

"Boundary Integrity." She let the words sit in her mouth like a stone. "That's a lovely phrase. It sounds almost like the opposite of what it means."

Halloran didn't rise to it. He smoothed the paper on his knee. "You were apprehended at Corrin Ford in company with two fugitives and a child of restricted classification. Your companions escaped. I'm not interested in how — that's being handled. I'm interested in where they're going."

"The mountains," Imre said.

"There are no mountains."

"No," she agreed pleasantly. "There aren't. Silly of me."

For the first time, something moved behind Halloran's eyes — not anger. Recognition. The look of a man meeting an old acquaintance in an unexpected place. "You're going to be difficult."

"I'm going to be honest," Imre said. "You've asked me where two fugitives are going, and I've told you: toward a country your office insists does not exist. If my answer is nonsense, that's because your question is. I can't navigate by a map you've erased. Neither, I suspect, can you — and that's why you're sitting in this cell at midnight instead of sending soldiers after them."

He studied her. Outside the containment-flame's perfect circle of light, the guard by the door shifted his weight, and the floor's suppression-hum throbbed once, like a heart remembering it had a job.

"You were a warden," Halloran said. "A good one, before the incident at the Third Circle. The reports say you could read a failing ward by taste."

"The reports are accurate. I could also read them by smell, sound, and occasionally by argument — wards have opinions, if you listen long enough. Most people never learn that because most people are taught that wards are furniture." She leaned forward slightly. "Is that why I'm here, Master Halloran? Not the child, not the smuggler's route. You want a warden. You've been erasing keepers for thirty years and now you've caught one, and some part of your office is quietly delighted."

The candle did not flicker. But Halloran's hand, resting on his knee, went very still.

"I'm here," he said carefully, "to ask you questions."

"Then ask one worth answering."

* * *

He asked four more that were not worth answering, and she gave him four answers that were technically true and practically useless, and by the second hour they had settled into something neither of them would have called conversation and both of them recognized as one.

It was Imre who broke the pattern.

"Your candle is wrong," she said.

Halloran paused, quill hovering over his useless notes. "Excuse me?"

"The containment-flame. It's drawn wrong." She nodded at the sconce. "Look at the base of it. See how the light sits heavy near the wax, like it's pooling? Whoever charged that flame anchored it from below. That's the old method — pre-Purge. It works, but it leaks. Everything said in this room presses down into the foundations and stays there, soaked into the stone like lamp-oil into a table. In fifty years, this prison will be saturated with every secret ever spoken under these candles. Tear down one wall in the wrong place and the whole building will start *talking*."

"That's absurd."

"It's architecture," Imre said. "Wards don't vanish, Master Halloran. Nothing your office erases actually vanishes — that's the dirty secret at the center of everything you do. Erasure isn't destruction. It's *storage*. You don't delete Thornfell. You put it somewhere. And storage has weight, and weight has consequences, and one day — " she spread her bare, bootless feet on the cold stones — "one day the bill comes due."

Silence. The guard by the door had stopped pretending not to listen.

Halloran set down his quill. "You're stalling."

"Of course I'm stalling. Every hour I sit here is an hour they're walking. But I'm also right, and you know I'm right, because you've seen the foundation reports for this building and there's a seepage problem in the east wing that your engineers keep patching with mortar and it keeps coming back." She watched him flinch — a small flinch, quickly mastered, but real. "Ah. There it is. How long has the east wing been damp, Master Halloran? Years? And has anyone told you *why*?"

"How could you possibly — "

"Because I can feel your prison's wards through the soles of my feet, and they're screaming." She said it flatly, without drama, and that was what made it land. "Eleven nodes I've counted from this cot, and node seven — the suppression lattice under this floor — is bleeding charge into the bedrock because whoever installed it grounded it wrong. It's been bleeding since the day it was lit. This entire building is a badly-tuned instrument, and everyone in it has simply gotten used to the sour note."

Halloran stared at her. And Imre saw the thing she had been fishing for since the moment he walked in: hunger. Not the interrogator's hunger for confession — the scholar's hunger, buried deep under thirty years of bureaucratic scar tissue, for someone who *knew things*. The Chancellery had spent a generation burning ward-theory, and it had burned along with it every man and woman who understood what the Chancellery was actually standing on.

"You could tell me how to fix it," Halloran said slowly. It wasn't a question. It wasn't even, quite, an offer. It was a man testing ice.

"I could teach you," Imre said. "Fixing is easy. Understanding is the work. So — shall we pass the night productively? You have candles, paper, and apparently hours. I have nothing but time and a grudge. Let's call it a lesson."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then we return to your questions, and I return to my answers about mountains that don't exist, and we'll both be very bored until dawn." She smiled, and knew it was not a kind smile. "Your choice, Master Halloran. But I'll note that you haven't called for the guards yet, and it's been several minutes since I accused your office of malpractice. Curiosity is a terrible security risk. I'd have flagged you myself, back in the day."

* * *

They started, as all lessons should, with fundamentals.

"A ward," Imre said, "is a promise made of geometry. Nothing more mystical than that. You draw a shape, you fill the shape with intent, and the world agrees — temporarily — to honor the contract. The failure modes are always the same three. The shape degrades. The intent fades. Or the world changes its mind."

"The world doesn't change its mind."

"The world changed its mind about Thornfell, didn't it?" She held up a hand before he could answer. "No — don't retreat into doctrine, it's late and I'm tired and you'll only embarrass yourself. Think instead. Your office erased a country. Where did it go?"

Halloran's jaw tightened. "That's above my clearance."

"Everything is above your clearance; that's how the Chancellery keeps its officers honest — or rather, obedient, which it confuses with honesty. But answer the pedagogical question. A boundary ward of that scale doesn't destroy. It *inverts*. It takes everything defined inside the boundary and folds it inward, into a space that has no outside. The country still exists. It exists *inside itself*, wrapped around its own fire like — " she searched — "like a fist around a coal. And the stars your astronomers can't find? Ask yourself where the sky goes when you fold a country. The sky was part of the boundary. It folded too."

She hadn't meant to say that last part. It slipped out — Nadya's theory, half-heard around a campfire, dressed now in lecture robes. But Halloran had gone pale in the candlelight, and Imre realized with a cold drop in her stomach that she hadn't told him anything he didn't already suspect. Somewhere upstairs, in offices with higher clearances, someone knew exactly where the stars were.

"Continue the lesson," Halloran said quietly.

"Gladly." And she did — grounding principles, load distribution, the reason suppression lattices needed breathing-room the way lungs needed air. She drew diagrams on the floor with her fingertip, tracing lines in the dust of the stones, and Halloran copied them onto his paper with the careful strokes of a man relearning a language he'd been punished for speaking.

And all the while, Imre counted.

Node twelve revealed itself during the second hour: a relay in the corridor outside, carrying the scrying-feed upward. Node thirteen when Halloran sent the guard for fresh water and she felt the doorway's compliance-net cycle and reset. Node fourteen was a surprise — a second suppression layer, deeper than the first, older, built by different hands. Pre-Chancellery hands. This prison had been something else once. She turned that over while she explained resonance damping, and turned it over again, and began, very quietly, to smile.

Old wards were honest wards. They predated the doctrine of erasure. Which meant the deep layer under this prison hadn't been built to hide secrets.

It had been built to hold something *in*.

"Master Halloran," she said, mid-lesson, casually, "what was this building before it was a prison?"

"A garrison."

"Mm." She pressed her palm flat to the floor, as if steadying herself, and let her senses sink through the suppression hum into the deep, slow pulse beneath it. Fourteen nodes. Fifteen, at the far corner of the foundation, nearly dormant. Sixteen — no. Sixteen wasn't a node. Sixteen was a *door*. Sealed, starved, forgotten, but a door, with the unmistakable hinge-feeling of a threshold that had once opened onto somewhere else. "This garrison has a basement your blueprints don't show."

"Every old building has a basement the blueprints don't show."

"Not like this one." She withdrew her hand and met his eyes. "But that's advanced coursework. Tonight we cover fundamentals. Node placement, load balancing, the three failure modes. Homework: go look at your east wing and think about what I told you."

He almost smiled. She saw him catch it, kill it, and mourn it — a small death, there in the candlelight, of whatever the Chancellery had left of the man. "You realize," he said, gathering his papers, "that none of this changes your situation. Tomorrow there will be more questions. Harder ones. My superiors are — less patient than I am."

"I know," Imre said. "I'm counting on it. Every hour you spend being patient is an hour I spend being useful, and the Chancellery never burns a useful asset on schedule. Delay is a strategy, Master Halloran. I taught it to wardens for twenty years. Consider this a refresher course."

He stood, hesitated, and then — she would remember this afterward, turn it over in the dark like a coin — he inclined his head. Barely. A bow of perhaps half an inch, from one practitioner of a dying craft to another.

"Same time tomorrow," he said, and was gone.

* * *

The candle remained. Of course it did; the room was still sealed. Imre lay back on the cot, bare feet crossed at the ankle, and let the silence close over her like water.

Sixteen nodes. One hidden door. One interrogator who bowed.

She mapped it all again behind her closed eyes, line by line, the way Rafael drew her impossible maps — because memory was the only cartography the Chancellery couldn't confiscate. Node seven, bled dry and singing sour. Node sixteen, the sealed door in the deep foundation, older than the office that owned the building. The east wing's seepage. The pooling light beneath a wrongly-anchored flame. A prison, she thought, was just a map of its makers' fears, and she had spent the whole night reading it.

They would come for her. Rafael would come — the girl navigated by stubbornness the way other people navigated by stars, and she had Ash, and Ash trusted her completely, and trust like that moved mountains that officially didn't exist. When they came, they would need what Imre had bought tonight: the layout, the weaknesses, the sour note in the instrument.

Her feet were freezing. Her ribs ached from the ford. Tomorrow there would be harder questions and men less patient than Halloran, and the odds were bad, and getting worse.

Imre Vashti smiled at the painted-still flame and began, silently, to draft her escape in dust-lines on the underside of the cot — and it was there, tracing the sixteenth node with one fingernail, that she felt it: far away, at the edge of perception, the deep pulse of the prison stuttered.

Once. Twice.

Like a great heart, somewhere north, missing a beat.

The Spire was guttering. And every ward in the world that had ever been kin to it — including, Imre realized with a chill that had nothing to do with the cold, the old sealed door beneath her cell — had just felt it too.
