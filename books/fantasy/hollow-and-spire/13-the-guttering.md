## 13. The Guttering

They saw it from the ridge above Kellin's Hollow, three days after the prison break, and Rafael would remember for the rest of her life that her first thought was not *the Spire* but *my map is wrong again.*

She had the satchel open on a flat stone, checking their route against Sela's master chart — the one she'd stolen back from the Chancellery archive at the cost of two burned fingers and a week of Nadya's nightmares. The chart showed the valley below them in fine brown ink: terraced fields stitched like quilt squares, the river bending through Kellin's Hollow in a lazy silver S, smoke rising from forty chimneys, the little bell tower with its green copper roof that Rafael had once marked as a smuggler's landmark because you could see it for miles.

The chart was right. The valley was wrong.

"Rafael." Ash's voice, small and strange. "Rafael, look at the edges."

Rafael looked up from the ink and the world tilted.

The edges of the valley were going soft. Not fading the way mist fades — mist has texture, movement, a kind of honesty about what it is. This was different. The far terraces were still there, still solid where she looked directly at them, but when her eye slid toward the ridgelines they seemed to lose their agreement with themselves, the way a word repeated too many times stops sounding like a word. A farmhouse near the western slope flickered. She blinked and it was whole. She blinked again and there was only a smear of color where a farmhouse had been, like wet paint dragged by a thumb.

"That's not possible," Imre said behind her. Her voice was flat, which Rafael had learned meant fear. "Wards don't fail like that. They fail all at once or they fray at the seams. They don't—"

"They don't dissolve," Nadya finished. She had her brass spyglass out, elbow braced on her knee, and her hands were shaking so badly the instrument chattered against her knuckles. "Except that they are. Look at the tree line. Look at the *tree line*, Imre."

Rafael took the spyglass from her because someone had to hold it steady.

Through the lens, the forest at the valley's northern edge was being erased in slow strokes. Trees nearest the ridge stood dark and certain. Beyond them, the canopy thinned into something translucent, green bleeding out of the leaves until they were sketches of leaves, then suggestions of green, then nothing — and beyond the nothing, the grey of the lowlands showed through, the plain that officially, according to every map and ledger and customs seal in the Chancellery, ran unbroken to the horizon. As if Thornfell had never interrupted it. As if the mountains were a hole in the world that was finally closing over.

The bell tower in Kellin's Hollow rang noon. The sound reached them thin and delayed, like a voice calling up from underwater.

"It's accelerating," Nadya said quietly. She'd stopped shaking, which was worse; Nadya steady was Nadya frightened past the capacity for tremors. "When we crossed the Teeth, the erosion was at the borders only. Outermost lattice anchors failing first, exactly as the mathematics predicted. But this—" she gestured at the dissolving treeline, at the flickering farmhouse, "—this is interior decay. The fire isn't just dimming anymore. It's guttering. There's a difference. A candle that dims can be shielded. A candle that gutters is drinking its own wax."

"How long?" Imre asked.

Nadya didn't answer immediately. She crouched, pulled her chalk stub from her coat, and began working numbers directly onto the flat stone beside Rafael's maps, fast and ugly, figures crossing each other out, half a proof abandoned mid-line. Rafael watched over her shoulder without pretending to understand more than a tenth of it. What she understood was the rhythm: Nadya wrote, stopped, stared, wrote faster.

"Nadya." Ash tugged the astronomer's sleeve. "How long?"

"Days." Nadya sat back on her heels. Chalk dust clung to her fingers and one cheekbone where she'd pushed her hair back. "Not weeks. I thought we had weeks. I was wrong. Days. Four, perhaps five, before the interior lattice loses coherence entirely. After that—" she looked at the valley, at the bell tower, at the forty chimneys' worth of people who did not know yet, "—after that there won't be anything left to relight."

Nobody spoke. Below them, unhurried and oblivious, a farmer drove a cart along the river road, and Rafael found herself staring at him with an intensity that must have been close to horror — memorizing him, though she'd never seen him before, the tilt of his hat, the way he raised one hand to shade his eyes and look up at the ridge where four strangers stood watching his world come apart.

He couldn't see them. That was the thing. The wards still held enough to keep Thornfell invisible from outside, even as it ate itself from within. To the farmer, the ridge was empty. To the farmer, everything was fine, and would be fine, right up until the moment it wasn't anything at all.

"We go now," Rafael said. "Tonight if we can. Now."

"The switchbacks take six days in good weather," Imre said. "We don't have six days."

"Then we find a way to make them take three." Rafael rolled the charts closed with hands that wanted to shake and refused. "Imre, you told me once there was a ward-keeper's path up the eastern face. Maintenance access. You said your mother used it."

"I said it existed. I didn't say I could find it after thirty years, on a mountain that's currently forgetting its own shape." Imre rubbed her face. In the three days since the prison, she hadn't slept more than a few hours at a stretch; the bruise from the interrogator's ring was fading yellow along her jaw, and she touched it absently, the way she touched everything now, as if confirming things were still real. "And even if we reach the Spire in time, we still need—" She stopped. Looked at Rafael. Didn't finish, because everyone knew how the sentence ended. *We still need an heir willing to burn.*

"Then let's not waste time standing here cataloguing everything that can go wrong," Rafael said. "I promise you, I've already done it. Twice. Before breakfast."

That got the ghost of a smile from Imre, which had been the entire point. Rafael shouldered the satchel and turned to call the order of march, and that was when Ash screamed.

* * *

It came out of the child like something torn rather than spoken — a high, raw sound with no words in it, the scream of an animal caught in a wire. She was on her knees in the scrub grass with both hands pressed flat against the ground, and the ground beneath her palms was smoking.

Not metaphorically. Thin grey threads of vapor curled up between her fingers, and where her skin met the earth the grass had gone black in a perfect handprint, five small scorch marks fanning outward.

"Ash!" Rafael dropped beside her. "Ash, look at me—"

Ash's eyes were open but unfocused, pupils blown wide and gold, gold flooding out from the pupil into the iris like ink dropped in water. Heat came off her in waves. Rafael had known since the refugee camp that the girl ran warm — she'd slept curled against Rafael's side through the cold passes, a small furnace of a child — but this was different. This was heat with nowhere to go, heat looking for a door.

"She's feeling it," Nadya said, kneeling on Ash's other side. "The Spire. Oh — oh, of course. Of course she is. She was born of the fire, Rafael, she's not watching it gutter, she's *connected* to it—"

"Ash." Rafael caught the small face in both hands and made herself be looked at. Up close she could see the fine cracks of light beneath Ash's skin, faint as veins of ore in stone, pulsing unevenly along her throat and temples. Flickering. Like the farmhouse. Like the trees. "Hey. Hey, little ember. You're here. You're with me. Breathe with me, can you do that? In through your nose. Come on."

Ash dragged in a breath that hitched and broke. "It hurts," she whispered. "It hurts, Rafa, it's *dying*, I can feel it dying, it's like — like my chest is full of it and it's going out and I can't — I can't breathe for it—"

"I know." Rafael pulled her in, and the heat of her was almost too much against Rafael's own skin, and she held on anyway. "I know, sweetheart. I'm sorry. I'm so sorry. We're going to fix it. Do you hear me? We're climbing to it right now, today, and we're going to fix it."

"You promise?"

Rafael had spent her whole life avoiding promises. Promises were debts, and debts were chains, and chains were the reason she'd become a smuggler's guide instead of a surveyor's daughter who stayed put. She looked down at the scorched handprints in the grass, at the child shaking in her arms, at the valley below where a farmer drove his cart through the last ordinary noon his home might ever have.

"I promise," Rafael said. And felt the chain click shut around her own ribs, and found, to her astonishment, that it didn't feel like a cage. It felt like an anchor.

The gold receded from Ash's eyes slowly, tide going out. When the girl finally sagged against her, spent, the cracks of light beneath her skin had dimmed to almost nothing — and Rafael understood, with a clarity that felt like a knife sliding between her ribs, that what she'd just witnessed wasn't a seizure. It was a countdown. Ash was a splinter of the Spire's flame walking around in a child's body, and the child was dimming in step with the fire.

Every hour mattered now. Every single one.

* * *

They made the ward-keeper's path by late afternoon, and it was worse than Imre remembered, which was saying something, because Imre remembered it as a goat track with delusions.

It clung to the eastern face of the massif in a series of ledges no wider than a cart, cut into living rock by hands that had trusted the mountain to stay put forever. In places the rock itself had begun the same soft dissolution they'd seen in the valley — edges blurring, handholds going vague under the palm like grips carved in wet clay. Rafael went first, testing each hold twice, calling warnings back down the line. Behind her came Ash, strapped to Imre's back with rope and stubbornness, because after the screaming none of them were willing to let the child climb alone. Nadya came last, white-knuckled and muttering calculations like prayers, which Rafael suspected was exactly what they were.

Halfway up the face, the mountain flickered.

There was no better word for it. One heartbeat the ledge stretched ahead of them, solid and grey and ancient. The next, for the length of a swallowed breath, Rafael could see straight through it — through the rock, down the sheer drop, to the valley floor a thousand feet below, where the river caught the low sun like a wire of fire. Then the ledge was back, and her boots were on it, and her heart was trying to exit through her teeth.

"Don't stop," she called back, and hated how thin her voice sounded in all that air. "Whatever happens, don't stop moving. Momentum's the only thing keeping us real right now."

"Charming theory," Imre panted. "Any evidence?"

"You're still here, aren't you?"

They climbed through the last of the daylight. Once, passing a shelf of sheltered stone, Rafael found old ward-marks cut into the rock — keeper's sigils, the same spiral-and-flame motif that ran through Sela's charts — and laid her palm against them the way you'd touch a sleeping animal. For a moment she thought she felt something answer, a warmth like a banked coal, there and gone. She didn't mention it. Hope was a currency she couldn't afford to spend carelessly.

They camped that night in a hollow below the final ridge, huddled around a fire that Nadya built with obsessive precision while Imre checked and rechecked the ropes. Ash slept against Rafael's side, fever-warm, twitching through dreams. Twice she whimpered the same word in her sleep — *out, out, out* — and Rafael stroked her hair until the word dissolved.

Above them, visible through the notch of the hollow, the Spire stood against the stars.

Or where the stars should have been. That was the thing Rafael kept having to relearn: the sky over Thornfell was empty. Had been empty since the night the wards snapped, since the night her maps began rewriting themselves in her hands. Nadya's held stars, locked in the failing lattice, waiting for a fire strong enough to fling them home. Tonight the darkness above the peak seemed deeper than the darkness anywhere else, a hole in the night shaped like everything that had been taken.

And the Spire itself — Rafael had seen it drawn in Sela's charts a hundred times, a needle of stone crowned with flame, the anchor-point of an entire country's reality. What she saw now was a needle of stone crowned with a light like a dying lamp. It pulsed. It stuttered. Between pulses it sank so low she could convince herself it was already out, and then it rallied, weak and stubborn, the way a candle rallies when a door closes against the draft.

"It's beautiful," Ash murmured, awake after all, watching it. "Even like that. Even dying. It's still the most beautiful thing I've ever seen."

Rafael didn't trust her voice, so she said nothing, and put her arm around the child, and watched the fire gutter.

* * *

Near midnight, Nadya shook her awake.

"Come look at this," the astronomer said. "You're the mapmaker. Tell me I'm wrong."

Rafael crawled out of the hollow into the bitter starless dark. Nadya had spread the master chart on a flat rock and weighted its corners, and by lantern-light she was comparing it against the skyline — except the skyline refused to match. Rafael saw it immediately, the way she'd once seen errors in smuggler routes at a glance: the chart showed the Spire's summit a full day's climb beyond the final ridge. The actual peak, looming black against the empty sky, was closer. Much closer. Close enough that the fire's pulse lit the clouds — no, not clouds, there were no clouds — lit the *darkness* above it in slow, arrhythmic surges.

"The mountain's contracting," Nadya said. "As the lattice fails, the spatial anchors pull inward. Everything is drawing toward the Spire. Which means—" she tapped the chart, tapped the peak, tapped the chart again, "—our six-day route is now a three-day route. Possibly less. The geography is doing our work for us."

"And the catch," Rafael said, because there was always a catch, and Nadya's face had the particular grimness of a woman holding one behind her back.

"The catch is that contraction accelerates as coherence fails. It's exponential. If the pattern holds, we have perhaps seventy hours before the collapse becomes total — and the last twelve of those will be inside a landscape that is actively coming apart." Nadya met her eyes across the lantern. "And Rafael — the Chancellery knows. An event this size, they'll have instruments on it. Whatever force they sent to erase Thornfell permanently, it will be converging on the Spire too. From the other side. From the lowlands. They'll arrive rested, supplied, and armed."

Rafael looked at the guttering fire on the peak. At the hollow where Ash slept, dreaming of drowning. At Imre, sitting watch with her sword across her knees and her jaw set like a woman who had already decided how she'd spend whatever was left.

Seventy hours. One exhausted cartographer, one disgraced keeper, one astronomer with chalk dust in her soul, and one small burning child.

"We climb at first light," Rafael said. "And we don't stop."

Above them, the Spire's flame pulsed once — bright, defiant, brief — and sank again toward the dark, like a heart deciding, beat by beat, whether to keep beating at all.
