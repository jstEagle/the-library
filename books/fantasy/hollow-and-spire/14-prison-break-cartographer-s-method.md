## 14. Prison Break, Cartographer's Method

The Chancellery archive in Vess smelled of dust and lamp oil and the particular sourness of paper that had been handled by frightened men. Rafael knew the smell. She'd grown up in her father's map room, breathing it like a second language.

She stood now at the top of the archive's outer stair, dressed in a courier's grey coat two sizes too big for her, her hair bound and tucked under a cap, a delivery satchel heavy against her hip. Below her, through the tall windows of the reading hall, clerks moved between the shelves with the slow, shuffling gait of people who had been told their work mattered and no longer believed it.

Three days since the ford. Three days since Imre had turned back into the reeds with a drawn ward-blade and a look on her face that Rafael was going to carry for the rest of her life — whatever length that turned out to be. Three days of Nadya hunched over borrowed star-tables in a rented room above a dyer's shop, calculating guard rotations from the rhythm of lanterns she could see from the window, while Ash sat pressed against Rafael's side and asked, every few hours, in a voice getting smaller each time: *Is tomorrow the day?*

Tonight was the day.

Rafael checked the courier's chit in her hand one more time — a forgery so good it had taken Nadya two hours and an argument about ink oxidation to produce — and descended into the belly of the building that had swallowed her friend.

* * *

Getting into the archive had been insultingly easy. That was the thing about institutions built on erasure: they were very good at making things disappear, and terrible at imagining anything coming back. The gate clerk glanced at the chit, glanced at her face, saw a courier and nothing else. People saw what the paperwork told them to see. She had spent her whole life among smugglers who understood this; apparently the Chancellery had never learned it.

Inside, the archive was organized by a logic only its creators understood, which meant it was organized by no logic at all. Rafael had expected order — catalogued shelves, numbered drawers, the tidy machinery of a bureaucracy confident in its own permanence. Instead she found chaos wearing a uniform. Whole sections had been re-shelved after the Night the Stars Went Out, when some panicking undersecretary had ordered everything relating to Thornfell pulled for review. Pulled, reviewed, and then shoved back wherever there was space, because review turned out to mean *burning*, and burning a country's records took time, and time was what the Chancellery suddenly didn't have.

So they hadn't burned them yet. They were waiting. And Sela Okafor's master chart — the original, the one confiscated from Rafael's own satchel in this same town, the one around which all the copies in her keeping had been made — was somewhere in this building, filed under a country that officially did not exist.

Rafael walked the aisles slowly, a courier delivering nothing, letting her eyes do the work. She didn't search the way a thief searches, hunting for a prize. She searched the way a cartographer reads terrain. Shelves had gravity here; the clerks' habits had left channels in the dust, worn paths where hands reached most often. The Thornfell material would be far from those paths — quarantined, awkward, shelved where nobody wanted to reach.

She found it in the northwest corner, behind a rack of defunct tax ledgers from provinces that had also stopped existing: a shallow drawer marked in faded pencil, *T— MISCELLANEOUS, PENDING*. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth that still smelled faintly of her own satchel's leather, lay the master chart.

Her hands shook as she unwrapped it. She hadn't realized until that moment how much of her had been convinced she'd never see it again — how much of her had accepted, somewhere below thought, that the Chancellery was right, that the map was a forgery, that she was a woman carrying papers for a place that had never been.

But there it was. Sela Okafor's hand in every line — the same hand Rafael had studied for years without knowing whose it was, the same stubborn downstroke on the north ridgelines, the same tiny stars inked in the margins like a signature written in sky. And across the whole of it, stamped in red wax that someone had tried and failed to peel away: *FORGERY — CHANCELLERY OF RECORDS.*

Rafael looked at the stamp for a long moment. Then she did something small and private and entirely unprofessional.

She laughed.

They had examined the truest document in the world and called it false. They had held a country in their hands and filed it under *pending*. For three days she had felt like the madwoman, the ghost, the girl who remembered things that never happened. But the madness wasn't hers. It had never been hers. It belonged to the men who had decided that if you couldn't hold a border, you would simply insist it had never existed — and who were now discovering, in the way of all liars eventually, that insisting is not the same as making true.

She refolded the chart along Sela's original creases — she could feel where they wanted to go, the paper remembering — slid it into the false bottom of her satchel beneath three genuinely boring tax schedules, and turned for the stairs.

That was when the bells began.

* * *

Nadya's diversion was, in Rafael's professional opinion, a work of art.

The plan had been simple: at the appointed hour, Nadya would present herself at the Chancellery observatory office — the branch tasked with explaining the missing stars to a public that was beginning to ask questions — with a "discovery." A new calculation. Proof, she would say, breathless and terrified in exactly the right proportion, that the vanished stars were not gone but *moving*, that they were descending, that on the basis of her figures something was going to come out of the sky within the week.

It was even true, more or less. That was Nadya's favorite kind of lie: the kind assembled entirely from facts arranged in an order designed to cause panic. The stars *were* held in the lattice, and the lattice *was* failing, and if it failed completely the stars would indeed come out — violently, all at once, in a release that would scorch the lowlands like a struck match. Nadya simply neglected to mention that the solution was a girl with dragon-blood standing on a mountain, and instead let the Chancellery's own astronomers arrive at the conclusion that the sky was falling and nobody had a ladder.

From inside the archive, Rafael heard the effect before she understood it. First a single bell — the observatory's signal. Then a second bell, different pitch. Then voices, rising, overlapping, the specific cacophony of competent people realizing simultaneously that they might be doomed. Doors banged along the corridor. Someone ran past the archive entrance holding a stack of charts like a man rescuing children from a fire.

"Seal the lower levels!" someone shouted. "Get the deep files up — if the sky comes down I want the records *out*—"

Which was, Rafael reflected, hauling open the service stair door, exactly the wrong instinct, and she loved Nadya Solenne with her whole heart.

The cells were in the lower level — the Chancellery kept its prisoners close to its records, because in Vess the two were often the same thing. She went down two flights into air that tasted of stone and cold iron, following the route she had memorized from Nadya's lantern-count and her own knowledge of how buildings like this were built. Every Chancellery structure she'd ever mapped shared a skeleton: service stairs behind the record rooms, because the archivists had refused, generations ago, to carry heavy boxes through public halls.

The copied key-map hung inside her coat, drawn in Nadya's precise hand from the impressions Rafael had lifted off the warden's ring three nights ago, pressed into warm wax stolen from a chandler's shop. Wax doesn't lie. Wax remembers pressure the way paper remembers creases. She had traced the wards etched into the cell-block door from those impressions — Imre's own design, ironically, from her ward-keeper days, before her disgrace; the Chancellery had hired the best and then imprisoned her for knowing too much.

The lock was a five-pin warded cylinder, and the wards were Imre's, and Rafael had spent two evenings learning them by candlelight while Ash slept against her shoulder. She worked the copied key-map into the mechanism, felt for the false gates, and turned.

The door opened onto a corridor of six cells, lamplit, empty except for one.

Imre Vashti sat on the cot with her back against the wall, hands folded, posture straight, looking — Rafael would remember this always — like a woman who had been expecting company and was mildly annoyed at the delay. There was a bruise along her jaw and her left wrist was splinted badly, but her eyes were clear and furious and alive.

"You're late," Imre said.

"The archive has terrible signage."

"Three days, Okafor."

"You're welcome," Rafael said, and threw her the second key-map, the one cut for the wrist irons.

Imre caught it one-handed, looked at it, and something crossed her face that Rafael had never seen there before — not gratitude exactly, something rawer and less comfortable. Recognition, maybe. The look of a woman realizing she had been rescued not by luck or muscle or money but by *craft*, by someone sitting down night after night with wax and wire and patience until the impossible became a procedure.

"Whose wards are on these locks?" Imre asked, working the irons free.

"Yours."

A short, barking laugh. "Of course they are. I built better than they deserved." She stood, tested her weight, winced at the wrist. "Nadya?"

"Causing a theological emergency upstairs. We have maybe twenty minutes before someone thinks to check whether the panic is real."

"Then we run," Imre said. "Ash?"

"At the safehouse with the dyer's wife, who has decided Ash is her granddaughter and can't be argued with."

Something in Imre's shoulders came down half an inch at that. Rafael pretended not to notice. Some things you let people keep.

They went up the service stairs two at a time, into noise.

* * *

The upper floor had come fully unmoored. Clerks streamed through the halls clutching documents; two junior officers argued over a barricade of filing cabinets that served no purpose anyone could name; a man in Chancellery grey stood at a window pointing upward at a perfectly ordinary, perfectly starless evening sky as though it had personally threatened him.

Rafael and Imre walked through the middle of it, unhurried, two more frightened employees. It was Imre's idea — "never run from a panic, walk beside it" — and it worked until the archive door.

Because the archive door had a keeper, and the keeper had not joined the panic. He was an old man, half-asleep at his desk, and as they passed he looked up with the bleary suspicion of the one person in any institution who has seen everything twice.

"Couriers don't leave through the record hall," he said.

"No," Rafael agreed pleasantly, and kept walking.

He stood. "Wait. Those are restricted—" His eyes went to the satchel, then to her face, and narrowed. "I know you. You're the map woman. From the customs house. There's a warrant—"

"*There's a falling sky,*" said a voice behind them, rich with scholarly outrage, and Nadya Solenne swept around the corner with three other astronomers in tow, all of them moving fast, all of them radiating urgency, none of them slowing down. "Every second you spend harassing couriers is a second the calculations aren't being distributed. Do you want to be the man who lost Vess its warning? Give me your name. *Give me your name.*"

The old keeper gave his name. He gave it several times, in fact, as Nadya wrote it down with great ceremony in a notebook, informing him that his obstruction would be entered into the official record of the crisis, and by the time she had finished ruining him, Rafael and Imre were through the door and down the outer stair and into the street.

They walked. They did not run. At the third corner, where the lamplight failed and the dyer's-shop district began, Imre finally exhaled — a long, shaking breath, the first honest sound she'd made since the cell.

"That astronomer," she said, "is going to get us all killed someday."

"Yes," Rafael said fondly. "But think how interesting the postmortem will be."

* * *

The safehouse was one room above the dye-works, blue-stained floorboards, a smell of mordant and tallow, and it had never in its life contained anything as important as what waited inside it now.

Ash heard them on the stairs. Rafael knew it before the door opened — felt the small body hit her at a dead run, arms around her waist, face buried in her coat, smelling of woodsmoke and the dye-wife's lavender soap. "You came back," Ash said into the fabric, fierce and wet. "You said you'd come back and you *came back*."

"I said I would."

"People say."

"I know," Rafael said, kneeling, holding on. "That's why I said it."

Then Ash saw Imre in the doorway and made a sound Rafael had never heard from her, high and cracked, and launched across the room, and Imre caught her one-armed and went down under it onto the blue boards, laughing — actually laughing, the disgraced ward-keeper of Thornfell laughing like a woman released from something heavier than a cell — and Nadya shut the door behind them all, leaned against it, and let her performance drop off her face like a mask.

"Well," Nadya said. "We're all going to die spectacularly, but tonight we dine like heroes. The dye-wife sold me a goose."

They ate on the floor, because there was no table, and it was the best meal Rafael had ever had, though the goose was slightly burnt and the bread was stale. Ash fell asleep halfway through her plate, mid-sentence, her head tipping against Imre's good shoulder, and neither of them moved for an hour afterward.

Later, when the others slept, Rafael unrolled the master chart on the floorboards and weighted its corners with candlesticks, and read it by the last inch of tallow light.

With fresh eyes — eyes that had seen the Chancellery call it a forgery, eyes that had broken into a prison to get it back — she finally saw what Sela had hidden in plain sight. Not roads. Not passes. The lattice itself, every ward-anchor in Thornfell rendered as a surveyor's mark, and at the center, at the Spire, a symbol she had always taken for a compass rose.

It wasn't a rose. It was a flame, drawn in fine concentric lines, and around it, in Sela's smallest, tightest script — the script Rafael now recognized as the same hand from the journal in the survey station — ran a single line of text:

*The fire holds what the sky has lost. Only blood may carry it. Only choice may light it.*

Below that, newer, added in different ink:

*If you are reading this, I am sorry, and I am proud, and both are true.*

Rafael sat back on her heels. Behind her, Ash murmured in her sleep, and the sound was like a coal settling in a grate.

Tomorrow they climbed. Tomorrow the Teeth, the Hollow Road, the switchbacks, the Spire, the fire, the choice. Tomorrow she would have to decide what she was willing to burn.

But tonight — tonight she dipped her pen and, beneath Sela Okafor's final words, added four of her own in the margin of the master chart, small and steady:

*Not alone. Never again.*

Outside the window, over a country that officially did not exist, the darkness where the stars should have been seemed, for just a moment, to flicker — as if something far away had felt her write it.
