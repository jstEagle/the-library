## 15. Nadya's Proof

Nadya Solenne had spent eleven years in a cell for the crime of being right too early, and she had learned one thing about truth in that time: it did not care whether you were ready for it.

She sat cross-legged on the floor of the safehouse's back room with Sela Okafor's master chart unrolled before her, weighted at the corners with four chipped teacups because nothing else in the house was heavy enough to hold down three hundred years of inked certainty. The candle guttered. Outside, Imre was arguing with Rafael about watch rotations, and Ash was asleep against the doorframe, curled like a cat in a patch of moonlight that should not have existed — because there were no stars left to make moonlight, and yet the child glowed faintly anyway, the way coals glow after the fire is raked over.

Nadya had not slept in two days. She did not intend to sleep tonight either. Tonight she intended to be right again — and this time, she suspected, being right would cost someone everything.

She smoothed her hands over the chart and began where all honest work begins: with measurement.

* * *

The master chart was not a map. Nadya had understood that within minutes of first seeing it, but understanding a thing and proving a thing were different disciplines, and only one of them could survive an interrogation by candlelight. The Chancellery had taught her that much. Every claim must be shown its own bones, or it would be dismissed as fever, as treason, as madness. She had been imprisoned on precisely those three charges, in ascending order of severity.

So: bones.

The chart depicted Thornfell entire — every pass, every valley, every village whose name the Chancellery had since scraped from its ledgers. But beneath the geography, visible when the light struck the vellum at a low angle, ran a second country: a lattice of fine silver lines, so dense and so regular that no human hand could have drawn them freehand. They converged on the Spire the way rivers converge on a sea. And along each line, at intervals too precise for coincidence, Sela Okafor had inked small circles — hundreds of them, thousands — each circle marked with a notation in a dead surveyor's shorthand.

Nadya had spent her first night decoding the shorthand. It had taken her until dawn, and when she finally understood what the circles represented, she had put down her pen and walked out into the cold and stood under the starless sky for a long time, breathing.

The circles were stars.

Not symbols of stars. Not representations. Coordinates — each circle marking the position in the sky where a specific star had hung before the night they went out, annotated with the date of its "extinction" as recorded by observers who had died generations before Nadya was born. Sela Okafor had catalogued the heavens of Thornfell the way other cartographers catalogued coastlines. And she had done it across three hundred years of entries, which meant the Okafors had been watching the lattice take the stars, one by one, for as long as the wards had stood.

They had known. All of them. Every keeper of the Spire had known that the fire fed on something, and they had known what, and they had written it down in a language only their heirs would read — and then, apparently, they had let the knowledge die with them, generation after generation, until only a smuggler with a satchel full of inherited maps remained.

Nadya found she was not angry. That surprised her. She had expected fury — at the concealment, at the arrogance of deciding on behalf of a whole sky. But what she felt instead was closer to recognition. She knew what it was to hold a truth too large for the room you were standing in. She knew what it was to look up at a wrongness everyone else had agreed to ignore, and to be unable to stop looking.

Sela Okafor had been an astronomer too, in the end. Nadya was certain of it now. The shorthand was not surveyor's notation; it was observational logbook convention, the kind used by the great observatories of the old capital, the kind Nadya herself had been trained in before the Chancellery burned the observatories and called the ash *reform*. Somewhere in this family's history, someone had looked up and refused to look away.

And that someone had worked out how to give the stars back.

Nadya turned to the final leaf of the chart — a separate sheet, older, cracked along its folds, covered in equations that made her heart beat faster the way certain kinds of music did. This was the proof she had been assembling all day, checking and rechecking, because if she was wrong this time she would not merely be imprisoned; she would send a woman and a child into a fire.

The mathematics was beautiful. That was the first thing she noticed, and the thing that frightened her most, because nature was not cruel but it was economical, and elegant solutions always exacted precise costs. The lattice, Sela had written, was a container. The ward-fire at the Spire was the seal. When the borders were drawn — when Thornfell had been bound to dragon-fire to save it from whatever war had nearly unmade it — the binding had required a sky's worth of energy, and the founders had paid it in the only currency large enough.

They had banked the stars. Drawn them down out of heaven and folded them into the wards, where they burned as the fire that held the borders real. A country purchased with its own constellations. And the plan — Sela's plan, written in a hand that grew shakier toward the bottom of the page — had always been that the debt would come due someday. That a keeper would relight the fire from within, releasing the stored light back into the lattice, and the lattice would spend it twice: once to mend itself, once to throw the stars home.

*Provided*, Sela had written, underlined twice, *the flame passes through Okafor blood. The binding knows its maker. No other hand will open the vault.*

Nadya checked the derivation eleven times. She checked the constants against observations she had memorized in prison, reciting the old sky to herself in the dark to keep from going mad. She checked the decay curve of the ward-lattice against the rate of star-loss recorded in the margins, and the numbers agreed to within a margin so small it made her eyes sting.

Then she did the last calculation, the one she had saved for last because she had hoped she was wrong about it.

The lattice was failing. Everyone could see that now — the fraying edges of the mountains, the villages going soft as breath on glass. What the eye could see, the mathematics confirmed: the container was breached, and the stars inside were bleeding out, dissipating into the space between things, becoming the sediment Ash spoke of on the Hollow Road. Lost things. Drifting things. In days — not weeks, days — the reserve would fall below the threshold needed for release. Below the threshold, the stars would not come home. They would simply finish dying, quietly, inside a country that had already forgotten it had a sky.

And the release required a flame passed through Okafor blood.

Nadya sat back on her heels and looked at the sleeping child in the doorway, and thought: *I am going to have to tell her.*

* * *

She found Rafael on the roof of the safehouse, which was not really a roof but a collapsed section of upper floor that someone had swept clean and furnished with two crates and a view of the eastern ridgeline, where the mountains faded at their edges like unfinished sketches. Rafael stood at the rail with her arms folded, watching the dissolution with the expression of a cartographer looking at terrain that refused to be surveyed. Imre was below, still arguing with nobody about the watch.

"You've finished," Rafael said, without turning. It wasn't a question. Nadya had apparently developed the same tell all prisoners developed — a particular quality of silence when the work was done.

"I've finished."

"And?"

"Come sit down. Please."

Rafael came. She sat on the crate across from Nadya, and Nadya saw her take in the chart spread between them, the equations, the candle, and braced herself the way people brace for bad news — shoulders up, chin level, eyes steady. A woman who had been lied to by an entire world and had survived it. Nadya made herself meet those eyes.

"The stars are alive," Nadya said. "That's the first thing. They're not destroyed. They were never destroyed." She laid her hand flat on the master chart. "Three hundred years ago, when your ancestors bound Thornfell behind the wards, the binding needed power beyond anything mortal. So they took the sky down. Star by star. Folded them into the lattice. Every light we lost on the night the wards snapped — and every light lost slowly across the centuries before that, which is why the old records show the heavens thinning — they're all still here. Stored. Burning as the ward-fire."

Rafael said nothing. Her jaw was very tight.

"The lattice is a vault," Nadya went on, "and the fire is the lock, and the lock can be opened from the inside. Relight the fire at the Spire — truly relight it, feed it — and the release sequence initiates. The wards draw what they need to mend themselves, and the surplus goes home. Back up. Back into place." She tapped the final leaf, the equations. "I've verified it against every observation I have. It works. The mathematics is sound. The stars return, Thornfell solidifies, the borders blaze. Everything we've been climbing toward is possible."

Something moved through Rafael's face — hope, actual hope, arriving like weather — and Nadya hated herself for what she had to do next.

"But," Rafael said.

"But there's a condition. Written into the binding itself. The vault doesn't recognize keys or spells or sacrifice offered by strangers." Nadya turned the final leaf toward her. "It recognizes blood. The flame has to pass through an Okafor. Through *the* Okafor. You're the last one on any record I've seen, and I've seen the Chancellery's genealogical archives — they were thorough about your family, once they decided to erase it."

The hope didn't leave Rafael's face so much as it changed state, the way water changes to ice. Same substance. Different structure.

"What does it mean," Rafael said quietly, "*pass through.*"

"It means the fire enters you and leaves you. It means you become the conduit. For some duration — hours, possibly longer — every star in this sky will be moving through your blood." Nadya kept her voice level, clinical, because that was the only mercy she had to offer: precision. "Sela's notes are explicit that the keeper survives. She designed it that way. She writes — here — '*the heir is the door, not the fuel.*' But she also writes that no one has ever done it, that the closest attempt killed her daughter, and that the failure mode is—" Nadya stopped. Recalibrated. "There are ways this kills you. I won't pretend otherwise. I'll spare you the details unless you ask."

For a long moment Rafael looked at the mountains fading in the dark. Then she laughed — short, disbelieving, almost fond.

"A week ago I was a smuggler," she said. "A good one. The best in the lowlands, if you asked me, and I did ask me, frequently. My whole plan was to get out. Get past the checkpoints, get to the coast, get a ship, and never see another mountain again." She shook her head. "Now the price of the sky is my blood, and do you know what the terrible part is? I'm not even surprised. Of course it's me. It's been me since chapter one of my life. The maps, the name, the satchel — I was conscripted before I could walk."

"You can refuse," Nadya said. "There's the Hollow Door. Sela's echo will offer it. It's real, and it opens once more, and you'd live."

"I know." Rafael's voice was flat. "That's what makes it a choice instead of a sentence. Someone was thoughtful enough to leave me a way out, so I'll actually have to pick." She looked down at the chart, at the neat circles where the stars waited in their long imprisonment, and her hand came to rest over the notation for a constellation Nadya recognized — the Cartographer's Yoke, which had hung over Thornfell every winter of Nadya's childhood, and which she had privately considered the most beautiful object in creation.

"How long," Rafael said, "do we have?"

"Four days. Perhaps five. After that the reserve drops below threshold and no blood in the world matters."

"Then I need to tell Ash tonight. And Imre. Before I lose my nerve or find a clever way to lie to them." Rafael stood, and paused, and looked back down at Nadya with an expression that was almost gentle. "You could have softened it. Rounded the corners. Made it sound survivable and simple."

"I spent eleven years in a cell because men in good coats rounded the corners of the truth until it fit their ledgers," Nadya said. "You'll get it straight from me or not at all."

"Yes," Rafael said. "I know. That's why I'm going to trust you with the most important night of my life." She headed for the stair, then stopped again. "Nadya. When the stars go back — will you be able to find yours? The ones you memorized? In the dark, in the cell?"

Nadya looked up at the blank sky and felt, for the first time in eleven years, something loosen in her chest.

"Every one," she said. "I never wrote them down. I couldn't risk it. So I keep them here." She touched her temple. "Forty-one constellations, nine comets, and the positions of six hundred and twelve named stars, current to the year of my arrest. I am, as far as I know, the last complete map of the old sky."

"Then you're coming to the Spire," Rafael said. "Someone has to check my work."

She went down the stairs. Nadya remained on the broken roof a while longer, alone with the chart and the candle and the empty heaven, and allowed herself — briefly, carefully, the way one tests ice — to feel the hope.

It was real. The mathematics was sound. The stars could come home.

But hope, she had learned, was a blade you carried by the edge, and somewhere below her, a woman who wanted nothing more than to escape was walking down the stairs to tell a child that the sky cost everything — and Nadya already knew, with the terrible certainty of someone who had been right too early her whole life, exactly what the child would say.

She blew out the candle, because the dark no longer frightened her, and followed.
