## 16. What Rafael Wants

They made camp in the shell of a shepherd's hut below the last ridge before the Spire's switchbacks, a place that existed on no map and, Rafael suspected, was rapidly ceasing to exist at all. The eastern wall had slumped into a drift of stone that crumbled like old bread when Ash climbed it. The roof beams were sound enough. The hearth still drew.

Imre built the fire with a ward-keeper's economy — three stones arranged in a triangle she claimed was older than any border, kindling laid north-to-south "so the flame remembers which way home is." Nadya said that was superstition. Imre said all of ward-craft was superstition that happened to work. They bickered the way people bicker when they are afraid and have agreed not to say so.

Rafael sat with her back against the wall and her satchel open across her knees, doing what she always did when her hands needed an excuse not to shake: she drew. The ridge line above them. The notch where the switchbacks began. The faint shimmer along the valley floor where the mist had eaten two farmsteads since morning — she marked those as dashed lines, then, after a moment, erased even the dashes. There was nothing left to map there. She was beginning to understand that some of her work now was cartography of absences, and that the absences were winning.

Ash sat close enough that their shoulders touched. The child had been quiet all day — quieter than usual, which for Ash meant something, because Ash's silences were never empty. She smelled faintly of woodsmoke even this far from any fire but their own, and every few minutes her small hand would find Rafael's sleeve and grip it, just briefly, the way you'd test a rope.

"You're drawing us," Ash said.

"I'm drawing where we are."

"Same thing." Ash leaned her head against Rafael's arm. "Maps are just places wearing their names."

Rafael didn't answer. She wasn't sure whether that was wisdom or six-year-old nonsense, and with Ash it was usually both.

The fire cracked. Somewhere up-valley, something that used to be a village sighed into fog, a sound like a long exhale through cloth. Nobody looked toward it. They had all learned not to look.

It was Nadya who broke the silence first, because Nadya could not bear a silence longer than a quarter hour; she filled them the way she filled notebooks, compulsively, with whatever came to hand.

"I've been thinking," she said, "about what happens after. If we make it. If the fire takes, if the lattice releases, if we walk out the other side of this with a country again." She turned her tin cup in her hands. It held melted snow with a single leaf of dried mint floating in it, rationed like everything else. "I've been thinking about what I'd do. If I got out."

"You've been out," Imre said. "You were born outside."

"I was *imprisoned* inside," Nadya corrected. "There's a difference. Prisoners dream about doors. I dreamed about sky." She tipped her head back. Above the broken roofline, the stars showed only in patches now, as though the night itself were going threadbare. "When they took me — the Chancellery, after my third paper on anomalous stellar occlusion — they put me in a cell with a slit window facing east. Just a slit. A hand's width of sky. And I told myself: when I get out, I will lie in a field somewhere and watch one full night, dusk to dawn, and I will not write down a single thing about it. No measurements. No tables. I'll just watch."

"And did you?" Imre asked. "That night, after we pulled you out?"

Nadya was quiet for a moment. "No," she admitted. "I started calculating the lattice constants by moonlight before dawn. Old habits." She laughed, but it came out thin. "So that's what I want. One night of sky I don't try to own. I want to be a person looking up instead of an astronomer taking inventory. I think I'd like to know what that feels like. I genuinely don't remember."

The fire popped. Ash reached over and fed it a stick with great ceremony, as though the fire had asked politely.

"What about you?" Nadya asked Imre. "If we get out. What do you do with a life?"

Imre snorted and fed the fire a longer stick. For a while Rafael thought she wouldn't answer. Imre answered questions the way she crossed rivers — testing the depth first, often deciding not to cross at all.

"My father kept the wards at Greywater Ford," she said finally. "His father before him. Eleven generations of Vashtis, and every one of them died within sight of the same stretch of river, and every one of them believed it mattered." She turned the cup in her scarred hands. "I wanted anything else. I wanted the capital, the colleges, a life where the most important thing I maintained was my own reputation. So I went. And I was good. And then I got proud, and I cut corners on a survey because I was certain I already knew what it would say, and four people drowned when the ford-wards failed, and the Chancellery stripped my license and my name from the rolls in the same afternoon." She drank. "Eleven generations keeping a river safe, and I'm the one who taught it how to kill."

"That wasn't—" Rafael began.

"It was," Imre said, without heat. "I've stopped arguing with facts. It's why I get along with your astronomer." She nodded at Nadya. "What I'd do, if I got out? Nothing noble. I'd find the families. The four of them. I'd kneel in their doorways until someone either forgave me or set a dog on me, and whichever it was, I'd deserve it, and then—" Her voice caught, just once, a hairline fracture. "And then I'd go back to keeping something. A bridge. A lighthouse. A fence, if that's all anyone would trust me with. Because it turns out I can't be anything else. I spent ten years trying to stop being a keeper and the whole time I was keeping things anyway. Badly. But keeping." She looked into the fire. "You don't choose what you are. You just spend years finding out."

*You don't choose what you are.* Rafael felt the words land somewhere beneath her ribs and stay there, sharp-edged, like a splinter working deeper the more you moved.

"Ash," Nadya said gently. "Your turn. If we get out of here — if we find the Door, or the fire, or whatever's waiting — what would you want?"

Ash considered this with the total seriousness she brought to everything, her strange amber eyes reflecting the fire in a way that human eyes shouldn't, pupils narrowing to vertical slits when the light hit them directly. She was curled against Rafael's side now, small and warm and smelling of smoke, and Rafael realized she had unconsciously put her arm around the child sometime in the last several minutes without deciding to.

"A house," Ash said. "With a door that opens."

Everyone waited. With Ash you learned to wait; the rest of the sentence always arrived, but on its own schedule.

"When people say *house*," Ash went on, "they mean walls. But walls aren't the important part. The important part is the door. A door is a thing that says *you may come in* and also *you may go out*, and both of those are true at the same time, and nobody disappears when they use it." Her voice dropped. "In the camps, the tents didn't have doors. Just flaps. And sometimes you'd wake up and someone's tent was empty and folded and gone, and the Chancellery men said there was never anyone there, and after a while you couldn't remember their faces right, because—" She stopped. Pressed her face briefly into Rafael's sleeve. Started again, smaller. "Because places that aren't real can't hold people. People need somewhere that's real, or they come apart like smoke. That's what happened to my mother. Not dead. Just — unheld."

She looked up at Rafael then, and the firelight made her eyes into two small furnaces, and she said, very quietly, in the tone of a child stating a fact about weather:

"I just want somewhere that's real. That's all. I don't need it to be big. I just need it to hold."

Nobody spoke. The fire burned. Up-valley, the mist advanced another few yards with its soft cloth-footsteps, patient as arithmetic.

* * *

Later — much later, when Nadya had fallen asleep mid-sentence over her calculations and Imre had taken first watch at the doorway, standing with her back straight and her hand resting on the old ward-stone as if greeting a colleague — Ash finally slept too, curled in the crook of Rafael's arm, breathing smoke-scented warmth against her collarbone.

Rafael should have slept. Tomorrow was the Spire. Tomorrow was whatever waited at the top of the switchbacks — Sela Okafor's chamber, the master chart's origin, the flame that had guttered for two hundred years on the blood of a family that kept dying near it. She knew this. She lay awake anyway, staring at the patchwork stars through the broken roof, and found herself doing the thing she had sworn off years ago: remembering.

She remembered being nine years old, in her grandfather's map room in Vess, the smell of ink and lamp oil, the great survey table where three generations of Okafors had bent over vellum. She remembered asking him — she could still feel the exact shape of the question in her mouth — *Grandfather, have you ever been to Thornfell?*

And he had laughed, that dry rasp, and said: *To Thornfell? Child, the whole point of a map is that you don't have to go.*

She remembered understanding, even at nine, that he was lying — that the maps in that room were love letters written by someone who had never stopped going. She remembered deciding, with the cold clarity children are capable of, that she would not become that. She would not spend her life bent over other places. She would see them. All of them. She would leave Vess at eighteen and she would keep leaving, city after country after coast, and she would draw the world from the middle of it, standing in it, and she would never, ever be a woman who loved a place so much she could only approach it sideways, on paper, forever.

And she had done it. Mostly. Until the smuggling runs brought her back to the passes she'd grown up hearing about, and until the night the stars went out, and until a child who smelled of smoke decided she was worth talking to, and until a disgraced keeper and a star-mad astronomer became, without anyone voting on it, hers.

*If we get out,* Nadya had asked. *What would you do?*

They hadn't asked her. She had let the question pass her by, the way she let a lot of things pass, and now, in the dark, with Ash's weight warm against her side, Rafael asked it of herself honestly for perhaps the first time.

If they found the Hollow Door tomorrow — if Sela's echo opened it the way the journal implied it could be opened, one final passage out of a country that officially did not exist — what would Rafael Okafor do?

Walk through. Of course she'd walk through. She'd been walking toward exits her entire life. She'd take her satchel, and Ash's hand, and she'd step out onto whatever ordinary road lay on the far side of erasure, and she'd find a coast, and she'd start a new map, and the nightmare would be a story she told in taverns for the rest of a long free life.

Except.

Except the image wouldn't hold. Every time she built it — the door, the road, the sea — the picture developed a flaw, the way a bad chart develops a flaw: a coastline that doesn't close, a river that flows uphill, a detail that's wrong in a way you can't ignore once you've seen it. And the flaw was always the same. It was Ash's voice saying *places that aren't real can't hold people.* It was the folded empty tents. It was her own name, she realized, sitting in the ledger of a bloodline that had bound itself to a mountain precisely so that this — this unraveling, this unholding, this slow smoke — would not happen to the people behind the wards. Sela Okafor hadn't drawn roads. She had drawn a promise, in ink and dragon-fire and eventually in her own death, and the promise was: *you will be held.*

Rafael had spent thirty years planning her escape from exactly that promise. And now the promise was dying, and she had discovered, with quiet devastation, that she could not imagine walking away from it — not because of duty, which she distrusted, and not because of blood, which she resented, but because of a sleeping child whose only request in the world was a door that opened onto somewhere real.

She thought: *I could carry her through. The Door would take her. She'd be safe, and real, somewhere else.*

But would she? Or would she be a girl from nowhere, in a country of official records, one bureaucratic afternoon away from a folded tent?

The choice crystallized the way ice crystallizes — not suddenly, but all at once, everywhere, along lines that had been forming for a long time. Rafael lay in the dark and felt it happen and grieved it happening, because she understood what it cost. It cost the coasts. It cost the cities she'd never mapped from the middle. It cost the woman she had planned to be since she was nine years old, the one who never bent over a table mourning a place she couldn't enter. That woman had been her companion for three decades, and Rafael said goodbye to her now, silently, in a ruined shepherd's hut, while a child breathed smoke against her shoulder.

*I stay,* she thought. *Not because I'm brave. Because it's mine. Because she's mine. Because somebody has to hold it, and I am so tired of pretending I don't already.*

She must have slept after that, a little, because she woke to grey light and Imre's hand on her shoulder and a wrongness in the air that raised every hair on her arms.

"Rafael." Imre's voice was level in the way that meant it was taking all her discipline to keep it level. "You need to come look at the ridge."

Rafael eased Ash down onto the bedroll — the child stirred, murmured *"door that opens,"* and slept on — and followed Imre to the doorway.

The mist had stopped advancing in the night. Instead it had begun to climb. It poured up the valley slopes like water running backward, and where it touched the ridge line, the ridge line *softened* — rock going vague at its edges, pines fading to sketches of pines, the whole spine of the mountain losing its conviction. And above it all, where the Spire's summit should have been a black needle against the paling sky, there was a glow. Faint. Unsteady. A heartbeat with a stutter in it.

Nadya stood in the yard with her instruments forgotten in her hands, staring up, and when she spoke her voice was hushed with a horror that was almost reverence.

"The fire's not guttering anymore," she said. "It's drowning. We have days. Maybe less."

Behind them, Ash woke on her own for once — woke screaming, hands pressed flat over her sternum as though something inside her were going out — and Rafael gathered her up, and shouldered the satchel of maps that remembered everything, and turned her face toward the dying light on the mountain.

*Days,* she thought, and began to climb.
