## 17. The Last Ascent

The Spire's switchbacks began where the world stopped pretending.

Rafael saw it first as a wrongness in the light — the way the morning sun struck the mountainside and passed through it, as if the rock were a sketch someone had pressed too lightly into paper. Below them, the valley of Hollowmere still held its shape: the refugee camp's cookfires, the blackened stubble of harvested fields, the river running silver toward the lowlands. But above the third switchback, Thornfell was coming apart at its edges like a wet page.

"Stay on the stone," Imre said, and her voice had the flat calm Rafael had learned to read as fear. "The path is warded. It's the oldest warding in the country — Sela Okafor cut it herself, if your family's story holds. The road will hold even if nothing else does."

"And if it doesn't?"

"Then we hold each other."

Ash walked between them with her hand buried in Rafael's sleeve, gripping the fabric so hard the seams strained. The child hadn't spoken since the ridge — since the Guttering — except once, in the grey hour before dawn, when she'd tugged Rafael down and whispered against her ear: *It hurts more when you look away from it.* So Rafael didn't look away. She kept her eyes on the mountain and climbed.

Nadya came last, burdened by the master chart in its oilskin wrapping, strapped across her back like a coffin lid. Every hundred paces she stopped, unwrapped one corner, and checked the lattice-lines against the dissolving landscape with a brass sighting-glass she'd taken from a dead surveyor's kit. "The fade line is climbing," she reported at the second switchback. "Yesterday it sat below the tree line. This morning it's eating the pines."

"Eating them how?" Imre asked.

"Quietly." Nadya wrapped the chart again. "They don't fall. They just stop having been."

The words settled over the party like snowfall. No one answered. There was no answer that wasn't a lie or a scream, and they had no time for either.

* * *

The fourth switchback crossed a shelf of meadow that should have been loud with late-summer insects. It was silent. Not empty — silent — and Rafael understood the difference with her whole skin. The grass was there. The wildflowers were there, blue and white and a yellow so precise it looked painted. But the small lives that should have threaded through them were gone, and without them the meadow was a portrait of a place rather than a place itself.

Ash stopped walking.

"Ash." Rafael crouched to her level. The child's amber eyes — slitted, dragon-gold, beautiful and terrible — were fixed on a patch of flowers near the path's edge. One of them was flickering. Not wilting: flickering, present and absent in slow alternation, like a candle behind blown glass.

"It's trying to decide," Ash whispered.

"Trying to decide what?"

"If it wants to keep being here." Ash's small hand opened and closed at her side. "That's what going out feels like. You get tired of holding yourself together, and then you have to choose, over and over, every moment, whether to do it again. And then one time you don't."

Rafael took the child's hand. It was warm — always warm, warmer than a child's hand had any right to be, the last banked coal of a fire two centuries old. "Then we'll choose for you until you're strong enough," she said. "Come on. Up the mountain. We're going to fix this."

Ash looked up at her, and for a moment Rafael saw something ancient move behind the young face — something that remembered the Spire burning bright enough to pin the stars in place. Then it was only a girl again, tired and frightened, and she nodded, and they climbed.

* * *

The Chancellery found them at the sixth switchback.

Rafael heard them before she saw them — boots on stone, the metallic whisper of drawn blades — and pulled the party flat against the inner wall of the cut. Below, winding up the path they'd just climbed, came a column of wardens in grey-and-silver livery, twenty strong, moving with the mechanical precision of soldiers who had been told exactly what they were doing and believed none of it.

"They can't see us yet," Imre breathed. She'd gone very still, one hand raised, fingers spread — reading the air the way she'd once read wards, before the disgrace, before everything. "The old warding on the path bends sight around anyone standing close to the wall. Sela built hiding places into her own road. Clever woman."

"Clever dead woman," Rafael muttered.

"The best kind." Imre's mouth twitched, almost a smile. Then it faded. "Rafael. They're not climbing fast. Look at their stride."

Rafael looked. And saw. The wardens moved like men wading through deep water — each step placed with exaggerated care, each breath visible as effort. Their officer at the front kept glancing at his own hands as though checking they were still there.

"They're fading too," Nadya said softly. "The Chancellery erased Thornfell from the maps, but erasure isn't free. Everything inside the border pays the toll. They've been marching through a country that's forgetting how to exist, and it's taking them with it."

The column reached the bend below them. Twenty feet. Fifteen. Rafael counted heartbeats, counted paces, did the smuggler's arithmetic of distance and blade-reach. If the warding held, they would pass unseen. If it failed—

It held. The wardens wound past beneath them, close enough that Rafael could see the frost of exhaustion on a young soldier's upper lip, could hear him mutter to his neighbor, "—swear the mountain wasn't this tall yesterday—" And then the officer at the rear stopped.

He turned. He looked directly up at the ledge where four people crouched in plain view.

His eyes went through them. Through the wall. Through the mountain. For three long seconds he stared at nothing with the terrible blankness of a man whose country had been deleted from behind him, and then he shook himself, muttered an order, and followed his men up the path.

No one breathed until the sound of the boots had faded entirely.

"He saw us," Ash said. "But there wasn't anything left in him to know it."

"Yes," Imre said quietly. "That's what erasure does. It doesn't kill you. It hollows you until there's no room left for the truth to land." She stood, knees cracking, and looked up the mountain. Above them the switchbacks narrowed toward a skyline of broken towers — the outer ruins of the Spire complex, still holding, still stubbornly real. "Two hours, maybe three, if the path keeps. Let's spend them well."

* * *

The eighth switchback was where reality began to argue with itself.

Rafael felt it as vertigo first — a lurch in her inner ear, as though the mountain had tilted though her eyes insisted it hadn't. Then the light changed. The sun stood in the east, but shadows fell westward. A stream crossed the path twice, flowing in opposite directions both times. And ahead of them, the switchback they needed to climb simply... wasn't. There was a gap in the road, thirty feet of raw dissolving air, through which Rafael could see the same meadow they'd left an hour ago, growing the same flickering flowers.

Nadya unwrapped the master chart with shaking hands. "The lattice is failing unevenly. The road exists in patches now — wherever Sela's original anchoring was strongest." Her finger traced a line of ink that pulsed faintly, like a vein. "Here. Here. Here. We have to move between the real places and trust the gaps."

"Trust the gaps," Imre repeated. "You want us to jump off a mountain onto memory."

"I want us to walk on my ancestor's work," Rafael said. "It's held for two hundred years. It'll hold for ten more minutes."

She went first, because it was her inheritance and her burden and because Ash's hand was in hers and she would not ask the child to step into nothing before she did. The gap was worse than it looked. Crossing it was like walking through a doorway into a room where someone else was already mid-thought — the air resisted, thick with the pressure of things unmade, and for one horrible stride Rafael felt herself becoming optional, a figure in a painting someone was considering painting over.

Then her boot struck stone. Real stone, cold and certain, and the ninth switchback rose solidly before her, and she was Rafael Okafor again, whole and terrified and alive.

"Nadya next. Chart-hand first. Then Ash — I'll carry her."

"You'll do no such thing," Imre said. "Your arms are shaking. I'll take her."

"She trusts me—"

"She trusts all of us, which is the miracle of her, but I'm the strongest climber and the least important person on this mountain." Imre said it without self-pity, the way she said everything, and held out her arms for the child. "Come here, little ember. Old ward-keepers are made for carrying heavy things."

Ash hesitated — looked at Rafael, asked permission with her eyes. Rafael nodded, hating it and knowing it was right, and watched Imre lift the child onto her hip with the practiced ease of a woman who had once carried a daughter of her own, before. Before the disgrace. Before the border fell. Before whatever grief lived behind Imre Vashti's flat calm like a stone at the bottom of a well.

They crossed the gap in pairs. Nadya, clutching the chart, made a small wounded sound halfway through and arrived on the far side gray-faced but intact. Imre and Ash crossed last, and for a moment — one heartbeat, two — they hung in the unmade air, mother-shaped and child-shaped silhouettes against a meadow that existed yesterday, and Rafael's chest clenched around something she refused to name because naming it would slow her down.

Then stone. All of them. All together.

"Three more gaps," Nadya said, consulting the chart. "And then the summit stair."

"Then let's not waste the solid ground," Rafael said, and ran.

* * *

The tenth switchback was where the wardens caught them.

Not the column from below — those had faded somewhere behind, their footsteps simply ceasing, one man at a time, until the mountain was quiet. These were different. These had come down from the summit garrison, and they were fresher, angrier, and led by a woman in silver half-armor with the Chancellery's seal burned into her pauldron: a circle with a line struck through it. The sign of erasure.

"Stop!" the officer shouted, and her voice cracked strangely in the thinning air. "In the name of the Chancellor, you are trespassing in territory that does not exist!"

"Then arrest us for nothing," Imre called back, placing herself between the wardens and the others. "See how that goes in your report."

There were eight of them. Eight against four, on a narrow road, with a child. Rafael's hand found her knife; Nadya raised the heavy sighting-glass like a club; Ash pressed back against the rock wall, and the stone behind her began, very faintly, to smoke.

"No," Rafael said sharply. "Ash — no fire. Not yet. Save it."

The wardens charged.

What happened next, Rafael would remember forever in fragments, because the mountain was coming apart around them even as they fought. She remembered the first warden's blade ringing off Imre's ward-keeper's staff. She remembered Nadya swinging the glass with a battle cry that was really a scholar's shriek of indignation. She remembered the officer lunging for Ash — not for Rafael, not for the chart, but for the child, because the Chancellery had finally understood what Ash was and what she could do —

And she remembered Imre.

Imre moved the way water moves, the way wards move, without hesitation and without waste. She stepped between the officer's blade and the child, and she did not dodge. She took the stroke across her side, turning with it so the edge slid along her ribs instead of through them, and she used the grip of that terrible closeness to drive her staff into the officer's knee. Bone cracked. The officer went down screaming. And Imre stood over Ash, bleeding freely through her fingers, smiling with blood in her teeth.

"Run," she said. "All of you. Up. Now."

"We're not leaving you," Rafael snarled.

"You are absolutely leaving me, right up until I finish limping after you, which I intend to do, so don't make it sentimental." Imre parried another stroke, staggered, held. "I spent fifteen years disgraced for failing to keep the borders standing. Do you truly think I'll let you carry me to the top of the thing I failed? I'll climb on my own feet or not at all. Go!"

A warden's blade flashed toward Ash. Rafael got there first — not with skill, with fury, catching the man's wrist and putting her knife against his throat, close enough that he could feel the answer in the steel. He froze. His eyes, she saw suddenly, were full of tears.

"I don't want to be here," he whispered. "I don't remember why we came. I don't remember why any of — please. Please just go."

"Then stop fighting," Rafael said, "and forget us faster than you forget everything else."

She shoved him back. It shouldn't have worked. It worked, because the mountain was dissolving and the war was dissolving with it, and hatred needs a country to stand on. One by one the wardens faltered, looking at their blades, at their hands, at each other, as if seeing uniforms for the first time. The officer crawled backward down the path, cradling her knee, shouting orders no one obeyed.

"Go!" Imre bellowed again, and this time they went.

* * *

They gained the eleventh switchback with Imre leaning on Rafael's shoulder, her blood soaking warm through Rafael's coat. Above them, at last, the summit stair rose out of the ruin-line: a thousand steps cut into the Spire's root, each one carved with a word in a script older than the Okafors, older perhaps than writing. The words glowed faintly. Fainter than they should have. Fainter every minute.

Nadya stopped at the base of the stair and looked back the way they had come, and Rafael, following her gaze, saw the lowlands.

Or where the lowlands had been.

Hollowmere was still there — the camp, the fires, the river — but beyond it, at the far edge of the valley, three villages were simply gone. Not destroyed. Absent. The space where they'd been was a smooth pale nothing, like fog that had decided to be permanent, and Rafael knew with sick certainty that the people who had lived there were standing in the refugee camp right now, unable to say why they couldn't remember their own doorways.

"How long?" she asked.

Nadya consulted the chart, and her voice, when it came, was very quiet. "Hours. Perhaps fewer. The lattice is releasing everything it can't hold anymore, and what it releases unmake—" She stopped. Corrected herself. "What it lets go stops being held, and what isn't held was never there. The stars first. Then the borders. Then..." She touched the chart, gently, the way you touch a dying friend. "Then everything inside them."

"Then we climb," Rafael said.

She turned to Ash. The child was staring at the pale nothing where the villages had been, and tears were cutting clean lines through the ash-smudge on her face, and the air around her shimmered with heat she couldn't contain.

"Ash. Little ember. Look at me."

Amber eyes lifted. Ancient and young. Terrified.

"When we reach the top," Rafael said, "you're going to breathe fire for me. Real fire. The first new fire in two hundred years. Can you do that?"

Ash swallowed. Put her small warm hand flat against Rafael's sternum, over her hammering heart.

"You'll be the wick," the child said. "I'm only the match."

"I know," Rafael said. She had known since the campfire, since the confession, since the night she'd admitted she'd always meant to leave. She knew what the flame would cost and who would pay it, and she had chosen, and the choosing was the only thing on this dissolving mountain that felt completely, unshakably real. "So let's go strike the light."

Imre, gray-lipped, bleeding, laughed — a short, cracked, magnificent sound. "Look at her. An Okafor, giving orders on the Okafor road. Sela's laughing in her grave, or cheering. With that family it was always hard to tell."

They climbed.

Step by step by step, past words that glowed and dimmed, past the last warden who stood aside and saluted them with tears freezing on his face, past the final gap where the road ended and only will remained. Rafael carried Imre's weight on one shoulder and Ash's hand in her other grip and the whole impossible weight of a dying country in her chest, and she did not stop, and she did not look away, and she did not let go.

At the top of the stair stood a door of black iron banded with gold, and on the door, in fresh-carved letters that had not been there two hundred years ago, was a name:

**SELA OKAFOR — KEEPER OF THE SPIRE — THE LAST WHO HOLDS**

Below it, a second line, newly cut, waiting empty.

Rafael put her palm against the cold iron.

The door swung open onto firelight — dim, guttering, dying, but firelight still — and a voice like dry paper spoke from within.

"So. The heir comes home at last. Come in, child. We have so little time, and so much to give you."
