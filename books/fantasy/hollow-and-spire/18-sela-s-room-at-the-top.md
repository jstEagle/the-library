## 18. Sela's Room at the Top

The last switchback ended at a door.

Not a grand one — that was the first thing Rafael noticed, and it undid her more than anything else on the climb. After the dissolving stairways, after the switchbacks where the mountain forgot its own stone and they had walked on memory and stubbornness, she had expected a threshold worthy of all this: bronze and dragonbone, wards blazing in every color fire could be. Instead there was a plain door of grey wood, iron-banded, scarred by weather, exactly like a hundred doors in a hundred villages she had mapped and never thought about twice.

Someone had lived here. Someone had climbed these stairs with aching knees and opened this ordinary door every morning for years.

Imre leaned against the wall beside it, one arm pressed to her ribs where the warden's blade had caught her. The wound was bound with strips of Nadya's cloak, and the binding was already dark. "Go," Imre said, before Rafael could speak. "We'll hold the stair."

"The Chancellery—"

"Is climbing slower than we did, because their soldiers keep forgetting why they're here." Imre's mouth twisted — not quite a smile, but near kin to one. "Every few minutes another one stops mid-step and looks at his hands like he can't remember what they're for. The erasure is eating them from the edges in. We have time. Not much. Go."

Ash stood close against Rafael's side, small hand fisted in her coat hem. The child's skin had gone ashen since the ridge, since the Guttering, since the Spire's fire had dimmed to something you could watch die. Her eyes were enormous and gold-ringed and fixed on the door with an expression Rafael had learned to read across a hundred miles of broken country: hunger and terror in equal measure.

"It's warm," Ash whispered. "Behind that door. It's almost out, but it's *warm*."

Rafael crouched, took the child's face in both hands. Ash's cheek was fever-hot against her palms. "I'm going in. I'm coming back out. Whatever happens in there, whatever I see — I'm coming back out. Do you believe me?"

Ash considered this with the terrible seriousness she brought to everything. Then she nodded once, and let go of Rafael's coat, and stepped back beside Imre, who put a steadying hand on her shoulder.

Rafael stood. Her legs shook — from the climb, from the thin air, from the way the world at this altitude came apart at the seams like wet paper. She put her hand on the iron latch. It was warm too. Everything here was warm, the way a body is warm when the warmth is leaving it.

She opened the door and went into her inheritance.

* * *

The room at the top of the Spire was not large.

It was a workroom. That was the second undoing. A desk under a window whose glass had long since blown out, the frame furred with frost. Shelves sagging under ledgers and instrument cases. A cot with a blanket worn thin at the fold. An inkwell gone to dry black crust. Charts pinned to every wall, layered so deep that the oldest showed only as frayed corners beneath the newest, like geological strata made of paper.

And in the center of the floor, a hearth.

No — not a hearth. Rafael had no word for it at first. It was a ring of black stone set flush with the floorboards, perhaps four feet across, and in its center burned a flame the size of a candle's. It should have been nothing. It was everything. When she looked at it directly, the room fell away — the walls, the shelves, the mountain itself — and she saw instead a lattice vast as a night sky, threads of fire running out from this single guttering point into darkness in every direction, holding up stars like a net holds up fruit.

She looked away, gasping. The room returned. The flame burned on, small and stubborn, and she understood suddenly why Ash screamed at ridgelines. To feel that thing dying inside your own chest —

"You're taller than I expected."

Rafael spun, hand going to the knife at her belt before her mind caught up with the impossibility of the voice.

A woman sat at the desk.

She was old — or she was shaped like an old woman, which was not the same thing. Her hair was white and cropped short, practical, the hair of someone who had never wasted a morning on vanity. She wore a surveyor's coat, patched at the elbows, ink-stained at the cuffs. Her hands were folded on the desk among scattered drafting tools, and they were translucent. Light from the window passed through them and printed faint golden shapes on the wood below.

Sela Okafor. Rafael knew it the way she knew north — not by evidence but by orientation. The jawline from the miniature portrait in the family Bible. The eyes, gray-green, that Rafael shaved around every morning in whatever water the road provided.

"You're dead," Rafael said. "You've been dead for two hundred years."

"Yes." The echo said it without drama, the way you'd confirm the weather. "What's left of me is what the fire kept. Which is less every year, now. Sit down, child. You look like the mountain's been chewing you."

"I don't—" Rafael's voice cracked; she dragged it back into line. "I don't have time. There are soldiers on the stair, and the fire is dying, and my friends are bleeding, and I need to know how to relight it. That's why I came. Tell me how, and I'll do it, and you can go back to being dead."

Something moved through the old woman's face — too quick to name. Sorrow, maybe. Or relief. "Straight to it. You map-makers. My mother was the same. No pleasantries, straight to the contour lines." She unfolded her hands. "Sit anyway. What I have to show you takes three minutes, and if you spend those three minutes standing you'll fall down, and then it'll take five."

Rafael sat. The chair was hard and real and the most solid thing she'd touched in days. She hated how much good it did her.

* * *

"There is a way out," Sela said.

The words landed in Rafael's chest like a dropped stone.

"Behind the eastern shelf. I built it myself, the year after I took the keeping, because I was young and frightened and I wanted insurance." The echo rose, translucent hands smoothing her coat, and crossed to the shelving. Her fingers didn't quite touch the wood; the shelf swung open anyway, revealing a narrow passage beyond, and at the end of the passage —

Light. Ordinary, clean, daylight light, the kind that falls through windows onto kitchen tables. And air that smelled of rain on dust, of some lowland town, of anywhere that wasn't here.

"The Hollow Door," Rafael breathed. "It's real. It's *here*."

"It was always here. Every keeper has known, and every keeper has kept it secret, because a door out of an erased country is worth more than gold and less than nothing if the wrong people learn of it." Sela turned back. The light from the passage gilded her edges and made her look thinner, more ghostly, a figure drawn in fading ink. "It opens once per keeper. Once, and only once, and only while the fire still lives — barely. Tonight it opens for the last time. Tomorrow, whether you light the flame or not, it closes forever, because there will be nothing left on either side of it worth walking between."

Rafael couldn't stop looking at the light. Rain on dust. A market street. Someone selling roasted chestnuts, maybe. Somewhere with a customs office that would stamp her papers and a sky full of stars that had never heard of Thornfell.

"Where does it come out?" she asked, and hated herself for asking.

"Wherever you need it to. That's the cruelty of it. It comes out wherever your heart says home is — and for me, that was a village on the coast that hasn't existed for a century." Sela's voice stayed level, but something underneath it trembled like a plucked string. "For you? I'd guess somewhere south. Warm. A city with good archives. You've been planning to leave since you were old enough to hold a pen. Don't look shocked — it's in how you draw. Your coastlines always face away from the mountains."

Rafael stood up. The chair scraped. "I came here to relight the fire."

"You came here because three people you love are standing between you and the soldiers, and you couldn't live with yourself if you ran while they watched." Sela's gray-green eyes held hers, and there was no accusation in them at all, which was infinitely worse. "That's not a condemnation, child. That's just the truth, and you've been carrying it since the ford, and you'll carry it whether you walk through that Door or stay. So let's be honest with each other, you and I, in the three minutes we have. Walk through it, and you live. You live, and you remember, and someday — thirty years from now, fifty — you'll be an old woman in a warm city with good archives, and you'll take out your maps of a country that isn't there, and you'll tell yourself you did the only sane thing. And some nights you won't believe it. Some nights you'll hear a child's voice asking if the fire is out yet, and you'll know the answer, and you'll know you could have changed it, and the Door will be closed, and it will be too late forever."

"Stop."

"And if you stay—" 

"*Stop.*"

The word came out ragged. Rafael pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes. Behind her eyelids: Ash's face lit gold by a campfire, saying *I just want somewhere that's real*. Imre on the prison cot, teaching ward-theory to her own interrogator with her wrists chained, dignity like a blade. Nadya's voice shaking with wonder: *they're not destroyed, Rafael, they're held, we can give them back.*

And behind all of it, older, quieter: her own voice, weeks ago, in a lowland tavern, telling a smuggler captain *this is the last run, after this I'm done with Thornfell forever.* She had meant it. She had been so sure she meant it.

"When you took the keeping," Rafael said slowly, lowering her hand, "did you want it?"

"No." Sela smiled, and the smile was tired and fierce and very like the one Rafael found in her own mirror on bad mornings. "My brother was supposed to inherit. He died of fever at nineteen, and I inherited a mountain and a fire and a life sentence, and I raged about it for a decade. And then one winter the wards flickered over Vess, and a ferry went down with forty souls aboard because the crossing-ward failed, and I understood what I actually held. Not a burden. A *hand on the wheel*. There were forty people on that ferry who would never know my name, and I kept them alive anyway, because that was the job, and the job was mine." She gestured at the guttering flame. "Two hundred years of keepers after me, same story. None of us chose it. All of us chose it. Both things are true, and the Door exists precisely because both things are true — because a keeper who cannot leave is a prisoner, but a keeper who *could* leave and stays is something else entirely."

"What?"

"Free," Sela said simply. "The only free people in any country are the ones who could walk away and don't."

The light from the Hollow Door lay across the floorboards between them, warm and golden and patient. Rain on dust. Chestnuts. Archives. Life.

Rafael looked at it for a long moment. Then she looked at the flame.

Small as a candle. Dying as a candle dies. And threaded out from it, invisible now unless she squinted sideways at reality, the whole burning lattice of a country — every ward-stone in every pass, every border-marker, every star held safe in its net, every village, every ferry-crossing, every child asleep under a roof that existed because someone sat in this cold room year after year and fed the fire with her bloodline.

Ash was downstairs. Ash, who smelled of smoke and remembered the fire, who trusted her completely, who wanted one real place in all the world.

"They're going to breach the walls," Rafael said. "The Chancellery. Before I can do whatever it is I'm supposed to do."

"Probably." Sela settled back into her chair, and even sitting she somehow managed to look like she was rolling up her sleeves. "They've been trying for two hundred years. They've never managed it while a keeper stood in this room. But then, none of them ever had to fight a keeper with your particular talents." The gray-green eyes glinted. "Tell me, cartographer. How well do you know this mountain?"

Rafael blinked. "Better than anyone alive. I've been mapping it since I was nine."

"Mm. And the Chancellery officers climbing your stair — do they know it at all?"

"They can't even see the passes. Their maps show nothing. That's the whole point of the erasure."

Sela smiled, slow and sharp, and for a moment the translucence seemed to burn away and she was solid, vivid, alive — a woman with ink on her cuffs and a war in her eyes.

"Then let's make them regret knocking," she said, "and then, child — let me teach you how to carry fire in your blood."

Below them, faint through the floor, came the first crack of the Chancellery's siege-ladders against the outer wall — and Ash's voice, high and clear, crying Rafael's name.
