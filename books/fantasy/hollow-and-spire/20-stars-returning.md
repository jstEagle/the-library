## 20. Stars, Returning

Nadya Solenne had spent eleven years in a cell for the crime of loving the sky correctly, and in all that time she had never once stopped calculating. She calculated now — because calculation was how she prayed, and because if she did not put numbers between herself and what was about to happen, she would weep, and there was no time for weeping.

The Spire's summit chamber was a bowl of old gold and older shadow. Rafael Okafor stood at its center with her arms open and her head bowed like a woman stepping into a river whose depth no one had ever sounded. The flame of Thornfell — the last guttering coal of it, small as a candle-end, stubborn as a heartbeat — hung in the air before her, waiting.

Behind them, down through the mountain's bones, came the sound of the Chancellery's final assault. Iron on stone. Boots. The rhythmic thud of a ram against doors that had held for three hundred years and were, by every law Nadya knew, already failing.

"Alignment window," she said, and her voice did not shake, which surprised her. "Forty seconds. Maybe less. When the lattice opens, it opens *all* the way, Rafael. There is no partial version of this."

"I know." Rafael did not look back. Her voice was very quiet and very steady, the voice of someone who had finished arguing with herself. "Do it."

Nadya pressed her palms to the master chart.

Sela Okafor had drawn it in ink that was not entirely ink — Nadya had established that much in the prison years, working from memory, from confiscated scraps, from the single glimpse of an original page a guard had used to wrap his supper. The lines were roads only if you were a fool or a cartographer; they were a lattice if you understood that a country could be a sentence spoken in fire, and that someone had to keep speaking it.

She began the sequence she had derived across eleven years of chalk dust and stolen starlight: anchor-points first, then the resonant pairs, then the release-notation that Sela had hidden in the chart's border like a signature folded into a letter.

The chamber answered.

Not with light — not yet. With *tension*. Every ward-line in the lattice woke at once, and Nadya felt them the way a musician feels a string tuned past comfort, the whole country drawn tight as a bow. Below them Ash screamed — not in pain, Nadya realized, but in recognition, the way a child screams when a song she has known since before memory finally begins again after years of silence.

"Thirty seconds," Nadya said. "Rafael. Whatever happens—"

"Whatever happens," Rafael agreed, "I stay."

And she reached into the flame.

* * *

Later — much later, when people asked her, and they always asked — Nadya would try to describe what happened next, and she would fail, and then she would try mathematics instead, and that would fail too, and eventually she would settle for telling the truth, which was:

It was like watching a prisoner count.

For three hundred years the stars of Thornfell's sky had been held inside the lattice — not destroyed, never destroyed, the Chancellery had simply needed them *somewhere*, gathered up like coins into a purse so that the country they anchored could be declared empty. And now the purse opened.

Nadya saw it first as pressure. The lattice, which had been taut, went slack — slack the way a bowstring goes slack when the arrow flies — and everything it had been holding poured upward through the Spire like water finding its level.

Then she saw it as light.

They came out of the mountain in rivers. Not one star at a time but constellations entire, whole seasons of sky, decades of nights that the people of Thornfell had been robbed of without ever being told they'd been robbed — the Shepherd and his Crook, the Lantern, the Long Serpent that the lowlanders called the River and the highlanders called the Road Home. They tore free of the summit like sparks off a struck flint, streaked up through dissolving cloud, and scattered.

Nadya threw back her head and counted them home.

"The Lantern," she whispered, watching it blaze into position over the western peaks, exactly where her charts said it had been before the Erasure, exactly where it had *always* been. "The Crook. The Sisters — both of them, both, oh, *both*—" Her voice cracked on the second Sister, because she had proven mathematically that the second Sister existed when she was nineteen years old and the Chancellery had taken her papers, her post, and finally her freedom for it, and here it was, burning, real, indifferent to every argument ever made against it.

Stars fell upward. It should not have been possible. It was happening anyway, and Nadya laughed — a wild, undignified sound, the laugh of a woman watching her life's work validate itself in fire.

Below, through the great broken windows of the summit stair, she saw the mountains remember themselves.

That was the only word for it. The edges of Thornfell — the fading places, the villages gone soft as breath on glass, the passes that dissolved when you looked at them too long — snapped back into definition. She watched a ridge line sharpen from mist to granite. She watched a valley fill itself in, tree by tree, roof by roof, smoke rising from chimneys that had been smoke-shaped absences an hour ago. The borders blazed — she could see them from the summit, a lattice of gold fire running along the mountain walls like veins of ore catching sunlight, wards renewing themselves along lines Sela Okafor had drawn and Rafael Okafor had paid for.

And at the center of it all, Rafael stood in a column of light with her eyes closed and tears cutting clean tracks through the ash on her face, and the flame passed through her, and she did not burn.

Mostly.

* * *

"There," Nadya breathed. "There, the River — the whole of it, look, it runs wall to wall—" She was pointing at nothing, at everything, at the sky, and she did not care. Eleven years in a cell for insisting the stars were held rather than destroyed, and here was the proof written across heaven in letters of fire, and no one would ever be able to call her mad again, and she found, to her astonishment, that vindication tasted almost exactly like grief.

Because Rafael was on her knees now, and the flame was settling into the Spire's heart behind her, and she was smiling, which was somehow worse than screaming.

Ash reached her first. The child crossed the chamber like a thrown spear, dropped beside her, and pressed both small hands to Rafael's face — hands that smelled of smoke and were warm as banked coals. Nadya heard her say, in the fierce imperious tone of a six-year-old who has decided reality will obey her: "*Breathe.* You have to breathe, that's how it works, I remember, you have to keep breathing or it thinks you're done—"

Rafael breathed.

And Imre Vashti — bleeding from a wound along her ribs, leaning on her ward-staff like a crutch, having held the stair alone against the Chancellery's vanguard while the sky came apart above her — looked up through the shattered windows at the returning stars, and this woman who had not wept through disgrace or exile or capture or a blade in her side sat down abruptly on the floor of Sela Okafor's chamber and wept like a girl.

Nadya understood. She was doing the arithmetic herself, even now, even mid-wonder, because arithmetic was how she prayed: *three hundred years of held stars, released in under a minute. Nine thousand nights given back. A country un-erased in the time it takes to boil water.*

The impossible, made ordinary again. That was the miracle's cruelest trick and its kindest — that within an hour, people would look up, see the Lantern burning over the western peaks, and simply say *there it is*, the way their great-grandparents had said it, the way everyone had said it before the Chancellery decided the sky was a ledger item to be seized.

* * *

The Chancellery officers forgot why they came.

Nadya witnessed it from the summit windows, and it was the strangest thing she saw that night, stranger than stars falling upward, stranger than mountains knitting themselves back into being — because it was small, and human, and almost pitiable.

They had breached the outer wall. Two dozen of them, in grey lacquered armor, wading up the switchbacks with suppression-pikes and writs of dissolution, and then the borders had blazed and the lattice had closed around Thornfell like a fist closing around a stolen thing, and the wards had reached out and touched their minds.

They stopped mid-stride. All of them. A whole army standing still on a mountainside in the dark, looking around themselves with the blank, blinking confusion of men waking in a room they don't recognize.

One of them — a captain, by his insignia, a broad man with a pike still raised — turned slowly in a full circle, taking in the blazing ward-lines, the solid granite, the stars overhead. His visor came up. Nadya saw his face do something complicated: fear, then wonder, then a kind of embarrassed settling, the expression of a man trying to remember why he had climbed a mountain at midnight and finding, wherever the reason had been stored, only smooth empty stone.

"Why are we here?" he asked his lieutenant, loudly enough to carry up the switchbacks.

The lieutenant looked equally lost. "Border survey," he guessed. "Reconnaissance?"

"It's cold," the captain said, with the aggrieved tone of a man who has located the only fact available to him. "Why is it always so cold in these mountains. Sound the withdrawal. We'll file a report."

"A report saying what, sir?"

The captain looked up at the sky — at the Long Serpent, newly returned, running bright and unmistakable from horizon to horizon — and frowned, and Nadya watched him reach for the official position, the doctrine, the *Thornfell does not exist*, and watch it slide away from him like wet rope.

"A report saying," he decided slowly, "that the passes are impassable this season. Which they are." He shivered. "Always have been."

They withdrew in good order, an army that had marched to erase a country and left convinced they'd merely failed to climb it. By morning, Nadya knew, the Chancellery's own records would show nothing — the wards had seen to that, folding the invasion into the same erasure the Chancellery had once inflicted, turning the weapon around with a bureaucrat's perfect indifference. Somewhere in a lowland archive, a clerk would find a requisition for two dozen sets of winter kit with no matching mission, and would shrug, and would file it.

Imre watched them go from the summit wall, her hand pressed to her bandaged ribs, and wore the expression of a woman swallowing something bitter.

"They won't stop," she said quietly. "Not forever. The wards made them forget *this*. They didn't make them forget wanting Thornfell gone. That want lives in the lowlands, in ledgers, in men who've never seen these mountains. We've bought time, not peace."

"Time is what I do," Nadya said.

Imre huffed — half laugh, half cough. "Eleven years in a cell and you come out arrogant."

"I came out *correct*," Nadya said. "The arrogance was always there. It just has evidence now."

Above them, the sky went on giving back what had been taken, star by star by star, and neither woman looked away long enough to finish the argument.

* * *

Dawn found the four of them on the summit terrace, wrapped in every blanket the old keeper's stores could provide, drinking tea brewed over Ash's cupped hands because the child had discovered, delightedly, that she could make water boil by holding it and thinking cross thoughts.

Nadya had her instruments out. Of course she had her instruments out. She had not properly observed the sky in eleven years, and there were positions to confirm, magnitudes to record, a lifetime of theory to check against a sky that had finally consented to exist. Her fingers were numb and her notes were smeared and she had never been happier in her life.

"The precession holds," she reported, mostly to herself. "Everything's where it was. Three centuries of storage and the lattice didn't lose so much as an arcminute. Sela Okafor built better than the Chancellery ever dreamed." She paused, quill hovering. "Better than *we* dreamed. I gave the lattice credit for precision. It turns out it also has taste. Look where it put the Lantern tonight — dead center over Vess. Over the customs house." She smiled thinly. "I choose to believe that's intentional."

"It's intentional," Ash said solemnly, mouth full of biscuit. "I helped."

"You helped."

"I pushed."

Nadya looked at Rafael, expecting the cartographer to share some dry remark, and found her staring east instead, at the light coming up over the Teeth, at a country emerging from the dark in every direction as far as the eye could see — solid, specific, *mapped* at last by the oldest instrument there was.

"You're cataloguing it," Nadya said gently. "Aren't you. Right now. You're sitting there drawing it in your head."

Rafael blinked, caught. "Old habit."

"No," Nadya said. "New habit. The old habit was drawing ways *out*. I've read your charts, Rafael. Every line ran toward a border." She nodded east, at the sunrise filling the valleys, at smoke rising honest and ordinary from a hundred remembered chimneys. "What are you drawing now?"

Rafael was quiet for a long moment. When she answered, her voice was rough, and she did not take her eyes off the light.

"A survey," she said. "The first one anyone's ever done of a country that chose to exist." She pulled her satchel into her lap — scorched, strap-bitten, the same satchel that had rewritten itself the night the stars went out — and drew out fresh paper, and Nadya saw that the first line was already down: not a road, not a border, but a valley, rendered in the patient loving strokes of a woman drawing a place she intended to learn by heart.

Ash leaned against her shoulder. Imre began, quietly, to sketch ward-anchor points in the margin, because a rebuilt country would need rebuilt wards and Imre Vashti had a school to found. Nadya opened a fresh page of her own logbook and wrote, beneath the night's observations, the heading she had waited eleven years to write:

*Sky of Thornfell. First clear survey. Conditions: excellent.*

Above them, the last of the held stars — one small latecomer, lagging near the horizon — hesitated, flickered, and slid home into its ancient place.

Nadya checked it against her charts, found it exact, and wrote down its name.

Then she looked up, and saw, far to the south beyond the new-blazing borders, a door-shaped absence standing open in the dawn mist — the Hollow Door, swinging wide one final time, showing a road going out into a world that had forgotten them.

It stood open for a long moment.

Then, softly, like a book closing, it shut — and the mist rolled over the place where it had been, and Thornfell's morning went on being ordinary, and no one needed it at all.
