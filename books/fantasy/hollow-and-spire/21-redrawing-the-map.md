## 21. Redrawing the Map

The first morning of the new survey, Rafael woke before dawn out of habit and lay in her narrow bed at the Spire's foot, listening to a sound she had not heard in six weeks: birdsong. Ordinary, unremarkable birdsong, thrushes arguing over the eaves of the keeper's house, and it made her throat tighten until she had to sit up and breathe through it.

The stars were still there. She checked every morning. She suspected she would check every morning for the rest of her life — stepping out into the blue-grey cold before the sun cleared the eastern ridge, tipping her head back, counting. Last night there had been all of them. The night before, all of them again. Nadya said the lattice was stable now, that the release had been total, that Rafael could stop holding her breath. Nadya said this with the gentle exasperation of an astronomer explaining to a child why the moon would keep rising. Rafael kept counting anyway.

She dressed, shouldered her satchel — the old one, its strap mended twice now — and took down the map she'd pinned to the wall above her desk. It was the one she'd drawn in the weeks after the relighting, working by lamplight while Ash slept curled on her floor like a cat too proud to admit she wanted company. It showed Thornfell entire: the Teeth, the Hollow Road's entrance marked with a small careful symbol Sela had used and Rafael had adopted, the Spire at the center with its radiating lattice inked in gold leaf Imre had produced from some ward-keeper's cache. It was the truest map Rafael had ever made, and it was already wrong.

Not wrong the way maps usually went wrong — not outdated, not inaccurate. Wrong because Thornfell was changing. The country was remembering itself. Rivers that had run dry under the erasure were running again along courses no chart had recorded, and villages dissolved to mist during the Guttering were reassembling themselves out of nothing anyone could name, sometimes in slightly different places than they'd stood before. The land was healing crooked, the way bones did, and someone needed to walk every mile of it and write down what it had become.

That someone, as far as Rafael could tell, was her.

She rolled the master survey blank into her satchel, tucked three pencils, two pens, and the ink-horn behind her ear where Ash said it made her look like a very serious bird, and went downstairs to see whether the child had eaten breakfast or simply declared breakfast a concept beneath her dignity.

* * *

Vess had changed too. That was where the survey began — at the lowland town where Rafael had learned, six weeks and a lifetime ago, that her country officially did not exist. The customs house still stood at the edge of the market square, but its shutters hung open and its officer's desk was gone, replaced by a fruit stall whose owner cheerfully admitted he had no idea who had worked there before. The Chancellery's men had woken one morning unable to say why they'd come, then unable to say what they'd come for, then — most of them — simply gone home, trailing confusion like smoke. A few had stayed. One of them ran the ferry now, and waved at Rafael with the uncomplicated friendliness of a man who remembered being her enemy only the way you remember a fever.

"Cartographer!" he called across the water. "Going up again?"

"Going up," Rafael said. "Third pass of the valley this month."

"You'll wear out those boots."

"I'll wear out three more pairs first." She stepped onto the ferry, and the boat rocked under her, and the water was real water, cold and brown and smelling of silt, and she gripped the rail and let herself feel it.

The strangeness of it never quite left her: that she could walk these roads openly now. For years she had moved through Thornfell like a smuggler in her own homeland, keeping to the hollows, avoiding eyes, carrying maps she'd been told were lies. Now farmers waved to her from their fields. Now innkeepers asked her to mark their villages properly, with the right names spelled right, because names mattered now — names were load-bearing, in a country rebuilt out of memory. In Marrowdale they'd argued for two days about whether the old bridge had been called the Kettle Bridge or the Kettleford Bridge, and Rafael had settled it by finding the bridge, which bore a carved keystone reading neither, and adding a third option to the record, which pleased absolutely no one and struck her as the most honest thing she'd done all year.

She surveyed the way she'd always surveyed, because craft didn't change just because its purpose did. Pace-counting, triangulation off the Spire's visible flame-tipped crown, notes in her cramped shorthand in the margins: *stream here, new; alder stands, healthy; village of Hesk re-formed at 200 paces east of former site — ask about that.* But something underneath the method had shifted, and she noticed it most in the evenings, when she camped alone under the returned stars and drew the day's work by firelight.

Before, mapping had been escape. Every line she drew was a line leading out — here is the pass, here is the ford, here is how you leave. Even when she'd told herself she was serving Thornfell, running goods through the passes, she had been memorizing exits. Her whole life had been a study of doors.

Now she drew a stream because the stream existed and deserved to be known. She measured a hill because no one had ever measured it, and the hill had waited a long time, and it seemed rude to keep it waiting longer. There was no exit anywhere in her work anymore. There was only the country itself, unfolding mile by mile, stubborn and scarred and real, and Rafael Okafor walking through it with ink on her fingers and her ancestor's symbols drying on fresh vellum, feeling — and this was the part that still startled her, that she had to set her pencil down and look at sometimes, like a strange specimen — feeling like she was home.

On the ninth day of the valley survey she climbed the ridge above Hesk at dusk and found the mist rising off the re-formed village in long silver banners, and below her the lamplights coming on one by one, and somewhere a fiddle, badly played, joyfully played. She sat down on the cold grass, opened the ledger she carried in the inner pocket of her coat — Sela's ledger, the ledger of keepers, rescued from the summit chamber — and looked for a long time at the last entry in her ancestor's hand.

*Sela Okafor, keeper. The fire is my blood and my blood is the fire. Let whoever reads this forgive me, if forgiveness helps them carry it.*

Rafael had read that line perhaps forty times since the relighting. She had been angry at it, early on — furious, in fact, at an ancestor dead two hundred years, for binding a bloodline to a burning tower without asking anyone's permission. She had raged at Sela in the privacy of her own head, pacing the keeper's house, telling her exactly what she thought of inheritance-by-ambush. And then, somewhere around the third week, mid-rant, standing at the window watching Ash chase Imre around the yard with a wooden practice wand, she had stopped, because she had realized that she wasn't angry anymore. She was grateful. Not for the ambush — never for the ambush — but for the country. For the fiddle music drifting up from Hesk. For the stars she counted every morning like a miser counting coins.

She uncapped her pen.

Beneath Sela's line, in her own hand, steadier than she expected, she wrote:

*Rafael Okafor, keeper. I did not choose the fire, but I choose it now. Let this country know itself, and let me be the one who draws it.*

She blotted the page, closed the ledger, and held it against her chest for a moment in the dark, while the mist came up the ridge and the fiddle played on below, and if anyone had seen her they might have thought she was praying, and they wouldn't have been entirely wrong.

* * *

The ward-school reopened on the first day of autumn, in the old granary at the foot of the Spire, because Imre Vashti declared that a country with a living lattice and no trained keepers was "a lamp left lit in a house full of children," and nobody argued with Imre when she used that tone.

Rafael came down from the survey to see the first class, and found the granary transformed: benches hewn from salvaged timber, chalkboards that were actually sheets of slate pried from a ruined chapel roof, and along one whole wall, mounted under glass, the original charts — Sela's charts, the smuggler roads that had never been roads, displayed now as the national treasures they had always secretly been. Imre stood before them in a clean shirt and her old ward-keeper's badge, restored to her by acclamation of exactly four people and accepted by her with a gruffness that fooled no one, and she was lecturing.

"—and so the anchor-point doesn't hold the ward the way a nail holds a picture. It holds it the way a root holds a tree. If you think of wards as things you *build*, you'll build them badly and they'll fall down. If you think of them as things you *grow*—" she caught sight of Rafael in the doorway and did not pause, but her voice warmed half a degree, which for Imre was a standing ovation, "—then you'll understand why the Okafors bound theirs to a bloodline. Sit down, cartographer, you're blocking the light."

There were eleven students. Some were refugees from the erased villages, grown men and women learning at forty what they should have learned at ten. Two were Chancellery-born, children of the officers who had stayed, sent by parents who wanted them to belong to the country they'd nearly unmade. And in the front row, spine straight, chin high, sitting with the ferocious attentiveness of someone determined to be the best at this or die, sat Ash.

Ash had grown in the weeks since the relighting. Not taller, exactly — though she was growing, eating like a small furnace, as Imre put it — but *more*. The ember-glow beneath her skin had steadied from a flicker to a banked warmth, visible now only when she was excited or angry, which at school was approximately always. When she breathed deeply, which she did before answering questions, a faint smell of woodsmoke filled the front row. The other students had stopped noticing. That, more than anything, told Rafael the country was healing: a half-dragon child breathing smoke in a classroom, and nobody blinking.

Rafael took a bench at the back and watched Imre teach. Watched her draw the lattice on the slate in quick confident strokes, watched her call on the farmwife from Hesk and wait with genuine patience while the woman worked through the answer, watched her catch Ash's hand before the girl could scorch a bench-slat in her frustration and say, quietly, so only Ash heard, something that made the child laugh and settle. Guilt had driven Imre to break Rafael out of a Vess cell. Love, Rafael thought, was driving her to this — to rebuilding, brick by brick and lesson by lesson, the institution whose failure she had spent ten years blaming herself for. She wore the guilt differently now. Lighter. Like a tool instead of a wound.

After the lesson, the students filed out chattering, and Ash detoured to fling herself against Rafael's middle hard enough to stagger her.

"You came! Did you see? I got the resonance question right and Marta got it wrong and I *didn't even gloat*."

"A heroic restraint. I'm marking it in the survey."

"Are you staying for supper? Imre's making the thing with the dumplings."

"The thing with the dumplings. How can I refuse."

They walked up to the keeper's house together in the long gold light, Ash skipping ahead and then circling back and then skipping ahead again, the way she did, orbiting like something that couldn't decide between independence and closeness and had solved the problem by alternating rapidly. At the door she paused.

"Rafa. Can I ask a survey question?"

"Survey questions are my favorite kind."

"When you draw the country — all of it, the whole thing — will there be a drawing of us? Me and Imre and Nadya? On the map?"

Rafael crouched to her height. Behind the child's shoulder the Spire rose against the darkening sky, its crown burning steady and gold, and beyond it the first stars were coming out, and Rafael did not count them this time. She trusted them.

"Maps show where things are," she said. "And you're here. So yes. Right at the center, where the fire is."

Ash considered this with the gravity she gave all matters of import, nodded once, and went inside to demand dumplings.

Supper was loud. Nadya arrived from her observatory halfway up the Spire's flank with star-charts under her arms and news she could barely keep civil about a meteor shower due in three weeks, the first Thornfell sky had seen in generations, and Imre told her if she brought instruments to the table again she'd eat outside, and Nadya said fine, she'd eaten outside for worse reasons, and Ash demanded to know what a meteor shower smelled like, and the noise of it filled the little house the way heat fills a room, and Rafael sat in the middle of it with dumplings going cold on her plate because she was too busy looking at all of them to eat.

Later, when the others had gone — Nadya back up her stairs, Ash asleep in the loft, Imre to the spare room she'd claimed and furnished with the particular permanence of someone who intended to stay — Rafael sat at her desk and unrolled the survey blank and began the evening's drawing. The valley of Hesk. The new river. The bridge with three names. Her pencil moved in the quiet, and the candle flame leaned when the draft came under the door, and on the shelf above her desk the ledger of keepers sat closed, two names in it now instead of one.

Outside, over the Spire, the stars burned in their thousands, patient and certain, and Rafael Okafor drew her country — not the way out of it, never again the way out, but the whole deep truth of it, mile by mile, line by line, home.
