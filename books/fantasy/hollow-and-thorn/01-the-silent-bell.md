## 1. The Silent Bell

There are a hundred ways to tell when a god has stopped listening, and I have tested most of them.

The candle still lights. That's the first lie of it. You strike the flint, the wick catches, and for one foolish heartbeat you think: *well, there's proof.* But candles light for anyone. Candles lit in my mother's kitchen, and she was a drunk with a heart of tin. A god who answers through candlelight is a god who answers through nothing at all.

So I tried the other signs. The bell at the shrine — a small thing, green with age, hung on a crooked post where the smugglers' path bends above the Greywater ford. Ring it three times, the old texts say, and the Vessel of the Quiet Hand will hear you. I rang it that morning, as I had rung it every morning for six years, and the sound went out over the valley like a coin dropped down a well.

No answer. The hundredth time, by my count. I keep count the way other mercenaries keep tally of kills or coins. It's a smaller number than either, and it means less, but it's mine.

"Still talking to nobody?" said Doss Halloway behind me.

I didn't turn around. Doss is the kind of man who reads your face like a ledger and always finds an entry he can use against you. "Talking to somebody," I said. "He's just not talking back."

"That's called praying to a rock."

"That's called *praying*, Doss. You wouldn't know. Your gods are all coin-shaped."

He laughed at that, because it was true, and men like Doss only laugh at truths they've already made peace with. He was loading the mules behind me, checking the cinches twice the way he does when the cargo matters more than usual. This run was silk and saffron out of the low country, worth more than the whole convoy's lives put together, which is why they'd hired me. Not for my charm. For the paths.

That's what I am, if we're being honest about it, and I promised myself this year I'd be honest at least once a season. Mara Brightwater, sworn of the Order of the Quiet Hand, guide of smugglers, mercenary of no particular ambition. There are eleven passes through the Thornfell mountains that appear on no map, and I know nine of them, and the two I don't know have probably eaten better guides than me.

Thornfell itself is a strange country to be born into, though most people here never think about it, which is the strangest part. We're a country the way a scar is a country. Somewhere back — four generations, five? — something happened, some erasure so thorough that even our grandparents' grandparents couldn't say what was lost. Our borders are mountains and our history is fog. Ask a Thornfell elder what our nation was before the crown absorbed us and they'll tell you about their grandmother's stew recipe. Ask them what the old capital was called and they'll look at you like you asked the name of a cloud.

You learn not to ask. That's the whole culture, really. Not asking.

But the god — the Quiet Hand, the Vessel, whatever you want to call him — the god predates the fog. My order is older than any border, older than the crown's oldest claim. And for six years he has been silent, and for six years I have been ringing bells at roadside shrines and getting back exactly the amount of divine response you'd expect from bronze and weather.

I gave the shrine's little alcove a last look. Moss on the stone. A wax stub melted into a puddle of its own devotion. Somebody had left an offering of dried apples, which told me another smuggler had come through recently and was also, apparently, talking to nobody.

"Mara!" Doss again, sharper now. "Wardens on the switchback. Crown colors."

That got me moving faster than any prayer ever had.

* * *

Crown checkpoints are a tax on honesty. That's the whole trick of them. A warden posts up at the only respectable road through a pass, and suddenly every merchant with clean papers starts sweating like the papers aren't clean, and every smuggler with dirty ones thanks whatever gods ignore him that he learned the goat tracks young.

We weren't taking the respectable road. But the respectable road ran parallel to ours for half a mile above the Greywater gorge, close enough that we could hear the wardens' horses on the gravel, and close enough — this was the part that mattered — that we could see the noticeboard they'd planted at the checkpoint's edge.

Doss wanted to push on. I wanted to look. Looking is free, usually, and in my trade information spends better than coin.

So I bellied up under a hawthorn at the ridge line while the convoy waited below, and I watched the checkpoint through the morning haze, and I read the broadsheets nailed to that board from eighty yards away, which is a skill you develop when your eyes are the best weapon you own.

The old notices were the usual: tariffs, road closures, a reward for a horse-thief whose portrait looked like every horse-thief's portrait. But pinned across the top, fresh paper, fresh ink, the crown's seal fat and red as a wound, was something new.

*BY ORDER OF THE REGENT'S COURT.* Big letters. The kind of letters that mean someone important is frightened.

*Let it be known throughout the provinces: persons found possessing the gift — hedge-craft, memory-work, bone-reading, luck-weaving, or any art unlicensed by the College of Wardens — are to be TAKEN ALIVE and delivered to the nearest warden-house. Reward: fifty crowns per head. Reward doubled for those who aid in the discovery thereof.*

*TAKEN ALIVE.* I'd seen gift-hunts before. Every few years the crown gets nervous about hedge-witches and luck-peddlers and sends out wagons to collect them, and every few years the wagons come back empty because everyone in Thornfell knows how to hide. But the old broadsheets said *driven out*, or *fined*, or once, memorably, *shunned*. Never *taken alive*. Alive implies a destination. Alive implies a use.

And there was a second line beneath, smaller, the ink slightly different, as if added later by a different hand:

*Note: memory-keepers to be reported above all others.*

Memory-keepers. I turned the word over. I knew the type vaguely — the way you know about tides or weather. Folk who could hold a thing in their heads and hand it to you whole. Songs, faces, roads, names. In the old stories they were librarians of the soul. In the crown's arithmetic they were apparently worth double.

Below me, one of the mules sneezed, loud as a musket shot. A warden at the checkpoint turned his head. I pressed myself flat under the hawthorn and counted his boot-steps as he came to the edge of the road, ten yards off, close enough that I could smell the leather and horse sweat of him. He stood there a long moment, looking out over the gorge, looking at — nothing. At fog. At the particular nothing that Thornfell does so well.

Then he went back to his fire, and I went back down the slope, and I didn't breathe properly until the convoy was three bends below the checkpoint and the crown's noticeboard was a white smudge behind us.

"Well?" Doss said.

"Fifty crowns a head," I said. "For gifted folk. Taken alive."

He chewed on that, and on a strip of dried mutton, in that order. "Fifty's steep."

"Fifty's a message. You don't pay fifty crowns for hedge-witches. You pay fifty crowns when you're afraid of something."

"And doubled for informers." He spat. "Half this country would sell their mothers for twenty."

"Which is why we take the goat tracks from here on, and why nobody in this convoy mentions what they saw." I looked back once, at the haze where the checkpoint had been. "Something's tilting, Doss."

"Tilting."

"The world. You can feel it in the roads. When the crown starts paying for people instead of goods, the roads change. Everything changes." I nudged my mare forward. "My order used to shelter the gifted, back when the Quiet Hand still answered. Old law. Shelter the small magics, feed the strange children. Half the shrines in these mountains were built for hiding things."

"And now?"

"Now the order's scattered and the god's gone quiet and I ring bells for practice." I said it lightly, because that's how you carry heavy things — you make them sound like luggage. But the words sat wrong in my chest all the same, the way a stone sits wrong in a boot.

Because here's the thing I didn't say to Doss, the thing I've been not-saying for six years: a god doesn't go silent for no reason. Gods go silent the way rivers go dry. Something upstream has changed.

And if the Quiet Hand was Thornfell's own — and every text my order kept agreed that he was older than the crown, older than the maps, older than the forgetting — then whatever had muted him might be the same whatever that had erased our history. And the crown, today, this morning, with red seals and fat rewards, had just started hunting the one kind of gifted soul whose art is *remembering*.

I'm not a scholar. I'm a guide. But even a guide can do that arithmetic.

* * *

We made camp that night in a hollow below the treeline, a smuggler's spot with good sightlines and bad cooking, and I took the last watch because I always take the last watch. The fire burned down to its red arithmetic. The mules breathed steam. Somewhere upslope, a fox screamed once and thought better of it.

I sat with my back against a boulder and did what I do, which is talk to nobody.

"Hundred and one," I told the dark. "In case you're counting. Which you're not."

The stars over Thornfell were thick and indifferent. Below us the valley fog moved like something breathing.

"I rang your bell this morning. Same bell, hundred and first time. You know what I asked for? Nothing grand. Just a sign. A cold wind at the wrong hour. A bird flying crooked. Anything I could point at and say *there, that's him, that's the Hand answering.*" I fed a stick to the fire. "Even a cruel god answers. Even a god who says *no* is telling you he's listening. Silence is just — absence. It's a room with the furniture gone."

The fire popped. The mules shifted. The world offered me its usual reply, which is to say none.

"But here's what keeps me up," I said, quieter now, because the quietest prayers are the honest ones. "If someone took your voice, Hand — if someone reached up the mountain and closed your mouth the way the crown closes roads — then whoever did it is still out there. Still doing it. And this morning they nailed paper to a board saying they want the rememberers alive."

I stared into the coals until they wrote nothing on me.

"So I'm going to ask the hundred-and-second question, and you can keep ignoring it like all the others." I leaned my head back against the stone. "What are they afraid we'll remember?"

No wind came. No bird flew crooked. The bell of the world hung silent over Thornfell, and I kept the watch alone, the way I had for six years, not knowing that somewhere above the treeline — half-frozen, hunted, flickering like a dying lamp — a stranger was stumbling toward my fire with a country in his head.

The fog breathed. The night tilted a degree further.

And far away, in a warden-house in the low country, someone lit a lamp over a list of names, and the hunt began in earnest.
