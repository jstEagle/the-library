## 2. A Stranger on the Switchback

There are three rules to surviving Thornfell's high passes, and I've broken all of them at least once. Rule one: never travel above the treeline after the first snow. Rule two: never trust a merchant who smiles too easily. Rule three: never, under any circumstances, stop for anything that looks like a person lying in the scree, because nine times out of ten it *is* a person, and the tenth time it's a trap dressed as one.

I broke rule three at midmorning, four days out from Cinderhollow with a convoy of untaxed brandy and a merchant named Osk Vell who smiled like he was doing me a favor by paying me.

The shape lay just off the switchback where the trail bent around a shoulder of gray stone — a dark smear against the white, too symmetrical for rockfall, too still for anything alive. I should have walked past. Every instinct I'd sharpened over eleven years of guiding said walk past, count your coin, keep your name off crown ledgers.

Instead I stood there with the wind trying to peel my hood back, and I heard myself say, out loud, to no one: "Don't."

My god didn't answer. My god hadn't answered in years, which is its own kind of conversation, and lately I'd started filling the silence myself. That was rule zero, the unwritten one: when the god goes quiet, you're on your own, so act accordingly.

I acted accordingly. I walked over to the body.

He was young — younger than me, anyway, which these days covers a lot of ground — curled on his side with his arms wrapped around himself like he was holding his own ribs together. Frost had built up in his eyelashes. His lips were the blue of deep river ice, and his hands were bare, which struck me as wrong even before everything else did. Nobody climbs above the treeline barehanded unless they left in a hurry or were made to.

"Dead?" I asked him, because I talk to corpses; it keeps the fear at a professional distance.

His eyelids fluttered. Not dead, then. Worse. Dying people ask things of you, and dying strangers ask more.

I crouched, checked the pass behind us — empty, just wind-combed snow and the black teeth of the ridge — and turned him enough to see his face. Sharp-cheeked, hollowed out, frostbitten at the cheekbones. And there, when his eyes half-opened, something odd: they caught the light wrong. Like looking into water and seeing a room reflected that isn't yours.

"You're not a smuggler," I said.

"No," he whispered. It came out cracked. He tried again. "No."

"Smugglers dress for the weather." I shrugged off my pack and dug out the emergency flask of plum brandy I pretend is for medicinal purposes only. "Wardens chase you?"

Something moved through his face — fear first, then a flicker of something else, something almost like light passing behind frosted glass. His eyes focused on me properly for the first time, and I felt it happen: a small, warm pressure behind my eyes, like someone gently opening a door in my head and peeking through.

Then it guttered out. He gasped like a man surfacing, and the warmth vanished, leaving nothing but cold and a faint ache, like a memory of a headache.

"What was that?" I asked.

"I don't—" He coughed. "I can't. It doesn't work anymore. It's *failing*—"

"Drink." I put the flask to his mouth myself because his hands weren't going to manage it. He drank like a starving man, then shuddered so hard I thought he'd shake apart. "Slowly. Who's failing?"

"They took—" His gaze went distant, unfocused, hunting through his own head and finding gaps. I know that look. I've seen it on men who took a bad knock at Sallow Bridge, wandering their own skulls looking for pieces. "They took my teacher. In Lowmark. The wardens. There were broadsheets." His voice dropped to a whisper, as if saying it quietly might make it less true. "*Taken alive.*"

I'd seen those broadsheets two days ago nailed to the checkpoint post at Gray Ford, right beside the toll schedule. *BY ORDER OF THE CROWN: ALL PERSONS POSSESSING THE GIFT ARE TO BE SURRENDERED TO WARDENS. TAKEN ALIVE. REWARD OFFERED.* The ink had been fresh. Somebody important wanted that message legible.

And here was a man with a gift that reached into my head without touching me, half-frozen on a smuggler's route, barehanded, alone, hunted.

Every mercenary instinct I owned lined up in a neat row and pointed south toward the nearest warden garrison. Turn him in. Collect whatever passed for a reward these days — probably coin, possibly a pardon for every untaxed keg I'd ever walked over a pass. A clean conscience, even, if you squinted: the broadsheet said *alive*, didn't it? The crown wasn't killing them. Yet. The yet was doing a lot of quiet work in that sentence.

But my gut — the same gut that had kept me alive through eleven winters of passes and betrayals — looked at this freezing, flickering boy and said something else entirely.

It said: *he looked at you like you were the last warm thing in the world.*

I hate my gut. I want that on the record.

* * *

Getting him down the switchback was a study in poor decisions. I couldn't carry him and my pack both, so I cached the brandy under an overhang, marked it with a cairn only I could read, and hauled him instead. He weighed about as much as a wet saddle blanket and apologized the entire way, which is a particular kind of exhausting.

"Ilen," he managed, somewhere around the third bend. "My name. I should — you should have it. If I die on you."

"Mara Brightwater. And don't die on me, I hate the paperwork."

That got a ghost of a laugh out of him, which was how I learned he was the stubborn sort. Good. Stubborn freezes slower than despair. I'd learned that from a mule named Duchess who once survived a whiteout purely out of spite.

Below the treeline the wind dropped and the pines closed overhead, blue-shadowed and quiet. I knew a place. Everyone who works these mountains knows places — caves, ruins, old shrines the faithful built before anyone decided faith was taxable. This one sat in a fold of the hillside half a mile off the main track: a watch-shrine, some forgotten order's outpost, walls of dry-stacked stone still standing, roof mostly collapsed but one corner sheltered under a slab of living rock. Moss on the floor. A cold hearth. An alcove where a statue of somebody's god had once stood; only the feet remained, worn smooth, pointing eternally at nothing.

I'd used it a dozen times to wait out weather or wardens. It had never once asked anything of me.

I got Ilen inside, got a fire going small and smokeless — dry pine, banked careful — and wrapped him in my spare cloak, which smelled like mule and regret. He held his hands to the flames and watched them like he was reading something written there.

"Your gift," I said. "The thing you did on the mountain. What is it?"

He was quiet long enough that I thought he wouldn't answer. Then: "Memory. I'm a keeper. I can... hold memories. Give them back. Show them." His hands trembled, and not from cold alone. "It's been getting worse for weeks. Like a lamp running out of oil. Things I've kept for years — gone, or blurred, or..." He swallowed. "Or eaten."

"Eaten," I repeated. "Memories don't get eaten. They fade. They get drunk away. They get knocked loose by a fence post. They don't get *eaten*."

"This one does." He looked up at me, and that strange lightless reflection moved in his eyes again. "Someone is taking them. From far away. I can feel it — like hooks. Every night, a little less of me."

Well. I've heard a lot of nonsense around campfires, most of it mine. But he wasn't performing. Performance has a shine to it, a salesman's polish. This was just a frightened man reporting the weather inside his own skull.

"So the broadsheets," I said slowly. "They're not arresting the gifted. They're *harvesting* them."

"I don't know what they're doing." His voice cracked. "I only know what's happening to me, and that my teacher told me to run north, into Thornfell, and find—" He stopped. Frowned. Reached for something and found air. "Find..."

"Find what?"

"I don't remember." He laughed, and it was the worst sound I'd heard all winter. "Isn't that funny? I don't remember what I was supposed to find. Someone ate the instructions."

Outside, the wind shifted, and beneath it — faint, maybe imagined — I caught something else. A horn note, low and carrying, from the direction of Gray Ford. Then another, answering it from the east ridge. Wardens ran horns in pairs when they were sweeping a slope. I'd heard that pattern enough times to feel it in my teeth.

Ilen heard it too. He went very still, firelight jumping in those wrong-water eyes.

"They followed me," he said. "They always follow me. I don't know how. Maybe they can smell the gift. Maybe—" He looked at me, and here was the thing, the thing I'd be turning over for nights afterward: he didn't beg. He just looked at me with terrible, exhausted honesty and said, "You should go. You've done more than anyone would. If they find you with me, they'll take your license, your routes, maybe your freedom. I'm not worth that. I'm barely worth anything now. Half of me is missing."

And my oath — the one sworn on the silent bell at the shrine outside Hollowbridge, the one that bound my tongue and my sword to a god who hadn't spoken since before I grayed at the temples — that oath sat up inside me like an old dog hearing its name. *The gifted are to be surrendered. Taken alive.* It didn't use words anymore; it just leaned. Eleven years of leaning, in one direction, always the same direction.

Turn him in. Walk out the shrine door, flag the horns, collect the reward, sleep soundly.

I looked at the remaining feet of the forgotten statue, worn smooth by centuries of kneeling pilgrims, pointing at nothing, worshipping nobody knew what. A god erased so thoroughly that even its own altar had been forgotten. Something about that itched at me, low and persistent, like a splinter working toward the skin.

"Half of you is missing," I said, "and the half that's left is the most dangerous thing on this mountain. So no. I'm not turning you in, and I'm not leaving."

"You don't even know me."

"No," I agreed, feeding another stick to the fire. "But I know wardens, and I know what 'taken alive' turns into once the reward changes to a bounty. I've guided enough crowns' men to know how they talk when they think the locals can't hear." I pulled my knife and began cutting pine boughs for a bed, not looking at him, because looking at him made the decision harder and I'd already made it, gods help me. "You'll stay here tonight. Tomorrow I move you deeper — there's a valley the horns don't reach. And you're going to tell me everything you *do* remember, starting with why a memory-keeper matters enough for the whole crown to go hunting."

He stared at me across the small fire. Somewhere above us, the horns called and answered, patient as wolves.

"Why?" he asked simply. "Why help me?"

I thought about lying. I'm good at lying; it's most of my trade. But the truth came out sideways, the way truths do when you're not watching for them:

"Because whoever's eating your memories left the instructions in," I said. "And I'd very much like to know what they didn't want you to forget."

Above the treeline, the horns fell silent all at once — which was worse than the calling, because it meant they'd stopped searching the heights.

They'd found the trail leading down.
