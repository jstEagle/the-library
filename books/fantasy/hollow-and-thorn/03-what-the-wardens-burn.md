## 3. What the Wardens Burn

I have kept the memories of kings. I have held a dying woman's last summer in my cupped hands like water, and carried it three hundred miles so her daughter could drink it. I once held the entire history of a drowned city, every street and every name, and I did not spill a drop.

So understand what it means when I tell you that I cannot remember my mother's face.

I know she had one. I know this the way you know a room exists behind a closed door. But when I reach for it—and I reach for it constantly now, the way a tongue worries a missing tooth—there is only a smooth blankness, polished by something that was *hungry*.

"You're doing it again," Mara said from ahead of me on the switchback. "That face."

"What face?"

"The one where you're chewing on your own thoughts." She didn't turn around. She walked the way she did everything, economical, like movement was a currency she refused to waste. "Stop it. You'll fall off the mountain, and then I'll have wasted a perfectly good hiding spot."

We had been walking since before dawn. My feet had passed through pain some hours ago and come out the other side into a strange numbness I suspected was not healthy. The Thornfell mountains do not have summits so much as they have moods, and today's mood was gray sleet with a side of menace.

"Where are we going?" I asked.

"Cinderhollow. Smuggler's village, or it was. There's a woman there who owes me." A pause. "Owed me," she corrected herself, and said nothing else for a mile.

I used the silence to take inventory of myself, which is a grim exercise when your inventory keeps shrinking. I am—or I was—a memory-keeper of the Vale Scriptorium, which no longer exists, which may never have existed, which is the sort of sentence I have become fluent in thinking. My gift lets me hold what others cannot carry. Griefs too heavy. Secrets too dangerous. Whole histories that powerful people would prefer to be forgotten. I keep them safe inside me until they can be returned to the world.

And something out there was keeping them safe from *me*.

It had started small, weeks ago. A name gone missing. Then the layout of the Scriptorium's third floor. Then larger things, whole seasons of my childhood, eaten out of me in the night—I would wake with the sensation of having been chewed, a tenderness behind the eyes, and another door in the house of myself standing open on an empty room.

The wardens wanted me taken alive. That much the broadsheets had made clear. But I had begun to suspect the taking-alive was not the crown's idea at all. Someone wanted my memories specifically, and the body they came in was merely packaging.

"Down here," Mara said, and left the trail.

* * *

You smell Cinderhollow before you see it. Or rather, you smell where Cinderhollow used to be: wet ash and char, that sweet-rotten stink of burned thatch that gets into your clothes and stays for days, a scent I will now apparently remember forever, because the universe has a sense of humor.

The village sat in a fold of the mountain like a cupped hand. Maybe forty houses once. I counted eleven still standing, and of those, several were more suggestion than structure. The rest were black rectangles of foundation, roofless, their stone chimneys rising out of the wreckage like fingers pointing at nothing.

Mara stopped walking so abruptly I nearly went into her back.

"Well," she said. It was all she said. But I had spent two days learning to read her, and I heard what lived under the word: a whole library of things she wasn't going to let herself feel yet.

A cat picked its way across one of the foundations, thin and deliberate, and regarded us with the flat contempt of the recently abandoned.

"The houses with the marks," I said quietly. On the doorposts of the burned homes, someone had painted a symbol in white—a circle with a vertical line through it. I knew it. Everyone gifted knew it. The crown's mark for *registered*, painted fresh over char. They had marked the houses first. Then they had come back.

"Herb-witch lived here." Mara nodded at the nearest ruin. Her voice had gone flat, which I was learning meant the opposite of calm. "Sefa. Deaf as a post, mean as a snake, best healer in three valleys. She'd have—" She stopped. Started again. "There'd have been children on that list too. Gifted kids get registered at birth in the villages. Somebody always tells the priest."

"And the priest tells the wardens."

"And the wardens tell the fire." She crouched, touched the ash, rubbed it between her fingers. Still soft. Days old, not weeks. "We just missed it."

"That's not necessarily comforting, Mara."

"No," she agreed. "It isn't."

Here is what nobody tells you about memory-keepers: we are not brave. Bravery is for people who can afford to forget. We carry everything forward, every fear and every grief, at full weight, forever. When I looked at those burned foundations, I did not see ash. I saw the last morning of each house—the smell of bread, a child arguing about shoes, a woman hanging wash with a registered mark on her own front door that she touched every day on her way past, the way you touch a sore tooth, telling yourself it doesn't hurt.

I sat down right there in the ashes of a stranger's kitchen and put my head in my hands.

"Ilen." Mara's boots stopped in front of me. "Get up. Ash on your knees is fine. Despair sitting down is how people die on mountains."

"They took them alive," I said. "The broadsheet says alive. So somewhere there's a cart, or a prison, or—"

"Or worse than a prison. Alive covers a lot of ground." She hauled me up by the arm with a mercenary's complete indifference to my dignity. "Which is why you're going to stop sitting in kitchens and start telling me what exactly the wardens want with you. And don't say 'my gift.' I've seen gifts. Luck-witches, storm-callers, a man who could talk to crows. Nobody burns forty houses for a crow-talker."

I should have lied to her. Every instinct of survival said lie; she was a stranger, a smuggler, a woman bound by an oath to a god who might well demand my head. But I was tired, and cold, and standing in the ruins of kindness, and lying felt like helping them win.

"I can open the Hollow Door," I said.

She went very still. Even the sleet seemed to pause around her.

"Say that again, slowly, and mean it."

"The Hollow Door. The way out of Thornfell." I watched her face while I said it, and I will keep that image as long as I am able, because it was the face of a woman hearing that the walls of her prison had a crack in them. "Every country has one. A way that was sealed when the borders were closed. Most are dead. Ours isn't. It sleeps, and it opens once a century, on the thirteenth turn of the moon, and it can only be opened by a keeper who remembers the words and the way and the—"

"Can you?" she cut in. "Remember?"

And there it was. The question underneath everything, the one I had been carrying since the first missing name, and I made myself answer it honestly, because she had earned honesty with two days of sleet and a hiding spot in a frozen shrine.

"No," I said. "Not anymore. Someone is eating my memories, Mara. From a distance. I can feel it happening—it's like being robbed by someone very patient, very skilled, who only comes at night. They've taken the Door's location. They've taken most of the opening rite. They've taken—" my voice did something humiliating, cracked, and I pushed through it, "—they've taken my mother's face. Whoever they are, they don't need to catch me. They just need to wait until I'm empty. And then the Door stays shut for another hundred years, and everyone the crown wants gone stays exactly where the crown wants them."

The wind came down the valley and moved the ash around our feet like gray snow.

"The thirteenth turn," Mara said finally. "When is it?"

"Six weeks. Perhaps less."

She looked out over the ruined village, at the white-marked doorposts, at the chimney-fingers pointing at nothing, and something happened in her face. I have held ten thousand memories and I know all the shapes of human resolve, and I watched one assemble itself in her, piece by piece, like a wall built against a flood.

"Then six weeks is what we have," she said. "And whoever is eating you—we find them, and we take it back."

"You don't even know if it *can* be taken back."

"Everything can be taken back. I'm a smuggler. It's practically scripture." She turned toward the one intact building at the village's edge—a low stone chapel with its windows darkened. "Come on. If any of Cinderhollow's people got out, they'll have gone to ground somewhere, and ground means tunnels, and tunnels mean I need to have a conversation with whoever's watching us from that doorway."

I spun toward the chapel. Nothing. Shadow. Then the shadow shifted, resolved, and became a girl—ten years old, maybe, soot-streaked, standing absolutely still in the manner of someone who has survived by not being noticed. She was holding a cat. The same cat, I realized, that had been auditing the ruins.

She looked at me, and then at Mara, and said, in the flat voice of a child reciting something learned by rote:

"You're the ones the bones said would come."

Behind her, deep in the chapel's dark, someone struck a light.
