## 4. The Company of Small Mercies

The cave smelled of wet stone and old smoke, the way smugglers' caves always did—like every fire ever lit in them had left a ghost behind and refused to move out. I'd been using this one, three miles off the Salt Road under a shelf of granite the maps forgot on purpose, for six years. It had never held more than me, a keg of contraband brandy, and my own bad thoughts.

Now it held five.

Ilen sat closest to the fire, wrapped in my spare cloak with his hands stretched toward the flames like a man begging forgiveness from them. His gift still flickered—that dying-lamp stutter I'd first seen above the treeline—but in the cave's dark I could almost see it working: little shivers of light passing over his eyes when he looked at something too long. Remembering. Or trying to.

"You're staring," he said, without looking up.

"I'm assessing."

"You're staring *assessingly*."

"Mercenary habit. Everything's either a threat, a payment, or a problem." I fed a split log to the fire. "You're currently all three."

He smiled at that, small and tired, and I pretended not to notice how much better it made the cave feel. That was the trouble with memory-keepers, I was starting to understand. They didn't just carry the past. They made you aware you were standing inside one.

* * *

Brannic came to us two days later, walking down the switchbacks with his warden's coat turned inside-out and a look on his face like a man who'd set down something heavy and discovered his arms didn't know what to do without it.

I had a knife at his throat before he cleared the boulders. Old habits.

"Easy," he said, hands wide. His voice was gravel poured slow. "I'm not here for him." He nodded past me, toward where Ilen had gone very still against the cave wall. "I'm here because of what I wouldn't do."

"Wardens don't defect," I said. "Wardens get promoted or buried."

"This one buried his oath instead." He pulled back the collar of the reversed coat, and there, where the crown's grey badge should have been, was only a pale rectangle of skin untouched by sun—the shape of a thing removed. "They ordered me to take three children from a farm outside Cinderhollow. Ages eight, ten, and eleven. The eleven-year-old could make bread rise by humming at it. That was the whole of her crime." His jaw worked. "I put my badge on the table and walked out. They'll have marked me traitor by now. Which means, mercenary, that you and I are in the same line of work these days: staying alive."

I should have sent him away. Every rule I lived by said send him away—a warden is a warden even stripped of the coat, and oaths are oaths even broken, and I knew better than anyone what a broken oath could do to the person holding the pieces.

But Ilen came forward and looked up at Brannic—Brannic was a head taller and built like a door—and said, quietly, "You hummed back at her, didn't you. Before you left."

Brannic went rigid. "How do you—"

"It's all over your face. You've been humming it ever since. Badly."

The big man's eyes went wet and furious at once, and he turned away as if the mountains had insulted him personally. And that, more than any testimony, decided it. A liar doesn't grieve like that. A liar doesn't carry a children's song in his chest like a stone.

"Fine," I said. "You can carry water and stand watch. If you betray us, I'll kill you with your own sword, which will save you the embarrassment of being arrested."

"That," Brannic said gravely, "is the kindest offer I've had in weeks."

* * *

Sefa arrived without arriving, which was somehow exactly right for her. One evening there were four of us eating nettle stew, and the next evening there were five, and a small brown woman was sitting on a rock at the cave mouth sorting herbs as though she'd grown there between one dusk and the next.

Brannic spotted her first and nearly drew steel. She looked up at him, entirely unbothered, and tapped her own ear twice, then shook her head.

"Deaf," Ilen translated softly. "She's saying she can't hear you sneaking up either, so you're even."

"How do you know that?"

"Because I can see what she remembers, and she remembers being born this way, and she remembers forty years of people shouting at her like volume was ever the problem." He crouched to her level, and she studied him with sharp black eyes, then took his hand and pressed it flat against her throat. "Feel that," he murmured. "That's how we talk. Here—" He touched his own throat and hummed a low note, letting the vibration speak.

Her whole face changed. She answered with a hum of her own, different pitch, different meaning, and I watched the two of them build a language out of nothing but resonance and patience while the rest of us stood around feeling roughly as intelligent as the stew.

"She says," Ilen reported after a while, grinning, "that she followed the smoke because any fool lighting a fire in a cave this time of year wants killing by exposure, and she'd rather do it herself, properly, with herbs. Also that your stew needs salt."

"It has salt."

"She says it needs *more* salt."

Sefa was a herb-witch of the old unlicensed kind—the kind the new edicts called gifted, though her gift was mostly knowing things: which moss drew fever, which spring water carried sickness, which silences in a conversation meant someone was lying. She couldn't hear a word we said, but she read lips like a scholar reads scripture, and she missed nothing. Within a day she'd reorganized our entire medicine stores, diagnosed Brannic's bad knee, and established via pointed gestures that I slept badly and drank too much, both of which were true and neither of which was her business.

I liked her immediately. I resented how much.

* * *

Pip we caught stealing from us, which is the traditional way of meeting Pip.

I woke in the grey hour before dawn to a small weight lifting my coin purse off my belt with a delicacy that would have shamed a surgeon. I let the fingers close around it, then closed my hand around the fingers.

"Ow ow ow ow—"

Small face, freckles, hair like a crow's nest after a storm. Eyes that went wide when they found mine and then did something strange—flicked sideways, just for a heartbeat, as if checking whether luck intended to intervene.

"Luck-gifted," I said.

"No! Nope. Absolutely not. Just lucky. There's a difference. Lucky is legal."

"The broadsheets disagree. Anyone with the gift, taken alive." I held up the purse. "And this is mine."

"That's a *tax*," Pip said with tremendous dignity. "For sleeping so loud. You snore like a landslide, lady, I heard you from the ridge."

"You're nine."

"Ten. And a half. And I heard everything you lot said yesterday through the crack in the rocks, so before you decide what to do with me, remember I know where the wardens' patrol rotates and which pass has the loose scree and that the big one—" a jerk of the chin at Brannic, who had appeared in the cave mouth looking enormous and betrayed, "—snores worse than you."

"He does not," Brannic said.

"He really does," Sefa signed, and Ilen laughed aloud, translating, and just like that the arithmetic changed. Four became five. Another mouth, another risk, another small warm body between us and the cold.

I gave Pip the heel of yesterday's bread and kept the purse. Pip ate the bread and stole my flint instead, which we all agreed was fair.

* * *

It happened slowly, the way water happens to stone.

Brannic took the night watches because he slept least, and taught Pip knife-drills in the mornings with a wooden practice blade and infinite patience, correcting the child's grip fifty times an hour without once raising his voice. Pip, in turn, appointed himself our official scout on the grounds that no one suspects a kid gathering berries, and came back each evening with intelligence of genuinely professional quality and pockets full of other people's buttons.

Sefa fed us. That sounds small. It wasn't. She turned nettle and pine bark and a snared rabbit into food that tasted like someone had decided we deserved it, and she doctored Ilen's worsening headaches with poultices that smelled like crushed summer, and when Brannic's old badge-shaped scar ached in the cold she pressed a warm cloth into his hands without a word, because she'd already understood what it was before any of us explained.

And Ilen—Ilen remembered for us.

That was the thing none of us had words for yet. When Brannic faltered mid-story about his years in the wardens, Ilen would quietly supply the name of the comrade he'd lost. When Sefa told a story in gesture and lip-read fragments, Ilen caught the whole of it and gave it back to the rest of us complete. He was a keeper in truth, keeping us—even me, though I fought it longest.

One night he caught me cleaning my blades and said, apropos of nothing, "You pray facing east. Toward the shrine at Hollowford. You've done it every night since I met you."

"It's habit."

"It's grief," he said gently. "Your god's gone quiet, and you keep praying anyway. That's not habit. That's love with nowhere to go."

I put the whetstone down harder than necessary. "Careful, keeper. Some memories are rented, not owned."

"Then I'll pay the rent," he said, and went back to the fire, and left me alone with a truth I hadn't invited.

Because that was the danger of it, wasn't it. The company. The fire. The warmth. Every mercenary instinct I had said: attachments are leverage, leverage gets people killed, walk light, owe nothing. But sitting there in the firelight—Brannic mending Pip's too-big coat with clumsy enormous stitches, Sefa humming a tune she couldn't hear so Ilen could feel it through the stone beside her, the child asleep against the big man's shoulder like a burr on a blanket—I understood that I'd already stopped walking light. Somewhere between the switchback and the stew, these people had become mine, and I had become theirs, and there was no version of the coming months in which that didn't cost us.

The moon was rising through the cave mouth, fat and white and patient.

Thirteen turns, the smugglers said, and the high passes closed. Thirteen turns until winter sealed Thornfell like a tomb.

I looked at Ilen's flickering gift, at the shadows under his eyes where something was eating him alive from the inside, at the family assembled around a fire in a hole in the ground—and I felt, for the first time since my god went silent, something that wasn't dread.

It might have been hope. It might have been premonition. With me, historically, those wore the same coat.

Above the cave, far off along the ridgeline, a signal fire bloomed—small, red, deliberate. Not a campfire. Campfires don't burn in threes.

Brannic saw it the same moment I did. His hand went to the empty place where his badge used to be.

"They've found the trail," he said.

The moon hung overhead, counting down.
