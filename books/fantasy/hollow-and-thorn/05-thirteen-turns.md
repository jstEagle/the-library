## 5. Thirteen Turns

I felt them arguing before I saw them. That's the joke of it—everyone assumes a deaf woman misses things, when in truth I read argument the way they read weather: in the set of shoulders, the way Mara's hands cut the air like she was filleting something, the way Ilen kept touching his own temple as if checking whether his head was still screwed on.

We were three days into the smuggler's cave above Cinderhollow, and the fire had gone from novelty to necessity to religion. Brannic was out checking the snares. Pip was asleep in a nest of stolen blankets, twitching through some dream where he was presumably robbing someone faster than he could in waking life.

Mara crouched beside me and tapped my knee twice—*look*—then held up her fingers: thirteen.

I already knew. I'd known since the night before, when I'd laid out the bones on my cloth and they had arranged themselves into an answer I didn't like.

*Show me anyway,* I signed, because bone-casts are like gossip; they want an audience.

She watched while I gathered the knucklebones of a mountain goat, two vertebrae from a crow that had died of natural causes (I checked), and the tooth I'd pulled from my own jaw twenty years ago when it ached and no herb-witch in Thornfell would treat a witch herself. Old habit. The bones know me. They've stopped being polite about it.

I cast them onto the cloth. They rolled, settled, and told the same story they'd told last night, because bones are honest even when you ask them twice hoping they'll lie.

The long bone—the road—lay pointing at the gap between the fire-stone and the hearth-crack. The moon-bone, which is really just a round pebble I chose because it looked like a moon and bones respect ambition, sat in the crack itself. Half in shadow. Half out.

Thirteen turns. The moon over Thornfell runs its cycle thirteen times before it *turns*—flips, the old stories say, like a coin, like a page—and when it completes the thirteenth turn, whatever doors were open close for another hundred years. My grandmother's grandmother had been alive the last time it happened. She'd left one sentence about it, written in ash on a hearthstone: *the mountain shut its mouth and we forgot what we were waiting for.*

I counted on my fingers for Mara, folding them down one by one. Six weeks. Forty-two days, if the weather held, and the weather never holds.

Mara went very still. Stillness looks different on her than on other people—on most folk stillness is peace, but on Mara it's a drawn bow.

Then she signed, slow and deliberate so I couldn't pretend to misread her: *Can the boy open it before then?*

I looked across the fire at Ilen. He was sitting against the cave wall with his hands open in his lap, palms up, the way he did when the memories flickered—when something tried to surface and couldn't quite break through, like a fish under ice. His gift wasn't dying exactly. It was being *eaten*. Something far away, patient and enormous, was drinking him down a sip at a time, and every day he knew a little less about how to do the only thing any of us needed done.

I picked up the bones again and cast a second time—not asking about the moon this time, but about him. About whether the Door would open.

The crow vertebrae crossed. The tooth landed face-down.

I swept the bones back into their pouch without showing her. Some answers are rude to say out loud, or in my case, to say at all.

Mara understood anyway. She always did. Her jaw tightened, and she reached over and squeezed my shoulder once—*thank you*—and went to sit by Ilen, and the two of them bent their heads together over a problem neither of them could solve alone, which is, if you ask me, how all the best and worst things begin.

*

The strangers came at dusk, and I heard them coming, which will confuse you until I explain that I hear everything—I simply don't hear it with my ears. Hooves carry through stone. So does the particular silence of men trying to be quiet, which is louder than noise, if you're paying attention.

I tapped the warning on the cave wall: three knocks, pause, three knocks. Pip woke instantly—he sleeps like a cat pretending to sleep—and slipped behind the rockfall without being told. Good lad. Terrible morals, excellent instincts.

Brannic came in from the snare-line with blood on his hands that wasn't his, carrying a hare and wearing the expression of a man doing arithmetic he hates. He held up four fingers. Four riders. Then he touched his chest, where a warden's badge used to sit before he tore it off, and made a shape with his mouth that I read easily enough: *mine.*

Not crown soldiers. Wardens. And not just any wardens—men who knew Brannic's face, which meant men who'd been looking for him too.

They stopped below the cave mouth, in the little hollow where the trail widens, and dismounted. I watched from the crack in the rock while Mara walked down to meet them alone, because she insisted, because she's the kind of fool who thinks standing between danger and her people counts as solving it.

There were four of them, grey-cloaked, and their leader was a woman with a voice like a file drawn slow across iron. I read her lips from thirty feet, which is farther than I should admit, and this is what she said:

"Sister Brightwater. The Order of the Silent Bell sends its regards."

Mara didn't move. But I saw her hands—the mercenary's hands, always half-curled, ready—and I saw them go flat. Open. The way hands go when they're no longer permitted to make fists.

"You're a long way from your chapter-house," Mara said.

"And you are a long way from your oath." The warden woman smiled without warmth, the way frost smiles. "You swore, sister. Body and tongue in service of the Bell. You swore to surrender the gifted into the Order's keeping, wherever found, whoever they be." She tilted her head. "You have a memory-keeper up in that cave. We can smell him. They all smell of smoke and other people's yesterdays."

Here is what I learned in that moment, watching lips I wished I couldn't read: an oath isn't a promise. A promise lives in your head, and heads can change. An oath lives somewhere lower—in the spine, in the blood—and it has teeth, and the teeth belong to someone else.

Because Mara's right hand began to move. Not toward her sword. Toward the cave. Toward *us*. Small at first—a twitch of the fingers, like a puppet feeling its strings—and I watched her fight it, watched her plant her boots and lock her elbow and sweat in the cold, and I understood that the Order hadn't come to negotiate. They'd come to *pull*. Every word out of the warden's mouth was another hand on the rope tied around Mara's spine, hauling her toward betrayal one inch at a time.

"Come down here yourself," the warden said, gentle as rot. "Bring the keeper. Kneel with us at the shrine and renew your vow, and the Bell will forgive the delay. Refuse—" she spread her hands, reasonable, terrible, "—and we'll declare you oath-broken. And then, sister, every blade in Thornfell that answers to the Bell comes hunting *you*, and we take the boy anyway, and we take your little family apart piece by piece to see what else you've been hiding. The deaf witch. The deserter. The child."

Pip, behind me in the dark, had gone rigid. I put my hand on the back of his neck, steady, and felt his heartbeat hammering against my palm like a bird in a fist.

Mara stood there a long moment. I watched her lips form words she didn't say aloud—prayer, maybe, or profanity, possibly both; with Mara it's usually both—and then her right arm rose, smooth and horrible, and pointed directly at our cave.

At us.

Her face didn't change. Her eyes did. And in them I saw the whole war happening at once: the oath hauling, the woman refusing, and underneath it all something older than either—the silent god she'd prayed to a hundred times without answer, watching, perhaps, or perhaps nothing at all, because gods go quiet precisely when you need them loudest.

"No," Mara said.

The word cost her. I don't mean courage—I mean *cost*, physical, like a bone breaking somewhere inside her. She said it through her teeth, and her whole body shuddered as though the oath had struck her, and the warden woman's smile widened, because a refusal given under compulsion is still a refusal, and now the machinery of the hunt could turn.

"So be it," the warden said. "Six days, Brightwater. Renew the vow by the next new moon, or be declared. Sleep on it. Oaths are heavy—we understand." She mounted. "But the boy comes with us eventually. The Bell is patient. It's the only thing about it that ever was."

They rode out down the switchbacks, and the mountain swallowed the sound of them, and Mara stood alone in the blue dusk with her right arm still raised, pointing at nothing, shaking, unable to put it down.

I went to her. Took her wrist in both my hands—her skin cold as streamwater—and brought the arm down myself, slowly, like lowering a drawbridge. She let me. When her eyes found mine they were wet and furious and more afraid than I'd ever seen them, and she signed, jagged, half-formed:

*I couldn't stop it. My own hand. Six days, Sefa. What happens in six days when it stops being my hand and starts being my feet?*

Above us, in the cave mouth, Ilen stood watching, and I saw him touch his temple again—that gesture, always that gesture—and I saw the look on his face of a man who has just realized that the Door he must open is closing, the friend he trusts is bound, and the enemy hunting him wears the face of her own god.

Six days to the new moon. Six weeks to the thirteenth turn.

And somewhere in the dark beneath the mountain, I swear the bones in my pouch rattled, all by themselves, like teeth.
