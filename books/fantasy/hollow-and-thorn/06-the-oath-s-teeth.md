## 6. The Oath's Teeth

The oath woke at moonrise.

I'd felt it sleeping in me for years—a hook behind the breastbone, a cold little weight I'd learned to ignore, the way you learn to ignore a bad tooth. My god had gone silent a decade ago, but the oath He'd taken from me still worked fine. Oaths are like that. They outlive the gods who witness them.

That night it opened its eyes.

I was on second watch, sitting with my back against the cave wall, watching Sefa's fire burn low and listening to Pip snore like a small congested bear. Across the coals, Ilen slept curled against Brannic's pack, and my body stood up without asking me.

I want to be clear about that: *without asking me.* One moment I was sitting; the next I was upright, boots on, walking toward him, and the only part of me that had voted on it was the part that wasn't consulted.

*Deliver him,* said the oath. It didn't use words, exactly. Words would have been kinder. It used *certainty*, poured straight into the marrow, the way a warden's writ uses wax and seal. *The gifted belong to the crown. You swore.*

"I swore to *you*," I muttered through teeth that weren't entirely mine. "And you've been dead to me for ten years."

The hook behind my breastbone pulled.

I dug my heels into scree. I am not a small woman and I have hauled cargo up cliffs that would make a goat weep, but this was different—this was being reeled, line by line, by something with no arm to break. My feet slid an inch. Another.

Brannic stirred. "Mara?"

"Fine," I said, which was a lie delivered in a steady voice, which is most of what mercenary professionalism is. "Bad dream. Walking it off."

He grunted and went back to sleep, because Brannic trusts people past the point of sense, which is how he ended up disgraced in the first place, and also why I hadn't killed him yet.

I fought it the whole way to the cave mouth. I will spare you the details—the details were mostly me losing ground one humiliating inch at a time—but somewhere near the entrance I did the only clever thing available to a body being dragged: I sat down and wrapped my arms around a boulder like a lover with poor judgment.

The oath pulled. The boulder held. We stayed like that, me and the rock, having the least romantic embrace of my life, while inside my chest something cold and patient counted the hours until dawn.

Because here's the thing about oaths sworn to gods: they're strongest at night. Night belongs to them. Dawn belongs to whoever's stubborn enough to reach it.

I reached it. Gray light came down the valley, thin as broth, and the hook slackened—not gone, never gone now, just... dozing. I peeled myself off the boulder finger by finger and went back into the cave, and nobody asked where I'd been, because smugglers know better than to ask questions before tea.

Sefa handed me a cup anyway. She looked at my hands, then at the boulder visible through the cave mouth, then back at my hands, and her eyebrows performed an entire conversation. Deaf herb-witches miss nothing. It's their superpower.

"I'm fine," I told her.

She patted my knee, which in Sefa's language meant *you are lying and I love you anyway.*

* * *

It happened again the next night. And the next.

By the fourth night I'd developed a system: rope around the waist, rope around the stone, knots I could tie half-asleep, which was fortunate, because I increasingly was. I told the company I'd taken up night-walking for my health. Pip offered to sell me a charm against it. I bought the charm. It did nothing, but the look on her face when I paid her was worth two coppers.

What I didn't tell anyone—and couldn't, literally couldn't, my tongue going thick and dumb whenever I tried—was that the oath had started whispering *where*. Not just *deliver him*. Deliver him *to the wardens at Salt Gate*. Deliver him *by the thirteenth turn of the moon*. It had a schedule now. Somewhere out there, my silent god's other servants were counting down too, and the oath in my chest was keeping time.

On the fifth night, everything changed, because Ilen talks in his sleep.

Not words. Worse. *Memories.*

I was roped to my boulder, fighting the pull, when warmth bloomed behind my eyes like someone lighting a lamp in a room I'd forgotten I owned. And I was standing in a city I had never seen, on a street paved with white stone, under a sky so blue it felt like a boast.

Thornfell. Before.

I knew it the way you know a face in a dream—totally, instantly, without evidence. White-stoned streets running with meltwater. Bells everywhere, bells in every tower, and every bell ringing the same note, a note I felt in my teeth rather than heard. People in the street wore wool dyed saffron and madder-red, and they greeted each other by touching foreheads, and above the highest tower hung a banner with no emblem on it at all—just blank cloth, because the country's sigil had been *taken*, though I wouldn't learn that word's full meaning for weeks yet.

Then the dream shifted, the way Ilen's dreams shifted, and I was in a kitchen—no, a shrine-kitchen, a small warm room where an old woman was teaching a boy to bake bread shaped like doors. The boy had Ilen's hands. He laughed at something she said, flour on his nose, and the laugh went through me like a blade goes through bread.

*Ilen,* I thought. *This is yours.*

And the dream, hearing its name, turned and looked at me.

For a heartbeat I felt him feel me—sleeping awareness brushing sleeping awareness, two strangers' hands meeting in the dark. He didn't wake. But the memories came faster after that, unspooling all night like thread from a dropped spool, and I sat on my boulder and let them, because it was either that or fight the oath with nothing in my hands, and a person can't fight all night on empty hands.

I saw harvest festivals where they burned effigies not of enemies but of *forgetting*—straw figures stuffed with written grievances, set alight so the year's sorrows would be remembered once and then released. I saw the Door itself, or its face: a seam of light in the mountain's heart, tall as a cathedral, humming the same note as the bells. I saw soldiers whose armor bore no crest, marching through white streets, and the bells going silent one by one, tower by tower, like lamps blown out.

I saw the old woman from the kitchen, years later, pressing her hand over the boy's eyes and saying words I somehow understood: *They can take the country, little door-keeper. They cannot take what we give away freely. Remember to give.*

I woke at dawn with frost in my hair and tears frozen on my face and no memory of my own childhood anywhere in my head—which was strange, because I'd gone to sleep with those, surely? No. They were there. Just quieter. Everything of mine was quieter, and everything of his was louder, and I sat there in the gray light understanding that Ilen's gift, flickering and dying as it was, had done what dying lamps do.

It had shared its oil.

* * *

"You were crying," Pip announced at breakfast, with the tact of a siege engine.

"It's called weather, Pip."

"On your *face*?"

"Pip."

Sefa signed something rapid at Brannic, who translated with the reluctance of a man reading someone else's mail: "She says the mountain's been talking to you. She says the mountain doesn't talk to people who are planning to leave."

"The mountain and I have an understanding."

"Mara." Brannic set down his cup. He has a warden's face even disgraced—honest, heavy, built for delivering bad news and absorbing worse. "Last night you talked in your sleep. In a language none of us knew. And the fire burned green for an hour, and Sefa says—" he glanced at her, and she signed again, shorter, harder, "—she says the flames bent *toward* you. Like they were listening."

I looked at Ilen. He was awake for once, hollow-eyed, wrapped in every blanket we owned, watching me with an expression I couldn't read. Fear, maybe. Or recognition.

"You dreamed," he said quietly. "Didn't you. White streets. Bells."

My spine went cold. "How—"

"Because I dreamed them first." He leaned forward. "Every night since Cinderhollow, pieces of me go missing—I told you, something's *eating* them—but last night, for the first time, I dreamed the whole city, start to finish, and it didn't hurt. And I woke up knowing things I didn't know yesterday. The Door's true name. The sacrifice it needs." His voice dropped. "And I woke up feeling like I'd spent the night talking to someone. Someone who held the thread so it wouldn't tangle."

The company was very quiet. Even Pip.

"That was you," Ilen said. Not a question.

I should have lied. A good mercenary lies the way other people breathe. But he was looking at me with the flour-on-his-nose boy's eyes, and the oath behind my breastbone was coiled and waiting, and I was so tired of carrying both alone.

"That was me," I said.

"And the thing you fight at night?" He nodded at the boulder, at the rope still looped around my waist, and I realized everyone had always known, the way families know, silently and completely. "What is it?"

"My oath," I said. "To the god who went silent. It wants me to deliver you to the wardens at Salt Gate before the moon turns."

Silence. Brannic's hand had moved to his knife without his permission. Sefa's face was stone.

"But it hasn't," Ilen said.

"Not yet."

"Why not?"

I looked at the fire, at the green ghost of it still clinging to the coals, and told the truth because I was out of everything else. "Because you keep giving me your dreams instead. Because every night you make me remember a country I've never seen, and it turns out a person can't betray a place she remembers." I laughed, and it came out cracked. "You're feeding the wrong wolf, Ilen. Keep it up."

He crossed the cave and took my hand—the roped one, the traitor's hand—and pressed something into it. A pebble, white as the streets, warm as a kitchen.

"So keep remembering," he said. "Both of us. Whatever's coming for us at Salt Gate—we'll meet it remembering."

Outside, far down the valley, a horn sounded. Once. Twice. Then a third time, long and low, and Brannic was on his feet with his face gone gray, because he knew that signal better than anyone alive.

"Wardens," he said. "They've found us."
