## 7. Ambush at the Salt Gate

I have stood in a lot of ambushes in my life, and I'll tell you the secret nobody puts in the training manuals: you almost always know. Not with your head—with your skin. The birds go quiet wrong. The wind stops smelling like anything. Your own shadow feels like it's being watched.

The Salt Gate pass had been telling me for an hour that it wanted to kill us, and I'd been pretending not to hear, because there was no other way through and because hope makes liars of even disgraced wardens.

"Talk to me, Brannic," Mara said, falling into step beside me. She had Ilen on her left like she'd grown him there, and Pip rode double on Sefa's mule up ahead, pretending to be asleep in the way children pretend—eyes shut too tight, listening to everything.

"It's quiet," I said.

"It's always quiet."

"It's quiet *wrong*. No choughs. There should be choughs on this scree by the hundred." I nodded at the black birds that weren't there. "And the snow on the south wall hasn't slumped. Somebody's been up there packing it flat so it won't slide and give them away."

Mara was silent for three steps. Then: "How many?"

"If they're good? Twelve. If they're average, thirty, and we're already dead."

"And which are they?"

"They're mine," I said, before I could stop myself. "They were mine."

She looked at me then—not with pity, which I'd have forgiven, but with something more like accounting. Weighing what I was worth against what I cost. It was fair. It was what I did to myself every morning when I shaved.

"Can you talk them out of it?" she asked.

"I deserted rather than arrest a nine-year-old girl who made flowers bloom out of season. Ser Halvard doesn't talk. He *sentences*." I spat. "But maybe I can buy you the first minute. A minute's a lot, in a pass."

"A minute," Mara said, "is everything or nothing, depending on how you spend it."

That was the sort of thing she said that sounded like a proverb until you realized she meant it as arithmetic.

* * *

We didn't get our minute.

We got about four seconds of it, and then Ser Halvard decided he'd rather not spend them waiting, and the pass lit up with horn-calls from both ends—the trap closing like a book.

They came down off the south wall in white cloaks, which is the cruelest possible camouflage against snow if you're the one being ambushed, because your last thought is always *how beautiful*, right before the crossbow bolts arrive. I saw Halvard at the head of them, older than I remembered, grayer, his warden's oath-knot still tied at his throat with the neatness of a man who has never once broken anything, including himself.

"Brannic Vael!" His voice carried off the rock like a thrown stone. "You know the law. Surrender the gifted one and the child, and the rest walk. That is the mercy of the crown, and I am its hand."

"He always did love the sound of his own mercy," I muttered.

Pip had come off the mule and gone small behind Sefa, which for Pip meant crouched like a cat in a doorway, all elbows and eyes. Ilen stood very still beside Mara, and I watched his gift flicker over him like heat-shimmer—he was doing something to their memories, blurring us, making us hard to see truly. It cost him. I could see it costing him, the way a lamp costs oil.

"Halvard!" I called back. "Twelve years I wore the knot. You taught me the arrest-litany myself. Say it now—say what we swear about the innocent."

"I know what we swore." For just a moment, something moved behind his face, some old furniture shifting in a locked room. "And I know what the crown unswore. The law changed, Brannic. Men don't."

"No," I agreed. "Men don't. That's rather the whole tragedy of us."

Then somebody on the wall got impatient, and the first bolt came down, and the minute was spent whether we liked it or not.

* * *

I will not give you a tidy battle. Battles aren't tidy; they're a fistful of moments dropped on a floor, and you pick up whichever ones survive.

Moment: Sefa screaming without sound, both hands full of burning herbs she'd crushed together, throwing a wall of stinging smoke into the first rank of wardens. They broke formation coughing, and their discipline—that beautiful, terrible discipline—cracked for exactly long enough.

Moment: Mara moving through that smoke like it was made for her, taking a warden's legs out with her staff, not killing, never killing if she could help it, and I understood suddenly that this restraint wasn't softness. It was the oath. Her god had rules, and she was still keeping them, even now, even silent.

Moment: Pip.

I heard the boy before I saw him—a whistle, sharp, the signal we'd agreed on for *found a way out*—and there he was, scrambling up the talus slope toward a gap in the rocks no grown body could have fit through, luck bending around him like water around a stone. Two bolts missed him in ways that made no ballistic sense whatsoever. He was laughing. Gods help us, the child was laughing.

Then a warden came off the wall above him. Not Halvard—young, new, the kind they recruit straight out of the border towns and fill up with fear and call it duty. He had a net, and he dropped it clean, and Pip went down under it with a sound I will hear until I die—not a scream, worse, a *surprise*, the sound of a child learning the world had lied to him about being safe.

"Pip!" Ilen's voice cracked like green wood.

I was forty feet away and every one of them was a mile. I saw the young warden slide down the scree, saw him kneel, saw him reach for the net's binding cord with the mechanical tenderness they train into you—*take them alive, take them alive*—and I saw Pip's luck finally run out, because luck is a loan, not a gift, and children always pay highest interest.

Mara got there first.

I don't know how. Later I would understand that none of us knew how, that she crossed ground no human crosses, but in the moment all I saw was her arriving between the warden and the boy like she'd been teleported by fury itself, staff sweeping the man's knees, tearing the net open with her bare hands while the barbs bit her palms bloody.

And then she did the thing I will think about for the rest of my life, however long the crown allows it to be.

She spoke her god's name. Out loud. In defiance of the silence of six years.

"*Veyra.*"

Nothing. The mountain held its breath. Even the fighting seemed to lean in.

Then the mountain answered.

I have felt earthquakes. I have stood in temple squares during the old rites, back when the gods still bothered to attend. This was neither. This was *attention*—the whole pass suddenly aware of itself, of us, of her, the way a sleeping dog becomes aware of a stranger in the house. The snow on the south wall—the snow somebody had packed flat—came down all at once, not as an avalanche but as a verdict, burying the eastern end of the pass and half the wardens' line in a single white exhalation.

And through it, in a voice that came from everywhere and nowhere and mostly from inside my own skull, something spoke. It did not use words, exactly. It used *certainty*. And the certainty was furious.

*MINE,* it said, meaning Mara. Meaning, I understood a heartbeat later, meaning all of us—meaning Ilen most of all, the flickering lamp of him, the memory-keeper, the key. *SHE IS MINE AND THEY ARE HERS AND YOU WILL NOT.*

The young warden fled. Most of them fled. Halvard stood his ground in the settling snow-dust, white cloak gone gray, staring at Mara with an expression I'd never seen on him—fear, yes, but underneath it, horribly, *recognition*. As if he'd just remembered something he'd been made to forget.

"Mara," Ilen said, and his voice shook. "Mara, your nose."

She was bleeding from both palms and now from her nose, thick and dark, and her eyes had gone somewhere else entirely—somewhere vast and cold and awake. When she spoke, her voice had harmonics in it that a throat shouldn't hold.

"The Door," she said, in that doubled voice. "Six weeks. Thirteen turns. *Bring him home.*"

Then she folded up like a tent with the pole kicked out, and I caught her, and the god was gone as fast as it had come, leaving only blood and silence and a pass full of groaning men.

* * *

We ran. There was no dignity in it. Sefa carried Pip, who was sobbing without any sound at all, which was somehow worse than noise. Ilen took Mara's other side, and between the two of us we got her up the goat track and over the shoulder of the mountain while behind us Halvard's surviving wardens dug their brothers out of the snow.

By full dark we'd made a ruined shepherd's hut, and Mara woke, and the first thing she did was look at her bandaged hands and say, in her own voice again, ordinary and dry:

"How many did I kill?"

"None," I said. "Snow did the work. Maybe a dozen buried, most of them breathing when we left."

"Good." She closed her eyes. "Because I don't think it was finished talking. And I don't think it was talking to them."

Ilen sat across the fire from her, watching her with an expression I couldn't read—hunger and terror in equal measure, the way you look at a door you've wanted your whole life and are afraid to open.

"It called me *key*," he whispered. "Brannic, it knew what I am. My order spent two hundred years looking for the Hollow Door. I thought it was a story we told novices." He looked up, and the firelight moved in his eyes. "It's not a story. And I think—I think my stolen memories aren't lost. I think someone took them *to* the Door."

Mara opened her eyes.

"There's a place," she said slowly, like a woman reading words underwater. "East of here. Ruins. I've smuggled past it a hundred times and never once looked at it. Couldn't. Something wouldn't let me." Her bandaged hands flexed. "I can look at it now."

"What is it?" Pip asked, small and hoarse from the corner where Sefa held him.

Mara smiled, and it was the strangest smile I've ever seen on her—grief and wonder sharing one face.

"Home," she said. "Apparently it's home. And I've never even seen it."

Outside, far to the east, above the black teeth of the mountains, the clouds had parted around a peak no map named, and something up there glowed faintly, patiently, like a lamp left burning in a window for a hundred years—for whoever was finally coming home.
