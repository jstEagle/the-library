## 8. The Country That Wasn't

The capital of Thornfell was a rumor with architecture.

That's the only way I can describe what we found at the end of the smuggler's road: a city that had been *forgotten so thoroughly* it had forgotten itself. Walls stood where walls should stand, but moss had eaten the inscriptions. Streets ran in sensible lines toward gates that no longer knew their own names. And everywhere—everywhere—the silence. Not the silence of emptiness. The silence of a held breath.

I'd like to tell you I recognized it. I'm a memory-keeper; recognition is supposed to be my whole trade. But walking those streets felt less like coming home and more like reading a letter written in my own handwriting that I had no memory of writing.

"You're limping," Sefa signed at me, which was her way of saying *sit down before you fall down*.

"I'm strolling," I signed back. "Dramatically."

Brannic grunted—the universal language of large men who have opinions about your life choices—and shouldered open the doors of what had once been a temple. Or a granary. Or possibly both; gods and grain have always shared real estate in poor countries. The roof had mostly given up, which meant moonlight, which meant I could see the floor was swept clean.

Swept. Recently. In an abandoned capital in an erased country.

"Someone lives here," Pip said, delighted, already checking the corners for anything worth pocketing. Her luck-gift made coins find her the way iron finds lodestone, though she'd deny it under torture and had, once, to a warden who believed her, which is why she was with us.

"No one's lived here for generations," Brannic said. "That's the whole point of the place."

"Then someone *swept*," Pip said, "for generations."

Mara said nothing. She'd said very little since the Salt Gate, since the night she screamed words at the sky and something in the sky screamed back. She stood in the doorway with her hand on her sword hilt like the building might owe her money, and when she finally spoke, her voice came out strange and flat.

"The bell."

We all looked up. There was indeed a bell—a great green thing hanging crooked in its frame above the altar-granary-whatever-this-was, thick with verdigris, its rope long since rotted to a stub.

"It's silent," Mara said. "It's been silent. I've passed shrines my whole life and they've all been—" She stopped. Recalibrated. Started again, quieter. "They've all been *answered*. Until they weren't. A hundred prayers, no answer. I thought my god had died."

"And now?" I asked.

"Now I think," she said, "that something ate him too."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about having your memories stolen: it doesn't feel like loss. It feels like *finished*. Like a book you set down and never picked back up, except you can't remember the title or why you cared. The wardens' memory-eaters don't burn things out—they *tidy*. They leave smooth places where the sharp parts used to be, and you walk around on the smooth places for years, wondering why certain songs make you cry.

But memories are stubborn. They pool in low places. And a memory-keeper's mind is nothing *but* low places—I built my whole gift on the principle that nothing given to me is ever truly gone.

The temple had been a keeper's hall. I knew it the moment I touched the walls, the way you know a handshake. Generations of keepers had pressed their palms to this stone and poured their hoards into it—not erasing themselves, just *banking*, storing the country's memory somewhere the eaters couldn't reach. If any of it remained—

I sat down on the cold floor, put my hands flat against the stone, and opened myself the way I'd been trained: like a door, like a throat, like a wound.

The first thing that came back was bread.

I know how that sounds. But it wasn't just bread—it was the *smell* of a bakery on a street called Vessel Row, at an hour called grey-morning, baked by a woman named Odile who sang off-key and gave the burnt loaves to crows she'd individually named. Vessel Row. Grey-morning. Odile, Ash-Crow, One-Eye, the Bishop. None of these things existed in any language I spoke anymore, because the language itself had been tidied away, and yet here they were, rising out of the stone like drowned things surfacing, and I was crying so hard Sefa put her arms around me and didn't ask questions, because Sefa never wasted a question on something your face could answer.

Then the rest of it came, and I would have traded every tear back to un-know it.

* * *

Thornfell was not conquered. Understand that first, because everything else follows from it like blood from a wound.

Thornfell *surrendered*—or rather, its last queen did, after the war went the way wars go against a larger neighbor, and the terms she negotiated were not land or gold or hostages. The terms were *oblivion*. Every soul in Thornfell would live. Every border would dissolve. And in exchange, the crown's great working—the one their court mages had spent thirty years and a mountain of stolen memories constructing—would erase the country itself. Not the people. The people were absorbed, scattered, re-papered with new histories. But Thornfell, the *idea*, the name, the shape of it on maps and in minds and in the mouths of mothers telling children where Grandma came from—that would be cut out, cleanly, like a tumor.

And the god. You can't erase a country without erasing what holds it together, and what held Thornfell together was a god whose name was—whose name was—

I still can't hold it. It slides. But I could feel its *shape*: a bell-god, a hearth-and-road god, patron of small mercies, which is a phrase I now understand Mara uses deliberately, because she's been praying to it her whole life without knowing its name, because its name was part of what got cut.

The spell needed fuel. Erasure on that scale doesn't run on goodwill. It ran on memories—harvested, hoarded, fed into the working by the crown's eaters for a generation before the war even started. And here is the detail that turned my grief into something colder: the Door, the Hollow Door, the only way out of Thornfell's borders—which persist, you see, even if the country doesn't; spells are lazy about cleaning up their own scaffolding—the Door was built *into* the erasure. A pressure valve. A flaw the last queen insisted on, paid for with the last of her leverage.

The Door opens once a century, on the thirteenth turn of the thirteenth moon. And it opens only for a price, because the queen understood her conquerors: they would never allow a free exit from their perfect forgetting. So the Door demands equivalence. To open a hole in an erasure, you must feed it an erasure of your own. Willing. Specific. A memory-sacrifice, freely given, large enough to balance the weight of a country's absence.

"That's why they want you," Mara said. I hadn't heard her come into the keeper's hall. Night had fallen properly; she stood in the moonlight with her arms crossed, and her face was doing something complicated. "Not dead. Alive. They need a keeper to *maintain* the erasure. Patch the leaks." She paused. "People like me. We're the leaks."

"You remember things you shouldn't," I said. "Roads that aren't on maps. Shrines to a god who doesn't exist. Your order kept the knowledge alive without knowing they were keeping it."

"My order is going to help them kill you for it."

"Yes," I said. "Probably."

She laughed—one short, ugly note—and then sat down beside me against the wall, close enough that our shoulders touched, which from Mara was practically a marriage proposal. We sat in the ruined hall of keepers, in the erased capital of an erased country, and below us I could hear Pip arguing with Brannic about whether a desecrated temple counted as trespassing if the god in question had been legally deleted.

"How much does it need?" she asked eventually. "The sacrifice. Ballpark."

"A lifetime," I said. "Of a specific kind. Not random memories—the Door wants *belonging*. Home. Childhood. The things that root a person to one particular patch of earth." I looked at my hands. "Which is why it can't be me. My roots were the first thing they took. I'm mostly smooth places, Mara. I couldn't power a candle."

"Mm," said Mara, in a voice I was too wrecked to read properly, and I want it on the record that I was too wrecked to read it, because what happened next was not my fault.

* * *

I slept badly, which for me means the dreams came.

Keepers dream sideways. Our sleeping minds sort other people's memories along with our own, and lately mine had been sorting *Mara's*—she sleeps three feet from me and her dreams leak like a cracked jug, and I'd been waking with tastes of her childhood in my mouth: a kitchen, a dog, a woman singing. I'd been trying not to notice. There are ethics about this. Most of them I follow.

But that night the dreams went deeper than leakage. The keeper's hall was full to the rafters with banked memory, and my open, grieving, half-empty mind must have looked like a vacancy. The hall poured itself into me while I slept—all of it, centuries of it, the whole drowned archive of Thornfell—and I woke at grey-morning (Odile's hour, the Bishop's hour) with dirt on my palms and blood in my nose and Brannic shaking me by the shoulders asking if I was dying.

"Better," I croaked. "Worse. Both."

Because now I remembered everything. The war. The surrender. The queen's face as she signed away her country's existence—she was young, younger than Mara, and she cried exactly once, and then never again. I remembered the bell-god's name, or almost, the shape of it sitting on my tongue like a coin I can't spend. I remembered the Door's exact requirements, down to the grammar of its opening. And I remembered something else, something the archive had saved like a knife saved for a specific throat:

The erasure has a seam. Every working does. The crown's mages anchored the spell to a single keystone-memory—the queen's own final act of renunciation, held in trust by the first keeper's order, hidden in this hall. Destroy the keystone and the erasure cracks. The country remembers itself. The god wakes.

The keystone isn't here anymore. The eaters found this place forty years ago. They took it to the capital of the *other* country—the one with the crown and the broadsheets and the hunt—took it into the palace itself, where it sits in their vault of harvested memories, feeding the spell, guarded by everyone we are running from.

So there it was. Two roads out of the story, and both of them terrible: open the Door with a willing sacrifice and flee, leaving the erasure standing and the god mute forever—or go *back*, into the teeth of the hunt, and steal a dead queen's last word out of the crown's own vault.

I told them all of it over breakfast, because they deserved the truth even if the truth was a choice between wounds. Sefa's bone-casts clicked in her fingers like she was counting votes. Pip stopped chewing. Brannic stared into the middle distance with the expression of a man doing arithmetic he hates.

And Mara—Mara set down her cup, and looked at me with an expression I finally, finally read correctly, and said:

"How attached are you all to the idea of me keeping my childhood?"

Outside, far off across the mountains, a horn sounded. Then another. Then a third, answering, closer.

Six days left on the moon. And the wardens had found us.
