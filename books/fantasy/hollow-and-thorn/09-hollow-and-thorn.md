## 9. Hollow and Thorn

The last day of a hundred years should have felt heavier. Instead it felt like any other morning in the ruins of Vael Corath: cold, gray, and smelling faintly of old stone and Sefa's feverfew tea. I sat on the broken steps of what had once been a temple—*our* temple, the god's own house, though I'd only learned that three days ago—and watched the sun come up over a country that officially did not exist.

Somewhere below us in the capital's bones, Ilen was sleeping off another night of remembering. Brannic was sharpening every blade we owned twice over. Pip was supposed to be mending socks and was almost certainly gambling with river stones instead.

And out there, past the shattered gates, the crown had arrived.

I'd counted their fires at dawn from the high wall. Nine of them, arranged in a crescent around the valley mouth like a hand closing. Wardens. Not the ragged checkpoint patrols I'd been bribing for years—real wardens, gray-cloaked, disciplined, with the kind of patience that comes from orders signed by someone whose name you never learn.

"One day," Brannic said, settling beside me on the steps. He didn't look at the fires. He looked at his whetstone, because looking at things directly had never been his way. "Thirteen turns complete tomorrow night."

"I can count."

"You count well. You plan badly." He tested the edge against his thumb. "We could still run. South ridge is unwatched until noon."

"And go where? The Door closes for a century, Brannic. There is no *south* after tomorrow. There's just more Thornfell, forever, with wardens in it." I turned my hands palm-up. The oath-mark on my left wrist—the bell-and-thorn sigil burned into me at nineteen—had been itching for days, like a limb waking up. Like something under the skin stretching after a long sleep. "Besides. I think it's time to stop running toward the exit and start standing in front of it."

Brannic finally looked up. "You've decided something."

"I've decided several things. Most of them are terrible."

"That's usually how your decisions run."

I laughed, which surprised both of us. Then I told him what Ilen had learned in the deep archive, what the god's silence actually was—not abandonment but erasure, a patron deity muted by the same spell that had scraped Thornfell off every map and mind. And what the Door required to open after a century shut: not a key, not a word, but a willing sacrifice of memory. Someone who gave up what anchored them so the Door could remember how to be a door.

"Your home," Brannic said quietly. "Your childhood. Everything before the mercenary life."

"Everything before the mercenary life *is* the mercenary life, mostly. There wasn't much before it." That was a lie and he knew it was a lie, because everyone in our little company knew about Brightwater farm, about the apple tree my father grafted himself, about my mother teaching me letters by scratching them in flour dust. About the reason I'd sworn myself to a silent god in the first place—a grief I'd never said aloud, wrapped in oath-silence the way the god itself was wrapped in spell-silence. "It's mine," I said. "That's the point. It has to be willingly given, and it has to matter."

"There might be another way."

"There's always another way. It's usually worse and arrives too late." I stood, brushing stone dust off my knees. "Get them ready. Everyone through by moonrise tomorrow. I'll hold the gate."

"Mara—"

"Brannic." I put my hand on his shoulder, this enormous gentle wreck of a man who'd thrown away his whole life rather than arrest children. "You deserted one oath already. Don't ask me to desert mine. Ask me to *finish* it differently."

He held my eyes for a long moment. Then he nodded, once, the way soldiers nod, and went down into the city to do as I asked, and I sat back down alone on the steps of my god's ruined house and tried to memorize an apple tree.

* * *

They came at dusk, because of course they did. Wardens love dusk; it flatters their gray cloaks and hides their numbers.

The first warning was Pip, skidding into the hall where we'd made camp, breathless, eyes huge. "They're moving! All of 'em, they're—they've got ladders, Mara, and something else, something big wrapped in canvas on a cart—"

"A ram," Brannic said grimly. "Or worse."

It was worse. When they unwrapped it at the foot of the outer wall, I saw the glint of warded iron and felt the oath-mark on my wrist flare hot enough to make me gasp. A spell-ram. Crown work. Built to crack doors that had been sealed by older magic than theirs.

"They know," Ilen said. He'd come up from the archive, pale as paper, memory-light flickering in his eyes like a lamp low on oil. "They've always known the Door was here. That's why the hunt. They're not just taking the gifted, Mara. They're making sure no one can ever leave."

"Cheerful. Good. Hate is excellent fuel." I pulled my sword and looked around at them—Brannic rolling his shoulders like a man stepping into an old coat, Sefa with her herb-knife and her fierce deaf silence, Pip clutching a sling and a sack of lucky stones, Ilen trembling and upright. My ridiculous, impossible family, assembled by accident across a mountain winter. "Here's how tonight goes. We hold the inner gate until the Door opens. The moment Ilen says it's opening, everyone goes through. No heroics, no goodbyes longer than two words. *Everyone.*"

"And you?" Pip asked, in the small voice she used when she already knew the answer.

"I'm the guide," I said. "Guiding people out of Thornfell is the only job I've ever been any good at."

The first ladder hit the wall before I finished speaking.

* * *

I have fought in maybe forty battles, and I'll tell you the truth about them: most of battle is noise and waiting, punctuated by moments of terrible clarity. The clarity came when the spell-ram struck the outer gate and the sound went through the stone like a bell through water—and my oath-mark answered it.

Heat crawled up my arm. And then, for the first time in seven years, my god spoke.

Not in words. In *pull*. The same drag I'd felt in chapter and verse when the order demanded Ilen's surrender, the same teeth the oath had sunk into me—but aimed outward now, toward the wall, toward the fight, and underneath it something vast turning over in its sleep, like a whale rising from the deep, furious at the harpoon.

*You kept faith,* it seemed to say. *When I could not answer. You kept faith.*

"Don't thank me yet," I snarled at the air, parrying a warden off the ladder-top and kicking him into the dark. "You want faith? Open your own damn door!"

The ram struck again. The outer gate split.

What followed was an hour of holding the inner courtyard by inches—Brannic a boulder in the breach, Sefa's smoke and stinging powders bleeding through the warden ranks, Pip's luck bending arrows wide in ways that made hardened killers mutter prayers. Ilen stood at the center of it all with his hands pressed flat to the ancient doorframe of the Hollow Door itself, feeding it memories, and I watched him flicker like a candle in wind, giving everything he had and it wasn't enough. The Door glowed faintly, sullenly, a seam of light in the mountain's heart that would not widen.

"It needs more!" he shouted. "It needs a *sacrifice*, a real one, willing—Mara, I can't remember enough, they ate too much of me—"

"I know." I cut down the last warden on the courtyard stair and turned my back on the battle, which is either madness or revelation depending on how it ends. I walked to the Door. I pressed my marked wrist against the stone.

"I'm the willing part," I said. "Take it. Take the farm. Take the apple tree. Take my mother's voice saying my name, take my father's hands grafting branches, take the flour-dust letters, take the grief that made me swear the oath—take all of it, every year before the road. Wake up. Open. Let them go."

The god rose.

I felt it happen the way you feel a dam break—not the water first but the ground shaking, the deep structural groan of something held too long finally letting go. Light poured up through the mountain like dawn arriving from below. The spell that had muted a god for a century met the one thing it couldn't digest: a freely given past, offered without bargaining, by the last sworn servant of the thing it had tried to bury.

My memories went out of me like heat from an open hand. I didn't see them leave. I just felt the cold arrive where they'd been, room after room going dark in the house of myself, and I stood in the dark and kept my wrist against the stone because that was the oath now, the new one, the one I was writing with my own emptying self.

The Hollow Door opened.

Not a door—a *sunrise*, standing upright, a wall of white-gold light splitting the mountain's heart, and through it I glimpsed somewhere green, somewhere unburned, somewhere the crown's long arm couldn't reach. The wardens in the courtyard fell back from it shrieking, their gray courage dissolving, because some things you cannot look at and keep your orders.

"GO!" I roared, and my voice came out doubled, mine and something vaster speaking through it. "ALL OF YOU—NOW!"

Brannic grabbed Sefa. Pip ran, hesitated, ran. Ilen stood at the threshold, tears cutting through the ash on his face, looking back at me with the expression of a man being handed a gift and a wound in the same instant.

"Mara—" he started.

"Two words maximum. I said no long goodbyes."

"*Thank you* isn't goodbye."

"Then say it inside, where I can't hear it." I shoved him gently, and he stumbled through the light, and the light took him, and the company of small mercies passed out of Thornfell forever while I stood in the doorway of a country only I remembered, holding it open with a past I could no longer name.

Behind me, the last of the wardens regrouped. Ahead of me, the Door began its slow, century-long closing.

And in the sudden quiet of my emptied mind, my god spoke one final time—clearly, in words, in a voice like bells underwater:

*"The Door needs a keeper, daughter. Stay, and be remembered—or pass through, and forget."*

I looked back at the light. I looked at the wardens closing in.

I stepped forward to meet them.
