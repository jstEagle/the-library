## 10. The Keeper of Names

The Door did not look like a door. It looked like a wound in the mountain that had decided, at the last possible moment, to forgive.

Light poured out of it—not the white of wardens' lanterns or the cold blue of bone-casts, but a warm gold like the light through honey, like the last hour of a summer evening that refuses to end. It filled the great hollowed hall of the old capital, gilded the broken columns, and turned the dust in the air into drifting sparks. Somewhere behind us, down the long throat of the mountain, I could hear the wardens still hammering at the Salt Gate they'd never breach in time. The god had seen to that. My god, who was silent no longer, and who had made his opinion of the crown very loudly known with an avalanche and, I'm told, a certain amount of screaming rock.

I stood at the edge of the light with nothing left in my head but the present tense.

That's not quite true. That's the wry lie I tell to keep from weeping. What I actually had was this: the smell of stone and snowmelt. The ache in my shoulder where a crossbow bolt had kissed me two days ago. The memory of the last six weeks, whole and bright, because those hadn't been mine to give. Everything else—my mother's kitchen, my father's laugh, the village of Brightwater with its crooked bell tower, the river I'd learned to swim in, my own name before *Mara* became just a sound other people used—I had poured into the Door like wine into a thirsty mouth. And the Door had drunk, and opened, and the god had woken inside it like a man rising from a hundred-year nap, furious and grateful and enormous.

"Go on, then," I said to the others, because someone had to say it, and apparently the role of Person Who Says Terrible Things Briskly had fallen to me by default. "Before I change my mind and start charging tolls."

Pip went first, of course. Pip went everywhere first; caution was for people whose luck wasn't a faint golden shimmer you could almost see if you squinted. She stopped at the edge of the light, though, and looked back at me with her chin up in that way she had, the way that said she was twelve years old and would be running the entire smuggling coast by fifteen.

"You'll write to me," she said. It was not a question. With Pip, nothing ever was.

"The post between worlds is unreliable," I said. "But I'll put letters under stones. You'll find them someday, when you're old and famous and insufferable."

She grinned, wet-eyed, and threw herself into the light, and the light took her the way water takes a dropped coin—quickly, completely, without cruelty.

Sefa came next. She took my hands in hers, these hands that knew every herb and every bone-cast, and pressed them once against her own cheek so I could feel what she couldn't say: the tremor in her jaw, the breath she was holding. Then she signed, slow enough even my untrained eyes could follow. *Stay. Remember. Live.* Three words, and the third one held the weight of the other two.

"I intend to be insufferably alive," I told her. "It's the only revenge worth having."

She smiled, kissed her fingers, touched them to my forehead like a blessing or a benediction or simply goodbye, and walked into the gold.

Brannic hesitated longest, which surprised no one who knew him. A man who'd spent his life guarding doors does not easily walk through one. He looked back at the dark passage where the wardens were, then at me, and his big ruined face did something complicated.

"There should be two of you," he said. "Guarding. It's bad tactics, one guardian."

"It's excellent tactics," I said. "No one expects the lone keeper. Ask any ballad."

"Mara." He said my name carefully, the way you set down something breakable. "What I did at the Salt Gate—"

"You deserted rather than arrest children, and then you bled for a country that isn't on any map. If that needs forgiving, I've got a list of my own sins twice as long, and none of them half as pretty." I shoved his shoulder, gently. "Go. Be somebody's legend."

He went. He didn't look back, which was how I knew he was crying.

* * *

Which left Ilen.

He stood at the threshold with the light washing over him, and he looked—restored. Whole. The stolen memories had come home to him in the ruins of the capital, flooding back over three days while the wardens gathered, and the man who emerged from that flood was taller somehow, or stood taller, his gift burning steady instead of guttering. The memory-keeper of a country that had been erased, carrying ten thousand years of Thornfell inside him like coals carried in cupped hands.

And he was looking at me the way he'd been looking at me since the night he'd half-dreamed his memories into my head and shown me the mountains green and the capital crowned and the god's bell ringing across a living land. Since love arrived exactly when duty turned predatory, which is either terrible timing or the only timing there is.

"You could come through," he said. "The Door knows your sacrifice. It would carry you."

"The Door carries people who have somewhere to *be*," I said. "I gave my somewhere away. All of it. Every road out of Brightwater, every face." I shrugged, and it cost more than the shrug appeared to. "Besides. Someone has to stay and make sure this thing doesn't get erased twice. The god can't write. Have you noticed? Enormous ancient deity, no opposable thumbs."

"Mara."

"Don't," I said. "If you say something noble I'll cry, and I have a reputation as a hard-bitten mercenary to maintain, and also I appear to have already spent all my tears on the child."

So he didn't say anything noble. He did something better. He stepped close, took my face in his hands—the same gesture Sefa had made, but different, warmer, a coal passed skin to skin—and he closed his eyes, and I felt his gift move.

It was small. Deliberately small. One memory, lifted out of everything I'd given, and returned.

His face. Not the abstract knowledge that Ilen existed—I still had that—but the *memory*: the first time I saw him, half-frozen above the treeline, frost in his black hair, his eyes opening and finding mine with that flickering lamp-light in them. And layered beneath it, folded like letters: our fire in the smuggler's cave, Pip asleep against Brannic's boot, Sefa laughing her silent laugh, the whole company of small mercies lit orange against the dark. And beneath that, written in the deep place where meaning lives:

*Stay remembered.*

"That's cheating," I whispered.

"That's keeping," he said. "It's what I do." His thumb traced my cheekbone, catching the tear I'd failed to prevent. "You gave a country its past, Mara Brightwater. Let one man give you back a fire."

"If you weren't walking into another world," I said, "I'd kiss you."

"So kiss me," he said, "and then I'll walk into another world. I've survived worse odds."

I did. It lasted exactly as long as it needed to and not one second less, and when I let go, he stepped backward into the light without breaking my gaze, and the light rose around him like water closing over a swimmer, and then the hall was empty, and the Door stood open, humming, patient, waiting for whoever might someday find their way to it.

I sat down on a fallen column and cried for a while. A mercenary's entitled to that much.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about losing your past: you don't feel hollow. You feel *light*. Like a pack taken off after a long march, except the pack was your childhood and you keep reaching for straps that aren't there. I know I had a mother. I know I loved her—I can feel the shape of the love, the way you can feel the shape of a hand in a glove. But the hand itself is gone, given gladly, spent on a door. Some mornings I catch myself turning toward a river that isn't there, toward a bell tower I can describe perfectly and cannot picture.

Ilen's fire helps. Every night I build one, and every night the memory returns: the cave, the company, the orange light. *Stay remembered.* He built me a hearth out of himself and left it burning in my chest. It's more than most people get. It's certainly more than I deserve, which has never once stopped me accepting good things.

The god helps too, in his fashion. He speaks now—not in words, exactly, but in weather and bells and the occasional extremely pointed landslide. He is Thornfell's forgotten patron, awake again inside his mountain, and I am his keeper, his priest, his sole remaining congregation of one. We get along the way an old mercenary and an old god get along: with mutual respect, frequent complaints, and an understanding that neither of us is going anywhere.

My work is simple and endless. The crown erased Thornfell from maps and memory once. They will try again—they always try again—and the spell that ate a country is still out there in the world, hungry, patient, eating memories from afar. So I walk the borders of the hollow land, and I write.

I write the name on stones. On cairns above the treeline. On the walls of the watch-shrine where I found a dying stranger and made the worst best decision of my life. On the lintels of burned houses in Cinderhollow, so the gifted dead are remembered too. Old script, the script Ilen taught me in the last days, the letters that predate the erasure and therefore cannot be eaten by it. *THORNFELL*, cut deep, again and again, until my hands know the strokes better than my mind knows anything.

A country cannot be erased twice if one person stubbornly keeps writing it down.

Today I am high on the eastern ridge, above the switchbacks where it began, chiseling the name into a granite face the size of a door. The wind smells of snow coming. Below me the hollow land stretches green and secret, and somewhere in its heart the Door hums its warm gold hum, waiting.

I finish the last stroke. I sit back on my heels. And that's when I see it—a small shape picking its way up the switchback below, hooded, alone, moving with the careful exhaustion of someone who has walked a very long way from somewhere very far.

The wind shifts. The shape stops. Pulls back its hood.

Black hair. Frost-pale eyes. A face I gave away everything to keep, standing in the falling snow with a travel-worn pack and an expression that says he has crossed a world and intends to argue about it.

"You're supposed to be remembering me," Ilen calls up, "not haunting me."

The chisel slips in my hand. The god, somewhere below, rings a bell that hasn't rung in a hundred years, and it sounds suspiciously like laughter.
