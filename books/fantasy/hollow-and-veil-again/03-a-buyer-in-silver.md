## 3. A Buyer in Silver

The message came the way bad news always came to Sable Quintana: wrapped in something beautiful.

It arrived at the Saltglass, the dockside tavern where Sable kept a room under the name *Maren Voss*—a joke they'd allowed themselves once and now couldn't shake, because the landlady had embroidered it on their door and correcting her would mean questions. The message was delivered by a child of perhaps nine, barefoot on rain-slick cobbles, who pressed an envelope into Sable's hand with the solemnity of someone paid well not to remember the face of the person who'd hired them.

The envelope was heavy paper, cream-white, sealed with silver wax. Not stamped—a seal pressed by hand, a crest Sable didn't recognize: a tower split by lightning, crowned.

Inside, a single line in a fine, slanted hand:

*The Gilded Meridian, third bell after dusk. Come alone. Come willing. A matter of maps.*

Sable read it three times by the light of the tavern's guttering lamp, while around them the Saltglass roared with its evening congregation—dockhands arguing over dice, a fishwife weeping into her fourth cup, two off-duty harbor guards laughing too loudly about nothing. No one looked at Sable. No one ever did; that was the point of Maren Voss, a name as gray and forgettable as weather.

But the envelope was not forgettable. The paper alone cost more than Sable's room for a month. And the hand that had written *a matter of maps* wrote like someone accustomed to being obeyed.

They should have burned it. They told themselves they would burn it, that night, holding it over the candle until the corner began to curl and brown—and then they thought of the map beneath the floorboard under their cot, wrapped in oilcloth, bloodstained at one corner where a dying courier's fingers had gripped it, and they blew out the candle instead.

Cowardice, they told themselves, was just caution wearing its evening clothes.

* * *

The Gilded Meridian stood on Caelport's high terrace, where the city stopped smelling of fish and started smelling of money. It was the kind of establishment that didn't have a sign, because people who belonged there already knew where it was, and people who didn't were gently but permanently discouraged at the door.

Sable went anyway. Not because they wanted to sell—they had spent two days telling themselves they hadn't decided—but because a summons like that was a hook, and hooks only grew barbed with time. Better to walk toward the thing than wait for it to walk toward you. That was smuggler's arithmetic. It had kept them alive through eleven years of erased names and forged papers.

The doorman took their wet cloak without being asked, which meant he knew who they were before they gave a name. That was the first wrong note. The second was the corridor: long, paneled in dark wood, lit by lamps turned low, arranged so that anyone walking it could be seen from three doorways and could see no one. Sable counted exits out of habit. There was one, behind them, and it felt less like a door than a mouth.

The third doorway opened onto a private dining room, small and warm, dominated by a table set for two.

The man rising from behind it was silver from crown to boot-heel. Silver hair swept back from a face too smooth for its age, silver thread glinting in a charcoal doublet cut in the capital fashion, a silver ring on each finger except the one that mattered—he wore no signet, which itself was a statement. Men who served Malvane wore no crests. Crests could be traced. Men like this preferred to be untraceable, which meant they were either very valuable or very deniable, and Sable had met enough of both to know the difference rarely mattered to the corpse.

"Maren Voss," the man said, and his voice was pleasant the way a well-oiled lock is pleasant. "Or should I say—no, let's keep the courtesy. Names are gifts, and I haven't earned yours yet." He gestured to the chair opposite his. "Please. The eels are genuinely excellent, and I say that as a man who has eaten eels in every port between here and the Ashen Straits."

"I ate," Sable said.

"Of course you did." He sat when they sat, folding himself down with the ease of long practice. "Suspicion digests poorly. I admire it." He poured wine into both glasses without asking—one motion, practiced, the bottle's label turned discreetly away. "Call me Master Ferren. Or don't call me anything. Most of my acquaintances prefer letters."

"What's the matter of maps?"

Ferren smiled, delighted, as if Sable had performed a trick. "Direct. Good. I'll be direct back, since directness is apparently the currency tonight." He lifted his glass, considered it, set it down untouched. "Somewhere in this city—perhaps in this very district, perhaps within a few hundred yards of this table—is a map. A route through Thornfell. Carried by a courier who entered Caelport eleven days ago and has not been seen since. My employer wants it."

"And your employer is?"

"My employer," Ferren said mildly, "is a person who pays for things. You'll find, if we do business, that I am scrupulous about my employer's anonymity. It's a professional courtesy I extend even to myself."

Sable leaned back. The chair was real walnut; they could feel the grain through their coat. Everything in this room was designed to make a body feel wealthy by proximity. "You've got the wrong smuggler. I move cargo. Cloth, brandy, occasionally a person who needs to not exist for a while. I don't deal in paper."

"No," Ferren agreed pleasantly. "You deal in routes. Everyone knows it—or everyone who matters. You got cargo through the Saltvein Pass during the embargo when half the guilds swore it was impossible. You know Thornfell better than any living guide, including the ones my employer employs, two of whom died there last spring learning exactly how much better." He laced his fingers. "So here is what puzzles me, Maren. A courier dies in your district—badly, messily, in the rain, where anyone might find them—and within a day, Maren Voss, who never takes commissions in winter, who hasn't left Caelport in four months, begins asking quiet questions about mountain passes. Coincidence is such an ugly word. I prefer *convergence*."

Sable kept their face still. Somewhere below their ribs, their heart had begun a slow, cold drumbeat. *He doesn't have proof,* they told themselves. *He's fishing.* But Ferren fished the way trawlers fished, dragging nets wide enough that anything in the water came up whether it wanted to or not.

"You've been watching me."

"I've been watching everyone," Ferren said, with genuine warmth. "It's the whole of my profession. Watching. Most people are so wonderfully predictable. They eat when hungry, lie when frightened, and sell when the price is right." He reached into his coat—slowly, theatrically, letting Sable see every inch of the motion—and produced a slim ledger bound in gray leather. He laid it on the table between them but did not open it. "Now. The offer."

"I haven't agreed there's anything to buy."

"Of course not. But indulge me, because the number is the interesting part." He tapped the ledger once. "Two thousand crowns."

The number landed in the room like a dropped chandelier. Two thousand crowns was a ship. Two thousand crowns was twenty years of smuggling, ten lifetimes of honest work, a new name in a new country with enough gold beneath it to never look back. Sable had risked drowning, hanging, and worse for sums that wouldn't buy the wine Ferren hadn't drunk.

"For a piece of paper," Sable said.

"For a *route*," Ferren corrected. "Paper is incidental. My employer can acquire paper. What my employer cannot acquire is the knowledge of how to survive what the paper describes—and that, I'm told, you also possess. Which brings us to the second part of the offer, the part I suspect will interest you more." He smiled again, and this time the smile had edges. "Guide the expedition yourself. Same fee, doubled on completion. You'd be rich beyond the dreams of your landlady, and safely out of a kingdom that, between us, is becoming an unhealthy place for people with your... history."

"What history?"

"Maren." Ferren's voice softened into something almost kind. "Let's not spend the evening pretending. You want to know why the price is two thousand crowns instead of two hundred? Because my employer isn't merely buying a map. My employer is buying certainty. And certainty, at present, is expensive—because the witch-hunters are in the city."

The word fell between them and neither moved.

"Witch-hunters," Sable repeated.

"Four arrived last week. Quietly, in merchant dress, though one of them has a face you'd remember—the woman, tall, gray-eyed, carries herself like a drawn bow. They're hunting something specific. Not smugglers—not tonight, at least. They're hunting the bearer of a certain document." Ferren finally opened the ledger, turned it around, and slid it across the table. "This is a list of couriers who passed through the eastern stations in the past month. Nine names. Seven are crossed out."

Seven lines struck through in red ink, neat as accounting. Sable looked at them and understood, with the slow dread of ice forming, that those weren't cancellations. Those were deaths. Seven couriers dead in a month, all carrying pieces of the same secret, all funneled toward the same route—and the eighth name on the list was written in a hand Sable recognized, because they'd watched it scrawl *the Door* against a courier's own chest in the rain while the life drained out of him.

"The ninth name," Ferren said quietly, "is the one my employer wants found first. Before the hunters do. Because the hunters, Maren, don't buy. They take. And they burn everything they take—including, historically, the buildings the taking happens in, and occasionally the neighborhoods." He closed the ledger. "My employer offers wealth. The hunters offer ash. I mention this not as a threat—I never threaten—but as context. Neutrality is a lovely country. Unfortunately, it has recently been annexed."

Sable stood. Slowly, deliberately, the way you stand when you want a room full of eyes to know you're not running. "I'll consider it."

"Consider quickly." Ferren remained seated, hands folded, entirely unbothered. "Three days. After that, my employer assumes the item is lost, and the hunters assume it's found, and both assumptions end badly for whoever holds it. Oh—" he raised one finger, almost playful, "—and Maren? If you're thinking of selling to someone else, or running, or burning it: the hunters have a way of knowing. Callers always leave traces. Weather remembers them."

The words went through Sable like a blade finding the gap in armor. *Weather remembers them.* They hadn't said a word about storms. Hadn't given him anything. And still he'd said it—lightly, idly, the way a man tests a floorboard to hear if it's rotten.

They walked out through the low-lit corridor, past the doorman who returned their cloak with a small bow, out into the rain-washed night where Caelport smelled honestly of salt and rot again. Their hands were steady. They noticed that their hands were steady and hated how much effort the steadiness was costing.

Behind them, in the warm room, Master Ferren finally drank his wine, and smiled at the empty chair, and made a small mark in his ledger beside a name.

* * *

Sable didn't go home. Home was a floorboard and a cot and a window facing the alley, and if anyone was watching the building—and someone was watching, Sable would have bet their other name on it—then walking straight there was the same as drawing them a map. Instead they walked. Down through the terraces, past the fish market shuttered for the night, along the seawall where the wind came off the water hard enough to make thinking difficult, which was sometimes the point.

*Weather remembers them.*

Eleven years, and the past had finally caught up—not as memory, which could be outrun, but as a price tag. Two thousand crowns for the map. Doubled for the guide. And underneath both numbers, unspoken but load-bearing: *or we stop needing you to agree.*

They stopped at the seawall rail and stared out at the black water. Somewhere out there, past the horizon, lay the free cities—real ones, unannexed, where a person could wake up and not calculate exits. Two thousand crowns bought that. Two thousand crowns bought a boat and a new name and a coastline with no Ferrens on it.

That was the arithmetic they'd carried out of the tavern district, and somewhere along the seawall it had come apart in their hands.

Because Ferren hadn't said *we'll kill you if you refuse*. Ferren had said something worse. He'd said *you're already dead, the only question is which way*, and dressed it in courtesy. Sell the map, and Sable guided Malvane's expedition through Thornfell—into whatever waited at the end of that bloodstained route, whatever the dying courier had spent his last breath trying to deliver. Refuse, and the witch-hunters came, and witch-hunters didn't knock. And either way, either way, the map ended up in the hands of the man who had erased a kingdom so thoroughly that children in Caelport learned its name only as a curse.

*The Door.* The courier's whisper rose up out of the rain-memory. *It's still there. Tell them it's still there.*

Tell *them*. Not tell Malvane. Not tell the buyers. *Them.* Whoever *them* was—a kingdom with no name, a resistance with no banner, a dead man's faith pressed into a stranger's hands because strangers were all he had left.

Sable gripped the rail. Far out, lightning flickered inside a cloud bank—silent, distant, a storm talking to itself over open water. Something old and half-buried in Sable's chest stirred toward it, the way a scar aches before rain, and they strangled the feeling the way they'd strangled it for eleven years. Not tonight. Not for a silver-tongued man in a warm room. Not for anyone.

But the illusion was gone, and they knew it. There was no neutral ground anymore. There hadn't been since the rain, since the bloodstained map, since the moment they'd chosen not to burn it. Every hour they held it, they were choosing. Every hour they didn't sell it, they were choosing. The map wasn't cargo. It had never been cargo. It was a question, asked in a dead man's blood, and Ferren's two thousand crowns was just the sound of the wrong answer.

Sable pushed off the rail and turned toward the city, toward the crooked streets and the watching windows and the floorboard under the cot.

Three days. Ferren had given them three days.

They intended to use two of them deciding—and the third getting out of Caelport before anyone, buyer or hunter, learned what they'd chosen.

They were halfway down the seawall steps when they saw the cat.

A small gray thing, soaked through, sitting precisely in the middle of the lantern-lit lane outside Sable's lodging house—as if placed there. As if waiting. Across the street, half-shadowed in a doorway opposite, a figure stood perfectly still, watching the window of Sable's room with the patience of someone being paid by the hour.

The cat looked up at Sable and yowled, once, indignant.

And the figure in the doorway turned their head—and looked directly at Sable, across forty feet of rainy lamplight, without pretense, without apology.

Sable's hand found the knife at their belt.

The figure didn't move. Didn't reach for a weapon. Just watched, rain streaming off the hood's edge, and made no attempt whatsoever to hide the fact of their watching—which, in Sable's experience, was either the most honest thing a spy could do, or the most dangerous.

The lantern above flickered. Somewhere inland, thunder muttered, soft and far, like something clearing its throat.

Sable picked up the cat, climbed the stairs to their room, and did not sleep.
