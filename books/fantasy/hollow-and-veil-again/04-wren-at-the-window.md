## 4. Wren at the Window

The rain had been falling on Caelport for three days, and Wren had been watching the window across the street for two of them.

It was a good window to watch from. The garret above the chandler's shop on Saltspray Lane had a dormer angled just so, a crack in the shutter wide enough for one eye, and a smell of tallow that masked everything else—tobacco, nerves, the particular sourness of a person who had not changed their clothes since Tuesday. Below, the lane ran slick and silver with rain, lamplight swimming in the puddles like drowned coins. And across the street, behind glass fogged with the warmth of a cheap stove, Sable Quintana sat at a table with a candle and a map, and did not know they were being watched.

Or perhaps they did. That was the trouble with Quintana. Wren had been following them for six days now, from the harbor taverns to the pawnbroker's to this narrow lodging over a fishmonger's, and in six days Wren had not once caught them doing anything a spy could use. No furtive glances. No coded signals. No meetings with buyers or brokers or anyone at all. They walked the city like a person who owned it and owed it nothing, shoulders loose under a salt-stiff coat, and twice they had doubled back through the fish market so cleanly that Wren had lost them for an hour among the barrels and the screaming gulls—and both times, when Wren found them again, Quintana was simply standing there, watching the water, as if patience were a thing you could practice in public without shame.

*Retrieve the map,* the orders said. *Or kill its holder.* The words were written in Ash's neat, merciless hand, folded into a hollow coin that currently sat against Wren's ribs like a second heart. *The bearer is compromised. The route must not reach Malvane. You have until the tide turns.*

Wren had read the orders eleven times. Eleven times, and each time the same question rose like a bubble through dark water: *what if the holder gives it up freely?* Because that was the thing no one at the resistance had planned for—the possibility that Quintana might not be a courier at all, might be nothing but a smuggler who'd stumbled into something too large, someone who would hand the map to the first buyer with enough silver and walk away whistling. In which case there was nothing to retrieve and no one who needed killing, and Wren could go home to Ash and say the work was done, and sleep, eventually, without dreaming of knives.

Except.

Except that tonight, for the third night running, Sable Quintana was feeding cats.

Wren leaned closer to the shutter crack, breath fogging the wood. Below, in the alley mouth beside the fishmonger's, three cats had gathered—a torn-eared gray, an enormous orange tom with the bearing of a deposed king, and a small black scrap of a thing that flinched from its own shadow. Quintana appeared in the doorway with a saucer, crouched, and set it down at arm's length from the wall. Fish scraps, by the look of them. Good ones. Not the heads and tails a body would throw away, but the pale sweet flesh near the bone, the part that cost money even in Caelport, where fish was cheaper than bread.

The orange tom approached first, tail high, insolent. Quintana held perfectly still, the way one holds still around wild things, and Wren found their own breath slowing to match, as if stillness could be passed through glass and rain and thirty feet of cold air.

"You're spending your last coin on cats," Wren murmured to the empty room. "You absolute fool."

Because that was the other thing Wren had learned in six days of watching: Quintana was nearly out of money. Wren knew the signs the way a physician knows fever—the pawned knife (a good one, river-steel, gone to Marlow's on the second day), the meals that had shrunk from tavern stew to bread and oil, the boots resoled with rope. A person with a fortune sewn into their coat does not eat like that. Which meant the map was the only wealth Quintana carried, and every day they didn't sell it was a day they chose hunger over gold, and Wren could not make the arithmetic come out right no matter how they turned it.

Smugglers sold. Smugglers always sold. Wren had grown up among the harbor kind, had watched men betray their own mothers for less than what that map was worth, had learned before they were ten that everyone had a price and the wise spy was the one who found it early. It was the first lesson Ash had ever taught them, back when Ash was still called something softer: *Everyone has a price, little wren. Your work is only to learn what currency they trade in.*

So what currency did Sable Quintana trade in?

Not silver—that much was plain. Not safety, because a person who wanted safety would have burned the map the hour the witch-hunters came to Caelport, and instead Quintana kept it, slept with it, Wren would wager, under their pillow like a child with a stolen sweet. Not revenge; there was no heat in them, none of the coiled fury that made vengeance-seekers so easy to play. They moved through the world like someone carrying a weight they had long ago stopped trying to put down.

Wren watched them crouch in the rain, watching the cats eat, and felt something in their chest shift sideways, small and treacherous, like a plank giving way.

*Stop it,* they told themselves. *You've watched marks feed beggars before. You've watched them weep over dead friends and then sell their locations by morning. Tenderness is a costume. Everyone wears it when it fits.*

But the thought rang false, and Wren knew why. Because Wren had spent six days learning the shape of this particular stranger, and the shape did not lie. It was in the way Quintana bartered at the chandler's—haggling hard, yes, but never cheating, never shorting the measure, once pointing out to the shopkeeper that she'd given change for a copper too many. It was in the way they stepped around the drunk who slept beneath the stairs, quiet as a thief, rather than waking him. It was in the small economy of their movements, the way they took up exactly as much space as they needed and no more, the way of a person who had learned somewhere that taking more than your share ended in fire.

Wren knew about fire. Knew about taking more than your share. The knowledge sat where the hollow coin sat, against the ribs, and did not warm them.

* * *

On the fourth night, the buyer came.

Wren saw him from two streets off, and knew him instantly, the way one knows a species of snake. Silver everywhere—silver buckles, silver rings, a silver-headed cane he did not need, all of it polished to a shine that said *I want to be seen spending.* His name, if he had one that mattered, was Corvess, and he bought secrets for Malvane the way other men bought wine: generously, publicly, and with a smile that never reached the eyes. Three informers Wren had known had sold to Corvess. Two had prospered. The third had been found in the harbor with his pockets full of silver and his lungs full of seawater, because Corvess paid twice—once for the secret, and once to be sure the seller could never sell it again.

He climbed the stairs to Quintana's lodging at half past nine, and Wren went over the roofs.

It was not difficult. Caelport's rooftops were a second city, one Wren had walked since childhood, and the chandler's garret backed onto a warehouse whose gutters made a ladder of sorts. Wren went up the drainpipe, along the ridge tiles slick with rain, and flattened against the slates beside the dormer window, close enough to hear voices through the glass if the wind cooperated. Rain drummed on their shoulders. Cold seeped through their coat. They did not move.

Through the fogged pane, shapes. Quintana rising from the table. Corvess settling into the offered chair uninvited, cane across his knees, silver catching candlelight. The murmur of talk, too low to catch words—then louder, Corvess's voice rising with the confidence of a man accustomed to being answered.

"—name your figure," he was saying, and the wind carried it clear as a bell. "Five hundred crowns. Gold. Tonight."

Silence. Long enough that Wren counted their own heartbeats against it. Seven. Eight. Nine.

Then Quintana's voice, low and even, with something underneath it that Wren had not heard before—not fear, not greed. Something older and wearier.

"Who do you work for?"

"Does it matter? Five hundred crowns buys a great deal of forgetting."

"It matters." A chair scraped. "Because I've heard that number before. From men wearing your master's colors. And the ones who took it—" a pause, thin as a blade, "—they're not alive to spend it. So here's my counter-offer, friend. You leave by that door, and I don't carve my name anywhere you'll find it."

Wren closed their eyes. *Fool,* they thought, and could not tell which of them they meant. Quintana, for refusing five hundred crowns—for refusing, in one breath, enough gold to vanish across the sea and live like a lord. Or Wren themselves, for the hot, unreasonable flare of pride that lit in their chest at the refusal, pride in a stranger they had been sent to rob or kill.

Corvess laughed. It was not a kind sound. "You misunderstand your position, Quintana. The offer was courtesy. The hunt is policy. There are four of my colleagues in this city already, and they are not courteous. Sell to me, and I protect what I buy. Refuse, and—" he spread his hands, silver glinting, "—well. The sea takes so many people here. Accidents. Weather."

"I've weathered worse weather than you."

"Have you?" Something shifted in Corvess's tone, a silk sliding off a blade. "They say the storm-callers are all dead. Burned or bound or fled beyond the veil. But my colleagues say otherwise. My colleagues say there's a caller in Caelport who walks through thunderstorms without getting wet."

The rain filled the silence. Wren lay against the wet slates and did not breathe.

"That's a story," Quintana said at last, and the voice was steady, steady, steady—but Wren had spent six days learning its music, and underneath the steadiness was a note they recognized, a single cracked string. Grief. Old grief, worn smooth as sea glass. "People tell stories about smugglers. It's how they avoid paying us."

"Mm," said Corvess. He rose; the candle guttered with the movement of displaced air. "The offer stands until the tide turns. After that, others will call on you. Less polite ones. Do think of the cats, Quintana." A pause, and Wren could hear the smile in it. "Yes, I know about the cats. I know about the honest bartering, the pawned knife, the rope-soled boots. I have known about you for six days. The difference between us is that I know what you'll do next, and you don't."

Boots on the stairs. The door below opening onto rain. Then quiet, except the drumming on the slates and, faintly, the cats in the alley complaining at the interruption of their supper.

Wren stayed on the roof a long time, watching the window. Quintana did not light another candle. They stood at the table a while, a dark shape against the darker room, one hand flat on the map Wren could not see. Then they moved to the window itself and stood looking out—at the rain, at the alley, at the darkness where Corvess had gone—and though they could not possibly have seen Wren against the wet slate, though no eye could pierce rain and roof-gloom at thirty feet, Wren felt the gaze pass over them like a searchlight, slow and thorough, and move on.

*They know someone's watching,* Wren thought. *Maybe they've known all week.*

And still they fed the cats. Still they gave the chandler her extra copper. Still they ate bread and oil and slept alone in a cold room with a fortune under their pillow and death climbing the stairs.

*Everyone has a price,* Ash had taught them. But what if the price wasn't silver? What if Sable Quintana's price was something no buyer in Malvane's employ could pay—something like absolution, or rest, or a door out of a burning country? What if the map wasn't merchandise at all, but a wound, and Quintana carried it the way one carries a wound, because putting it down meant admitting it had happened?

Wren slid down the drainpipe into the alley, landing soft as the name they wore, and stood in the dark listening to the rain.

The tide would turn within the hour. The orders were explicit. *Retrieve the map or kill its holder.* Six days ago those words had been simple, a task like any task, and Wren had recited them like a prayer against doubt. Now they sat in Wren's mouth like stones.

Because here was the truth Wren had been avoiding since the second night, since the moment they'd watched Quintana give a beggar-child the whole of a meat pie and keep the crust: the resistance needed that map. Somewhere beyond Thornfell, in the erased kingdom's forgotten passes, there might be a road out for the thousands crowding the free cities' docks—refugees with nowhere to go, families rotting in camps, children who had never seen a country that wasn't burning. If Malvane got the map, that road died with whoever carried it. If the resistance got it, maybe—maybe—it lived.

And between those two necessities stood one tired smuggler feeding cats in the rain, who had done nothing wrong except survive, and whom Wren's orders required to betray or bury.

"There has to be another way," Wren whispered to the rain, and hated how young the voice sounded.

There wasn't. There never was. That was the lesson of the spy's life: every choice was a choice between wounds, and the only skill was choosing which bleeding to cause. Ash had taught them that too.

But Ash had never taught them what to do with this—this ache in the chest, this tenderness arriving uninvited like a stray cat at a door, rubbing against the shins of a person holding a knife. Wren looked down at their own hands, pale in the dark, hands that had picked locks and pockets and throats with equal competence, and tried to imagine them doing what the orders required. Taking the map from that room. Or worse.

From across the street, the candle in Quintana's window went out.

Wren stood in the alley a moment longer, rain running down their collar, the hollow coin pressing its orders against their ribs like a hand. Then they made their decision—or rather, admitted the decision they had already made, sometime between the meat pie and the cats, sometime they couldn't pinpoint and couldn't undo.

They would not take the map tonight. They would not kill. Instead they would do the one thing a spy should never do, the thing Ash would flay them for: they would introduce themselves. Walk in the front door, invent a face and a history, offer what a person like Quintana seemed to need most—another pair of hands, another set of eyes, company that asked nothing. Get close. Learn the price. And then decide, with full knowledge, whether Sable Quintana was a mark or something else entirely.

*Something else entirely.* Wren almost laughed at the phrase. Six days, and they were already writing poetry about a stranger.

They turned toward the fish market, where tomorrow's chaos would begin at dawn—and stopped.

At the far end of the alley, where it opened onto Saltspray Lane, a lantern swung. Not a watchman's rhythm—too fast, too purposeful. Four figures in oilskins moved through the rain with the particular economy of professionals, and even at this distance Wren caught the glint of the badge they wore pinned inside their collars, the one that showed only when the light struck it sideways: a circle bisected by a blade.

Witch-hunters. Not Corvess's polite kind. The other kind.

And they were heading for the chandler's shop, moving in the practiced spread of a door-team, one to the front stair, two to the back, one to the street.

Wren's body decided before their mind did. They were moving—low, fast, silent along the alley wall—before the thought fully formed, and the thought, when it arrived, was simple and terrible and true:

*I can't let them reach that room.*

Below the eaves of the fishmonger's stall, Wren drew the only weapon that mattered tonight—not the knife at their belt but the lie they'd need to build in the next thirty seconds, a lie big enough to hide a storm-caller in. Above them, through the rain, they could see the candle relight in Quintana's window, quick and sharp, and knew that Quintana had heard the footsteps too.

The hunters reached the stair. Wren stepped out of the shadows into the lantern-light, smiling the smile of a fellow smuggler with urgent business, and began to lie.
