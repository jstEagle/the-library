## 5. Knives in the Fish Market

The fish market of Caelport woke before the sun did, the way old things wake—slowly, grudgingly, with a smell that clung to everything it touched.

Sable had learned its rhythms in three weeks of hiding among its stalls and gutting-tables: the first carts rumbling in from the harbor before dawn, their wheels groaning on wet cobbles; the ice-sellers shouting prices over the gulls; the great silver heaps of mackerel and herring laid out like treasure no one wanted to steal. By midmorning the whole quarter stank of brine and blood and lemon rind, and the cobbles ran slick with meltwater and worse, so that every step had to be chosen like a word in a negotiation.

It was good ground for someone who did not want to be found. Bad ground for someone being followed.

Sable knew they were being followed. Had known since yesterday, since the meeting with the man in silver and his soft voice and his fortune offered like a knife laid flat on a table. *Malvane's witch-hunters are already hunting the map's bearer,* he'd said, smiling as though delivering good news. They had let Sable walk out of that room alive, which meant only one thing: they hadn't needed to kill them yet. They were following the map home—or following Sable to whoever else might come looking for it.

So this morning Sable did what smugglers do when hunted. They became boring. They bought a string of dried herring they didn't want, argued over two coppers with a fishwife whose forearms could have cracked walnuts, complained about the weather, and drifted through the crowd like any other small-time trader with salt in their boots and hunger in their purse.

But under the boredom, every sense was open. The storm-caller's gift had never entirely left them—it lived now like an old scar that ached before rain—and it ached this morning, a low pressure behind the eyes, a taste of iron at the back of the throat. Not weather. People. The particular charged stillness that gathered around violence waiting to happen.

Three of them, maybe four. Moving with the crowd but not as the crowd moved. Hunters walked differently. They walked like men reading a page, and Sable was the sentence they were tracing with one finger.

*Don't run,* Sable told themselves, turning a herring over as if inspecting its spine. *Running tells them everything.*

The market square opened ahead, all awnings and smoke and the gray light of a morning that couldn't decide whether to rain. Beyond it, the harbor stairs went down into the tide-smell and the rope-walks, and beyond those, if a body knew the way—and Sable knew the way—the mouths of the old drainage tunnels yawned under the seawall like drowned throats. Every city has veins. Caelport's ran with sewage and secrets in equal measure, and Sable had spent enough coin in enough dark corners to know which vein led where.

Get to the waterline. Get below. Disappear for a week, sell the map through a broker in the Saltrows, be gone before Malvane's hounds even understood they'd lost the scent.

That was the plan. It was a good plan. It was, in fact, exactly the plan Sable deserved after twenty years of surviving by never doing anything brave.

Then the bells began.

Not the harbor bells—those rang in patterns, trade-rhythms, a language every dockhand spoke. These were the temple bells above the square, and they were ringing wrong: fast, arrhythmic, panicked. And beneath them came a sound Sable knew from the other end of a long and ugly life—the sound of a crowd's murmur changing pitch. The sound of fear finding its feet.

"Fire!" someone screamed, and then, worse, closer: "*Witch-hunters!*"

The square broke like a wave against rock.

*

Chaos is a country, and Sable had citizenship.

They moved with the stampede instead of against it, letting the panic carry them, shoulders down, head low, the string of herring still absurdly in hand because some animal part of the mind insists on holding what it holds. Around them, stalls overturned in dominoes; a mountain of ice slid across the cobbles in a glittering ruin; gulls rose in a shrieking white sheet, wheeling and screaming like the souls of the drowned come back to complain.

Through the crowd's gaps, Sable saw them.

Witch-hunters. Four of them, in ash-gray cloaks with the black chevron of Malvane's Inquisition stitched at the shoulder, moving *against* the current of fleeing bodies with the terrible calm of men who had done this a hundred times in a hundred markets. Not chasing randomly. Converging. Two peeling left along the awning line, two cutting right past the fountain, closing a net whose center was—

Was Sable.

Except the center wasn't quite Sable anymore, because between Sable and the closing pincers stood a figure in a patched oilskin coat, hood back, dark hair plastered by the drizzle, holding a gutting-knife in a way that said *I have held knives my whole life and I am not finished with them.*

Sable knew that face. Had seen it twice from a window, once across a courtyard, once reflected in a brass door-plate. The watcher from the rooftops. The shadow that had been shadowing them for days.

*Wren.* Though how Sable knew the name was a question they filed away for later, in the drawer where smugglers keep things they don't understand yet.

One of the hunters reached the stranger first—a big man, sword half-drawn, shouting the litany Sable had heard only in nightmares since childhood: *"In the name of the Usurped Throne, halt and be counted—"*

The stranger moved like water finding a crack. The gutting-knife flicked—not to kill, Sable noted with a professional's eye, but to open the man's sword-hand along the wrist, a butcher's cut, precise and cheap—and the hunter's blade rang off the cobbles and the crowd swallowed him screaming. The second hunter lunged; the stranger caught his cloak-hem on the knife-point and *pulled*, spinning him into an overturned cart of eels, which expressed their opinion of witch-hunters with admirable enthusiasm.

And then the stranger turned, looked directly at Sable across fifteen feet of rioting fish market, and shouted:

"You're going the wrong way, you idiot! Waterline's east!"

*Fellow smuggler,* the look said. *Obviously. Nothing to see here.*

Sable had been playing this game for twenty years and had never once seen anyone play it so brazenly, or so well, or with such a suspiciously perfect instinct for where Sable would run. Which meant either miracle or trap, and in Sable's experience miracles were just traps with better manners.

No time to choose. The remaining hunters were regrouping, and one of them had raised something to his lips—a whistle, bone-white, and the note that came out of it was not a whistle at all but a *summons*, a thin piercing thread of sound that made the hairs on Sable's arms stand up and made the storm-scar behind their eyes flare white-hot.

They were calling more. There were always more.

East, then. Toward the waterline. With the stranger.

*

They ran together through streets gone mad, and running together is its own kind of conversation.

The stranger took the lead without asking—knew the quarter, or pretended to, cutting down a fish-gut alley Sable themselves would have chosen, vaulting a collapsed awning, sliding under a cart whose owner was too busy cursing the heavens to curse them. Sable followed half a step behind and slightly to the side, the position of a person who intends to survive their companion as well as their pursuers.

Behind them, boots. More than four now. The whistle-note again, closer, and with it a new sound: a low hum, almost subsonic, felt in the teeth rather than heard. Sable's scar flared again, harder, and they stumbled on the wet cobbles.

*They have a caller-finder.* Of course they did. Malvane's hounds didn't track faces; they tracked the gift. Twenty years of burying the storm deep, living quiet, touching no magic—and one whistle had painted Sable across the city like a beacon.

"They're not following you," the stranger gasped, glancing back. "They're following your *signature*. You've got to smother it."

"I haven't used magic in twenty years," Sable snarled.

"Doesn't matter. It leaks when you're afraid." The stranger's eyes—gray-green, sharp, annoyingly steady—met Sable's for half a stride. "And you're leaking like a broken barrel."

*I should leave them here,* Sable thought. *Whatever game they're playing, I owe them nothing. Cut down the rope-walk alone, lose them in the tunnels.*

Instead Sable said, "Left here. The right arch floods."

The stranger took the left without argument.

*

The harbor stairs descended into gloom and the reek of low tide, forty steps of green-slick stone down to where the seawall met the water and the old drainage mouths opened in the wall like a row of missing teeth. Above and behind, the whistle-notes multiplied, echoing off the cliff-face, and the hum grew until Sable's molars ached with it.

At the bottom, the stranger stopped, chest heaving, and pressed their back to the wall beside the largest tunnel-mouth. From somewhere inside their coat they produced a stub of tallow candle and struck fire to it with hands that, Sable noticed, were shaking only slightly less than steady.

"Well," the stranger said. "That went well."

"It went terribly," Sable said.

"And yet here we are." A gesture at the tunnel mouth, at the darkness breathing cold air and sewage-reek. "After you?"

"After you," Sable said sweetly. "I insist. I'd hate to miss whatever happens next."

Something flickered across the stranger's face—amusement, quickly banked, like a match cupped against wind. They stepped into the tunnel, candle first, and Sable followed close enough to put a knife between them if it came to that, which it might, which it probably would, eventually, because nothing in Sable's long career of bad decisions had ever arrived wearing a name as convenient as *fellow smuggler* without being a lie.

The tunnel swallowed them. Behind, at the top of the stairs, torchlight bloomed, and voices called orders in the clipped cadence of the Inquisition, and the hum swept the stairhead like a lantern sweeping a room—hung there, trembled—

And passed on. Westward. Away.

Sable let out a breath they'd been holding for twenty years.

*

The sewers of Caelport were older than the kingdom that built them and better built than anything since. Brick barrels high enough to walk upright, junction chambers where five tunnels met under a shaft of gray light from a grate far overhead, and everywhere the sound of water going about its ancient business. Rats watched them pass with the jaded eyes of customs officials.

They walked in silence for a long time. Sable counted turnings, marked distances, kept the map of the underground updated in their head out of habit. The stranger kept pace, held the candle, asked nothing.

Finally, at a junction where the channel forked and the air moved cooler, Sable stopped.

"All right," they said. "Talk fast. Who are you, why were you watching me for six days, and why does a gutter-knife fighter know what a caller-signature smells like?"

The stranger set the candle on a dry ledge and turned. Up close, in the unsteady light, their face was younger than their competence and older than their years, both at once, which is a particular kind of tiredness Sable recognized intimately.

"Wren," they said. "Smuggler, mostly. Runner, sometimes. I work the Caelport–Saltrows circuit." A pause, honest by a hair's width. "Mostly."

"And the watching?"

"I was hired to watch you." Wren's chin came up, defiant and weary in equal measure. "There. Now you know the worst of it. Someone paid me to find out where you kept the map and who you talked to. I've been following you for six days trying to make myself do it."

"And?"

"And you feed stray cats behind the chandlery. You barter honest weight with a fishwife who'd rob a bishop. You gave your coat to a dock kid in the rain and told him it was stolen goods you needed moved." Wren's mouth twisted. "Do you know how annoying that is? I had a whole ledger ready. *Dangerous courier, armed, sells to anyone.* Instead I've got—" they gestured vaguely at all of Sable— "*this.*"

Sable said nothing. The drip of the tunnel filled the silence, patient as a clock.

"The people who hired me," Wren went on, quieter, "aren't the ones with the whistles. That much is true. But I'm not going to insult you by swearing they're saints either. Everyone wants your map, Quintana. The usurper wants it burned. Other people want it *used*. And I want—" they exhaled— "I want to stop lying to someone for the first time in three years, and apparently I picked the worst possible person to practice on."

"You realize," Sable said slowly, "that this is the least reassuring confession ever delivered in a sewer."

"I realize." Wren almost smiled. "But you're still here. You could've cut away at the fork and vanished. You know these tunnels better than I do—I could tell by the way you count turnings. So why am I still talking to you?"

Because the whistle had blown and the hum had sung and for the first time in twenty years someone had stepped between Sable and the hunters without knowing what they were protecting. Because *you're going the wrong way, you idiot* was the stupidest, bravest thing anyone had said to Sable in a decade. Because the alternative was the tunnels alone, and the tunnels alone had started to feel less like safety and more like the inside of a grave.

"Because you fight like a fish-gutter and lie like a priest," Sable said. "And I need both, and trust neither, and that's the most honest alliance on offer today."

Wren studied them. Somewhere far above, the city bells resumed their proper rhythms, ordinary again, as if the morning had decided to pretend none of it happened.

"Terms?" Wren said.

"No names given freely. No questions answered kindly. You watch my back in daylight; I watch yours in the dark. And the moment either of us decides the other's sold them—" Sable drew the gutting-knife an inch from its sheath and let it whisper home— "we settle it quick and clean. Agreed?"

"Agreed," Wren said. Then, ruining it completely: "You know they'll bring dogs next time. And the caller-finder will sweep the sewers by nightfall. We can't stay in Caelport."

"I know where we can go," Sable said, and hated how easily the words came, hated the way the map seemed to warm against their ribs like a live coal, like something with a heartbeat. "Roads through Thornfell. Old ones. Roads nobody's walked since the kingdom was erased."

Wren went very still. In the candlelight their eyes changed—not greed, not quite, but the look of someone seeing a door they'd only heard described in whispers.

"You know what's at the end of those roads," Wren said softly. It wasn't a question.

Sable turned toward the deeper dark of the tunnel, toward the north and the mountains and the thing they had sworn for twenty years never to think about.

"I know what's written on the seal," they said. "Come on. We've a long way to walk and a shorter time to walk it."

Behind them, faint as memory, the bone-whistle sounded once more across the city—hunting, patient, certain—and the two of them went down into the dark together, strangers bound by knives, lies, and the single shared truth that neither of them trusted the other farther than the next turning of the road.

Which, Sable thought grimly, wading into the cold black water, was still farther than they trusted anyone else alive.
