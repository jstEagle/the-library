## 6. The Road Through Thornfell

The mountains announced themselves long before they appeared.

Sable felt them first in the soles of their boots—a faint tremor, like a pulse beneath the world's skin—and then in the air itself, which grew thinner and sharper the farther north they walked, until every breath tasted of iron and old snow. By midmorning the peaks had risen over the foothills, white-capped and indifferent, their summits lost in a haze of cloud that never seemed to lift.

Thornfell. Sable knew these roads the way a sailor knows the lines of his own palm—not because he chose to memorize them, but because he'd traced them so often, in fear and in flight, that they'd worn grooves into him.

They climbed through pine forest for most of the day, the trees tall and dark and dripping with last night's rain. Wren walked half a pace behind, as they had since dawn, saying little. Sable could hear them breathing—measured, economical, the breath of someone who had learned to spend nothing they didn't have to.

At a fork where the trail split around a lightning-scarred boulder, Sable stopped and pointed left without breaking stride.

"Left," Wren said.

"You have good eyes."

"I have good ears. You muttered it."

Sable almost smiled. Almost. "Old habit. I used to run this route with cargo that didn't appreciate being talked about."

"What kind of cargo talks?"

"The kind with teeth." Sable stepped over a root system grown thick as ship's cable. "Smuggling's mostly boring, if you're any good at it. You memorize the dull things—the fork, the streambed, which stones are loose after rain—and then when the exciting things happen, you already know where to put your feet."

"And are you any good at it?"

"I'm alive."

Wren made a sound that might have been a laugh, quickly swallowed. They walked on.

* * *

By late afternoon the forest thinned and the trail began to switchback up a shoulder of bare granite. The wind arrived properly here—not the gusting, indecisive wind of the lowlands but something steadier and more patient, a wind that had crossed a hundred miles of empty country and intended to cross a hundred more. It pulled at Sable's cloak and found every gap in it.

They made camp in the lee of an outcropping just below the tree line, a hollow of packed earth and stone where a fire would be invisible from the valley floor. Sable built it small and fed it slow, stacking wet peat over dry twigs so it smoked less and lasted longer. Wren watched the whole operation with an attention that was becoming familiar—the particular stillness of someone cataloguing a skill.

"You've camped in worse places," Wren said. Not a question.

"Once slept three nights in a salt-boat's bilge with a hold full of contraband pepper and a dead man two feet away." Sable held their hands to the flames. "The pepper got me through customs. The dead man got me through the following year—I sold his story to a ballad-maker in Caelport for enough to eat through winter."

"That's horrible."

"That's *profitable*. There's a difference, and the difference is dinner."

Wren did laugh then, briefly, before catching it again like something escaped from a cage. Sable noted it. Every unguarded moment from this one was a coin in a purse whose owner Sable hadn't yet identified.

They ate hard bread and dried fish, and the light died out of the sky by degrees, blue going to grey going to a black so complete the stars seemed less like lights than like holes punched through to somewhere brighter. The cold came down off the glacier fields above them in a slow flood. Sable pulled their blanket tighter and found Wren doing the same, and for a while there was only the fire's small crackling and the enormous silence of the mountain.

It was Wren who spoke first, quietly, into the dark.

"My mother sang," they said. "When she thought no one was listening. Old songs—court songs, really, though she'd have denied it. Songs about a kingdom that doesn't exist anymore."

Sable kept their eyes on the fire. "There are a lot of songs like that these days."

"Not like this one." A pause. The fire popped; a knot of peat collapsed into orange ruin. "This one had words in it nobody uses anymore. Words from—" Wren stopped. Shifted. Sable heard the sound of a decision being unmade. "It doesn't matter. It was a pretty tune. That's all."

"That's never all," Sable said. "But you don't have to tell me. I know what it is to carry a story you can't say out loud."

"Do you."

"I burned my own village down around my family's heads with weather." Sable said it flatly, the way you'd read a shipping manifest. "So yes. I have some practice."

The silence that followed was different from the earlier ones—heavier, but not unfriendly. Wren fed another twig to the fire, and its light moved across their face, and for a moment Sable saw past the careful blankness to something raw underneath: not pity, exactly. Recognition.

"They told me about you," Wren said finally. "Before we—before I found your road. That there was a storm-caller in the south who'd sworn it off. That the stories said you killed everyone you loved and walked away."

"The stories are accurate. That's the worst part of them."

"And yet you're walking toward a door out of legend with a stranger who was sent to watch you." Wren's voice was very even. "You're either brave or you're tired."

"Both. Mostly tired." Sable looked up, met Wren's eyes across the fire. "You want to ask if I'll use it again. The storm. Everyone wants to ask, eventually. So ask."

Wren held the gaze for a long moment. Then: "No. I want to ask what it cost you to stop."

Sable hadn't expected that. The question went in under their ribs, quiet and precise, and sat there.

"It cost everything," they said at last. "And it bought nothing. That's the arithmetic of grief—you pay in full and receive a receipt for goods never delivered." They poked the fire. Sparks rose and died. "I stopped calling storms because I couldn't trust myself with one. But I never stopped hearing them. Do you know what that's like? To feel weather building two valleys over and know you could shape it, turn it, break it—and choose not to? Every day. Like having hands and being told not to catch anyone falling."

"No," Wren said softly. "But I know what it's like to be told to watch someone fall and do nothing."

The words hung between them, and neither took them back, and neither explained them. The fire burned lower. Somewhere far below, a night bird called once and received no answer.

"We should sleep," Sable said. "Pass tomorrow. It's exposed for six miles—if weather comes, we'll feel it before we see it."

Wren nodded and settled against their pack, and Sable lay back on the cold stone and stared up at the wheeling stars, and found—to their mild surprise—that they were not afraid to close their eyes near this stranger. Wary, yes. Always wary. But not afraid.

Sleep came slowly, and when it came, it came gently, without storms.

* * *

Dawn found frost on the blankets and breath hanging in the air like smoke.

They broke camp fast, fingers stiff, and started up the pass while the light was still thin and grey. The trail narrowed as it climbed, hugging the mountainside above a drop that deepened with every switchback, until Sable stopped looking down and started reading the mountain instead—the lean of the scree, the color of the lichen, the way the wind had changed sometime in the night from steady to gusting, from west to southwest.

Southwest meant moisture. Moisture meant weather.

"How far to shelter?" Wren asked. Their voice was carefully casual. They'd felt it too, then.

"There's a waystation—an old smuggler's cache, roofed, half-buried—about four hours on. If we push." Sable squinted at the sky, which was bruising purple along the western peaks. "If we don't, we'll be up here when it breaks."

"Then we push."

They pushed. The trail deteriorated into broken rock and frozen mud, and twice Sable had to haul Wren up short stretches where the path had sheared away entirely, leaving only a shelf of stone wide enough for one cautious foot at a time. Wren took it without complaint, without even visible fear, moving with a sureness that told Sable something about their history—no one learned to climb like that in a townhouse.

"You've done mountain work," Sable said, when they'd cleared the worst of it.

"I've done a lot of work."

"Evasive."

"Efficient." Wren paused to get their breath, cheeks red with cold, eyes bright. "Ask me again when we're somewhere warm. Or don't. Some doors open easier from the other side."

"A door metaphor. From you. On this road." Sable shook their head and started climbing again. "You're worse than the ballad-makers."

Above them, the pass crested at a saddle of naked stone, and beyond it the trail descended toward a long green valley threaded with silver water. And there—far off, at the valley's mouth, where the river flattened and spread—they saw it.

Smoke. Not one fire's worth. Hundreds of fires' worth, a brown smudge staining the morning air, rising from a column of people on the move. Tiny at this distance, ant-like, unmistakable.

Refugees. Hundreds of them, with nowhere to go.

Sable stood at the crest of the pass with the wind pulling at their coat and the map a heavy weight against their chest, and understood, with terrible clarity, that the route they carried was no longer a secret worth selling.

It was a corridor. And it was filling with people who would die without it.

Beside them, Wren had gone very still, watching the smoke with an expression Sable couldn't read—grief or recognition or guilt, or all three braided together.

"Sable," Wren said quietly. "I think it's time I told you what I really am."
