## 8. The Witch-Hunter's Hymn

The signal had to be sent before moonrise.

Wren knew this the way they knew the weight of a knife in a sleeve, the way they knew which floorboards in a rented room sang underfoot: not as knowledge but as instinct, worn smooth by years of use. The handler's window was fixed. Every third night, when the lamplighter made his rounds along Caelport's Saltquay district and the tide turned in the harbor below, a shuttered lantern hung from the sign of the Gilded Eel would blink three times—*all quiet*—or four times—*come quickly*—and somewhere across the dark water, a pair of patient eyes would read the message and write it down in a ledger Wren would never see.

Tonight there would be no blinking at all. Tonight Wren would hang the lantern and leave it burning steady, unshuttered, its light spilling yellow onto the wet cobbles like an open wound. *The trail is cold. The quarry has gone to ground elsewhere. Look for your witch-hunter's prize in Vellamare, or in the hill country past it. Anywhere but here.*

A lie, then. A lie told upward, through channels, to the crown itself—or what remained of the crown that Wren had sworn to serve at seventeen, with cold ink on warm skin and a priest's hand heavy on their shoulder. The old king was dead. The old kingdom was a story told in cellars. But the resistance endured, and the resistance had orders, and the orders were simple enough to carve on a coin: *Find the courier. Recover the map. If the courier is compromised, end them.* And now the courier was dead in a ditch outside Thornfell's foothills, and the map rode in the pack of a storm-caller who fed stray cats and haggled over coppers like any other honest smuggler, and Wren stood at a rain-dark window deciding which oath to break.

Below, in the narrow lane beneath Sable Quintana's lodgings, a cat was crying.

Wren knew the cat. It was a ragged grey thing with one ear chewed short, and it had appointed itself to the corner across from Sable's door three nights running, and every night Sable came down with a heel of bread and a saucer of fish-oil bought—Wren had checked—with coin that should have gone to candles. Tonight the cat sat in the drizzle, patient as a stone saint, and after a while the door opened and Sable came out with their collar up and their hair plastered flat, and crouched in the mud without a thought for their cloak, and said something low to the animal that Wren could not hear but could almost feel, the way one feels a hearth through a wall.

*This is the person I am ordered to kill,* Wren thought, and the thought had teeth.

They turned from the window before the sight could do more damage than it already had. The room behind them was mean and useful: one bed, one chair, one table bearing the tools of a trade that had no name in polite company—a coil of signal-wire, a wax tablet half-erased, a purse of silver that grew lighter as the days passed because watching someone required bribing porters and innkeepers and the lamplighter himself, whose rounds were the clock by which all of Wren's messages ran. On the table's edge lay the hymnal.

It looked, to any searching eye, like what it appeared: a cheap devotional book, its cover stamped with the sunburst of the Concord faith, its pages soft with handling. Witch-hunters carried such books. They sang from them at executions, four-part harmony rolling over the crowd while the fire took hold, and the singing was the worst part—not the flames, not the screaming, but the sweet, unhurried voices, as though mercy were being performed rather than withheld. Wren had heard the hymn once, years ago, from the back of a crowd in Vellamare, and had never managed to wash it out of their ears. *Blessed is the blade that cleanses. Blessed is the flame that frees.* They had learned the melody despite themselves. Their mouth knew it the way a scar knows rain.

Inside the hymnal's binding, slit and sewn again with thread fine as hair, lay the true dispatch: a strip of rice-paper bearing code in ink that would bloom only under the handler's particular reagent. *Courier dead. Map recovered by Sable Quintana, ex-storm-caller, currently lodging Saltquay, Caelport. Recommend extraction via—*

Wren stopped writing. That was the trouble with lies; they wanted to be complete. *Recommend extraction* meant telling the handler where the map was, which meant sending hunters where the map was, which meant—

Which meant the cat got no more fish-oil. Which meant the door across the lane opened one last time onto nothing.

Wren crossed out the line with a single stroke and wrote instead: *Trail lost south of the marsh road. Quarry likely bound for Vellamare. Requesting redirection of search assets.* Then they signed it with the old cipher—the rising sun over three waves—and slid it into the binding, and set the hymnal inside their coat, against their heart, where a book of that kind belonged.

* * *

The lamplighter came at the turn of the tide, as he always did, a stooped man with a ladder and a whistle he played tunelessly between lamps. Wren watched him from the shadow of the cooperage doorway, counting his lamps the way other people counted sheep. Nine. Ten. Eleven—the Gilded Eel, where the signal lantern waited in the alcove above the sign, dark until now.

The lamplighter paused beneath it. He did not look up—that was part of his pay—but his whistle faltered for half a breath, one flat note hanging in the fog, and Wren felt the old familiar sickness rise in their throat. Twelve years of this. Twelve years of small betrayals arranged like flowers in a vase, each one cut and trimmed so it would stand straight in the arrangement, and never once had the arrangement included a face at a window, or a voice saying something gentle to a one-eared cat in the rain.

They climbed the cooper's stair two rungs at a time, unshuttered the lantern, and left it burning steady and bright. *All quiet. Nothing here. Go and look somewhere else.*

Then they went back to their post across from Sable's lodgings, folded themselves into the doorway's dark, and began to wait for morning, and for whatever the lie would buy.

It bought less than a day.

* * *

The reply came at dusk the following evening, and it did not come by lantern or by whisper but by a child—a girl of perhaps nine, barefoot despite the cold, who trotted up to Wren in the fish market with the fearless insolence of the harbor poor and held out a folded paper. "From the lady with the grey parrot," she said. "She said you'd give me a penny."

Wren gave her two, because guilt was cheap at that price, and walked until they found an alley deep enough to read in. The paper smelled faintly of almonds. The handler's hand was small and precise, a schoolmistress's hand, and it had never once in twelve years wasted a word:

*Redirection denied. Confirm identity first. Description insufficient. Is it truly Quintana? Prove it. You have three days. —V.*

Three letters at the end instead of the usual cipher-signature. *V.* As if she were impatient, or careless, or—as Wren suspected, with the cold spreading feeling through their chest—as if she wanted Wren to know exactly who was asking. Captain Ilsa Voss did not sign her orders with pet names. Captain Ilsa Voss signed them with knives. Unless she had been promoted since Wren's last briefing, or unless she had taken personal charge of the hunt, which was worse, because Voss did not take personal charge of anything she intended to lose.

*Confirm identity first.*

Wren leaned against the alley wall and let the words settle. To confirm Sable Quintana's identity to the witch-hunters was not merely to name them. Everyone in the trade knew what confirmation meant in Voss's ledger: it meant proof, and proof meant capture or corpse, and either way it meant the map passing into the hands of the usurper's church, and the Last Door sealed forever, and every soul in the erased kingdom locked inside Malvane's new history with no page torn out for escape. Wren had known this when they took the assignment. They had told themselves the arithmetic was clean: one life weighed against thousands, and the life in question a stranger's, a smuggler's, a storm-caller's—the very sort of person the old crown had burned at the border shrines, once upon a time, before the usurper made burning fashionable again.

The arithmetic had been clean for exactly as long as Wren had not watched Sable Quintana feed a cat.

They thought about the night before last, when the rain had come down in ropes and Sable had stood in the doorway of a chandler's shop, arguing—not fighting, *arguing*, with gestures and grimaces—with the chandler over the price of tallow scraps. Wren had been close enough to hear it, close enough to smell the tar and rain on them, and the argument had ended with Sable paying the man's full asking price and then, muttering, pressing an extra copper into his palm "for the roof leak you're pretending you don't have." The chandler had stared after them as though a fish had recited poetry. So had Wren, though they'd had the sense to do it in the dark.

And they thought about the other thing, the thing they kept folded away like a letter too dangerous to open: the way Sable flinched at thunder. Not jumped—flinched, a small involuntary tightening around the eyes, the shoulders drawing in as if expecting a blow. Wren had seen soldiers flinch like that. Wren had seen survivors flinch like that. Whatever storm had driven Sable Quintana out of the world and into the smuggling lanes, it lived still behind their ribs, and they carried it the way one carries a dead sibling: carefully, constantly, and without ever speaking its name.

*Is it truly Quintana? Prove it.*

Oh, it was Quintana. Wren could prove it in a dozen ways—the scar splitting the left eyebrow, the sailor's roll in the walk, the way their fingers moved restlessly when they thought no one watched, shaping weather that wasn't there. Proof was easy. Proof was the easiest thing in the world.

Proof was also a death warrant written in someone else's hand.

* * *

That night Wren did not sleep. They sat in their mean room with the hymnal open on their knees, reading the false dispatch they had sent, reading Voss's reply, reading them alternately like a heretic reading scripture against scripture, hunting for the seam where salvation might be stitched in.

There was a seam. There was always a seam; Voss was thorough but the world was larger than her thoroughness. *You have three days.* Three days in which Wren could manufacture a proof that confirmed the name and damned the hunt—point the hunters at a Quintana-shaped absence, a cold lodging, a boat gone east. Three days in which Wren could get a message to the resistance's Caelport cell and have Sable moved, quietly, before the net closed. Three days in which Wren could do what they had done a hundred times before: serve two masters, keep both ledgers balanced, and tell themselves at the end of it that no one had truly been betrayed.

But the ledgers were not balancing anymore. Wren could feel them tipping, feel the whole careful architecture of double service groaning like a ship in ice. Because there was a third entry now, an entry that had never been in any ledger before, and it would not stay put on the page. It kept standing up and walking across the lane and crouching in the mud to feed a cat.

And beneath that—deeper, older, the thing Voss's letter had scraped raw—was the question Wren had been refusing to ask since the courier died: *loyalty to what, exactly?* To a dead king? To a resistance that spent informants like lamp oil? To a crown that had, in its living days, hunted storm-callers nearly as eagerly as Malvane's church hunted them now? Wren had sworn their oaths in a chapel that no longer existed, to a throne that was a memory, and the memory had been gilded by grief until it shone like righteousness. But gilding was gilding. Underneath, the metal was the same dull ore every crown was minted from, and it had never once, in all Wren's years of service, asked what it cost the people who paid it.

The hymn rose in their head unbidden, sweet and horrible: *blessed is the blade that cleanses.* They shut the hymnal hard enough to sting their palms.

Across the lane, a light moved behind Sable's window. A silhouette crossed it—Sable, pacing, restless, the map no doubt spread on the table before them, that beautiful fatal route through Thornfell drawn in a dead courier's blood. Wren watched the shadow pace, and pace, and stop, and lean, as though studying something, as though weighing something on scales only they could see. Selling it, probably. Deciding which buyer, which price, which port to vanish to. That was the sensible course. Wren found themselves praying to a god they didn't believe in that Sable would take it—sell the map, take the silver, sail away, be safe and selfish and far from here.

Because in three days, Voss's hunters would come to Caelport regardless of what Wren reported. Voss did not send one bird when a flock would serve. And when the flock arrived, everything would depend on where Wren stood—in front of Sable, or beside the hunters, pointing.

The lamp across the lane went out. The shadow vanished. Somewhere below, the grey cat cried once at a closed door and gave up.

Wren took out fresh rice-paper, and the almond-smelling ink, and began—for the second time in two nights—to compose a lie. This one would be harder. This one would need to be proof-shaped: a description, a sighting, a false certainty, all of it aimed like a thrown knife at empty ground. By dawn it would be sewn into the hymnal's spine, and by tomorrow's dusk it would be in Voss's hands, and then the clock would start in earnest.

They wrote slowly. Outside, the tide turned, and the lamplighter's distant whistle threaded through the fog, one flat note among the true ones, and Wren thought: *that is me. That is what I am now. The wrong note in the hymn, and the only reason the song hasn't yet found its ending.*

They finished the dispatch as the sky went grey. And just as they were melting the seal, they heard it—faint, from the street below, cutting through the harbor sounds like a wire through silk.

Singing. Four voices, in harmony, unhurried, sweet.

*Blessed is the blade that cleanses. Blessed is the flame that frees.*

Wren froze, the hymnal half-open in their lap, their own heartbeat suddenly the loudest thing in the room. The singers were still distant—streets away, maybe, moving closer with the patience of people who owned every street they walked. But the hymn carried, the way it always carried, and across the lane a shutter cracked open, and Wren saw Sable's face appear in the gap, pale in the early light, listening.

Listening the way one listens to a sound from a nightmare—one already survived, and coming again.

Wren closed the hymnal, tucked the dispatch inside their coat, and reached for their knives.
