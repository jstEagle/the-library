## 9. Thunder Over the Pass

The mountain had been watching them for two days.

Sable felt it the way they felt everything now—a prickle along the spine, a taste of iron at the back of the throat, like standing too near a struck bell. Thornfell did not welcome travelers. It tolerated them the way an old dog tolerates children: patiently, until it didn't.

They climbed through pine shadow and scree, the trail narrowing to a shelf of stone barely wide enough for one. Below, the valley floor had fallen away into blue haze. Above, the peaks wore their snow like crowns too heavy for any head. The air was thin enough to chew.

"Tell me again why smugglers don't just go around," Wren said behind her, breath ragged.

"Because around is three weeks through Malvane's patrol lines." Sable didn't turn. "Because this pass takes four days if the mountain allows it."

"And if it doesn't?"

Sable said nothing. Wren laughed once, short and without humor, and the sound skittered down the cliff face after them.

* * *

They made camp that night in the lee of a granite shoulder, a hollow the wind had carved out of the rock centuries ago. Sable built the fire small—habit, always small—and they ate salt pork and hard bread while the stars came out in their cold multitudes. This high, the sky felt close enough to touch, close enough to bite.

Wren sat with their back against the stone, knees drawn up, turning a smooth pebble over in their fingers. They'd been quiet since noon. Quiet in a particular way Sable had learned to read during years of smuggling runs: not the silence of exhaustion but of someone rehearsing words.

"Ask it or don't," Sable said.

Wren glanced up. "Ask what?"

"Whatever you've been chewing on since the switchbacks. You keep looking at me like I'm a lock you're trying to pick."

A long pause. Firelight moved on Wren's face, catching the sharp line of jaw, the bruise-yellow remains of the fish-market fight still fading along their cheekbone.

"The way you read the mountain today," Wren said carefully. "You stopped twice before anything happened. Once before the ice bridge cracked. Once before that rockfall near the narrows. You knew."

"I've crossed Thornfell since I was nine. The mountain talks. Most people just don't listen."

"That's not—" Wren set the pebble down. Picked it up again. "That's not what I mean, and you know it's not."

Sable fed a stick to the fire and watched it catch. Somewhere above them, wind moved through the high snowfields with a sound like a long exhalation, like something enormous settling deeper into sleep.

"You want to know what I am," Sable said.

"I want to know who you are," Wren corrected softly. "There's a difference."

"There isn't. Not for people like me. Not anymore." Sable looked up at the stars rather than at Wren's face. "Malvane's witch-hunters don't burn people for what they do. They burn them for what they *are*. So no—I'm not going to hand you the answer to a question you might be asking for someone else."

Wren was silent for a moment. Then, quietly: "If I were asking for someone else, would knowing change what I did?"

"That depends on what you are."

"And you won't ask that either."

"No," Sable agreed. "I won't."

It should have been a bitter exchange. It wasn't, quite. There was something almost gentle in it, two people circling the same fire, each holding a blade they refused to use. Wren huffed something that was nearly a laugh and pulled their cloak tighter.

"For what it's worth," they said, "I hope the answer's nothing terrible."

"It's worse than terrible," Sable said. "It's mine."

* * *

The mountain spoke at dawn.

Not in whispers this time. Sable woke with the taste of ozone flooding their mouth and their heart already hammering, every hair on their arms lifted, and lay still in the grey half-light listening to a silence that was wrong—the birds gone, the wind gone, even the meltwater trickle beneath the rocks gone, as though the whole pass were holding its breath.

"Wren." Their voice came out flat and strange. "Get up. Now."

Wren surfaced from sleep with a soldier's speed, hand already finding their knife. "What—"

The sound arrived then. Deep under the earth, under the stone, a groan like a ship's timbers tearing—and above them, up where the snowfields clung to the shoulders of the peak, Sable heard the other sound, the one they had heard only once before in their life and never forgotten: the soft, vast *whump* of a snowpack letting go.

"Move," Sable breathed. "Downslope. Away from the chute—*run*."

They ran.

The trail disintegrated beneath them into loose shale as they plunged toward the valley side, packs bouncing, breath tearing white behind them. Sable risked one glance back and saw it: a seam of white cracking open across the upper slope, widening, the whole face of the mountain beginning to move. It did not look like falling snow. It looked like the mountain shrugging off its own skin.

They weren't going to make it. Sable knew it the way they knew weather coming—the arithmetic was simple and merciless. The slide would reach the trail before they reached the bend. It would take them, fold them into itself, and carry them down into the dark of the ravine where no one would ever—

No.

The word rose in Sable not as thought but as refusal, older than thought, rising from somewhere below memory, below guilt, below the vow they had sworn over three graves in the rain. And with it rose everything they had buried: the pressure behind the breastbone, the singing in the blood, the sky's attention swinging toward them like a lighthouse beam.

*Sable, don't.* That was their mother's voice, twenty years dead. Or their own voice, every day since. *Don't.*

The mountain did not care about vows.

Sable stopped running. Turned. Faced the avalanche coming down like the wrath of some blind god, and raised both hands, and called.

It was not a shout. It was not even a word. It was a *pulling*, deep in the chest, the way one pulls a splinter from flesh—and the sky answered. Clouds that had been mere morning haze convulsed, darkened, boiled upward into a towering anvil-head. Thunder cracked across the pass, once, twice, a third time, so loud the stone rang with it. And then the lightning came down—not wild, not the mindless slaughter of that night in the village, but *aimed*, great branching spears of it striking the slope above the slide, hammering the snowfield's flank, and the thunderclaps rolled down the mountainside in deliberate waves, a wall of sound and pressure shoving the avalanche sideways, shearing it away from the trail, steering ten thousand tons of death into the empty ravine instead of onto two fleeing bodies.

The world went white. Then it roared. Then, slowly, it settled into the long rumbling echo of snow coming to rest where it could hurt no one.

Sable stood in the aftermath with their arms still raised, shaking, drenched in rain that fell only from the cloud they themselves had summoned, and realized—with a calm that felt like falling—that Wren was standing three paces away, staring at them.

The cloud broke apart even as Sable watched, unravelling like spun wool, the stolen storm returning to the ordinary sky. Sunlight came through, pale and indifferent. Sable lowered their arms. Their hands would not stop trembling.

"Well," they said. Their voice sounded scraped raw. "Now you know."

* * *

The silence stretched. Sable let it.

This was the part they had rehearsed for twenty years without ever wanting to perform it: the moment of revelation, the widening of another person's eyes, the slow recoil. They had imagined it with buyers, with crewmates, with the few lovers they'd allowed themselves. In the imaginings there was always a moment where the other person's face changed—where warmth became calculation, where *you* became *it*. Witch-hunter catechisms called storm-callers *vessels of the Unchained Sky*, as if the sky needed chaining and they were its broken shackles. Villages called them worse. Sable's own village had called them daughter, neighbor, friend—right up until the night the sky answered those names with fire.

So Sable stood in the wet snow and waited to be reclassified.

Wren's face did something complicated. Fear passed across it—Sable saw it, honest and involuntary—and Sable braced against it like bracing against a wave. But then Wren did the last thing in the world Sable expected.

They turned away. Deliberately. Looked back up the trail, scanning the slopes, the ridgelines, the haze-filled distance, with the practiced sweep of someone checking for witnesses.

"Clear," Wren said quietly. "Nobody saw. Nobody could have—we're above the tree line, and the refugee column's a full day behind us." They turned back, and their voice stayed low and level and utterly practical. "Your hands are bleeding."

Sable looked down, vaguely surprised. Split skin across both palms, weeping pink into the snow. They hadn't noticed. They never noticed, afterward; the body kept its own ledger and presented the bill later.

"Lightning leaves a toll," Sable said. "Even when you're the one calling it."

"Sit down before you fall down."

"I'm fine."

"You're swaying like a mast in a gale. Sit *down*, Quintana."

The surname landed strangely—Wren had never used it before—and Sable found, to their own irritation, that their knees obeyed. They sat heavily on a boulder, and Wren crouched, unwound the strip of linen they used for wrapping the map's oilcloth, and took Sable's hands in theirs with a matter-of-factness that undid Sable more thoroughly than horror would have.

"You're supposed to run," Sable said. "That's what the catechism says. Civilians flee and report to the nearest hunter-post. There's a reward."

"I've heard the catechism." Wren worked the linen around Sable's left palm, firm, careful, not looking up. "I've also just watched you argue with an avalanche and win. Forgive me if the catechism has lost some authority."

"Why aren't you afraid?"

Wren's hands paused. For a moment the only sounds were wind and the distant tick of melting snow.

"I am afraid," they said finally. "But not of you." They tied off the bandage, neat as a knot in rigging, and looked up, and their eyes were steady and very dark and very close. "I watched you aim it. Do you understand? You didn't lash out. You didn't lose yourself. You stood between that slide and me and you *chose* where it went. Whatever the witch-hunters say your kind are—whatever you believe you are—that wasn't it. That was the most controlled thing I've ever seen anyone do."

"You didn't see what happens when I don't control it."

"No," Wren agreed quietly. "But I can guess. It's written all over how you flinch when it rains."

Sable looked away. Above them the mountain stood innocent again, white and golden in the new sun, wearing its fresh scar of torn snow like it had always been there. As if nothing had happened. As if the sky had not bent down to touch the earth because Sable Quintana had asked it to.

"I swore it off," Sable said. The words came out before they could stop them, which was how all true things seemed to come out around Wren. "Twenty years ago. After—there was a village. There was a night. I swore I'd never call again, and I've kept that oath through things that would have broken most oaths, and today I broke it in about four seconds flat because I didn't want to die." A laugh escaped them, jagged. "Some martyr."

"Some martyr," Wren echoed, and there was no mockery in it at all. "You know what I saw? I saw someone who spent twenty years starving themselves of the one thing that could save lives, and the first time they touched it again, they used it to save one. Me. One unremarkable smuggler with a knife and bad boots." They squeezed Sable's bandaged hand, brief and warm. "If that's damnation, the theology needs work."

Sable wanted to tell him. Then. Right there, sitting in the snow with their ruined palms wrapped in map-linen—they wanted to pour out the whole of it, the village and the night and the three graves, to say *this is what I am, look at all of it, all of it, and see if your steadiness holds.* It rose in them like floodwater.

And then Wren stiffened.

It was slight—a fractional turn of the head, eyes going to the southern ridge—and Sable caught it only because they had spent two weeks learning to read this person. Wren rose smoothly, shading their eyes.

"What is it?" Sable asked.

"Dust," Wren said. "On the far ridge. Moving fast. Too fast for refugees." A pause, precisely measured. "Could be hunters. Could be nothing. We should get off this shelf either way—avalanche country draws scavengers and patrols alike."

Something cold slid down Sable's spine that had nothing to do with the mountain. Because Wren had looked at that dust for exactly as long as it took to recognize it, and no longer. Because Wren had turned away *just* before, at the perfect moment, with the perfect practicality. Because kindness this timely was also cover, and Sable had survived twenty years in the trade by knowing that the most dangerous lie is the one told to protect you.

Or—because Wren had seen lightning come down like a shepherd's crook and decided, in the space of a heartbeat, that some things were worth hiding from whoever that dust belonged to.

Friend or trap. Both, perhaps. Perhaps the distinction had stopped mattering.

"Lead on, then," Sable said, and smiled the smile they saved for customs men. "You know these passes better than you've been admitting, Wren. Don't think I haven't noticed."

Wren met the smile, and returned it, and for one unguarded instant something in their eyes looked almost like grief.

"There's a shrine ahead," they said. "Old one, pre-Malvane, half-swallowed by the rock. We can shelter there tonight." They shouldered their pack and started up the trail, then added, without turning, so quietly the wind nearly took it:

"And maybe it's time I told you what I was praying to, back when it mattered."

Sable stood a moment longer among the wreckage of the avalanche, palms throbbing inside their borrowed bandages, the ghost of the storm still singing faintly in their blood like a struck bell that would not quite fall silent.

Then they followed.
