## 12. The Hunter Ascendant

The interrogation rooms beneath the Bastion of the Vigilant Eye had no windows, and Captain Ilsa Voss preferred it that way. Windows admitted weather. Weather admitted mood. Mood was a solvent that dissolved discipline, and discipline was the only architecture she trusted.

She stood at the head of a long table in a room that smelled of wet stone and lamp oil, her gloved hands folded before her, and watched the man across from her decide how long he could afford to be brave.

He had been brave for six hours. It was, she noted with something adjacent to respect, longer than most. The resistance called him Brother Aldous, though his real name—Ferren Hale, chandler's apprentice turned courier, turned conspirator—was written neatly in her ledger along with everyone he had ever loved. That was the secret of her work, the one the ballads never sang: witch-hunting was not a matter of fire and iron and shouting. It was a matter of paper. Of lists. Of patience.

"Captain," Hale rasped. His voice had gone to gravel hours ago. "You should know—I've already told you nothing true."

"I know," Voss said. "You told me the network's cellars are under the Tanner's Row granary. You told me your leader is a woman named Maren with red hair. You told me you'd rather die than speak." She turned a page in her ledger without hurry. "All three were lies. The granary holds salt pork. Maren is a fiction you invented to protect a man named Corvel. And you would very much like to live—you simply haven't yet found a version of living you can accept."

His eyes flickered. There it was: the small animal motion behind the martyr's mask. Voss did not smile. Smiling was expenditure without return.

"You have a gift for this," Hale said bitterly.

"No. I have a method." She closed the ledger. "Gifts are for storm-callers and prophets and other people who believe the world owes them miracles. Method is for those of us who understand the world owes us nothing at all."

* * *

She had come up through the Vigilant Eye the way water comes up through stone—slowly, invisibly, wearing down everything soft. Twenty-two years in the order. She had burned her first witch at nineteen and felt nothing, which had frightened her for exactly one night, until she understood that feeling nothing was not a wound but an armor, and armor could be worn deliberately.

Her superiors called her cold. Her subordinates called her worse things, quietly, and she let them, because fear traveled faster than affection and covered more ground. What she called herself was *accurate*. The kingdom had been drowning in magic since before Malvane took the throne—storm-callers drowning villages, hedge-witches poisoning wells with blessings, seers selling futures they had no right to see. The old king had tolerated it out of sentiment. Malvane understood what sentiment cost. Malvane had erased an entire nation from the histories rather than leave a single loose thread of legitimacy dangling, and when Voss read the sealed edicts—the ones describing how a country of two hundred thousand souls had been unwritten, its name struck from maps and memory alike—she had felt, for the first time in her life, something close to admiration.

Order required terrible instruments. Someone had to be willing to be the instrument.

Now the instrument had a task. For three months the resistance had been moving something through the mountains—a route, a relic, a rumor—and three of her best hunters had gone into Thornfell and not come back. She did not tolerate unsolved arithmetic.

Which was why Brother Aldous sat across from her, and why the room's single lamp burned low, and why she waited now, hands folded, while he arrived at the only exit left to him.

"They'll kill me," he said finally. "If I talk. My own people will—"

"Yes," Voss agreed. "Probably. Though I'd point out that my people are considerably better at killing, and we do it professionally, which means cleanly. Whereas your comrades will do it out of shame, which means slowly." She leaned forward by a degree. "Here is what I can offer, Ferren. Not freedom. Freedom is a currency I don't trade in. But your sister lives in Millbrook with two children. When this is finished, she keeps living there. Unwatched. Untouched. That is the whole of my offer, and it expires when this lamp does."

Hale looked at the lamp. Small, steady, indifferent. He began to cry—not loudly, not dramatically, just the quiet leaking of a man whose structure had failed—and Voss waited with the patience of someone who had learned that tears were not resistance but drainage.

"The Door," he whispered.

"Say it properly."

"The Last Door. In Thornfell. The valley—" His voice cracked. "There's a valley above the old pass. The shrine road. The Door is set into the cliff on the north face. It opens onto the free cities. It's keyed to royal blood and storm-fire together—that's why they need a caller. They've found one. An exile. A smuggler carrying the map."

Voss wrote it all down in her precise, upright hand. Valley above the pass. Shrine road. North face. Keyed to blood and storm-fire. A caller in transit.

When she finished, she blew out the lamp herself, as promised, and signaled the guards at the door.

"Take him to the cells," she said. "Feed him. He cooperated."

One of the guards hesitated. "And after, Captain?"

"After is a matter for later." She gathered her ledger. "We keep our word within the horizon of our convenience. That is more mercy than the world usually offers."

* * *

The storm-anchor arrived at dusk, in a wagon drawn by six horses and escorted by twelve men who would not look at it directly.

Voss met it in the Bastion's lower yard, where the light was failing and the mountains to the east had gone the color of bruises. The crate was iron-banded and rune-etched, and even through the wood she could feel it—a wrongness, a pressure against the ear, like standing near a struck bell that made no sound. The artificers of the College of Chains had built it for the old king's wars and then, wisely, tried to forget it. Malvane's people had remembered.

"Open it," she said.

They broke the seals. Inside, packed in wool and salt, lay a device like a great iron harp crossed with a fishhook: curved tines of blackened steel radiating from a central ring, each tine inscribed with binding-script, each ending in a barbed hook. It hummed. Not audibly—she felt it in her teeth, in the fillings of old wounds.

"It's attuned," said the artificer, a stooped woman named Gretch with ink-stained fingers and no imagination whatsoever, which Voss considered a professional virtue. "Calibrated to storm-calling specifically. Within a league, any caller working their craft will feel it. Within a hundred paces, active calling becomes... difficult. Painful." Gretch adjusted her spectacles. "Within ten paces, Captain, the anchor doesn't suppress the gift. It *takes* it. Hooks into the caller's channel and draws the power out through the tines. Whatever they call, we call instead. Whatever they are, we hold."

"And the caller?"

Gretch shrugged. "Alive. Mostly. It's not gentle. Think of it as bleeding, but from somewhere that doesn't clot."

Voss studied the thing for a long moment. Somewhere beyond the walls, thunder muttered over the eastern hills—distant, directionless, ordinary. She thought about the reports from Thornfell: hunters dead, a village of witnesses, and now this—a caller, an exile, walking a map toward a door that could unmake everything Malvane had built. One person with weather in their blood, standing between a battalion and a legend.

People romanticized such figures. Ballads, campfires, desperate hope. Voss had spent twenty-two years learning what such figures actually were: uncontrolled variables. Storms that chose. And a storm that chooses is merely a disaster that has been flattered.

"Harness it to the lead wagon," she said. "Double teams. If it slows the column, we march at night."

"Captain," Gretch said, "the hooks need a focus. A conductor. Someone to aim what it takes." She hesitated, which was unlike her. "It wants a hand on the ring. A will. Otherwise it just... drags. Randomly. Like a net through deep water."

"Then I will put my hand on the ring myself."

Gretch blinked. "It's not—it isn't pleasant, Captain. The feedback. Callers have been known to—"

"I am not a caller," Voss said. "I am the opposite of a caller. Put it in the wagon."

* * *

They marched at first light.

Two hundred picked men of the Vigilant Eye, veterans all, in grey lacquered mail that shed rain like oiled feathers. Four wagons: powder, rations, the anchor, and the portable gallows-frame that Voss insisted on bringing because she had learned long ago that the sight of an empty scaffold did more work than a full company of soldiers. Fear was logistics too.

The road climbed northeast out of the lowlands, into country that grew steeper and quieter with every mile. Thornfell rose ahead of them—black ridgelines stitched with cloud, passes choked with early snow, forests that swallowed sound. The locals called these mountains the spine of the world. Voss thought of them as a corridor. Everything that mattered was a corridor, if you approached it correctly.

She rode at the head of the column with her ledger open against her saddle-bow, reviewing the file on the target one final time.

Sable Quintana. Exiled storm-caller. Family dead—cause: the subject's own uncontrolled calling, age nineteen. Subject subsequently renounced the craft, took up smuggling, operated out of Caelport. Known associates: minimal. Known weaknesses: guilt, self-isolation, a documented aversion to using the gift. Current status: bearer of the Quintana-route map, believed en route to the Last Door, possibly accompanied by an unknown second party—Wren, surname unknown, affiliation unknown.

Voss read the last line twice. *Affiliation unknown.* She disliked unknowns the way sailors disliked reefs. But she had interrogated enough broken couriers to assemble the shape of the thing: the loyalists had a spy inside their own operation, or beside it, or around it, and spies were useful precisely because they were unreliable. She would account for the variable when it surfaced. Variables always surfaced. People could not help becoming legible; it was their deepest weakness, deeper than love. Everyone wanted to be seen, and seeing was her profession.

"Captain." Lieutenant Brannic reined up beside her, a solid, unimaginative man with a face like a closed door. "Scouts report the shrine road is clear. But there's refugee traffic on the southern ridges. Columns of them. Hundreds, maybe more, moving toward the high pass."

"Refugees." Voss said the word flatly, tasting it. "Fleeing what?"

"The advance, Captain. Our advance. Word travels ahead of us." Brannic shifted in his saddle. "Some of the men are asking—"

"The men are not paid to ask." She closed the ledger. "Note the columns' positions and headings. If they cross our line of march, we funnel them. If they block the pass, we move through them. We are not in the business of refugees, Lieutenant. We are in the business of doors."

Brannic saluted and dropped back. Behind them, the anchor wagon groaned over a rutted stretch of road, and Voss felt the hum of it climb into her jaw—the iron harp with its patient hooks, sleeping, waiting for a throat to sing into.

Thunder rolled again off the eastern peaks, closer now, and she noticed the men glancing skyward, the way soldiers always did, the way animals did. Superstition bred in weather like mold in damp. Let them look. By the time this campaign ended, they would learn that storms were not gods and not omens. Storms were phenomena, and phenomena could be captured, harnessed, and sold.

That was the whole difference between her and the exiles and the loyalists and the ballad-makers, Voss reflected as the column wound upward into the gathering dark of Thornfell. They believed the world was full of doors that opened onto better places. She knew better. Doors opened onto whatever stood behind them holding the key—and she intended to be standing there, with her hand on the ring, when this one swung wide.

Ahead, lightning stitched the ridge-line, brief and white, illuminating for half a heartbeat the long line of wagons and grey riders crawling up the mountain's flank like a scar.

Voss did not glance at it. She had stopped finding weather beautiful twenty-two years ago, and she had never once missed it.

"Make speed," she said. "I want the valley mouth by tomorrow's dusk."

The column answered with the only hymn she had ever needed: two hundred pairs of boots, striking stone in time, marching toward the Door out of nowhere.
