## 14. The Price of the Anchor

The storm came before the soldiers did.

Sable felt it first as a wrongness in her teeth, a low hum beneath the ridge like a plucked string that would not stop singing. She had been checking the picket lines at dusk, moving among the refugees' lean-tos with a lantern she did not need—she could feel every living thing on this mountain now, warm and flickering, like coals banked in ash—and the hum rose through her boot-soles and settled into her bones with a familiarity that made her sick.

She knew that hum. It was the sound of her own magic, called and held and *bent* by something that was not her.

"Voss," she said to no one. The name came out flat, tasting of copper.

She found Wren at the eastern watchfire, sharpening a knife against a whetstone with the patient rhythm of someone who had done it ten thousand times. Around them the refugee camp sprawled along the ridge like a wound that refused to close: three hundred souls, maybe more, huddled under oilcloth and pine boughs, children asleep in piles of adults because there were not enough blankets to go around. Behind them, Thornfell's peaks stood black against the stars. Ahead, the pass narrowed toward the valley where the Last Door waited, though only Sable and Wren knew how close they truly were now. Two days. Perhaps less.

"Wren." Sable crouched beside them. "It's here."

The whetstone stopped mid-stroke. "How far?"

"Close enough that I can feel its anchor pulling at me." She flexed her fingers. They ached, deep in the knuckles, the way they ached before a storm broke. "It's like—someone has hooks in me. In the storm. Dragging."

Wren set down the knife. In the firelight their face was all planes and shadow, the scar above their brow silvered by flame. "Then we wake the camp."

* * *

They woke the camp quietly, which is to say they woke it the way a tide comes in—inevitable, unhurried, terrible. Sable walked the rows with Wren at her shoulder, touching shoulders, murmuring, and one by one the refugees rose and began to move. Mothers rolled children into blankets. Old men struck tents with hands that shook but did not fumble. A girl of perhaps nine gathered up a wooden bird with a broken wing and tucked it into her coat like it was made of glass.

Sable had learned their faces over the past days. The cooper from Marrow's Bend whose hands still smelled of sawdust. The twin sisters who had carried their grandmother on a litter for six days without complaint. The innkeeper who kept counting heads and coming up one short, his son lost somewhere behind them in the advance, and who counted anyway, every hour, because stopping would mean admitting it.

These people trusted her. That was the miracle and the horror of it. They had watched her call lightning down to shield their camp from Voss's scouts, had seen the sky bend around her like a bow drawn taut, and instead of fleeing her they had begun to leave offerings at the edge of her fire—bread, salt, a child's carved bird—and she did not know what to do with any of it except carry the weight.

"They'll hit the rear guard first," Wren said, spreading a scrap of map across a flat stone. Their voice was low, clipped, all business—the voice Sable had learned meant fear being put to work. "Voss wants the stragglers. Panic does half her work for her. If the column breaks and runs, we lose them into the dark, and the dark on this mountain kills faster than her battalion."

"So we don't let the column break."

"So we don't let the column break." Wren looked up. Firelight caught the edge of something raw in their face before they buried it. "I've drawn the wagons across the narrows. We hold there. You—"

"I know what I do."

"Sable." Wren caught her wrist. Their grip was careful, always careful, as if she were something that might go off. "The anchor. If it drags you—if you feel yourself slipping past where you can come back—you pull out. You hear me? Whatever happens to the rest of us, you pull out."

"The last time I pulled out, my family burned."

"The last time you didn't, so did everyone else's chance of living." Wren's voice cracked on the words, and they hated it, Sable could see them hate it. "I'm not asking you to be careful. I'm asking you to come back. There's a difference, and I need you to know it."

For a moment Sable said nothing. The hum in her teeth deepened, and far below, down the black throat of the pass, she felt the anchor *set*—felt it like a fishhook finding flesh, a cold precise tug at the storm that lived in her chest.

"It's found me," she whispered.

* * *

The vanguard came up the pass an hour before dawn, when the cold is deepest and men's blood runs thinnest, and Voss had chosen her hour well.

Sable stood on the narrows with the storm coiled around her arms like a second skin, and the first thing the anchor did was *pull*. It came through her like a hand thrust into a river to seize the current itself—not blocking her, not yet, but dragging at everything she raised, bending her lightning a degree off true, weighting her wind like wet wool. She threw a bolt at the leading rank of witch-hunters and watched it strike two paces left of where she had aimed, splashing off stone, and heard Captain Ilsa Voss's voice carry up the pass, calm as a woman reading scripture:

"There she is. Hold the anchor steady. Let her tire herself against us."

They had brought ladders and grappling lines and long pikes wrapped in copper—the copper to ground her strikes, the pikes to keep her people at distance. They had brought hymnals too; she could hear them chanting in the dark below, low and rhythmic, a sound designed to unmake nerve rather than flesh. The witch-hunter's hymn. Wren had told her once what it was for: not a battle-song but a *hunting* song, pitched to make a caller's magic stutter, to make the storm doubt itself.

And it worked. That was the worst of it. Every verse dragged at her like the anchor's twin, and Sable stood in the narrows with three hundred lives behind her and felt her own power turn foreign in her hands, a wild animal suddenly unsure whether it loved her.

"Hold the line!" Wren's voice cut through the hymn, sharp and clear. They had taken the left flank with a dozen of the younger men, armed with boar-spears and hatchets, and they moved like water finding cracks—fast, economical, brutal when brutality was needed. "Pikes low! Let them climb to you!"

The first hunters crested the barricade of wagons and died on boar-spears. The second wave came with shields locked and copper mesh overhead, and Sable tore the mesh away with a fistful of wind and hurled it screaming into the gorge. Lightning walked down the pass in white branches. Thunder filled the world to its edges. And through it all the anchor *pulled*, and the hymn *dragged*, and Sable felt herself being spent like a purse emptied coin by coin.

She was stronger than they knew. But they did not need to beat her. They only needed to outlast her.

* * *

It went wrong at the third hour, when the grey light came and showed everyone how tired everyone was.

A wedge of hunters broke through on the right, where the cooper from Marrow's Bend held a gap between two wagons, and they came through fast and low with crossbows, and Sable saw—one heartbeat, stretched thin as wire—a bolt arc toward the innkeeper who was still counting heads, still counting, and she moved without deciding to move, storm blazing up her spine, both hands rising to tear the bolt from the air—

And the anchor *wrenched*.

It was not pain, exactly. It was worse than pain. It was her own magic seized mid-flight and turned in her grip like a fish on a line, and for one terrible instant Sable Quintana stood in the narrows holding a thunderbolt she could not release and could not put down, and the storm screamed through her looking for anywhere to go, and she thought, with perfect clarity: *this is how it starts, this is how it started last time—*

The bolt turned toward the camp.

Toward the children. Toward the girl with the wooden bird.

"No—" Sable wrenched at it, hauling the lightning back against the anchor's drag, muscles tearing somewhere inside her that had no name, and the bolt crawled sideways, inch by inch, a serpent fighting its handler—

And then Wren was there.

Not running. Already there—had been moving since the wedge broke through, Sable realized later, had read the whole disaster a breath before she did. Wren stepped into the path of the deflected bolt with their body angled to take it, one arm flung wide as if to say *not this one, not these*, and the lightning took them across the shoulder and chest and threw them ten feet into the snow.

The world went very quiet for Sable after that.

What happened next, the survivors would tell differently for years. Some said the sky split open. Some said the storm cried out. What Sable knew was this: she stopped fighting the anchor and *became* the thing it was trying to drag, poured herself down the hook like poison down a vein, and found Ilsa Voss's storm-anchor on its iron carriage two hundred yards down the pass, surrounded by chanting priests, and she *unmade* it. Not gently. Not cleanly. She reached through her own agony into the heart of the machine that dared put hooks in her and she tore its copper heart out through its iron throat, and the explosion lit the pre-dawn like a second sunrise, and when the light faded the anchor was slag and the priests were scattered and the hymn had stopped.

The vanguard broke. Sable barely noticed. She was already running through the snow toward Wren.

* * *

They had laid Wren by the largest watchfire, and someone had cut away the burned shirt, and the wound ran from their collarbone down across the ribs—a lightning-strike's signature, a great branching bloom of burst vessels and seared skin. Bad. Not killing-bad, if the gods were kind, and Sable had stopped believing the gods were kind a long time ago, but the fever would come, and the fever on this mountain killed as surely as steel.

"You're supposed to pull out," Wren said. Their voice was a ruin, scraped raw by thunder or smoke. "That was the deal. You pull out."

"You stepped in front of my lightning."

"Somebody had to. You were busy." They tried to smile and mostly managed it. "Besides. I've been shot, stabbed, poisoned twice, and once thrown off a bridge in Caelport. This is hardly—"

"Don't." Sable took their hand. It was very cold. "Don't do the thing where you make it small."

Wren looked at her then, really looked, and whatever they saw in her face made them stop performing. "It was going to hit the camp," they said quietly. "You were bringing it back. I just... got there first."

"You got there first." Sable bowed her head over their hand. Around them the camp was taking stock—tents down, a wagon axle snapped, the cooper dead with his spear still in a hunter's throat, and the stores. The stores were worst. The blast and the melee together had taken the supply wagons' flank, and grain sacks lay split and trampled in the churned snow, and half of everything they had was gone, soaked into the mountain, feeding crows.

Half their food. Half their medicine. Two days from the Door, with three hundred mouths and a wounded spy and a storm-caller who had just torn a machine apart with her bare will and had nothing left in her but shaking.

"We lost the wagons," the innkeeper reported, appearing at her elbow with his endless head-count finally finished. He looked hollowed. "Half the grain, most of the salt meat. The twins are organizing what's left. People are..." He hesitated. "People are scared, miss. Some are saying we should never have followed you up here."

"Are they?" Sable asked.

"Some are saying it loud."

She nodded. She did not blame them. She looked at Wren, grey-faced and shivering by the fire, and at the ruined stores, and at the dark mouth of the pass ahead, and she felt the truth of the day settle over her like snowfall: they had won, and winning had cost nearly as much as losing would have.

Survival purchased with blood. Always that currency. Never anything else.

* * *

Night came again, and with it the first touch of fever.

Sable sat by Wren's side with a cloth and melted snow, doing the small arithmetic of nursing—cool the skin, count the breath, watch the eyes. Wren drifted in and out, and in the drifting they talked, fragments surfacing like debris after a flood.

"...should've told you about the signal sooner..."

"I know," Sable said.

"...the omission. The one I didn't tell you. When we get through the Door, I'll..." A frown moved across the burned face. "No. Before. I'll tell you before. You shouldn't carry it into a place like that."

"Then tell me now."

But Wren's eyes had closed again, breath going shallow and even, and the confession sank back beneath the fever like a stone into a lake.

Sable sat alone by the fire with the camp breathing around her—three hundred sleepers minus one cooper, minus one son still uncounted, minus whatever tomorrow would take—and she listened to the mountain wind and felt, faint and far below, the empty socket where Voss's anchor had been. Torn out. Dead. But anchors were tools, and tools could be rebuilt, and Ilsa Voss had not retreated so much as *withdrawn to reload*.

Two days to the Door. One wounded spy with a secret still held between their teeth. Half the food they needed, and a battalion somewhere in the dark, learning from its mistakes.

Sable fed the fire another branch and watched the sparks climb, and made herself a promise with the flavor of iron in it: whatever Wren was carrying, whatever confession waited on the other side of the fever—they would reach the valley first. She would open the Door. She would put every one of these people through it.

Whatever it cost. She was beginning to understand, now, exactly what shape that price would take.

And far down the pass, so faint she might have imagined it, the wind carried the sound of singing.
