## 15. Night of Confessions

### Sable

The fever came with the moonrise.

Sable had seen it before—on the smuggler's roads, in the holds of ships where a cut gone bad meant a burial at sea. It announced itself first as a shimmer in Wren's eyes, a brightness that had nothing to do with health. Then the shivering started, deep and rhythmic, like something trying to break out of the body from within.

They had made camp in the hollow beneath an overhang of granite, half a mile off the ridge road, far enough from the refugee column's fires that its smoke wouldn't draw eyes. The wound in Wren's side was a long, ugly tear where Voss's vanguard blade had found them—a wound meant for Sable, taken without hesitation, without even a shout. Sable had cleaned it with the last of the spirit, packed it with moss and the bitter leaves the herb-woman among the refugees had pressed into their hands, and stitched it by firelight while Wren bit down on a strap of leather and refused to make a sound.

Now the sound was worse. Now it was silence, broken only by the wet rasp of breath.

"Talk to me," Sable said, pressing the back of their hand to Wren's brow. Heat bloomed there like a struck flint. "Anything. Insult my cooking. Tell me I snore."

Wren's lips moved. What came out was barely a word at all. "Cold."

"I know." Sable piled on every blanket they owned, then the oilskin, then their own cloak, and sat close enough that their shared warmth might count for something. Outside, the wind combed through the high pines with a sound like distant surf, and somewhere below, the refugees' fires made a scatter of orange stars on the black shoulder of the mountain. Three hundred souls, maybe more, sleeping on stone because two strangers had told them a door out of legend was real.

And one of those strangers was burning alive from the inside.

The night stretched. Sable fed the fire stick by stick, measured out sips of melted snow laced with willow bark, and watched the fever climb. Wren's skin went from pale to flushed to a gray that frightened Sable more than any color. The delirium arrived sometime past midnight, arriving not as thrashing but as conversation—with people who weren't there.

"Maren." Wren's voice cracked on the name. "Maren, I didn't—I couldn't carry them all. You have to believe me."

Sable's hands went still over the fire.

"You said you'd wait," Wren whispered to the dark. "You said—" A shudder took them, head to heel. "I know what I am. I've always known. But not this. Don't ask me for this."

Sable sat with that for a long time, watching the flames lean and recover, lean and recover. They thought about all the things Wren had confessed at the ruined shrine—the resistance, the old royalist network, the truth of the erased kingdom—and how even then, even stripped bare, Wren had held something back. Some omission they were ashamed of, offered as a shape rather than a name.

Maybe it had a name now. Maren.

The wind rose. The fire guttered. And Sable, who had spent eleven years building a wall of silence around the worst night of their life, felt that wall begin, very quietly, to crack.

It wasn't decision so much as gravity. Wren had bled for them. Wren lay feverish and unguarded, speaking to ghosts, and here in this granite hollow there was no one left to perform for—no buyers, no hunters, no refugees depending on their steadiness. Just the fire, the wind, and a dying spy who deserved to hear a true thing, if true things could reach anyone through fever.

"Her name was Odessa," Sable said.

Their own voice startled them. Rough from disuse, in that particular way—rough from never being used for *this*.

"My mother. She was a net-mender in Vell's Hollow, a fishing village on the coast, forty houses and a chapel and a smell of salt and tar you never got out of your clothes no matter how far you walked." The words came slowly, each one hauled up from somewhere deep, like stones from a well. "She used to sing when she worked. Badly. Off-key in three directions at once. My father said the fish came up just to get away from it."

A sound escaped Wren—not quite a laugh, not quite a moan. Still fevered. Still listening, maybe. Sable chose to believe so.

"I was fourteen when the storms started answering me. Nobody taught me; nobody in Vell's Hollow had ever been a caller. It just—woke up. Like a second heartbeat. At first it was small things. Wind shifting when I was angry. Rain stopping when I begged it to. I thought it was luck." Sable turned their hands over, palms up, watching firelight pool in them. "Then the season turned bad, and the boats stopped going out, and I got angry at the sky, and the sky got angry back, and I learned I could do more than beg."

The fire popped. Somewhere above, a loose stone clattered down the slope.

"There was a storm coming that autumn. A big one—we'd all seen them before, you bar the shutters and you sit it out and you lose a roof or a boat and you rebuild. But I was fourteen, and I'd just learned I had power, and power is exactly the wrong thing to hand a fourteen-year-old who's scared." Sable's throat tightened. "I thought I could hold it off. Push it out to sea. I stood on the headland with my arms out like I was catching the whole weight of the sky, and for a while—for a few hours—I actually did it. I held a storm off my village. I remember feeling *proud*."

The memory rose the way it always did: complete, merciless, in full sensory detail. The ozone bite in the air. The green-black light under the clouds. The moment the pressure broke.

"But a storm isn't a thing you hold, it's a thing you are holding *with*. Every ounce of force I pushed against it, it pushed back through me. And I was tired, and I was scared, and near dawn I slipped. Just for a second. Just one second." Sable heard their voice go flat, the voice they used for prices and distances, the voice that lied least. "The storm came down all at once, everywhere, and it came down *through* me. Forty houses. The chapel. The boats in the harbor lifted and thrown like toys. Twenty-three dead, and I knew every name, because it was a village of forty houses."

Wren breathed. In, out. Wet and slow, but steady.

"My mother died holding onto me. Did you know that? Everyone else I never saw—they were just gone when I dug myself out. But she'd reached me. She was holding my arm when the water took her, and her last word was my name, and it wasn't angry. That's the worst part. That's the part I can't—" Sable stopped. Pressed the heels of their hands against their eyes. "It wasn't angry. She was still trying to comfort me. While she drowned. Because of me."

The wind outside rose to a keen and fell again. Sable let the silence sit until it hurt, and then kept talking anyway, because the wall was down now and there was no rebuilding it by morning.

"I ran. Of course I ran. I swore off the storm—swore it like an oath, like a religion. Eleven years I never called so much as a breeze. And then a dying courier put a map in my hands, and a witch-hunter put a knife at my throat, and a mountain dropped an avalanche on my head, and I called a storm to save us, and the world didn't end." A breath that shook more than Sable wanted it to. "And now there are three hundred people down that ridge following me toward a door, and the only reason Voss hasn't slaughtered them already is because I finally stopped hiding. Eleven years of guilt, and it turns out the guilt was never the price. The hiding was."

They looked at Wren—at the gray face, the sweat-dark hair, the bandage spotted rust-brown at the center. At the person who had taken a blade meant for them and hadn't yet asked for anything in return except, perhaps, in delirium, forgiveness from someone named Maren.

"So that's the ledger," Sable said quietly. "That's what you signed on to protect. A storm-caller who killed their own mother. If you wake up and want to walk away, I'll point you down the mountain myself."

The fire burned low. Sable fed it the last dry sticks and settled in to keep watch, and did not expect an answer, and did not get one.

Not for another hour.

### Wren

Fever is a country. Wren knew its borders well—had crossed into it twice before, once from a knife in Caelport and once from bad water in the Thornfell lowlands—and knew its customs: the way time went soft, the way the dead grew talkative, the way everything true became simple and everything simple became unbearable.

Maren was there. Maren was always there, in the fever-country, standing in the doorway of the safehouse on Rill Street with the light behind her, the way she'd been the last morning. *You said you'd wait,* Wren told her, the same argument they'd been having for six years. *You said you'd wait and I was three streets away when they came.*

*I know,* Maren said, in the voice the fever gave her, which was her real voice, which was the worst kindness of it. *I know what you are, Wren. Go be it somewhere useful.*

And then the fever shifted, the way it does, and Maren's voice became another voice—lower, rougher, weathered like sea-glass—and Wren realized with a strange floating clarity that the voice was real, that it was *here*, in the cold waking world, telling a story.

Telling *the* story.

Wren surfaced partway. Not awake—awake would come later, with pain—but risen enough to hear. To understand. The words fell into the fever like stones into dark water, and each one rang.

*A village called Vell's Hollow. A girl of fourteen with a storm caught in her chest like a bird. A mother singing off-key in three directions.*

Wren had read files. That was the trade—files, reports, the dry arithmetic of other people's catastrophes. There had been a file once, early in their training, about the disaster on the coast: an unregistered caller, a village destroyed, subject fled and presumed dead or scattered. The file had been four pages long. It had contained numbers. It had not contained a mother's arm, or a last word that wasn't angry, or eleven years of a person punishing themselves with silence.

*So that's the ledger,* the voice said. *That's what you signed on to protect.*

Wren wanted to say: *you fool.* Wanted to say: *do you think I don't know what a ledger looks like? Mine has a street on it, and a woman in a doorway, and thirty names of the network's dead because I was three streets too slow.* Wanted to say that guilt was a currency everyone in this war carried, and that the ones worth following were the ones who kept spending it on other people instead of hoarding it in the dark.

But the fever-country had its own rules, and speech was expensive there. What came out instead, slurred and small and utterly sincere, was:

"Not your fault."

The voice stopped. The fire ticked.

"Storms fall," Wren murmured, drifting, the words rising from some place below strategy, below duty, below the self that counted and calculated. "People stand in the rain. That's all. That's—all it ever is. You were standing in the rain."

A long quiet. Then, very softly, the voice said: "You're delirious."

"Yes," Wren agreed, and felt the ghost of a smile move across their face. "But I'm right. Ask me when I'm—when I'm better. I'll say it again. I'm very stubborn."

"You're burning up is what you are."

"Mm." The fever pulled at them, gentle as a tide. "Forgiven, though. Before you even asked. Write that down. Put it in your—your ledger. First entry in the black."

The voice made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth, or a sob wearing a laugh's clothes. A cool hand touched Wren's forehead—careful, calloused, trembling slightly—and Wren leaned into it the way a leaning thing leans, without policy or plan.

"Sable," Wren said. The name cost almost everything they had left. "Hey. Listen. The omission. The thing I didn't tell you at the shrine."

Silence. Attention. He could feel it, the whole of the other person gathered close like weather.

"It wasn't that I reported you. I never did. It's—" The fever dragged at him, but he fought it, because this mattered, because she deserved to hear it while she thought he couldn't lie. "The night I signaled my handler from the pass. I told them the courier was dead and the map destroyed. Before I ever met you. I burned the network's chance to retrieve it, on a gamble, on a *feeling*, because I'd watched you feed stray cats in Caelport and I decided you were worth betting a kingdom on." A breath. "So whatever happens next—whatever you find out about me—it started there. With me choosing you. Before knives. Before mountains. Before any of it made sense."

The hand on his forehead went very still.

"That's the confession," Wren whispered. "Even trade. Even—ledger." The tide was taking him now, warm and total. "Don't forgive that one yet. Make me earn it. I'm—stubborn. I told you..."

He went under. The fever-country closed around him like water, and the last thing he heard, faint and fierce and sounding very much like someone refusing to cry, was:

"Earn it, then. So live. *Live*, Wren—that's the payment. Everything else I'll take in installments."

### Sable

Dawn came up gray and clean over the pass, the kind of light that made the world look newly made and briefly forgivable.

Wren slept—not the drowning sleep of fever but something deeper and more ordinary, breath evening out, the terrible gray receding from their skin by degrees. Toward morning the shivering stopped. By full light, the heat had broken to warm instead of scalding, and when Sable pressed a cloth dipped in snowmelt to Wren's brow, their patient frowned in their sleep and muttered, distinctly, "*the third entry goes in the black, Maren, I told you*," and rolled over.

Sable sat back and laughed—one short, cracked, astonished sound that fogged in the cold air.

They stepped out of the hollow into the blue morning. Below, the refugee column was stirring, cook-smoke rising, children's voices carrying thin and bright up the rock. Two days' hard walking to the valley mouth. Perhaps three, with the wounded and the old.

Sable looked at their own hands, palms still bearing the memory of last night's gestures, and found that the story sat differently in them now. Not lighter—grief didn't lighten, it only changed shape—but *carried*, the way a load carried long enough becomes part of the body's architecture. Spoken aloud, it had edges. Edges could be gripped.

Behind them, Wren stirred and groaned awake, and Sable turned back toward the hollow, toward the fever-broken spy who had chosen them before there was any sense in choosing, toward the impossible arithmetic of a debt that could only be paid forward—down the ridge, through the pass, to a seam of light in a cliff face that was waiting, patient as a held breath, for what Sable would have to become to open it.

"Wren," they called softly. "How do you feel about earning your keep?"

From inside the hollow, hoarse but unmistakably wry: "Define *keep*."

Sable smiled, and shouldered the pack, and began breaking camp.
