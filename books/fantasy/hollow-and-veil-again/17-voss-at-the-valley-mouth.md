## 17. Voss at the Valley Mouth

### Sable

The storm Sable had called to shield the valley still hung overhead like a held breath, and into that breath came the sound of drums.

Not the wild percussion of a raiding party—something worse. Measured. Patient. The cadence of an army that knew it had already won and was merely arriving to collect. Each beat rolled down the valley walls and pooled in the hollow where six hundred refugees huddled among the ruins of a village that no map remembered.

Sable stood on the ridge above the Door, hands open at their sides, feeling the storm answer their pulse. Below, the last of the children were being moved toward the cliff face, where the seam of light waited—dormant now, patient as a sleeping animal. It would need everything Sable had left. Everything.

But first there was the matter of the battalion filling the valley mouth like black water poured into a bowl.

"They're forming lines," Wren said beside them, glass raised to one eye. "Pikes in front, crossbows behind. And—" the glass lowered slowly, "—that thing on the wagon."

Sable didn't need to look. They could feel it, the way a man feels a hook set in his shoulder before he sees the line. The storm-anchor sat on its iron carriage amid the witch-hunters' ranks, a cage of silver rods around a core of something that drank light. Even at this distance it tugged at the magic coiled in Sable's chest, a slow, greedy pull, like drowning in reverse.

"I felt it at the pass," Sable said. "It's been singing to me for two days. Ugly song."

"It's meant to be." Wren's jaw was tight. "Voss doesn't capture callers to kill them, Sable. She captures them to *use* them. That anchor can hold a storm-caller's power and pour it out through her own will. You'd become a weapon with someone else's hand on the haft."

"Then we'd best not let her catch me."

Wren looked at them then—a long look, the kind that carried more than either of them could afford to say aloud with an army watching. Since the night of confessions, since the fever broke and Wren's wound began its slow knitting, something had settled between them that neither had named. There was no time to name it now. Perhaps there never would be.

"Sable." Wren's voice was very quiet. "If this goes wrong—"

"It won't."

"If it goes *wrong*," Wren insisted, "I want you to know that everything I did—the signal, the handler, all of it—I'd do differently. But I wouldn't undo finding you. Whatever else happens on this ridge today, I need you to carry that through whatever door you open."

Sable's throat tightened. Below them, the drums stopped.

Into the silence rode a single figure on a grey horse, white cloak immaculate despite the mud of the march, moving up the valley floor alone. Captain Ilsa Voss stopped a hundred paces from the refugee camp, close enough to be heard, far enough to be safe from anything but weather—and she had come prepared for weather too. Sable could see the anchor's shadow bending toward her like iron filings toward a lodestone.

"She wants to talk," Wren said.

"Then let's hear what treachery sounds like when it's polite."

They walked down together. It was not courage that made Sable's legs move; it was arithmetic. Six hundred people against a battalion. One storm-caller against an anchor built to unmake them. Every step down the slope was a step into a ledger whose columns did not balance, and Sable had learned long ago that the only way to face such mathematics was to walk straight into them before they finished calculating your worth.

The refugees watched them go. Old Marta clutched her grandson's hand. The ferryman Doss, who had carried three hundred people across the flooded Vell in a single night, stood at the camp's edge with a woodcutter's axe held like a relic. They believed, Sable realized, that the two figures descending the slope were going to save them.

That belief weighed more than the anchor ever could.

Voss waited, reins loose, helmet under her arm. Up close she was younger than her reputation—grey at the temples only, eyes the pale flat blue of winter ice over deep water. She looked at Sable the way a jeweler looks at a stone: assessing cut, weight, flaw.

"Storm-caller," she said. Her voice carried without effort, pitched for the whole valley to hear. "Sable Quintana of Thornfell. Exile. Smuggler. Killer of your own kin, if the old reports are true."

The words landed like stones dropped in still water. Behind Sable, the refugees murmured. They had not known. Of course they had not known—Sable had kept that story locked even from Wren until fever pried it loose. Now six hundred people knew what walked beside them.

"You've read my file," Sable said evenly. "Impressive. I hope it mentioned I don't negotiate with butchers."

"I offer amnesty." Voss said it simply, without theater, which somehow made it worse. "Full pardon. Restoration of citizenship. Your crimes erased as thoroughly as this kingdom was erased. All of it—in exchange for one thing." She gestured, unhurried, at the crowd behind them. "The refugees. They come with me. Hostages, if you like the ugly word. Leverage, if you prefer the honest one. Malvane wants the free cities to understand that harboring his runaways has a price. You may walk away rich, pardoned, and *free*, Quintana. They were never going to be free anyway. You're only deciding who profits from that fact."

Sable heard the trap in it, elegant as all her work. Refuse, and Voss would say the caller valued their own freedom over six hundred lives. Accept, and the refugees went into slavery and Sable lived the rest of their life as the person who sold them.

And beneath the trap, the hook: every second spent talking was a second the anchor's song grew louder, threading itself into the storm overhead, learning its shape.

"You mistake me," Sable said loudly, for the valley, for the refugees, for the record of whatever spies Voss kept. "I'm not deciding anything about these people. They decided for themselves when they followed a smuggler and a spy toward a door out of legend. I'm just the one who opens it."

Something flickered across Voss's face—not anger. Respect, cold and brief, like sun on a blade.

"A pity," she said. "I hate wasting good material." She put her helmet on. "You have until dusk. Then my men come up the slope, and the anchor comes with them, and you'll learn what your storms feel like inside a cage."

She turned her horse. And that was when Wren stepped forward.

### Wren

Wren had rehearsed this moment for three days without admitting it, the way one rehearses a confession in the dark. Now, with Voss's horse half-turned and the whole valley holding its breath, the words arrived on their own, fully formed, as if they had been waiting years for permission.

"Captain Voss."

The name cracked across the valley. Wren stepped past Sable, out into the open ground between the camp and the witch-hunters' lines, arms spread slightly from the body—the posture of surrender, or of someone making themselves impossible to ignore.

Sable's hand caught their sleeve. "*What are you doing?*"

"What I should have done at the fish market," Wren murmured. "Trust me. Please."

Then louder, to Voss: "You're hunting a traitor. Someone inside the loyalist network who fed the resistance its routes, its codes, its maps. Someone who turned crown intelligence against the crown. You've torn apart half the network looking for them. Burned safehouses in Caelport. Hanged a printer's apprentice who couldn't possibly have been guilty." Wren's voice did not shake. Wren made certain it did not shake. "You can stop looking. I'm the one you want."

Voss reined in. Turned. Studied Wren with those winter-blue eyes, and Wren watched recognition arrive in them piece by piece—the way a lock recognizes a key, tumblers falling one after another.

"The handler's reports," Voss said slowly. "The signals from the ridge. The misdirection that cost us the Thornfell column." A pause, delicate as a scalpel. "Agent Wren. Twelve years of service. Decorated twice. The crown's best ear inside the loyalist underground." Something almost like grief crossed her face, and was gone. "Why?"

Because the crown stopped deserving loyalty the day it started burning villages, Wren thought. Because I watched a woman feed stray cats in a city that wanted her dead and understood that I had been on the wrong side of every war I'd ever fought. Because love is the only order I ever took that I intend to keep.

But none of that was useful here. What was useful was time.

"Does it matter?" Wren said instead. "I'm offering you the traitor you've hunted for a year. Myself, unresisting. My testimony—names, caches, every code the resistance ever trusted me with. In exchange, you delay your assault by one day. One day, Captain. Give me that, and Malvane gets the entire network in a cage instead of six hundred starving peasants who'll die of cold before spring regardless."

It was a lie braided through with truth, and Wren told it beautifully, because twelve years of espionage had taught Wren that the best sacrifices are the ones dressed as bargains. The truth underneath: every name Wren would give was already empty, every cache already cleared, every code already changed. The resistance had burned its own trails weeks ago. Wren was selling Voss ash and calling it gold—and buying, with the only currency left, a single day.

Behind Wren, Sable had gone very still. Wren did not look back. Looking back would have undone everything.

"One day," Voss repeated.

"One day. And my full confession, witnessed by your officers, sealed for the usurper's own eyes."

Voss was silent for a long moment. Wind moved her white cloak. Somewhere in the ranks behind her, the anchor hummed its hungry note, and Wren felt Sable flinch at the edge of hearing—or perhaps that was Wren's own heart.

"You understand," Voss said at last, quietly, "that I will execute you for this. Not today. But when your usefulness ends. There is no version of this where you ride away."

"I know," Wren said. "I counted on it. It's why the offer's credible."

For the first time, something human moved behind the ice in Voss's eyes—something that might, in another life, in another kingdom, have been admiration. She looked past Wren at Sable, at the refugees, at the seam of light faintly visible in the distant cliff like a scar of dawn.

"One day," she said. "Your confession begins at dawn tomorrow. Until then, you'll be held in my camp. And Quintana—" her gaze found Sable like a spear finding a shield's seam, "—if that door opens before dawn, the traitor dies first. Remember that while you decide how much their sacrifice is worth to you."

She wheeled the grey horse. Two hunters detached from the ranks and marched up the slope to take custody of Wren, pikes leveled, faces blank behind their masks.

As they closed around Wren's arms, Sable finally spoke, low and fierce enough to be a wound: "You absolute fool. You magnificent, impossible—"

"Open the door, Sable." Wren smiled, and it cost everything, and Wren let it cost everything because some prices are the point. "That's the only way any of this means anything. Open it wide enough that she can't close it again. Don't you dare spend one second of your storm grieving me. Spend it all on them."

The hunters pulled Wren away, down the slope, toward the black lines and the humming cage.

Wren did not look back. If Wren had looked back—at Sable standing alone on the scorched grass with the storm gathering in their hair and their hands trembling at their sides—it would have broken the performance. And the performance was the only wall standing between six hundred people and a battalion.

So Wren walked into captivity with their spine straight and their heart hammering out a rhythm that had nothing to do with fear and everything to do with the words they had not said, saving them, hoarding them, for a dawn that might never come:

*I chose this. I choose you. I would choose it again.*

### Sable

Night fell on the valley, and Sable climbed to the Door.

The seam of light in the cliff face pulsed faintly in the dark, patient, expectant, waiting for what Sable now understood it must be fed. Royal blood and storm-fire, the old shrine had said. Wren's blood had opened its secrets; Sable's storm would open its mouth. And once it began to drink, it would not stop until the caller who fed it was empty.

Sable pressed a palm to the warm stone and felt the vast slow heartbeat beneath.

"One day," Sable whispered to the dark. "She gave us one day, and it cost her everything. So we will not waste a minute of it."

Below, the refugees were already moving—packing, forming lines, mothers binding children to their backs, Doss sharpening his axe though everyone knew axes would mean nothing by morning. Old Marta caught Sable's eye across the firelight and nodded once, the nod of a woman who had buried a kingdom and intended to outlive its murderer too.

Sable turned back to the Door, and reached into the storm overhead, and began—to the sound of distant drums resuming in the dark—to gather everything they had ever sworn away.

Dawn was coming. The Door would be open before it.

Whatever it cost.
