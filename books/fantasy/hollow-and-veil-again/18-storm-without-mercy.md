## 18. Storm Without Mercy

The pass had a name once. The old maps called it the Throat of Veles, a narrow wound between two shoulders of granite where the wind came down like something starving. Now it had only smoke and screaming and the sound of Voss's battalion grinding up the switchbacks like a millstone turning.

Sable stood at the mouth of the valley with their boots planted in wet scree and let the storm find them.

It came the way it always had—first as a pressure behind the eyes, then as a taste of copper on the tongue, then as memory. Every storm they had ever called lived somewhere in their blood, waiting to be remembered: the squall off Caelport's breakwater that they'd pulled apart like thread; the dry lightning over Thornfell's high meadows that they'd coaxed into rain for a farmer who never learned their name; and beneath all of them, older and heavier than the rest, the storm that had come when they were nineteen years old and grieving and had not asked the sky for anything at all.

That one was always first in line. That one always wanted out.

*Not this time,* Sable told it. *You don't get to drive.*

Behind them, the refugees were moving—a river of people funneling toward the Door, children carried on hips, grandmothers leaning on sons, the wounded lashed to cart beds that jolted and groaned over the broken ground. Wren was there somewhere, white-faced, one hand pressed to the bandage at their side, shouting directions in a voice gone raw. Sable could not look back. If they looked back, they would see the faces, and if they saw the faces, they would remember what storms did to faces, and fear would put its cold hand around the power in their chest and squeeze until either the fear broke or they did.

So they looked forward instead, down the Throat of Veles, at what was coming.

Voss's vanguard filled the lower switchbacks like water filling a cup. Pikes in ranks, lanterns guttering under oilcloth, and above them all, riding a mule-drawn sledge like a relic on a war-altar, the storm-anchor. Even at this distance Sable felt it—a wrongness in the air, a dragging sensation, like hooks set gently in the flesh of the sky. It hummed. It *hungered*. Somewhere under its iron lattice, captured lightning turned in circles, dreaming of freedom it would never have, and Sable understood suddenly, intimately, what it must be like to be the thing inside the cage.

*That's what she wants to make of you,* said the old storm, whispering up through their bones. *A caged thing. A tool.*

*I know,* Sable answered it. And for the first time in eleven years, they answered it as an equal rather than a victim. *But not today.*

* * *

The first arrow came out of the dusk trailing fire.

Sable raised one hand—not a dramatic gesture, nothing a ballad would bother with, just a palm opening like a door—and the arrow's flame bent sideways, guttered, died. The shaft tumbled into the scree. Around it, the vanguard began to chant: the witch-hunter's hymn, low and rhythmic, words designed by scholars of suppression to bind and blind. Sable had heard it once before, from across a market square, and had run. They did not run now.

They breathed in.

And the sky came down to meet them.

It did not feel, this time, like drowning. That was the difference, and Sable clung to the difference the way a climber clings to a crack in the rock. Eleven years ago, the storm had poured *through* them, using their grief as a channel, wearing their body like a glove. They had been a doorway left open in a hurricane. But a doorway could become a keeper of the door. All those years of refusal—all those gray, careful, magicless years in Caelport, hating themselves, rationing even their anger so it wouldn't spark the sky—had not been wasted. They had been learning the shape of the thing they feared. You cannot hold a storm unless you know exactly how much of you it wants to take.

Sable knew now. To the ounce.

Lightning gathered overhead, not as a single blinding strike but as a slow, crawling luminance, sheet after sheet of it banking behind the peaks like clouds of burning wool. Thunder rolled down the Throat and met the hymn and swallowed it whole. Below, the front ranks faltered. Sable heard a sergeant screaming orders, heard the squeal of the anchor-sledge's wheels as the crew whipped the mules faster, and felt the anchor answer—felt it *pull*, hooks sinking into the gathering charge, trying to drag the storm sideways, trying to make it theirs.

Pain lanced through Sable's chest, real and physical, like fishhooks set behind the sternum. They gasped. Their knees dipped. The luminance flickered.

*There you are,* said the anchor, in no words at all, in pure appetite.

And here was the razor's edge, the place Sable had always fallen before. Because the instinct—the animal, human, terrified instinct—was to fight the pull. To wrench against the hooks. And every time they had ever fought their own power, they had torn loose wild, and wildness was the village burning, was the family dead, was eleven years of ash in the mouth.

So Sable did not fight. They leaned in.

They let the hooks catch—and then they *gave*, pouring more storm into the anchor than its lattice could drink, flooding the cage until the captured lightning inside screamed against its bars, until frost bloomed across the iron and the nearest handler recoiled with his gloves smoking. Somewhere below, metal shrieked. The anchor groaned like a ship in a gale.

"You want my storm?" Sable said aloud, and their voice came out doubled, harmonized with thunder. "Drink, then. Choke on it."

* * *

What followed was not a battle in any way the songs would recognize. There was no duel, no clash of champions. There was weather.

Sable walked the edge of the valley mouth, barefoot now—they had kicked off their boots without deciding to, because the stone spoke more clearly through skin—and everywhere they stepped, the mountain obeyed. Rain fell upward in the narrows, a curtain of silver beads that turned the witch-hunters' torches to hissing stubs and made the switchback stones treacherous as ice. Wind came down the Throat in measured gusts, each one placed, each one aimed—not the indiscriminate fist of the storm that had destroyed their village, but a craftsman's hand. A gust here to strip a shield wall's footing. A wall of hail there, small and stinging, to turn a column's flank. Fog pooled in the defiles like spilled milk, and in the fog, the battalion's perfect order dissolved into clusters of shouting men who could not tell friend from comrade from cliff-edge.

They were not killing. That was the discipline that cost Sable more than violence would have. Every gust they shaped, every bolt they grounded harmlessly into bare rock instead of flesh, was a choice made at full stretch, a muscle held trembling at the edge of cramp. The easy thing—the thing the storm *wanted*, singing its old seductive song of release and ruin—was to stop aiming. To simply open. Sable had done that once, and it had taken everything they loved.

"Control isn't the absence of the storm," they whispered, to the sky, to themselves, to the child they had been standing in a burning village square with lightning still leaking from their fingertips. "Control is holding its hand while it rages."

The thunder seemed to consider this. Then it leaned its weight against them, vast and testing, like a horse testing a new rider, and Sable held.

* * *

Twice, the edge nearly took them.

The first time was the anchor. Voss's engineers, desperate, unshackled its limiters, and the thing went from hunger to frenzy—a black vortex of stolen charge that tore the rain sideways and began, visibly, to *drink the sky*. Sable felt their own called storm thinning, siphoned away into that iron throat, and panic rose in them like floodwater: the old panic, the losing-panic, the I-am-being-taken panic. For three heartbeats they were nineteen again, powerless inside their own power.

Then Wren's voice cut through the gale—from somewhere behind and below, ragged but clear: *"Sable! Don't pull—FEED it!"*

Of course. Of course. The same trick, larger. Sable laughed—a wild, cracked sound torn away by the wind—and opened every gate they had. The storm they had banked behind the peaks, the storm they had been husbanding for hours, came down the Throat in a single answering peal, and the anchor drank, and drank, and drank, until its lattice glowed cherry-red and its handlers fled the sledge and, with a report like a felled tree, it burst. Shrapnel of iron and liberated lightning scattered across the lower switchbacks; the freed charge, drunk on sudden sky, grounded itself harmlessly into the wet stone in a hundred blue threads, like roots of light. The dragging hooks in Sable's chest vanished. They swayed, caught themselves, stood.

The second time was simpler and worse. A squad of hunters, braver or more foolish than the rest, scaled the eastern rim while the fog held the pass—six shadows with crossbows and a net weighted with anchor-shards, coming for the source of the storm, coming for *them*. Sable saw them late. Fear did the rest. Lightning leapt to their hand before thought, hungry, eager, begging to be loosed, and for one suspended instant Sable stood at the exact spot where their whole life had split in two, six targets in the dark and death coiled in their palm—

—and chose.

They brought the bolt down on the rock face between the squad and the path, splitting a shelf of granite that crashed across the climbers' route in a curtain of dust and rubble. Not on them. Near them. The squad scrambled back, alive, and Sable stood shaking in the rain with the unused death still crawling on their skin, and understood that this—this trembling restraint, this harder choice—was what mastery actually was. Not the absence of terrible power. The refusal to use it badly, again and again, every single second, forever.

Their family would have recognized them now, they thought. That was the thought that undid them, briefly, tears indistinguishable from rain. They let it. Grief and storm moved through them together, and neither one burned the world down.

* * *

By the time true night fell, the Throat of Veles belonged to the weather.

Voss's battalion had broken contact, falling back down the switchbacks in good-enough order to prove their captain had not finished learning her lesson—but broken nonetheless. Sable watched their lanterns shrink and scatter through the rain like embers washing downstream. They kept the fog thick and the hail cycling anyway, hour after hour, because exhaustion was a luxury and the last refugee carts were still crossing the high meadow toward the valley, toward the Door, toward the seam of light that Sable could feel at their back like a second heartbeat.

Wren found them there, near midnight, standing barefoot in the rain with lightning walking gentle circles around their shoulders like a trained thing, like a companion. Wren stopped a few paces off, favoring their wounded side, and for a long moment neither of them spoke. The rain fell around them both and touched neither, held off by a courtesy of the storm that Sable hadn't consciously arranged.

"You're doing it," Wren said finally. Their voice was hoarse with shouting and fever and something else. "Holding it. All night. I've been watching." A crooked, disbelieving almost-smile. "You're not losing yourself."

"No," Sable said. And found that the word fit. "I'm finding out I was never lost. Just... carrying too much alone."

Wren crossed the distance between them and pressed their forehead to Sable's, rain and fever-heat and blood-smell and ozone, and for a moment the storm itself seemed to go quiet, listening.

Then the quiet broke.

Because down in the dark of the Throat, where the lanterns had scattered and retreated, new lights were kindling—dozens of them, then hundreds, a rising constellation in the black. And threading up through the rain, faint but unmistakable, came a sound that froze the blood colder than any anchor ever had: voices. Hundreds of voices, chanting the witch-hunter's hymn in unison, no longer a weapon but a *choir*.

Voss had not retreated. Voss had regrouped. And whatever she was raising down there in the dark, she was raising it with every soldier she had left.

Sable lifted their head from Wren's and turned to face the lights, and the storm gathered close around them like a mantle.

"She's coming back," Sable said. "Tonight. Before the last of them reach the Door."

The thunder, low and ready, agreed.
