## 20. One Side of the Veil

Wren had stood at death's door before. Twice, in the service of a crown that no longer existed, they had felt its cold breath on their neck—once beneath the gallows-tree at Vellmark, once in the flooded cells below Caelport where the water rose by inches and no one came. Both times, Wren had bargained, schemed, lied their way back into the light. That was what spies did. They did not accept doors. They went through them, or around them, or they picked the lock.

So it took Wren far too long to understand what they were seeing.

The Door was open. It hung in the cliff face like a wound in the world's skin—a seam of white-gold light taller than three men, humming with a note that lived in the teeth rather than the ears. Through it: green hills, smoke from honest chimneys, the grey glitter of the free cities' harbors under a kind sun. Refugees poured through in a ragged river—grandmothers clutching salt-boxes, children carrying nothing but each other, a one-eyed ferryman herding forty souls with a broken oar for a shepherd's crook.

And Sable stood at the threshold with both hands raised to the storm.

Wren saw it then, the thing they should have seen hours ago, on the ridge, when Sable first touched the Door and flinched as if it had bitten them. The light did not feed itself. It drank. Every thread of lightning that coiled off Sable's fingers, every rolling growl that answered their heartbeat—the Door took it all, swallowed it, burned it as fuel to hold the seam wide. And Sable gave. Freely. Endlessly. The way a candle gives, which is to say without any thought of tomorrow.

"Sable." Wren's voice cracked against the storm-noise. They pushed through the crowd, shoulder past the ferryman, grabbed Sable's arm—and let go instantly, because the skin beneath the sleeve was cold as riverbed stone. "Sable, look at me."

Sable looked. Their eyes were still theirs—grey-green, sharp, alive with fury and focus—but behind them, Wren could see the cost accumulating like silt in a harbor. The fine tremor in the jaw. The blood at the corner of the mouth, black in the storm-light. The way Sable's knees locked, not standing so much as *braced*, like a mast in weather it was never built for.

"You're feeding it," Wren said. "You're *feeding* it. With everything."

"It has to be fed." Sable turned back to the light. A knot of refugees stumbled through—a mother with twins, an old man who wept openly as the free cities' sunlight touched his face. "Every second I hold it, more of them cross. Do the arithmetic, Wren. You're good at arithmetic."

"The arithmetic ends with you dead."

"The arithmetic ended with me dead a long time ago." Sable smiled, and it was the worst thing Wren had ever seen—not bitter, not brave, just *settled*. A debt being paid. "This is the only door out of a kingdom that doesn't exist anymore. When I stop, it closes. Forever. There is no second opening. There is no key. There is only this, and now, and everyone still on the ridge."

* * *

They argued there, at the edge of the light, while the storm raged above the valley and neither of them raised their voice, because raising a voice would have meant admitting what the argument was actually about.

"Voss is retreating," Wren said. "Broken. Her battalion is running for the pass. In an hour, maybe two, you can stop. Rest. We find another way—"

"There is no other way. You told me yourself, at the shrine. Royal blood and storm-fire. The resistance spent thirty years looking for another caller. There isn't one. There's me, and I'm it, and I'm here."

"Then I'll stay too."

"No." Flat. Immediate. The word fell like a gate dropping.

"Yes." Wren stepped closer, into the arc of stray lightning that haloed Sable's shoulders, and did not flinch even when it bit at their cheek and left the smell of ozone in their hair. "You don't get to decide this alone. You've been deciding alone your whole life—it's why you're so bad at it. I'm staying."

"You have people waiting for you." Sable's voice frayed. "Caelport. The network. Children who call you *the sparrow* because you bring them candied ginger out of smugglers' caches—don't look surprised, I've known since the fish market, you talk in your sleep—"

"That was one time—"

"—and a whole kingdom's worth of stories that need telling, and someone who survived this to tell them. That's you. Not me. I buried my story in a village grave eleven years ago; I've just been walking around on top of it."

"And what am I supposed to do?" Wren heard their own voice break and hated it, hated the nakedness of it, hated that the storm would carry it into every ear within a mile. "Walk through that door? Turn around on the other side and watch it seal with you on this one? Spend the rest of my life drinking tea in some free city café, knowing that somewhere behind a veil of light, the person I—" The word snagged. Wren forced it through anyway, because if they were going to lose, they would lose honestly. "The person I love is pouring themselves into a hole in the world?"

Sable went very still.

The storm went still with them—for one impossible heartbeat, every bolt hanging motionless like rain in a painting—and in that silence Wren saw the truth land in Sable's eyes, saw it hurt worse than any anchor-hook Voss had ever dragged through their magic.

"Oh," Sable said softly. "*Oh.* You absolute fool. You beautiful, stubborn, loyalist fool."

"I had it handled," Wren said. "I had a whole speech prepared. Very dignified."

"Forget the speech."

And Sable kissed them.

It was not a gentle kiss. It tasted of copper and rain and the electric tang of a sky about to split, and Sable's hands—cold, so cold, cold as the Door itself—framed Wren's face as though memorizing it. Wren kissed back with everything they had, with eleven years of watching and wanting and lying for love's sake, with the desperate arithmetic of someone counting seconds and finding them all stolen. Lightning crawled down Sable's spine and jumped to Wren's lips and Wren did not care, would never care, would stand in this fire until it unmade them both—

Then Sable broke away, gasping, and the storm roared back to life around them, and Wren understood with terrible clarity that the kiss had cost Sable something. Focus. Seconds. Somewhere behind them, the light of the Door had flickered, and a woman crossing with a child had cried out in fear.

"Don't," Sable whispered. "Don't make it harder. Please. I have exactly enough strength for one thing, and I've already spent half of it loving you."

"Then spend the rest wisely," Wren said, "and *stop*."

* * *

They fought after that. Properly, viciously, the way only two people who knew each other's worst truths could fight.

"You swore," Wren snarled, backing Sable toward the cliff wall while refugees streamed between them like fish through a net. "On the road, night nine, campfire—you swore you'd never again choose the storm over the living. This is that. This is exactly that. You're choosing a door over every person who loves you."

"I'm choosing four hundred people over one."

"One! One person who—" Wren's voice splintered. "Do you think I will survive it? Do you think I walked through witch-hunters and avalanches and my own treason to deliver you to a *wall*?"

"I think you'll survive because you always survive." Sable's hands shook; the storm shook with them; a bolt struck too close and the crowd screamed and surged. "That's your whole cursed talent, Wren. You survive things. Go survive this."

"No." Wren planted themselves in front of Sable, arms spread, absurd, immovable—a single body blocking a storm. "Change the terms. Give me the storm. Teach me, lend it, throw it—I don't care. Two callers, half the cost each. There has to be a way to share the weight—"

"There isn't. I asked the Door." Sable laughed, hollow and awful. "It doesn't bargain. It's not Malvane. It takes what it takes."

"Then I'm not leaving. Last child through or not, I'm not leaving. I'll stand right here until the veil seals and whatever happens after that happens to both of us."

Something moved behind Sable's eyes—temptation, horrible and bright—and Wren watched Sable kill it, watched them strangle it with the same iron discipline that had held a catastrophic storm leashed for three days straight.

"If you stay," Sable said, very quietly, "I will stop holding the Door. Right now. Four hundred people stay in a dead kingdom with Voss's battalion regrouping in the pass. Is that what you want? Because that's the price of your grand gesture. My life for your company, and everyone else pays the difference."

Wren stared at them. The storm howled. And slowly, hating every inch of it, Wren lowered their arms.

"You'd really do it," they said. "You'd really call my bluff with four hundred lives."

"I'd do anything to keep you alive." Sable's voice finally cracked clean through. "That's the whole disease of me, Wren. I killed my family trying to protect them. I will kill myself keeping you safe, and I will not apologize for it, and you will *go through that door*."

* * *

The last child came at dusk.

She was six, maybe seven, a refugee from the salt-villages with a burn scar down one arm and a wooden horse clutched to her chest—the last of the column, separated from her aunt in the panic at the valley mouth, found at last by the ferryman and carried up the slope wailing. She stopped when she saw the Door. All children stopped when they saw the Door. But this one looked past it, past the light, to the two figures standing in the storm, and she tugged the ferryman's sleeve and pointed at Sable.

"Is the storm lady coming?" she asked. Loudly. The way children ask things. The way children ask things *in front of everyone*.

The crowd nearby went quiet. Hundreds of faces—faces Sable had shielded at the ridge, fed from Sable's own packs, led through Voss's fire—turned toward the threshold. Toward the storm-caller who had given everything.

Sable knelt. It cost them visibly; their knee buckled and Wren caught their elbow and pretended not to. Up close, the child's burn scar was old, long-healed, and Sable touched it with one shaking finger.

"What's your name?" Sable asked.

"Pell."

"Pell. Can you keep a secret?" Sable leaned close, and though the whole hillside leaned close with them, only Pell heard what was said. Whatever it was, it made the child nod solemnly, wipe her nose on her sleeve, and walk through the Door with her chin up like a queen crossing a border.

Then Sable stood, and turned to Wren, and the look on their face was the end of all arguments.

"Go," Sable said. "Please. While I still have the strength to watch you go and keep smiling."

Wren did not move. The Door hummed. The storm wept lightning into the valley. Behind Wren, the ferryman and a dozen others began, gently, remorselessly, to herd them backward toward the light—hands on shoulders, voices soft, survivors doing the mathematics that Sable had refused to do aloud: that the Door needed feeding, that the feeder was staying, and that the one person Sable loved could not be permitted to stand here and unmake that choice with their presence alone.

"Wren." Sable's voice was almost tender now. Almost at peace. "Look at me. Eleven years I've been hollow. You filled me up just enough to know what I was missing. That's not a small thing. That's the biggest thing that ever happened to me. Let it be enough."

"It's not," Wren said. "It was never going to be enough, and you knew that when you kissed me, and you did it anyway, and I hope it haunts you—"

"I hope so too."

"—and I am NOT—" Wren's voice tore loose entirely, raw and young and furious with grief, "—I am not leaving until the last child is through, and the last child is through, so you don't get to—"

Hands pulled them. The crowd, gentle and pitiless, pulled them toward the light, and Wren fought—not hard, not truly, because some treacherous part of them understood that fighting meant Sable spending strength to hold the Door against a struggle, meant seconds bleeding away from the crossing—and in that hesitation, that fatal merciful hesitation, the ferryman's grip closed like iron and the light took them.

The last thing Wren saw, on this side of the veil:

Sable, standing alone in the storm, one hand raised to the sky, the other pressed flat against the Door's frame—as if holding it open were the same as holding on.

Mouth moving. No sound coming through.

But Wren had learned to read lips across dark courtyards and crowded markets, in a dozen languages, under worse conditions than these.

*I love you. Live loudly.*

Then the light swallowed everything, and the world on the other side was green hills and honest chimney-smoke and a silence where a storm used to be—

—and Wren came out the far side screaming, clawing at the air, at the closing seam, at the hands that held them, as behind them the Door narrowed to a ribbon, to a thread, to a line of gold drawn thin across the face of a cliff in a kingdom that no longer existed.

And then it was gone.

And Wren stood in the free cities' evening sun, in a field of refugees falling to their knees in gratitude, and felt absolutely nothing at all except the shape of a hand pressed flat against a doorframe, holding it open, holding it open, holding it—

No.

Wren spun. The cliff face was blank stone. Blank, blank, blank—but the locals, the ones who had crossed generations ago, spoke of echoes. Of storms heard through stone. Of a valley that sometimes, on wild nights, smelled of rain.

Wren pressed their palm flat against the rock. Cold. Solid. Silent.

"Eleven years," they whispered to the stone, to the storm, to the person inside. "Give me eleven years. I'll find a way back. I found you once through a kingdom that didn't exist. I'll do it again."

Behind them, Pell tugged at their sleeve, wooden horse tucked under one arm, and asked the question that would shape everything that came after:

"Are you the one who's going to tell us the storm lady's story?"

Wren looked at the child. At the hundreds gathering. At the horizon where the free cities glittered, and the future waited, unbearably bright and unbearably empty.

"Yes," Wren said, and their voice did not shake, because spies are good at that, at sounding steady when the ground has gone out from under them. "Yes, I am. But first—"

They turned back to the blank stone one last time.

"—first, somebody tell me everything about this valley. Every legend, every echo, every rumor of doors. Because I have a promise to keep, and I intend to keep it *loudly*."
