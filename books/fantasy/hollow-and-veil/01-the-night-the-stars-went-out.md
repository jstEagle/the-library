## 1. The Night the Stars Went Out

The trouble with smuggling moon-orchid tincture through Thornfell, Mara Dravenmoor had long ago decided, was not the guards, nor the tolls, nor the mud that came up to a horse's fetlock and stayed there like a grudge. The trouble was that it was boring. And boredom, in Mara's experience, was the universe's favorite moment to drop something on your head.

They were three miles from the border cairns, leading their mule Biscuit along a smuggler's cut so old it predated the kingdom's maps, when the stars began to go out.

Not all at once. That would have been dramatic, and drama drew eyes. No — they went out politely, one by one, like candles being snuffed by a careful hand. Mara noticed it first as an absence: a patch of sky over the Thornfell ridge where there should have been the Weaver's Chain, seven bright sisters every child learned to count. There was nothing there. Just black.

Mara stopped. Biscuit stopped too, because Biscuit had better sense than most people.

"Huh," said Mara, to no one.

Then the oath-mark burned cold.

It sat over their heart — everyone's did, in this kingdom; Veyl was the silent god, the god who kept oaths instead of granting wishes, and his mark warmed gently when you spoke true and stung when you lied. It had been a mild, forgettable warmth for eleven years, since the night Mara swore themselves into the trade with a knife, a ledger, and a lie about their name. It had never once burned cold.

Cold, it turned out, felt like swallowing winter. Like a key turning in a lock they hadn't known was in their chest.

Mara pressed a palm flat against their sternum and looked up again. Three more stars went out while they watched. The dark didn't spread like cloud cover — clouds had edges, texture, honesty about themselves. This dark simply *was*, as though those pieces of sky had never held anything at all.

"Right," Mara told Biscuit. "We're going faster."

Biscuit expressed an opinion about this involving her ears, but she walked.

* * *

The waystation at Hollowmere Ford was supposed to be lit.

That was the first wrong thing. Old Fenwick ran the station the way other men ran temples — with ritual, with pride, with a lantern hung in every window so that travelers could find him from a mile off and grumble about the toll from half of it. Fenwick believed light was a courtesy owed to strangers, and he'd once lectured Mara for twenty minutes on the subject while charging them double for oats. Mara had liked him enormously.

The windows were dark. All of them.

Mara left Biscuit tied at the tree line — habit; you never brought a nervous animal into a quiet building — and went up the path with their hand resting easy on the knife at their hip. The wards along the fence posts flickered as they passed. Border wards, ward-singers called them hearth-wards, little woven spells that hummed against the skin like a cat's purr if you knew how to listen. Mara didn't know how to listen, exactly, but after eleven years of running cargo past them they knew what a healthy ward felt like: a faint resistance, a sense of being *noticed*. These felt like walking through cobwebs someone had already torn down. They flickered — guttered, really — and above the roofline of the waystation, another star went out.

The door wasn't locked. Fenwick locked his door against bandits, tax collectors, and his own nephew, in ascending order of threat.

Inside, the common room was cold. The fire in the hearth had been built but never lit — logs stacked neat as an offering, kindling beneath, flint sitting on the mantel where Fenwick always left it. As though he'd been interrupted mid-thought. As though he'd forgotten what fire was *for*.

"Fen?" Mara said. Their voice came out smaller than intended. "Fenwick. You've got a customer, you old miser, and I'm not paying full rate."

Nothing. Then, from the back room, a sound like wind through a keyhole.

Mara found him in his counting room, slumped in his chair behind the ledger desk, and for one merciful heartbeat they thought he was only asleep. He was a big man, Fenwick, built like his own furniture, gray-bearded and permanently unimpressed. Sleep sat strangely on him anyway — his face was slack in a way faces weren't meant to be, smoothed of everything, the deep lines around his mouth gone shallow as if something had sanded him down.

Then Mara saw the smoke.

It rose off him. There was no other word for it. Thin gray wisps lifting from his skin — from his hands, his temples, the hollow of his throat — curling upward and unraveling before they reached the ceiling. Not burning. Not ash. It moved like memory moves behind closed eyes, shapeless and insistent, and where it lifted away, Fenwick's skin beneath went pale and blank as fresh paper.

"Fenwick." Mara crossed the room in three strides and caught his shoulder. Cold. Not dead-cold, not yet — there was a thread of warmth under the collarbone, stubborn as a banked coal — but cooling. "Hey. Hey, look at me. What happened? Who came through here?"

His eyes opened. That was worse, somehow. They were gray. Fenwick's eyes had been brown as walnut wood; Mara had seen them every month for six years, usually narrowed over a scale, weighing their honesty and finding it wanting. These eyes were gray as the sky over the ridge, gray as the empty places where stars used to be, and they looked at Mara without a single flicker of recognition.

"Who—" His voice was a ruin, dry leaves and dust. "Who are you?"

"It's Mara. I come through every month, you charge me double for oats and I complain and we both pretend I don't notice." They kept their grip on his shoulder, kept their voice level, because panic is contagious and he had enough of something already. "Fen, what happened here? Was someone here tonight?"

He frowned. The frown cost him visibly, like a man hauling a bucket up a very deep well. "There was... someone. A traveler. Gray..." His brow creased deeper. "Gray something. Eyes. Coat. A man, I think. Or—" The thought slipped through his fingers; Mara could see it go, could see the exact moment it left him, because his face went smooth again, sanded, blank. "Was there someone? Why am I..."

"You were telling me about a traveler," Mara said steadily. Inside, something was screaming. "Keep going. Gray coat. What did he want?"

"Directions," Fenwick whispered. Something surfaced in the gray eyes — fear, maybe, the oldest kind, the kind that doesn't know its own name. "No. Not directions. He asked... he asked me to remember something. To hold it for him. He said it was heavy and he needed somewhere to set it down, just for a moment, just to rest, and I said—" His hand twitched toward his own chest, toward the place where a man keeps the things he can't afford to lose. "I said of course. Of course, traveler, sit down, rest your—"

He stopped. The smoke thickened. It poured off him now in ropes, and Mara watched — helpless, horror rising slow and cold as floodwater — as whole shapes formed in it and dissolved. A woman laughing. A boy on a pony. A debt repaid. A song, half a bar of a song, gone before it finished. Fenwick's life, leaving him in wisps, and he couldn't even grieve it because grief requires remembering what you've lost.

"No," Mara said. "No, you give that back, you—"

But there was nothing to say it to. Nothing to fight. The traveler was gone; the smoke went wherever smoke goes; and Fenwick's eyes drifted shut again, and this time when Mara pressed fingers to his throat they found only the last ember of warmth, fading.

He died quietly, between one breath and the next, without a name in his mouth. Mara wondered — and would wonder for a long time after — whether he'd known his own name at the end. Whether anyone does, once everything else goes.

For a while Mara simply knelt there, one hand still on a dead man's shoulder, doing the arithmetic of dread.

Because here was the thing. Here was the thing they had built their whole invisible life on top of and never once examined, the way you never examine the ground: Mara Dravenmoor had a childhood. Everyone did. But when they reached for theirs — reaching now, frantically, the way you pat your pockets after hearing a pickpocket mentioned — they found the shape of it without the substance. A village. A grandmother's kitchen, yes, but what color were the walls? A grandmother's voice, certainly, but what did she call them? There had been a sister, hadn't there — or a cousin, or a neighbor girl, someone who dared them to climb the mill roof — and the mill roof itself was there, solid, but the person beside them on it was a smudge, a gap, a star-shaped absence in a familiar sky.

They had never questioned the holes. Holes were normal. Everyone's past had soft edges.

Everyone's past didn't leak smoke.

Mara stood. Their legs were steadier than they deserved. Out the window, over the Thornfell ridge, the sky was missing a constellation entire now, and the border wards along the fence had gone dark as snuffed wicks, and the mark of Veyl over their heart burned cold and colder, like a god shouting in the only language he had left.

"Okay," Mara said aloud, to the empty room, to the dead man, to themselves. "Okay. Someone has to remember all this. Someone has to write it down before it — before whatever this is gets hungry enough to cross the ford."

They took Fenwick's ledger off the desk — the big one, the one with thirty years of travelers and tolls and grudges recorded in his crabbed hand — and wrapped it in oilcloth, and tucked it inside their coat against their heart, next to the cold-burning mark.

At the door they paused. Behind them, Fenwick sat in his chair amid the last thin wisps of himself, looking almost peaceful, looking like a man who had finally set down something heavy.

"Double rates for oats," Mara told him softly. "You absolute thief. I'll remember it. Somebody should."

Outside, Biscuit whuffled at them, and Mara swung up into the saddle and turned the mule's head away from the border, away from the delivery, away from the invisible life they'd spent eleven years building — because invisible things, they understood now, could be erased, and the erasing had already begun.

Behind them, over Hollowmere Ford, the last star in the western sky went out.

Ahead, on the road toward Grayhollow, something waited that would change the shape of everything — though all Mara knew then was that the night had gotten very dark, very fast, and they were suddenly, desperately afraid of forgetting why they were riding.
