## 2. What the Wards Were Made Of

Sera Ilyich had been singing to the border wards for eleven years, and in all that time the wards had never once told her a lie.

Wards were honest things. Woven from recorded memory and anchored in song, they could not dissemble, could not flatter, could not pretend to be stronger than they were. When a ward thinned, it sang thin—a reedy note where there should have been a full chord. When a ward cracked, it cracked audibly, like lake ice in springtime. A ward-singer's whole profession rested on this honesty. You listened, you mended, you listened again.

Which was why, standing on the wall at Thornfell Gap with her ear pressed to the cold stone of the anchor-stone, Sera knew with absolute certainty that something was very wrong indeed.

The ward wasn't broken. Broken wards made noise. This one was making *nothing*—a silence shaped exactly like the place where sound used to be, the way your tongue keeps returning to the gap where a tooth was pulled. She ran her fingers along the stone's carved channels, feeling for the thread of the weave, and found smoothness. Not damage. Smoothness. As though someone had sanded away a section of the pattern and polished the edges so neatly you'd never know anything had been there at all.

"Ward-singer." The guard captain's voice behind her had the particular flatness of a man delivering bad news he'd been ordered to deliver. "The Magistrate requests your presence."

"The Magistrate can request it while I work," Sera said, not turning around. "There's a hole in the Thornfell ward the size of a barn door and no one seems interested in the interesting part, which is that there are *no torn edges*."

"Captain," said a second voice, silkier, "the Magistrate did not request. He summoned."

Sera turned. The man in the doorway of the watchtower wore court gray and had eyes the color of unpolished pewter—gray, she would think later, so very gray, gray enough that she couldn't afterward have sworn to his face at all. Only the eyes stayed with her, and only as a color, the way a note stays after the instrument is packed away.

"You're not from the Magistrate's office," she said.

"I'm from the Crown." He smiled. It was a perfectly adequate smile, the kind sold by the yard. "You've reported irregularities in the border wards for three years running. Your reports have caused distress at court. The Crown wishes to understand why a journeywoman singer of middling rank believes she knows more about the king's wards than the king's own assessors."

"Because I listen to them," Sera said, "and your assessors read ledgers."

"*Middling* rank," he repeated, gently, as if that were the wound.

"It is my rank. I'm proud of it. Middling means I do the work instead of the politics." She wiped her hands on her coat and finally gave him her full attention, which was, she'd been told, like being looked at by a lighthouse. "Now. Either arrest me or help me. There's a third option where you stand there being decorative, but I'll warn you, I narrate when I'm nervous, and I'm always nervous, and I go on *at length*."

He arrested her.

* * *

The cell in the Thornfell magistrate's house was clean, cold, and equipped with one cot, one bucket, one window too high to see out of, and one guard named Doss who talked the way other men breathed.

"—so what I don't understand," Doss said, for the fourth time, "is how you sing a *building*. My mum sang, but she sang songs. 'The Miller's Lament.' 'Six Ducks Went Over.' Songs with words in them."

"Wards are woven from recorded memory," Sera said, also for the fourth time, because Doss was the first person in two days who'd asked an actual question. "Someone remembers a thing—really remembers it, holds it whole—and the weaving takes that remembering and spins it into the pattern. Old strong memories make old strong wards. That's why the great border wards were commissioned from the royal archive. Kings remember their kingdoms the way mothers remember births. Nothing holds shape like that."

"So the ward's made of the king *remembering*?"

"Of the record of it. Memory taken down true and bound into thread. The king doesn't feel it going. It's a copy, not the thing itself." She paused. "Was. Mostly."

"Mostly?"

Sera lay back on the cot and looked at the ceiling. Two days in a cell, and here was the thing she kept circling: the wards hadn't failed. They'd been *eaten*. And eaten so cleanly, so completely, that every assessor in three provinces had looked straight at the gaps and seen nothing at all—because the gaps had been polished over, smoothed, made to look like they'd never held anything. Except to a singer's ear. To a singer's ear, the silence screamed.

"Doss," she said. "If I showed you proof that the wards weren't broken by any enemy you've got a name for—that they're being *consumed*, year over year, by something that leaves no scar—would you get in trouble for listening?"

"Probably," Doss said cheerfully. "I'm always in trouble. Me and trouble are wed."

"Then come here and listen anyway."

She put his hand flat against the cell wall—not an anchor-stone, nothing, just wall—and hummed the low finding-note ward-singers used, the one that made hidden weaves ring faintly audible, like wet glass rubbed at a rim. Even through plain mortar, the Thornfell ward's edge-note came through, thin and wrong.

Hear it? she mouthed.

Doss frowned. Then his frown deepened. Then he took his hand off the wall as if it had bitten him.

"That's—" He swallowed. "That's like the quiet in my gran's room after she passed. Like where somebody *was*."

"Yes," Sera said softly. "Exactly like that. Now imagine that quiet spreading along the whole border, a finger's width every season, and every assessor certifying the wards sound and whole because the theft is *tidy*. Because whatever's eating them eats the evidence of eating too." She sat up. "Three years of reports, Doss. Three years of being told I'm ambitious, hysterical, or mad—mad's the popular one, mad comes with a pension. And every year the quiet gets wider."

Doss looked at her for a long moment. Then he did something small and enormous: he sat down on the floor across from her cell bars, at eye level, like a man settling in.

"Tell me what you need," he said.

* * *

What she needed, it turned out, was the assessment ledger from the spring convocation—the official record where the court's own assessors had certified the Thornfell ward, complete with their resonance measurements. Because Sera had done something slightly illegal during that convocation, which was to take her own measurements with her own tuning-fork and keep her own notes, and when you laid the two records side by side—

"They match," Doss whispered, holding the pages close to the lamp in the records room where Sera absolutely should not have been. "Your numbers and theirs. Exactly."

"Exactly," Sera agreed. "Which proves I'm right, and also proves the assessors *heard it too*. They measured the thinning. They wrote it down. And then somewhere between this page and the report that reached the capital, the thinning became 'sound and whole.'" She tapped the second document, the court copy, where the same date bore different figures—figures showing a ward in perfect health. "Something edits. Something goes back through the records and makes the past agree with the theft."

"That's not possible."

"No," Sera said. "It isn't. And yet."

It was at that moment—because the universe had a sense of humor, and it was a cruel one—that the lamp guttered, and the gray-eyed man from the wall stepped out of the shadow between the shelves as though shadow were a door he owned.

"Journeywoman Ilyich," he said. "You've been busy."

Doss moved first, bless his trouble-wed heart, shoving the ledger pages into Sera's coat and putting his considerable body between her and the door. The gray-eyed man didn't even look at him. He looked at Sera, and his eyes were so gray, so perfectly, uniformly gray, that she felt a strange sliding sensation, like a word on the tip of the tongue slipping back down the well.

"You'll come to the capital," he said. "You'll recant your findings. You'll state that the wards were damaged by weather and smugglers' tools, and that you were mistaken."

"And if I don't?"

"Then you'll find you can't quite remember why you didn't." He smiled the yard-bought smile again. "Everyone forgets eventually. It's what we're *for*."

And Sera—who had spent three years being called mad, hysterical, ambitious; who had been patronized by assessors and dismissed by magistrates and pitied by colleagues; who had a temper her grandmother had compared, fondly, to a kettle—felt the fury arrive all at once, total and clarifying, like a chord resolving.

"Noted," she said. "Filed under 'confessions of the guilty.'"

She threw the lamp.

Not at him—at the shelf beside him, the one holding forty years of wax-sealed assessment scrolls, which caught beautifully, because wax seals catch beautifully, and the smoke went up black and thick, and Doss was already hauling her through the records-room window that he had, God bless him, unscrewed the latch of an hour earlier "just in case," because Doss planned for trouble the way other men planned for supper.

They ran. Behind them the magistrate's house began to shout. Ahead of them lay the dark, the hills, the smuggler roads Sera had never walked but had, in three years of boring reports, memorized the maps of, because she was middling rank and thorough.

At the tree line she stopped, once, and looked back. The gray-eyed man stood in the burning doorway, and he was not chasing them. He was watching her with his head tilted, almost curious, and even at fifty yards his eyes were the grayest thing in the world.

*You'll forget,* his posture said. *Everything forgets.*

"I remember everything," Sera said aloud, to the night, to him, to herself. "It's my entire personality. Ask anyone."

Then she ran, with stolen evidence against her ribs and a stolen future ahead of her, and behind her the border wards sang their terrible polished silence, and somewhere far to the north, above a kingdom that was forgetting its own king, the stars were going out one by one—and Sera Ilyich, furious, breathless, alive, swore to the dark that she would find the thief.

Even if she had to be mad to do it.

*Especially,* she thought, ducking under a branch, *if mad comes with a pension.*
