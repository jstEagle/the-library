## 3. A Thief Named Tamsin

Tamsin had three rules, and she'd never broken any of them.

One: never lift from anyone who looked hungry. Hungry people watched their purses like hawks and had nothing worth taking anyway. Two: never work the same square twice in a week. Three: never, ever take from someone whose eyes were the wrong color.

She'd made rule three herself, only two days ago, after the gray-eyed man in the fish market had turned around mid-sentence—mid-sentence!—and looked straight through her as if she were a fence post. He'd been talking to a woman about eels. Then his face went smooth as a dropped egg, and he walked away, and the woman stood there holding her basket like she'd forgotten why she came to market at all.

Tamsin didn't know what that was. But she knew it wasn't anything good, and she knew it had eyes, and so: rule three.

The tall stranger by the well broke rules one and two simultaneously, which was practically an invitation.

They sat on the fountain's rim with the stillness of a person who'd learned that moving too fast got you noticed, and they weren't hungry—they had a traveler's pack with a bedroll strapped tight and the particular way of sitting that said *I've slept rough but I've eaten today*. Their hood was up against the drizzle, but Tamsin could see the line of their jaw, sharp and unimpressed with the world. A sword under the cloak, probably. Definitely. The way they sat, weight forward, one hand loose near their hip—that was sword-sitting.

And they'd looked at her twice.

That was the thing. Everyone else in Grayhollow looked at her once—the quick flick of *street rat, ignore it*—or not at all. But this stranger had glanced at her when she'd passed the first time, and then again when she'd come back around the baker's cart, and both times their eyes had actually *landed*, like she was a person and not a piece of weather.

Tamsin told herself that didn't matter. It didn't matter at all. She was going to rob them anyway.

The purse hung at their left side, tied with a sloppy knot that some smuggler's habit had loosened over years of use. Tamsin drifted past behind them, let a passing cart block the view of the fishmonger across the lane, and did the thing with her fingers that Old Nan Wren had taught her before Nan Wren forgot how to do anything except sit by the harbor and ask children their names over and over.

The purse came away light as a bird leaving a branch.

Tamsin was three streets gone, tucked into the gap between the cooper's shed and the wall where nobody ever looked, before she opened it.

Coppers. A few silvers. And a coin she'd never seen before.

It was gold—or gold-colored—and heavier than it should have been, warm in her palm like it had been held recently, which was strange because the purse had been hanging cold against the stranger's hip. On one side was a face: a man with a strong jaw and a crown, rendered in fine detail, the kind of detail that cost real money at the mint. On the other side were words worn nearly smooth, and beneath them a star.

Tamsin frowned at it. She knew every coin in Grayhollow, because knowing coins was staying alive. Copper bits, silver marks, the fat old gold crowns with King Aldric's—

With the king's—

She turned the coin over. Turned it back. The face on it was a king's face. She was almost sure it was a king's face. Crowns meant kings; that was simple. But when she tried to hold the name that should have gone with it, the name slid away like wet soap, and what remained was just... a crowned man. A shape. *He rules,* said something in the back of her head, uselessly, the way the grown-ups all said it. *The king rules.*

Well, obviously. That was like saying water was wet. But kings had faces, and names, and birthdays that got celebrated with bonfires, and she couldn't—

"Stupid," she muttered, and pocketed the coin, because stupid or not, it was gold, and gold was food for a month.

That was when she heard the voice from the mouth of the alley.

"—told you, the mark is here somewhere. Tall. Cloak. Sword." A pause. "There was a child too. Was there? There was a—" The voice snagged, caught, like a boot in mud. "There was a child."

Tamsin went very still between the shed and the wall.

Two figures stood at the alley's mouth. They wore traveling clothes, plain and good, the kind of clothes that wanted you to think *nobody important*. And their eyes—even in the dim, even at a distance—were gray. Not storm-gray or river-gray. Gray like a page with nothing written on it yet.

"The child," the second one said slowly, "is not relevant."

"The child saw us."

"The child will forget us. They always forget us." The first one shook their head, and the motion had something wrong in it, a smoothness, like a puppet worked by someone who'd only read about elbows. "Sweep the square. The purse will be found on the thief, and the thief will be found by the coin."

*By the coin.* Tamsin's hand closed around it in her pocket, and the warm metal seemed to pulse against her palm, and she thought, with the cold clarity of eleven years spent surviving: *they're talking about me, and I'm standing in the one place in Grayhollow with no other exit.*

Rule four, she decided on the spot: *when the egg-faced people start saying your name, run.*

She went up.

The cooper's shed had a lean-to roof, and the lean-to had a stack of barrel staves leaning against the wall, and Tamsin had climbed worse things before breakfast. She went over the roof on hands and feet, quiet as a cat with a guilty conscience, dropped into the tanner's yard beyond—which stank, but stink was free—and ran.

* * *

She found the tall stranger again at dusk, at the cheap boarding house near the south gate, and spent a full hour watching the door from the shadow of a rain barrel before working up the nerve.

This was the part that didn't make sense, and she kept turning it over like the coin. She should run. Rule four said run. Gray-eyed people who forgot things mid-sentence were hunting a coin, and she had the coin, and the sensible thing—the *surviving* thing—was to pitch it in the harbor and vanish into the warrens until spring.

But the gray-eyed people had said *the child will forget us*, the way you'd say *the rain will stop*. Like forgetting was just something that happened to everyone eventually. Like it was happening to everyone, everywhere, all the time, and Tamsin had simply never noticed because—because—

Because Nan Wren used to know her name. Used to call her *little magpie* and give her boiled sweets wrapped in wax paper. Now Nan Wren asked children their names over and over, and everyone said *poor old woman, her memory's gone*, and Tamsin had nodded along, but Nan Wren wasn't old-old. Not forget-your-own-name old. And half the fishermen couldn't tell you their mothers' faces anymore, and the priest at the shrine had started giving sermons made entirely of *he rules, the king rules, he has always ruled*, and everyone laughed it off, the whole town laughing together, because it was easier than noticing.

The stranger had looked at her twice. In a town full of people whose eyes slid off her like rain off oilcloth, this one person had *seen* her.

So when the street emptied and the boarding house windows went amber with lamplight, Tamsin walked up to the door, knocked, and when the stranger opened it—with the speed of someone who slept armed—she held out the purse with both hands.

"You dropped this," she lied.

The stranger looked at the purse. At her. At the purse again. Their hood was down now, and up close Tamsin could see they were younger than she'd guessed—not old like a merchant, more like twenty-some years, with dark hair going gray early at the temples and a face that had decided long ago not to give much away.

"I didn't drop it," they said.

"Well," Tamsin said, "it was *near* you."

Something happened to the stranger's mouth then. It took Tamsin a moment to recognize it, because she'd hardly ever seen one aimed at her: a smile. Small and crooked and tired, but real.

"And you picked it up," the stranger said, "out of the goodness of your heart."

"I have a very good heart."

"Mm." The stranger stepped back from the doorway—an invitation, or a test, Tamsin couldn't tell which. "You might as well come in. People who return stolen goods deserve dinner, at least."

"It wasn't stolen. It was *near* you."

"That's what I said."

Inside was one small room: a bed, a pack, a candle, a whetstone, and a sword that was definitely a sword and not a walking stick, no matter what the strap said. The stranger—Mara, they said, just Mara, no family name offered—took the purse, counted its contents without comment, and paused when they reached the last coin.

Tamsin watched their face very carefully. She was good at watching faces; it was most of her profession. What she saw was fear. Not the jump-startle kind. The slow kind, the kind that had been living in a person for a while and had just gotten new furniture.

"Where did you find this?" Mara asked quietly.

"In your purse."

"Before that."

"On the ground? Near your hip?" Tamsin shrugged. "Does it matter?"

"It matters." Mara set the coin on the table between them, in the candlelight, and the little stamped star seemed to drink the flame. "Do you know whose face that is?"

"A king's."

"Which king?"

Tamsin opened her mouth. Closed it. The name was right there, right at the front of her head, the way *bread* was there, the way *Grayhollow* was there. She reached for it and her hand came back empty.

"That's the thing," she said, and her voice came out smaller than she wanted. "Nobody knows. Everybody knows there's a king, and nobody knows who he is. I thought it was just me being stupid, but it's not, is it. It's not just me."

Mara was silent a long moment. When they spoke again, their voice had changed—lower, careful, the voice of someone deciding something heavy.

"No. It's not just you." They looked at the coin, then at her, really looked, the second look, the landing kind. "Those people looking for this. Did you see them?"

"Two of them. Gray eyes. One of them forgot what he was saying in the middle of saying it." Tamsin hugged her knees up onto the chair, making herself small out of habit. "They said the child would forget them. Like it's a thing that happens. Like everybody forgets everything, all the time, and nobody minds."

"Nobody minds," Mara repeated softly, "because they can't remember enough to mind."

Outside, the drizzle had stopped. Through the shutter cracks, Tamsin could see the sky over Grayhollow, and she noticed—for the first time, the way you notice a toothache only when it stops—that there were no stars. There should have been stars. There were always stars. She tried to remember the last time she'd seen them and couldn't, and the *couldn't* had teeth.

"They'll come here," she said. "The gray-eyes. For the coin. For me."

"Probably."

"So I should go."

"Probably," Mara agreed. Then they pushed the coin across the table toward her, face-up, the nameless king staring at the ceiling. "Or you keep it. You found it fair—near me, you said. And you know things about those people that I need to know, and I know things about that coin that you need to know, and neither of us survives tomorrow alone. So."

Tamsin stared at the coin. At the stranger who'd looked at her twice. Her whole life, adults had come in exactly two kinds: the ones who didn't see her, and the ones who saw her and wanted something small and terrible. This one seemed to want something large and strange instead, which was either much better or much worse.

"What things do you know?" she asked.

Mara leaned forward, candlelight catching the edge of their face, and outside the shutters the starless dark pressed close against the glass like something listening.

"I know," they said, "that this coin was minted in a kingdom that's being eaten—and I think we're standing in its mouth."
