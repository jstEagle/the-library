## 4. The Road Through Thornfell

The smuggler roads of Thornfell were not roads at all, which was rather the point.

Mara led them out of Grayhollow before dawn, through a tanner's yard that smelled like a crime against the gods, and into a dry streambed that wound north beneath a canopy of black pines. No map showed this path. No ward-singer had ever thought to sing a ward across it. It existed only in the memory of people who had spent their lives moving things that other people didn't want found — and so far, it seemed, the Hollow's gray-eyed agents hadn't thought to eat it.

"Is this legal?" Tamsin asked, hopping a fallen log with the ease of someone who had been climbing over things since she could walk.

"Nothing about us is legal," Mara said. "That's why we're alive."

"That's very comforting," said Sera, who was not. The ward-singer picked her way along the stones like a woman crossing a frozen lake, arms out, dignity abandoned. She had insisted on bringing her satchel of tuning forks and chalk and three books that weighed as much as a small child, and every mile she muttered something about *sediment* or *acoustics* or how in a civilized country there would be bridges.

"There are bridges," Mara said. "On the king's road. Where the gray-eyed men are standing."

Sera shut up about bridges.

They made eight miles before the first trouble found them, because trouble always did. It came at a ford where the streambed dipped under an old stone arch — a good ambush spot, and Mara knew it was one, and took it anyway, because the alternative added half a day and half a day was blood they might not have. Two men waited among the alders. Not gray-eyed. Just bandits, hungry ones, with rusted swords and the particular desperation of people who had chosen the wrong decade to be criminals.

Mara put one down with a boot knife and a lesson in anatomy, and the other ran. Simple. Clean. Except that afterward, Sera caught Mara's arm and said, "What did he look like?"

"What?"

"The man you fought. Describe him."

Mara opened their mouth. Closed it. There had been a shape, a movement, a sword arm — but the face was gone, like a word on the tip of the tongue that kept sliding away. "Tall," they said slowly. "I think. He had... a beard?"

"You cut his cheek open," Sera said quietly. "You watched him fall two feet from you. And you can't tell me the color of his eyes."

The cold that went through Mara then had nothing to do with the mountain morning. They looked down at the trampled mud, at the smear of red already washing away in the shallows, and understood what the skirmish had cost. Not blood. Something worse. Every time the Hollow's people came close — every brush with them, every fight — the fight itself got eaten. The violence stayed; the *witnessing* of it drained away like water through sand.

"Keep moving," Mara said, and their voice came out flatter than they intended.

* * *

By the third day, Mara had begun teaching them the trade, mostly because teaching kept the fear quiet.

"A smuggler's first rule," they said, crouched at the mouth of a badger sett that was actually the entrance to a cellar once used for unmarked brandy, "is that the best hiding place is the one nobody remembers exists. Not hidden. *Forgotten.* There's a difference."

"Like me," Tamsin said, wriggling into the hole without hesitation. She fit perfectly, being roughly the size of a well-fed cat.

"Nothing like you," Mara said. "You're unforgettable. Loudly. Constantly."

"I'm going to take that as a compliment."

"It wasn't."

But it was, a little, and Tamsin knew it, and grinned up at them from inside the dark like a lantern lighting itself.

Sera stood apart, chalk in hand, marking the sett's entrance with a sigil Mara didn't recognize. "I'm tagging the route," she said, catching their look. "If we have to come back this way, I want the wards to know us. Wards remember, even when—" She stopped. Even when people don't. Nobody finished the sentence. Nobody needed to.

That night they camped in a hollow beneath a shelf of granite, and Mara built the fire small and shielded, the way they'd built a thousand fires alone, and was startled to find the ritual felt different with two other silhouettes around it. Bigger. Warmer. More dangerous, some treacherous part of them whispered, because now there were things to lose.

Tamsin produced dinner: two apples, half a loaf, and a wedge of cheese whose provenance Mara decided not to investigate. "Found them," she said, with the innocence of a professional.

"You stole them."

"I *found* them. In my pocket. Where they'd been all day." She bit her apple. "Anyway, the baker owed me. He forgot he'd promised me bread yesterday, so really I was just collecting a debt."

"He forgot?"

Tamsin's chewing slowed. "Yeah. Looked right at me and asked if I wanted to buy something. Like he'd never seen me before. His wife remembered, though. She slipped me the loaf and told me to run." The girl shrugged, too casually. "People forget things lately. Whole towns are getting foggy. Didn't you hear? In Millbrook nobody can remember the miller's name, not even the miller."

Mara heard themselves say, "That happens sometimes," and heard, underneath it, the voice of a child saying the same thing about something else. *Grandmother just gets confused. It's normal. Everyone's grandmother loses whole years.*

Was that true? Was that ever true?

"Mara?" Sera was watching them with those too-attentive eyes. "Where did you grow up?"

"Thornfell. South side. A farm near the—"

Near the what. The sentence stood there, waiting for its ending, and the ending wasn't there. There was a farmhouse in their memory, yes, stone walls, a crooked chimney. But no village name attached to it. No neighbors' faces. A mother-shaped warmth and a father-shaped absence and a grandmother whose face was—

A grandmother whose face was smooth as an egg.

"I grew up south," Mara said finally. "That's the part that matters."

Sera didn't push. That was the thing about her that Mara was learning to be grateful for: she catalogued everything and confronted almost nothing, saving her battles like coins. But she wrote something in her little book by firelight, and Mara pretended not to see it.

* * *

The second skirmish came on the fifth night, and it cost more.

Gray-eyed men this time — four of them, walking out of the dark with the unhurried confidence of people who knew exactly where their prey would camp, which meant somewhere behind them the route had leaked, or been read off a captured mind. The fight was short and ugly in the bracken. Mara killed two. Sera, to everyone's surprise including her own, dropped a third with a sung note that hit him like a wall of falling air — her wards could do more than mark doors, apparently, and she looked nearly as frightened of herself as of them. The fourth fled.

And afterward, sitting with their backs against a tree, bleeding from a scalp wound and shaking, Tamsin said, "What's my mother's name?"

Nobody answered.

"I had one," the girl said, very fast, the way words come out when you're afraid they'll stop working. "I know I had one. I used to say it at night so I wouldn't lose it. *Maren. Maren. Maren.* And now there's just — the shape of saying it, and nothing inside." Her chin crumpled, and she was eleven years old suddenly, not a pickpocket, not a survivor, just a child in the dark with a hole where a person used to be. "Did they take her too? Or did I just — lose her? Which is worse?"

Mara pulled her in before they'd decided to. The girl shook against their chest, small and furious and grieving, and Mara held on and stared over her head at Sera, and saw the same horror mirrored there — the ward-singer touching her own temple as if checking for leaks.

"We keep new ones," Mara heard themselves say. "That's the trick of it. They steal what we had. So we make more. Louder ones. Ones with witnesses." They tipped Tamsin's chin up. "Starting tonight. You get a story. An invented one, all ours, that no thief has ever heard — which means they can't miss what they never knew existed."

"That's not how stories work," Sera murmured.

"It's how ours work now."

So they built one, badly, around the fire. It began as a tale of a fox who robbed a king — Tamsin cast herself immediately, demanded a cape — and grew, by committee, into an epic involving a singing bear (Sera), a mercenary with a heart of granite that kept cracking (nobody had to ask who), and a kingdom where the taxes were paid entirely in cheese. Tamsin laughed until she hiccupped. Sera laughed in a surprised way, like a door opening in a house she'd thought empty. And Mara sat in the middle of it feeling something they had no clean name for, except that it was the opposite of hollow, and it terrified them more than the gray-eyed men had.

Because here was the arithmetic they couldn't escape: everything they loved was becoming a target. The Hollow ate memories, and these two were carving themselves into Mara's memory deeper every day. Every laugh was collateral. Every campfire was a debt accruing interest.

And still — still — when Tamsin fell asleep mid-sentence against Mara's shoulder, cheese-tax kingdom unfinished, Mara found they didn't move. Let the debt pile up. Some things were worth owing.

* * *

On the seventh morning, Mara woke before the others and took stock of themselves the way they'd take stock of cargo: itemized, unsentimental, thorough.

Childhood: a farmhouse, a crooked chimney, a smell of bread. No village name. No friends. No winter they could name, no summer, no scraped knee, no first fight, no teacher, no dog. A childhood like a house after thieves — furniture gone, walls standing, rooms full of the shapes of missing things.

Their oath-mark, cold on their forearm since the night the stars went out, gave a single pulse. Almost like attention. Almost like pity.

"You're counting the holes," Sera said. She was awake, of course. She was always awake first, watching the treeline, chalk-stained fingers curled around her book.

"Yes."

"How many?"

"I stopped counting at thirty." Mara flexed the marked arm. The skin around Veyl's sigil had gone pale as birch bark. "There's a pattern to them, though. That's the interesting part. The holes aren't random. Everything about my family is gone — faces, names, places. Everything else is intact. Someone harvested a specific field."

Sera went very still. "Selective erasure isn't how the Hollow feeds. I've seen its work. It eats like fire eats — nearest fuel first, everything, indiscriminately." Her eyes lifted to Mara's. "Someone *chose* your losses. Someone curated them."

The pulse came again under Mara's skin, colder this time, and with it a fragment rose unbidden — not a memory, just the husk of one: an old woman's voice saying *we pay what we agreed to pay*, and the taste of wax, and a door sealing.

"Sera," Mara said, and their voice sounded like someone else's, "when we reach the capital, we're not going to the crown first."

"No?"

"No. We're going to wherever Dravenmoors are buried." They looked east, toward the low hills and whatever waited under them. "I want to see what my family paid. And I want to see the receipt."
