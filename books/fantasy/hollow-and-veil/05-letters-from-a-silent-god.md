## 5. Letters From a Silent God

The shrine had been a shrine once. Now it was mostly weather.

Sera stood in the doorway of what had been the sanctuary and let the wind off the mountain comb through her hair, carrying snow and the smell of old stone and, faintly, something sweeter underneath—resin, maybe, from candles burned centuries ago by people who had come here to speak to Veyl and expected an answer.

People were fools, she thought fondly. Wonderful fools. She had been one herself for thirty years, singing wards into the border stones and believing, the way every ward-singer believed, that the silent god was listening even if it never spoke. Silence was just patience, they said. Silence was how a god listened.

She'd stopped believing that somewhere around the fourth ward collapsing under her hands like wet bread.

"Is it safe?" Tamsin asked from behind her, which was rich, coming from the child who had picked three pockets on the road up and defended it as *economic activity*.

"No," Sera said cheerfully. "But neither is anywhere else lately. Come on."

* * *

Inside, the sanctuary was a long room with a collapsed roof letting in a stripe of gray sky, and along one wall—intact, impossibly intact—the letters.

They covered the stone floor to ceiling. Not carved, not painted: grown, Sera thought, the way frost grows on glass, silver-white script in a hand no mortal could hold a pen at that angle. Thousands of them. Hundreds of thousands. The whole wall was a letter, and the letter went on into the dark of the side chapel, and when she pressed her palm flat against the coldest patch of script, she felt it hum against her skin like a struck bell that refused to finish ringing.

"Oh," she said softly. "Oh, you *idiot*."

"Sera?" Mara's voice, wary, from the doorway. "What did you find?"

"Come here." She didn't take her eyes off the wall. "Both of you. And Mara—breathe first. I mean it. Breathe first."

* * *

It took her most of the afternoon and evening to read enough. Ward-script and god-script shared a grammar the way sisters share a face, and Sera had spent her life reading the younger sister. The older one took longer, but not forever. By the time Tamsin had built a fire in the ruined apse and produced, from nowhere, an entire smoked fish, Sera sat back on her heels with her hands shaking and her eyes burning and knew.

She made herself say it plainly. They deserved plain.

"Veyl didn't go silent because it abandoned us." Her voice cracked on the second word; she pushed through. "It went silent because silence was all it had left. Every word it spoke, the Hollow ate. Every prayer it answered, the answer was devoured before it reached the pray-er. So it stopped speaking. It stopped *spending*. It folded everything it was into one last act and hid it where the Hollow would never think to look."

"Where," Mara said. Flat. Already knowing. Sera watched their hand drift up, without permission, toward the oath-mark on their collarbone.

"Inside a mortal," Sera said gently. "Inside its oath-bearer. Inside you. Your mark isn't a brand, Mara. It's not a promise. It's a *key*. Veyl hid the last of itself in you the way a dying man swallows a ring so the robbers won't get it. You've been carrying a god in your chest for twenty years and none of us—not you, not me, not the whole smug hierarchy of the Ward-Singers' Conclave—ever thought to ask why the mark burned cold instead of warm."

The fire popped. Tamsin had gone very still, fish forgotten, looking between them with the wide careful eyes of a child who has learned that adult truths are usually heavy objects dropped from a height.

Mara stood up. Walked to the wall of letters. Stood there a long moment, shoulders rigid, and then laughed—one short, ugly sound with nothing in it.

"A lockbox," they said. "I'm a *lockbox*. Twenty years I prayed to it. Twenty years I kept its silence like it was sacred, like I was *chosen*, and the whole time—" Their voice climbed. "The whole time it wasn't listening because there was nothing left to listen *with*. It spent itself and stuffed the change in my pocket and called it faith!"

"Mara—"

"Don't." They turned, and Sera saw the tears and the fury fighting for the same face. "Don't tell me it must have hurt. Don't tell me it had no choice. I know what having no choice costs—I've been paying someone else's version of it my whole life. It should have *asked*."

"It couldn't," Sera said. "Asking takes words, and words were the first thing it lost."

"Then it should have found another way or died honestly!" Mara's fist came down on a slab of fallen altar, and the crack of it echoed up into the broken roof. "Gods don't get to be cowards just because they're dying!"

Silence. Real silence, the mountain kind, pressing in.

Then Tamsin, small and matter-of-fact, from beside the fire: "Did it though? Die honest, I mean. Or did it do the thing where you give your bread to the kid even though you're starving, and then you pretend you weren't hungry?"

Mara stared at her.

"I'm just saying," Tamsin said, poking the fire with a stick she had absolutely stolen from somewhere, "my ma gave her coat away like that. Winter she died. Everyone said it was stupid. But she didn't do it because she was stupid. She did it because she'd rather freeze a little than watch somebody else freeze more." She shrugged. "Maybe gods are like mas."

Nobody answered that. There wasn't anything to answer it *with*.

* * *

Sera worked through the night by firelight, translating the last passages while the other two slept—or lay still pretending to, which amounted to the same thing in a ruin this cold. Near dawn she found the final lines, and they were not what she expected, and she sat with them for a long hour before she woke the others.

"The key turns two ways," she said, when Mara's eyes opened. "That's what the last passage says. A key hidden in a mortal can be used *by* the Hollow to unmake what remains—or it can be carried to the Hollow and used to bind it again. Veyl couldn't choose for you. That part it did leave free. The mark doesn't command, Mara. It only... offers."

"Offers," Mara repeated.

"An invitation. The first honest one it ever gave you, and it couldn't even stay conscious to deliver it in person." Sera smiled, tired and crooked. "I'm furious too. Don't mistake me. I've sung to that silence my whole life and it turns out I was singing to a wound. But rage aimed at a dead god is wasted heat. Rage aimed at the thing that *killed* it—that we can use."

Mara sat up slowly. In the gray pre-dawn light their face did something complicated: grief and betrayal and, underneath both, the stubborn thing that had kept them alive through every bad contract and worse employer. Choice. It came down to choice, always, and no one had ever given Mara Dravenmoor one until now.

"So I'm not a lockbox," they said at last, quietly. "I'm a door. And doors open from either side."

"And the Hollow," Sera said, standing, brushing ash from her knees, "is about to learn what happens when everyone on this side of the door decides together whether to open it."

Tamsin raised her stick-torch like a tiny general. "Do we get to pick who goes in first? Because I vote Mara."

"*Tamsin.*"

"What! You're the key!"

But Mara was almost smiling—a thin, real thing—and Sera felt, for the first time since the stars went out, something that was not dread. Purpose, maybe. Or the beginning of it.

Then the wind shifted, and from far down the mountainside came a sound that froze all three of them: bells. Many bells, out of tune with each other, ringing from Grayhollow in the valley below.

Ringing wrong. Ringing the way a town rings when half its people have woken at dawn unable to remember their own children's names.

Sera looked at the wall of silver letters—at the last words of a dead god, offering a choice—and understood that whatever they decided, they would have to decide fast.

"We leave within the hour," she said. "And Mara? Whatever's waiting down there—we go as rememberers. Not victims. Rememberers."

Mara nodded once, hand pressed flat over the cold-burning mark, and began to pack.
