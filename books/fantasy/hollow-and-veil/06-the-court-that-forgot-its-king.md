## 6. The Court That Forgot Its King

Tamsin had been a beggar child in four cities and a pickpocket in six, and she had learned early that the best disguise was not rags or dirt or a convincing limp. The best disguise was being boring. Nobody looked twice at boring.

So she made herself boring. She smeared ash on her cheeks, tied her hair back with string, and practiced the particular slouch of a child who had learned that standing up straight invited attention, and attention was where the trouble lived. Then she sat herself down on the steps of the capital's great fountain — dry now, its stone mermaid holding an empty basin like a beggar's own cupped hands — and watched the palace gates for two days.

The city was called Vaelcrest, though half the signs said *Vaelcr* because someone had chiseled off the last letters years ago and nobody remembered why. That was the thing about the capital. It was full of missing pieces that everyone had agreed, without ever saying so out loud, to stop noticing.

A baker's stall had a gap in its awning shaped exactly like a crest. A mural of the old kings had one figure scraped smooth as an egg. And when Tamsin asked a fruit-seller how long the stars had been gone, the woman smiled at her with warm, empty eyes and said, "Stars? There never were stars here, little one."

"There were," Tamsin said. "My ma told me stories about them."

"Your ma had an imagination," the fruit-seller said kindly, and gave her a bruised apple, which was the most useful thing anyone in Vaelcrest had done all day.

* * *

Getting into the palace was, honestly, the easy part. Palaces always thought their danger lived at the front door, behind guards with shiny halberds and opinions about posture. But palaces also had kitchens, and kitchens had deliveries, and deliveries had carts, and carts had children under sacks of onions if those children were small enough and stubborn enough, and Tamsin was both.

She rode into the palace under three hundred onions, sneezing, and slipped out through the scullery while a cook screamed at a boy about gravy. From there it was a matter of the servant corridors — every great house had them, built so that lords wouldn't have to see the people who kept them alive — and Tamsin moved through them like smoke through a keyhole.

Her instructions from Mara had been simple. *Look. Don't touch. If you can find out what the king looks like, that alone is worth more than gold.* Sera had added, wringing her hands: *And do NOT go near the treasury.* Which was why Tamsin was currently walking toward the treasury.

In her defense, Sera's rules had a way of pointing at exactly the interesting things.

The first strange thing happened on her second day inside, in the gallery above the throne room. She'd found a dusty perch behind a tapestry — some enormous weaving of a hunt, though the hunters' faces had faded to blank ovals, which Tamsin found rude of them — and she watched the court gather below for morning audience.

The king sat on his throne at the far end.

Or. Something sat there. The figure wore a crown, and robes, and held a scepter at the correct angle, and when petitioners approached, it raised one hand in the correct gesture. But it didn't move otherwise. It didn't shift its weight. It didn't cough, or fidget, or lean its cheek on its fist the way bored rulers did in every story Tamsin had ever heard. It just *sat*, still as furniture, and the courtiers bowed to it and backed away and bowed to it again.

Tamsin waited for the king to speak. He never did. A herald read the petitions aloud, and officials answered them, and the king's hand rose and fell like a drawbridge.

Afterward, in the servants' hall, she tried a little experiment. She'd gotten good at experiments since falling in with Mara and Sera; mostly they consisted of asking questions nobody else would ask, then running.

"Excuse me," she said to a chamberlain's assistant, a plump young man eating his lunch with the dedication of a true professional. "I'm new. What's the king like?"

The man blinked. "He rules," he said.

"Yes," Tamsin said patiently. "But what's he *like*?"

"He rules," the man said again, with the confidence of someone reciting a lesson. "He has ruled for many years. He rules well."

"Is he tall? Short? Does he have a beard? Is he kind? Does he laugh?"

The assistant opened his mouth. Closed it. Opened it again, and something flickered across his face — a tiny snag, like a fishhook catching on cloth — before smoothing away. "He rules," he said, but quieter now, and he looked faintly frightened, though Tamsin could tell he didn't know why. "Why are you asking? Everyone knows the king rules."

Everyone knew the king ruled. Nobody knew anything else. Tamsin asked six more people that day — a laundress, two guards, a fussy old steward, a page boy, and a lady-in-waiting who smelled of lavender and secrets — and got the same answer in six flavors. *He rules. He has always ruled. He rules well.*

One guard, a big soft-looking fellow near the kitchens, got further than anyone. "He's..." The guard squinted, reaching. Tamsin actually saw him reach, saw the effort move through his face like wind through wheat. "He's got... I think he used to laugh? At something. Some joke. I can't—" His eyes went glassy, then cleared, and he smiled down at her pleasantly. "He rules, little miss. Run along."

Tamsin ran along. Her heart was going like a rabbit's.

Because here was what she knew, from Grayhollow, from the gray-eyed strangers who forgot people mid-sentence: this was what it looked like when the Hollow ate. Not torn pages. Smooth ones. Whole shelves of a man simply *gone*, and everyone too polite to notice the library was empty.

The king wasn't hiding. The king was mostly eaten. And whatever was left sitting on that throne wearing his crown—

That night, pressed into her hiding spot in the linen loft, Tamsin heard it for the first time clearly. A hum. Low, deep, patient, coming up through the stones from somewhere near the throne room. Not a sound exactly. More like the feeling of standing next to a very large, very hungry animal. Her teeth ached with it. Her oath-mark-less skin prickled — she wasn't marked like Mara, wasn't bound to any god, but even unmarked, she could feel the thing below thinking about food.

It was thinking about *her*. About everyone. The whole city was a pantry, and the pantry was nearly bare, and the thing wearing the crown was getting hungry enough to start licking the jars.

* * *

Sera had said don't go near the treasury. Sera had explained, at length, that royal treasuries were warded, trapped, guarded, and generally designed by people whose entire job was making life difficult for eleven-year-olds.

What Sera hadn't known — couldn't have known, because she'd never been an eleven-year-old in Vaelcrest — was that the treasury's wards were memory-wards. Sera had taught them that much on the road: the kingdom's protections were woven from recorded royal memory, thread by thread, and the Hollow had spent years eating the threads. So Tamsin stood in the treasury's antechamber, having gotten past two guards with a stolen tray of wine and a convincing stagger, and looked at the ward-seal on the vault door, and understood something none of the adults had quite put together.

The wards were made of memories. The memories were mostly eaten. Which meant the wards weren't strong anymore.

They were *mostly* strong. Mostly was a word Tamsin had built her whole life on. Mostly-fed, mostly-safe, mostly-not-caught. Mostly was where she lived.

The seal was a disc of pale stone carved with a spiral, and it should have shown whoever touched it their worst fear, or so the legends said. Instead, when Tamsin pressed her palm to it, she felt it grope around inside her head like a blind man patting a stranger's face, looking for something to use against her — and finding holes. Finding the places where her mother's face should be, and wasn't. Finding the fog where her seventh winter had gone.

"Oh," the ward seemed to say, confused. "Oh, you're already..."

And it let her through, because a child with no childhood looked, to a memory-ward, like no threat at all.

Inside, the treasury was smaller than she'd expected and colder than it should be. Gold and silver sat in neat stacks, untouched — money was useless to the Hollow, apparently, which Tamsin found deeply offensive — and along the walls stood the real treasure: reliquary cases, each holding a small crystal vial that glowed faintly, warmly, like bottled summer. Memory-vials. Royal memory, recorded and stored, the raw material the wards had been woven from. Most of the cases stood open and *empty*, the vials gone dark as river stones.

But one case, tucked in the corner behind a stand of ceremonial swords, still held a vial that glowed. Faintly. Guttering. Like the last candle in a house where everyone had gone to bed.

Tamsin picked the lock on the case with two hairpins and a lot of swearing under her breath. The vial was warm in her hand, and through the crystal she could see movement — not liquid, but *shapes*, impressions, the ghost of something. A face, maybe. A laugh, maybe. A king, before.

The hum beneath the floor changed pitch.

It was subtle. A lesser thief might have missed it. But Tamsin had spent her whole life listening for the moment a sleeping dog stopped snoring, and she knew attention when she felt it. The Hollow had noticed the light going out of its pantry.

Down the corridor, boots. More than two pairs. Moving fast, moving *together*, and gray-eyed strangers didn't run unless something mattered.

Tamsin did the fastest thinking of her life. She couldn't outrun them through the servant corridors carrying a glowing bottle — the glow would give her away in any shadow. So she didn't carry it. She drank it.

Sort of. She uncorked the vial and tipped it against her lips, and the memory poured in cold and bright and *vast*, a king's coronation seen from the throne itself, trumpets and incense and a crowd roaring a name that tore like paper as it entered her head — and she clamped both hands over her mouth to keep from screaming, because it was too big, it was a cathedral being stuffed into a birdcage, and she was suddenly, horribly aware of why the Hollow liked eating these.

Then she grabbed the nearest empty vial, breathed on it, rubbed it against her sleeve until it held a faint oily shimmer, and set it in the case where the real one had been. A decoy. Crude. But gray-eyed strangers were used to finding things already empty.

She was over the scullery wall and three streets gone before they sealed the palace, hugging the memory inside her chest like a coal, feeling it burn holes in her that somehow felt less like damage and more like *company*.

* * *

She found Mara and Sera at the safehouse above the dyer's shop, where everything smelled of vinegar and the sheets were permanently purple. They were arguing about routes — Sera wanted the river, Mara wanted the cliffs, both of them gesturing with bread — and when Tamsin came through the window, purple-stained and shaking, they both went silent in that way adults did when terror arrived wearing a familiar face.

"You were supposed to look," Mara said. "Not touch."

"I looked," Tamsin said. Her voice came out steadier than she felt, which was her finest skill. "I looked, and then I took it, because you need to hear this." She climbed onto the table, because important announcements deserved height. "The king doesn't exist anymore. Not really. Nobody knows his name, his face, nothing — they just say 'he rules,' like it's a prayer. The crown's a shell, and something's inside it, and it's hungry, and the wards are dying because the memories are almost all eaten." She tapped her own sternum, where the borrowed memory sat glowing behind her ribs. "Except this one. This one's the last piece of him. And now it's in *here*."

Sera made a sound like a kettle. Mara crossed the room in three strides and took Tamsin's face in their hands, checking her eyes, her pulse, the grayness of her gaze — and whatever they saw there made them exhale.

"She's still herself," Mara said to Sera. "Mostly."

"Mostly is my whole personality," Tamsin said.

Mara almost smiled. Almost. Then they went still, head tilting, listening to something Tamsin couldn't hear yet — and when they spoke again, their voice had gone flat and cold, smuggler-cold, the voice they used when the roads turned bad.

"The fragment you're carrying. Your grandmother's name was Dravenmoor. And the memory you swallowed—" Mara's hand closed gently around Tamsin's wrist. "I think it knows that name. Because it won't stop whispering it."

Outside, far off toward the palace, the hum beneath the city rose — and for the first time, it sounded less like hunger, and more like a door being unlocked.
