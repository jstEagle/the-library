## 7. Grandmother's Bargain

The memory fragment weighed nothing and everything.

Mara had carried heavier things—a chest of Veyl-blessed iron across the Thornfell passes, a dying friend down a mountain in the rain—but never anything that felt like this: a sliver of royal memory no bigger than a thumbnail, wrapped in Sera's ward-cloth, humming against their ribs like a second heartbeat. It knew where it was going. That was the unsettling part. Every time Mara's hand drifted near their pack, the fragment warmed, patient as a hound on a scent.

"It likes you," Tamsin said, walking backward ahead of them on the smuggler's road so she could watch Mara's face while she talked. Eleven years old and she'd already mastered the art of saying terrible things cheerfully.

"It doesn't like me. It's a piece of someone's wedding day, not a cat."

"Everything likes me," Tamsin said. "That's different."

Sera, bringing up the rear with her staff and her perpetual expression of a woman doing arithmetic she resented, said, "It's resonating because of your blood. The vault will be keyed to Dravenmoor line-memory. The fragment is royal, but it was *stored* by your family. It wants to go home."

"My family didn't store royal memories. My family stored grain. Badly. There are stories about the granary fire."

"There aren't," Sera said gently. "That's rather my point."

Mara walked on and did not answer, because there was nothing to say to that, and because the road was climbing into hills they suddenly could not remember ever crossing before, though their feet clearly had. The path forked at a lightning-split oak and Mara's boots chose the left branch without consulting them. Their body remembered. The map of it lived somewhere below the hole where their childhood should have been, like furniture in a flooded house—still there, still shaped right, just underwater.

* * *

They found the door on the third night, in the side of a hill that was not on any map Mara owned, behind a curtain of ivy older than the kingdom's current dynasty.

Tamsin found it first, actually, because Tamsin found everything first and made sure everyone knew it. "There's a stone here that's lying," she announced, crouched at the hillside with her palms flat against the moss. "All the other stones are just being stones. This one's pretending."

"Lying how?" Sera asked, already kneeling beside her, fingers tracing for wards.

"It hums wrong. Like when Cook used to sing with her mouth full."

Sera sang three low notes against the stone, and the ivy shivered, and the hill opened—not dramatically, not with grinding stone and torchlight, but quietly, the way a person decides to trust you. A stair went down into warm dark, and the air that rose to meet them smelled of cedar oil and candle smoke and, underneath it, faintly, of Mara's grandmother's kitchen: browned butter and dried lavender.

Mara stood at the top of those stairs for a long time.

"Mara?" Tamsin said.

"I've been here before." Their voice came out strange, flattened. "I can't remember it. But I know where the loose step is." They pointed. "Fourth one. You have to step over it or it claps you on the heel."

They went down together, stepping over the fourth stair, and the dark received them like family.

* * *

The vault was small. That surprised Mara most, afterward—they had expected a cathedral of secrets, and instead it was a room the size of a generous pantry, lined floor to ceiling with niches, each niche holding a single object under glass: a christening cap, a soldier's letter, a lock of hair braided around wire, a child's tooth on a ribbon. Memory-vessels, hundreds of them, generations of them, each one humming faintly with the same warmth as the fragment in Mara's pack.

And in the center of the room, on a plain wooden table beneath a skylight open to the starless sky, lay a ledger, a quill, an unbroken wax seal—and a letter addressed in a strong, sloping hand:

*To whichever of my blood finally comes home.*

Mara read it standing up. Then they sat down on the cold floor without deciding to, and read it again aloud, because Sera and Tamsin deserved to hear it too, and because saying it out loud was the only way to make it real.

*"If you are reading this, then the Hollow has worn the crown long enough to come collecting, and you have followed its crumbs back to the pantry. I am sorry. I was proud once, and clever, and I thought cleverness was the same as wisdom. Let me tell you what I did.*

*"When the Hollow first came—hungry, formless, eating the edges of the world—we could not kill it. Nothing that forgets can be killed; it simply forgets that it has died. But it can be *fed*, and it can be *bound*, and binding is a bargain, and bargains want collateral. The crown was my design. Royal memory woven into gold, a meal that would hold it dreaming for centuries. But the court's own rememberers refused to pay the price, so I paid it myself. I offered my descendants' memories as surety—the Hollow may take from my line, slowly, across generations, whatever it needs to stay fed and asleep.*

*"I told myself it would be little things. Faces. Birthdays. The name of a childhood dog. And for a long time it was. Your mother could never recall her own christening, and we laughed about it. Your uncle lost every memory of his wedding day and built the marriage again from other people's stories. Small tolls, I told myself. Fair tolls. The kingdom keeps its past; one family pays in pieces.*

*"I am dead now, or nearly, and I have watched what the toll became. It was never fair. It was never small. If you are angry at me, good. Anger is a kind of remembering, and you will need every scrap.*

*"One more thing, and it is the important thing: the bargain is not a chain. It is a contract, and contracts can be renegotiated by any heir who understands the terms. The Hollow will offer you an easy price. It always offers the easy price first. Refuse it. Find another way. I was too proud to ask for help; do not inherit that along with the debt.*

*"With love I did not deserve and grief I earned,*
*Ysolde Dravenmoor"*

The silence after was very long. Somewhere above them, through the skylight, the empty sky pressed down like a held breath.

"So," Tamsin said at last, in the small careful voice she used maybe twice a year. "The holes in you. That's what they are? She sold them?"

"She didn't sell them." Mara heard their own voice and hated how level it was. "She *spent* them. On a kingdom that never knew her name. On a crown that—" They stopped. Started again. "I used to think I was just bad at childhoods. Other people had stories. First snow. A dog named something. I had a shape where the stories should be, and everyone acted like that was normal, so I decided it was normal. I built myself on top of a cellar and never once looked down."

"You're looking now," Sera said.

"I'm looking now." Mara laughed, and it came out cracked down the middle. "Gods. My grandmother bound the thing wearing the king. My grandmother *built the crown*. And she wrote it all down and locked it in a hill and let us all go on forgetting, generation after generation, paying her bill like dutiful little—"

"Mara." Sera's hand closed over theirs, warm and steady. "Read it again. The last part."

"The last part is her apologizing."

"The last part is her telling you the truth: the contract can be broken. She left you the key, Mara. She spent forty years writing letters to a descendant she'd never meet, leaving doors unlocked, because she couldn't undo what she'd done but she refused to make it unfixable. That's not a woman washing her hands. That's a woman leaving a lamp in the window."

Mara looked at the letter. At the ledger beside it, filled with columns in Ysolde's hand—dates, withdrawals, a grim accounting of exactly what the Hollow had taken from which grandchild, itemized like a merchant's inventory. *Joren: his father's face. Ellet: the smell of rain. The child born 3rd of Thawmonth: everything before age seven.*

The child born 3rd of Thawmonth. Mara did the arithmetic and their stomach dropped through the floor of the world.

"That's me," they said. "That's the year I was born. Everything before seven." They looked up at Sera, and Tamsin, and the walls of little glass coffins full of other people's kept moments. "I've been collateral since before I could walk. I never agreed to any of it. None of us did. She signed us away like—like cargo."

Tamsin climbed onto the bench beside them, which was a liberty nobody else in the world would have taken, and leaned her shoulder against Mara's arm. "You're not cargo," she said. "Cargo doesn't get to be mad. You get to be mad. I checked. It's in the rules."

A wet, undignified laugh escaped Mara despite everything. "Is it."

"Chapter one. Right after 'orphans get extra bread.'"

Mara wiped their face with the heel of their hand, and when they spoke again, the levelness was gone and something harder and cleaner had replaced it. "Then here's what I'm not going to do. I'm not going to honor it. Not the way she meant. I'm not going to stand in front of that thing in the throne room and negotiate a better interest rate on my family's souls. Sera—you said the burden could be shared. Hundreds of rememberers, each carrying a sliver. Could it work here? Could the *debt itself* be split?"

Sera was quiet for a moment, and Mara watched her do the arithmetic she resented, and watched something kindle behind it. "A bargain is a pattern," she said slowly. "Patterns can be re-woven. If enough willing voices took up the collateral—if the Hollow's claim was distributed across hundreds of oaths instead of concentrated in one bloodline—it wouldn't void the contract. It would *outnumber* it." Her eyes were shining. "Mara, that's not just possible. That's a *chorus*. That's the strongest ward-shape there is."

"Then that's the inheritance I choose," Mara said. They picked up the letter, folded it, and tucked it inside their coat, over their heart, next to the oath-mark that burned its slow cold fire. "Not her guilt. Not her bill. Her lamp in the window. We take it to the capital, we gather every soul who'll swear, and we tell that hungry thing in the crown that the Dravenmoor account is closed—and that the new one has ten thousand names on it."

Tamsin raised her hand. "Do I count? I'm not a Dravenmoor."

"You're my heir," Mara said. "Declared and witnessed. You count double."

"I *knew* it," Tamsin said, glowing.

But as they turned to climb the stairs—stepping over the fourth step, out of habit, out of love for a childhood they could not recall—Mara paused at the threshold and looked back once at the little room of kept things, at their grandmother's ledger lying open like a confession.

Far above, through the skylight, the starless sky had begun, very faintly, to press closer. And in Mara's pack, the royal memory fragment had gone cold as ice, and was pulling—hard—toward the capital.

The Hollow knew they were coming. And it had started offering the easy price early.
