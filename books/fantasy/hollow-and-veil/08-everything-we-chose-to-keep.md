## 8. Everything We Chose to Keep

The village of Millbrook had forgotten its own name by the time they arrived.

Sera knew it the way she knew all things now—too much, too fast, a flood of other people's pasts pressing against the inside of her skull. The sign at the crossroads still read MILLBROOK, but when she asked the miller's wife what the place was called, the woman smiled pleasantly and said, "A village, I suppose," and went back to sweeping a doorstep that led to a house she no longer recognized.

"They still function," Tamsin whispered, pressed close to Sera's side. The girl had learned to whisper in the last month, which was how you could tell she was frightened. Tamsin did not whisper for anything less than terror. "They're walking around. They're doing chores."

"Hands remember longer than heads." Sera kept her voice low too, though there was no one left here to hear them wrong. "It's the last thing to go. My grandmother sang until her fingers forgot the chords, and even then—" She stopped. That memory wasn't hers. It belonged to a ward-singer dead forty years whose song Sera carried like a stone in her chest. Too much remembering. That was always the joke about her, wasn't it? *Sera Ilyich remembers too much.* Well. The world had caught up to her affliction.

Mara crouched at the well in the village square, turning the crank with hands that moved on their own knowledge. Water came up. Mara stared into the bucket as if it might explain something.

"There were two hundred people here," Mara said. "I ran cargo through this village six years ago. There was a fiddler who played outside the Gilded Ewe every market day. A woman named Odile who made the best plum brandy in three counties." Mara's voice was flat, careful, the voice of someone counting their own teeth to make sure none were missing. "I can't find their faces anymore. Not faded—*gone*. Like reaching into a drawer that's been emptied and refilled with cotton."

"The Hollow's accelerating." Sera knelt beside them. Up close, she could see the oath-mark on Mara's wrist, the cold silver sigil of Veyl, pulsing faintly like something drowning. "When we started, it took years to eat a border ward. Last week it took the garrison at Cael Ford in four days. Whole garrisons don't forget themselves in four days, Mara. It's not feeding anymore. It's *gorging*. Something's spooked it."

"Us," Tamsin said. "We spooked it."

"Or it's nearly full," Sera said quietly. "And a thing that eats memories doesn't stop when it's full. It stops when there's nothing left."

* * *

She proposed it that night, in the ruined tithe-barn outside Millbrook where they'd made camp, because there was nowhere else left to propose anything. The fire was small. Small fires had become a habit; light drew the gray-eyed ones.

"I know how to bind the Hollow," Sera said.

Mara looked up from sharpening a knife that didn't need sharpening. They'd been doing that a lot lately—small repetitive tasks, checking and rechecking, as if the holes in their childhood might be patched through sheer diligence. "You've known for a week."

"I've known *how*, not whether to say it out loud." Sera pulled the memory fragment from the treasury from her coat—the shard of royal recollection Tamsin had stolen at terrible cost, wrapped now in three layers of cloth because it hummed if you let it touch your skin. "The binding at the crown's heart is a lock with one keyhole. One person gives everything—all of it, every memory of everyone they love—and the Hollow is held. That was my grandmother's design. That was *your* grandmother's bargain. One soul pays so everyone else goes free."

"And?" Mara's knife stopped moving.

"And it's a stupid design." Sera heard her own voice crack with fury and let it. Fury was fuel; she'd been running on it since the court called her mad. "One person. One life hollowed out entirely, so the rest of us can sleep soundly. That's not a ward. That's an altar, and we've been burning people on it for generations without knowing. Veyl went silent trying to hold the thing back alone—a *god*, Mara, and even a god couldn't carry it solo. What makes us think one mercenary can?"

"So what's your alternative? We've been over this. There's no second keyhole. The vault was clear."

"No second keyhole." Sera stood. Her legs ached; everything ached; she was fifty-three years old and had walked four hundred miles on righteous anger. "But who says a lock needs a keyhole? Who says it needs a *key*?"

Tamsin frowned. "Locks need keys. That's the whole—that's the entire point of locks."

"That's the point of *cheap* locks." Sera began to pace, the way she paced in lecture halls before the court had her dragged out of them. "The Hollow eats memory. Fine. The old binding dammed the river—one person becomes the dam, holds back the whole flood, drowns in it. But you don't have to dam a river. You can *irrigate*. Split the flood into ten thousand channels, each one small enough to carry. Each one survivable."

Silence. Even the fire seemed to lean in.

"A shared binding," Mara said slowly. "Thousands of people, each carrying a sliver of the burden."

"Each carrying a sliver of the *hunger*. Willingly. Knowingly. Sworn." Sera's hands were shaking now, and she let them. "The Hollow feeds on taken memory—on erasure. But memory *given*, freely, held in common—that's different. That's the oldest ward-magic there is, older than the crown, older than the kingdom. A covenant. Every ward-singer knows it in their bones: what is chosen cannot be stolen. If ten thousand people choose to hold a piece of this thing—if they *agree* to remember the hunger instead of forgetting it—"

"It would starve," Mara breathed. "You'd starve it inside its own prison."

"I'd starve it inside ten thousand tiny prisons, each one guarded by someone who chose to be there." Sera sat down heavily. The fury burned out of her all at once, the way it always did, leaving only the fear underneath. "It's heresy. The Conclave will call it worse than madness—they'll call it reckless. Ten thousand minds touched by the Hollow. And maybe they're right. Maybe it breaks everyone who swears. But the alternative is one person giving up everyone they love, and I have watched you practice dying for a month now, Mara Dravenmoor, and I refuse. I *refuse* to build your altar."

Mara was quiet for a long time. Then, very quietly, in the voice of someone setting down a weight they'd carried so long they'd forgotten it was heavy:

"You know I would have done it."

"I know."

"You know I was going to do it tomorrow."

"I know," Sera said. "That's why I'm proposing heresy tonight."

Tamsin raised her hand, solemn as a magistrate. "I vote yes. Also I want it noted that I thought of splitting things up first, when I stole the treasury fragment and hid half of it in my boot. So really this whole plan is boot-derived."

"Tamsin."

"Noted for history," Tamsin said, and grinned, and for one moment she was just a kid again, eleven years old and pleased with herself, and Sera laughed despite everything, because that was the whole point, wasn't it? That was the entire point.

* * *

They gathered allies the way you gather a storm—slowly, then all at once.

The smugglers came first. Mara sent word down the old Thornfell roads, the routes that existed on no map, and the smugglers came because Mara Dravenmoor had never once sold a friend to save a purse, and in the smuggling trade that counted for sainthood. Brannock Hale arrived with thirty river-runners and a barrel of the good whiskey and said, "So we're to swear an oath that puts a piece of a god-eating horror in our heads. Is there a signing bonus?" and Mara said, "You get to keep existing," and Brannock said, "Hm. Marginal," and signed anyway.

The ward-singers came next. Sera wrote letters to every sister and brother of her scattered order, and half of them came—not because they believed her theory, but because they had watched the wards fail and the stars go dark and the Conclave do nothing, and a person can only watch so much nothing before choosing something. Old Mother Yeva, ninety-one and blind, felt Sera's face with both hands and said, "Ah. The one who remembers too much. Good. We'll need that," and appointed herself the oath's first witness.

The orphans came with Tamsin. That was the part that undid Sera a little, watching it happen. Tamsin went into Grayhollow's rookeries and came back with children—pickpockets, chimney-climbers, gutter-scholars, a one-eyed girl named Pip who claimed to be able to smell lies ("She can't," Tamsin confided. "She just likes saying it.")—and told them, in the flat honest way of a child explaining the rules of a game, exactly what the oath would cost and exactly why it mattered. And the children listened the way children listen, completely, and swore first of anyone.

"Why them?" Sera asked, watching two dozen orphans practice the oath-words by firelight, giggling when they flubbed them.

"Because grown-ups always think somebody else will fix it," Tamsin said. "Kids know nobody's coming. So we come."

By the third week there were nine hundred of them camped in the ruins of the star-shrine where Veyl had gone silent, and by the fourth week there were two thousand, and Sera stood on the shrine's broken steps looking out at cookfires strung across the mountainside like a fallen constellation and thought: *this is either the bravest thing anyone has ever done or the last mistake the world makes.* Possibly both. Most true things were both.

The oath itself was simple, because Sera had stripped away everything ornamental. Ward-song had a hundred ceremonies and she'd discarded ninety-nine of them. What remained was this: each rememberer would take the treasury fragment's pattern into themselves—a sliver of the Hollow's hunger, a thread of the binding—and hold it, willingly, named and witnessed, forever. Not forgotten. Carried.

"What if I change my mind later?" asked a smuggler named Olla, the night before the swearing.

"Then you tell two witnesses, and they take up your sliver between them," Sera said. "No chains. That's the whole difference between this and the old bargain. Nobody's trapped. Everybody's *chosen*."

Olla nodded slowly. "My mother used to say love that's forced isn't love. Same thing, I suppose."

"Same thing," Sera agreed. "Everything worth keeping is the same thing."

* * *

The swearing began at dusk on the last night, under a sky with one star left.

Sera had watched the stars die for months—first the outer constellations, then the bright anchors, until only a single ember hung over the northern peaks, and she knew, the way she knew too much of everything, that it was the last star-memory in the world, the final unswallowed piece of the sky's own recollection of itself. When it went, the Hollow would be strong enough to crack any binding ever woven. Tonight, or never.

Two thousand voices took up the words in rounds, wave after wave, smugglers and singers and orphans and farmers who'd walked a hundred miles on rumor alone. Sera sang the lead, her voice cracking on the high phrases, Mother Yeva's hand gripping hers like an anchor. Beside her, Mara spoke the oath in a voice that shook, and Tamsin spoke it in a voice that didn't, and when the sliver settled into Sera's own chest she gasped—it was cold, it was *hungry*, a small gnawing winter behind her ribs—but it was small. Bearable. Held.

Around the mountainside, two thousand faces went pale and steadied, each one now a tiny prison with a willing guard. The covenant held. The covenant *sang*.

And above them, the last star flickered—

—and did not go out.

It hung there, trembling, like a candle cupped against wind, fed suddenly by two thousand small refusals. Not bright. Not saved. But *held*, the way a found family holds a person: not perfectly, not painlessly, but together, on purpose, again tomorrow.

"We did it," Tamsin whispered. "We actually—we *did* it—"

Sera opened her mouth to answer, to say something wise and ward-singerly, something worthy of the moment.

Then every oath-mark in the crowd flared silver at once—including the cold sigil on Mara's wrist, blazing now like a brand—and Sera felt the message arrive in her bones the way Veyl's messages always arrived, wordless and enormous:

*The door is open. Come to the throne. Come now.*

And beneath the mountain, far below the shrine, something ancient turned over in its sleep and began, for the first time in a thousand years, to be afraid.
