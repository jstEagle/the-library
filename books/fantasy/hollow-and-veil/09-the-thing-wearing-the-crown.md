## 9. The Thing Wearing the Crown

The throne room had no roof.

That was the first thing Mara understood, standing in the doorway with Sera's hand on their shoulder and Tamsin pressed close behind them both. The ceiling that should have arched overhead—carved, according to every history book anyone could still half-remember, with a thousand painted stars—was simply gone, peeled away like the lid of a box, and above it there was nothing. Not night. Night had texture, had weather, had the faint gray suggestion of clouds. This was an absence so complete it made Mara's eyes water to look at it, like staring at the place where a tooth used to be.

"The last star-memory is in there," Sera whispered. Her ward-singer's cords were wrapped around her fingers, humming so faintly that only someone who'd spent weeks listening for it would hear. "Aldric's coronation day. The sun came out afterward. Everyone sang." She swallowed. "It's the last one, Mara. When the Hollow eats this, there won't be anything left to anchor him. Or us."

"Then we'd best not be late," said Tamsin, who was eleven and dealt with terror by treating it as a scheduling problem.

Mara stepped forward, and the doors closed behind them without being touched.

* * *

The throne room was vast and wrong. The banners on the walls were blank—cloth that had once borne heraldry, now bleached to a colorless gray, because no one alive could recall what the kingdom's crest looked like. The courtiers' chairs sat empty, and dust lay on them undisturbed, though Mara had the crawling certainty that they were not empty in any way that mattered. And on the throne, beneath the hole where the stars should have been, sat King Aldric.

He looked almost ordinary. That was the horror of it. A broad man gone soft with years of sitting, a beard trimmed by careful hands, a crown of old gold sitting slightly crooked, the way crowns do when the head beneath them has stopped noticing its own weight. His eyes were open. They were gray—not the gray of storm or steel but the gray of paper left too long in rain, washed clean of everything written on it.

"Mara Dravenmoor," said the thing wearing the king.

Its voice was Aldric's voice, mostly. Underneath it ran something else, something like wind through an empty house, like a page being turned very slowly forever.

"You know my name," Mara said. Their oath-mark burned cold against their forearm—the opposite of pain, a kind of aching clarity—and through it they felt Veyl, silent these long years, pressing close. *Here,* the silence seemed to say. *This is why.*

"I know everyone's name," the Hollow said gently. "I keep them. Names are the last things people surrender, you know. They hold onto names the way drowning men hold onto rope. It's rather touching." The king's face smiled, and the smile did not reach anywhere near his eyes. "You've come a long way to die in front of me. Or did you think you'd come to win?"

"We came to unbind you," Sera said, stepping up beside Mara, her cords singing low and steady. "From the crown. From the throne. From him."

"Him?" The Hollow tilted Aldric's head, curious, birdlike. "There is hardly any him left. Shall I show you?"

And the king's face *changed*—the gray washing out of his eyes for one terrible instant, replaced by a blue so human and so exhausted that Mara's breath caught. Aldric looked at them. Aldric *saw* them.

"Please," the king whispered. His hands gripped the arms of the throne, knuckles white. "Please. I can feel it chewing. I remember my daughter's wedding and then I don't, and then I do, and each time there's less of it, like bread going stale in my mouth. I have been dying in here for nine years and no one—" His voice cracked. "No one even knew I was gone."

"Aldric," Mara said carefully, "we're going to get you out."

"No," he said, and there was such terrible clarity in it. "No, child. You're going to kill me. Do it while I still know enough to be grateful. Don't let it finish me. Don't let it wear me into the ground like a—a coat." His eyes flicked sideways, fighting for control of his own face. "*Do it now.*"

Then the gray flooded back, and the Hollow settled into the king's features like a man settling into a comfortable chair.

"Well," it said. "That was embarrassing for everyone."

* * *

"You should know," the Hollow continued conversationally, "that I've been looking forward to this. You especially, Mara Dravenmoor. Grandmother's little key. Did Sera tell you what your oath-mark really is? Did she tell you that Veyl hid a lock inside your flesh and never asked your leave? Mm. She did. I can smell old rage on you. Rage keeps well. Like salted meat."

"It does," Mara agreed. "Would you like to know what else keeps well?"

"Enlighten me."

"Love," said Tamsin, from behind them, "apparently. Because these two absolute idiots walked into your house knowing exactly what you are, and neither one of them would stay behind, and I couldn't stop them, so here we all are." She crossed her arms. "Also I pickpocketed a god once. Sort of. It counts."

The Hollow regarded her for a long moment. Something moved behind Aldric's gray eyes—not hunger, for once. Something almost like confusion.

"You are very small," it said, "to be so loud."

"She's the loudest thing in the kingdom," Mara said. "We're working on it."

"And yet." The Hollow rose from the throne, and the movement was wrong, joints bending a half-beat out of time, the crown staying perfectly level as though it were nailed to the air itself. "And yet here is the arithmetic of your evening, Mara Dravenmoor. Listen closely, because I will only offer once.

"Your grandmother bound me into this crown. To seal the binding, she pledged collateral: the memories of her bloodline, generation upon generation, mine to collect as needed. Your childhood was the first installment. Your mother's face. Your grandmother's kitchen. The sound of your own name spoken in love before you learned to speak it yourself. All of it, already eaten. Already *mine*.

"But debts can be renegotiated." The Hollow spread Aldric's hands, magnanimous. "Forgive the debt entire. Every memory of yours I hold, returned—or better than returned, for some cannot be returned, only compensated. In exchange, you give me two smaller debts instead. The ward-singer's memories. The child's memories. Everything either of them has ever loved, remembered, or been. Two lives, unwritten, and your own past restored, and your grandmother's bargain dissolved, and you walk out of here whole."

The silence after that was enormous.

Sera's hand found Mara's. Tamsin's small fingers hooked into Mara's belt, the way she did when the world got too big.

"That's it?" Mara said at last. "That's your offer?"

"It is a generous offer."

"It's a *terrible* offer. It's the worst offer I've ever heard, and I once watched a smuggler try to pay a toll bridge in counterfeit geese." Mara stepped forward, and their oath-mark blazed cold, and they found—to their own surprise—that they were smiling. "You've been eating this kingdom for nine years, and you still don't understand what you're looking at. You think memory is property. You think love is a ledger. You think if you take everything someone remembers, you take everything they are."

"Is that not so?"

"No," said Sera softly.

"Nope," said Tamsin.

"Because here's the thing about found family," Mara said, and their voice didn't shake, which was a miracle, or maybe just practice. "You don't inherit it. You don't get it from blood, so you can't collect it as debt. We built it. Campfire by campfire. Bad joke by bad joke. Tamsin taught Sera to cheat at cards. Sera taught me to sing again after twenty years of silence. I taught Tamsin how to fall off a roof properly, which is a real skill, ask anyone. And none of that lives in any single one of our heads. It lives *between* us. You could eat every memory in this room and the shape of what we made would still be standing, because it isn't kept in one place. It's kept in all of us."

Something flickered across the Hollow's borrowed face. For the first time, it looked less like a king and more like a very old thing confronted with an idea it had never had to chew.

"That is sentiment," it said. "Sentiment is not structure."

"Everything is structure," said Sera, and began to sing.

* * *

Her voice filled the ruined room the way water fills a cup—suddenly, completely. The ward-cords blazed. And Mara understood, in the wordless way of battle, what Sera was doing: she wasn't attacking. She was *anchoring*. Every note was a hook thrown into the dark, catching the frayed edges of the last star-memory and holding it taut, giving Mara something to unbind toward instead of unbinding into nothing.

Mara raised their marked arm and spoke the words Veyl had hidden in their skin, words they hadn't known until this moment and somehow had always known:

"By the debt I did not choose, I choose. By the silence I inherited, I speak. Unbind."

The oath-mark opened.

Not pain—worse. *Opening.* Mara felt themselves unlaced like a boot, like a letter, like a wound, and the Hollow lunged into the gap with a hunger that had waited nine patient years, and it began to tear.

It took the smell of woodsmoke first, and Mara grieved it. Then the weight of their mother's hand on their hair. Then the name of the village they were born in—gone, a smooth gray place where a home had been. It went for Sera next, and Mara held on, held the memory of her laugh the way you hold a door against a flood, and it cost, oh, it cost, it took pieces of Mara's hands and Mara's courage and the taste of the stew they'd made three nights ago—

And then Tamsin's voice, shrill and furious and *laughing*: "Hey! Ugly! You missed one!"

And Sera's song, threading through the tear in Mara like stitching through cloth.

And Mara understood the trick of it, the beautiful, impossible trick: the Hollow could not eat what was shared. It pulled at the memory of Tamsin's grin and found it also living in Sera's mind; it wrenched at Sera's song and found it also carved into Mara's bones; it tore and tore and every piece it seized was *held from the other side*, a thousand small hands in the dark, smugglers and ward-singers and orphans and strangers who had sworn the shared oath in a hundred rooms across the kingdom, all of them pulling back.

*You cannot eat a chorus,* Mara thought, bleeding light. *You can only join it.*

The crown cracked.

* * *

The sound it made was not metal failing. It was a sigh—nine years of held breath leaving a body all at once. The gray poured out of Aldric's eyes like silt from a broken dam, and the king sagged forward, and Mara caught him, and he was so much lighter than a king should be, because kings carry kingdoms and Aldric had finally set his down.

"Thank you," he whispered. His hand fumbled up, found Mara's cheek, patted it twice, absurdly, like a grandfather. "Tell my daughter—I had a daughter—tell her—"

"We'll find her," Mara promised. "We'll tell her everything."

He smiled, and it was a whole man's smile, and then he was gone, gently, between one breath and the next, and the throne room was very quiet.

Above them, through the missing roof, the sky shuddered.

And the first star in nine years lit—small, white, trembling, like a candle cupped against wind—and then another beside it, and another, a constellation remembering itself stitch by stitch, and Tamsin made a sound Mara had never heard from her before, a small broken wondering noise, and grabbed Sera's sleeve and pointed up and up and up.

But Mara was looking down, at the shattered crown on the floor, at the fragments of old gold scattered across the stones.

Because one fragment was moving. Crawling, small and black and patient, toward the shadow of the doorway, toward the stairs, toward the city full of sleeping people.

Toward the hundreds of willing rememberers who had just taken a sliver of it into themselves.

"Oh no," Sera breathed. "Oh, Mara—it didn't die. It *scattered*."

The second star lit overhead. Down in the dark, the fragment slipped through the door, and was gone.
