## 10. Half-Remembered, Whole

The stars came back on a Tuesday.

Tamsin knew it was a Tuesday because Old Wren the smuggler had sworn an oath-sliver that included every market day of his life, and he'd spent the whole morning grumbling about how he still owed a woman in Grayhollow three coppers and couldn't for the life of him remember her face. "Tuesday," he kept saying, like a man testing ice. "It's Tuesday, and I owe somebody three coppers."

Then the sky cracked open with light.

Not all at once. That was the thing nobody had expected—not even Sera, who had done the arithmetic of souls and slivers and said *it should work* in the voice of someone who'd bet her life on sums. The stars came back one by one, hesitant as candles relit after a draft, and each one drew a gasp from the crowd gathered on the palace steps, and each gasp was a memory being handed back to the world.

Tamsin stood on the top step with Sera's hand on her shoulder and watched the first star find its place overhead.

"That's Veyl's star," Sera said quietly. "Or it was. The god gave up its name to hold the Hollow, do you remember? So now it's just... a star."

"It can be ours," Tamsin said. "We've got room."

Sera laughed—a wet, cracked sound—and pulled her close, and above them the sky kept filling in, star by star by star, like the world remembering itself one small bright piece at a time.

Inside the throne room, King Aldric died at sunset, a man again, held upright between two Rememberers so he could see the sky through his own windows. He didn't say anything grand. He looked at the stars for a long time and then said, "I had a dog," and smiled, and that was all anyone needed him to say.

Mara Dravenmoor did not come out of the throne room until well past dark, and when they did, they walked like a man crossing a frozen river—careful, listening for cracks—and stopped in front of Tamsin and looked at her the way you look at a door in a house you used to live in.

"Hello," Mara said.

And Tamsin, eleven years old, declared heir to an order that hadn't existed a week ago, heir to a mercenary with holes where a childhood used to be, felt her whole chest go tight and hot.

"Hello," she said back. "You're Mara. I'm Tamsin. You're stuck with me."

Mara frowned, slow, like the frown had to travel a long way. "Am I?"

"Yeah," Tamsin said, and took their hand. "Come on. I'll show you around yourself."

* * *

The work of relearning Mara Dravenmoor took a winter, and Tamsin supervised most of it, because she had appointed herself supervisor and no one had the heart to argue with a girl who'd stolen a king's memory out of a locked treasury.

The Hollow had taken nearly everything. Not violently—that was the horror of it, Sera explained; the Hollow had been *tidy*. It had eaten along the seams, leaving structure without substance. Mara knew what a sword was. Mara could field-strip a crossbow blindfolded and could read three languages and could cook a rabbit over a fire with the bored efficiency of someone who'd done it ten thousand times. But ask them why they cooked the way they did, whose recipe it was, whose kitchen, whose voice saying *more thyme, girl, thyme is free*—and there was nothing. Smooth stone where a person used to be.

So they rebuilt. Sliver by sliver.

That was the covenant's whole trick: the memories weren't gone, only scattered, carried willingly in hundreds of chests and heads across the kingdom. Every Rememberer who'd sworn the oath carried a piece of Mara—or of Aldric, or of the border villages, or of whatever else the Hollow had chewed. And once a week, on Tuesdays, because Old Wren insisted Tuesdays were owed something, the Rememberers of Grayhollow gathered in the great hall of the palace—which Sera had immediately renamed *the House of Keeping*, because she had no sense of humor and excellent instincts—and traded memories back like coins.

Tamsin ran the table.

"Number forty-one," she'd call out, consulting her ledger, which she kept in a code of her own invention involving fish. "Wren, you've got the river. Mara, sit down, stop pretending you're going somewhere."

"I wasn't," Mara would say, already halfway to the door.

"You were. Sit."

Old Wren would clear his throat, close his eyes, and give it up: a summer river, cold to the ankles, a smaller Mara laughing while someone—her brother? a friend? the memory came with a name attached, that was the miracle of it—*Davin*, Davin pushed her in, Davin got shoved in after, both of them soaked and shrieking under a sky full of ordinary stars.

Mara would receive it the way you drink water after a long thirst. Sometimes they cried. Mostly they didn't. Once they laughed so hard at the memory of Davin's face hitting the water that they fell off the bench, and Tamsin sat on the floor next to them and laughed too, though she'd never known any Davin, and never would, and found she loved him anyway.

That was the other thing about the covenant, the thing Sera had predicted and Tamsin understood in her bones: the memories came back *shared*. When Wren gave up the river, he kept the shape of it—the cold ankles, the joy—even as the specifics went home to Mara. Nobody lost. Everybody kept a little. It was like a song passed mouth to mouth: everyone who sang it still got to love it.

"You built a religion out of a smuggling run," Mara told Sera once, watching the hall full of people trading pieces of themselves like the world's strangest market.

"I built a *covenant* out of a heresy," Sera corrected. "The smuggling was your contribution."

"My contribution was getting robbed by a child."

"The child," Tamsin said, not looking up from her ledger, "is your heir, and will remember that comment."

* * *

Some things didn't come back, and Tamsin learned to let those be.

Mara's grandmother stayed gone. The bargain, the vault, the debt—all of it had been eaten root and branch, and none of the Rememberers carried it, because the Hollow had swallowed it whole generations before the oath. Sera said it was mercy. Tamsin thought it was more like a scar: proof something terrible had happened, smooth where the wound used to be.

But Mara's hands remembered things their head didn't. They braided Tamsin's hair one evening—clumsy, frowning, muttering "I don't know why I know this"—and produced a braid pattern no one in Grayhollow used, tight and strange and beautiful, and Sera went very quiet and then said, softly, "Dravenmoor style. Border clans. It's in the old ward-songs." And Mara stared at their own fingers for a long time afterward, like they were holding a ghost.

"There'll be more of that," Sera said. "The body keeps what the Hollow missed. We'll dig it out bit by bit. Years of work."

"Good," Mara said. "I was worried I'd run out of things to be bad at."

Tamsin snorted into her soup.

* * *

In spring, Tamsin took Mara to the roof of the waystation outside Thornfell—the same one where a dead handler had leaked smoke-memory into the dark, a lifetime ago, back when the stars were gone. It was repaired now. New roof beams. A new handler, a round cheerful woman named Petra who owed Tamsin a permanent debt of gratitude because Tamsin had once, in the early chaotic days, returned her missing husband's entire personality via ledger trade, and Petra cried for an hour.

They sat on the roof with their legs hanging over the edge, sharing a bag of roasted chestnuts, under a sky so thick with stars it looked like spilled salt.

"Do you ever get them back all at once?" Mara asked. "The Rememberers. Do you ever think about just—taking it all home? One big trade?"

Tamsin considered this seriously, because Mara deserved serious answers even when the question was sad.

"No," she said. "Because then it'd be yours alone again. And that's how it got eaten last time—one person carrying everything." She bumped her shoulder against theirs. "This way, if the Hollow ever comes back—and Sera says it might, someday, from some other crack in the world—it won't find one big meal. It'll find a thousand little ones, and every single one of us will fight for our piece of you."

Mara was quiet for a while. A star slid slowly westward, or maybe the world turned; Tamsin had never gotten a straight answer out of Sera on the subject.

"When did you get wise?" Mara asked finally.

"I've always been wise. You just forgot." She grinned. "Get it? Because—"

"I get it, Tamsin."

"Because you *forgot*—"

"*Tamsin.*"

She cackled and stole the last chestnut, and Mara let her, which was itself a kind of memory: the old Mara would have fought her for it, hand-speed like a striking snake. This new Mara just watched her eat it with an expression of long-suffering fondness that Tamsin decided, privately, was better than the old one. The old one she'd never met. This one she was building, chestnut by chestnut, Tuesday by Tuesday.

Below them, lights were coming on in Thornfell. Somewhere in the town, Wren was probably discovering another copper he owed. Somewhere in the capital, Sera was teaching the first class of new Rememberers, children mostly, orphans mostly, kids who'd learned early that family was a thing you chose and guarded. Tamsin had recruited six of them herself. She was very persuasive. She'd been a professional.

"Mara," she said.

"Hm?"

"What was my laugh? Before. Did you have it?"

Mara went still. Then, carefully, feeling along the seams: "I don't have the memory. But—" They closed their eyes. "I know I liked it. There's a... a warm spot. Where it used to be. Like a chair that remembers you."

"Oh," Tamsin said. "Good. Keep that. Don't trade that one."

"I won't."

"And make a new one," she said. "Plenty of room."

Mara opened their eyes and looked at her—really looked, the way they had that first night on the palace steps, like a door in an old house—and then they laughed. Not the old laugh. A new one. Rough and rusty and real, startled out of them like a bird off a wire, and Tamsin memorized it instantly, the way she memorized faces worth stealing, and filed it away in the deepest pocket of her ledger, under a code that meant *mine*.

Above them the stars burned on, nameless and stubborn and bright.

"Come on, heir," Mara said, standing, offering a hand down. "Petra owes us dinner, and I intend to collect before either of us forgets."

"You can't forget," Tamsin said, taking it. "I won't let you."

"No," Mara agreed, pulling her up. "I don't suppose you will."

And they climbed down together into the lamplight, into the smell of Petra's stew, into the long slow work of keeping—two thieves, one inheritance, and a whole sky's worth of small bright things that everyone, now, remembered together.
