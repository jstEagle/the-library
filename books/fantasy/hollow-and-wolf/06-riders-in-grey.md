## 6. Riders in Grey

I had been in Vessa three days when the grey riders came, and I had spent all three of them learning the city the way a soldier learns a city: by its doors.

The tavern keeper called it the Saltcellar and watered his wine like a man who thought no one noticed. The alley behind it ran to the fish market, which ran to the harbor stairs, which ran nowhere useful unless you were willing to swim. The guildhouse sat on its hill above the ink quarter like a fat ledger dropped on a table — four stories of pale stone, iron-shuttered windows, one main door wide enough for carts of paper and narrow enough, I noted, for a man with a knife to hold against ten men without them. I had stood across the square from it twice that first day, arms crossed, watching the clerks come and go with their ink-stained fingers and their eyes on the cobbles, and I had thought: *she will be the last one out. People like her always are. They lock up behind themselves.*

I did not know her face yet. I knew only what Tam had told me with his last breath, blood on his teeth, his hand crushing mine harder than a dying man should have been able to crush anything: *the map goes to the Guildhall at Vessa. There's a woman there. Marchetti. Ledger-keeper. Trust the ledgers, Wolf, if you can't trust anything else.*

And then he had said *Wolf* — my name, or the name I wore, the only one I had — and died with his eyes open, looking at me as if I were an answer to a question he'd been asking his whole life.

I hated him a little for that. Dying men hand you things you can't put down.

* * *

On the third night, the bells rang wrong.

Every city has a grammar of bells, and Vessa's was simple: the hour, the half-hour, the alarm for fire on the docks. What came at moonrise was none of these. It was a single bell struck hard and repeated, from the watchtower by the gate, over and over, the sound of a man hitting a thing because he cannot think of anything else to do.

I was out of the Saltcellar before the second strike. Down the alley, past the sleeping fish stalls, up the harbor stairs two at a time, and out onto the ridge road where I could see the whole bowl of the city laid out below me in the dark — and there they were.

Grey riders. Twelve of them coming down the coast road in column, unhurried, torches unlit, which told me more than torches would have. Men who light torches want to be seen; they're announcing something, taxing something, performing. Men who ride dark at night into a free city want to take something and leave before anyone counts what's missing. Their cloaks drank the moonlight. Their horses moved like horses trained not to startle — which meant warhorses, which meant money, which meant the usurper's reach had grown longer than Tam had feared.

Twelve riders. One guildhouse full of clerks who had never held anything sharper than a penknife. And somewhere inside it, a woman named Marchetti holding a map that could unmake a crown.

I went down the hill faster than was wise.

* * *

The guildhouse door was locked, of course. Everything about the place said *locked*. But the receiving room had a loading entrance off Ink Row — I'd watched porters bring in crates of vellum through it that morning — and the bar across it was old oak, and old oak burns.

I won't pretend I planned it well. There wasn't time to plan well. I bought a lamp of oil from a night-stall keeper who didn't ask questions because I paid him not to, soaked the bar's lower edge, and set it alight, and while it chewed through the wood I climbed the drainpipe on the building's flank to the second-story shutter, wrenched it open, and dropped into a storeroom that smelled of gall-nut ink and tallow and dust, dust so thick it lay on everything like snow that had never been disturbed.

Below me, through the floorboards, I heard voices. Then the bar giving way with a crack like a bone breaking. Then boots.

They were inside before I reached the stairs.

* * *

I will say this for guild clerks: they do not scream well, but they do not scream at all, either. When I came down into the receiving room, the night porter was standing very straight beside his desk with a rider's sword-point resting under his chin, and he was saying, with the pedantic calm of a man reciting regulations to his own executioner, "—and furthermore, entry outside receiving hours is prohibited under Article Nine, and I am obliged to inform you that any goods removed—"

"Where is she?" the rider said. He had pulled his hood back. Underneath it his face was ordinary, which is always the worst kind of face. "The woman who received a delivery tonight. Three nights ago. Find her, and no one here needs to remember your faces."

"There has been no delivery requiring—" the porter began, and the flat of the sword took him across the mouth, and he sat down hard among his own dignity.

There were six in the room. Two more at the door. The rest would be circling the building, closing the exits, doing this properly, because whoever sent them did nothing improperly. Six swords, and me with a knife, a bad position, and a promise.

I put out the nearest lamp with a thrown inkpot — the darkness in that room was total, the shutters drawn, the clerks' world built of lamplight — and in the shouting that followed I went up the back stairs three at a time, following the one thread I had: *Marchetti. Ledger-keeper.* Tam had said she worked late. Tam had known her habits the way dying men know everything too late.

The third floor was offices. Rows of desks under dust sheets like a field of small ghosts. At the far end, one doorway leaked candlelight onto the floorboards in a thin gold line, and from behind it came the scratch-scratch-scratch of a pen moving fast, and underneath it, barely audible, a voice reading aloud in the flat, precise cadence of someone checking her own work:

"—entry four hundred eleven, compass bearing south-southeast, deviation nil, condition of needle: weeping. Witnessed, M. Marchetti."

I opened the door without knocking. Soldiers don't knock. But I should have, maybe, for her sake, because she came out of her chair with a letter-opener in her fist and her shoulders squared like a duelist's, and for one heartbeat we looked at each other across a desk buried in ledgers, and I saw exactly what Tam had seen.

She was not beautiful in the way the word usually means. She was beautiful the way a well-drawn line is beautiful — economical, exact, nothing wasted. Dark hair escaping a knot she'd clearly pinned that morning and lost the war against by midnight. Ink to the second knuckle. Eyes that went over me once, cataloguing: the sword, the scars, the lack of livery, the sweat, and arrived at a conclusion in less time than it takes to blink.

"You're not one of theirs," she said. Not a question. "Their boots are louder."

"Wolf," I said. "Tam sent me."

Something crossed her face — grief, quick and banked, a fire poked down rather than put out. "He's dead, then." She set the letter-opener down, deliberately, the way one closes a ledger. "I've been expecting someone for three days. You're late."

"The riders are downstairs," I said. "Six in the receiving room, more outside. We're leaving now."

"We," she said, and I watched her do the arithmetic — the odds, the exits, the weight of what she carried — and arrive somewhere I hadn't expected. She reached under the desk and came up with a satchel, already packed. She'd been expecting this too, then. Or hoping. Or dreading. With people like her, I would learn, those were often the same thing.

Then she turned and started pulling ledgers off the shelf behind her.

"No," I said.

"These are the audit records for the last nine years."

"They're books."

"They're *evidence*," she snapped, and for a moment the fear in her was entirely eclipsed by outrage, which told me something important about her that I would spend a long time being grateful for. "Do you know what's in these? Every irregular transaction the guild has buried, every map sold twice, every seal forged, every payment that traces back east to people who wear grey cloaks? If they burn this building tonight, they're not burning paper. They're burning testimony."

Boots on the stairs. More than one pair. Coming up without hurry, because they owned the building and knew it.

"Take two," I said. "Choose fast."

Her jaw set. For a moment I thought she would fight me over paper, and I braced myself to throw her over my shoulder like contraband. Then she looked at the shelves — I saw her actually *read* them, scanning spines the way I'd scan a treeline — and pulled down three volumes anyway, because apparently even her compromises were over budget, and jammed them into the satchel along with a folded square of oilcloth I recognized, from Tam's description, though I'd never seen it: the map.

Smoke was beginning to find us. My little fire at the loading bar had grown opinions.

* * *

We went out through the records vault, because she insisted, and because the vault had a window too narrow for a rider and too high for a casual glance, and because — she explained, breathless, hauling the satchel — the vault's outer wall backed onto the roof of the chandler's shop next door, and the chandler's roof backed onto the lane, and the lane ended at the fish market stairs.

"How do you know that?" I asked, boosting her up to the sill.

"I've audited the guild's property leases," she said, "twice," and went through the window like water through a crack.

I followed. Behind us, the first grey boot kicked open the office door and found only candle smoke and an empty chair still warm.

* * *

The chandler's roof was slick with sea-damp, and we crossed it bent double, her satchel clutched to her chest, my hand at her elbow because the tiles gave nothing back. Below us, the guildhouse began to burn.

It started quietly, the way big fires do — a glow in the lower windows, a whisper of flame finding the dry century of paper in the archive — and then it found its voice. By the time we dropped into the lane, half the ink quarter was lit orange, and the bells had finally sorted themselves into a proper fire-alarm, and people were pouring out of their houses in nightshirts with buckets they would not use, because everyone in Vessa understood instinctively what I understood: you do not fight a fire that was set by twelve men in grey. You stand back and you watch it eat.

Mirabel Marchetti stood in the lane and watched it eat.

I had expected her to run. I had expected tears, or at least the shaking aftermath, the human cost arriving on schedule. Instead she stood with the satchel hugged tight and her face lifted to the firelight, and I watched her old life end in real time — every desk, every drawer, every footnote she'd ever written going up in a column of black smoke that leaned east on the wind like a man bowing out a door.

"They'll say it was a lamp left burning," she said. Her voice was steady. That steadiness frightened me more than screaming would have. "Article Eleven covers negligence. They'll fine the porter. It will be in the ledger by spring, entered by someone who never knew what was actually kept here." A pause. The fire roared. "Nine years of my handwriting is in there."

"I'm sorry," I said, and meant it, and she glanced at me sideways with an expression I couldn't read yet — measuring me again, adding a new column.

"Don't be sorry," she said. "Be useful. Which way?"

"Harbor stairs. There's a boat."

"I don't suppose the boat has a name."

"It has a leak and a sail."

"That's fine," she said. "Everything true does."

And then the riders came around the corner of the burning guildhouse, four of them, cutting between the bucket-lines like sharks through swimmers, and there was no more time for talk.

* * *

I have fought in a dozen engagements and I can tell you the truth about fighting, which is that most of it is geometry. Ground, angles, distances. Whoever owns the geometry owns the fight. The lane was narrow, the fire was at our backs heating the air into a shimmer, and four horsemen in a narrow lane are worth less than four horsemen in a field — but only if you make them come to you one at a time, and only if the ground under your feet doesn't betray you.

Vessa's lanes were cobbled with harbor ballast, round and greasy with tar-spray. I knew that. The riders' horses knew it too, and hated it.

"Run," I told Mirabel. "Fish market. Don't stop for anything."

She ran. I turned and let the first rider commit to the charge, and at the last moment stepped sideways off the lane's crown into the gutter-shadow, and his horse's hooves skated on the wet cobbles and the animal went down screaming, and I put my knife through the gap in the rider's gorget as he tried to rise — quick, clean, no pleasure in it, just work. The second rider hauled up short, suddenly respectful of the geometry, and that hesitation was all I needed, because Mirabel — who I had told to run, who I had assumed would run —

Mirabel had stopped at the mouth of the alley and was *looking at the ground.*

"Left!" she shouted at me. "The lane floods at high tide — the grating, twenty paces, it won't hold a horse!"

I didn't ask how she knew. Later I would learn she had audited the harbor drainage contracts too, because of course she had, because this woman had read the entire city the way other people read letters from home. I sprinted for the grating. The second rider, recovering his nerve, spurred after me — and his horse's foreleg punched straight through the rotten grate, and the animal pitched forward, and the rider went over its head and landed among the fish barrels like cargo badly loaded.

I didn't kill him. There wasn't time, and besides, he'd learned the same lesson I had: listen to the clerk.

The last two riders had stopped at the edge of the firelight. Smart ones. They sat their horses and watched me with the patience of men being paid by the month, and one of them raised a hand — not a threat, almost a salute — and called out in a voice pitched to carry:

"The Regent thanks you for the exercise. He'll have the map by week's end regardless. Roads bend, friend. All roads bend toward the crown."

Then they wheeled and were gone into the smoke, because their work tonight was done whether they caught us or not. The guildhouse was burning. The guild's records were ash. The message had been delivered: *we know where you sleep, and we can reach it.*

I stood in the reeking lane with my knife wet and my heart going like a drum, and Mirabel Marchetti walked back to me out of the shadows with her satchel and her ink-stained hands and her chin up, and said, "You bleed."

It took me a moment to understand her. Then I felt it — a slice along my forearm I hadn't noticed taking, shallow, from a horse's hoof-iron or a stray point. Blood running down into my palm.

"It's nothing," I said.

"Nothing gets recorded too," she said, and tore a strip from the hem of her sleeve — ruining, I would later learn, a sleeve she'd mended three times — and bound it with quick, competent turns, her fingers cold and exact. Up close her eyes were brown shot through with amber, like ink diluted with honey, and they did not flinch from mine. "You came for me. Why? Tam trusted you with a name and a stranger. That's a strange inheritance."

"He trusted you with a map," I said. "Stranger."

Something almost like a smile touched her mouth and didn't land. Above us the guildhouse groaned, shifted, and shed a waterfall of sparks into the night, and she turned her face up to it one last time.

"Goodbye," she said quietly — to the building, or the years in it, or the woman she had been when she walked through its doors each morning believing the walls meant something. Then she squared the satchel on her shoulder, checked the strap twice the way careful people check everything, and said, "The boat. Before they think to watch the water."

* * *

We rowed out of Vessa harbor under a sky the color of a bruise, the city burning behind us and the open dark ahead, and she sat in the stern with the oilcloth bundle in her lap and her ruined ledgers at her feet, and she did not look back. I watched her not look back. It took more courage than the alley had.

"Where are we going?" she asked at last, when the fires had shrunk to a smudge on the horizon.

"East," I said. "Into the Hollowlands."

"Everyone says the roads there move."

"They do."

"Good," said Mirabel Marchetti, ledger-keeper of a guild that no longer had a hall, opening the oilcloth on her knees as the first grey light found the map beneath — and I saw the ink shift, crawl, redraw itself like something waking — "because I'm done trusting roads that stay where they're put."

Behind us, Vessa burned. Ahead of us, the map was already drawing the road we would take, and neither of us yet knew what it was drawing us toward.
