## 9. The Stranger's Sister

The lullaby led us down.

That was how I thought of it afterward, though at the time I told myself we were simply following the sensible logic of a city at dusk — that the low quarter was where people went to be unfindable, and unfindable was what we needed. But the truth was simpler and stranger: Wolf heard the song and could not walk away from it, and I could not walk away from Wolf, and so the three of us descended together — the soldier, the clerk, and the melody — into the tangled dark below Aldenmere's terraces.

The low quarter did not appear on any map I had ever audited. That was not unusual; guild cartographers were paid by the cities, and cities preferred their maps flattered. What *was* unusual was how thoroughly the quarter resisted being mapped even now, with my own boots on its stones. Lanes doubled back on themselves like sentences a nervous scribe kept rewriting. Stairways went down and arrived up. A tanner's yard sat where, by my reckoning of the hill's slope, there should have been open air. I stopped three times to correct my mental sketch and three times the sketch corrected me instead.

"Stop doing that," Wolf said quietly.

"Doing what?"

"Fighting it." He walked with the unhurried ease of a man who had learned streets as a soldier learns them — as cover, as choke points, as places to die if you hesitated. "The Hollowlands drift. You told me. This is just the edge of the drift, wearing a city's clothes."

"It's not drifting," I said. "It's *negotiating*. There's a difference." And there was — I felt it in my teeth, the way one feels a change in weather. The border road had moved like an animal, willful and sudden. This place moved like a market, haggling with itself, settling on new arrangements only when no one was watching. "Which means someone here knows what they're doing. Someone is holding this shape loosely enough to let it breathe."

Wolf glanced at me sideways. In the lamplight spilling from a cookshop door his face looked carved and re-carved, a map of itself drawn over too many times. "You say things like that," he said, "as if they're ordinary."

"They are ordinary. They're just said by no one."

He almost smiled. It was the third almost-smile I had counted since Vessa burned, and I was keeping count the way I kept count of everything — because counting was the only prayer I knew.

Above us, the council towers of Aldenmere glowed with their fat, self-satisfied lamps, where men in good wool were still deciding, in committee, exactly how much of themselves to sell to the grey envoys. Down here the lamps were thin and the wool was patched and nobody was selling anything, because down here there was nothing left to sell but the morning.

And through all of it, threading between the smells of tallow and wet stone and frying onions, the lullaby went on.

* * *

We found the singer by accident, which is to say we found her by listening, which is the same thing done properly.

She was in a courtyard behind a chandler's shop, a small square of packed earth where washing lines crossed overhead like rigging, and she was singing to no audience at all — or so it seemed until I saw that her audience was a row of potted herbs she was watering, one clay pot at a time, with the gravity of a woman feeding soldiers. She was perhaps fifty, broad-shouldered, grey-streaked hair pinned up with what looked like a pencil. Not a pencil. I would learn better. A stylus, silver-tipped, the kind mapmakers use for fine work on waxed vellum.

I knew that stylus the way you know a voice across a crowded hall.

Wolf had gone rigid beside me. His hand had found the knife at his belt without his permission; I saw the knuckles whiten and then, deliberately, one by one, release. The song had done something to him in the tavern district — I had watched him stop mid-stride as if hooked through the chest — and now, seeing its source, whatever splinter it had snagged in him was twisting.

The woman set down her watering jug. She did not turn around.

"You've been standing in my doorway for the length of two verses," she said. Her voice was low and even, worn smooth like a river stone. "In the low quarter, that's either thieves or grief. Thieves don't listen that hard."

"We're looking for someone," I said.

"Everyone is."

"A man named Corvess," I said. "Tomas Corvess."

The silence that followed had texture. It had weight, like wet canvas. The woman picked up the jug again, held it, and then set it down without pouring anything, which told me more than any answer could have.

"Who told you that name?" she asked.

"He did," I said. "With his last breath. At the Guildhall in Vessa, six days ago. He was bleeding, and the grey riders were close behind him, and he put a map in my hands and your brother's name in my ear, and then he died on our receiving-room floor while I held pressure on a wound no needle in the building could have closed."

Now she turned. Her face was her brother's face grown older and softer — the same long jaw, the same deep-set eyes, but where Tomas Corvess's eyes had been frantic at the end, hers were the eyes of someone who had already done all her franticness years ago and filed it away. Grief lived in her the way a scar lives in skin: present, permanent, and past the bleeding.

"You're the clerk," she said, looking at me. Then at Wolf: "And you're the reason he ran himself hollow getting south."

"I don't know what I am," Wolf said.

"No," Sera Corvess agreed. "I imagine you don't. Come inside before someone who does know finds you standing in the open like a pair of painted targets."

* * *

Her rooms were above the chandler's shop, one long chamber that smelled of beeswax and old paper, and it was the smell more than anything else that undid me. Beeswax and old paper is the smell of every drafting room I have ever loved. Shelves lined the walls, but they held no books — they held maps, hundreds of them, rolled and flat and stacked in pigeonholes, and every single one of them, as far as I could see in the lamplight, was wrong.

Not badly wrong. Beautifully wrong. They were maps of the Hollowlands, and they showed roads that forked into roads that looped back into themselves, villages marked with dates like tide tables — *Fennwick, here, springtide; Fennwick, two leagues east, autumn* — rivers annotated with the words *wants to bend north* in a small, confident hand. The hand was Tomas's. I knew it now from the map pressed against my ribs, from the way his letters leaned slightly eastward, like travelers leaning into wind.

"You're a cartographer," I breathed. It came out half accusation, half reverence.

"My brother was the king's cartographer," Sera said. "I was his ink-mixer and his sister and, when he let me be, his second pair of eyes. The king's court had eleven mapmakers. Tomas was the only one who understood that the Hollowlands isn't a place that moves. It's a place that *remembers*, and the remembering looks like movement if you're standing still." She hung her cloak on a peg with the unhurried motions of a woman who had decided, sometime in the last six days, that hurry was finished with her. "Sit. Both of you. You look like a funeral that's running late."

We sat. Wolf took the chair nearest the door, of course, and positioned himself so he could see the window, of course, and I noted both facts and filed them under the growing ledger of things about Wolf that a nameless dockside brawler has no business knowing.

"The map he gave me," I said. "It draws itself. It responds to truth — spoken truth. Lies smear the ink. And it shows a crowned figure in chains of light, somewhere in the deep Hollowlands, and the chains are anchored to—" I stopped, because I did not actually know what the chains were anchored to, only that the anchor-point on the map was rendered as a blank, a deliberate absence, a hole cut in the world's fabric where a name should be.

Sera had gone very still. "It shows the binding," she said. "Then he finished it. All those years, and he actually finished it." She laughed, once, a short terrible sound with nothing in it of humor. "He wrote to me last spring. *The map is nearly whole, little sister. When it is whole, I must carry it to the one man alive whom the crown cannot find, because the crown ate his name.* I thought the fever of his work had gotten into his letters. Tomas was always prone to treating his obsessions like children."

"The crown ate his name," Wolf repeated. His voice was carefully, deliberately flat — the voice of a man walking across ice and testing each step before committing his weight. "Whose name."

"Yours," Sera said, not unkindly. "Though I'd wager my brother never told you that either. He had a talent for handing people lit fuses and calling it trust."

The lamp flame leaned in a draft from the shutters. Somewhere below, the chandler's dog began barking at nothing, and stopped, and the nothing seemed louder than the barking.

"Tell us," I said. "All of it. From wherever it starts."

* * *

It started, as these things do, with a war.

"Twenty-two years ago," Sera said, "the Hollowlands had a king. A real one — Aldous the Third, of the line that goes back to the first treaties between the free cities and the deep country. He was not a great man, by the accounts Tomas gave me. He was a decent one, which is rarer, and he understood something his grandfather built and his father ignored: that the drift of the land was not a curse. It was the land's loyalty. The Hollowlands moved for its king the way a dog circles before lying down — adjusting itself around the one fixed point it trusted. Roads rearranged to bring travelers home. Villages drifted toward water, toward safety, toward the king's banner. The whole country was a compass rose, and he was the needle."

"And then?" Wolf asked. His hands were flat on his knees, motionless, the stillness of a man holding himself down.

"And then a man named Maurevan came to court." Sera spat the name like a seed. "Charming. Generous. The kind of guest who arrives with gifts and leaves with the house. He spent four years learning everything about the crown — not its history; its *mechanics*. Because the crown of the Hollowlands was never just gold, you understand. It was made to be bound. Bound to the land, yes, but bound first to a name — freely given, freely spoken, renewed at each coronation. That was the safeguard. A crown that answers to a name can be refused by one. No usurper could simply take it; the crown would sit on his head like a dead bird unless the true line released it."

"But the true line did release it," I said slowly, working it forward the way one works a knot. "Or was made to."

"Was tricked into it, near as Tomas ever determined. The old king was dying, his heir was young, and Maurevan engineered a crisis — a plague scare, a false invasion — some emergency that made the abdication look like mercy. The renunciation was spoken. Freely given, in the letter of the law, if not the spirit. And the crown passed to Maurevan, and Maurevan discovered the flaw in his beautiful theft." She looked directly at Wolf. "A stolen binding rots. The crown needs renewal — needs the name spoken again, willingly, by blood of the original line — and Maurevan can never get that, because the true heirs are dead or scattered or hidden, and because the crown, tasting the lie in him, has been eating *him* instead. Twenty-two years, and the thing wearing Maurevan is less and less Maurevan every year. The light-chains your map shows? Those aren't the crown imprisoning him. Those are the crown holding what's left of him together, the way a splint holds a broken limb. It cannot let him go, and it cannot make him whole, and it is *hungry*."

"Hungry for what?" I asked, though I already dreaded the answer.

"Names. Truths. Anchors. Anything solid enough to bind to. It has been reaching out for twenty-two years, feeling along the world for a handhold, and everything it touches it wears smooth. That's why the usurper's country is the way refugees describe it — no drift, no change, everything nailed down. The crown hates movement. Movement is freedom, and freedom is refusal." She paused. "And that is why your friend there matters, though I'd stake my life he doesn't remember why."

"Tell me anyway," Wolf said.

"Your father's name was Aldric." Sera said it plainly, the way you tell a man the road ahead is washed out — not cruelly, just truly. "Ser Aldric, knight of the Hollowlands, the old king's sworn shield and closest friend. When the renunciation was spoken and the crown passed to Maurevan, Aldric understood before anyone what had been done. He understood that the binding was incomplete — that the crown would search forever for the true line's name to finish what the theft started, and that if it ever found a willing speaker of that blood, it would bind to *him* instead, and the theft would become permanent. So Aldric did the only thing left. He performed a counter-rite. He took his own name — the name that anchored the old binding, the name the crown knew best — and he erased it. Unspoke it. There are rites for that, old ones, ugly ones. He fed his name to the dark between the words, and the crown lost its favorite handhold, and the search has been blind ever since."

The room was very quiet. The lamp hissed. On the shelves around us, twenty-two years of Tomas Corvess's patient, impossible maps leaned in their pigeonholes like a congregation holding its breath.

"And his son?" Wolf asked. His voice had gone somewhere far away and come back thin. "What happened to Aldric's son?"

"He was seven years old," Sera said, "and he stood in the doorway of the rite chamber and watched his father do it, because Aldric wanted one witness — someone who would remember that the erasure had been a choice, a sacrifice, and not a punishment. And then Aldric hid the boy beyond the crown's reach, among people who owed him debts, and the boy grew up as no one, because a nameless man is invisible to a hungry crown." She reached across the table and, with a gentleness that seemed to surprise even her, laid her hand over Wolf's white-knuckled fist. "You were that boy. Whatever name they gave you after, whatever name you lost along the way — the name the crown is searching for, the counter-anchor, the one word that can either finish the theft or break it — that name was your father's last gift. And your forgetting of it may be the only reason you're alive."

* * *

I want to record, because this ledger of mine keeps nothing else honest: I watched Wolf receive the architecture of his own soul, and he did not shatter. He sat in that chair with Sera's hand on his and the lamp burning and the truth sitting on his chest like a stone, and what broke in him broke silently, the way things break underwater. I saw it only in his eyes, which went from the flat watchfulness I knew to something older and more frightened and, strangely, more certain — a man who has spent his whole life lost suddenly shown the map of the country he'd been wandering. The country was terrible. But it had borders. It had a shape. He had, at long last, a *where*.

I knew the feeling. I had felt it on the border road, watching the land move and understanding it for the first time. It is a particular mercy, cartography. Even a map of drowning is a kindness, if you're the one drowning.

"Tomas," I said, when the silence had gone on long enough to become its own presence. "Tell me about Tomas. How he died. Why he was in Vessa at all, so far from here."

Sera withdrew her hand and busied herself at the shelves, pulling down a pigeonhole box, and I recognized the maneuver — grief converted immediately, reflexively, into work. It was the most Corvess thing I had seen yet, and I had known the man for exactly one dying minute.

"He came back here eight days ago," she said, her back to us. "Half-dead already — the greys caught him crossing the Serein ford, took the messenger he'd hired, took most of his blood besides. He wouldn't see a physicker. He sat where you're sitting and he told me he'd finished the map, the true map, the witness-map, and that it had to reach the son of Aldric before the crown found another anchor. Because the crown was close, Mirabel Marchetti. It had begun to dream of names. And a dreaming crown, given time, will eventually dream the right one." She turned, and in her arms was a book — a thick journal bound in green leather gone soft with handling, its corners worn round as river stones. "So he rested one night under my roof, and he kissed my forehead, and he said, *if I don't come back, send them to me — the clerk and the nameless man — because I've written it all down and grief is useless unless you make it useful.* And then he walked out my door toward Vessa, knowing the greys were hunting him, because the map had shown him that the son of Aldric was waiting in a dockside tavern in Vessa, and Tomas Corvess never once in his life failed to deliver."

She set the journal in front of me. Her hands were steady. Her eyes were not.

"He chose you," she said. "Both of you. Out of every courier, every knight, every loyalist in the free cities, he read his map and it told him a clerk who fights for rules and a ghost who fights for debts, and he trusted it, because the map does not lie." She looked from me to Wolf and back. "So. Read it. And then tell me what my brother died for, because I intend to make sure it wasn't wasted — and I intend to start tonight."

I opened the journal. The first page bore a date twenty-two years old, and beneath the date, in that eastward-leaning hand, a single line:

*The crown is bound to a name. I have seen whose.*

And beneath that, underlined twice, the words that turned my blood to well-water and set the clock ticking in my chest:

*Nine days remain. After the new moon, the crown dreams true.*
