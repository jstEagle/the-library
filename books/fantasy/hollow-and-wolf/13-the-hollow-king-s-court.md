## 13. The Hollow King's Court

The mountain did not want us to find it.

I say that with a cartographer's certainty, because I spent three days watching the pass rearrange itself on the map in my satchel, the trail to the exiled court sliding westward each morning like a man edging away from a beggar at a shrine. Wolf said the Hollowlands had always done this — that the mountains here were old and contrary, that they had learned to hide their king the way a body hides a wound, folding skin over it and pretending nothing bleeds beneath. But I had begun to suspect the land was not hiding him from us.

It was hiding him from everyone. Including, perhaps, from himself.

We climbed on the fourth morning through a country of grey stone and white silence, our breath pluming ahead of us like small fleeing ghosts. The pines came in ragged ranks, then gave out entirely, and then there was only rock and sky and the wind, which had a voice like a woman reading names from a ledger — a long, patient, mournful recitation that I could not make myself stop listening to. The map had stopped moving. It lay flat in my satchel with its ink settled into a single unwavering line, and I understood, with a small cold shock, that the map was not guiding us anymore.

It was following. The way a hound follows a scent it has known since it was a pup.

"Look," Wolf said.

I followed his gloved finger up the mountainside. At first I saw nothing — the cliff face was the same grey as every other cliff face, seamed and wind-scoured and indifferent. Then the light shifted behind a cloud, and I saw the road. It was not carved into the rock so much as remembered into it: a stair of pale steps ascending in switchbacks toward a notch in the ridge, each step worn in the middle by centuries of feet, each one faintly wrong, the way a word looks wrong when you have stared at it too long. The stone of the steps did not quite match the stone of the mountain. It was as if someone had taken a road from somewhere else and pressed it here, hoping the mountain would not notice.

"His court was not always in the mountains," Wolf said quietly. "The journal said the palace at Carreth had a stair of white marble. Nine hundred steps. Pilgrims used to climb it on feast days."

I counted as we climbed. I could not help myself. At step nine hundred the notch in the ridge opened around us like a held breath released, and there it was.

* * *

I had imagined a fortress. I had imagined banners and sentries, a gatehouse, the grim machinery of a government in waiting. What I found instead was a village pretending, very hard, to be a capital.

It sat in a high bowl of stone — a scatter of buildings that had clearly begun life as a shepherd's settlement and been promoted, reluctantly, to a court. There was a hall of grey timber with a roof patched in three different shades of slate. There were cottages with heraldic shields nailed above their doors, the paint peeling, the devices sun-bleached to ghosts of themselves. A banner hung from a pole in the central square, and the wind had worried it so long and so thoroughly that the sigil on it — a crown above a hollow, if the journal was right — had frayed into a shape like a bird seen at dusk. You could believe it was a crown. You could believe anything, if you were tired enough, and everyone in that square looked tired in a way that had gone past exhaustion into something more permanent. Tired the way stone is tired.

But they were proud. That was the thing that caught in my chest and stayed there. A woman sweeping the steps of the timber hall straightened as we passed and inclined her head with the precise, practiced grace of someone who had been taught court etiquette in a palace and now performed it on a mountainside for shepherds and crows. An old man mending a fishing net — there was no water within a day's walk — wore the faded tabard of a royal groundskeeper and worked with the dignity of a man tending the gardens of a living palace. Children paused in their play to watch us, and one of them, a girl of perhaps seven, dropped a stiff little curtsy before her mother pulled her away by the hand.

"They still do it," I murmured. "After all this time."

"Twenty-two years," Wolf said. "The usurper took the crown twenty-two years ago, and these people have been waiting on a mountain ever since." He said it without contempt, which somehow made it worse. "Loyalty is the only fixed point in the Hollowlands. You told me that. Or the map did."

"The map," I said, "is not always right."

But it was right about this. I felt it in the square's silence, in the way the swept steps gleamed, in the fishing net mended and mended again for a lake that did not exist. These people had made loyalty into a discipline, a craft, a daily labor — the way I had once made precision into one. I recognized them, and recognizing them, I pitied them, and pitying them, I was suddenly afraid. Because I knew what happened to people who poured everything into a single devotion. I had been one of them, in the Guildhall of Vessa, bent over ledgers of impossible things, and I knew how the devotion hollowed you out from the inside while the outside stayed neatly, terribly intact.

The court was loyal. The court was also, I suspected, hollow. And I did not yet know which of those facts frightened me more.

* * *

They received us in the great hall, which had been a barn within living memory and still smelled faintly of hay beneath the incense someone was burning to disguise it. The incense was not working. The result was a scent like a temple built over a stable, which I suppose was honest, in its way.

The court assembled for us. That was the phrase that came to mind, and it assembled the way a broken vase assembles if you hold the pieces in the right shape — carefully, precariously, every fragment touching its neighbor but nothing truly joined. There was a chamberlain in a robe that had been magnificent thirty years ago, its gold thread gone the color of straw. There were four knights in armor polished to a shine that could not quite hide the dents, standing at angles that suggested they were more used to leaning on things than guarding them. There was a cluster of lords and ladies in finery worn soft as handkerchiefs, and among them, at the center-right of the group where a man confident of his position would stand, a tall figure in dark grey whose name I did not yet know and whose eyes I did not yet stop watching.

But all of that — the chamberlain, the knights, the lords, the grey-eyed man — all of it was prologue. Because at the far end of the hall, on a chair that had been a throne in a country none of them could return to, sat the king.

He was real.

I want to write that sentence larger than the page allows, because I had carried a doubt up the mountain with me, folded small between the map and the journal, and the sight of him burned it away. He was real. An old man, tall once and still mostly tall, with white hair cropped short in the soldier's fashion and a face lined like a well-used map — the same kind of lines, the same record of long journeys and bad weather. He wore a plain grey tunic with no crown on it anywhere, and his hands, folded in his lap, were a working man's hands, scarred and blunt.

And he was fading.

There is no other word for it, and I am a woman who keeps a ledger of impossible things, so believe me when I tell you I searched for a better one and found none. His edges were wrong. Where his shoulder met the gloom behind the throne, the boundary was not a boundary but a suggestion — a softness, a blur, like ink applied with a brush that was running dry. When he turned his head to look at us, the profile of his jaw shimmered, just slightly, the way heat shimmers over a road in summer. His left hand was the clearest thing about him, scarred and solid and certain. His right hand was fainter. I could see the grain of the wooden chair arm through his wrist.

He was not dying. Dying has a direction, a slope, an end you can chart. This was something else. This was a man being *unwritten* — slowly, patiently, from the outside in — and the horror of it was not the fading itself but the fact that he sat there bearing it with the same quiet dignity the sweeping woman had shown on her steps. He had made his fading into a discipline too.

"Lord Wolf," the chamberlain announced, and then, with a hesitation that told me our arrival had been argued over, "and the Lady Mirabel, cartographer, bearer of the map of Aldric."

The name went through the hall like a stone through a window. I saw it land on every face — the lords going still, the knights' hands moving toward absent swords, the ladies exchanging glances quick as knife-work. And on the king's face, the name landed differently. It did not make him still. It made him *present*. For one moment the blur at his edges firmed, the way a dreamer's face firms at the sound of their name, and his eyes — dark, intelligent, exhausted eyes — fixed on me with an attention that had weight and temperature.

"Come forward," he said. His voice was quiet and rough, like wind through a doorway. "Both of you. Let me look at what my brother-in-exile sent me."

We crossed the hall. I was aware of every eye, and aware, more sharply, of the grey-eyed man at the center-right, whose gaze followed us with an expression I could not read and did not trust myself to read, because I had learned in the Guildhall that the most dangerous people in any room are the ones whose faces tell you nothing.

I stopped before the throne and bowed the way one bows to a king — fully, without irony, because whatever else he was, he was the thing the map had been walking toward for a thousand miles, and I had learned on that road to honor the fixed points. Wolf went to one knee. The king watched us with his fading face and his unblinking eyes, and then he leaned forward, and his left hand — the solid one — extended toward the map case at my hip.

"May I?"

"It was never mine to refuse you, Majesty," I said, and unslung it, and opened it, and laid the map on the small table beside his throne.

He looked at it for a long time. The hall held its breath. And I watched the king of the Hollowlands read his own kingdom — watched his eyes move across the drifting roads and the re-formed villages and the crowned figure bound in chains of light at the map's heart — and I saw something in his expression that I have no better word for than *homesickness*. A man looking at a portrait of the house he was erased from.

"It still draws," he said softly. "Aldric's work. I told them it would. I told the council, year after year — the map remembers, the map waits, the map is the realm's own memory, and you cannot kill a memory, you can only make everyone agree to forget it." His faint right hand rose and hovered above the map's heart, not touching, and where it hovered the ink stirred, the crowned figure turning its bound head as if toward him. The king's breath caught. "It knows me. Even like this. Even half-gone." He looked up at me, and the shimmer at his jaw was brighter now, and I understood that the effort of presence was costing him, and that he was paying it gladly. "You have brought me the one thing in the world I thought I would never see again, Lady Mirabel. The realm's memory. My own face, drawn by a loyal hand."

"Your Majesty," I began, "there is a rite. In the journal. A way to unbind—"

"I know what there is." He said it gently, but he said it the way one closes a door. "And I know what it will ask, and whom it will ask it of, and we will speak of that when the court is ready to hear it — which it is not, and may never be, and that failure will be mine and my chancellor's." His eyes moved past my shoulder, to the grey-eyed man, and something passed between them that I could not parse: command, or warning, or a marriage of both. "Tonight you will eat with us. Tonight you will rest. You have walked a long way through a country that moves, and even the truth needs a night's sleep before it is spoken in council."

He drew his left hand back from the map, and I saw that where his fingers had hovered closest, the ink had brightened — the bound figure's chains glinting, the crowned head clearer. And I saw, too, with a cartographer's cold precision, that the king's own outline had gone fainter by a degree I could measure. Presence cost him. Every moment of being seen, of being *real*, was paid for out of whatever remained.

"Thank you," I said, and meant it in a way that had nothing to do with courtesy.

He smiled. It was a good smile, a king's smile, worn like a well-kept sword, and it was the saddest thing I had seen since the guildhouse burned.

* * *

They gave us a room in the east cottage — a real room, with a bed apiece and a window that looked out on the cloud sea churning below the ridge, silver and endless in the late light. A maid brought water and bread and a stew of mountain goat and turnips that tasted like the best meal of my life, because I was twenty-six years old and had walked a thousand miles and was, for the first time since a stranger died at the ink door, indoors behind a closed door with no one hunting us closer than three days' ride.

Wolf checked the window latch, the door bar, the roofline. Old habits. Then he sat on his bed across from mine and began, with great concentration, to clean a knife that did not need cleaning.

"You saw it too," I said.

"I see most things. Say which one you mean."

"His hand." I turned my own palm up, tracing the memory of the shimmer. "The right one. When he reached for the map, the fingers went through the light wrong. And his outline, afterward. He's thinner, Wolf. Not in his body. In his *edges*. Every minute we stood there, being present for us, he spent something he doesn't have a way to earn back."

Wolf set the knife down. "The journal said the binding feeds on him. Slowly. The crown drinks, and he fades, and the usurper sits on a stolen throne wearing a stolen face, and the land drifts because the land knows its king is being unmade and cannot decide what shape to hold." He was quiet a moment. "Twenty-two years of that."

"Then the rite can't wait for the court to be ready. He doesn't *have* ready. Whatever's left of him is running out like ink from a cracked well, and every day we spend being feasted and housed and announced, the man on that throne loses another degree of himself." I heard my own voice rise and made myself stop, made myself breathe, because I had learned on the road that Wolf did not answer volume, only sense. "I'm sorry. I just — I keep ledgers. It's what I am. And the ledger in that hall is bleeding."

"I know." He looked at the window, at the silver cloud sea, and his face did the thing it did when he was deciding how much truth a moment could hold. "Then we move quickly. But Mirabel —" and here his voice dropped into the register I had learned to fear, the one he used for the things he saw in rooms that other people didn't see at all, "— did you watch the chancellor?"

"The one in grey. At the king's right hand."

"Left, actually. Center-right of the lords, left of the throne. Close enough to touch the king, far enough to watch the door." He turned to me. "When you said the word *unbind* — the whole hall flinched. The lords looked at each other. The knights looked at their king. But the chancellor didn't flinch, and he didn't look at the king. He looked at the map. And then he looked at *you*. And the expression on his face was not the expression of a man hearing dangerous news." Wolf's eyes held mine in the lamplight. "It was the expression of a man checking an inventory."

I felt the warmth of the stew go cold in me. "Checking it against what?"

"Against something he already owns," Wolf said. "Or already owes."

Outside, the wind came down off the ridge and moved through the village, and I heard it pass the great hall, where the king sat fading on his barn-born throne, and I thought of the map lying somewhere under guard in that hall, its ink bright and honest and awake, drawing everything true — and I wondered, with a chill that had nothing to do with the mountain air, what it would draw if we laid it open tomorrow before the whole assembled court.

Because I had seen what the ink did to lies. And I had begun, that night, to count the number of people in the Hollow King's court whose oaths I would not have wagered a single line of ink on — and the number was larger than one, and smaller than the court deserved, and it included, I was almost certain, the man who stood closest of anyone to the throne.
