## 14. The Court Does Not Want Them

The exile court woke early, the way old men do, and I lay in the narrow guest cell listening to it stir — the shuffle of slippers on stone, the cough of a brazier being fed, somewhere below a kettle singing its one thin song. The mountains kept their own hours, and the Hollow King's household kept the mountain's. Everything here was slow and cold and proud, like a sword hung on a wall too long.

Mirabel was already gone from her room when I passed it. I knew because I had checked, which was a habit I didn't examine too closely. Her door stood open to the grey morning light, her blanket folded with corners sharp enough to cut, and on the sill sat her ink pot, capped, waiting for her like a dog.

I found her in the great hall.

Or what they called the great hall, though it had been built as a granary two hundred years ago and never quite stopped smelling of rye. The loyalists had hung their banners from the rafters anyway — the stag and the seven stars, sun-faded to the color of weak tea — and beneath them the court of the true king conducted its daily business of not ruling anything: petitions that could not be granted, titles that could not be enforced, grievances against a usurper who had never once thought about any of them.

And at the center of it, on a plain wooden chair that had been carried up from some better hall and never returned, sat the king.

I still hadn't gotten used to looking at him. He was old — older than he should have been, though I couldn't have said how I knew that — and his edges were wrong. Not blurred exactly. *Unfinished*, Mirabel had said, and she'd been right, because she was always right about ink. The light seemed to lose interest in him partway through; his left hand faded at the fingertips like a sketch abandoned by a tired artist. When he moved, you saw the movement arrive a half-breath late, as if it had to travel a long way to reach him.

He was listening to a woman plead for grain allotments for a village that no longer existed, and he was giving her his whole attention, and that was the thing about him that made my chest hurt: whatever else the binding had eaten, it had not eaten his decency.

Which is why it took me three days to work up the nerve to say what needed saying.

Because saying it meant accusing Lord Vane.

* * *

Vane held court the way a spider holds a web — lightly, from the center, touching everything without seeming to move. He was tall and dry and precise, silver at the temples, dressed in black so well-cut it made every other man in the granary look like he'd slept in his clothes. He spoke softly. Everyone leaned in to hear him, and I understood that this was not an accident but a technique, one he had practiced until it looked like modesty.

He was also, I noticed, the only man in the hall who never once looked at the king while speaking of loyalty to him.

"Your petition will be heard," Vane told the grain woman gently, "in due season. His Majesty's resources are stretched, as you know. We must all bear our portion of the burden." And the woman bowed and went away with nothing, comforted, somehow, by the softness of the voice that had refused her.

Mirabel stood near the map table at the hall's far end, and I drifted toward her the way I'd learned to drift — unhurried, a soldier's trick, arriving places without anyone marking your arrival. She had the map out. Of course she did. She'd barely let it leave her sight since we'd climbed into these mountains, and I couldn't blame her; the thing had cost us enough blood that it felt less like a document now than a wound we were carrying between us.

She had it half-unrolled, weighted at the corners with candlesticks she'd borrowed without asking, and she was bent over it with her stylus hovering, not drawing, just watching. Watching the way she watched things — completely, hungrily, like the world might do something worth recording if she stared hard enough.

"Don't," I said quietly.

"I haven't done anything."

"You're about to."

Her mouth twitched. "It wants to be read, Wolf. It's been *pulling* since we crossed the pass. Look." She turned the map a few degrees so I could see. The Hollowlands spread across its face in living ink, roads crawling like veins, and there — high in the eastern ranges — a small cluster of marks had appeared overnight. A fortress shape. A ring of walls. A single star at its heart.

"The court," she said. "It drew the court. Last night, while we slept. It knows where we are, and it's — I don't know. Settling. Like a compass needle finding north." She looked up at me, and her eyes had that shine they got, the one that made me want to both follow her anywhere and lock her somewhere safe, and I knew those two impulses would never stop fighting each other. "A map that draws itself is a witness, Wolf. And witnesses get called to testify."

"That's what worries me."

"Then worry efficiently and help me hold the corner down."

I put my hand on the map's edge. Under my palm the paper was warm, faintly, the way a stone is warm after a day of sun even in winter. Around us the court murmured through its rituals of powerlessness, and nobody paid the least attention to the two strangers and their strange paper, and I remember thinking, later, how much I would give to have that ignorance back.

Because that was when Lord Vane came over.

* * *

He didn't hurry. Men like Vane never hurried; hurrying admitted that something mattered. He simply arrived, the way cold arrives under a door, and suddenly he was standing at the map table with his hands folded behind his back and his pale eyes moving across the parchment with an expression I recognized and hated on sight.

Hunger wearing the mask of scholarship.

"May I?" he said, and reached for the map before Mirabel answered.

"No," she said.

His hand stopped. To his credit — or his training — the surprise lasted only a heartbeat before smoothing away. "Forgive me. In His Majesty's court, all documents pertaining to the realm are—"

"This isn't His Majesty's court," Mirabel said. "With respect. This is a granary in the mountains where a king in exile does what good he can. The realm belongs to whoever holds it, and right now that's the man with the stolen crown, and I don't intend to hand him anything through carelessness." She said it evenly, without heat, the way she recited coordinates, and I watched several nearby courtiers go stiff as fence posts. Nobody talked to Lord Vane like that. Nobody had, apparently, in years.

"And you are?" Vane asked, though he knew. He'd taken our names the night we arrived, written them himself in a ledger I would think about more than once in the days to come.

"Mirabel Marchetti, of the Cartographers' Guild of the free cities. Journeyman rank." She paused, and I heard the small steel underneath the words. "Formerly."

"A guild clerk." Something shifted behind his eyes — not contempt, exactly. Calculation. "Journeyman Marchetti. Do you know what I find interesting about your map?"

"Its provenance?"

"Its silence." He smiled thinly. "You keep it covered. You sleep with it under your cloak. You consult it in corners. A clerk guards a ledger because the ledger has value. What value, precisely, does a map of a country everyone can see have, that it requires such jealousy?"

"The same value truth always has," Mirabel said. "It's rare."

Vane laughed — a dry, courteous sound, two notes long. "Charming. But the court has protocols, and I am its chancellor, and I am afraid I must insist. Any artifact bearing on the succession, the binding, or the crown falls under the chancellor's seal until His Majesty is restored to rule it properly. You may lodge it with me. It will be kept safe. It will be *respected*." He extended his hand, palm up, patient as a tax collector.

And I stepped between them.

I didn't plan it. My body simply decided, the way it had decided things in other lives I couldn't remember, and then I was standing in the space between Vane's hand and the map, and the hall had gone quiet around us, and forty pairs of loyalist eyes were watching the strangers insult their chancellor.

"Lord Vane," I said. "The map stays with her."

"You are her servant, I take it."

"I'm her—" I stopped. Found the word. "I'm her escort. Guild law gives her custody. If you want the map, take it up with the Guildmaster of Vessa, and when you've finished that correspondence, take it up with the dead man who entrusted it to us."

Something flickered across Vane's face at the word *dead*. Quick. Controlled. But I had spent years — however many years I had — reading men in rooms where reading them wrong meant dying, and I caught it. Not grief. Not curiosity.

*Relief.*

Gone in half a breath, buried under the thin smile, but I had seen it, and once seen it could not be unseen.

"Escort," Vane repeated, tasting the word. "How useful you must be." He withdrew his hand, folded it neatly behind his back again, and inclined his head to Mirabel with perfect courtesy. "Keep your map, journeyman. The court is generous. But understand —" and here his voice dropped, softening further, so that only the three of us occupied the sentence — "everything in these mountains eventually passes across my desk. It is simpler for everyone when it does so willingly."

He bowed, slightly, and moved away, and the hall resumed its murmur like water closing over a dropped stone.

Mirabel exhaled slowly. "Well," she said. "That went badly."

"That went *exactly* as far as it could go without blood," I said. "Come on. Not here."

* * *

We walked the outer gallery of the granary, the cold corridor above the main floor where stored grain had once been winched up through a roof hatch now boarded over. Below us, through gaps in the rail, the court glittered dimly — candlelight, banners, the king's unfinished profile nodding over some new petitioner. Mirabel walked close to me. Not touching. Just close, the way she'd started doing somewhere on the road without either of us naming it, two bodies agreeing on a distance smaller than politeness required.

"He wants the map," she said.

"Yes."

"Everyone wants the map. That's not news. What's news is—" She stopped at the rail, staring down at the hall. "Wolf, he didn't ask about the binding. Or the crown. Or the heir. He asked about *value*. He priced it. Chancellors don't price things. Merchants price things."

"Spies price things."

She turned to look at me. In the cold gallery light her face was all angles and seriousness, and I thought, not for the first time, that she was the most dangerous person I knew, and that the danger lived entirely in how much she noticed.

"Say it plainly," she said. "You've been circling it since last night. I've watched you circle it through two meals and a prayer service you clearly didn't understand."

So I said it plainly. I made myself stand still while I did, because some part of me wanted to pace, and pacing would have made it worse.

"Vane's accent," I said. "Listen to it next time. Not the words — the accent. The vowels. He flattens the back of his throat on the long vowels, and he swallows the ends of place-names. It's a southern court accent. High-born, southern, from the capital country." I paused. "The usurper speaks the same way. Exactly the same way. Same region, same class, same — I don't know the word for it. Same *shape* of voice. I noticed it the first night and told myself it meant nothing. Half the nobility of the south sounds alike. But it's not just the accent, Mira. It's the mannerisms. He touches his rings when he lies — the usurper does the same. He stands with his weight on his left foot. He says 'in due season' when he means 'never.' I've heard the usurper use that exact phrase through heralds, word for word."

The silence stretched. Somewhere below, the kettle-song came again, thinner.

"You're saying the chancellor of the true king's exile court," Mirabel said carefully, "speaks like the man who stole his throne."

"I'm saying more than that. I'm saying he reacted to the name of the dead man. Corvess — the cartographer. I mentioned him and Vane flinched with *relief*. You don't feel relief that a man is dead unless his death benefits you." I gripped the rail. The wood was old and damp and it grounded me. "Mira, I think Corvess wasn't fleeing the usurper when he died. I think he was fleeing *this court*. I think he found out what Vane is, and he ran, and he gave the map to strangers because there was no one inside these walls he could trust."

"Then we tell the king."

"We tell the king," I agreed, "and the king hears us through Vane. Who schedules his audiences. Who reads his correspondence. Who appointed the captain of his guard and the steward of his kitchens and probably the man who carries his cup." I shook my head. "You saw the hall today. Every petition goes through him. Every ear the king has belongs to Vane first. We accuse the chancellor of treason in front of a court full of men whose offices depend on the chancellor, with no proof but an accent and a feeling in my gut—"

"Your gut has been right before."

"My gut got us out of a ford ambush. It won't topple a chancellor. They'll call us liars, or worse — agents of the usurper sent to sow discord in the loyal court. Which is exactly what we'd look like. Two strangers, no history, guarding a self-drawing map, accusing the most trusted man in the mountain." I laughed, short and bitter. "Mira, if I were Vane, that's precisely the story I'd tell about us. He may already be telling it."

She was quiet a long moment. Below us, the audience was ending; petitioners bowed their way out empty-handed, comforted, and the king rose from his plain chair with that half-breath delay, and Vane was at his elbow instantly, murmuring, steering, a hand hovering just behind the old man's shoulder — guiding without touching, the way you lead someone who cannot see.

Watching that, Mirabel's jaw set in a way I was coming to know very well.

"They won't hear it," she said. "Not as accusation. Fine. Then we don't bring accusation." She turned to me, and there was a light in her face that frightened me a little, because I'd seen it before, on the night she first understood what the map did, and it was the light of a woman about to commit professional heresy. "Accusation needs proof. So we'll make proof. Wolf — the ink responds to truth. Lies smear. Oaths, confessions, testimony — spoken truth makes the lines *hold*, and spoken falsehood makes them run. I've tested it. I know its rules."

I saw where she was going and my stomach dropped. "You want to map the court."

"I want to map the court. A survey of the great hall. Every oath sworn in the king's presence, every pledge of loyalty, every pretty speech — with the map present, and the ink doing what the ink does." Her hands were moving now, unconsciously, sketching shapes in the cold air. "Vane will demand we surrender it the moment he understands what we're doing. So we don't tell him. We do it openly, innocently — a clerk making herself useful, charting the hall for the royal archive. Let him watch. Let him *approve* it. And let every false oath in that room bleed red on the page in front of the whole court."

"And when it bleeds red in front of Vane's seat specifically?"

"Then," Mirabel said, "we'll finally have proof. And we'll need to be ready for what happens the moment we have it — because a man like that doesn't wait for trials."

I looked down at the hall one more time. The king had settled back into his chair, fading at the edges, decent and diminished, and beside him Vane stood straight and dark and complete, more solid than the king he served, more solid than any man in the room — as if reality itself had decided whose side it was on.

The enemy was inside the sanctuary. Had perhaps always been inside the sanctuary. And no one would hear it, not from us, not yet.

But ink remembers. Ink testifies. And in three days' time, at the court's weekly assembly, every soul in that granary would swear the seasonal oath of fealty to the Hollow King — all together, all aloud, all in one room.

"All right," I said. "But Mira — we do it my way. Slow. Quiet. And the first sign Vane moves against us, we're gone over the wall, map or no map. Promise me."

She laced her fingers through mine, brief and firm, a contract sealed in the cold. "Promised," she said. "Now come help me measure a granary. A clerk making herself useful needs somewhere to start."

Below us, unheeded, the candles burned down over the king and his chancellor, and the shadows they threw on the banner of the stag and seven stars made the crown above the stag's head look, for a moment, like a cage.
