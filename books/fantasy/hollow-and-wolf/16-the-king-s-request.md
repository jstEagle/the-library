## 16. The King's Request

They gave Wolf a room with a door, which in the exile court of the Hollow King was either an honor or an oversight.

He lay on the narrow bed without sleeping and listened to the mountain breathe. The court had been carved into the living rock of the Graymantle peaks generations ago — he could feel it in the walls, stone that remembered being warm, hearths that had gone cold one by one as the loyalists thinned. Somewhere below, a sentry coughed. Somewhere above, wind worked at the arrow slits like a clerk trying to open a stubborn drawer. And beneath all of it, faint as a pulse heard through a floor, the king faded.

Wolf had felt it the moment they'd been brought before him that morning. He'd expected a throne room; what he'd gotten was a long hall where the banners hung grey as old cobwebs, where forty-odd courtiers stood in clothes two decades out of fashion, mended with the particular care of people who had nothing left to be proud of except their pride. And at the end of it, on a chair that had never quite become a throne, a man whose edges did not hold.

It was hard to describe to anyone who hadn't seen it. The Hollow King was not transparent, exactly. He was *unfinished* — like ink applied by a hand losing its conviction. His left hand was solid enough, knuckles and scars and a signet ring worn smooth. But his right shoulder blurred when he moved, and the light behind him came through the edge of his jaw as though his profile had been sketched in charcoal and someone had dragged a thumb across it. When he spoke, his voice came from slightly too far away, as if the man were standing a step behind himself.

Mirabel had stared. Wolf had watched her stare, and watched her do what she always did with the impossible: take out her little book and begin, quietly, to record it. He'd thought the chancellor would object. Lord Vane had objected to everything else — their arrival, their weapons, the map, the very fact of them breathing the court's thin air. But Vane had only smiled at the king's request for a private audience with "the soldier," and the smile had sat wrong on his face, like a borrowed coat.

Now it was night, and a knock came at the door anyway.

Wolf was on his feet before the second knock landed, knife out of his boot, body remembering doors before his mind did. He crossed the room in silence and put his eye to the seam.

A boy. Fifteen at most, in the grey-and-white livery of the royal household, holding a candle that guttered in the corridor draft. Behind him, half-shadowed, stood a figure Wolf knew by its wrongness: Lord Vane, arms folded, watching from the far end of the hall with the patience of a man who counted other men's heartbeats.

"The king will see you," the boy said. "Alone."

"Alone," Wolf repeated.

"The king's word is alone." The boy swallowed. "The chancellor says you may bring your knife if it makes you feel brave."

Down the hall, Vane's smile didn't move. Wolf looked at him for a long moment — long enough to make it a message — then sheathed the knife where Vane could see him do it, which was its own kind of answer, and followed the boy.

* * *

The king's private chamber was small. That surprised him more than anything else had. After the long cold hall, after the banners and the ceremony of ruin, here was a room barely larger than a cell: a bed with a quilt patched in six colors, a desk buried under papers, a window slit framing a slice of star-hard sky. A single candle. And on the wall above the desk, pinned like a common tavern notice, a map so old its vellum had gone the color of tea.

Wolf knew that map. Not this copy — but the hand. He'd spent three weeks watching Mirabel trace that same confident line-work, that same habit of marking uncertain ground with tiny question marks instead of leaving it blank. The dead stranger's hand. Sera's brother. The king's cartographer.

"You're staring at Edran's work," the king said.

Wolf turned. The king sat by the candle in a plain wool robe, and up close the fading was worse — or perhaps just more honest. This near, Wolf could see that the blurring wasn't random. It moved. The king's outline shifted like something seen through heat-haze, and every few breaths a part of him simply... waited, unfinished, until the rest of him caught up.

"Your Majesty." Wolf bowed the way you bow to a general you don't yet trust — shallow, eyes up.

"Sit. If I'm to ask you an impertinent question, the least I can do is let you refuse to answer it comfortably." The king gestured at the room's only other chair, then almost smiled. "I'm told I used to be funny. Forgive me if the timing has gone."

Wolf sat. The chair creaked. Neither of them spoke while the king poured two cups of something dark from a clay jug, and Wolf noticed that the pouring hand was solid, and that the king watched his own hand while it worked, the way a man watches a horse he suspects has gone lame.

"Edran mapped my crown," the king said at last. "Before it was taken. Before it was bound. He was the last honest surveyor of what we are, and the first to understand what had been done to us." He set a cup in front of Wolf. "You carry his map now. Or rather, the Marchetti girl does, and you carry the Marchetti girl, which may amount to the same thing."

"We carry each other," Wolf said. "Your Majesty."

"Yes." The king studied him over the rim of his cup. "That's what I heard. Vane tells it differently, naturally. In his version you're a mercenary who found a dying fool and a gullible clerk and saw profit in a fairy tale." The king's voice dried out a little. "Vane's versions are always the ones with no one decent in them. I've stopped believing them, mostly. Mostly."

"Why am I here?"

"Because I asked myself a question tonight, and I've grown too faded to lie to myself about wanting the answer." The king set down his cup. Outside, the wind changed its grip on the arrow slit and the candle flame leaned. "When Aldric bound the crown — the true binding, the rite beneath the usurper's citadel — the rite required an anchor. Do you know what an anchor is, in binding-craft?"

"I know anchors," Wolf said. "Rope, iron, the bottom of the sea."

"A binding needs something that cannot be moved by force. Not strength — force can be outlasted. Not wealth — wealth can be stolen. It needs a thing freely given that cannot be taken back. A vow. A truth. A—" the king's blurred hand opened, palm up, "—a name. Freely spoken, freely surrendered. Aldric gave his own name to anchor the crown against unworthy hands. That was the rite's genius and its cruelty: the crown cannot be unbound by any name except the one that bound it. And Aldric erased that name from every record, every memory, every mouth. He made himself unfindable so the crown would be unbreakable."

"He didn't count on someone wanting it broken," Wolf said.

"No one ever counts on their own children." The king said it lightly, and it landed like a dropped anvil. He let it sit there between them, steam rising off two untouched cups, and then he asked it.

"Wolf. Soldier. Nameless man trusted with a dying cartographer's final breath. Are you Aldric's son?"

The candle leaned again. Somewhere in the rock overhead, water ticked down through stone, patient as a ledger-keeper.

Wolf opened his mouth to say *no*, because that was the safe answer, the answer that kept him nothing and nobody and free. And found that he couldn't. Not because some oath sealed his tongue. Because the word wouldn't come out true, and some part of him — the part that had survived twenty years of forgetting by never lying to himself about anything that mattered — refused to spend it on this.

"I don't know," he said.

The king nodded slowly, as if that were the answer he'd both feared and hoped for. "You don't know. Tell me what you do know."

"Burned regiment. Twenty years ago, maybe more. I woke in a river with no name and a sword callus older than my memories. There was a man — the cartographer, Edran — who found me once, years back. He looked at me like he knew me. He never said from where. Last week he died putting a map in a clerk's hands and a word in her ear, and the word was me." Wolf turned the cup in his fingers. "I've spent twenty years being nobody on purpose. Nobody doesn't get asked questions like this."

"And yet here you are, being asked." The king rose — and the rising cost him; Wolf saw the effort of it travel across the king's unfinished face like weather. He crossed to the old map on the wall and touched the border of the Hollowlands with one solid finger. "Do you know what happens to a land when its king fades? The roads move. The villages drift. The borders forget themselves. Everyone thinks the Hollowlands are cursed. They're not cursed. They're *grieving*. The land is losing its shape because its shape was held by a crowned man, and the crown holds him, and the crown is bound to a name that no longer exists anywhere in the world." He turned. "Except possibly in a river-soaked soldier who won't say no."

"If I were his son," Wolf said carefully, "what would that make me?"

"It would make you the counter-anchor. The heir of the binding. The one man alive whose freely given name could release the crown — or seal it forever." The king came closer, and this near, Wolf could see stars through the edge of the man's ear, and it took everything he had not to lean away. "And it would make you the most hunted person in these mountains, which is why I am asking you privately, at night, with no witnesses, instead of shouting it from my unfinished hall. Vane must never hear your answer. Whatever it is."

"Because Vane serves the usurper."

"Because Vane serves whoever is winning," the king said, "which is worse, and more useful to him."

Silence. The candle burned. Wolf became aware that his hands had gone still in his lap — the total stillness of a man aiming at something — and he made himself move them.

"Your Majesty. Suppose I say yes. Suppose I'm Aldric's son and I know it in my bones by morning. What then? You want me to give up my name? I don't have one to give. That's the joke. Whoever built me built me hollow first."

"That is precisely why I believe it might be you," the king said gently. "Aldric didn't lose his name. He *unmade* it — and everything downstream of him. A son raised inside that erasure would wake in rivers with nothing. Would be a wolf and nothing else. Would be exactly what the binding needed hidden and exactly what the unbinding needs found." He sat back down, heavily, the fading pulling at him like a tide going out. "I am not asking you to answer tonight. I am asking you to stop pretending the question is someone else's."

Wolf stood. It seemed suddenly important to be vertical. "And if the answer destroys me? Names aren't free, from what you say. Yours is costing you everything."

"My name is costing me nothing," the king said. "Its *absence* is. There's a difference, soldier, and it's the whole difference between us." He looked up, and for one moment the fading stilled — the whole man, solid, present, a king in a patched robe in a cold stone room — and Wolf understood that this was what the Hollowlands had lost. "Go. Sleep, if you can. And when the girl asks you what the king wanted—"

"She'll ask."

"She'll ask," the king agreed, and there was real warmth in it, the first warmth Wolf had felt in the mountain. "Tell her the truth. She reads maps that punish lies. Best not to train yourself out of the habit around her."

Wolf reached the door. The handle was cold iron under his palm, and he stood there a moment without turning, because turning meant answering with his face what he couldn't answer with his tongue.

"Your Majesty. If I'm his son — if the binding ends because of me — what happens to the man who wakes up afterward? The one with the name?"

"I don't know," the king said. "No one has ever come back from giving one away. But I'll tell you what I believe, for whatever a fading man's beliefs weigh." The candle flame steadied. "I believe a name isn't what you remember. It's what remembers *you*. Give yours away, and whatever comes back will know the road it walked — even if it doesn't know why."

Wolf opened the door. The boy was gone; the corridor was empty except for the draft and the distant sound of Vane's door closing somewhere below, soft as a lid.

He walked back through the carved halls past the grey banners, past the sentry who pretended not to see him, and did not go to his room. He went to Mirabel's door and stood outside it in the dark, fist raised, hearing her through the wood — the scratch of her pen, steady as a heartbeat, recording the impossible things in her careful hand.

The question had teeth now. It had his blood in it.

And tomorrow, or the day after, or the moment the map chose, someone was going to make him open his mouth and find out what lived behind twenty years of silence — while somewhere below them, a door closed softly on a man who already knew the answer, and was deciding what it was worth.
