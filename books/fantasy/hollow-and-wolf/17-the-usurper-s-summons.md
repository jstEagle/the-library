## 17. The Usurper's Summons

The herald came at dawn, when the mountain light was still the color of pewter and the exile court had not yet finished its argument with the night.

I knew something was wrong before anyone spoke of it. The map told me first.

It lay across my knees in the corner of the guest chamber Wolf and I had been given — a room meant for honored allies, though it smelled of dust and old incense and the particular loneliness of furniture that has waited decades for guests who never came. I had taken to sleeping with the map near me the way other people sleep near a window they can see the sky from. It was not comfort, exactly. It was witness. After everything it had shown me — the crowned figure in chains of light, the roads that moved like slow animals, the red bloom of Aldenmere's burning that had soaked its parchment through two nights past — I no longer trusted any morning that began without consulting it.

And that morning, as the first bell of the court rang thin and cold down the corridors, the ink began to move without my asking.

Not the restless drift I had learned to read in the Hollowlands' border country, where villages wandered and fords relocated like herds seeking water. This was different. This was purposeful. Across the lower third of the map, where the usurper's territory sat nailed flat and gray — that dead country with no drift in it, no breath, every road pinned like a moth to a board — a single line of ink rose up out of the fixed land and began to crawl northward. Toward the mountains. Toward us.

It moved the way a snake moves, if a snake were drawn by a careful hand: segment by segment, deliberate, unhurried, certain of its destination. And where it passed, small letters surfaced in its wake, written in an angular script I did not recognize but understood anyway, the way one understands a threat in any language.

*Parley,* the letters said. *Parley. Parley.*

"Wolf," I said, and my voice came out steadier than I felt, which is a skill the Guild taught me and which I have since come to regard as the single most useful thing I ever learned there. "Wake up. We have company coming."

He was awake already. He was always awake already; I had stopped wondering how a man could sleep so lightly and so little and still function. He crossed the room in three strides and looked down at the map over my shoulder, and I felt rather than saw the change in him — the stillness that came over him when his soldier's mind took over from whatever softer thing lived underneath it.

"How long?" he asked.

"Since the bell. Maybe less." I traced the crawling line with one finger, careful not to touch the ink itself. The map had opinions about being touched by hands carrying lies, and while I tried very hard not to lie to it, I had learned respect the hard way. "A day's ride behind the line, if the line moves at the speed it's drawing itself. Faster, if they don't care about being seen."

"They don't care about being seen." Wolf said it flatly, the way he said most true things. "A parley herald travels under a white banner and everyone on the road gets out of his way. That's the point. He wants us to watch him come."

"Then we should watch him come," said a voice from the doorway, and we both turned.

Lord Vane stood in the corridor with two of the king's house-guards behind him, and I will grant him this: he was very good at looking like a man who had been awake all night worrying on the king's behalf. His robes were immaculate. His expression was grave. His eyes went to the map on my knees with a hunger he didn't quite manage to disguise, and I pulled the edge of my blanket over it with a motion I hoped looked casual and suspected looked anything but.

"The king summons you both," Vane said. "There is a herald on the mountain road under a banner of parley. His Grace wishes his... cartographer present when the terms are read."

*His cartographer.* As if I belonged to the court. As if the map belonged to the court. I stood, folding the map into its oilcloth with the quick economy of long practice, and tied it beneath my coat against my ribs, where it rode warm and faintly alive against my skin like a second heartbeat.

"We'll come," Wolf said, before I could decide whether to say something I'd regret. He touched my elbow once, briefly — our shorthand now, after everything — and followed Vane out.

* * *

The great hall of the exile court had been a temple once, or a fortress chapel, or possibly both; the Hollowlands did not distinguish between the places people prayed and the places people made their last stands. Its vaulted ceiling was stained black with centuries of smoke, and the banners of houses that no longer existed hung from the rafters like the skins of enormous dead animals. The Hollow King's throne sat at the far end on a dais of rough-hewn stone, and the king himself sat in it the way a man sits in cold water — enduringly, without complaint, but visibly diminished by the hour.

He was worse than when we had arrived. I noticed it immediately and hated that I noticed it, because noticing was my profession and I could not stop doing it even when what I noticed broke something in me. His edges had blurred further. Where his left hand rested on the arm of the throne, the fingers faded at the tips into something like smoke, or unfinished sketching — the hand of a man an artist had abandoned partway through. When he turned his head to look at us, the light seemed to pass through the line of his jaw.

But his eyes were still eyes. Dark, patient, and terribly sad, and still, unmistakably, a king's.

"He comes under parley," the king said, without preamble, as we were brought to stand before the dais. Around him the court assembled itself — Lord Vane at his right hand, the chancellor's place of honor, a fact that sat in my stomach like a swallowed stone given everything Wolf and I suspected and could prove to no one. The loyalist lords and their retainers filled the benches, maybe forty souls altogether, wrapped in threadbare finery, wearing the pride of a kingdom the way beggars wear a dead man's coat. "So we will hear him under parley. Whatever filth he has brought to say, we will hear it standing, and we will not give him the satisfaction of seeing us flinch."

"Your Grace," Vane murmured, "perhaps it would be prudent to consider the terms privately—"

"I have considered privately for thirty years, Lord Vane." The king's voice was dry as old paper, but it carried. "It has accomplished nothing except keeping me alive long enough to watch my kingdom forget my face. Let the court hear. Let them all hear."

The horns sounded from the gatehouse then — three notes, low and formal, the ancient signal for a parley granted. And we waited.

They brought the herald in through doors tall enough for a giant, and I will confess that my first thought, absurdly, was of the Guildhall of Vessa. Because the herald walked like a guildsman. He walked with the measured, self-important tread of a man who represents an institution larger than himself and has made his peace with being its mouthpiece. His grey cloak was fine wool, spotless, and the white parley banner he carried was so clean it hurt to look at in that smoky hall — a rectangle of pure surrender-to-conversation, held high.

He was young. That was the worst of it. Twenty-five at most, with a scholar's soft hands and a courier's satchel at his hip, and he looked around the ruined hall with an expression I recognized because I had worn it myself in another life: the polite condescension of someone from a well-ordered place visiting a place that had failed to remain ordered.

He stopped twenty paces from the dais, planted the banner, and bowed — correctly, precisely, insultingly shallow.

"Hollow King," he said. His voice was trained, pleasant, and carried to every corner of the hall without effort. "I bring greetings from the Crown Regnant of the Hollowlands, Warden of the Fixed Roads, Protector of the Peace."

*Warden of the Fixed Roads.* I heard Wolf breathe out slowly beside me. In the usurper's country, nothing drifted. Nothing moved. Nothing lived, really — the land there was nailed down like a specimen in a case, and I had felt the wrongness of it in my bones when we'd crossed within sight of its borders. To name yourself warden of such a place was to boast of having embalmed a kingdom.

"The free cities have petitioned the Crown for peace," the herald continued, unrolling a scroll with a snap of practiced wrists. "The Crown, in its mercy, has agreed. The terms are these."

He read them. I stood with my hands folded and my face composed — the ledger-keeper's face, the auditor's face, the face I had spent ten years perfecting in the Guildhall of Vessa while men twice my age explained to me why precision was unfeminine and footnotes were impertinent — and I listened to a price list being read aloud.

Peace for the free cities. Their charters confirmed, their walls unmolested, their trade protected in perpetuity. Every debt forgiven, every grievance settled, every soldier on every border sent home to his family. It was generous. It was magnificently, obscenely generous, and every word of it was a lie built on top of a truth, which is the most dangerous architecture there is.

In exchange, the Crown required only two things.

"The first," the herald said, "is the document known as the Marchetti Map — the self-drawing chart created by the traitor Edran Sera's brother, the king's former cartographer, currently in the possession of persons within this court. The Crown requires its delivery, intact, together with the woman who keeps it."

My own name, spoken in that pleasant trained voice, in that smoky hall, in front of forty strangers. I had imagined many versions of how my name might one day matter to history. None of them had involved being listed as a term of surrender.

"And the second," the herald went on, and here his gaze lifted from the scroll and found Wolf, deliberately, the way a fencer finds his line, "is the man who travels with her. The one the Crown's records name as the thief of names. The Crown requires his delivery as well. Alive."

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight and temperature. Somewhere in the rafters, a raven shifted its feet, and the sound of it was enormous.

Wolf did not move. I watched him not move, and it was the most controlled thing I have ever seen a human being do. But his hand, hanging at his side, had closed into a fist so tight the knuckles had gone white, and I understood — with the sudden terrible clarity the map had taught me — that *thief of names* was not an accusation the usurper had invented. It was a description. Someone knew what Wolf was. Someone knew what he carried, even if he didn't carry it knowingly. Even if he couldn't remember it.

The crown wanted its counter-anchor back. Or wanted him destroyed. With the crown, I suspected, those were the same request.

"You may tell your master," the Hollow King said, and his voice did not rise, but it filled the hall the way water fills a cup, "that the free cities are not mine to sell. They never were. That was rather the point of them."

"Your Grace misunderstands—"

"No." The king leaned forward, and for a moment — just a moment — the fading stopped. The edges of him sharpened. The smoke-fingers solidified, and the light stopped passing through his jaw, and I saw, briefly, the man he must have been before the binding: a king who had held a living kingdom in his two solid hands. "You misunderstand, herald. You stand in the last court of the Hollowlands and you offer me peace purchased with two hostages, and you call the purchase mercy. I know your master's grammar. I wore this crown before he stole it, and I remember what he was when he knelt at my father's table." He paused. "Tell the free cities their king does not accept terms bought with other men's freedom. Tell them the offer is refused. And tell them—" his voice caught, just slightly, on the next words, and I realized with a jolt that he was not speaking to the herald anymore, he was speaking past him, through him, to every ear in the free cities that might someday hear this message repeated, "—tell them I would rather be forgotten entirely than remembered as a man who paid his debts with his friends."

The herald rolled up his scroll. He did not seem surprised. I had the uncomfortable sense that he had expected exactly this answer, that refusal was simply the next line in someone else's script, and that the generosity of the terms had never been meant to succeed. They had been meant to divide. To make forty frightened people in a smoky hall look at the old fading king on his stone throne and do arithmetic: *two lives against every city wall from here to the sea.*

I watched the arithmetic happen. I watched it in the faces along the benches — the flickers, the glances exchanged, the lord whose lips moved slightly as he counted soldiers he didn't have. And I watched Lord Vane, standing at the king's shoulder, wear an expression of profound, sorrowful reasonableness, the face of a man preparing to say the sensible thing at exactly the right moment.

"Your Grace," Vane said softly, when the herald had been escorted out, "the court should deliberate."

"The court has deliberated for thirty years," the king said. "Deliberate faster."

* * *

They put us in a side chamber afterward — Wolf, Sera, and I — while the court argued in voices that carried perfectly through walls built for defense rather than discretion. Sera had arrived mid-parley, summoned from the archives where she'd been transcribing her brother's journal, and she stood now by the narrow window with her arms crossed, watching the mountain road below where the herald's escort was descending, the white banner furled at last.

"He'll report more than the refusal," Sera said. She had her brother's dark hair and none of his warmth; grief had burned the softness out of her and left something harder and cleaner. "That's what parleys are for. Not the offer — the observation. He came to count us."

"He counted well, then," Wolf said. He stood with his back to the wall, arms folded, in the posture I had learned meant his mind was three moves ahead of his body. "Forty fighting men at most, half of them old. A king who fades a little more each week. A chancellor nobody trusts, including me." His jaw tightened. "Especially me."

"And a map," I said quietly.

All three of us looked at each other, and nobody said the rest of it, because the rest of it was obvious: *and a map, and the woman who carries it, and the man the crown is hungry for.* The noose had not been thrown over a branch. It had been thrown over all of us, and the herald had merely read out the dimensions of the rope.

"The terms were too good," I said, because someone had to say it aloud, and precision was my one weapon and I intended to use it. "Charters confirmed, debts forgiven, armies withdrawn. No usurper offers that unless he expects to take it back later. The offer wasn't peace. It was a wedge. He wants the court to refuse — publicly, memorably — so that when the free cities suffer for it, they blame the king. He's building the story of our failure in advance." I heard my own voice go flat and hard, the auditor's voice, the one that had once made grown cartographers apologize to their ledgers. "Every entry balances, gentlemen. This one balances against us."

Sera looked at me with something new in her face — respect, maybe, or recognition. "My brother wrote something like that in the journal. 'The crown does not conquer,' he wrote. 'It contracts. It lets other people do the conquering and signs the paperwork.'" Her mouth twisted. "Edran always said the usurper's greatest weapon was a quill."

I thought of the Guildhall. Of course I did. I thought of the Guildmaster's smooth voice explaining why the Delivery Clause was an archaism, why exceptions eroded institutions, why the wise thing — always, forever, the wise thing — was to surrender the difficult object to the vault and let the institution absorb the loss. The usurper ruled a whole country that way. Perhaps he had begun by ruling a guildhall.

Below the window, the herald's party reached the switchback where the mountain road met the forest, and paused. And I saw — because I was looking, because noticing was the only prayer I knew — the herald turn in his saddle and look back up at the fortress. Look directly at our window, though we stood in shadow and he could not possibly have seen us.

He raised one hand. Not a wave. Something smaller. Two fingers, touched briefly to his lips, then extended toward the mountain — toward the king's hall, toward the fading man on his stone throne.

A salute? A promise?

Then the party descended into the trees and was gone, and the white banner went with them, and the road below stood empty in the morning light.

"It begins soon," Wolf said. "Whatever the refusal bought us, it bought days. Not weeks."

Behind us, muffled by the old stone, the court was still arguing. And beneath my coat, against my ribs, the map stirred — a slow, deliberate movement of ink, like a sleeper turning over — and when I found a private moment and unfolded its corner, I found that it had drawn something new without being asked.

Not roads. Not armies. Just words, in the moving hand I had come to think of as the map's own voice, surfacing pale and patient in the margin where the mountains met the usurper's fixed gray country:

*The price list grows. Read the names before they are read to you.*

And beneath the words, in fresh ink still glistening, a single new entry had appeared — a name I did not recognize, written in the angular script of the herald's masters:

*Sera, of the house of the king's cartographer.*

I looked up at Sera, laughing at something Wolf had said, alive and sharp and unaware, and I folded the map away fast, and I did not tell her.

Some entries, I had learned, you read in private first. Some prices, you carry alone until you find a way to strike them from the ledger.

Outside, far below, the mountain road lay empty — and somewhere beyond the tree line, already moving, the thing the refusal had purchased was coming for all of us, and it had a list, and the list had room on it for every name in this hall.
