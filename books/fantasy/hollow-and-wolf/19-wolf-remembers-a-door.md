## 19. Wolf Remembers a Door

The hills above the Hollow King's court were called the Shepherd's Teeth, though no shepherd had grazed them in a generation. They rose behind the loyalists' shabby stronghold like the spine of some buried beast, grey and wind-scoured, and it was there that Sera went each evening to gather what passed for herbs in that thin soil — wormwood, stone-thyme, a bitter leaf she called king's sorrow because, she said, it only grew where someone had waited too long.

Wolf went with her.

He told himself it was to keep watch. The court was shaken after Mirabel's mapping of it — Lord Vane's flight had left a wound in the assembly's pride, and wounded things attracted wolves of every kind. Someone had to walk the perimeter. Someone had to count the sentries and check the treeline and do all the small soldier's arithmetic that kept people alive. That was his work. That had always been his work, as far back as his memory reached, which was not far.

But the truth was simpler and he did not look at it directly: Sera hummed when she worked, and when she hummed, something at the bottom of him stirred like a fish under ice.

It happened on the fourth evening.

* * *

The sun was going down in long copper bands, and the wind carried the smell of cold stone and distant pine. Sera crouched among the rocks with her skirt full of leaves, her brother's journal tucked under one arm — she still carried it everywhere, as if the dead man might need it where he was going. And she hummed.

It was nothing, at first. A tune without words, five notes rising, three falling, the kind of melody mothers make up out of tiredness and love. Wolf stood at the edge of the rocks with his arms crossed against the wind and let it wash over him the way you let rain wash over you, without thinking.

Then she added words.

*Sleep, little door, sleep.*
*The key is in the keeping.*
*Sleep, little door, sleep.*
*No hand shall find the opening.*

The world tilted.

Not the ground — the ground stayed exactly where it was, honest grey rock under honest worn boots. It was *he* who tilted, some interior floor giving way, and Wolf caught himself against a boulder and heard himself make a sound he did not recognize, low and broken, an animal noise of grief or recognition or both.

Because he knew the song.

He knew it the way you know your own hands in the dark. He knew it sung low by a woman's voice in a kitchen that smelled of bread and tallow smoke; he knew it hummed off-key by a man mending a harness by firelight; he knew it sung by a child — *by himself*, a child with scraped knees standing on a chair to reach the shelf — and the knowledge came up through him like floodwater through a cellar stair, and every note dragged something with it.

A door.

A door of black oak, iron-banded, older than any house should be, set into a hillside — no, set into the *root of a hill*, down and down beneath flagstones and earth, beneath a house with a crooked chimney. The door had no handle. It had a keyhole shaped like nothing he had ever seen since: not a circle, not a slit, but a shape like a closed eye.

And his father's voice.

Ser Aldric — the name was still only a word to him, a coin he could hold but not spend — but the voice came whole, deep and deliberate, the voice of a man who did not say things twice. Wolf was seven years old, standing on those flagstones in his nightshirt, and the torches made the iron bands shine like wet blood, and his father knelt before the door with his palm flat against the oak and recited:

*"By my name I bind it. By my blood I anchor it. What is given freely cannot be taken, and what is freely given cannot be undone — save by the giver, or the giver's heir, speaking true."*

His father turned then. Looked at him. Seven years old in a doorway of torchlight, and his father's face was terrible and tender at once, the face of a man saying goodbye to something while it still breathed.

*"Remember this room,"* the voice said. *"One day you will need to forget it. When you have forgotten everything else, remember this room."*

"Wolf."

Sera's hand on his arm. He had not felt her cross the distance between them. He was on his knees among the king's sorrow leaves, he realized distantly, and his hands were shaking, and the copper light was failing, and somewhere very far away Sera was saying his name again — the name that wasn't a name, the placeholder, the *wolf* they had hung on him like a coat.

"Your song," he managed. "Where did your brother learn it?"

She frowned, searching. "Our mother sang it. She said it came from the old country, from before the binding." Her eyes widened slowly, the way frost forms on glass. "Wolf. You know it."

"I know more than the song."

* * *

Mirabel found them by lamplight in the chamber the court had given her — a cell, really, with a cot and a desk she had commandeered from somewhere, its surface drowning under maps and notes in her small fierce handwriting. She worked nights now. Since Vane's flight she trusted the dark hours least, and she said the map drew best by candlelight anyway, its ink moving like something swimming.

She looked up when he came in, and whatever was in his face stopped her quill mid-stroke.

"What happened?"

"I remembered a door."

He sat across from her, on the floor, because his legs had decided the matter for him. He told her all of it, in order, the way you report to a commanding officer because order is the only thing holding you together: the lullaby, the kitchen, the hillside house with the crooked chimney, the black oak door with the eye-shaped keyhole, the rite. His father kneeling. The words of the binding, spoken slow so a child could carry them.

*"By my name I bind it. By my blood I anchor it."*

Mirabel had gone very still. He watched her arrive at it a heartbeat before he said it aloud — watched her lips move, counting, checking, the ledger-keeper's reflex even now.

"The crown is anchored to a name," she said quietly. "The journal says so. Only a freely given name can release it. And your father—" Her breath caught. "Your father bound the crown with *his own name*. And then he erased it. He erased it so no one could ever speak it. So the binding could never be undone."

"And a man cannot give what he has forgotten," Wolf said. His own voice sounded far away. "Unless he remembers."

The candleflame leaned. Neither of them moved to right it.

"But that's not the worst of it," he went on, because it had to be said, because she deserved the whole weight of it and not the half. "My father didn't just hide the name. He hid it *in me*. 'Remember this room,' he said. 'When you have forgotten everything else.' He wiped me clean, Mirabel. Burned regiment, lost name, nobody from nowhere — none of it was accident or war-wound. He made me a vault. He sealed the name inside his own son and walked away, and the stranger — Sera's brother — spent his life looking for the vault, because the king's cartographer knew what the journal knows: that the crown can't be unbound by any name but the anchor's, freely given."

"So the name isn't lost," Mirabel whispered. "It's *you*."

"It's me." He laughed, and there was no humor in it, only the hollow ring of a lock turning. "I'm not a man who forgot his past. I'm a key that learned to walk."

Silence. The ink on her maps shifted in the candlelight, tiny roads rearranging themselves like sleeping animals.

Then Mirabel did something he did not expect. She came around the desk and knelt on the cold stone in front of him, took his scarred hands in her ink-stained ones, and held them hard enough to hurt.

"Look at me."

He looked.

"You are not a key," she said. "You are not a vault, or a vessel, or any other word men use to make a person into a thing. You are the man who pulled me out of a burning guildhouse. Who held a ford against twenty riders. Who carries water for Sera every morning without being asked. Whatever name is locked inside you, it doesn't own you. Do you hear me? Names don't own people. People *give* names. That's the whole point of the rite — *freely given*. Which means it will be your choice. Yours alone. No crown, no king, no dead father's vow gets to make it for you."

"And if the choice is to die?" His voice cracked on it, finally, the fear he had been carrying since the hills. "If speaking it unmakes me? If I open the door and there's nothing behind it but the lock breaking?"

Her grip tightened. For a moment he saw her struggle — he watched her want to promise, watched the promise die in her throat because she was Mirabel Marchetti and she would not lie to him, not even kindly, not even now. The map had taught them both what lies cost.

"Then we'll find another way," she said. "Or if there is no other way — then whatever it costs, you won't pay it alone, and you won't pay it unremembered. I will write down every day of you, Wolf. Every road, every fight, every stupid joke about my ledgers. If the world takes your name, it will have to take mine to get it, and I don't intend to let go."

Something in his chest, something that had been clenched since the hills since the song since the door, loosened by a single degree. Not peace. Nothing so kind as peace. But the terror had a shape now, and a shape could be fought.

"There's one more thing," he said. "The door itself. The rite chamber — my father performed the binding somewhere real. Black oak, iron bands, under a hill. If the crown's threads run back to the citadel, the original anchor may still lie there. My father's bones, maybe. The nameplate, if he was clever enough to hide one." He met her eyes. "We may not have to guess. We may be able to *read* it."

Mirabel was already reaching for fresh paper, already pulling the cork from her ink bottle with her teeth, and God help him, he loved her — loved her with a love that felt exactly like the door felt: old, iron-banded, and built to hold something terrible.

Outside, far below in the valley, a horn sounded from the court's watchtower. One long note, then two short.

Riders coming. Or worse — riders *summoned*.

Mirabel's quill paused over the page. In the candlelight, on the map spread between them, fresh ink was bleeding outward from the usurper's citadel like a bruise — thin red lines, marching south.

Toward Aldenmere.

"Wolf," she said softly, "the map is drawing something. And I don't think it's good news."
