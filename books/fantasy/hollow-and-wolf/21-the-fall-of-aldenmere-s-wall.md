## 21. The Fall of Aldenmere's Wall

We rode through the night, and the night rode with us.

I had thought the Hollowlands strange when I first crossed them — roads that wandered like drunkards, villages that bloomed overnight where meadows had been. But the land between the hills and Aldenmere was stranger still. It was *fixed*. Every stone in every wall sat where it had been set a hundred years ago. Every milestone stood at its measured distance. The road ran straight as a ruled line, and nothing along it moved except us.

Mirabel felt it before I did. She kept reaching for her map, unrolling it across her saddle-bow whenever we paused to breathe the horses, and each time her face grew grayer.

"It won't draw," she said on the third stop. "The whole country. It's like — " She pressed her palm flat against the parchment as if she could warm it into life. "Like trying to write on iron. Everything here is nailed down."

"Whose doing?"

"The crown's." She said it quietly, but I heard the fury underneath. "This is what he does. What it does. It takes a living land and pins it like a moth to a board. No drift. No choice. No *change*." She rolled the map away with hands that weren't quite steady. "Wolf, if Aldenmere falls, this is what the free cities become. Streets you can't redraw. Doors that only open one way."

I didn't answer. There was nothing to say that the horizon wasn't already saying better. Ahead of us, past the last black line of hills, the sky over Aldenmere glowed the dull orange of a forge left burning too long — not yet fire, but fire's promise. The usurper's army marched under that glow, and the red stain on Mirabel's map had shown us its shape: three columns, converging on the city from north and east and west, timed to arrive together like fingers closing.

Sera rode between us. Her brother's letter was folded against her heart, and her face was the face of a woman who had already buried everyone she loved and was deciding whether to start again. She hadn't spoken since the ford. When she finally did, her voice came out flat and clear as a blade laid on stone.

"How many?"

"Twenty thousand," I said. "Maybe more. The map showed supply trains. You don't bring supply trains for a raid."

"And Aldenmere's wall?"

"Old. Good stone, badly maintained. The council spent the defense levy on envoys' banquets." I had seen the wall once, years ago, in another life whose edges I still couldn't find. "There's a weak point where the river gate meets the water. If I were planning this assault, that's where I'd hit."

"You *were* planning assaults," Sera said. Not a question. An accusation, or maybe just an inventory.

"I don't remember," I said, which was true, and not enough, and we both knew it.

Mirabel reached over and touched my wrist. Just that. Two fingers, brief, gone again. It steadied me more than any memory could have.

"We ride faster," she said.

* * *

We came down off the hills an hour past midnight, and Aldenmere was already dying.

The city sat in its shallow bowl of farmland like a coin at the bottom of a well, and around it the dark fields were alive with torchlight — thousands of points of flame moving in the disciplined patterns I knew too well, siege lines unspooling, ladders and rams flowing toward the walls like blood toward a wound. The grey cloaks made the army look like fog given purpose. Above the eastern gate, something was already burning.

"They're early," I said. "They were supposed to close the fingers together. Someone panicked, or someone got greedy." Greed, I thought. Armies that move early are armies led by men who want the prize before their rivals can share it. Somewhere in my missing past there were lessons like that, worn smooth by use, and they surfaced now like drowned things rising.

"The river gate," Mirabel said.

"The river gate."

We took the west road, the one the fleeing farmers had emptied, and passed them streaming the other way — carts and children and old men leading goats, faces lit orange by the burning east. None of them stopped. None of them asked who we were. Fear had burned everything out of them except motion.

Aldenmere's western gate stood open. Of course it did. The council of cowards had spent the day negotiating with envoys, and even now, with the enemy at the walls, half of them believed a treaty might still be signed if only everyone stayed polite. A single bored watchman looked at our weapons and our horses and decided we were someone else's problem.

Inside, the city was a held breath. Lamps burned behind shutters. Dogs barked and were silenced. In the great square, a handful of council guards stood around a brazier arguing with a merchant about whether the market would open in the morning.

"The market," Sera said softly, "will open in hell."

We found the river gate by following the sound of water. It was worse than I remembered — a squat arch where the Alden flowed beneath the wall, barred by a portcullis so furred with rust it looked grown rather than forged. And standing at it, arguing in whispers, were six soldiers of the city watch and a man in the silk-trimmed cloak of the council.

"The envoy," said the oldest watchman, "is currently watching the east gate burn."

"That is a matter for the full council — "

I walked up and put my hand on the winch.

The councilman turned, saw my sword, saw my face, and made the mistake of looking at me the way powerful men look at people they believe are furniture. "You'll be arrested for this."

"Probably," I said, and began to turn the winch.

The rust screamed. The portcullis shuddered, ground upward a foot, stuck. Behind me I heard Mirabel's voice, low and fast: "Wolf. The mechanism's seized on the left chain. Cut the pin and the counterweight will do the rest." I cut the pin. The gate slammed up hard enough to crack stone, and the councilman fled, shouting about treason, and none of us laughed, because there was no time and because he was right. It probably was treason. Treason against a council that had sold its own city's sleep for the hope of a comfortable surrender.

"Get them out," I told the watchmen. "Every soul in the low quarter, down the river road, now. Don't pack. Don't argue. Go door to door and lie if you have to — tell them the treaty failed, tell them the wall's breached, tell them anything, but get them moving."

The oldest watchman looked at me. "Who are you?"

Nobody. That was the true answer, and useless. "Someone who's held a gate before," I said, and found, saying it, that it was the truest thing I knew about myself.

* * *

What follows, I will set down as plainly as I can, because Mirabel will ask me to, someday, when she writes all this into her ledgers. Plainly, because the other way of telling it — the way it felt — is not fit for ink.

They came at the river gate two hours before dawn.

Not many, at first. A probing party, forty or fifty grey cloaks wading the shallows, quiet as men who have done this work before. We let them reach the arch. Then we dropped oil and fire, and the river burned, and the screaming started, and after that the night belonged entirely to noise.

They came again with a ram. They came with hooks and ropes. They came a third time in such numbers that the water below the gate was less river than raft of bodies, and still they came, because whatever drove them — fear, compulsion, the crown's threads running down into their skulls the way I'd later see them run into the usurper's — they could not stop. I understood that, even then. Even swinging a sword into faces lit by burning water, some part of me pitied them. They were moths too. Pinned and driven.

I fought at the arch itself, because the arch was narrow and narrow places are where few defeat many. Six watchmen held the left. Sera held the right with a boat-hook she'd taken from a fisherman's shed and used with a fury that made me glad she was on my side. And Mirabel —

Mirabel stood behind us with a lantern and her map, and she *commanded*.

"Left chain's fraying — Wolf, give it three breaths then fall back to the second arch. They're massing by the tannery row — they'll come over the wall there within the hour, it doesn't matter how long we hold here. Watchman! The barrels by the chandlery — roll them to the stair, block it. *Now.*"

She read the battle the way she read terrain — its intentions, its drift, its next truth — and she shouted it to us in that precise clerk's voice that never rose and never broke, and we obeyed her, because she was never wrong. Once. Not once all night.

Dawn was maybe an hour off when the east wall went.

We heard it before we understood it — a deep groan, then a crack like the world's spine breaking, then a roar of fire that turned the underside of the clouds red. The eastern gate, assaulted properly, with towers and the ram they'd saved for the killing blow, while we held six men and a boat-hook at the river. The fingers had closed after all. We had been the distraction they allowed us to be.

"Fall back," I said. "All of you. Get to the river road, get the people out — "

"The people are *out*," Sera said. "Most of them. Because of you." She gripped my arm, hard, once, and let go. "Because of both of you."

So we retreated, and retreat through a falling city is its own particular hell. Smoke moves faster than people. Fire jumps streets like a rumor. We went with the last of the refugees, Mirabel carrying the map wrapped in oilcloth against her chest like a child, and I looked back exactly once, from the far bank of the Alden, at the city I had failed to save.

It was fully light by then, or something like light. The wall was broken along its whole eastern curve, and through the breach the grey cloaks poured in a silent, orderly flood, and above them, on the shattered gatehouse, someone raised a banner: a crown worked in thread-of-gold on grey, and even at that distance I swear the crown seemed to *move*, the way ink moves, the way living things move.

Behind me, Mirabel made a sound I had never heard from her — small, and furious, and final.

"Diplomacy is dead," she said. "The councils, the treaties, the envoys and their banquets. All of it. He was never going to let the free cities stay free, and now they know it, and now *we* know it." She turned to me, and her eyes were red-rimmed and absolutely steady. "There's only one thing left. The rite. The name. *You.*"

"Not yet."

"Soon." She said it gently, and it was worse than if she'd shouted. "Soon, Wolf."

Sera stood beside us, watching the smoke rise off her brother's city, off the place where his sister had made her life, and when she spoke her voice was ash. "He gave you the map so this could end. Don't waste what it cost him."

"I won't," I said.

And I meant it. God help me, standing in the trampled mud of the river road with a burning city at my back and twenty thousand enemies ahead of us and a name buried somewhere in my skull like a knife in a floorboard — I meant it.

But I also knew, in the way you know weather coming, that meaning it and surviving it were going to be very different things.

The road to the mountains lay open before us. We took it, the three of us, and did not look back again.
