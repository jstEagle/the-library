## 23. The Chancellor's Bargain

The message came at dusk, folded inside a dead courier's glove.

Wolf found it where Sera said it would be — tucked beneath the loose stone in the shrine wall of the low quarter's chapel, the one dedicated to the Wanderer, patron of roads and those who lose them. A fitting altar for a traitor's correspondence. He almost left it there. Then he thought of Mirabel's voice that morning, steady as a plumb line: *We need a way into the citadel, and we don't have one.* So he took the glove, shook the paper free, and read by the light of the chapel's one guttering candle.

*To the man called Wolf. You want what I can open. I want what you can carry. Come alone to the tannery on Tanner's Row when the third bell rings. Bring no map. Bring no woman. — V.*

No signature beyond the initial. No seal. Vane had never needed one; his arrogance was authentication enough.

Wolf read it three times, and each time the same cold settled deeper into his chest, the cold he had learned to trust above hope or fear. It was the cold of a soldier reading ground before a battle — the shape of an ambush, the weight of a trap, the geometry of a lie.

Lord Vane was alive. Lord Vane wanted to talk.

He should have burned the letter. Instead he folded it back into the glove and went to find Mirabel.

* * *

She was working in the rented room above the dyer's shop, spread across the floor like a tide. Maps everywhere — hers, the stranger's, copies she'd made of Aldenmere's walls, sketches of the citadel drawn from rumor and secondhand memory, all of them wrong in ways that made her jaw tighten every time she looked at them. Candle stubs ringed her work like a fortification. Ink stained her fingers to the second knuckle.

She read the letter twice. Then she did something Wolf had not expected, something that told him more about the woman than any confession could have.

She didn't say no.

"We need a way in," she said, laying the letter flat beside a sketch of the usurper's fortress — a black thumbprint of stone on the eastern edge of the fixed country, where nothing drifted and nothing breathed. "And here is a man who has one."

"He sold out the exiled court. He'd have sold us with it if we hadn't run."

"Yes." She looked up at him, and her eyes were calm in a way that frightened him more than any fury would have. "So we don't give him anything true. We let him believe he's buying what he wants, and while he talks, I take everything else."

"You're proposing we sit across a table from a snake and pretend to bargain."

"I'm proposing," Mirabel said, "that we do what the guild does to every client who walks through its doors. We smile. We quote a price. And we keep the real ledger in our heads." She tapped the letter once. "He thinks he's the cleverest man in any room. That's a weakness, Wolf. Clever men show you their cleverness if you ask the right questions."

"And if he asks questions we can't afford to answer?"

"Then we lie." She said it simply, without flinching, and Wolf felt something shift in his chest — a small grief, and a strange pride tangled up with it. He had spent years being nobody precisely so he would never have to lie to anyone worth trusting. Now the woman he loved was learning the trade he'd abandoned, and learning it faster than he ever had.

"He'll search us," Wolf said. "He'll have the room watched before we arrive. He'll know the map isn't with us only if it isn't."

"It won't be." Mirabel began gathering her papers, sorting with quick, precise hands. "I'll memorize what I need tonight. Tomorrow I go in carrying nothing but my eyes."

"And me?"

"You carry your sword and your silence. You're the soldier negotiating terms. I'm—" she paused, and a dry light came into her face that he had learned to love and fear in equal measure, "—I'm your clerk. Every commander has one. No one looks twice at a clerk."

"No one should look at you at all," Wolf said. "Vane saw your face at court."

"Vane saw a frightened woman clutching a map tube while his oaths curdled around him." She rolled a blank sheet tight and slid it into her sleeve — plain paper, unremarkable, the kind of thing a clerk might legitimately carry. "People remember fear, not faces. Fear all looks the same."

It was a good plan. It was also, Wolf knew, the kind of plan that worked right up until the moment it didn't, and then it killed everyone involved. He said as much. Mirabel smiled — the small, crooked smile she saved for arguments she'd already won.

"So does standing outside a locked citadel waiting for the free cities to grow spines," she said. "At least this way we're doing something."

* * *

Tanner's Row smelled of lime and rot and old blood, which was appropriate, because everything about the meeting stank of the same. The tannery had been abandoned since the grey riders first came through Aldenmere's low quarter; its pits stood open to the sky, half-filled with rainwater gone the color of bruises, and its long shed offered a roof, four walls, and exactly two exits — which meant, Wolf noted grimly, that Vane controlled both.

They arrived separately. Wolf walked in through the front, alone, as instructed, letting the guards at the door — two of them, grey-cloaked, trying too hard to look like hired muscle rather than usurper's soldiers — pat him down with rough hands. They took his knife but left the sword, which told him Vane wanted this to look like a parley even if it wasn't one. Mirabel came in through the drainage gate twenty minutes later, dressed in a clerk's drab wool, hair pinned severely, a leather folio under her arm containing nothing but blank pages and a stub of charcoal. The guards barely glanced at her. *No one looks twice at a clerk,* she had said, and she had been right, and Wolf hated how right she'd been.

Vane waited in the tannery's counting room, at a table scarred by decades of ledger knives. He had aged badly since the court in the mountains — or perhaps he had simply stopped pretending otherwise. His fine chancellor's robes were gone, replaced by a merchant's dark coat cut close enough to pass unnoticed. His hands, always his most eloquent feature, lay flat on the table like two pale animals at rest.

"Well," Vane said, and his voice still carried that accent Wolf had marked in the mountain court — the soft vowels of the capital, the usurper's own country, worn thin now by years of exile and scheming. "You came. I confess I gave you odds of three to two against."

"Your odds are wrong," Wolf said. "They usually are."

"Ah." Vane smiled, and it was a genuine smile, which made it worse. "Still the blunt instrument. Tell me — do you actually remember being a soldier, or is that just how the body moves?"

The question landed closer than Vane could have known. Wolf kept his face still, the way he'd learned to keep it through interrogations whose memories he no longer owned. "You sent for me to talk business. Talk it."

"Directly. Good. I've had enough of courts." Vane leaned forward, lacing his fingers. "Here is my offer. The citadel of the usurper — Thornmark, they call it now, though it had a better name before — is warded. Not walled. Warded. Its gates read intent. Its corridors rearrange for strangers. An army could besiege it until the sun burns out and never breach the inner keep. But I served there. I helped design the wardings, in another life, under another master. I can walk you through them like a man walking through his own house."

"In exchange for?"

"The crown." Vane said it plainly, and Wolf gave him credit for that much honesty. "Not the crown itself — I have no illusions about wearing it. The *power* of it. When the binding breaks — and it will break, whether by your hand or someone else's — the energies released will be immense. Anchored power doesn't vanish. It disperses, or it's caught. I want it caught. I want enough of it to make myself safe from every throne and guild and god that will come sniffing afterward. Give me that, and I'll give you the king's freedom on a plate."

"And why," Wolf said slowly, "should I believe a word of this?"

"You shouldn't." Vane's smile widened fractionally. "That's what makes this conversation honest. You need me. I need you. Neither of us trusts the other further than we can spit. That's the strongest foundation a bargain can have — better than trust, which rots. Distrust keeps both parties sharp." He spread his hands. "So. Terms."

Behind Vane, seated at a side table with a clerk's diligence, Mirabel bent over her folio and asked, in a colorless voice, "My lord mentioned the wardings were designed under a previous administration. For the record — under whose authority? Dates, if you have them."

And Wolf nearly laughed aloud, because it was perfect. Nothing disarms a suspicious man like bureaucracy. Vane glanced at her with mild irritation, then — as she'd predicted — dismissed her entirely, a woman taking minutes, beneath notice. He began to talk dates. He talked names. He talked gates and corridors and the sequence of keys, the words that opened the warded doors, the rhythm of the guard rotations, the location of the rite chamber beneath the foundations — and every word of it went down onto Mirabel's blank pages in shorthand only she could read, and every word of it was also going somewhere else, somewhere Vane couldn't see.

Because Mirabel wasn't just writing. Wolf caught it only glancingly, in the moments when Vane's attention swung back to him and the haggling: the faint shimmer along the edge of her sleeve, the way her charcoal paused at certain phrases and her lips moved silently, testing them. She was mapping. Not the citadel on paper — though that too, later, from memory, in their room, in ink that would tell her if anything she'd written was false. She was mapping *Vane*. Where his eyes went. Which details he volunteered freely and which he guarded. What he lied about.

She caught three lies in the first hour. Wolf knew because of the tiny stillness that passed over her each time, the way a compass needle quivers over buried iron. The first lie was small — Vane claimed he'd left the citadel voluntarily, before the usurper's rise, and the quiver came. The second was larger: the number of guards on the inner stair. The third—

The third was the offer itself.

Wolf saw it land in her face, a flicker behind her eyes like lightning seen through cloud, and he understood, and kept talking, kept haggling, demanded a larger share of the crown's residue for himself — playing greedy, playing the mercenary Vane expected — while inside his chest a cold knowledge spread like frost across a window.

Vane was lying about wanting to open the citadel for them. Or lying about what he'd do once they were inside. Or both. Possibly all of it was lies, wrapped around one true thing, and the true thing was the trap.

Fine. Two could play at that game. Three, counting the woman with the charcoal.

* * *

They walked out through the drainage gate an hour past midnight, separately again, and met three streets away in the doorway of a shuttered chandlery, where the smell of old tallow covered the smell of the tannery if you breathed shallow.

"Well?" Wolf said.

Mirabel was already unwrapping the folio, spreading the pages across a barrel in the thin light of a lamp she'd shielded with her coat. Her shorthand crawled across them in columns. "Three lies," she said. "Maybe four. The guard count on the inner stair is wrong — he inflated it, which means either he wants us to bring more force than we have, or he wants us to think the approach harder than it is so we'll rely on him completely. The story about leaving the citadel voluntarily is false. And the offer—" she stopped, pressed her lips together, started again. "The offer is poisoned. Somewhere in it. I can't isolate which clause yet. I'll need to write it out clean and watch the ink."

"Which means the whole meeting was theater."

"The whole meeting was *fishing*." She looked up at him, lamplight catching the tired angles of her face. "He wasn't selling us a way in, Wolf. He was finding out what we know. Every question I asked, he answered a little too eagerly — and then he asked his own. Did you notice? Four times he steered back to the same subject."

Wolf had noticed. He'd been too busy performing greediness to name it. "The rite chamber."

"The rite chamber. Where the binding was done. Where the anchor is. He asked who we thought the anchor was. He asked whether we'd identified the counter-name. He asked — and this is the one that matters — *whether we had found the bones.*"

A cold wind blew through Wolf that had nothing to do with the night. Bones. His father's bones, nameless under the citadel, hidden these thirty years. Vane knew they mattered. Vane wanted to know if *they* knew.

"He suspects," Wolf said. "About me."

"He suspects something. He may not know what." Mirabel gathered the pages quickly, decisively, folding them away. "But here's what he gave us anyway, whether he meant to or not: the gate sequence, the corridor wards, the rotation of the watch, and the location of the rite chamber relative to the keep — northeast foundation, below the old chapel level. That's a map, Wolf. A partial one, threaded with lies, but maps are my trade. I can pull the truth out of a document full of poison. I've been doing it since I was nineteen."

"You're going to redraw it tonight."

"I'm going to redraw it tonight, and tomorrow I'm going to cross-check it against Sera's brother's journal, and the day after that against whatever the exiled court's loyalists remember, and when the versions disagree, the disagreements will show me exactly where Vane buried his traps." She rolled the folio closed and looked at him, and there was something fierce and quiet in her expression that steadied him the way no amount of courage could. "We can beat him at this table. We just have to stop playing honest."

"I stopped playing honest years ago," Wolf said. "I just forgot how."

"Then remember." She touched his arm, brief, warm, gone. "Because in a few days we're going to walk into that citadel pretending to trust the man who betrayed everyone who ever trusted him. And we cannot afford one true thing showing on our faces when we do."

* * *

They worked until dawn.

In the rented room above the dyer's shop, with the shutters barred and the lamp turned low, Mirabel rebuilt Vane's poisoned map the way a surgeon rebuilds a shattered limb — patiently, from fragments, testing each piece for truth. She wrote the gate sequence in fresh ink and watched it dry clean. She wrote the guard numbers and watched them blur, corrected them, watched them set firm. She drew the corridors as Vane described them and found two that smeared — phantom passages, designed to lead intruders into killing grounds — and struck them through with a single line, and drew instead the routes the journal implied, and those held true.

Wolf sat across from her and watched her work, and understood, finally, what the dying stranger had seen in her. Not the precision, though the precision was there, honed to an edge that would have shamed any master of the guild. It was the way she treated truth as a physical substance — something with weight and grain and texture, something that could be smelted from ore, tested for flaws, forged into a weapon. Everyone else in this war fought with steel and gold and oaths. Mirabel fought with accuracy.

"Toward morning," she said quietly, not looking up, "he told one more lie. I almost missed it. He said the rite chamber's entrance was sealed since the binding — that no one had opened it in thirty years." Her charcoal stopped. "The ink ran sideways. Someone opens that chamber regularly, Wolf. Someone goes down there. The crown feeds, or the usurper prays, or Vane visits something he keeps in the dark. Whatever it is, it's alive down there, and it's expecting company."

Wolf looked at the window, where the sky was turning the grey of old ash, and felt the chessboard tilt beneath them. They had come to the table to learn the citadel. They had learned it. But they had also taught Vane — taught him that they were desperate, that they would deal, that they would come. And somewhere between the lines of a traitor's careful performance, a fourth lie had been planted like a seed: *come to my citadel. Walk through my gates. Trust my map.*

"He knows we're coming," Wolf said. "However we come. However we disguise it. He built us a door and he's standing behind it."

"Probably." Mirabel set down her charcoal at last and flexed her ink-stained fingers, one by one, like a musician after a long performance. "But he built the door from plans he stole — plans that are thirty years old, plans that describe a citadel that has been remade around a living crown. And I have something he doesn't." She laid her hand flat on the finished map, on the clean routes and the struck-through lies, on the small neat annotation in the corner in her own hand: *NE foundation, below chapel — entrance used recently — approach unverified.*

"I have ink that knows when a man is lying," she said. "And I intend to bring it with us."

The lamp guttered. Outside, the first bell of morning rang across the rooftops of Aldenmere, and somewhere across the city, in a dark room of his own, Wolf did not doubt that Lord Vane was writing his own report — setting down what he'd learned, planning what he'd spring — and that somewhere in that report was a sentence that would make Mirabel's ink run red.

Three days, by Wolf's reckoning, before the game moved to the board that mattered.

He intended to spend all three of them learning to lie better than a chancellor.
