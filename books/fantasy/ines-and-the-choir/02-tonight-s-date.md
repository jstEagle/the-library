## 2. Tonight's Date

The prophecy ledger lived on the third shelf of the restricted stacks, between a treatise on rivers that flowed uphill and a box of maps that had to be kept in the dark because they drew themselves when observed.

Ines knew this the way she knew the location of her own heartbeat — not as information but as architecture. Fourteen years in the Guild of Unmapped Realms and she could walk the archive blindfolded, could name every volume by the sound its cover made against the shelf: the wet slap of the tide-almanacs, the dry whisper of the star-charts, the heavy, reluctant thud of the ledgers no one was permitted to read and everyone was required to preserve.

She had come down for something else entirely. That was the thing she would remember afterward — that it began with an accident so small it barely qualified as one.

The eighth bell had finished striking. The archive was empty except for her and the lamps and the particular silence of old paper, which is not an absence of sound but a presence of held breath. She had been sent to retrieve the Corvane toll-records for Master Ellery's audit, and she had them, wrapped in oilcloth under her arm, and she was turning back toward the stairs when she saw that the third shelf had shifted.

Not much. A finger's width. The prophecy ledger sat proud of its neighbors by exactly enough to catch the lamplight along its spine, and the spine read, in faded gilt:

*ANNALS OF THE UNWRITTEN YEAR.*

There was no unwritten year. Every year got written; that was the entire point of the guild. But the ledger existed, and it had been catalogued — she knew the entry by heart, because she had catalogued it herself at nineteen, during her induction, copying entries from the older registers into her own hand: *Annals of the Unwritten Year. Provenance unknown. Contents sealed by order of the Founding Circle. Do not open. Do not copy. Do not index.*

Do not index. Which meant that for fourteen years, the ledger had existed outside the great web of cross-references Ines maintained like a spider at the center of the guild's memory. It was the one book in the building that did not know it was known.

And it was sticking out of the shelf.

Ines stood very still. The oilcloth crackled faintly under her arm. Somewhere above her, water moved through pipes in the walls — the guildhall was old enough to have a pulse.

There were explanations. There were always explanations; that was half her job, supplying the mundane skeleton beneath impossible flesh. Shelf settled. Damp swelled the wood. A junior archivist had pulled it and failed to seat it properly, though no junior archivist had clearance to touch it, which meant it would have to be Halvard or one of the council, and none of them came down here after bell.

The other explanation was that the ledger wanted to be found.

She set the toll-records on the reading table first. Ritual mattered; ritual was what kept hands steady. Then she took down the Annals of the Unwritten Year, and it was heavier than she remembered, or she was lighter than she'd been at nineteen, and the clasp was cold enough to bite.

She should have carried it up to Halvard. She knew that too. The correct procedure was written into her bones alongside the shelf-codes: anomalies went upward, always upward, and the guild survived on the principle that no single person ever held a whole secret — only fragments, distributed like doses of poison too small to kill.

But the ledger was already open in her hands before she had decided anything. The clasp had given with a click so soft it might have been her own knuckle, and the first page lay bare under the lamp, and there was no un-seeing it.

* * *

The handwriting was beautiful. That was her first thought, absurdly — the thought of a woman who spent her days judging script. A fine, slanted hand with flourishes on the ascenders, ink gone brown with age, the kind of penmanship they didn't teach anymore because it took years and the guild no longer kept anyone long enough to waste years on beauty.

The opening page was dated one hundred and six years ago. It read:

*What follows was set down in obedience, though obedience is the wrong word, for I was not commanded. I heard, and having heard, I must record. The Choir sings tomorrow. What it sings will be true whether or not anyone writes it down. I write it down so that someone, someday, will know we were warned — and will be able to say, honestly, that we chose anyway.*

Then the hand changed. Not gradually — abruptly, mid-page, as if the writer had set down the pen and another had taken it up, or as if the same writer had stopped being entirely herself. The new hand was looser, wilder, letters leaning like trees in wind. And the dates began.

Ines turned pages. The lamp hissed. Her breath made small ghosts in the cold air of the stacks, and she noticed distantly that she had stopped feeling her feet.

It was a ledger of predictions — no, not predictions; that word implied guesswork. These were statements. Entries in the flat, procedural format the guild used for everything, *observed* and *recorded* and *sealed*, except that the observations were of things that had not happened yet. A fire in the western stacks, dated forty years past — and Ines felt the hair rise on her arms, because there *had* been a fire in the western stacks forty years ago; it was in the disaster registers; three volumes of tide-almanacs had burned. An eclipse out of season, dated and described to the minute. A flood in the Salt Marches. A queen drowning in a dry room.

Page after page. A century of tomorrow, recorded yesterday.

And then she reached the last written page, near the end of the book, where the remaining leaves were blank and waiting, and the date at the top of the final entry was rendered in the guild's own notation — the day-month-year cipher she wrote two hundred times a day into catalogues and receipts and the margins of maps.

Tonight's date.

Ines did not move for a long moment. The lamp flame leaned and righted itself. Down the corridor, the dark went on being dark.

She read the entry once at speed, the way you snatch your hand from a stove, and then forced herself to read it again slowly, line by line, because she was the keeper of the ledgers and her whole life was built on the discipline of looking directly at things.

*On this date,* the wild hand had written, *the Voice comes to the door. She is already among us; she has been among us for years, counting, keeping, believing herself invisible. Her birth-name is Ulyana Pemberton. The guild will call her Ines, and the name will fit her like a coat cut for someone else, and she will wear it so long she forgets the shoulders underneath. But the Choir knows its own. When the usurper crosses the salt, the Voice will be asked to choose, and the choosing will cost more than any ledger can hold, for the song takes payment in the singer.*

Below that, smaller, almost an afterthought, pressed hard enough into the paper to emboss the page behind it:

*The Choir's Voice. Let no one bind her. We were bound, and look what we became.*

* * *

She did not remember climbing the stairs. She had some impression of the oilcloth still under her arm, of the toll-records delivered to Master Ellery's desk with a note about folio discrepancies that she composed in perfect professional sentences while her mind stood elsewhere, in a cold corridor a century deep, staring at her own name in a dead woman's handwriting.

*Ulyana.* No one living knew that name. She had buried it at fifteen, when the guild took her in — orphans were given guild-names, fresh and unencumbered, and hers had been drawn from a list of names of the unremarkable, chosen precisely so that no one would ever look twice. Ines. A name like a closed door. For fourteen years she had been Ines so thoroughly that *Ulyana* had become a fact about a stranger, a girl who had died quietly in some border town whose name Ines had also, deliberately, forgotten.

Except she hadn't forgotten it. That was the horror unfolding in her chest now, slow and cold, like ice across a pond: she had never actually let it go. It had sat inside her all these years the way the ledger had sat on its shelf — unindexed, undiscussed, present. And a hundred years ago, someone had written it down. Written *her* down. Beside tonight.

Halvard's study was on the second floor, at the corner where the guildhall met the old wall, and his lamp was burning, because Halvard's lamp was always burning; the man seemed to regard sleep as a clerical error he intended to appeal. Ines knocked, and waited through the pause that meant he was hiding whatever was on his desk, and entered.

He looked up over his spectacles. He was seventy if he was a day, thin as a bookmark, with ink permanently stained into the creases of his fingers like a second set of fingerprints. Fourteen years ago he had found her sleeping in the guild's doorway and had not, as the doctrine prescribed, directed her to the poorhouse. He had said, *Can you keep a record?* and when she said yes, he had said, *Then you can keep everything else too,* and she had loved him ever since, cleanly and completely, the way you love the person who built the room you live in.

"Ellery's audit," she said. "I've delivered the toll-records." Her voice came out level. She was vaguely proud of it, the way you're proud of a bridge holding in a storm.

"Mm." He returned to his papers.

"Halvard."

That got his attention; she almost never used his name without the honorific. He lowered the spectacles. "Ines?"

"The third shelf," she said. "The restricted stacks. The Annals of the Unwritten Year was standing proud of the shelf. I opened it."

Something crossed his face. She had catalogued Halvard's expressions for fourteen years the way she catalogued everything, and what crossed his face was not surprise. It was recognition — brief, weary, and immediately filed away, the way a man files away a bill he has been expecting.

"You shouldn't have done that."

"No," she agreed. "I shouldn't have. But I did, and I read the last entry, and it bears tonight's date, and my name. My *birth*-name, Halvard. A name no one in this building has ever heard me speak. Written a hundred and six years ago." She kept her hands folded. She kept her voice in the register she used for reporting discrepancies. Underneath both, something was screaming in a small, disciplined way. "So before you tell me what I saw, I'd like you to understand that I am not asking whether it's real. I'm asking what it means."

For a moment he only looked at her, and the lamp threw his shadow long across the floorboards, and Ines thought — absurdly, treacherously — that he looked old. Not elderly, which he had always been, but *old*, the way a house is old, the way a debt is old.

Then he sighed, took off the spectacles, and rubbed the bridge of his nose, and when he spoke it was in the tone he used for teaching, gentle and unhurried, and she hated how much comfort she took from it even as it failed her.

"Clerical echo," he said.

"...I beg your pardon?"

"It happens." He gestured at the shelves around them, the hundreds of volumes, the weight of a century's record-keeping. "A scribe works long enough, the hand develops habits. Dates repeat. Names repeat. You've seen it yourself — a catalogue copied from an older catalogue inherits its errors, and the errors get copied forward until they look like facts. We call it echo. The Annals are a strange book, Ines — provenance unknown, authorship disputed, possibly a hoax, possibly a meditation, possibly the work of a mind that had been alone in this building too long. Someone with a taste for theater wrote a list of dramatic dates and dramatic names, and the book sat waiting for a hundred years until a dramatic date arrived and a nervous young woman with an excellent memory went looking for meaning." He smiled at her, and it was a kind smile, and it did not reach anywhere near his eyes. "You found an echo. Old ink, playing tricks. It's why we seal such books — not because they're dangerous, but because they're *embarrassing*. The guild cannot afford to be seen taking its own ghosts seriously."

Ines stood very still.

It was a good answer. It was, in fact, an excellent answer — measured, plausible, delivered with the exact blend of authority and affection that Halvard had deployed to soothe frightened apprentices and skeptical buyers for longer than she'd been alive. If she had heard it secondhand, reported by someone else about someone else's crisis, she would have accepted it without a flicker.

But she had spent fourteen years learning to hear what documents said underneath what they said. She knew the difference between a man dismissing a fear and a man performing the dismissal of a fear, and Halvard's left thumb was pressing hard against the edge of his desk, and he had not once asked her what the entry actually contained. Not the date. Not the phrase. Not *the Choir's Voice*.

If a clerk brought him an anomaly, any anomaly, his first question was always the same: *What did it say, exactly?* Words mattered to Halvard. Words were the whole trade.

He hadn't asked. Because he already knew.

"I see," Ines said.

"Do you?" Something in his face flickered — hope, maybe, or its opposite.

"Yes," she said. "Clerical echo. The hand repeats itself. Ink plays tricks." She unfolded her hands and smoothed her skirt, and heard herself continue in the flattest possible tone: "Of course, that raises a question of procedure. If the Annals contain echoes, the echo is still an entry in a sealed ledger, and per the Founding Circle's own rules, any entry bearing a current date must be flagged for council review within one bell. Even spurious ones. Especially spurious ones — that's rule nine, subsection four. You taught it to me yourself. You said the guild's safety lives in its willingness to examine even its nonsense."

The lamp hissed. Halvard looked at her for a long time, and Ines watched him weigh something, and she understood with a clarity that felt like falling that whatever he was weighing, it was her.

"That rule," he said finally, "applies to active ledgers. The Annals are sealed. Sealed means sealed, Ines. Some doors exist so that we have somewhere to put things we cannot afford to carry." He picked his spectacles back up, and the lesson was over, and the warmth returned to his voice like water filling a footprint. "Go home. Sleep. Tomorrow the world will be ordinary again; it usually is. And Ines—" she paused at the door— "if you find yourself tempted to go back down there, don't. Curiosity is a fine quality in a cartographer and a fatal one in a keeper. Your value to this guild has always been that you *don't* look. Guard that."

She walked back through the corridors with the lamps guttering behind her, past the map room, past the sealed east wall, past the great clock ticking toward midnight, and her face was calm and her step was even and inside her chest the ice finished crossing the pond.

*Clerical echo.* He had known the words before she said them. He had known them the way you know a sentence you've rehearsed.

She reached her small room under the eaves, lit her candle, and sat down at her desk without removing her coat. On the desk lay the evening's catalogue work, neat as arithmetic, and beside it her personal notebook — the one she kept, in mild violation of doctrine, recording not secrets but *patterns*: discrepancies, coincidences, the small impossibilities that never rose to the level of report.

She opened it to a fresh page and wrote, in her careful keeper's hand:

*Tonight. The Annals named me before I was born. Halvard knew the entry before I described it. Conclusion: either the guild has known for a century what I am — or what I am is something the guild has needed to know about for a century. Neither possibility is survivable ignorance.*

She considered the page. Then, beneath it, she added the phrase she had not yet spoken aloud to anyone, the phrase from the dead hand, the phrase that had been sitting in her mouth all evening like a stone:

*The Choir's Voice.*

And below that, because she was who she was, because fourteen years of habit do not break in a night, she wrote the closing formula of every entry she had ever made — and for the first time heard it as a question instead of a prayer:

*Recorded and sealed.*

The candle guttered. Below her floor, far below, behind brick and plaster and a hundred years of not-being-discussed, the sealed thing in the east wall seemed, very faintly, to shift its weight — and in the morning, before the ninth bell, a buyer would arrive at the guild's front door asking after a compass that pointed to things that did not exist, and the ledgers would say there was no such compass, and Ines would watch her mentor lie, fluently, beautifully, with the same voice that had told her the century was only an echo.
