## 3. What the Guild Trades

The buyer arrived on the third day of the month of Ashes, which the ledgers recorded as a day of no consequence, and which Ines would later understand to be the day the guild's long silence began to crack.

She knew he was trouble before he crossed the threshold, because Halvard had spent the morning being pleasant to her. He never was, unless something required it. He'd brought her tea—tea, unasked, in the blue cup he kept for visitors of consequence—and stood in the doorway of the counting room watching her shelve the morning's entries with the expression of a man rehearsing a speech.

"You'll want to be at the front desk today," he said. "A client. I'd like your hand on the record."

"Which record?"

"The one we don't keep."

Ines set down her pen. In eleven years of service she had learned that the Cartographers' Guild of Unmapped Realms spoke in a grammar of negation. The one we don't keep. The door that isn't there. The ledger of small impossibilities, which was, of course, the largest ledger in the building. She had catalogued compasses that pointed at grief, ink that dried only in the presence of liars, a folding chair that unfolded into a meadow. She had recorded all of it in her small, exact hand, and she had never once asked what happened to the things after she wrote them down. That was the arrangement. That was the comfort. The guild traded in impossibilities and pretended to be clerks, and Ines pretended not to notice the pretending, and everyone slept soundly in their narrow beds above the archive.

"Who is he?" she asked.

"A surveyor," Halvard said. "Or so his letters claim. He'll claim to want a route through the Glasswaste. I'll tell him we don't sell routes." He paused, and something moved behind his eyes like a fish under ice. "I'd like you to watch how I say it."

* * *

The man who came up the guild steps at the tenth bell was broad as a door and moved like spilled water, which was to say: wrong. Surveyors walked with their weight forward, hungry for horizon. This man walked like a man who had spent years making sure no one heard him coming. His coat was good wool, undyed, the color of a page left blank. He carried a leather case that he set on the front desk with the reverence people usually reserved for relics.

"Master Halvard." His voice was a cellar voice. "Sarel Vane. I've corresponded with your office."

"You have." Halvard's courtesy was flawless. Ines, seated at her ledger to the left and one step down, as tradition demanded of the record-keeper, wrote the time, the name, the weather. Rain, thin and gray, the kind that made the city's slate roofs shine like fish scales.

"I'm mapping the northern approaches," Vane said. "For a patron. The Glasswaste crossing has defeated four expeditions. I'm told—" and here he smiled, a careful, practiced smile, the smile of a man laying down a card he'd been holding face-down all game— "that your guild once held a survey of the Glasswaste. A complete one. With a route marked."

Ines's pen did not stop moving. That was the first discipline: the hand never stops, whatever the ears hear.

Halvard folded his hands. "The guild holds no such survey."

"Your own catalog of 1187 disagrees. Folio nine. *A crossing of the Glasswaste, complete, with waymarks.*"

"An error of transcription," Halvard said, so smoothly, so gently, that Ines felt the lie pass over her like a draft of warm air. "The entry refers to a survey *requested* and never commissioned. The clerk who made it was dismissed. We keep his error as a lesson in humility."

"I'd like to see the folio."

"The folio is a record of a request that was never fulfilled. There is nothing to see."

"Then you won't mind showing me."

And here was the thing that Ines would turn over and over in her mind for the rest of her life, the small hinge on which a great door would eventually swing: Halvard did not mind. He rose, unhurried, and led Vane himself down the archive stairs, and Ines followed with the ledger of visitors because that was her place, and he took down the catalog of 1187 and opened it to folio nine and turned it toward the man as if showing him a photograph of his own mother.

The entry was there. She had read it herself a hundred times in the course of her duties, one line among thousands: *A crossing of the Glasswaste, complete, with waymarks. Held. Not for sale.* It was written in the hand of her predecessor, old Maren, dead these nine years, and Maren had never made transcription errors. Ines knew this because she had audited Maren's work in her first year, line by line, out of the particular pride of the young, and had found it flawless.

But when Vane bent over the page, Ines saw—she was certain she saw, though the light was bad and her angle worse—that the folio he looked at was not the folio she knew. The line was there, yes, but the ink was wrong. Too brown. Too fresh. And the paper around it had none of Maren's foxing, none of the little tea-colored stains of a century.

The page had been made recently. The entry had been *written* recently. Someone had forged a hundred-year-old record in order to have something to refuse.

Vane studied it for a long moment. Then he straightened and smiled his careful smile again. "An error of transcription," he agreed. "How humbling." He closed the catalog with a soft, terrible gentleness, the way one closes the eyes of the dead. "My patron will be disappointed. But she's a patient woman. She collects disappointments."

"Who is your patron?" Halvard asked.

Vane looked at him for a moment too long. "You know," he said. It was not a question. He gathered his leather case, inclined his head to Ines—*to her*, the record-keeper, the furniture, the invisible hand—said, "Mistress," in that cellar voice, and went out into the gray rain.

The door closed. Halvard stood with his hand flat on the catalog of 1187, and for a moment, just a moment, he looked old. Then he turned to Ines and his face was the face she had trusted for eleven years, mild and lined and faintly amused at the world's expense.

"You saw nothing unusual," he said.

Ines looked at her ledger, at her neat record of the visit. *Sarel Vane, surveyor. Inquiry refused. No such survey held.* Her pen had already written the lie. Her hand had known before her mind did; that was what eleven years of training did. It trained the hand to lie before the heart was consulted.

"No," she said. "Nothing unusual."

* * *

She did not sleep that night. She lay in her narrow bed above the archive listening to the rain and to the building, which had its own weather: the tick of the great clock, the settling of shelves, and—once, past the second bell, so faint she might have dreamed it—a sound from below the floors. Not a sound, exactly. A *resonance*. The way a wineglass hums when a note is sung somewhere in the house, too low and too far to hear, only to feel in the bones of the glass.

The guildhall had no cellars. She had been told this in her first week, cheerfully, by the under-clerk Peth, who told her everything cheerfully because none of it mattered. *No cellars. The building sits on bedrock. Old Maren used to say the guild sat on its own foundations, and he meant it as a joke, and no one laughed.*

In the morning she went down to the archive before the fires were lit, when the light through the high windows was the color of dishwater, and she began to audit.

Not the current ledgers—Halvard would notice, and besides, the current ledgers were hers, and she trusted her own hand the way a drowning woman trusts a plank. She went back. Maren's ledgers, and before Maren, old Corvus, and before him the spidery hand of a clerk named Ilsette whose entries spanned sixty years and whose dates, Ines noticed with a small cold feeling in her stomach, were occasionally *impossible*—entries dated on days that had been struck from the calendar, entries that referenced surveys of places that had never been surveyed because the places themselves had been, by every other record, *never there*.

She worked through the morning, and the morning became the afternoon, and she built for herself a small private architecture of doubt.

Here was what she found, laid out in her mind like a hand of cards she didn't know how to play:

The guild's public trade was real but small. Wayfinding instruments of mild impossibility. Maps of places that were difficult rather than untrue. The trade that paid for the candles and the tea and Halvard's blue cups.

The guild's private trade was real and not small. Every few years—never on a schedule, never twice from the same direction—an entry appeared in the inner ledgers marked only with a symbol Ines had always taken for a clerk's flourish: a small circle with seven strokes through it. Beside these entries, the sums were not recorded in coin. They were recorded in *concessions*. A border redrawn. A tax forgiven. A war postponed. Once, in Ilsette's hand, in the year the last great war ended: *Paid in full. The crossing is closed. Let no one ask after the singers.*

*Singers.*

Ines sat back from the page. The archive was very quiet. The rain had stopped and the silence had that ringing quality of silence after rain, and somewhere below the floor—below the floor where there were no cellars—she felt it again, that hum in the bones of the building, like a glass remembering a note.

*Let no one ask after the singers.*

She thought of the forged folio. That was the part that would not sit still in her mind, the splinter she kept turning. Halvard had not merely lied; he had prepared the lie. He had forged a page in advance, which meant he had known Vane was coming, which meant he knew who Vane's patron was—*you know*, Vane had said, and Halvard had not denied it—and he had chosen to meet the inquiry with a manufactured record rather than simply saying no. Why forge a refusal when a refusal needs no forgery?

Because the lie was not for Vane. The lie was for the record. For her. For the ledger, and for whoever came after the ledger. Halvard had not been hiding the survey from the surveyor. He had been manufacturing evidence that the survey had never existed, so that the guild's own books would say what the guild needed them to say. He was not keeping records. He was *correcting* them.

And Ines was the one who kept them. Which made her, what? An innocent? Or the instrument?

She looked down at her own ledgers, eleven years of her small exact hand, and for the first time she wondered what she had been writing *around*. What the white space on her pages was for. She thought of the compass she had catalogued on her first day, the one that pointed at grief, and how Halvard had taken it away after she'd recorded it, and how she had never asked where it went, because not asking was the arrangement, was the comfort, was the whole soft architecture of her life.

*Survival bought with silence,* said a voice in her that she did not recognize, in a tone she did not like. *Is that what this is?*

She closed the inner ledger. She did not put it back on its shelf. She wrapped it in her shawl and carried it up to her room, past the front desk, past Halvard's closed door, past Peth cheerfully sorting the morning's post, and she told herself that she was only borrowing it, that an audit required it, that this was within her office as keeper of records.

But her heart was hammering like a fist on a door, and she knew, in the place where she kept the things she would not say, that she had just committed her first theft.

That evening Halvard knocked at her door. He did not come in. He stood in the corridor with the blue cup in his hand, empty now, and looked at her with those mild eyes, and said: "The archive is cold this time of year. Don't work too late, Ines. Old paper burns fast, and some of it—" he smiled, the fish moving under the ice— "some of it is worth more than the building it sleeps in."

Then he wished her goodnight and went down the stairs, and Ines stood in her room with the stolen ledger under her blanket and the hum in the floorboards beneath her feet, faint and patient, like something singing to itself in the dark, waiting a hundred years for someone to finally ask after it.
