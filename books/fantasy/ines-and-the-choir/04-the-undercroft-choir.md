## 4. The Undercroft Choir

The discrepancy was small enough that anyone else would have called it a smudge.

Ines had been reconciling the survey ledgers against the master atlas for three nights when she found it: the river Vell, which ran south of the village of Ashmoor in every map drawn before the year 1147, and north of it in every map drawn after. Rivers did not move. Rivers eroded their banks by inches over centuries; they did not leap across a mile of farmland between one autumn and the next, taking an orchard with them and leaving a mill standing dry as a bone.

She checked the field reports from 1147. There were none. No surveyor had been sent to Ashmoor that year. No buyer had purchased a correction. The river had simply been redrawn, and no one had recorded why.

Ines sat with the two maps spread before her until the candle burned down to a puddle, and then she did what she always did with things that should not exist: she wrote them down. A new entry in her private ledger of small impossibilities, the one she kept locked in the third drawer, the one whose existence Halvard had never asked about because asking would make it real.

*Vell River, transposition, unrecorded. Third such entry this month.*

That was the part that frightened her. Not one discrepancy. Three.

* * *

The second was a road. The King's Spur, a trade route through the Greymarsh hills, appeared on the 1151 atlas where no road had ever been — not built, but *present*, as though it had always been there, its milestones weathered, its ruts worn deep. The guild's own records showed no commission for it. And the third was worse: a village called Lowmere that existed in the census rolls of 1160 and in no map at all, before or since, as if someone had taken a place with four hundred souls in it and folded it out of the world like a letter.

Ines told herself the old lie first, because it was comfortable and she had worn it smooth with use: clerical error. Copyists made mistakes. Ink bled. But copyists did not invent roads with weathered milestones, and they did not lose villages whole.

On the fourth night she laid all three discrepancies side by side and noticed what she should have noticed immediately. They shared a date. Not the same year — the same *week*. The last week of Harvestmonth, 1147 for the river, 1151 for the road, 1160 for the village — no. She looked again. She had misread. The dates were identical. All three events, separated by decades in the official chronology, were recorded in the same hand, in the same ink, on the same page of some ledger she had never seen.

The hand was Halvard's.

Or rather, it was the hand Halvard had copied his own from — a spidery, deliberate script that belonged to someone dead, someone whose name had been struck through so thoroughly the vellum had thinned beneath the strikes, someone the guild had decided, a century ago, to stop being.

* * *

"You've been in the deep stacks."

Halvard said it plainly, without accusation, the way he said everything, and Ines felt the practiced calm settle over her like the grey wool cloak she wore against the archive damp. "I've been reconciling surveys," she said. "There are discrepancies."

"There are always discrepancies." He poured tea without looking at it, an old man's economy of motion, and slid the cup toward her across the reading table. "The world is badly kept, Ines. That is why the guild exists. To keep it."

"The Vell moved a mile overnight."

"Did it." He drank his own tea. His eyes, pale as riverwater, stayed fixed on some middle distance above her head. "And what do you intend to do about it?"

"Record it."

"That's all?"

"That's the doctrine," she said. "We record. We don't intervene. We don't explain. We don't—" she stopped herself before *ask*, because the question forming behind her teeth was not a clerk's question, and she could feel him hearing it anyway.

Halvard set down his cup. For a moment — half a breath, no more — something moved behind his composure, something old and tired and afraid, and Ines realized she had never once seen her mentor afraid, in eleven years, through floods and fires and the night a buyer had put a knife to a junior cartographer's throat over a disputed boundary. Then it was gone, filed away behind the same calm he wore like livery.

"Some doors are marked with locks," he said quietly, "and some doors are marked with silence. The second kind are harder to open and much harder to close. Go home, Ines. Sleep. Reconcile your surveys. Let the river be where the newest map says it is."

He left the reading room first. He always left first. It was a courtesy of rank, or so she had believed for eleven years.

Tonight it looked like flight.

* * *

She did not go home.

The undercroft was spoken of the way sailors speak of the sea's edge — technically, dismissively, as though it were a matter of plumbing rather than geography. *Storage below. Damp. Nothing of interest.* The stair that led down to it was behind the map-press room, behind a door she had walked past perhaps ten thousand times, a door of black oak with no lock and no handle on the outside, only a shallow carved depression shaped like a closed hand.

It opened to her touch. That was the first impossible thing, and she noted it with the detached precision of long habit: the door had no mechanism she could see, and yet it knew her palm, and it swung inward on hinges that made no sound, into a dark that breathed out cold air smelling of stone and wet iron and — she could not name it at first, and then she could — of *singing*. Not sound. The memory of sound. The way a bell smells after it has stopped ringing.

She went down.

The stairs were older than the guildhall above them, older than the free cities, cut from rock that predated the kingdom's founding and bore tool-marks no modern smith could have made. They descended farther than the building was tall. Ines counted two hundred steps and then stopped counting, because counting had begun to feel like a superstition, and she was already carrying more superstitions than a keeper of records should admit to.

At the bottom: a corridor. And along the corridor, set into the wall at intervals, small alcoves, each holding a single object under glass.

She stopped at the first. Inside was a compass — the very compass she had catalogued her first week at the guild, the brass one whose needle pointed not north but *toward*, toward whatever the holder most needed to find. She had entered it in the ledger as *instrument, anomalous, non-functional* because the doctrine required her to call true things false, and she had done it without flinching, and here it sat behind glass like a saint's fingerbone, and she understood suddenly that the alcoves were not storage.

They were reliquaries.

The guild did not store impossibilities. It *worshipped* them, privately, in the dark, while telling the world above that nothing down here existed.

Seven alcoves. Seven objects. A feather that fell upward. A key that fit every door including doors that had never been built. A mirror that showed the viewer as they would be remembered. She catalogued them silently, automatically, her clerk's mind running like a waterwheel even as terror rose in her chest like floodwater, and then the corridor turned, and the singing began.

* * *

Not memory this time. Sound.

It came up through the floor of the final chamber like heat through a hearthstone, and it was the most beautiful thing Ines Pemberton had ever heard, and she hated it within four bars, and both of those facts remained true for the rest of her life.

The chamber was round, a hundred feet across, vaulted in stone so ancient it had gone glassy at the edges, and around its circumference stood seven plinths of black basalt, and bound to each plinth — not chained, not caged, *bound*, in a manner she could not parse and would spend years failing to describe — were seven figures.

They had been people once. She was sure of it, the way you are sure of a face in a fire. Shapes of light and smoke wearing the suggestion of human form: a woman with her hair still braided in a farming style two centuries out of fashion; a boy who could not have been older than Tam, whoever Tam was, though Ines did not know yet that she would come to know him; an old man whose smoke-hands were raised as if mid-blessing, frozen in the gesture of giving. They glowed faintly, each a different color — iron-grey, moss-green, dawn-gold, dusk-violet, river-blue, ember-red, and one that was simply the color of the space between stars — and from all seven of them, together, came the song.

It had no words. Or rather, it had words the way a river has stones: present, countless, mostly submerged. Her mind kept trying to resolve it into language and kept failing, and each failure left an afterimage, a phrase half-heard: *—the border shall be as the singer wills—* *—let the mountain kneel—* *—we were promised rest—*

That last one was not part of the song. She was almost certain it was not part of the song. It was underneath it, a bass note of pure entreaty, and it did not stop.

Ines stood at the chamber's threshold with her hand pressed flat against the doorframe and listened, and the awe came first, as it comes to everyone, and it was total. This was the voice that had moved the Vell. This was the choir that had sung a road into existence through the Greymarsh hills, complete with weathered milestones, because somewhere in the guild's ledgers a client had paid for a shortcut and the guild had decided that a road that seemed old would raise fewer questions than a road that was new. This was what had happened to Lowmere. Four hundred people, folded out of the world. Perhaps they lived still, somewhere in the fold. Perhaps they had never lived anywhere else. Perhaps the song did not distinguish between creating and destroying, because to a thing that rewrites geography, a village and its absence are the same sentence with one word changed.

The awe curdled slowly, like milk left out, and what it curdled into was arithmetic.

Seven spirits. Bound for a century — the dates matched now, all of them, the prophecy ledger and the discrepancies and the struck-through name, everything converging on the same terrible decade. Bound by whom? By the guild. By the hands that signed themselves with that spidery script. Bound *to what end?* To end the last war, some buried record had hinted, in a phrase she'd skimmed past a hundred times: *the cessation achieved by extraordinary means.* Extraordinary means. A chorus. A weapon that did not kill armies but unmade the ground they stood on, the borders they defended, the certainty that any place belonged to anyone.

And the guild had kept it. After the war ended. After the peace was signed. The guild — *her* guild, Halvard's guild, the quiet order of record-keepers who traded in impossibilities and pretended to be mere clerks — had kept a living weapon in the cellar and used it, occasionally, carefully, profitably, to move rivers for clients and fold away inconvenient villages, and had written none of it down, because the doctrine was not *we record everything*.

The doctrine was *we record what we choose*.

"No," Ines said aloud, to the empty chamber, to the seven bound lights, to herself. Her voice sounded obscene in that harmony, a single wrong note, and the nearest spirit — the woman with the braided hair, the moss-green one — turned toward her. Smoke has no eyes, and yet it turned, and the song bent around Ines's presence like water around a stone, and for one unbearable moment she heard a single voice separate itself from the chord, clear as a struck bell:

*You hear us.*

Then the chord closed over it again, and the woman's light dimmed back to its patient glow, and Ines understood that the spirit had not spoken to her. It had spoken *through* the binding, the way a prisoner taps code through a wall, and the message was not for her particularly. It was for anyone. It was for the next hundred years of anyone, in case no one ever came.

Someone had come.

Ines backed out of the chamber. She told herself it was to fetch help, to fetch Halvard, to fetch witnesses, to do the correct procedural thing, but her body knew better; her body was fleeing, and her body had spent eleven years learning to read the truth under the guild's calm, and the truth it read now was this: no one was coming. No one had ever come. The alcoves along the corridor were not reliquaries of wonder. They were trophies, or warnings, or both — the collected impossibilities of a century of careful, profitable, unforgivable silence.

She climbed the two hundred steps and more, and the singing followed her up through the stone, fading, fading, never quite gone, and by the time she pushed through the black oak door into the lamplit ordinary noise of the map-press room, it had settled inside her chest like a splinter she could feel but not grasp.

* * *

It was nearly dawn. The guildhall slept. Ines sat in the reading room with the prophecy ledger open before her, the page bearing tonight's date and her birth-name and the words *the Choir's Voice*, and she looked at her own name written in ink a century old, and for the first time she did not try to explain it away.

A clerical echo. Halvard's word for it. A coincidence. The doctrine's word for it. She had accepted both explanations because accepting them was comfortable, and comfort, she saw now, was the guild's true currency — not gold, not secrets. Comfort. The comfort of clients who paid to have the world adjusted around them. The comfort of cartographers who drew maps of a world they had secretly rewritten. The comfort of an old man pouring tea with steady hands while a chorus of the dead sang *we were promised rest* two hundred steps beneath his reading room.

Ines dipped her pen. She opened her private ledger of small impossibilities to a fresh page, and instead of recording an anomaly, she recorded a decision, which was the most dangerous thing she had ever entered anywhere:

*I heard them. They know I heard. I am going back.*

Below it, after a moment, she added the question that had been forming since the threshold of the chamber, the question that made her hand shake, the question that was not a clerk's question at all:

*If the Choir can rewrite the world — who taught it the song? And why does the prophecy call me its Voice?*

The candle guttered. Somewhere above her, in the direction of Halvard's rooms, a floorboard creaked — once, twice, then deliberately still, the sound of an old man standing at his window, watching the guildhall he had guarded for forty years, waiting for morning, or for her.

Ines blew out the candle and sat in the dark, listening to the splinter of song in her chest, and made herself say the next part aloud, because saying it made it real, and making it real was the only doctrine she had left:

"They're going to give me to it. Or it to me. And either way, someone is going to sing."

Outside, the sky over the free cities went grey with a dawn that no one had ordered, that no client had purchased, that arrived the way truth arrives — unbidden, unrecorded, and impossible to unmap.
