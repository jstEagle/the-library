## 5. Vessia Crosses the Salt

The refugees came up the coast road on the third day of the grey dawn, and Ines knew something had broken in the world before she knew what, because the gulls had gone quiet.

She was at her desk in the counting room when the first of them passed the window — a woman with a child on her hip and a bundle that was not a bundle but a rolled carpet, carried the way you carry the last thing you own. Then another. Then a family with an ox so thin its ribs read like ledger lines. They moved without speaking, which was the worst part. Refugees who weep are still negotiating with the world; refugees who walk in silence have already signed.

Ines set down her pen. The entry beneath it — *one brass astrolabe, provenance disputed, sold to the monastery at Vell for eleven crowns and a favor to be named* — suddenly seemed obscene, like counting spoons while a house burned next door.

"Halvard," she said, though he wasn't there. She said it anyway, because saying things made them real, and she needed this one real: *something has happened.*

By midmorning the courtyard of the guildhall had filled with the smell of woodsmoke and wet wool. The Cartographers' Guild had no doctrine of charity — invisibility did not extend to feeding the hungry; that drew eyes — but Halvard stood at the top of the steps directing the under-stewards to bring out bread anyway, his face arranged into the mild benevolence he wore for outsiders, and Ines watched him and thought, with a coldness that surprised her: *he already knows.*

He always knew first. That was what being First Cartographer meant, perhaps. Not drawing the maps but reading the ones the world drew of itself, days early, in smoke and bird-flight and the direction refugees walked.

She found him at noon in the map room, standing before the great wall chart of the western reaches, and she saw immediately why he hadn't turned around when she entered. His shoulders were doing the work of a man holding up a door against a flood.

"The Salt Marches," she said.

"Gone." He said it the way he confirmed inventory. "Vessia crossed the Salt three nights ago. Corvane's garrison surrendered before her vanguard even arrived — opened the gates, handed over the keys, asked only to be allowed to keep their uniforms. By yesterday evening her banners were flying over the Marcher towns from Brast to the Weep." He touched the wall where the salt flats were rendered in white and silver leaf, the guild's most expensive pigment, reserved for places too empty to bother mapping properly. "The white is wrong now. All of it."

"Wrong how?"

"It belongs to someone else." He finally turned. He looked older than he had a week ago, and Ines understood that the week was the cause — that whatever he'd learned about the prophecy ledger, about her name inked beside *the Choir's Voice*, had been aging him a day at a time, privately, the way he did everything. "Ines. There are people coming up the coast road. Hundreds by tomorrow. I want you in the counting room taking their names."

"Names?"

"For the ledgers. Where they came from, what they lost, what they saw." His voice dropped half a tone, into the register he used when a sentence had a second floor. "And listen. Especially listen. If anyone mentions a voice — if anyone uses that word — I want to know within the hour."

Ines looked at him for a long moment. "You think she's looking for one."

"I think," Halvard said carefully, "that conquerors collect things. Relics. Prophets. Weapons with good provenance." He smoothed the front of his coat, a gesture she had seen him perform before difficult entries a thousand times, and it struck her now as less like composure than like a man checking his pockets. "Take the names, Ines. And take care what you write down. Everything that enters our books becomes ours to protect."

*Or ours to bury,* she thought, and did not say it, and hated herself a little for the silence, and noted the hatred in the margin of her mind the way she noted everything — precisely, uselessly, in a hand no one would ever read.

* * *

The counting room smelled of tallow and fear by nightfall.

They came to her table in twos and threes, and she wrote their names in the refugee ledger — a book that had not been opened in forty years, its spine cracking like a knuckle when she lifted it down — and she listened, because Halvard had told her to listen, and because listening had become, without her quite noticing, the only prayer she knew.

A fisherman from Brast whose boats were burned in the harbor. A salt-gatherer whose pans were now, technically, imperial property, though no one had come to claim them yet; Vessia's officers, he said, didn't loot, they *inventoried*. A schoolteacher who had walked nine days with nothing but a slate. An old woman who sat across from Ines with hands folded like a woman in church and said, conversationally, "They took the singing out of us."

Ines's pen stopped. "The singing?"

"In Brast. Before the banners came. Her criers went through the streets a week ahead of the army and posted notices — no gatherings, no processions, no *singing in groups larger than four*. You could sing alone. You could sing to your baby. But four voices together, that was treason." The old woman smiled, and it was a terrible smile, patient as rust. "People laughed. What kind of conquest is afraid of a lullaby? But then the army came, and they weren't afraid of anything anymore, and we understood the notices hadn't been for us. They'd been instructions. She was teaching her soldiers what to listen for."

"What to listen for," Ines repeated. Her mouth had gone dry.

"A voice." The old woman unfolded her hands. "That's the word going up and down the coast road, girl, in case your master hasn't heard it yet. She's hunting a voice. Has been since before the Salt. Her scouts pay in silver for it — real silver, not scrip. Anyone who sings strange. Anyone whose singing makes the dogs howl or the milk sour or the weather turn. My neighbor's boy whistled a tune off the wind once and his whole family was taken for questioning. They came back. Mostly." She leaned forward. "You're a records-keeper. Write that down. Someone should write it down somewhere it can't be corrected."

Ines wrote it down. Her hand was steady, which frightened her more than shaking would have; she had been trained too well, trained until even horror arrived in neat columns.

When the old woman had gone, Ines sat with the ledger open and read back over the day's entries, and found that seven separate accounts — seven, she counted twice, then a third time because the number refused to behave — mentioned the same rumor in the same shape: *the usurper hunts a voice.* A voice that could do what armies couldn't. A voice out of the old stories, the ones about the Last War, the ones the guild's histories dismissed as embroidery. A voice that had ended that war a century ago, and might be found again, and would finish what it started.

*A century ago.* The binding. The undercroft she had not yet seen but now felt beneath her feet at all hours, a second floor to the world, humming.

Seven accounts. Either the rumor was everywhere, or it had been seeded — planted along the road by Vessia's criers the way you plant flags. Both possibilities pointed at the same place. Both pointed down.

* * *

She went to the archive wing that night, ostensibly to file the day's ledgers, actually because she could not bear her own room, where the prophecy lay hidden under a loose floorboard like a splinter working toward her heart.

The archive was cold and candlelit and perfect, ten thousand volumes in their cases, the guild's memory arranged in the order the guild preferred: everything in its place, everything accounted for, nothing screaming. Ines walked the aisles running her fingers along the spines and tried to think like a cartographer instead of a person. What did the old records say about voices? About the Last War? About—

She stopped.

There was a gap.

It was small — a finger's width of dark shelf between two fat registers of coastal surveys — but Ines had kept these shelves for six years and she knew their silences the way a musician knows rests. Something had been there. Something had been removed recently; the shelf dust showed a clean rectangle, sharp-edged, undisturbed long enough to settle at the edges but not enough to soften.

She checked the removal log chained to the end of the aisle. The last entry was hers, four days old. Nothing since. Which meant whoever had taken the volume had not logged it, and there were perhaps five people in the world with keys to this wing, and one of them was Halvard.

She stood in the cold aisle with her heart ticking and made herself count what she knew, because counting was her sacrament:

One. Vessia held the Salt Marches and was moving east toward the free cities, and her method was not fire but inventory — she conquered by cataloguing, by claiming, by making the world legible to herself. Ines recognized the method. It was the guild's method. It was *her* method, weaponized, and the recognition sat in her chest like a swallowed stone.

Two. Vessia hunted a voice, and had taught her soldiers to hunt it, and paid silver for rumors of strange song.

Three. The guild possessed a Choir of bound spirits whose harmonized song rewrote geography itself, sealed in an undercroft a century ago, and the guild's founding doctrine — *we record; we do not intervene; we are invisible* — was either a philosophy or an alibi, and Ines was no longer sure which, and was increasingly sure it didn't matter, because both produced the same behavior: silence, filing, feeding bread to refugees while the thing beneath the floor hummed.

Four. Someone had removed a volume from this shelf without logging it, and the gap was exactly the width of a book of the size the guild used for prophecies.

She pressed her palm flat against the empty space on the shelf, as if she could take the missing book's pulse.

"Ines."

She turned. Halvard stood at the end of the aisle, lantern in hand, and behind him, half in shadow, two of the council's wardens in their grey coats — the ones who escorted difficult clients off the premises, the ones whose existence the guild officially described as *building maintenance*. Ines had never seen them in the archive at night.

"You shouldn't be here after hours," Halvard said gently.

"I'm filing."

"At midnight." He came closer, and the lantern light climbed his face and made him look, for one unguarded moment, exactly as old as he was. "Go to bed, child. Tomorrow will be a long day. The roads will be full, and the council meets at dawn, and—" he paused, and she watched him choose, in real time, between two sentences, and she would remember for the rest of her life which one he picked— "and there is bread to count."

"Bread," Ines said. "Yes. There's always bread."

She walked past him. As she did, she let her eyes drop, casually, to the warden's coat pocket, where a corner of oxblood leather protruded — the guild's binding leather, the kind used for volumes too dangerous for open shelving — and she saw the gilt lettering on the visible sliver, and her blood went cold and then hot and then very, very quiet.

She did not read the title. She didn't need to. She knew the width of that gap. She knew the shape of the absence the way she knew the shape of her own name.

Outside, the grey dawn was coming up over the free cities, and far to the west, beyond the edge of every map on the guild's walls, an army was marching east behind a queen who collected voices, while underneath Ines's feet, beneath the flagstones of the guildhall, seven imprisoned singers waited in the dark — and somewhere above them, in a locked drawer, in a hand a century dead, a prophecy waited too, and it was getting shorter every day, the way all debts do.

Ines stood on the guildhall steps and made herself say it aloud to the empty courtyard, quietly, the way you sign for a delivery:

"She's coming here. For the Voice. And the council knows — and they've started hiding the books."

Behind her, inside the hall, faint as breath, she heard — or imagined she heard, and could no longer tell the difference, and that itself was a kind of answer — a single note rising from below the floor. Not a song. A tuning.

As if something down there had heard the rumor too, and was warming up.
