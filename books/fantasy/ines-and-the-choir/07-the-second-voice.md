## 7. The Second Voice

Tam had been dead for six years, and it was going very well until the woman from the Guild came to the tannery district.

Dead, in the way the undercroft taught you. Not a name struck from a ledger—Tam had never been in a ledger—but a body folded out of the world's attention like a letter no one wanted to read. The trick of it was simple and terrible: stop being worth looking at. Walk with your shoulders curved inward so your outline apologized for itself. Wear colors the eye slid off, mud-brown and ash-grey. Answer questions with answers so dull they left a residue of boredom, and people would forget they'd asked. Tam had been eleven years old when they'd learned this from the Choir itself, which knew more about disappearing than anything alive, because the Choir had been made to disappear even while it sang.

Six years of being nobody. Six years of tuning fiddles in exchange for soup, sleeping in the loft above Vetch's Tannery where the smell kept casual visitors away, keeping the hum locked behind their teeth.

And then, on a grey morning that smelled of lye and wet hides, a woman stood at the foot of the loft ladder looking up at Tam with eyes the color of ledger ink, and said, "You're humming it wrong."

* * *

Tam had rules. Rule one: never hum where anyone could hear. Rule two: if broken, rule one, leave. Rule three: never, ever let a Cartographer see your face.

The woman at the ladder's foot wore a guild cloak with the badge unpicked—the ghost of stitches, a paler rectangle on the wool—and she held her hands open and empty, palms out, the way you'd approach a dog that had been kicked. Her hair was coming loose from its braid. There was ink on her fingers, three days old at least, worked into the creases.

"I'm not going to tell them," she said. "I need you to know that first. I'm not telling them anything."

"Wrong person," Tam said. "I tune fiddles."

"You were humming under the bridge on Fetterlane last night. A descending fourth with a suspended seventh." She said it the way other people named flowers. "That's not folk music. That's maintenance. That's what you sing to keep a binding from fraying."

The cold went through Tam like well water. Six years. Six years of being careful, and one night of weakness beside the river where the sound carried over the water, and here was the bill come due.

"Get away from my ladder."

"I'll go." She didn't move. "My name is Ines Pemberton. I keep the ledgers. I found something written down that shouldn't exist, and then I found the thing itself, and now I can't find any way to unknow either one." Her voice stayed level but her hands had begun to shake, small tremors she clearly hated. "There are seven spirits under the guildhall. Bound. And someone has to have tuned them, because bindings don't hold themselves, and every record of who did it has been burned or blotted or buried. Except the work is still precise. Someone maintained it. Someone with an ear."

"You should go," Tam said again, and heard their own voice come out younger than they wanted, thinner.

"The third spirit from the east wall," Ines said. "The one that sounds like a bell underwater. Its binding is failing. I could hear it. I don't know how I could hear it, but I could, and it sounded like—" She stopped. Swallowed. "It sounded like something asking for help."

For a long moment the only sound was the tannery yard: hides dripping, Vetch cursing at a vat, the ordinary noise of a world that didn't know what was singing underneath its cities.

Then Tam climbed down the ladder, took the woman by the sleeve, and pulled her into the shadow between the drying sheds, because if the Guild had sent her, they'd already be surrounded, and if they hadn't—if she'd truly just *heard*—then everything Tam had believed about the safety of silence was already dead, and there was no point pretending otherwise.

"How long," Tam said, "has the bell-spirit been slipping?"

"A week. Maybe less."

"Then we have maybe a month before it comes loose on its own." Tam looked at her ink-stained hands, her unpicked badge, her eyes that didn't know how to lie yet. "And you'd better sit down, Keeper, because what happens when a bound spirit comes loose is worse than anything your ledgers have words for."

* * *

They talked in the loft, door barred, a single candle turned down to a bead of flame. Tam sat on the upturned crate that served as a chair; Ines perched on the edge of the cot as though she might be asked to leave it at any moment. Outside, the tannery stink rose like a moat.

"Start with how you got out," Ines said.

"That's not first."

"It's first for me."

Tam almost laughed. Almost. "Fine. But you won't like it." They wrapped their hands around a cup of cold barley tea for something to hold. "I was born down there. You understand? Not brought. Born. My mother was a tuner before me, and hers before her, back four generations. The Guild doesn't hire tuners, Keeper. It breeds them. Like hawks. Like hounds."

Ines's face did something complicated. "I keep the ledgers," she said, as if that explained it, and maybe it did. "I balance columns. I catalog compasses that point at things that aren't north. I thought—" She pressed her lips together. "I thought the worst thing the Guild did was hoard."

"The worst thing the Guild does is keep a song alive by feeding it people who don't get to have names." Tam heard the bitterness come out raw and unpolished and didn't try to sand it down. "Tuners live below. We learn the seven voices before we learn letters. My mother taught me which harmonics soothe each spirit and which ones fret them. When a binding starts to slip, you don't patch it with iron or chalk—you sing it tight again. Every night. Two hours, minimum. Miss a night and the spirits dream badly, and when bound spirits dream badly, the maps around them start to lie."

"Lie how?"

"Roads bend. Rivers remember old courses. Distances stretch for some travelers and shrink for others." Tam shrugged. "You've seen the ledgers. All those 'corrected anomalies' the Guild attributes to surveyor error. Half of those are us, missing our nights."

"And the other half?"

"The Choir dreaming. Even well-tuned, it dreams." Tam set down the cup. "Now the part you really won't like. How I left."

Ines waited.

"There was a night my mother didn't come up from the deep gallery. She'd gone in alone to retune the sixth spirit—the storm-voiced one—because the senior tuner was sick and the schedule couldn't slip. And the sixth spirit—" Tam's hands had gone still in their lap, deliberately still, the stillness of someone holding a door shut against weather. "It knew her. It had known her for thirty years. And I think—I've had six years to think—that it loved her, in whatever way a thing like that loves. And when she sang the tightening, it sang back a loosening. On purpose. It unwove her half of the harmony and took the whole weight of the binding into itself, and it tore. And in the tear, for about nine seconds, nothing in the undercroft was bound at all."

"What happened?"

"Everything got very loud and very quiet at the same time. The maps upstairs—we felt them go wrong all through the rock. And in the middle of it, my mother was just... gone. Not dead the way bodies die. Unwritten. The tear passed through her and there wasn't a body, there wasn't blood, there was a shape of clean air where she'd been standing, and the sixth spirit screaming in a voice like a landslide, and the other six wailing around it." Tam's voice had gone flat, recitation-flat, the tone you used for inventory. "Nine seconds. Then the senior tuner dragged himself down on his sickbed and slammed the emergency harmonies back into place, and the world snapped shut like a book. And I ran. Because in those nine seconds I'd felt what the spirits feel when the binding goes slack, and do you know what it feels like?"

Ines shook her head.

"Like waking up. Just for nine seconds, they were *awake*, all seven, and the first thing they did with their freedom was try to save the person hurting them." Tam looked at the candle bead. "I was twelve years old and I understood that everything I'd been told was backwards. They weren't kept calm for the world's sake. They were kept asleep for the Guild's."

The silence stretched. Somewhere below, Vetch began scraping a hide, a long rhythmic rasp like breathing.

"The records say the Choir was bound to end the last war," Ines said quietly. "A century ago. Seven spirits whose song can rewrite borders. The Guild caught them mid-song—or summoned them, the accounts disagree—and bound them, and the war ended, and the free cities survived. Halvard calls it the one necessary sin."

"Halvard." Tam tasted the name. "Your mentor. The one who told you the prophecy ledger is a clerical echo."

Something flickered across Ines's face—how did she know that?—and settled into grim humor. "Word travels."

"Tuners hear things. The walls down there are thin in ways that aren't physical." Tam leaned forward. "Here's what your necessary sin looks like from underneath, Keeper. A century of two-hour shifts, every night, no missed nights, no sick children excused, no funerals attended. Four generations of my family who never saw the sun except on sanctioned errands. And the spirits themselves, dreaming in the dark, dreaming of being awake, and every year the binding needs a little more song to hold because they wake up a little more each time. Your necessary sin isn't a sin, it's a debt. And debts compound."

"So it breaks," Ines said. "Eventually. On its own."

"On its own, catastrophically, with no one ready. Or—" Tam hesitated. This was the ledge. This was the place where six years of careful nothing ended, one way or another. "Or someone unmakes it properly. Gently. In order. With consent."

"Can that be done?"

"My mother thought so. She used to say the binding wasn't a cage, it was a chord—a chord held so long everyone forgot it was being played by someone. Stop playing it note by note, resolve it instead of cutting it, and the spirits go free without the tear." Tam's mouth twisted. "She died proving that the cut version kills. She never got to show anyone the slow version. I was twelve. I only got the theory, the fragments, the things she whispered while she braided my hair. I've spent six years trying to reconstruct it from memory and I'm maybe—maybe—halfway."

Ines had gone very still, in the way of someone doing arithmetic they don't want finished. "Vessia crossed the Salt Marches three days ago."

"I know. The whole city knows."

"She's hunting a voice. Refugees say it outright—she wants the Choir. To sing the free cities into her empire without a battle." Ines laughed, short and airless. "Mercy, weaponized. And the Guild council met last night and voted to do *nothing*. Doctrine of invisibility. Wait it out. As if Vessia's cartographers won't find the undercroft inside a season, and as if the Guild will fight her when she does. They won't. They'll negotiate. They'll hand it over like a warehouse lot."

"They might hand *you* over too," Tam said. "If the prophecy names you."

Ines flinched. Full-body, involuntary, a bird striking glass. "How—"

"The walls talk. I told you." Tam studied her: the ink-stained keeper of secrets, the woman trained to be invisible from above stairs, from the comfortable side of the ledgers. Different training than Tam's, same curriculum. *Be nothing. Be safe. Let the institution do your existing for you.* "So here's where we are. You can't unknow the Choir. I can't untune it alone. Vessia wants it, the council will sell it, and the bell-spirit is fraying whether any of us act or not." Tam spread their hands. "I don't trust you. You're Guild. You were Guild this morning and you'll be Guild in your sleep for years. But you're the first person in six years who heard the bell-spirit slipping and didn't write it down in triplicate and file it."

"I filed it," Ines admitted. "Last night. Before I came here. I wrote it in the margin of an anomaly report."

"And?"

"And then I went back and blotted it out with lamp-black at dawn." She said it like a confession, and it was one, and they both knew it. First blot. The first small treason of ink. Tam had committed theirs at twelve, running up the stair with the tear still ringing in their bones.

"All right," Tam said. "All right. Here's what happens next, and you're not going to like it. I need to hear the bell-spirit myself. From outside, if I can—there are listening-points in the city, old foundation stones where the undercroft's harmony bleeds up. If it's fraying as fast as you think, I can confirm it tonight. And if it's confirmed..." They exhaled. "Then somebody has to get back down there, Pemberton. Through the sealed door, past the wardens, into the deep gallery. Somebody the Guild trusts enough not to search. Somebody with keys."

"I'm a record-keeper," Ines said. "Nobody searches record-keepers. We're furniture."

"Furniture with keys."

"Furniture with keys," she agreed, and for the first time since the foot of the ladder, something moved through her face that wasn't fear. It took Tam a moment to recognize it, it had been so long since they'd worn it themselves: the look of a person who has just stopped waiting to be saved.

They worked out the signals—chalk marks on the tannery gate, a whistled phrase that was genuinely just a fishmonger's call, meeting points ranked from safe to desperate. Ines wrote none of it down, and Tam noted that, and revised their estimate of her upward by a degree they hadn't meant to spare.

At the ladder's head, pausing to descend into the ordinary world, she turned back.

"The slow unbinding," she said. "Your mother's way. If we did it—if we resolved the chord instead of cutting it—what would it cost? Bindings like that always want a price."

Tam had asked themselves the same question for six years, in the dark, in the space between one held breath and the next. They gave her the honest answer, because she'd earned exactly that much and no more.

"I don't know. My mother thought the price was paid up front—a century of it, by everyone who kept the thing caged. But bindings always read the fine print differently than the binder intended." They looked at the candle bead, guttering now, the wax pooling like something melting out of shape. "Ask me again when we're standing in front of the door."

Ines nodded once and went down the ladder, and her footsteps faded into the tannery stink and the grey morning, and Tam sat alone in the loft with the dead candle and the hum rising unbidden behind their teeth—the descending fourth, the suspended seventh, the sound of a cage being held together by four generations of swallowed names.

Tonight they would go to the listening-stone beneath Fetterlane bridge and put their ear against the city's bones.

And if the bell-spirit was fraying as fast as Ines had heard—

Then somewhere below the guildhall, in the dark, something that had spent a hundred years dreaming of waking was about to get its wish, and Tam only hoped that when it opened its eyes, it would remember that the last tuner who'd shown it kindness had been a girl with braided hair, and that the girl's child was finally, finally coming home.
