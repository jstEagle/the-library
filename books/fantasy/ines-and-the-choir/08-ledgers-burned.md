## 8. Ledgers Burned

The fire started in the west wing, where the oldest ledgers slept.

Ines knew it before she knew it — the way she always knew things in the guildhall first, through the building itself rather than her senses. A wrongness in the air pressure. A taste of hot iron on the back of the tongue. The archive had a breath to it, the slow exhalation of vellum and binding-glue and a century of lamp oil burned clean, and at half past the second bell that breath changed.

She was three floors down in the counting room, reconciling the quarter's salt-tithe against the caravan manifests, when the taste came. Her pen stopped mid-stroke. A drop of ink swelled at the nib and fell, blooming black across the entry for *Corvane, wool, forty bales* — and she sat very still, listening with her whole body, the way Halvard had taught her in her first year. *A ledger keeper who trusts only her eyes,* he'd said, *is a clerk. You are meant to be an instrument.*

The instrument told her: smoke.

She was moving before the second sign arrived — a bell, then another, the alarm-ropes in the stairwells pulled by hands that were, she would later realize, far too slow about it.

* * *

The west wing stairs were already breathing heat when she reached them. Orange light stuttered along the walls, throwing her shadow long and broken ahead of her, and the smell was no longer smoke but paper — that terrible sweet char of a hundred years of records going up like autumn leaves. Someone was shouting below. Someone else was shouting above. Nobody seemed to be doing anything.

She should have turned back. She understood that even as she climbed, some cold clerical corner of her mind drafting the report of her own death: *Ines Pemberton, entered the burning wing at the third bell, cause of entry unknown, remains unrecovered.* The guild's doctrine of invisibility did not extend to dying heroically in archives. There was no column for it in any ledger she kept.

But the prophecy ledger was in the west wing. Third gallery, iron case, fourth shelf. The book with tonight's date inked a century ago and her birth-name written beside the phrase *the Choir's Voice* — the book Halvard had dismissed as a clerical echo with eyes that never quite met hers while he said it.

She went up into the fire.

* * *

Later, she would reconstruct those minutes the way she reconstructed any corrupted entry: from fragments, inference, and the stubborn arithmetic of what must have happened. She remembered heat like a hand pressing her face sideways. She remembered the third gallery's door swollen shut in its frame and her shoulder hitting it once, twice, the pain arriving late and abstracted, and then the door giving with a crack that sounded almost like speech. She remembered the galleries themselves — long aisles of shelving, each bay labeled in the old cartographic shorthand, *Rivers Reversed*, *Cities Twice-Built*, *Borders That Moved at Night* — the guild's whole quiet empire of impossible things, and all of it burning with a hunger that felt less like accident than appetite.

Because that was the thing she understood somewhere between the door and the iron case, coughing, eyes streaming, the light so red now that the shadows had gone black as fresh ink: this fire was *eating selectively*.

She saw it in the way the flames moved. They ran along the shelves of the outer bays but hesitated at the aisle ends. They leapt across gaps no fire should cross and yet left one whole stretch of wall untouched, as if following instructions. And the sprinkler-cisterns in the ceiling — every gallery in the west wing had them, brass valves on pull-chains, guild-standard since the Ashwinter — hung dry and silent overhead. She could see the chains. She could see that they had been *cut*.

Someone had prepared this room to burn completely, and to burn in a particular order.

Her hands were blistering on the iron case. The lock was a guild pattern; she'd carried its key since she was nineteen, because Halvard believed every keeper should be able to open anything in an emergency, and because — she saw it clearly now, the way you see a figure only after staring at the negative — because he had known there would be an emergency someday, and had wanted one person small enough and loyal enough to reach the truth before anyone else could destroy it.

Or perhaps he simply hadn't imagined the destroyer would wear a guild badge.

The lock turned. Inside the case, behind glass gone warm as skin, lay the prophecy ledger — and beside it, she registered with a strange detached grief, two other volumes she recognized: the founding charter of the undercroft binding, and a slim book of correspondence in a hand she knew better than her own. Halvard's. Thirty years of his letters, all of them, apparently, worth killing a library over.

There was no time for three books. The aisle behind her was a wall of orange now, and the air had begun to do the thing air does when a room is about to flash — that shivering, expectant stillness, the whole gallery inhaling.

She took the prophecy ledger, wrapped it in her coat, and held the charter and the letters one last moment — long enough to understand that leaving them was itself a choice, that the fire would read them so no one else ever could — and then she ran.

* * *

She came out the east loading door into snow and night and the sound of the guildhall screaming. Half the courtyard stood watching the west wing burn, faces lit gold, and not one of them moved toward it. That was the detail that lodged in her like a splinter: the stillness. Fire crews existed. Buckets, chains, the sand-vaults beneath every wing. She had drilled with them herself, twice a year, Halvard's flat voice counting cadence.

Instead the guild's people stood in the falling snow and watched their own history burn with the expressions of men reading a sentence they'd been dreading.

And near the archway to the inner court, half-turned away, stood a man in the grey livery of the record-wardens with fresh soot on his cuffs.

Soot on the *inside* of the cuffs. Soot that hadn't come from standing in a courtyard watching.

Their eyes met. He didn't startle. That frightened her more than the fire had — the way he simply looked at her, looked at the bundle under her arm, and then walked, unhurried, toward the gatehouse, and was gone into the dark beyond the walls. No alarm raised. No accusation shouted. As if her survival were either irrelevant or expected.

*Ines Pemberton, carrying one volume out of a fire set by her own order.* She stood in the snow and let the arithmetic finish itself. The guild had spent a century perfecting invisibility, and tonight it had applied that craft to murder — not hers, though perhaps hers later, if the bundle under her arm mattered enough — but to the destruction of its own memory. You don't torch your archives to hide from Vessia. You torch them so that no one can ever prove what you did, or what you agreed to give away.

The council wasn't preparing to fight the usurper. The council was preparing to make sure that when the surrender came, there would be no evidence of anything worth prosecuting. Clean hands, bought with ash.

She looked down at the bundle in her arms. Through the scorched wrapping she could feel the ledger's corners, its weight, its unreasonable, impossible survival. One book out of ten thousand. The fire had been ordered to eat everything, and she had robbed it of the single page that mattered.

Which meant someone, somewhere, was already composing the entry that explained her.

* * *

She did not go to her rooms. She thought about it — the bed, the bolt, the familiar dark — and rejected it in the space of one heartbeat, because her rooms were the first place anyone would look, and because the part of her that had served the guild for eleven years kept offering helpful suggestions like *report to Halvard* and *file an incident account* and *wait for instructions*, and every one of those suggestions now sounded like a hand closing around her wrist.

Instead she went down. Past the counting room, past the vaults, to the map-closets where the working copies lived — the guild's daily lies, the beautiful smoothed-over charts sold to merchants and magistrates — and she took the narrow service stair that smelled of tallow and cold stone, the one that descended below the water line, below the wine cellars, to a corridor she had entered exactly once before.

The undercroft door stood at its end: black oak, iron-banded, sealed with seven locks whose keys hung on seven hooks inside a glass-fronted cabinet that required three signatures to open.

She had no signatures. She had no keys.

But she had spent eleven years among the guild's locks, and here was the thing the guild had never understood about people like her — the invisible ones, the ones who kept the books rather than made the history: we know where everything is filed. Including the master patterns. Including the locksmith's folio, third shelf, catalogued under *Miscellaneous Hardware, Obsolete*, because the guild could not bear to label a file *Keys to Our Greatest Sin*.

She had taken the folio an hour before the fire. She had taken it without letting herself think about why, the way you swallow medicine without tasting it. Now, kneeling in the cold corridor with the burnt-smelling bundle pressed to her chest, she fitted the first key and understood that she had known, on some level beneath knowing, since the night she read her own name in a century-old hand.

The locks opened one by one, each with a sound like a small verdict.

Behind the last of them, the door swung inward onto darkness and a faint, impossible warmth, and from somewhere deep in that darkness — thin, wavering, like a note held too long by a throat grown tired — came singing.

Seven voices. Bound. Waiting. Singing to an empty room for a hundred years.

Ines stood on the threshold with the prophecy ledger against her heart and the fire's glow still staining the snow-melt on her shoulders, and she made the decision that would unmake everything: she stepped through, and pulled the door shut behind her.

Whatever the guild intended to surrender to Vessia, whatever bargain the council was striking in ash and silence — it would not be struck with these voices still caged. Not if the only person standing between them and the flame was a ledger keeper with blistered hands and nothing left to lose.

Above her, muffled by stone, the west wing collapsed inward with a sound like a great book closing.

She descended toward the song.
