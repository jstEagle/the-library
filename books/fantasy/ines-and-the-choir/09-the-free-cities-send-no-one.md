## 9. The Free Cities Send No One

Corvane announced itself the way all proud cities do: by smell. Woodsmoke and river-mud, tanneries and frying onions, the sweet rot of a market three days past its best. After nine days on the road—first the smuggler's barge down the Vell, then a mail-coach that smelled of wet wool and other people's breath—the stink of Corvane felt almost like welcome.

Ines stood at the top of the Bridge of Chains and let the city wash over her. She had spent her whole life in the guildhall at Greyhollow, where the air was paper-dust and lamp-oil and the particular silence of rooms where important things were deliberately not said. Corvane was loud. Corvane said everything, most of it twice, none of it true. Banners hung from the balconies along the Processional in the free cities' colors: seven stripes for seven cities that had never once acted as one.

*They will listen,* she told herself. *They have to.*

She had rehearsed the argument the entire way downriver. She had it structured like a ledger entry: provenance, corroboration, valuation. The prophecy ledger with tonight's date written a century ago. The undercroft with its seven bound spirits. The Choir's song that could unmake borders—and Vessia crossing the Salt Marches with her claim-maps and her hunger for a voice. She had even brought proof, or what passed for proof among people who dealt in impossible things: rubbings of the prophecy's pages, Tam's testimony sworn and witnessed before a river-shrine priest who charged her two coppers and didn't ask questions, and a fragment of chalk-song Tam had taught her, folded up small inside her mind like a knife wrapped in cloth.

She had not expected to be afraid. She had faced down Halvard over an open ledger; she had walked into the undercroft and heard the Choir sing the shape of the world. A city council should have been nothing.

But standing on the bridge, watching the banners snap in the river wind, she understood suddenly how small she was. One woman with a satchel of papers, asking an institution to stop being what institutions are.

*Survival bought with silence is just another form of corruption.* She had written that line herself, years ago, in the margin of a training manual, and been made to copy out the doctrine of invisibility forty times as punishment. Halvard had watched her do it without a word. She wondered now if he had been proud.

The Council Hall of Corvane sat at the head of the Processional like a fat merchant at the end of a banquet, all pale stone and arched windows, its steps worn into shallow bowls by four centuries of petitioners. Ines climbed them at midmorning, when the queue was shortest, and presented her letter of introduction to a clerk whose eyebrows performed a small, eloquent tragedy.

"The Cartographers' Guild," he repeated. "Of Greyhollow."

"You'll find us in the registry of chartered orders. We hold mapping rights across six of the seven cities."

"I don't doubt it." He stamped something without looking at it. "Petitions to the Council on matters of defense go through the Office of Public Safety. Office of Public Safety is closed until Second Bell. Come back after Second Bell. Unless your matter is urgent."

"It is urgent."

"Then come back after Second Bell," he said, with the serenity of a man who has heard urgency every day of his working life and outlived all of it.

* * *

Second Bell came and went. So did Third. Ines spent the interval in a scriptorium-café near the Hall, paying by the hour for a desk by the window, and used the time the way she knew how: she read Corvane.

Cities kept records too. Not ledgers as honest as hers—no city's books ever balanced against its own soul—but records nonetheless. The public notice-boards told her the first thing: grain prices up a fifth since midsummer, which meant the Salt Marches' fall had already reached the markets, which meant the Council knew and was doing arithmetic instead of anything else. The shipping manifests posted at the customs house told her the second: three Vessian-flagged traders moored at the deep-water quay, flying merchant colors, riding too low in the water to be carrying only silk. And the third thing she learned from a pair of clerks at the next table, who talked the way clerks talk when they think no one is listening—that Councilor Maren Ossel had moved her household accounts out of Corvane banks and into Meridian, and that everyone pretended not to know what that meant.

*They know,* Ines thought, copying nothing, remembering everything. *They've known for weeks. They're just waiting to see whose side the arithmetic lands on.*

It was, she realized, exactly what the Guild would have done. Exactly what the Guild *had* done, for a hundred years: watch the storm from behind good glass, keep the ledgers clean, call it prudence.

At Fourth Bell she was admitted—not to the Council itself but to a committee, because committees were where councils sent things they intended to bury politely. Three members sat behind a crescent table in a room that smelled of beeswax and old decisions: a heavy man with a magistrate's chain (Councilor Brade), a thin woman with ink-stained fingers (Councilor Yeva, formerly of the Chancery, if the badge was right), and a young man who spent the entire audience sketching something on a slate and never once looked up.

"State your business," said Brade.

Ines had rehearsed this. She opened her satchel, laid the rubbings on the table, and began the way she'd planned: provenance, corroboration, valuation.

"A century ago," she said, "the Cartographers' Guild bound seven spirits beneath our hall and called the binding a map correction. Their song can rewrite geography—move rivers, erase roads, unmake borders. We kept them as a weapon and hid them as a filing error. There is a prophecy in our oldest ledger, written a hundred years before tonight, that names this date and names me beside something called the Choir's Voice. And Queen Vessia has taken the Salt Marches, and every refugee coming off the salt road says the same thing: she is hunting a voice. She doesn't want to fight the free cities, Councilors. She wants to *sing* them. One song, and your walls mean nothing, because your borders won't mean anything either."

Silence. The sketching man's charcoal scratched on.

Yeva picked up one of the rubbings and held it to the light. "This is a rubbing of a page you say is a century old."

"Yes."

"And I am to take your word for its age."

"You're welcome to send a verifier to Greyhollow. The Guild will confirm—" *and then lie fluently, the way Halvard lied to the buyer,* she thought, and amended: "—the Guild's archivists can authenticate the hand."

Brade leaned back until his chair creaked. "Let me save us all some time, mistress...?"

"Pemberton. Ines Pemberton."

"Mistress Pemberton. You are telling this committee that a quiet order of mapmakers has been sitting on a magical army for a hundred years, and that the usurper Vessia—who, I note, has conquered half the Salt Coast with entirely ordinary soldiers—is now marching here to collect it. Is that correct?"

"That is correct."

"And your evidence is some rubbings, a sworn statement from a runaway"—he glanced at a sheet a secretary had passed him—"choir-tuner, and a story about a prophecy that happens to feature yourself rather prominently."

Heat crawled up Ines's neck. "I'm not asking you to believe the prophecy. I'm asking you to believe the refugees. Ask them yourselves. They're camped outside your north gate in numbers your own granary clerks are already complaining about. Vessia isn't a rumor. The Salt Marches aren't a rumor."

"The Salt Marches are four hundred miles away," said Brade, "behind the Vell, behind the fens, behind three cities that haven't fallen. Corvane has walls that have never been breached and treaties with Meridian and Osterhale. What you are proposing, as I understand it, is that we mobilize the militia, recall the fleet, and declare war on the basis of a cartographer's bad dream. Do you know what that does to grain futures? To the insurance syndicates? Do you know what it does when it turns out to be nothing?"

"It won't turn out to be nothing."

"People always say that." He said it gently, almost kindly, and that was the worst part. It was Halvard's voice. It was the Guild's voice. "Mistress Pemberton, I have sat on this committee for eleven years. In eleven years, I have heard the end of the world predicted nine times. Eight times it was nothing. The ninth time it was a grain blight. We act on evidence. Bring us evidence—an army at the ford, a Vessian banner on the far bank, a declaration of war—and I promise you this committee will move faster than you'd believe. Until then—"

"Until then you'll wait," Ines said, "until waiting stops being safe. And then you'll discover it stopped being safe about a month too late, and you'll spend the rest of your lives explaining why."

Yeva set down the rubbing with a small click. "That's out of order."

"Yes," Ines said. "Everything about this is out of order. That's rather my point."

The sketching man finally looked up. He was younger than she'd thought, twenty-five at most, with the flat, patient eyes of someone counting things. He looked at her for exactly as long as it takes to memorize a face, and then he looked back down at his slate.

The committee thanked her for her testimony. The committee noted her concerns. The committee referred the matter to the Office of Public Safety, which was closed.

* * *

She argued for two more days. She was thorough about it, because thoroughness was the only weapon she had. She got an hour with a deputy of the harbor master and showed him the Vessian ships riding low in the water; he agreed they were suspicious and explained that the harbormaster's office could not inspect foreign merchant hulls without a writ from the Council, and the Council did not issue writs on suspicion. She found the refugee camps beyond the North Gate—four thousand souls under canvas in the rain, and among them a salt-widow named Odile who had walked out of the Marches with her children and described, in a voice scraped hollow, the Vessian heralds going village to village asking after "any who sing uncommonly well," and what happened to the village of Sarn Anset when no one answered. Ines wrote it all down. She took it back to the committee's secretary, who accepted it into a file, and the file disappeared into the building the way water disappears into sand.

On the third evening she stood again on the Bridge of Chains, watching lamplighters work their way along the Processional, and admitted to herself what she had known since the first hour: Corvane was not going to act. Not out of stupidity. Out of *procedure*. Every person she had met in that building was reasonable, competent, and terrified, and each of them had found a perfectly defensible reason why the fear belonged to someone else's office. No single coward anywhere in the machine—just cowardice distributed so finely through the whole that it looked like caution.

*The Guild does the same thing,* she thought. *We just do it quieter. We call it invisibility. They call it stability. Same ledger, different binding.*

A bell rang somewhere—the alarm bell, her hindbrain registered a half-second before her conscious mind did, the one that meant fire or flood or gate-close. Then it stopped, one stroke only, and she realized it was just the evening gun, and laughed at herself, alone on the bridge, a short ugly laugh with nothing funny in it.

"You shouldn't be out after dark, mistress."

He was leaning on the parapet ten feet away, and she had not seen him arrive, which on a bridge with two approaches and nowhere to hide was its own kind of message. Young. Neat brown coat, traveling cloak, the kind of face you forgot while looking at it. The sketching man from the committee—or his twin, or his employer's idea of him.

"I beg your pardon?" Ines said.

"Corvane after dark. Cutpurses, press-gangs, worse." He smiled apologetically. "Though I suppose a woman who walks out of a legend probably handles cutpurses fine."

"I don't know what you mean."

"No." He nodded agreeably. "You wouldn't." He turned his elbows on the parapet and looked out at the black water. "Funny thing. I've been in this city a month, listening to that committee explain why four hundred miles is very far away. And then you walk in with your rubbings and your runaway's affidavit, and suddenly the faraway thing has a name and a date and a face." He glanced sideways at her. "Yours, as it happens. The Voice of the Choir. That's what they're calling you in certain company. Did you know that?"

Ice moved down Ines's spine, slow and deliberate, the way cold moves through a locked room. "Whoever told you that told you wrong."

"Probably." He pushed off the parapet and straightened his coat. "Here's the trouble, though. My queen doesn't need you to be the Voice. She just needs the Council to believe you might be. And look at that—they already do. Three days you've been here, and there are men in this city being paid to remember your face. Some of them work for me. Some of them work for the other kind of people, and those ones don't ask questions afterward." He produced something from his pocket—a stub of red wax, a signet ring—and pressed the ring briefly against his own thumbnail, leaving a mark like a small sealed wound. "Consider yourself marked, mistress Pemberton. By me, which is nearly harmless. And by others, which isn't. If I were you—and I'm glad I'm not—I'd be on a boat tonight. Any boat. Because in about a week, when the Council finally decides whether to sell you to buy peace, you want to be somewhere they can't reach you."

"Why tell me any of this?" Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "If you're hers."

He considered the question honestly, which frightened her more than the threat had. "Because I read the same reports you do. Because Sarn Anset bothers me. And because my queen wants the Choir, not a corpse, and dead witnesses make poor guides." He stepped back toward the northern end of the bridge, dissolving already into the lamplight and foot traffic. "Also, mistress—because a marked thing runs, and running things leave tracks, and tracks are how you find the undercroft. Good evening."

Then he was gone, and Ines stood on the Bridge of Chains with the river talking underneath her and the word *undercroft* ringing in her skull like a struck coin.

They didn't know where it was. That was the one mercy—the Guild's secrecy, the doctrine she had despised for a month, holding like a wall between Vessia and the Choir. But he was right about the rest. Marked things run. Running things leave tracks. And she had left tracks all over this city: the committee, the harbor office, the refugee camp, the scriptorium-café where she'd paid by the hour under her own name because hiding her name had started to feel like the corruption she'd sworn off.

*Halvard would have used a false name,* she thought. *Halvard would have been invisible by now.*

And Halvard, she remembered with a lurch, was dying, and the council was meeting, and every day she spent arguing with committees was a day closer to Vessia's heralds reaching Greyhollow and asking, very politely, after anyone who sang uncommonly well.

She went back to her lodging and packed in the dark, by touch, the way Tam had taught her—fast, silent, nothing that rattled. The prophecy rubbings went into the oilskin wrap against her skin. The affidavit. Two days' bread. At the last moment she took the café's receipt too, crumpled in her fist like a talisman: *Madam Pemberton, scriptorium no. 4, five hours.* Proof of her own foolishness, kept as a lesson.

There was one boat leaving tonight—the night-barge down to Osterhale, loading at the coal quay, no manifest, no questions, a smuggler's arrangement that would cost her another piece of the impossible. She had one left to give. She touched the place behind her ribs where Tam's chalk-song lived, folded small, and hoped it wasn't the price.

The streets between the bridge and the coal quay were long and dark, and somewhere in them, she knew, men were being paid to remember her face. She pulled her hood up, became—insofar as she still could—a woman shaped like silence, and started walking.

Behind her, high on the Council Hall, the lamps were going out one window at a time, each one a small, orderly surrender.

Ahead, at the bottom of the coal stairs, a barge waited with its lanterns hooded, and a voice from the dark said, "You're late, mistress. Hope whatever you're carrying is worth what you're about to pay."

Ines put her hand on the rail and stepped down into the dark.
