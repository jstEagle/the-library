## 10. Price of Passage

The road out of Corvane was a wound in the world.

Ines had walked it once before, years ago, as an apprentice carrying sealed dispatches to the Salt Marches, and she remembered it then as a pleasant enough track: hedgerows, waystones, the lazy brown ribbon of the Liss winding alongside. Now she saw it with new eyes—the eyes of someone who had spent three days in the city's underbelly learning what Vessia's scouts did to travelers who looked like they were carrying something worth taking.

The hedges were gone. The waystones had been pulled up and stacked like ammunition. And the river—she stopped at the crest of the hill and stared at the river, because the Liss was not where the maps said it was.

It had moved.

Not much. A few hundred yards east, carving a fresh channel through what had been somebody's barley field, the old bed lying empty and cracked beside it like a shed skin. She knew, intellectually, why. Tam had told her on the second night, in the cellar behind the dye-house where they'd hidden while Corvane's council dithered: *the Choir sang here once, long ago, before your guild bound them. Rivers remember being sung.* But knowing a thing in the ledger-sense and seeing it with her own boots in the mud were different countries entirely.

"You're staring," Tam said.

"I'm auditing." Ines crouched and pressed two fingers into the cracked clay of the dead riverbed. Dry as old vellum. "The Guild holds eleven charts of this valley. Every one of them shows the Liss here." She tapped the ground. "Every one of them is wrong, and every one of them has been wrong for longer than I've been alive, and no one ever filed a correction."

"Because corrections are how you get found," Tam said. There was no bitterness in it, only the flat recitation of someone who had learned the guild's catechism from the inside of its basement. "You taught me that yourself, Ulyana. First lesson of the ledger: if an entry embarrasses the order, it isn't an error. It's a decision."

Ines stood and wiped her fingers on her coat. Three days ago she would have flinched at hearing her guild-name spoken aloud on an open road. Now it felt like a disguise she'd forgotten to take off—a coat that fit too well and weighed more every mile.

"We should keep moving," she said. "The smuggler expects us at dusk."

* * *

His name was Osk Verren, and he operated out of a tannery that smelled like a crime against God, on the far bank of the relocated Liss, where the free cities' authority ended and the Salt Marches' chaos began. He received them in a back room furnished with one table, two stools, a lamp burning whale oil that must have cost more than the room, and a wall of pigeonholes stuffed with documents he clearly could not read but clearly understood better than most scholars.

He was a small man, soft-bodied, with the hands of someone who had done violence often enough to stop apologizing for it, and eyes that performed arithmetic on everything they touched.

"The Pemberton girl," he said. "I know the face. Your guild bought passage off me twice before you were weaned. Halvard himself, once, hood up, paid triple, asked nothing." He smiled without warmth. "So you'll understand when I say I'm curious what makes *you* worth the same courtesy."

"I need to reach the Thornwood," Ines said. "South, past the Marches, off any road Vessia's patrols watch. Two travelers, no cargo, no questions at the crossings."

"That's not a route," Osk said. "That's a favor. Favors are expensive."

"How expensive?"

"For anyone else? More than you have." He leaned back until his stool creaked. "But you're not anyone else. You're the ledger-keeper. Which means—" and here he raised one finger, savoring it, "—you can tell me things. Things written down. Things the Guild says don't exist."

Ines felt it then, the first cold touch of it, like stepping into a stream whose depth the light had lied about. She had rehearsed this negotiation on the walk down from the city. She had prepared to offer coin, and when coin failed, to offer the one thing she'd sworn on her apprentice oath never to move outside the archive walls: knowledge. She had told herself it was a small thing. A single entry. A victimless disclosure.

She had not prepared for how easily the words would come.

"There is a ford," she said quietly, "on the upper Sennet, forty miles west of here, that appears on no chart in any chancery in the free cities. It appears in ours. It is not a natural ford. The river there runs over a causeway of worked stone older than the kingdoms, submerged, and the water above it is only knee-deep year-round—shallow enough for a loaded wagon, deep enough everywhere else within ten miles to drown a horse. Armies have marched past it for six hundred years looking for exactly what it is." She held his gaze. "One crossing. One use. Then I expect you to forget I said it, the way my guild forgot everything else."

Osk Verren went very still, the stillness of a man watching a strongbox open. "And the Guild simply *has* this."

"The Guild has everything," Ines said. "That's the problem."

She watched him decide. It took less time than she'd hoped, which was worse than if it had taken longer. Because she understood, with the terrible clarity of the newly corrupted, that she hadn't just given him information. She had given him power—an edge over every army, every toll-lord, every rival smuggler between here and the mountains—and power does not stay where you put it. Power is a ledger entry that copies itself.

"A wagon crossing," Osk mused. "Undetected. Unwatched." His eyes came back up to hers, and now the arithmetic in them had a sum. "Done. Dusk tomorrow, the reed-cutters' path south of the tannery. My man Brannic drives; he doesn't speak, which saves us both the trouble of trusting him." He stood, extended a hand she didn't take, and added, almost kindly: "You'll do well out there, ledger-keeper. You've got the instinct."

"What instinct?"

"The one that knows a secret is just money that hasn't been spent yet."

* * *

They camped that night in a stand of alders above the tannery, close enough to hear the dogs, far enough to be smoke in the dark if it went badly. Tam built no fire. They sat shoulder to shoulder against a trunk and shared hard bread and harder cheese, and Ines found she couldn't eat her half.

"You gave him the Sennet ford," Tam said. Not a question. Tam missed nothing; that was what five years in a cage beneath the world taught a person—the habit of counting exits even in conversation.

"He'd have named his own price otherwise. Something worse."

"No," Tam said. "He wouldn't have. He'd have taken coin, or he'd have turned us in for the bounty, and either way the ford stays buried." They tore their bread with deliberate calm. "You chose to spend it. That's the part you're not saying out loud."

Ines looked at the dark where the tannery lamps glowed like drowned stars. "It's one crossing."

"It's never one crossing." Tam's voice stayed level, patient, the voice of someone who had watched masters make this exact argument over larger things. "A secret spent is a secret spent. He'll sell it twice before winter. A garrison will find it. A war will find it. And somewhere downstream of tonight, people die at a ford that was safe for six hundred years because a woman who kept honest books decided honesty was negotiable when it was her skin on the line."

"Do you think I don't know that?" It came out sharper than she intended, cracking on the last word, and she hated herself for the crack, because it wasn't grief, it was *discovery*—the ugly surprise of finding the rot already in you, established, comfortable, waiting.

Tam said nothing for a long moment. An owl called somewhere across the dead channel of the old Liss, and the sound seemed to come from the wrong direction, from a landscape that no longer agreed with itself.

"My grandmother tuned the third voice," Tam said at last. "Before the binding. She used to say the Choir doesn't corrupt you. It just removes the floor, so you can see how far down you were always willing to go." They brushed crumbs from their knees. "You went maybe a foot tonight. Halvard went a mile a year for a century. Vessia—" they shrugged. "Vessia dug through the bottom and kept digging. It's the same hole, Ulyana. Only the depth differs."

"And yours?" Ines asked. "How deep have you gone?"

Tam was quiet long enough that she thought they wouldn't answer. When they did, their voice had changed—lower, stripped of its careful flatness.

"When I ran, I took the tuning-fork. The silver one. Without it, the binding loosens by a hair each season—that's why the undercroft leaks, why the maps drift, why rivers wander. I knew that. I knew what leaving would do, slowly, to everyone living near the drift zones, and I left anyway, because staying meant dying by inches in the dark." They met her eyes. "So don't ask me to judge you. Ask me to keep count. Someone has to hold the ledger while the keeper spends it."

Ines laughed—a short, broken sound with no humor in it—and put her head back against the alder bark. Above them, through the branches, the sky was doing something she had never once seen in thirty-one years of clear nights: the stars near the horizon were *shimmering*, faintly, rhythmically, like reflections disturbed by a footstep in a pond.

"The drift," Tam said softly, following her gaze. "Getting stronger. We're closer than I thought."

"To what?"

"To the reason the prophecy picked you." Tam pulled their coat tighter. "Sleep. Tomorrow we buy our lives with your sin, and the day after, I teach you what the sin was for."

* * *

Brannic the silent driver was exactly as advertised: a slab of a man who communicated in gestures and smelled of tallow and iron. He loaded them into a false-bottomed grain wagon before dawn, wedged them among sacks that stank of the tannery's cheaper trade, and drove them south along the reed-cutters' path with the unhurried competence of a man who had delivered many things no one wanted found.

Ines slept in fragments and dreamed of ledgers. In the dreams the columns would not stay closed; figures crawled out of the ruled lines like insects and arranged themselves on her hands, her arms, her throat, and every figure was a number she recognized—entries she had certified, balances she had confirmed, small impossibilities she had catalogued and shelved and *forgotten*, each one a door she had locked with someone on the other side of it. In the dream she tried to write a correction and her pen produced only the words *not an error, a decision*, over and over, in Halvard's handwriting.

She woke to the wagon stopping.

Gray light. Reeds. The smell of moving water. Brannic set two fingers to his lips, then pointed east through the reeds, and Ines parted them carefully and saw the patrol: six Vessian cavalry on the far rise, motionless, scanning the river country with the patience of men being paid to wait. On the breastplate of the lead rider, catching the early sun, the sigil she was going to see in her sleep for the rest of her life: a compass rose with one point torn away.

Vessia's mark. The empire that mapped by erasure.

"They're not hunting us specifically," Tam breathed. "They're sweeping. Looking for anyone leaving Corvane after the council refused her envoy." A pause. "Which means the council's cowardice just painted a target on every road out of the city. Well done, Corvane."

Brannic waited, motionless, with the professional serenity of a man whose silence had been purchased at rates Ines didn't want to calculate. The patrol lingered. Shifted. Turned, finally, and moved off westward along the ridge, and only when the last rider dropped below the skyline did Brannic click his tongue and roll the wagon forward, down through the reeds, onto the Sennet's bank.

And there it was.

The ford.

Even knowing what it was, Ines's breath caught. The river ran broad and brown and deceptively gentle, and beneath its surface—visible now in the low morning light as a pale suggestion, a ghost of geometry—the causeway stretched bank to bank in perfect straight lines. Worked stone. Pre-kingdom. Older than anything the Guild admitted to having charted, and the Guild had charted it anyway, centuries ago, and then folded the knowledge into a drawer and thrown away the drawer.

*A decision,* she thought. *Not an error.*

The wagon rolled onto the causeway and the water rose around the wheels to the hubs and no higher, obedient, knee-deep, exactly as the oldest entry in the oldest volume had promised. Ines sat in the grain sacks with her heart hammering and watched the drowned road carry them across six hundred years of other people's ignorance, and she understood—with a clarity that felt like a fever breaking—that this was what the Guild had always been. Not record-keepers. Not cartographers. Keepers of doors. And she had been the one who polished the locks.

On the far bank, Brannic halted, gestured south with two fingers—*three days to the Thornwood, no further*—and was gone back into the reeds before she could thank him, which was probably just as well, since she had nothing left to pay him with except more of herself.

They walked. The land opened ahead of them, green and unmapped and full of drifting, shimmering wrongness, and Tam hummed under their breath as they walked—three notes, four, a fragment of something that made the grass lean very slightly toward the sound.

"What are you doing?" Ines asked.

"Tuning," Tam said. "Practicing. Because in three days, Ulyana Pemberton, you're going to learn your first song—and I need to know, before we reach the undercroft again, whether the Choir chose right about you." They glanced sideways, and for the first time since the cellar behind the dye-house, they smiled. "Or whether I'm walking toward another hundred years of darkness with the wrong person."

Ahead, the river bent where no river should bend, and followed them.
