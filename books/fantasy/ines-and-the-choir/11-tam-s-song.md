## 11. Tam's Song

Tam had not sung in four years.

Not properly. Not the way the undercroft demanded, with the whole chest and the whole self, every breath a thread in something larger. What Tam did instead was hum in the bath, tuneless, deliberately tuneless, a habit like biting nails. A way of keeping the instrument rusted shut.

But Ines Pemberton kept asking questions, and questions were worse than commands. Commands you could refuse. Questions got inside you and set up housekeeping.

They were camped in the hollow beneath a fallen watchtower, three days out of Corvane, where the smuggler's road bent south toward the hills and the salt marsh gave way to birch scrub. The fire was small. Tam had built it small on purpose; small fires looked like nothing from a distance, and they were both of them experts at looking like nothing. It was the first thing the undercroft taught you, before music, before maps: *be forgettable*. The guild's tuners were not listed in any ledger. They were not paid in coin but in erasure—rooms that forgot them, records that never held their names. Tam had been a line drawn in pencil and then rubbed out.

Ines sat across the fire with the prophecy ledger open on her knees, reading it for the hundredth time as if the words might rearrange themselves into mercy if she stared long enough. Her lips moved when she read. She would have hated knowing that.

"You're going to ask me again," Tam said.

"I wasn't going to." She didn't look up. "I was going to wait until morning."

"That's how you ask. By not asking."

That earned a flicker of a smile, quickly suppressed, the way she suppressed everything—grief, fear, the particular fury of a woman who had spent her life believing an institution only to find its hands around a throat. Tam knew that fury. Had worn it for years, badly, like a coat cut for someone else.

"Show me," Ines said finally, closing the ledger. "The fragment. The one you used to open the door."

"There is no door-opening song. That's not how it works."

"Then show me what there is."

Tam looked at the fire instead of her. This was the conversation Tam had been circling for three days, the way water circles a drain, and now it had arrived, and there was nothing left to do but fall in.

"It's not a spell," Tam said. "That's the first lie people tell about it, that it's words you say and things happen. It's closer to—" Tam searched. "You know how a bridge has a frequency? And if soldiers march across it in step, in the wrong step, the bridge comes apart? Not because the marching is powerful. Because the bridge was already holding itself together by a kind of agreement, and the marching asks it to agree to something else."

"A resonance."

"You've been reading the ledgers too long. Yes. Resonance. The world holds itself together the same way. Rivers agree to run in their channels. Stone agrees to be a hill and not a wave. The Choir doesn't command anything. It just sings a different agreement, and the world—which is tired, which has been agreeing to things for a very long time—listens."

"And the binding? How Halvard's predecessors bound seven spirits into one voice?"

Tam's hands had gone cold. Tam noticed this distantly, the way you notice weather happening to someone else. "The binding is a song too. That's the ugly joke of it. You can't cage a spirit with iron or ink. But you can teach it a harmony so complete, so perfect, that it forgets there was ever a part of itself outside the chord. The binding isn't a wall. It's a chorus so beautiful the singers stop wanting solos."

*And then one day you realize you're the solo,* Tam didn't say. *And you climb out through the coal chute while the seventh spirit is singing your part for you, and you run north with no name and no trade except the one thing you swore never to do again.*

"The fragment I know," Tam said aloud, "is called the Turning. It's two bars. Maybe less—it's hard to count, because time goes strange inside it. It won't unmake anything. It won't free anyone. All it does is ask a small thing to reconsider a small agreement. Water is easiest. Water is always homesick for somewhere else."

Ines was very still. Firelight moved on her face. "Why haven't you taught anyone before?"

"Because I'm the only one left who knows it, and I'd like to keep it that way." Tam fed a stick to the fire. "And because everyone who learns it wants more. Two bars becomes four. Four becomes the whole verse. And the whole verse—" Tam stopped. Started again, quieter. "The whole verse is what Vessia wants, and what the guild buried, and what the prophecy is written *in*, actually, if you look at the margins right. Ink over ink. The Choir wrote its own warning in the hand of the woman who bound them, and everyone since has read it as permission."

"I know," Ines said softly. "Halvard told me."

"Then you know why I don't sing."

"I know why you're afraid to. It's not the same thing."

Tam wanted to be angry. Anger was available; anger was always available, it was the cheapest coin Tam carried. But Ines said it without cruelty, the way she entered everything in her ledgers—accurately, without ornament—and accuracy is very hard to hate.

"Water first," Tam said. "Tomorrow. If I'm going to ruin my one rule, I want daylight and a river to blame."

* * *

The river was called the Sennet on the old maps and the Weep on the new ones, and it ran brown and quick through a gorge of white stone, loud enough that they had to lean close to speak. Tam stood on the bank with boots in the mud and felt the old terror rise like water in a well: the sense of standing at the edge of a room where something enormous slept, knowing that the first note would begin waking it.

"Ines. Before we start. There are three rules, and they're not suggestions."

Ines waited. She was good at waiting. It was, Tam suspected, the whole architecture of her: patience laid over pressure, like ice over a river.

"One: never sing it angry. The Turning takes your intent and gives it back amplified, and anger amplifies into damage. Two: never sing it alone in a place you can't leave. If the fragment catches—if it keeps going after you stop—you need someone to pull you out by the shoulders and shake you. Three." Tam hesitated. "Three is the one nobody believes until it happens. Whatever you change will change back. Rivers return to their channels. Hills settle. The world is stubborn. But the world remembers being asked. Something in it stays... attentive. Afterward. Things notice you that didn't notice you before. So don't do it for wonder. Only do it when the alternative is worse."

"Has anything ever noticed you?"

"Yes," Tam said, and left it there.

The method was simple, which was the trap of it. No incantation, no gesture. You found the river's own sound—the low continuous tone underneath the noise, the note the water made simply by agreeing to be water moving downhill—and you matched it. Then, gently, without forcing, you leaned the note sideways. A third up. Then you added the second bar, which was the same melody inverted, so that the request became a question rather than a push: *what if you went home another way?*

"Match me first," Tam said. "Just the drone. Find the river's note and hold it."

Ines closed her eyes. For a moment nothing. Then her voice came, low and uncertain, wavering—and then finding it, catching the undertone beneath the rush of the Sennet the way a hand finds a rail in the dark. Tam felt the hair rise on both arms. Of course. Of course she could. The prophecy hadn't chosen at random; the guild hadn't hidden her in the archives by accident. Somewhere in the last century, a woman with a ledger and a conscience had been exactly the vessel the binding required, and everyone involved had told themselves stories about coincidence.

"Good," Tam said, and hated how much it cost to say. "Now hold it while I add the turn. Don't follow me. Don't harmonize. Just—stay where you are. Anchor."

Tam sang.

It came back the way language comes back, the way grief comes back: all at once, in the body, older than thought. Two bars. The drone bending upward into the third, the inversion folding the request back on itself, and the Sennet—

The Sennet stopped.

Not dried up. Not dammed. It *paused*, the whole brown length of it visible in the gorge, hanging for one impossible moment like a ribbon pinned to the air, spray suspended, fish turning slow as planets. The silence was total and enormous and wrong in a way that reached down into the spine, because rivers are supposed to be the one thing that never stops, and here was the proof that everything agrees to its own behavior, and agreements can be renegotiated.

Then the water turned.

It turned the way a thought turns—no violence, no splash, just a vast gentle revision, the current peeling away from its channel and sliding toward the northern fork, the dry one, the abandoned one, filling it with a sound like applause heard through a wall. Birch leaves shivered on the far bank though there was no wind. The suspended spray fell all at once, a soft rain.

Ines opened her eyes.

Tam saw it happen—the exact moment the intoxication arrived. It was in the face like sunrise: the widening, the hunger, the sudden knowledge that the world was not a wall but a door, and that she, Ines Pemberton, keeper of other people's secrets, had just been handed the key to everything. Tam had seen that face before. On apprentices. On Halvard, once, decades ago, according to the oldest tuner's account. On Vessia, if the rumors from the Salt Marches were true—that she had stood on a dune and sung a harbor into existence and laughed like a girl.

"Again," Ines whispered.

"No."

"It worked. It *worked*, Tam, do you understand what that means? If I can move a river, I can move a road. I can move an army's certainty. I can—"

"You can lose yourself, is what you can do." Tam crossed the mud and took her by the shoulders, and the contact seemed to startle her, as if she'd forgotten bodies existed. "Look at the water. Look at it *now*."

Ines looked. Tam watched her look, watched the understanding come slower than it should have, dragged down by the weight of wanting.

The Sennet was still in the wrong channel. That was expected; revisions took hours to wear off. But between the two forks, in the bed it had abandoned, the river had left something behind—a seam, a faint silver line in the stone, like scar tissue, like a crease in paper that has been folded and unfolded. And along that seam, the air moved strangely. Birds had gone quiet. The birches on the far bank were leaning—not falling, leaning—toward the place where the song had been, the way flowers lean toward light, or the way the drowned lean toward the surface.

"It remembers," Tam said. "I told you. The world remembers being asked. Every time you do this, you leave a crease. And creases collect. And one day something comes along that knows how to read them—all of them, everywhere, every place anyone ever bent the world—and follows them home to the person who made them." Tam let go of her shoulders. "Vessia has cartographers, Ines. Real ones. Ones who can read a crease like you read a ledger. Every miracle you perform is a signature."

Ines stood very still. The intoxication drained out of her face slowly, reluctantly, like tide off a flat, and what it left behind was worse: not fear exactly, but accounting. Tam could see her doing it, the ledger-keeper's reflex, weighing columns. *One river against one signature. One signature against an empire.* The horror of it was that the arithmetic was not obviously wrong.

"I felt it," Ines said quietly. "While I was singing. Or—humming. I felt the river agree. It wasn't like commanding. It was like being trusted." Her voice caught. "Do you know how long it's been since anything trusted me? Since I trusted myself? For ten years I've kept books for liars and called it honesty because my *columns balanced*." She looked at her own hands as if they belonged to a stranger. "And now I ask a river to bend and it bends, and some starving, secret part of me wants to do it again just to feel trusted once more. That's the corruption, isn't it. Not the power. The loneliness it cures."

Tam said nothing, because there was nothing to correct. That was the true lesson of the undercroft, the one the guild had never written down: the binding didn't hold the spirits with beauty alone. It held them with belonging. Seven voices who had been nothing alone, who had been *unheard* alone, given a chord where each of them mattered infinitely—and asked, in exchange, never to stop. Everyone sang longest for the choir that makes them real.

"We should move," Tam said. "Before the crease draws anything."

They packed the camp in silence. But at the edge of the birch scrub, shouldering her pack, Ines stopped and looked back at the gorge—at the silver seam in the empty riverbed, already fading, already becoming the kind of detail only a madman or a mapmaker would notice.

"Teach me the rest of it," she said. Not begging. Stating. The voice of a woman entering a figure in a ledger she knows will never balance. "Not today. But before we reach the guildhall. Because Vessia is coming for the Choir, and the council is going to hand it to her, and when that day comes I need to know more than two bars."

Tam looked north, where the hills went blue and beyond the hills the guildhall sat like a paperweight on a century of sins, and somewhere past that, the Salt Marches, and the usurper who sang harbors into being and laughed.

"You'll wish you'd stayed a bookkeeper," Tam said.

"I already do," said Ines, and walked on.

Behind them, in the gorge, the Sennet returned to its channel with a long sighing rush, like a sleeper turning over—but the birds did not sing again that day, and far to the south, in a tent full of instruments and ink, a cartographer named Sarel woke from a dream of silver seams and reached immediately for her pens.
