## 13. What Vessia Wants

They did not bind her hands.

That was the first thing Ines noticed, and the noticing itself frightened her more than irons would have. The Vessian scouts who had taken her on the Corvane road—three of them, mud to the shoulders, speaking in the clipped cadence of soldiers who had stopped expecting orders to make sense—had searched her satchel with a strange reverence. They'd lifted out her ledgers, her ink-stained fingers' worth of a lifetime's careful record-keeping, and passed them between themselves like relics.

"She's the one," the youngest had said, not to Ines but to the road.

Now she sat in a tent that was not a tent—or not only a tent—and waited to be brought before Vessia Marchmain, usurper of the Salt Marches, breaker of the border-forts at Kellin's Gap, the woman the free cities called the Red Cartographer behind closed doors and nothing at all aloud.

The tent's walls were hung with maps. Not the guild's maps—Ines knew those the way a priest knows scripture, every convention and deliberate omission—but something newer, rougher, drawn fast and revised faster. Whole regions appeared and vanished between versions pinned one over another. A coastline she recognized as the Weeping Reach existed in four different shapes, and in the oldest version it simply wasn't there at all, just blank vellum and a note in a sharp hand: *to be decided.*

"To be decided," Ines murmured. "As if the sea were waiting for permission."

"It is."

She turned too fast. Sarel stood in the entrance, backlit by grey morning, holding two cups of something steaming. Vessia's cartographer wore no uniform—just travel clothes and an air of terrible competence—and carried herself the way Halvard carried himself through the guildhall: as someone whose authority was so complete it had become invisible.

"You didn't sleep," Sarel said, offering a cup.

"Neither did you."

"I don't, much. Occupational hazard." She sat across from Ines without invitation, which Ines recognized as a courtesy; Sarel was treating her as a colleague, and colleagues didn't wait to be asked. "You looked at my maps."

"They were hanging on the walls of the room I'm imprisoned in."

"Billeted," Sarel corrected mildly. "You're billeted. There's a difference, and the difference is that you could walk out of this camp any time you liked and no one would stop you, because Her Majesty has ordered that no harm come to you." She blew steam from her cup. "Which should tell you something about what she wants."

"It tells me she wants something badly enough to be patient. That's worse."

Sarel smiled—the first genuine expression Ines had seen on her, and it made her look ten years younger and infinitely more dangerous. "You'll do well here, I think. Or very badly. With people like us it's usually the same thing."

*People like us.* Ines wrapped both hands around the cup and said nothing, because the cup was warm and her hands were not, and because anything she said would confirm something.

* * *

They came for her at midday, when the camp's noise had settled into its long afternoon grind of hooves and hammering. Two guards flanked her—not gripping, just present, the way weather is present—and Sarel walked beside her through rows of tents toward the largest structure at the camp's heart.

Ines had imagined a warlord's pavilion: furs, captured banners, the skulls of enemies arranged with theatrical taste. What she entered instead was a field office. Tidy. Lamplit even at noon. Desks with clerks bent over correspondence, and everywhere, everywhere, paper. Paper stacked, paper pinned, paper weighted under river stones brought from conquered rivers. Vessia ruled an empire of paperwork, and the paperwork was winning.

At the center, alone at a table barely larger than a writing desk, sat a woman reading.

Vessia Marchmain was smaller than the stories. The free cities had built her up into a colossus—red-haired, red-handed, seven feet of conquest—but the woman at the table was slight, greying at the temples, with the folded, economical posture of someone who had spent decades making herself take up less room than her ambition required. She wore plain wool. Her only ornament was a signet ring she turned absently as she read, around and around, like a woman worrying a rosary or counting something.

"Ines Pemberton," Vessia said without looking up. "Keeper of ledgers, Guild of Unmapped Realms. Third chair, audit division. Eleven years." Now she looked up, and her eyes were the color of old ice, and they were kind, and that was the worst thing Ines had seen in weeks. "You catalogued the compass of Saint Ferren's Reach in your fourth year. The one that points at what you've lost instead of north. I've read your entry. It's the finest piece of technical writing the guild has produced in a generation. You described an impossible object so precisely that for three pages it stopped being impossible."

Ines kept her voice level. "Your Majesty has read my work."

"I read everything." Vessia set down her papers. "Sit, please. Sarel, thank you. Leave us—no, stay. You'll want to hear this, and I'm tired of telling you twice."

Sarel took up a post by the map-hung wall. Ines sat. The chair was ordinary. Everything about the scene was engineered to be ordinary, and Ines understood suddenly that this was the performance: not grandeur, but reasonableness. Vessia wanted to seem like the only sane person in a mad world.

"Do you know why I crossed the Salt?" Vessia asked.

"The Salt Marches were undefended after Kellin's Gap. Any strategist—"

"No." Vessia's voice didn't sharpen; it *simplified*, the way a blade simplifies a knot. "Why does anyone cross a desert? For what's on the other side. And what's on the other side of the Salt is the free cities, and beyond them, the guild that pretends it isn't an empire of its own." She turned the ring again. Around, around. "You think I want to burn Corvane. You think I want ash and widows and a generation of graves. Everyone thinks that. It's very convenient for everyone that they think that, because it lets them hate me cleanly instead of listening."

"Then listen I will," Ines said, "if Your Majesty wishes to be heard."

Something flickered across Vessia's face—surprise, quickly mastered, and beneath it something almost like relief. "You sound like a ledger entry. 'Received, the following statement.' Good. Ledgers don't flatter. Sit there and receive, then."

She rose, went to the wall of maps, and pulled down the topmost layer with both hands, letting it fall in a heap. Beneath it: a single sheet, enormous, older than everything else in the tent. The vellum had gone the color of weak tea. The ink had faded to brown. But Ines knew the hand immediately—knew it the way you know a face in a dream, a face you've never seen but somehow recognize.

It was the prophecy's hand. The century-old script from the sealed ledger. The words written beside tonight's date and her own birth-name.

"This was recovered from the guild's own archive," Vessia said quietly. "Bought, before you ask. Twelve years ago, from a junior cartographer who wanted money more than he wanted his order's trust. He's dead now—unrelated, I assure you, though I understand why you'd wonder." She smoothed the great sheet flat. "Do you know what this is?"

"A forgery," Ines said. "Or a theft. Either way, it isn't yours."

"It's a promise." Vessia traced one line of faded text with a fingertip, not touching the ink. "A hundred years ago, the last empire fell. Not to armies—to exhaustion. To the simple arithmetic of war: eventually you run out of sons. My grandmother's grandmother watched it happen from the wrong side of a burning harbor, and she told my grandmother, who told my mother, who told me: *never again make people die for a border.* Borders are lines. Lines are drawings. Drawings can be redrawn." She looked up. "You keep ledgers, Keeper. Tell me what a border actually is."

"A line two groups of armed men agree to bleed along," Ines said. "Until they don't."

"Exactly. Exactly." Vessia's voice warmed with something dangerously close to delight. "You see it. Most people never see it—they think borders are real the way rivers are real. But you, of all people, know better. You've *catalogued* things that prove better. So tell me: if a border is a shared belief enforced by blood, what happens when the belief changes?"

Ines thought of the undercroft. Seven voices in the dark below the guildhall, singing geography into new shapes. She thought of Tam's fragment of song, and the river bending obediently away from its bed, and the way the water hadn't seemed forced—it had seemed *persuaded*.

Her stomach turned over slowly, like a body in deep water.

"No," she said.

"Yes," Vessia said gently. "You've seen it. I can tell. Don't insult us both by denying it." She came around the table and stood over Ines, close enough that Ines could smell lamp oil and clean wool and, faintly, ink. "I don't want the Choir to destroy the free cities, Keeper. Anyone can destroy a city. I've read enough history to be bored by it. I want the Choir to *sing* them. Sing Corvane into understanding that it was always part of something larger. Sing the harbor lords into remembering their grandmothers' stories. Sing the border-garrisons into going home to their mothers, whole and unbled, because the line they were dying along simply—" she snapped her fingers, softly, "—isn't there anymore. No battle. No siege. No widows. The gentlest conquest in human history, and the last one, because after it there will be no lines left to argue about."

"And the people inside those cities?" Ines heard her own voice go thin. "Do they get to decide whether they want to be sung?"

Vessia tilted her head, genuinely considering the question, which was somehow more horrifying than dismissal. "Decide? They decide every day, Keeper, and look what they decide: tariffs that starve the Salt Marches while their granaries rot. Councils that debate for a decade while bandits take the roads. Guilds that sit on miracles for a hundred years rather than share them." She gestured at the great vellum. "Your own order found this prophecy and buried it. Found *them*—the Choir—and locked them under the floor. Is that freedom? Is that consent? At least I'm honest about what I intend. Your guild hides its chains in a basement and calls itself humble."

Every word landed where it was aimed. That was the horror of it—not the army outside, not the maps of cities yet unconquered, but the accuracy. Ines thought of Halvard's fluent lies to the buyer. Of the council's vote still to come, or already taken. Of the doctrine of invisibility recited like scripture: *we record, we do not act.* Survival bought with silence. She had written the phrase herself once, in a margin, as praise.

"The binding hurts them," Ines said. "Did your stolen page mention that? Did your dead informant describe what a century of singing on command does to a spirit? They aren't instruments. They're prisoners. And you want to march them from city to city like a—like a medicine show, dosing populations with consent they never gave."

"I want to end war," Vessia said, and for one unguarded moment her voice cracked with something real—grief, or its fossil. "Do you know how many died at Kellin's Gap? Four hundred. Do you know how many would have died taking Corvane by storm? Forty thousand, by Sarel's estimate, and Sarel is never wrong about numbers." She nodded toward the wall; Sarel didn't move, but her eyes closed briefly, confirming. "Forty thousand mothers' sons. And you're asking me to weep for seven spirits who have been suffering anyway, under your guild's floor, for a hundred years, accomplishing *nothing*. At least under me their pain buys peace. Under your guild it buys a filing system."

"That's not—"

"It is exactly what it is." Vessia returned to her chair and sat, folding her hands, reasonable again, terrible again. "Here is what happens now. You will return to your guild—I will release you myself, today, with an escort to the gates of your precious hall. You will tell them what I've told you. And when they vote—as they will vote, because institutions always choose the filing cabinet over the conscience—you will bring me the Choir. Or you will bring me yourself, Keeper, because the prophecy names a Voice, and I have read enough of your handwriting to believe the page might mean you."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then forty thousand people die at Corvane in the spring, conventionally, messily, and their blood is on the ledger of whoever chose pride over arithmetic." Vessia smiled, small and cold and sincere. "I am offering you mercy, Ines Pemberton. Mercy with a deadline. It's the only kind that ever works."

* * *

They let her walk back to her billet alone. Sarel fell into step beside her halfway there, silent until the tents thinned and the camp's noise dulled to a background hum.

"Well?" Sarel said finally.

"She's right," Ines said, and hated the words as they left her mouth. "That's the trap of her. Every word of it is true, or true enough. The guild did bury the Choir. The councils are cowards. War is arithmetic." She stopped walking. "And none of it matters, because she's describing the rape of a mind and calling it mercy. Consent that's sung into you isn't consent. Peace that's imposed from underneath the skin isn't peace. It's just a prison with no visible walls—which, I notice, is exactly what she'd say the free cities already are."

Sarel studied her for a long moment. "You know," she said, "I drew the maps for Kellin's Gap. Four hundred dead, and I drew the ground they died on, precise to the pace. I've done the arithmetic every night since. And I still can't make it come out clean." She glanced back toward the command tent, where lamplight was already kindling against the afternoon. "So I'll tell you something I shouldn't. The page you saw—the prophecy. Her Majesty believes it commands her. *A Voice shall rise; the Choir shall answer; the lines shall forget themselves.* She reads it as permission. Destiny. But I've spent twelve years copying that document, and I've noticed something she hasn't, or won't."

"What?"

"It's not addressed to a conqueror." Sarel's voice dropped. "Read the grammar sometime. The prophecy doesn't say *a Voice shall be given to the Choir.* It says *a Voice shall come to free it.* Every line. Every tense. It's not a weapon's instruction manual, Keeper. It's a prisoner's letter—and someone a hundred years ago wrote it hoping that whoever finally read it would have the courage to do the opposite of what every reader so far has planned."

The ground seemed to tilt very slightly under Ines's feet. Somewhere across the camp, a farrier's hammer rang against iron, steady as a pulse.

"Why are you telling me this?"

Sarel was already turning away. "Because I draw maps," she said over her shoulder, "and a map is a claim, and I am so tired of claims." She paused at the edge of the lamplight. "Your escort leaves at dawn. Whatever you're going to do—run, warn them, steal her, sing to her—do it before then. After dawn you belong to the schedule, and Her Majesty's schedule does not include detours."

She was gone. Ines stood alone in the trampled grass between the tents, the great vellum's faded words burning behind her eyes, and understood at last what she was carrying back to the guildhall: not a warning, not a bargain, but a choice that had already been made a century ago by someone whose name the guild had buried with the rest of its sins.

*A Voice shall come to free it.*

Tam. She had to reach Tam. Tonight, before the escort, before the schedule, before Vessia's mercy arrived at the guild's gates wearing its reasonable face.

Ines turned toward the horse-lines and began, quietly, to plan a theft.
