## 14. Escape by Unmapping

Tam had spent six years learning to be small.

Small in the way a tuner learns: breath shallow, footsteps placed on the seams between floorboards, voice kept below the threshold where stone remembers sound. The undercroft had taught it first, and the roads of the free cities had refined the lesson. You survived by being less than a rumor. You survived by being nobody's entry in anybody's ledger.

So when Ines Pemberton came stumbling out of the Vessian supply camp at dusk with rope burns on her wrists and a scrap of song folded into her like a knife hidden in a boot, Tam's first instinct was the old one—shrink, vanish, let the tall woman with the haunted eyes walk past into the dark and become someone else's problem.

The second instinct was newer, and worse. It was the memory of Ines humming three notes beside the river and watching the water bend.

"They took my boots," Ines said, as if that were the important part. "And my coat. And they have Sarel's map of me—the one she drew while I was their prisoner. Which means somewhere in that camp there is a piece of paper that knows my face better than I do."

"Then we go around," Tam said. "There's a drover's track north of here. Two days to the marshes. They won't look for us off the road."

"They're not looking for us." Ines sat down hard on a fallen wall, and in the last grey light Tam saw how young her hands looked, and how old her eyes had gotten in four days. "They're looking for *me*, specifically. Sarel told them what I am. Or half of it. The scout captain was very polite about it. He said the Empress extends her protection to the Choir's Voice." A laugh with no humor in it, dry as old ink. "He said it like an offer. Like a title."

"And you said?"

"I said I'd think about it. Then I stole a horse, fell off the horse, and ran through a beet field until the shouting stopped." She pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. "I'm sorry. I'm so tired I can't even lie well anymore."

Tam crouched in the ditch beside the road and did the arithmetic they'd both been avoiding. The Vessian column was behind them, maybe three miles, moving fast because Sarel's maps made every road fast for them. Ahead lay two days of open ground and then the marsh country, and beyond that the guildhall, where Halvard was dying and the council was doing whatever councils did when they were frightened, which was nothing, slowly.

Two days was too slow. Three miles was too close. And Ines had a fragment of song in her chest that could move rivers.

"There's another option," Tam said, and hated the words before they finished leaving their mouth.

Ines lowered her hands. "No."

"You don't even know what—"

"I know what you're going to say. I've been saying it to myself since the camp. *Unmake the road.*" Her voice cracked on the word unmake. "Tam, when I hummed that fragment at the river, the river changed course. A river. Water. It wanted to move; I just asked it nicely. A road isn't like that. A road is—" She stopped. Swallowed. "A road has people on it."

"Yes," Tam said quietly. "It does."

* * *

Here is what the undercroft teaches you about song, and no one who hasn't stood inside the binding circle can understand it:

Every melody is a claim. Every harmony is an argument about what things are. The Choir sang borders out of existence once, whole mountain passes folded shut like letters, and the guild bound them because a world that can be rewritten should never be easy to rewrite. That was the doctrine. That was the catechism Tam had memorized at nine years old, kneeling on cold stone while Master Ellery explained why the seven spirits wore chains of notation instead of iron.

But the doctrine left something out. It left out what the song felt like from the inside. It left out that the spirits didn't sing to destroy anything. They sang to *ask*. And the world, most of the time, said yes.

The road south of Corvane was called the King's Mile, though no king had held this land in two hundred years. It ran straight as a ruled line across the flats—raised, gravelled, drained, the pride of some dead surveyor—and tonight it carried the vanguard of Vessia's army: forty riders, two supply wagons, and a scout captain with Sarel's map of Ines Pemberton in his breast pocket.

Tam watched them from the crown of a drainage embankment, flat on their belly in wet grass, and felt the old training settle over them like a coat. Breathe shallow. Be small. Be nothing.

Except that tonight, being nothing wasn't the plan. Tonight the plan was to teach Ines the rest of the fragment.

"It's not a spell," Tam had told her an hour before, sitting knee to knee in the dark while the Vessian campfires glowed on the horizon like a low second sunset. "Stop thinking of it as a spell. It's a question with a tune attached. The spirits used to say—you'd hear them through the walls—they used to say the world is mostly *maybe*. Stone is maybe solid. Distance is maybe far. The song just asks the maybe to consider other options."

"And if the world says no?"

"The world almost never says no. That's the horror of it, Ines. Not that it might refuse you. That it won't."

Now, lying in the grass, Tam heard Ines breathing beside them—too fast, too shallow, the breathing of someone standing at the edge of a high place. Below, the column moved at walking pace, torchlight bobbing, hooves crunching on gravel. Close enough to count. Close enough to see a young soldier yawning in the saddle, a boy no older than Tam had been when they climbed out of the undercroft through the coal chute and never went back.

"Don't look at them," Tam murmured. "Look at the road."

"The road," Ines repeated. Her voice was thin.

"The road is maybe straight. Maybe raised. Maybe *there*." Tam reached over and found her hand in the dark. Her fingers were ice. "You're not asking it to vanish. Nothing vanishes. You're asking it to stop agreeing to be a road. Just for a while. Just behind us."

"Just behind us," Ines whispered.

"And then we run, and we don't look back, and we never speak of it again except to each other, and only ever at night."

"That's not a plan, that's a penance."

"It's both. Everything worth surviving is both. Now breathe. Four counts in. The way I showed you. Let the note sit low, where you keep the names of people you've lost."

Tam felt rather than heard her begin. A vibration through their joined hands, faint as a pulse under skin. Then the air above the road changed texture—the way a room changes when someone opens a window in another part of the house—and the torch flames all leaned sideways at once, though there was no wind.

Three notes. Ines only had three notes of it, the stolen fragment Sarel had unknowingly sung to her in the prison tent, thinking herself clever, thinking the guild's music was a weapon you could aim. But three notes was enough for a question.

The gravel began to whisper.

Not loudly. That was the worst part, Tam thought later, lying sleepless a hundred miles away with the boy-soldier's yawn still stuck behind their teeth. It didn't scream or shudder or blaze with light. The road simply grew *uncertain*. The raised bed of it softened, sagged, spread, like a sentence losing its grammar mid-clause. The drainage ditches forgot which way was downhill. The gravel forgot it had been gravel, and became the thing it had been before some dead surveyor's crew had argued it into shape: dark soil, wet clay, the flat indifferent ground of a place where no one had ever decided to put a road.

The lead horses balked. Then the horses behind them. Somewhere in the column a man shouted—not alarm, confusion, the particular anger of a soldier whose map has stopped matching his feet. Torches swung. The scout captain's voice rose, clear across the flats: *"It was HERE. I rode it yesterday, it was HERE—"*

And it was true. It had been here. That was the cruelty of it, and Tam understood now why the guild had chained the Choir, understood it in their marrow: the song didn't kill anyone. It just took away the certainty that the world would stay agreed upon. And soldiers without certainty were worse than dead soldiers. Dead soldiers stayed where you put them.

"Enough," Tam breathed. "Ines. Enough. Let it settle."

She let it go all at once, gasping, and the three notes died in her throat, and the darkness where the King's Mile had been simply remained darkness—a stretch of formless ground, a mile long, holding forty riders and two wagons in a country that no longer contained any path out of it. Not permanently. By morning the road would remember itself; roads are stubborn, older than songs. But by morning Tam and Ines would be in the marshes, and the marshes had never agreed to be mapped by anyone.

They ran.

* * *

They ran until the campfires were gone and the stars came out mean and cold over the flats, and then they walked, and then Ines's legs gave out and they sat in the lee of a ruined milestone—one of the old kind, carved with distances that hadn't been true for centuries—and Ines put her head in her hands and shook.

Tam sat beside her and waited. This, too, was undercroft training: you learned to wait with people. The spirits had been waiting a hundred years.

"He was yawning," Ines said finally. "The young one. On the gray horse. He was just—he was riding along, tired, thinking about supper probably, and now he's standing in a field that doesn't have a road in it, in the dark, and he's going to stand there all night, and some of those men are going to panic, and panicked men with weapons—" Her breath hitched. "I didn't kill anyone. I keep telling myself that. I didn't kill anyone. I just made them lost."

"Lost kills," Tam said. "Sometimes. Slowly. You know that. I know that better than anyone—we were lost the day we climbed out of the chute, and it nearly finished us twice before we learned the cities."

"Then why aren't you telling me to stop? Why did you teach me the fragment at all?" Ines lifted her head. Her face was filthy, tear-streaked, furious. "You of all people. You were *inside* the binding. You know what that song cost the ones who sang it. Why hand it to me like a torch?"

Because, Tam thought, because the alternative was watching Vessia's army arrive at the guildhall gates with maps that made every distance short and every border negotiable, and watch the council open the doors, and watch seven chained spirits get handed over like silver plate to a queen who would use them to sing the free cities into her empire without spilling a drop of her own blood. Because mercy weaponized was still a weapon, and someone had to hold the counterweight.

But what they said was: "Because you asked the road, not the men. Did you notice? The fragment—it's built so you can't help it. The song goes where you point your grief. You grieved for the road itself, for the idea of it, and that's why it only took the road." Tam pulled their knees up, wrapped their arms around them. "Sarel will point hers at armies. At cities. At everything that says no to her empress. That's the difference. That's the whole difference, and it's the only thing the guild never understood about the Choir—they thought the danger was the power. The danger was always the singer."

Ines was quiet a long time. Out on the flats, far behind them, a single torch flared and guttered—the scouts, perhaps, finding their bearings, or failing to. The milestone leaned over them both, its ancient numbers worn to ghosts.

"When we get to the guildhall," Ines said at last, "and I make the council listen—if they listen—I'm going to need more than three notes."

Tam looked at her. Small, filthy, exhausted, hands still trembling from the cost of a mile of road—and underneath all of it, steady as a plumb line, the thing the prophecy had named a century before anyone thought to check the date.

"I know all seven parts," Tam said. "I've known them since I was six. I used to hum them into the chains when no one was listening, and the spirits would go quiet, and I think—I think it was the only kindness anyone paid them in a hundred years." Their voice caught, and they let it. "So yes. When we get there, I'll teach you. All of it. And Ines—when you hear the seventh part, you'll understand why Halvard buried the composer's name. And you're going to wish he'd buried it deeper."

Ines turned to look at them fully. "What's in the seventh part?"

Tam stood, and offered her a hand up, and looked north toward the marshes and the distant smudge of the guildhall hills, where tomorrow—or the day after, if the marshes were kind—Halvard was dying, and the council was counting votes, and seven spirits waited in the dark beneath the stone, singing their hundred-year warning to no one.

"A name," Tam said. "Come on. We've got a swamp to cross before the road remembers itself."

They walked north together through the unmapped dark, two small figures on ground that had forgotten how to be a road, carrying between them a song that knew the way.
