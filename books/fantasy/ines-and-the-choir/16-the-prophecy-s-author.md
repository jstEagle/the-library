## 16. The Prophecy's Author

The guildhall smelled of smoke and old paper, and beneath both, faintly, of the tallow they burned when someone was dying.

Ines climbed the narrow stair to Halvard's chamber with the prophecy ledger pressed against her chest, its scorched cover still warm from the archive fire two nights past. The volume had survived because she had carried it out through flame and falling beam, because she had chosen it over the guild's founding charters, over the census of impossible things, over everything. She had told herself she saved it because it was evidence. She was no longer certain that was true. She had saved it because it was the only book in the guild's keeping that had ever said her name.

The door stood ajar. Candlelight leaked through in a thin gold line, and with it came the sound of breathing—wet, labored, the breath of a man whose lungs were filling with something they could no longer hold.

She pushed the door open.

Halvard lay in the narrow bed he had refused for three days, propped against pillows gone gray with age. He was seventy-one years old and had looked sixty for as long as she had known him; now, in a single night, he had overtaken his age and passed beyond it. His skin had the translucence of vellum held to a lamp. His hands, those precise hands that had taught her to draw a border so fine a customs officer would weep, lay folded on the coverlet like instruments retired from use.

"You came back." His voice was a ruin. "I told them you wouldn't."

"You told them I would. You told them I'd come back for the ledgers, and that I'd come back for you, and that both were predictable enough to plan around." She set the book on the table by his bed. "I've been listening at doors, Halvard. It's remarkable what this guild says about me when it thinks I'm gone."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth. Not quite a smile. The ghost of the expression he'd worn when she'd first caught an error in his own work, eleven years ago, a transposed coordinate in the map of the Glasswater Delta. *Good,* that expression had said. *You'll do.*

"They voted, then."

"They voted. Tomorrow. After Vessia's herald arrives." Ines pulled the chair to his bedside and sat, and found that her hands would not be still, so she pressed them flat against her knees. "They're going to give me to her. The Voice the prophecy demands, delivered with a bow and a bill of lading. I'm to be the guild's tribute, so the guild can go on being invisible."

"Yes." He said it without flinching, and that was somehow worse than denial. "I argued against it."

"You argued for it for a century. You bound them, Halvard. You kept them singing in the dark under our feet while we sold maps of the world they'd rewritten."

"I kept them *safe*," he said, and then coughed, a long terrible sound, and when it passed he was smaller somehow, as though the cough had carried something of him away with it. "No. That's the lie. That's the lie I've told so long I'd begun to believe the shape of it. Let me tell you the true one, Ines. I don't have the strength to tell it twice, and I find I don't want to die carrying it."

She should have said something. She should have told him to save his breath, or that she already knew, or that she had stopped needing his confession the moment she'd stood in the undercroft and felt the seven of them singing through the soles of her boots. But she was a keeper of ledgers, and a ledger is nothing but a machine for making people tell the truth, and she had spent her whole life waiting for this entry. So she said nothing, and took the prophecy ledger from the table, and opened it across her lap like a penitent with a psalter.

Halvard watched her hands on the cover. "You saved that one."

"It's the only honest book left."

"It isn't honest either." He drew a breath, and the breath seemed to cost him a coin he could not easily replace. "But it's the truest thing we have. Read me the passage. The one with your name. I want to hear it in your voice before I tell you what it is."

She found it by feel. She had read it so many times in the last weeks that the page opened under her thumbs without her looking. The ink was brown with age, the hand a fine, careful script that no guild scribe used—too individual, too alive, each letter leaning slightly as though walking into wind.

*When the salt is crossed and the crown is stolen twice, the Voice shall rise from the house of ledgers, and the Choir shall be asked what it was bound to answer. Woe to the ones who read this page as a summons, for it is a door, and doors open both ways.*

"Again," Halvard whispered. "The last line."

"*Woe to the ones who read this page as a summons, for it is a door, and doors open both ways.*"

"Yes." He closed his eyes. "Now. Who wrote it?"

"The guild's records say a scribe named Aldous Vey, first cartographer of the third founding. He died the year the Choir was bound. The council has always taught that he wrote the prophecy in the Choir's last hours of freedom, that he heard their song and saw the future in it—"

"The council has always taught a great many things." Halvard opened his eyes, and Ines saw that they were clear, terribly clear, the clarity of a man who had set down every weight he meant to carry no further. "Ines. There was no Aldous Vey."

The candle flame stood perfectly still. Somewhere below them, in the bones of the guildhall, the Choir was singing its endless, patient song, and Ines realized she could feel it in the floorboards, in the arms of the chair, in the roots of her teeth, as she had felt it every day of her life without ever once knowing what it was.

"There was no Aldous Vey," Halvard said again. "The name was invented forty years after the binding, by a council that needed an author. Because a prophecy needs an author, you understand. A prophecy with no author is just a voice in the dark, and a voice in the dark must be answered or ignored. But a prophecy with a name attached, a founding father, a revered dead man—that can be studied, interpreted, *governed*. We put a man's name on it the way you put a lock on a door."

"Then who wrote it?"

He turned his head on the pillow, slowly, and looked at her, and Ines felt the floor of her understanding tilt the way a ship's deck tilts when the water beneath it proves deeper than the charts said.

"Read the hand," he said. "Look at it. Not the words. The hand."

She looked. The fine script, the letters leaning into wind. She had catalogued ten thousand hands in her years as keeper; she knew what scribes' hands looked like, what forgers' hands looked like, what the hands of the dying looked like when they wrote against the pull of sleep. This was none of those. This was a hand that had never been taught to write by any human school. The letters were beautiful the way the undercroft had been beautiful—precise, harmonized, each stroke in perfect relation to every other stroke, and utterly without a self. No hesitation. No vanity. No *person*.

"No," she said.

"The ink predates the guild's founding charter by eleven years," Halvard said. "Tam's people tested it, before Tam's people were scattered. The page was written before the binding was finished. Before the Choir was *bound*, Ines. While they were still free. While they still had voices of their own."

The words went into her and kept going, falling through her like stones through deep water.

"They wrote it themselves."

"They wrote it themselves." He said it gently, the way you tell a child that the dog has died. "Seven spirits who could rewrite the world with a song, and who saw, in the last nights of their freedom, what was coming for them. They saw the guild. They saw the binding. They saw a hundred years of a war ended by theft, and they saw further—they saw Vessia, they saw the salt crossed, they saw a voice rise out of a house of ledgers. And so they wrote down what they saw, and they hid it in the one place they knew their jailers would keep it safe, because the guild keeps everything, Ines. That is our one virtue. We are a vault that mistakes itself for a conscience."

"But the last line. *Woe to the ones who read this page as a summons.* That's a warning."

"It is the whole of it. Everything else is description. The Choir looked into the current of things and wrote down what they saw, the way you'd write down the course of a river. But the last line—that was them *speaking*. That was seven voices screaming across a hundred years, trying to tell whoever found the page: this is not a commission. This is not your destiny. This is a door, and we are on the other side of it, and we are asking you to open it." His breath caught. "And we read it as a job posting."

Ines looked down at the page, and the page looked back, and every certainty she had carried out of the burning archive came apart in her hands like wet paper.

She had believed the prophecy was the guild's sin written down in advance—a record of what the powerful intended, disguised as fate. She had built her fury on that. The guild had known for a century that a Voice would come, had waited for her the way a spider waits, and had shaped her whole life toward this night: the ledgers given to her young, the undercroft discrepancies left where she would find them, the doctrine of invisibility drilled into her so deep that when the moment came she would be too well-trained to scream. That had been terrible enough. That had been a betrayal she could hold.

But this—

"They weren't predicting me," she said. Her voice came out strange, flattened. "They were *asking* me. A hundred years ago, before they were taken, they wrote down a plea and hid it in their own prison, and the guild found it and read a jailbreak as a coronation. They read *woe* as *instruction*."

"The council read it as permission," Halvard said. "That is the precise evil of it. Not that they misunderstood. That they understood perfectly, and chose the reading that let them keep what they'd taken. A warning that says *open the door* is inconvenient. A prophecy that says *a Voice will come* is useful. So they kept the useful half and buried the true half, and they buried the true half so deep that even the councilors who came after believed the lie, because the lie had been built into the walls before any of them were born." He coughed again, shorter this time, and when he could speak, his voice had dropped to something almost tender. "I found the truth when I was younger than you are. Forty years ago. There was a marginal note in Vey's supposed hand—a second hand, underneath, the way scribes sometimes write over palimpsests. I had it analyzed. I took the findings to the council. And do you know what they said?"

Ines could guess. She could guess with horrible precision, because she had sat in that chamber three nights ago and heard them vote.

"They said the guild's survival mattered more than the Choir's freedom."

"They said the guild's *credibility* mattered more than the Choir's freedom. Survival they never doubted—they were right not to; we are very hard to kill. But credibility, Ines. If the prophecy was written by the bound, then the guild's founding act was not the ending of a war. It was a kidnapping with a paperwork trail. And an order built on a kidnapping cannot confess without collapsing. So they chose the collapse that came slowly over the collapse that came at once, and they called it doctrine, and I—" His hand moved, an inch, across the coverlet, and Ines took it without thinking, and his fingers closed around hers with a strength that surprised them both. "And I stayed. That is my entry in the ledger. I found the truth and I stayed, and I told myself I could do more good from inside, and I taught you to keep the books of a prison for thirty years because I was too much a coward to burn them."

The candle guttered. In the walls, faint as a heartbeat under snow, the Choir sang on.

"Tam knew," Ines said. "Tam's people. The tuners. They knew the binding was wrong at its root."

"The tuners were the only ones who ever listened to the Choir rather than at it." Halvard's eyes had begun to lose their focus, but his grip on her hand did not. "That is why the guild scattered them, when the last of them started asking why the binding required grief to hold. Grief, Ines. Do you understand what that means? A binding that feeds on grief is not a cage. It's a wound being kept open on purpose. The Choir cannot be freed by force or by craft. Tam will have told you. It can only be freed by someone who answers the question they asked a hundred years ago."

"*What were you bound to answer,*" Ines quoted. "That's in the prophecy. *The Choir shall be asked what it was bound to answer.*"

"And the answer is in the last line, and it always was." Halvard's voice was fading now, each word set down like the last coin from a purse. "They were bound to answer to the guild. To the seal, and the council, and the doctrine. The door opens both ways, Ines. When you unbind them—and you will, I have watched you not-quite-decide it for three weeks, and it is the most hopeful thing I have seen in forty years—you will not be breaking a lock. You will be *answering*. You will be the first person in a century to give them back their own words and say: I read it as you wrote it. I read the woe. I am sorry."

Ines bowed her head over their joined hands. She had rehearsed this confrontation a hundred times on the road from Corvane—Halvard unmasked, Halvard condemned, her voice steady and cold as a customs seal. She had not rehearsed this: the jailer dying with her hand in his, asking nothing, offering the only thing he had left, which was the truth, unvarnished, unledgered, free.

"Why tell me now?" she asked. "Forty years, Halvard. Why now?"

"Because the council voted to give you to Vessia." His eyes found hers again, and in them was something she had never seen there before—not the careful warmth of a mentor, but grief, raw and unarchived. "And because I taught you, all those years, that the guild's invisibility was a virtue. That the world was safer with us unseen. I need you to know, before I go, that I was wrong, and that I knew I was wrong, and that knowing and saying are two different entries and I only ever made the first." He drew a breath that rattled. "You will be tempted, after tomorrow, to become what they made you. A symbol. A Voice. Vessia wants a weapon; the council wants a sacrifice; the free cities will want a saint. Refuse all three. You are a keeper of records, Ines Pemberton. Keep the record. That is all the prophecy ever asked of anyone. Someone to read it *right*."

"Halvard—"

"The Choir wrote the page," he said, and now his voice was barely a thread, and she leaned close to catch it, close enough to see that his lips were gray. "But they wrote it *hoping*. Hope has a hand too, if you look. It leans into the wind. Remember that when you sing. They were not a weapon, and they were not a warning. They were seven people who believed that someone, someday, would come down the stairs and *listen*—"

The thread broke.

Not dramatically. There was no last arch of the body, no cry. He simply stopped, the way a scribe stops when the sentence is finished, and his hand in hers went from gripping to resting, and the candle flame leaned as though a door had opened somewhere in the room, and did not right itself for a long moment.

Ines sat with him until the candle burned down to the socket. She closed his eyes. She straightened his hands on the coverlet, the precise hands, and folded them the way he had taught her to fold a finished ledger: left over right, sealed, complete.

Then she took up the prophecy, and read the last line one final time, aloud, in an empty room, to an audience of one dead man and seven imprisoned voices.

"*Woe to the ones who read this page as a summons, for it is a door, and doors open both ways.*"

"I read it right," she said quietly. "I'm sorry it took a hundred years."

Below her feet, so faint she might have imagined it, the song changed. One voice among the seven wavered—broke its long note, held a silence that had not been there for a century, and then resumed, and the resumed note was different. Not louder. Not wilder. Just *aware*, the way a sleeper's breathing changes when they know someone has entered the room.

They had heard. They had been listening at their own door for a hundred years.

Ines closed the ledger and stood. Her legs were steady. Her hands, for the first time in weeks, were still.

Tomorrow the council would vote, the herald would come, and the guild would try to hand her to Vessia like a parcel with her name written on it in a hand that was never human at all.

She climbed the stairs toward the council chamber, carrying the truest book in the guild, and began, in her head, to draft the entry no one had authorized her to write.
