## 17. Council of Knives

The summons came at dawn, folded twice and sealed with grey wax—the guild's color for matters that must never be spoken of outside a locked room. Ines had sealed a hundred such letters herself. She knew the weight of the wax, the precise angle of the fold, the way the paper was cut slightly narrower than ordinary correspondence so it would not be mistaken for anything that mattered. She had always found a certain comfort in that. The guild did its cruelest work in the smallest handwriting.

She dressed as if for an audit. Dark wool, no ornament, her hair pinned back in the severe knot she wore when numbers had to balance or when someone was about to die on paper. Tam watched her from the doorway of the map room, arms crossed, still half-asleep, still smelling faintly of lamp oil from the night they'd spent copying the prophecy by hand.

"You're going to tell them," Tam said. Not a question. "After everything."

"I'm going to *ask* them," Ines said. "There's a difference. It's small. It's the only difference I have left."

"And if they say no?"

"Then I'll know exactly what this place is." She straightened her collar. "I've spent thirty years suspecting. I'd rather know."

Tam's mouth twisted. "You already know. You watched them vote to burn their own archive rather than let Vessia read it. You carried Halvard out of a fire they set."

"I carried him out of a fire *someone* set," Ines corrected gently. "That's the guild way. No one sets fires. Fires simply occur, and ledgers are lost, and the loss is entered in a new ledger, and the new ledger is sealed, and the seal is wax, grey wax, and life goes on." She picked up the prophecy volume—her copy, the original now ash—and tucked it under her arm like a hymnal. "I'm going to put it on the table in front of them. All of it. Every name, every date, every sin. And then we'll see whether the Cartographers' Guild can read its own map."

Tam caught her sleeve at the door. "Ines. If they vote against you—"

"Then run," Ines said. "Don't be clever. Don't come back for me. You're the only person alive who knows how the binding comes apart, and if they take us both in the same room, Vessia wins without marching a single step." She looked at Tam's hand on her sleeve, at the chipped ink stains on the younger woman's knuckles, and something in her chest went tight and quiet. "Promise me."

"I promise," Tam said, and lied beautifully, and Ines pretended not to notice, because that too was the guild way: two people agreeing to disbelieve each other in writing.

* * *

The council chamber sat at the heart of the guildhall like a stone in a fruit, windowless, lit by twelve lamps that were trimmed to identical brightness every evening by a junior clerk whose entire job was symmetry. Around the long table sat nine chairs; seven were filled. Master Ondrey of the Salt Chapter, his face the color of old vellum. Mistress Kavell of Boundaries, who had once taught Ines that a border was merely a line everyone agreed to be afraid of. The twins Ferren and Ferro, who between them controlled the guild's entire network of couriers and who finished each other's sentences the way other people finished each other's debts. And four others whose names Ines knew intimately from forty years of minutes and could not, at this moment, have sworn to under oath—which she suspected was the point of them.

Halvard's chair stood empty at the head. They had not removed it. They had simply begun speaking around it, the way water speaks around a stone, and Ines understood that this was their mourning: not grief, but geometry.

She took the seat prepared for her at the table's foot. A clerk brought water she didn't touch. Master Ondrey called the session to order with the phrase he used for everything—"Let the record show"—and Ines felt the words settle over her like a net she had helped knot.

"Master Pemberton," Ondrey said. "You've asked to address this council on a matter of first importance. The floor is yours. The record will show you spoke freely and without coercion."

"The record will show whatever you write," Ines said, "which is rather the problem I've come to discuss."

A flicker passed along the table, the small collective flinch of powerful people hearing an inconvenient tone. She opened her satchel and laid the copied prophecy on the green felt, squaring its edges to the table's edge out of pure habit, and pushed it to the center where all of them could see it.

"A century ago," she began, "this guild bound seven spirits beneath our own foundations. We called it an act of peace. We wrote it down as an act of peace. We taught three generations of apprentices that the undercroft was flooded, unstable, haunted by nothing worse than damp. Meanwhile the Choir sang below us, and every border within two hundred miles bent toward the shape the guild preferred, and the free cities stayed free because we quietly made sure they would—not because anyone fought for them, but because we held the pen."

Kavell leaned forward. "Ines. This history is known to this council. We lived it. Some of us signed the binding orders."

"Then you'll have no trouble confirming the next part." Ines turned the volume to the page bearing tonight's date. "The Choir wrote this. Not a prophet, not a seer, not some mad archivist—the binding itself, dreaming upward through a century of stone. It named a Voice. It named me. And it gave us a choice, written plainly enough that even a clerk could read it: unbind the Choir and pay its price, or keep it bound until someone comes to take it. Someone is coming. Her name is Vessia, and she crossed the Salt with claim-maps in her baggage and a cartographer named Sarel drawing her empire one honest-looking line at a time."

"We know about Vessia," said Ferren.

"Do you?" Ines let the question sit. "Because here is what I learned in Corvane, and on the road, and in a Vessian camp with my hands tied: Vessia does not want to destroy the free cities. Destruction leaves ruins, and ruins make martyrs, and martyrs make wars that outlive empires. Vessia wants the cities to wake up one morning already hers—borders redrawn overnight, histories amended, resistance unmapped before it can find itself on any chart. She wants the Choir. And this council, right now, today, is deciding whether to give it to her."

Silence, of the particular quality the guild cultivated: silence as instrument, silence as weapon, silence as the sound a decision makes while pretending not to be made.

Ondrey spoke first, carefully. "No one is giving anything to anyone."

"No," Ines agreed. "What happened in Corvane was that the city council refused to act without proof, and the proof arrived as smoke, and the city fell anyway. I've seen how institutions die, Master Ondrey. They don't fall. They negotiate the terms of their surrender so gradually that each individual step seems reasonable, and then one morning there's a Vessian banner on the roof and everyone remembers having always intended it."

"You're describing us," said Kavell.

"I'm describing every ledger I've kept for forty years," Ines said. "Every entry where we chose the quiet thing over the true thing. Every impossibility we catalogued instead of confronted. Every buyer we turned away with a fluent lie while the real transaction happened downstairs. I am the guild's memory, and I am telling you what the memory says." She turned another page. "It says we taught ourselves that survival bought with silence was a bargain. It isn't. It's just corruption with better bookkeeping."

Ferro shifted in his seat. "And your alternative? Unbind the Choir. Pay its price. Do you even know the price?"

"Yes."

"Tell the council."

"It takes names," Ines said. "Memories. Futures. Each spirit freed demands payment from the hand that frees it. I've spoken with someone who was bound to the tuning of that song for six years, and who has been paying installments ever since. The price is real. It is terrible. And it is *ours* to pay—not Vessia's, not the free cities', ours, because we are the ones who chained them in the dark and then wrote the chains up as peace."

"And if we refuse?" Ondrey asked. "If the council concludes—as councils do—that the risk of unbinding outweighs the certainty of it, that the prophecy may be misread, that your informant may be mistaken, that the prudent course is to open negotiations with Vessia's envoys and secure terms—"

"Terms." Ines laughed once, briefly, without any humor reaching her eyes. "You want to negotiate with a woman who eats cities whole. Fine. Let me save you the postage. I met her envoy. I met her cartographer. I sat across a campfire from Sarel and listened to her offer me the new world, drawn fair and square, and I understood that the offer was real—that's the horror of it. Vessia's people don't lie. They *amend*. They'll give this guild a charter, a stipend, a wing of the imperial archive with our name over the door. And in exchange they'll ask for one item. A single line item. Small. Reasonable. Easily overlooked in a long treaty."

She let her voice drop, and the lamps seemed to dim with it.

"They'll ask for the Voice."

* * *

The debate that followed lasted four hours, and Ines sat through all of it the way she had once sat through audits of shipping manifests for goods that had never existed: patient, precise, taking notes no one would ever read.

Ferren argued precedent: the guild had survived a century by never acting first. Ferro argued arithmetic: seven spirits, unknown cost, versus a negotiated settlement of known terms. Kavell argued—gently, and therefore most dangerously—that Ines herself might be mistaken, that grief for Halvard and fury at the burning had addled her judgment, that no reasonable reading of the prophecy required the guild to disarm itself on the eve of invasion. One by one, the four quiet members found their voices, and their voices all said versions of the same sentence: *someone else should bear this.*

Only one voice surprised her. Master Ellery-Doone, ancient, half-blind, keeper of the deep archive since before Ines was apprenticed—a man she had assumed was furniture. He spoke for perhaps ninety seconds total across four hours, and every second of it was this:

"I knew the author of the binding order," he said, in the third hour. "I was a junior clerk when the signatures were gathered. I watched them sign. And I have spent fifty years telling myself that what I watched was necessity." He paused, feeling for the edge of the table. "Miss Pemberton came to me last winter asking after a discrepancy in the 1189 folios. I told her it was clerical echo. I told myself the same. I would like the record to show that I no longer believe either statement, and that I intend to vote as though I never said them."

The record, Ines noted dryly in her margin, showed nothing of the kind.

They recessed at midday for bread and salted fish none of them ate, and reconvened at the turn of the afternoon, and Ines felt the room's weather change the moment the doors closed again. Something had been decided during the bread. Decisions in this building were never made at the table; the table was where they were performed. She had drafted the minutes of enough performed decisions to know the choreography: the long speeches first, for the record, then the short silences, then the vote, arranged in advance, unanimous or nearly, so that no single name could later be carved onto any monument of blame.

Ondrey rose. "Before the vote," he said, "the council has directed me to put a proposal to you directly, Master Pemberton, in the interest of—"

"Don't," Ines said.

"—in the interest of resolving this matter without schism." He continued smoothly, unruffled, a man who had delivered bad news to better people than her. "Vessia's envoys have indicated, through channels this council has maintained for some months, that the empire's demand is narrow. They require the Voice named in the prophecy. They have further indicated that the identity of that Voice is, to them, a matter of some flexibility."

Ines went very still.

"What they require," Ondrey went on, in the tone of a man reading shipping weights, "is a fulfillment. A person, delivered, who answers to the description. The prophecy names Ulyana Pemberton. But prophecies, as this guild has documented across eleven centuries, are notoriously elastic instruments. What cannot be elastic is the delivery. The free cities survive. The guild survives. The Choir remains bound, intact, transferable at a future date under terms more favorable than conquest. And the cost—one life, one member, already named by forces beyond our control—is contained."

He looked at her across the green felt, and his face did not change, and that was the worst of it. There was no cruelty in it. There was bookkeeping.

"Ines," he said. "The council is prepared to offer you passage. Safe conduct to the Vessian lines. An escort. A written guarantee—witnessed, sealed—that the Choir will not be surrendered during your lifetime. You would go to Vessia as what you have always been: the keeper of the guild's secrets, carrying this last one where it has to go. And history, which is only the longest ledger, would record that the Cartographers' Guild bought a century of peace with a single entry."

The chamber waited. Seven faces, and the empty chair, and the twelve identical lamps.

Ines thought of Halvard, dying upstairs, teaching her with his last lucid hour that the prophecy was the Choir's own voice crying out through the stone—*a warning they chose to read as permission.* She thought of Tam, somewhere above her, promised escape and refusing it. She thought of the compass she had catalogued on her first day, thirty years ago, the needle that pointed at what you wanted instead of north, and how she had written in the margin, in her young neat hand: *harmless, if the holder wants good things.* She had believed that once. She had built a life inside that belief, shelf by shelf, folio by folio, and the belief had held her weight all this time, and now, in this room, she heard it crack down the middle like ice in spring.

"You're voting to hand me to her," she said. Not asking. Entering it into the record herself, because no one else would.

"We are voting on a proposal," Ondrey said, "which you are free to accept."

"Which changes nothing about what it is." She stood. Her legs were steady; forty years of standing at attention before men reading numbers aloud had trained them well. "Let the record show, then. Let it show everything. That the Guild of Unmapped Realms, faced with a choice between its soul and its survival, convened a council, heard the truth read aloud from its own stolen book, deliberated four hours, and voted—"

"The vote hasn't occurred."

"—voted, in every way but the formality, to purchase peace with a woman's name. Mine. Entered cleanly. Balanced to the column. Witnessed and sealed." She picked up the prophecy and held it against her chest. "I have kept this guild's ledgers for thirty years. I know what a clean entry looks like. This is the cleanest one you will ever make, and it will stink for a hundred years."

Ellery-Doone raised his hand before any vote was called. His voice shook, and he let it. "I vote no."

Kavell, after a silence, raised hers for the proposal. Then Ferren. Then Ferro. Then the four quiet ones, in sequence, like a row of lamps going out. Ondrey did not vote at all; the master of a session abstains, and abstention, Ines wrote in her margin, is the guild's favorite vote, the one that leaves no ink.

Six to one. Carried.

"Master Pemberton," Ondrey said, "you will be escorted to your quarters. Preparations will begin at once. The escort departs within the day."

* * *

They walked her out through the archive wing, past the scorched skeleton of the burned stacks, past clerks who would not meet her eyes and two who did—one of them a girl of maybe nineteen, an apprentice, who mouthed something at Ines as she passed. Ines read the shape of it without sound: *run.*

Her escort was courteous. That was the detail she would carry longest, she thought, as they climbed the stairs toward the residential wing—two juniors she had trained herself, walking beside her with the careful politeness of men moving furniture that happens to breathe. Corwin, who had a wife and a mortgage of guild-debt he'd never escape. Pell, who still thanked her for correcting his shorthand three years ago. Good boys. Obedient boys. Boys who would stand outside her door tonight and feel terrible about it in the morning, and enter the feeling nowhere, because feelings were not entries.

At her door, Corwin paused. "Master Pemberton. Is there... anything you need?"

"Yes," Ines said. "A pen. And an hour."

They gave her both. Of course they did; what harm could a pen do, to a woman already sold?

She sat at her own desk, in her own room, with the light going amber through the one narrow window, and she drew a fresh sheet of guild paper toward her, and she began to write. Not a plea. Not a confession. A ledger entry—the last one, the truest one, the kind the guild had taught her to make and never once let her make honestly:

*Item: one Voice, per prophecy, delivered to Vessia by arrangement of the Council, six votes in favor, one against, one abstained. Price: to be determined by the buyer. Condition: willing. Notes: the Council believes itself to have purchased peace. The Council has purchased time. These are different commodities, and the difference will appear on someone's books eventually, and it will not be mine.*

She signed it. Then, beneath the signature, in the space where the guild's forms required a witness, she wrote a single word: *None.*

Below that, smaller, pressed hard enough to bite through the paper, she wrote the thing she actually needed to write—the message that mattered, the one she would leave where only one person in the world would think to look:

*Tam. They voted. Six to one. Escort at dusk, south road, four riders, Corwin and Pell among them—they'll hesitate if you show them this page. The undercroft stair behind the 1189 folio case is unwatched; the council thinks the Choir is their bargaining piece and won't post guards on a door they mean to sell. I'm not going to the south road. I'm going down. Come with me or don't, but if you don't, take the prophecy east and give it to whoever's still free to read it. Thirty years I kept their secrets. Tonight I'm keeping mine. —U.P.*

She folded the page around the ledger entry, sealed it with the last of her grey wax, and set it on the desk where a certain runaway tuner, trained her whole life to notice what institutions tried not to show, would find it.

Then she took off her shoes—for silence, for old times' sake, for the girl who had learned to walk unheard through these corridors—and went out into the corridor, past Corwin's post while he looked the other way with the disciplined blindness she had taught him herself, and down the back stair, and into the long dim throat of the guildhall, toward the folio cases and the hidden door and the seven voices waiting in the dark below, singing still, after a hundred years, the song that had named her.

Above her, the council was composing the letter to Vessia's envoys. Below her, the stone grew cold under her bare feet.

Somewhere between the two, Ines Pemberton stopped being theirs.
