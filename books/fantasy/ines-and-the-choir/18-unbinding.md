## 18. Unbinding

The undercroft door had three locks, and Tam had picked them all before, years ago, with a bone sliver and hands that wouldn't stop shaking. Tonight the locks were already broken. The guild council's seal lay split on the flagstones, and beyond it the stairs went down into a darkness that breathed.

"They're going to hand them over," Ines said behind Tam. Not a question. She'd heard the vote herself. She'd stood in that marble room while seven old men and women decided, with the tidy arithmetic of ledgers, that seven bound spirits were cheaper than one city. "We have until the gates open."

"Then we work fast." Tam started down. The walls sweated. The stone here was old in the way that stone gets when it has been sung to for a hundred years—grained, resonant, faintly warm under the palm. Tam had been a child the last time, small enough to slip through the maintenance galleries with the tuner's tools, small enough that no one thought to count the apprentices. Small enough to hear the Choir and live.

The stairs ended at the great door of the vault, and the vault door was open, and inside, the Choir was singing.

Not aloud. That was the horror of it, the thing Tam had never found words for. The song happened somewhere behind the ears, in the marrow, in the wet dark of the body. Seven voices braided into one chord that held the world's seams together in this one buried room—and held them *wrong*, held them the way a fist holds a bird. Tam's knees went soft. Ines caught the elbow.

"You've heard it before."

"Every night since I left," Tam said. "That's the problem. You don't stop hearing it. It doesn't stop hearing *you*."

* * *

The vault was a sphere of worked stone, and the Choir hung at its center in a cage of notation.

That was the only honest way to describe it. Seven figures of light and smoke, each wrapped in lines of silver script that circled them like the rings of a slow planet, and the script was music—actual music, staves and clefs and rests, a century of composition turned into chain. The spirits turned within their bindings, and where their light touched the walls, the stone remembered being other places. A stretch of granite rippled and became, for a heartbeat, the white cliffs of the Salt Marches. Another seam showed a forest that had been cut down before Tam's grandmother was born.

Seven of them. Tam counted the way you count your own heartbeats, needing the number to be true.

"They're awake," Ines whispered.

"They're always awake. That's the sentence." Tam lifted the tuner's kit—the real one, recovered from a cache under the apprentice bunks, brass tines and bone wands and a tuning fork of black metal that hummed even in a closed box. "A binding isn't a wall. It's a song played *at* them, forever, so loud they can't hear themselves. To unbind, you have to let them hear themselves. One at a time. And they have to agree to go."

"Agree? They're prisoners. Of course they'll agree."

Tam set the black fork against the first stave-ring and felt it answer, a shiver up the arm like a struck bell heard through the teeth. "No. Listen to me, Ines. A binding this old—when you cut it, everything it held comes out at once. A century of held breath. The spirit comes free, but the *price* comes free too. The binding was paid for when it was made. Somebody paid. And when the chain breaks, the debt finds the nearest hand."

"Whose hand?"

Tam looked at the fork, at the seven turning lights, at Ines Pemberton with her ink-stained fingers and her face that had learned to show nothing. "Ours. Whoever's closest. Whoever's willing."

Ines was quiet for the length of one full turn of the nearest spirit in its cage. Then: "Which one first?"

* * *

The first was the smallest.

It hung low in the vault, its light the color of a candle behind paper, and its binding was the thinnest of the seven—a single stave, a lullaby's worth of notation. Tam knew the tune. Every tuner learned it first. It was the song guild mothers sang to guild children, the one about the cartographer who wandered so far he mapped himself home.

Tam set the fork against the first bar.

The note that came out was not a note. It was a door opening in the middle of the body. The stave-ring shivered, brightened, and began to unravel from the end like thread pulled from a hem, and the little spirit inside it stopped turning and *looked* at them—no eyes, but a looking, a pressure of attention like the moment before lightning.

It spoke without sound, straight into the marrow, the way the song came.

*Price,* it said.

Tam's hands were steady. Tam had practiced this steadiness for six years in attics and ditches, tuning stolen fragments of stolen song, and now the steadiness was worth something. "Name your price," Tam said aloud, because the spirits answered speech better than thought, and because Ines needed to hear the shape of the bargain. "The binding was paid once. Break it and the payment comes due again. What do you want?"

The spirit drifted closer. Its light touched Tam's face, and Tam felt it reading—down through the skin, the muscle, the practiced blankness, into the shelves where a person keeps what they are made of.

*You were a tuner,* it said. *You tuned the third voice when it faltered. You were seven.*

"I was seven," Tam agreed. The memory surfaced whole: the undercroft, the faltering chord, a child's hands on the brass tines, an old tuner's voice saying *gentle, gentle, you're holding a life.* Tam had thought, even then, that the spirit had been grateful.

*A memory,* the spirit said. *Give me a memory, and I will go.*

"Which memory?"

The light pressed closer, gentle as a hand. *The one where you were kind to me. I will need it, where I'm going. It is the only proof I was ever anything but a chain.*

Tam understood then what the price was. Not a theft. A *trade*. The spirit would carry the memory out of the world, and the world would go on without it, and Tam would be a person who had never knelt in the dark and been kind. The kindness would exist only inside a freed thing, traveling away forever.

"Do it," Tam said, before the steadiness could fail.

The spirit touched Tam's brow. There was no pain. There was an *absence*, a single clean tooth pulled from the smile of a life—and then the little light rose, and the last thread of its binding snapped with a sound like a plucked string, and it was through the ceiling, through the stone, through the world, gone. The vault rang. The remaining six spirits turned faster.

Ines was gripping the workbench. "What did it take?"

"A memory." Tam found that the sentence came out level, which was its own kind of horror. "I don't know which one. That's the design of it. You pay, and you can't even mourn properly, because you can't find the grave."

Ines crossed the floor and took Tam's hand, ink-stained and shaking now that it was allowed to shake. "Six more."

"Six more," Tam said. "And they get more expensive. The little ones were bound with little songs. The deep ones—" Tam looked up at the center of the vault, where the largest spirit turned inside rings upon rings of notation, a century of the guild's best composers folded into a cage. "The deep ones were bound with whole lives. They'll want whole lives back."

* * *

The second wanted a name.

It was a river-spirit, or had been, before the guild caught it and taught it to be a border instead. Its binding was three staves deep, and when Tam cut the outer two, the vault flooded with the smell of water and the sound—everywhere, inside the ribs—of a current that no longer existed anywhere in the world, because the guild had sung the river straight and the straightening had killed it. The spirit turned in its last ring and considered them with the patience of water.

*A name,* it said.

"Whose name?" Ines asked. She had stopped whispering. Tam noticed that. Somewhere between the first spirit and the second, Ines Pemberton had decided that the undercroft deserved a full voice.

*The name you were given,* the spirit said, and its attention settled on Ines like a stone settling in a streambed. *Ulyana. The name your mother spoke over you. The name no ledger holds. Give it to me, and I will carry it down to the dead river, and the river will remember it, and somewhere a water will rise that answers to it.*

"No." Ines said it fast, and then stood in the wreckage of her own refusal. Tam watched her do the arithmetic—Tam, who had been raised on this arithmetic, who knew the guild's grammar of cost better than any of the council. *Ulyana* was the name under the prophecy. The name Vessia's spies would be hunting. The name the guild council had voted to hand across the walls like a sack of coin. Giving it away was a wound and a shield at once.

"It's a fair price," Tam said quietly. "It's the fairest one yet."

"I know it's fair. That's why it's hard." Ines closed her eyes. When she opened them, she was the ledger-keeper again, and she spoke the way she wrote, in a hand that could not be unwritten. "Ulyana. My name is Ulyana Pemberton. My mother chose it because it means *strong winter*. Take it. Let it do some good somewhere that isn't a cage."

The spirit took it the way water takes a dropped coin—swiftly, wholly, without cruelty. The last ring broke. The light poured downward, through the floor, and for a moment the whole vault smelled of snowmelt, and then it was gone, and Ines stood in the ringing silence with her hand pressed flat over her sternum as if checking that something still lived there.

"What is it?" Tam asked. "What does it feel like?"

"Like a room in my house is gone," Ines said. "I can see where the walls were. I can see the marks on the floor where the furniture stood. But the room is just—not." She lowered her hand. "Five more. Ask them quickly. I don't want time to think."

* * *

The third wanted a future.

It was the oldest of the low bindings, a hearth-spirit, and it had been a hearth once for a whole city that no longer appeared on any map because the guild had sold the map and burned the city's claim to itself. Its cage was four staves deep, and cutting the outer three took an hour and both of Tam's tuning forks and left Tam's fingers blistered and the black fork cracked down the middle, singing a quarter-tone flat.

When the last ring hung by a single bar, the hearth-spirit came close to them both, warm as an oven door, and said: *A future. One of yours. I will take the years you have not yet lived and burn them for light on my way home. I have been cold a hundred years.*

Tam looked at Ines. Ines looked at Tam.

"I'll pay," they said, both of them, at the same moment, and then stood glaring at each other in the middle of a buried vault while a spirit waited to be fed.

"You've paid twice already," Ines said.

"And you gave up your *name*. Do you understand what that is? The prophecy named you. Vessia is marching on a name. You just handed the only true one you had into a river bed." Tam's voice cracked on the last word, and Tam hated it, the old training failing at the worst hour. "You don't get to spend yourself twice while I stand here holding the tools."

"It's not spending, it's—" Ines stopped. Pressed her lips together. Started again, slower, the way she talked to the council when they were being stupid. "Tam. The song at the end. The final one. You told me what it costs. It takes the singer's name. I have no name now. Do you understand? I already paid the toll. Whatever the Choir takes from me at the end, it cannot take the thing that matters, because I gave the thing that matters to a river." She smiled, and it was the worst thing Tam had ever seen, that smile: calm, bookkeeperly, certain. "I'm the cheap one now. You're the expensive one. So you stand behind me, and you keep your futures, because I am going to need you to remember."

The hearth-spirit waited, patient as embers.

Tam wanted to argue. Tam had a future, actually—had begun, in the stolen years, to build one, a small one, a tuner's shop in a free city, honest work, a door that locked from the inside. The spirit's light lay against that future like a hand on a book, waiting to be given it.

"Take the years after the war," Tam said at last. "Only those. The ones I'd have lived in peace. Take those, and leave me the fight."

The spirit considered. *A hard bargain,* it said, almost gently. *But I was a hearth. I know what it is to keep only enough fire to get through the night.* It touched Tam's brow, and Tam felt it—not pain, not absence, but a *closing*, like a door at the end of a corridor, like a road that now simply ended where it had once gone on. The years after the war. Gone. Whatever Tam survived to, Tam would survive to nothing past it, a life with a wall at the end of the garden.

The last bar broke. The hearth-spirit rose through the stone trailing warmth, and for one breath the whole undercroft was a kitchen, was a city, was home, and then the dark came back, and four spirits turned in the ringing dark.

* * *

The fourth was the first of the deep ones.

It hung high in the vault's crown, and its binding was not staves but a *score*—a full composition, pages of it, the life's work of some guild composer a century dead who had built his masterpiece as a cage. Tam stood beneath it with the cracked fork and felt the size of the thing the way you feel the size of a wave by the shadow it casts.

"It will want more than a memory," Ines said. "More than a name. More than years."

"It'll want something it can't name until it sees us." Tam's hands were blistered; the fork hummed its flat, wounded note. "That's how the deep ones work. Halvard told me once—he was drunk, he thought I was asleep—that the last binding can't be paid by the person who opens it. It has to be *witnessed*. Paid by everyone who benefits. That's why they never unbound them, Ines. Not only cowardice. The arithmetic. The whole guild, the whole *world*, owes the debt, and no one wants to be the hand that collects."

Above them, the great spirit turned, and its light swept the vault, and where it swept, the walls showed not places but *people*—faces in the stone, hundreds of them, everyone the Choir's song had ever moved or mended or unmade. Ines's face was among them. Tam saw it: a woman made of ink and silence, standing in a buried room, choosing.

The spirit spoke, and its voice was not one voice. It was the chord itself, all seven minus three, speaking together.

*We know why you come,* it said. *The usurper comes for us, and the jailers would sell us, and you two would rather break us than see us bartered. We accept. But hear the price before you cut the fourth ring, hearth-tuner and nameless clerk. The fourth ring was bound with a vow. The vow must be answered with a vow.*

"Name it," Ines said.

*When we are free—when all of us are free—we will sing. The last song. And the one who joins us must mean it, must give it wholly, must not hold back one thread of herself to survive the singing. We will not be freed into a world of people who flinch. Swear it. Both of you. Swear that you will not flinch.*

The vault was silent except for the turning. Tam looked at Ines. Ines looked at the spirit, and Tam watched her understand it fully—the whole shape of it, the end of the road she had already half-chosen when she gave her name to the river—and watched her not flinch now, at the threshold, which was the only moment that mattered.

"I swear," Ines said. "I will not flinch."

And Tam, with the wall at the end of the garden and the memory-grave that could not be found and six years of learning to disappear, heard herself say: "I swear. None of us flinch."

The cracked fork rose. The fourth ring's last bar waited under it like a held breath.

And from far above—through the stone, through the vault, faint as a pulse—came the sound Tam had been dreading all night: the groan of the guildhall's great gates, beginning, hinge by hinge, to open.
