## 19. The Siege of the Guildhall

The sound of the gates opening reached the undercroft as a long, low groan, like something ancient being asked to betray itself one more time.

Ines felt it in her teeth before she heard it with her ears. She had spent eleven years in this building; she knew its voice the way she knew her own handwriting. The gates were bronze over oak over iron, and they had not moved for anything less than a coronation or a funeral in a hundred years. They were moving now.

"They're letting them in," Tam said. Not a question. Her hands did not stop working—she had the fourth ring's last bar half-lifted, the cracked fork wedged beneath it like a lever under the world's smallest, most terrible door. "The council is *opening the gates*."

"Of course they are." Ines heard her own voice come out flat and precise, ledger-voice, the voice she used when the numbers refused to be kind. "It's what we do. We survive. We pay what must be paid and call it prudence." She looked up through the dark at the vaulted ceiling, as if she could see through all that stone to the council chamber above, to Halvard's empty chair, to seven robed figures voting with their silence. "I appealed to them an hour ago. I gave them everything. The prophecy. Vessia's claim-maps. The truth about the Choir."

"And?"

"And they thanked me for my service," Ines said, "and asked me to wait in the reading room."

* * *

She had waited in the reading room for exactly four minutes. Then she had gone back down into the earth, because waiting was the one thing the guild had ever taught her that she now refused to learn.

The undercroft was coming apart around them. That was the thing no one had planned for—not the council, not Vessia, not even Tam, who understood the binding better than its makers had. Seven spirits held in rings of song and salt and iron for a century do not stay still while their prison shakes. The walls sweated. The map-shelves along the corridor above had begun to bleed ink, every cartouched border softening like a wound left too long in water. Somewhere overhead, a whole shelf of coastal charts had simply stopped existing—the paper there, but the coastlines gone, blank vellum where the Sundering Sea used to be.

"The unbinding destabilizes everything anchored to it," Tam had explained, hours ago, in the garden, in the dark. "The Choir holds the guild's maps true. Pull the pins out one by one and every map in the building starts to forget what it knows."

Ines had thought then about the ledgers. About a century of entries written in her own careful hand and the hands of keepers before her: *one compass that points at what you fear; one key to a door that has not been built yet; one song, fragmentary, provenance unknown.* All of it catalogued, cross-referenced, made safe by being written down. She had believed, once, that recording a thing was a form of honoring it.

Now the building was teaching her what the records had actually been doing all along. Not honoring. *Anchoring.* Every impossible thing the guild had ever mapped had been pinned here like a moth to a board, and the board was splintering, and the moths were waking up.

The fifth ring's spirit—a tall, sorrowful shape that might once have been a river-god or might have been a woman who loved one—had already gone. Its price had been paid in the garden: six years of Tam's memory of sunlight, traded away without hesitation, and Ines had watched her friend come back from that transaction lighter and somehow older, like a house with a room bricked shut.

Three rings remained after this one. Three prices after this one. And above them, the gates were open, and Vessia's army was walking into the guildhall like water finding a crack.

"Hold the fork steady," Tam said. "When I lift the bar, the ring will try to sing. It will offer you anything. Do not answer it."

"I know."

"You said that before the third ring, and you answered it."

"I answered it because it offered me Halvard's confession, complete and true, and I wanted it more than I have ever wanted anything." Ines set her jaw. "This time I'll want less."

Tam looked at her for a moment—looked at her the way she looked at maps, checking for errors—and then nodded once and threw her weight against the fork.

* * *

The fourth ring broke like ice on a pond in spring: a crack, a shudder, a bloom of light that was not light but *certainty*, pouring upward through the stone. Ines felt it pass through her chest and had to grip the workbench to keep from kneeling. For one white instant she knew the exact weight of every border in the world, every line ever drawn by any hand, and how little any of them weighed.

Then the spirit rose out of the broken ring—a small figure, child-sized, made of the color of dusk—and stood before them, and spoke without speaking, directly into the marrow of Ines's bones.

*A price,* it said. *A future.*

"No," Ines whispered.

"It always asks for a future," Tam said quietly. "That's why the guild never freed them. A memory you can live without. A name you can lose. But a future—" She didn't finish. She didn't have to. Ines could feel the spirit waiting, patient as arithmetic, and she could feel what it wanted, because it was showing her: a vision of herself old, gray, sitting in a garden that did not exist yet, at peace, finished. A life with an ending in it. That was the future. Not immortality. Just an ending, held in trust, that would now never arrive.

"What happens if I refuse?" Ines asked.

"Then it stays bound until someone pays, or until the binding fails entirely," Tam said. "And if the binding fails entirely, all seven go free at once, wild, with a century of rage in them, in the middle of a city full of people." She swallowed. "And Vessia's army is already inside."

Above them, muffled by stone, came the first sounds of the occupation: boots, many boots, and the particular disciplined quiet of soldiers who have been told they will meet no resistance. The council had kept their word. There would be no resistance. There would be a bill of sale.

Ines thought of the prophecy ledger burning in the archive wing—no, not burning; she had saved that one, she had carried it out with her own hands while the rest of a century went up—and of the line she had read so many times now that it lived behind her eyes: *and the Voice shall pay what voices pay.*

"Take it," she said.

"Ines—"

"*Take it.*" She looked at the small dusk-colored figure and made her voice as steady as a ledger line. "But hear me first. I am paying so that you go free. Not so that I survive. If freedom means vengeance on this city, then take my future and give me back my coin, because I will not have bought nothing."

The spirit tilted its head. And then, gently, terribly, it reached into her—not her body, her body felt nothing, which was somehow worse—and took.

* * *

Afterward, Ines sat on the cold floor of the undercroft and tried to remember what she had lost, which was the cruelest part: you couldn't. It was like running your tongue along a gap where a tooth had been. She knew she had owned a future. She could see the shape of the absence, the way you can see the outline of a missing book on a shelf. Inside the outline: nothing. A clean white page where the last sixty years of her life should have been.

"Do you feel it?" Tam crouched beside her. Her face was streaked with salt-dust and soot from the broken rings, and her hands were shaking, and she was still, somehow, the steadiest person Ines had ever known.

"I feel like a sentence with the ending torn off," Ines said. "Is that what it's like? Living like that?"

"That's what it's like." Tam stood and pulled her up. "You get used to it. You get very good at today." She glanced toward the corridor, where the groaning of the building was getting worse, deeper, structural. "Two rings left. We have to move faster."

"We won't make it two rings." Ines said it calmly, because it was simply true, and lying to herself was the guild's sin, not hers. "Listen to the walls. When the fifth anchor failed, the whole eastern vault shifted. This place is coming down whether Vessia burns it or not. We get one more ring, maybe, and then we run, and the last spirit—" She stopped. Calculated. Hated the calculation and did it anyway, because that was what she was for. "The last spirit goes wild when the binding collapses. Here. Under the guildhall. With Vessia's army standing on top of it."

"Then we don't let the binding collapse," Tam said. "We finish it. All three. Before the roof does."

Ines looked at her. "You've seen the prices. Memory. Name. Future. You've paid one already. What's left in you to pay twice?"

Tam smiled, and it was the saddest and bravest thing Ines had seen all night, in a night that had contained a burning archive and an opened gate. "Doesn't matter," she said. "I'm good at disappearing. Let's find out how good."

* * *

They came up out of the undercroft into the map-corridor because the sixth ring's mechanism required it—the final anchors were rooted in the great floor-mosaic of the entrance hall, the oldest map in the guild, the one that showed the world as it had been before the last war, before the binding, before the doctrine of invisibility. To break the sixth ring they had to walk through the guildhall's public heart. Through the army.

Ines pressed herself into the shadow of a chart-cabinet at the corridor's mouth and looked out across the entrance hall, and her heart did something complicated and painful that was not quite grief and not quite fury but contained the entire alphabet of both.

Vessian soldiers filled the hall. Perhaps two hundred of them, standing in loose formation among the map-tables, their boots on the mosaic, on the painted oceans, on the mountains some dead cartographer had rendered in lapis ground fine as flour. They were not looting. That was the horror of it. They were *receiving*. Officers moved from table to table with satchels, collecting charts, sealing them with black wax. Vessia hadn't sent an army to sack the guild. She'd sent clerks with swords. The conquest of paper, arriving to collect its empire.

At the far end, beneath the seal of the Guild of Unmapped Realms, seven robed figures stood in a row before a single officer in silver-trimmed black. The council. Ines recognized First Cartographer Ondrel by his height, by the way he held his hands folded, by the fact that he was *bowing*—slightly, precisely, the bow of one professional acknowledging another.

She could not hear the words. She did not need to. She had audited enough transactions to read one from posture alone. The guild was delivering its inventory. The Choir's location, the binding's mechanics, the terms of transfer. Everything Ines had begged them to destroy or free, itemized and handed over, priced at exactly the cost of the councilors' own lives.

"There," Tam breathed, pointing.

The sixth ring was not hidden. That was the last joke of it, the joke a century in the telling. The binding's master-anchor was set into the center of the mosaic floor, beneath a brass medallion that generations of apprentices had been told was decorative—a compass rose, beautiful, worn smooth by ten thousand feet. Everyone in the guild walked over the Choir's heart every day. Ines had walked over it every day for eleven years. She had taken visitors past it and told them it was merely ornamental, and she had believed herself, because believing comfortable things was the guild's native language.

"It's guarded," Ines murmured. Four soldiers stood near the medallion, not guarding it—they didn't know what it was—but simply posted there, part of the furniture of occupation.

"Not for long." Tam's eyes had gone distant, calculating. "When the fifth ring broke, the maps started forgetting. Look at the officers' satchels. Look at the tables."

Ines looked. One of the collected charts, half-protruding from a satchel, was visibly wrong—the coastline of Corvane crawling slowly westward as they watched, the city sliding toward the sea like a ship slipping its moorings. Another map had gone entirely blank except for a single word in the corner, repeated over and over in a fading hand: *here, here, here, here.*

"The unbinding is reaching up through the anchors," Tam said. "Every minute it gets stronger. Another hour and every map in this hall is blank, and Vessia's clerks are holding two hundred pieces of expensive paper." A thin, fierce smile. "Soldiers whose orders depend on maps start to get nervous when the maps die. Nervous soldiers look at things. They stop standing in rows."

"So we give them something to look at."

"I was going to say we wait for the panic," Tam said, "but honestly, yes. Give them something to look at. Something loud."

* * *

What they found, three cabinets down the corridor, was the guild's collection of acoustic impossibilities—the drawer Ines herself had catalogued in her second year, entry 4471 through 4490, each item tagged and sleeping. A whistle that only the listener's enemies could hear. A bell that rang once for every lie told within earshot. And, wrapped in oilcloth at the back, entry 4488: *one horn, war-grade, unsounded since the Salt War; when blown, all who hear it believe themselves betrayed by their nearest comrade.*

Ines unwrapped it with hands that remembered the catalogue entry perfectly and hated themselves for remembering.

"This is a weapon," she said. "A real one. The guild swore these were never to leave the vault."

"The guild also swore the Choir didn't exist," Tam said. "Blow the horn, Ines. Give two hundred men a reason to draw on each other, and we walk across that mosaic in the middle of it."

"And afterward? When they figure out what happened? When Vessia's clerks reconstruct the event and add 'the horn of the guild' to their inventory of reasons to burn this place down?"

"Afterward," Tam said, "there is no afterward. There's the seventh ring, and the song, and whatever's left of the night. There's no version of tonight where we're the good quiet record-keepers anymore. That version died when the gates opened." She took the horn from Ines's hands and pressed it back against her chest. "You taught me that. In the garden. *None of us flinch.*"

Ines closed her eyes for half a breath. She saw the ledger of her life laid open, and understood that from this line forward, every entry would be written in red.

"Give it to me," she said. "You'll need both hands for the fork."

* * *

The hall, when they hit it, was louder than a landslide.

Ines blew the horn from the corridor mouth and the world curdled. She felt the sound go through her too—she had forgotten to think about that, or had chosen not to—and for three endless seconds she was certain, absolutely certain, that Tam had sold her to the council, that the whole night had been a transaction and she was the goods. She raised the horn to strike her friend with it. She caught herself with the thing an inch from Tam's skull, sobbing, while around them two hundred soldiers discovered the same certainty about their brothers-in-arms.

The hall detonated.

It was not a battle; battles have sides. It was a hall full of people suddenly convinced of betrayal, in the dark, among unfamiliar furniture, holding weapons. Blades came out. Someone shouted a word in Vessian that Ines didn't know and everyone else clearly understood. An officer went down under three of his own men. A lantern hit a map-table and the old charts went up like autumn leaves, twenty years of the Sundering coastline burning in a bright spreading sheet, and the smoke, and the noise, and the mosaic underfoot slick with spilled ink from the bleeding shelves—

They ran through the middle of it.

Tam went first, low and fast, the cracked fork in both hands, and Ines followed with the horn still in her fist because she could not make her fingers let go of it. A soldier lunged at them from the smoke and Tam ducked inside his reach and put her shoulder into him and he spun away into someone else's blade, and Ines did not look to see whether he got up. She would carry that, too. She was beginning to understand that the night had a ledger of its own, and that she was writing in it with every stride.

The medallion. Ten feet. Five. Soldiers grappling on either side of them, none of them looking down, none of them ever having looked down—

Tam dropped to her knees at the compass rose, spat on her palms, and drove the cracked fork into the seam of the brass.

"Hold them off!" she screamed, and Ines turned, back to the mosaic, horn raised like a club, and faced the burning hall.

The sixth ring began to sing.

It came up through the floor, through Ines's boots, through her bones—the Choir's harmony, one voice short, wounded, furious, gorgeous beyond anything music had any right to be. Around the hall, the fighting stuttered. Men froze mid-swing. An officer dropped his sword and stared at his own hands. On the burning tables, the surviving maps began to move, borders breathing, coastlines shifting like sleeping animals turning over, and Ines understood with a clarity that felt like falling: this was what Vessia wanted. This, exactly this. A song that could rewrite the world, and here it was, leaking up through her own guildhall floor, and all the usurper had ever needed to do was follow the sound.

The brass medallion split down the middle with a crack like the world's spine breaking.

And the sixth spirit rose—taller than the others, made of storm-light and the smell of rain on dust—and it did not ask for a price.

It looked at Ines. It looked at the burning hall, the fighting soldiers, the sealed satchels of stolen maps, the row of robed councilors now pressed against the far wall watching their doctrine of invisibility die in fire. And it spoke into her bones, and what it said was:

*The last ring cannot be broken from below. It was made to be broken from within. The Voice must sing it open—and the song will cost what songs cost.*

Behind her, Tam pulled the fork free and stood, swaying, soot-black, alive. Above them, through the smoke hole where the ceiling had begun to cave, Ines could see the sky over the guildhall filling with the orange glow of the city burning, and hear, faint and rhythmic and patient, the drums of the main army taking up positions outside the crumbling walls.

One ring left. One price left. And the stairs down into the undercroft were already collapsing behind them.

"Together," Tam said, grabbing her hand.

"Together," Ines said, and jumped into the dark.
