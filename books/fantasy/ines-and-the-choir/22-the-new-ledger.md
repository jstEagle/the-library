## 22. The New Ledger

The guildhall at Harrow's Ford had been a granary once, then a chapel, then a granary again, and now it was neither, which Tam thought made it the perfect home for what he intended to build. The walls still smelled faintly of rye and older incense. The floorboards creaked in a way that suggested they had opinions. There were no cells beneath it, no undercroft, no sealed doors with seven locks and a century of dust on the hinges. He had checked. He had checked twice, and then a third time on a bad night when the memory of harmonies came back to him in his sleep and he woke with his hands pressed over his own mouth.

That was the thing about founding something: you did it with your body as much as your hands. You walked the rooms until they stopped being someone else's. You swept floors that no one had asked you to sweep. You learned where the roof leaked and fixed it yourself because there was no one else yet, and because fixing things with your own two hands was a kind of prayer, or the closest thing to one that Tam trusted anymore.

He set up the ledgers on the long table where the grain-tithes used to be counted. Three of them, bound in undyed linen, their pages ruled by his own hand — he had done the ruling himself, night after night, because he wanted every line in this place to have been drawn deliberately, by someone who knew what lines cost.

The first ledger was for maps. Not the guild's old kind of maps — not the beautiful lies, the smoothed borders, the rivers bent politely around property lines — but honest ones. Maps that said *here the road floods in spring*. Maps that said *here the surveyor was afraid and turned back*. Maps that admitted their own edges.

The second ledger was for the Choir.

He had started it eleven times. He had burned four of those attempts in the stove behind the counting room, and buried one under the floor, and torn one into strips that he fed to the river off the mill bridge, watching the ink bloom and dissolve like something drowning. It was not that he didn't know what to write. He knew exactly what to write; that was the problem. He had been a choir-tuner for six years before he ran. He knew how the binding worked — the iron collars of notation, the harmonic locks, the way you kept a spirit obedient by giving it a single note to hold forever, a note so narrow it became a cage. He knew how the binding broke, because he had broken one, once, accidentally, and seen what a freed spirit looked like when it came out of its note after eighty years: like a scream stretched thin enough to pass for silence.

And he knew the whole truth, which was the part no ledger had ever held. That the Choir had written the prophecy themselves. That the guild had read a warning as a permission slip. That Ines Pemberton — Ulyana, in her birth-ledger, the name she'd traded away — had unbound them all and paid for it with the only currency the song accepted from a living singer: her name, her self, the thread of her that said *I am*.

He wrote it anyway. All of it. On the eleventh attempt he sat down at dawn with cold fingers and did not stop until dark, and when he finished the last line — *she chose this, and we will not make her choice smaller by forgetting it* — he closed the book and put his forehead against its cover and stayed there a long time, breathing the smell of fresh ink and linen, which is not a holy smell, but was close enough that morning.

The third ledger was the one people asked about.

It was the newest, and the thickest, and except for a single line on its inside cover, it was empty. Travelers who came to the guildhall — cartographers mostly, at first, then surveyors, then refugees who had heard rumors of a place that told the truth about borders — would see it sitting apart from the others, and they would ask, and Tam would give them the same answer he always gave.

"That page is waiting," he said. "For whoever hears the song next."

* * *

They came slowly, the way true things travel.

First it was Wren, a former Vessian road-surveyor with burn scars on both forearms and a talent for finding water in dry country. She arrived in the second month, slept three nights in the doorway because she didn't believe any door would stay unlocked, and then stayed nine years. Then the twins, Osk and Maret, who had mapped smugglers' routes through the Salt Marches and wanted, badly, to map something that wouldn't get anyone killed. Then old Sef, who had worked for the Cartographers' Guild itself before the fall, and who stood in the doorway of the great hall on his first day and looked at the long table and the honest ledgers and said, quietly, "I helped bury things for forty years. Teach me to dig them up instead."

Tam taught him. Tam taught all of them, and they taught each other, and within three years there were more of them than beds, and they built bunks along the north wall, and the granary-chapel-granary filled up with the particular noise of people who had decided, together, that the world could be recorded without being owned.

The rules were few, and Tam had carved them into the beam above the table rather than writing them down, because rules on paper can be amended, and these were not meant to be:

**Map what is. Say what you did. Leave nothing out that someone will need.**

And beneath those, smaller, almost an afterthought, though everyone who joined learned it was the truest rule of all:

**No voice is bound here.**

* * *

Ines came north in the spring of the fourth year.

Tam knew she was coming before he saw her — a scrap of parchment had reached him through three sets of hands, unsigned, bearing only a date and the words *Harrow's Ford, when the thaw ends*. He spent the weeks before her arrival doing absurd things: sweeping the same stretch of floor four times, re-inking ledger entries that didn't need it, standing at the window on the south road like a wife waiting for a ship.

When she finally walked up the lane — travel-worn, gray-cloaked, a satchel over one shoulder and her hair cut short in a way that suited her — she looked, for one terrible moment, exactly like a stranger. And then she saw him in the doorway and her face did the thing it had always done, the small crooked almost-smile that preceded everything important she ever said, and the world snapped back into register like a map smoothing flat.

"You look well," she said.

"I look tired," Tam said. "You look like someone who doesn't know why she's here."

"I don't." She came up the steps. "I know a lot of things. I know I was somebody's keeper of secrets. I know I can hum a fragment of a song that makes rivers embarrassed about their courses. I know there's a hole in me shaped like a name, and everyone I meet looks at me like they're deciding whether to tell me what goes in it." She stopped in front of him. "You're the last one left who knows."

"Yes," Tam said.

"And you've been carrying it for four years."

"Longer than that," Tam said. "I carried it before you paid it. I just didn't know I was carrying it forward."

She looked past him, into the hall — at the long table, the ledgers, the apprentices looking up from their work with open curiosity, the carved beam overhead. Her eyes found the third ledger, the empty one, and stayed there.

"What's that one for?" she asked, and Tam understood, with a grief so clean it was almost peaceful, that she was going to ask that question about herself too, and that he would answer both questions the same way.

"It's waiting," he said. "Come inside. I'll show you everything."

* * *

He showed her everything.

He showed her the honest maps, and she ran her fingers along a coastline rendered with its true raggedness and made a sound low in her throat, half laugh, half ache. He showed her the Choir ledger, and she read it standing, all of it, for hours, while the light moved across the floor and the apprentices pretended not to watch and Sef finally lit lamps without being asked. She read the account of the binding. She read the account of the prophecy — *written by the Choir, a warning, misread as permission*. She read the account of the unbinding, each spirit's price set down in Tam's careful hand: a memory, a name, a future, itemized like the terrible arithmetic it was.

She read the last entry, the one about her, and when she finished it she was quiet for a long time.

"So that's who I was," she said at last.

"That's who you are," Tam said. "The name was never the whole of you. It was just the handle."

"The song took the handle." She touched the page, lightly, the way you touch something that might still be warm. "And left the rest of me walking around." She looked up. "Do you know what the strangest part is? Reading it, I don't feel like I'm learning about a stranger. I feel like I'm remembering something I agreed to forget. Like the shape of the decision is still in me, even if my name isn't."

"That's why I wrote it all down," Tam said. "Memory has to live somewhere sturdier than a skull. Skulls are unreliable. Skulls flood, and burn, and get captured by queens." He tapped the ledger. "Paper remembers differently. Paper remembers *on purpose*."

She was quiet again. Outside, the thaw-melt ran loud in the ditches, the sound of a world agreeing to move.

"The council members who voted to hand me over," she said. "What happened to them?"

"Some fled east. Two are dead — ordinary deaths, fevers, falls. One of them, Corvane's delegate, came here last autumn." Tam watched her face. "He asked to join. We turned him away. Not out of cruelty. Out of honesty — the first rule cuts both ways. We record what is. What is, is that some doors close."

"Good," Ines said, and then, softer: "I think I needed to hear that some things stay closed."

* * *

She stayed.

Not as a founder — she refused the title, laughed at it, actually, a short bright sound that made half the hall look up — but as a teacher. She taught the apprentices the old guild's accounting methods, so they would recognize concealment when they saw it dressed as bookkeeping. She taught them how power talks when it wants something mapped. She walked the survey teams out to the field edges and showed them how to stand in unmapped country without panicking, which was, she said, the entire skill, everything else was penmanship.

And sometimes, on evenings when the work was done and the stove was banked and the apprentices had gone up to their bunks, she and Tam would sit at the long table with the third ledger between them, and she would ask him to talk about the Choir. Not the history — she'd read the history. The music. What it sounded like from the outside. What it sounded like from the inside, if he could bear to say.

He could bear it, mostly. He told her about the moment in the collapsing undercroft when the seventh spirit came free and the whole chamber rang like a struck bell, and how the sound hadn't been beautiful, exactly — it had been *true*, which is different, and harder, and stays with you longer. He told her about the fragment of song she'd once hummed to bend a river, and how he had watched her face in that hour and seen both the intoxication and the terror, and how he had understood then that she was the only person he'd ever met who could hold that much power and keep asking whether she should.

"Why do you think the song wanted a name?" she asked him once, late, the candle down to its last thumb-width. "Really. You knew the Choir better than anyone. Why names?"

Tam thought about it. He had thought about it for years, in fact, and the answer had settled in him slowly, the way sediment settles.

"Because a name is the smallest complete map of a person," he said. "Every name is a territory. When the Choir takes one, it isn't theft. It's *surveying*. They carry her with them now — the real her, the whole border and interior of her. Somewhere, right now, seven freed spirits are singing a woman named Ines Pemberton into the fabric of the world, and that's why the roads hold, and the treaties stick, and the free cities rebuild without another war. She's not gone. She's *infrastructure*."

Ines laughed — really laughed, head tipped back — and then went quiet, and looked at the empty ledger, and said, "Then leave the first page for her too."

"It's already hers," Tam said. "It's already everyone's. That's the point of leaving it blank."

* * *

Nine years after the founding, on a bright cold morning in late autumn, a girl came up the south road to Harrow's Ford.

She was perhaps sixteen, travel-stained, alone, with the particular walk of someone who has been listening to something no one else can hear for a very long time and has finally decided to follow it. She stopped at the gate and stood there a while, looking at the guildhall — at the carved beam, at the smoke from the chimney, at the windows full of lamplight and bending heads — and Tam, who was splitting kindling by the door, watched her the way you watch weather change.

Wren came out beside him and murmured, "Another refugee?"

"No," Tam said. His heart was going strangely, the way it had gone in the undercroft, the way it had gone the day Ines read her own history and remembered the shape of her choice. "Look at her hands."

The girl's hands were moving. Faintly, absently, the way other people drum their fingers — tracing shapes in the air, small precise figures, over and over. Notation. Harmonic notation, the iron-collared script of the binding, except that no one alive had taught it to her, and no one alive was using it anymore except in Tam's ledgers, and she was writing it *backwards*. Unbinding it. Reversing every lock.

She looked up and saw him watching, and she wasn't frightened. She smiled the small crooked almost-smile of someone preceding everything important she would ever say.

"I heard a song," she said. "On the road, three nights ago, near the old ford. Seven voices. They sang my name — my real one, the one my mother gave me before she died, the one nobody else knows." She lifted her chin. "They said if I wanted to know who was singing, I should come here. They said someone keeps a ledger with a blank page."

The kindling had gone still in Tam's hands. Behind him, in the warm hall, Ines Pemberton — nameless, whole, humming under her breath as she corrected an apprentice's coastline — looked up without knowing why, and listened, and smiled at nothing.

"Come in," Tam said, setting down the axe. "We've been keeping it for you."
