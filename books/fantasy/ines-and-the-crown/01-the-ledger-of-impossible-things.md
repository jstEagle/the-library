## 1. The Ledger of Impossible Things

The vaults beneath the Vellum Guild kept their own weather.

Ines Ishikawa had worked in them long enough to know this — long enough to feel the change in pressure when a shelf of bottled storms shifted in its cradle, long enough to taste copper on her tongue when she passed the aisle where borrowed years were kept in stoppered glass. The air down there was old. Not stale; old, the way a cathedral is old, the way a grave is old, holding its breath around something that had once been alive.

She carried her ledger against her hip the way other women carried infants. It was a fat book bound in grey calfskin, its corners worn to the color of bone, and it went everywhere she went, because Ines trusted ink the way the devout trust prayer: completely, and without expecting to be answered.

"Vault four," she said to the door, because the door required a spoken word, and because she had long ago stopped feeling foolish about talking to doors. "Audit, quarterly, Ishikawa."

The lock thought about it, then opened.

Inside, the griefs were shelved by weight.

That was guild practice — not by vintage, not by donor, but by weight, because grief, properly bottled, had mass the way water had mass, and a shelf of the light griefs could be carried by one clerk while the heavy ones required two and a signed waiver. Ines moved down the rows with her lamp held high and her pen behind her ear, reading labels in the small exact hand that had made her name among the masters.

*One mother's grief for a son lost at sea, decanted 14th of the Reed Moon. Clarity: high. Sediment: salt.*

*Grief for a house burned with everything inside it. Clarity: medium. Note: smells of smoke even through the glass.*

*A grief too small to name, found in a coat pocket. Donor unknown.*

She counted. She verified. She noted two vials in the third case whose stoppers had loosened — grief escaping as a faint gray mist that clung to the glass like breath on a cold morning — and she flagged them for re-sealing with the little brass stamp that meant *this is a problem someone else will solve.* She did not wonder, as a newer clerk might have, whose grief it was, or whether it missed its owner, or whether the owner missed it. Wondering was not in her job description. Precision was.

Ines Ishikawa was very good at precision.

She had been told so by Guildmaster Corvane himself, once, in his office that smelled of cedar and cold iron, his long fingers laced on the desk while he looked at her with the mild appraising attention of a man examining a well-made tool. *You have no noise in you, Mistress Ishikawa,* he'd said, and she had taken it for the compliment it was meant to be, and had gone home to her narrow room above the bindery and slept without dreaming, which was, she understood, unusual, and had never once wondered why.

She moved on to the years.

The borrowed years were kept in a separate case, behind glass that had been spelled to keep out dust, moths, and sentiment. They were beautiful, the way all stolen things were beautiful — slender vials of gold-amber liquid that moved when you looked at them, turning slowly, showing you glimpses: a summer afternoon with the light coming through a curtain; a wedding dance seen over someone's shoulder; the specific quality of a grandmother's kitchen in winter, all steam and brown bread and someone humming. The guild sold these to the rich and the dying. A year of someone's ordinary happiness, decanted and drunk, could buy a dying man a season of remembering what it felt like to be well. The guild's price was obscene. The donors' price was a bowl of soup and a warm room and a signature they usually couldn't read.

Ines catalogued forty-one vials. She did not drink any of them. She did not hold them up to the light longer than verification required.

She had learned, in her eleven years at the guild, that the safest way to handle impossible things was to make them entries. A grief became a line in a ledger. A year became a number. A person became a donor code, and a donor code could not look at you, and could not make you feel the particular vertigo that came from understanding that the amber liquid trembling in your hand had once been a Sunday.

* * *

At midday she climbed the ninety-one steps to the counting room, where the other clerks worked at long desks under windows of green glass, and where the light always looked like the light at the bottom of a pond.

"You've the face of someone who found a discrepancy," said Merrin, the junior appraiser, not looking up from her own work. Merrin was round and quick and laughed easily, and had been trying for eleven years to become Ines's friend, and had gotten as far as sharing a table at the Lamplighter on feast days.

"Two loose stoppers in the grief case," Ines said. "Flagged for re-sealing."

"That's not a discrepancy, that's a Tuesday." Merrin's pen scratched on. "You know what I heard? Corvane bought three vials of first-love off the Night Market last week. First love, Ishikawa. Who *sells* their first love?"

"People who need the soup more than the memory."

"You say that like it's fine."

"It's inventory."

Merrin did look up then, and gave Ines the particular expression — half pity, half exasperation — that she reserved for Ines's more flawless moments. "One day something in those vaults is going to look back at you, and I want to be there."

Ines almost smiled. It was the closest she came to humor: the acknowledgment, without agreement, that a joke had been made. "I'm taking the deep archive this afternoon. The pre-consolidation holdings."

Merrin's pen stopped. "Nobody audits the deep archive. The deep archive audits *you.*"

"The deep archive is a room with poor lighting and bad filing. I've survived worse."

But her chest had done the small cold thing it always did when the deep archive was mentioned, the thing she had trained herself not to examine, because examination was the first step toward wondering, and wondering was the first step toward the vertigo, and Ines Ishikawa did not permit herself vertigo. She had a ledger to keep. Ledgers required steady hands.

* * *

The deep archive was below the vaults, below even the foundations, a place where the guild stored what it could not sell and could not bear to destroy. The stairs down were stone worn hollow in the middle by feet older than the guild's charter. Her lamp made a small yellow island in the dark, and the dark pressed in around it with the patience of something that had been waiting a very long time.

The pre-consolidation holdings were the guild's inheritance from its own past: acquisitions made before the great cataloguing, before Corvane's reforms, back when the guild had been less a business than an appetite. Things down here were labeled in hands long dead. Things down here were labeled in no hand at all — objects that labeled *themselves*, whispering their provenance to anyone who came close enough to hear.

Ines kept her eyes on her ledger and her ledger's rules in her mind. *Record what is. Do not interpret. Do not wonder.*

She worked the first three aisles without incident: a crate of unlabeled dreams gone to vinegar; a mirror that reflected the room as it would look in a hundred years (dustier; the same); a jar of last words, forty of them, each no bigger than a fingernail, rattling faintly when the door far above opened and closed.

The fourth aisle was where her lamp began to gutter.

Not from wind. There was no wind below the foundations of the world. It guttered the way a candle gutters when something *drinks* the light, and Ines stood very still and made herself finish the entry she was writing — *Aisle 4, holdings unverified, lamp behavior anomalous, recommend* — and her pen stopped on *recommend*, because she had heard, from the far end of the aisle, a sound like a page turning.

There were no open books in the deep archive.

She raised the lamp. The light steadied, sulky. At the aisle's end stood a cabinet she had no record of — and Ines had memorized the deep archive's inventory at twenty-two, all four thousand entries, because memorizing inventories was the kind of thing her mind did instead of dreaming. This cabinet was not among them. It was tall and narrow, black wood gone soft with age, and on its single drawer there was no label, only a mark burned into the grain: a circle, crossed by a line, like a moon half-eaten.

She knew the mark. Everyone at the guild knew the mark; it was stamped on the oldest charters and no one could say what it meant. The guild called it *the Old Seal* and treated it the way people treat a word in a dead language — with reverence, and without understanding.

The drawer was not locked. That was the first wrong thing. Everything in the deep archive was locked; the guild trusted no one, least of all its own history.

The second wrong thing was what was inside: a single vial, unstoppered, empty, and a card in a hand she did not know, which said only:

*Sold. Payment rendered. Do not look for the buyer. Do not look for the seller. Do not look for what was in it.*

And beneath that, in smaller letters, in a hand that made the base of her skull go cold because it was *her* hand, her own small exact script, though she had never in her life written these words:

*It was mine.*

Ines stood in the drinking dark with her lamp and her ledger and her steady hands, and she felt the vertigo come up through the stone floor like water through a crack, and she did the thing she had trained herself to do for eleven years. She closed the ledger. She closed the drawer. She told herself the hand was a coincidence, that hands ran in families, that she had no family she knew of and therefore no one to run in, and that the cold in her chest was the archive's chill and nothing else.

She climbed the ninety-one steps. She did not count them, which was how she knew she was shaken; she always counted.

* * *

The counting room at dusk was quiet, the other clerks gone to supper, the green windows gone black. Ines sat at her desk with the quarterly audit half-finished and did not work on it. Instead she did something she almost never did. She turned to the back of her own ledger, past the audits, past the inventories, to the personal pages — the ones the guild required each clerk to keep, a record of their own history for identification purposes, as if a person were an acquisition that might someday need verifying.

*Ines Ishikawa. Entered guild service at age nineteen. Prior history: [gap]. Family: [gap]. Place of origin: [gap].*

She had written those gaps herself, at nineteen, in her own small exact hand, and had never once thought it strange that a girl could arrive at the guild's door with no history at all and be admitted anyway, with a warmth she had never examined, as if someone inside had been expecting her.

Her pen hovered over the gaps.

Below her, through nine floors of stone, the deep archive held its breath around an empty vial and a warning written in her own handwriting, and above her, through the black windows, the moon hung over the city — and Ines, who did not wonder, wondered, for exactly three seconds, at the moon. It was thinner than it should have been. She was almost certain it was thinner than it should have been, as if something up there were being spent, turn by turn, and the sky was a ledger no one was auditing.

Then she capped her pen, because precision required steady hands, and steady hands required not looking, and she had eleven years of practice at not looking.

She was blowing out her lamp when the knock came at the guild's great door — three small knocks, low down, at the height of a child.

Ines stood in the dark counting room, listening, her cold chest suddenly colder.

Curfew had rung an hour ago. No one knocked at the Vellum Guild after curfew. No one *small* knocked at the Vellum Guild after curfew.

The knocks came again. Patient. Certain. As if whoever stood outside had been knocking on that door for a very long time, in one form or another, and knew — with the terrible certainty of the impossible things Ines catalogued and never wondered about — that someone inside was finally going to open it.

Ines picked up her lamp and went down to answer.
