## 2. A Knock Before Dawn

The door of the Vellum Guild was older than the city that had grown up around it, and it had opinions.

Ines knew this the way she knew most things about the guild — not from anyone telling her, but from the ledgers themselves. There was an entry, in a hand four centuries dead, recording the door's purchase from a carpenter who had wept while selling it, and another entry, sixty years later, noting that the door had been rehung *against its preference* and had sulked for a season, refusing to latch in wind. The guild's founders had been precise people. They wrote down everything, even the moods of wood.

So when Ines set her lamp against the iron-studded oak and put her hand to the bar, she was not surprised to feel the grain shiver under her palm like the flank of a nervous animal.

"Easy," she murmured, which was absurd, because you did not soothe doors, and because Ines Ishikawa did not murmur to anything. She recorded. She catalogued. She kept her voice for figures and her warmth for ink.

The knocks came again. Three of them, spaced like a heartbeat that had learned patience.

She lifted the bar. The weight of it was familiar — she had opened this door at strange hours before, for couriers carrying bottled monsoons, for debtors paying in years they could ill afford, for the guild's own retrievers returning with their lanterns burning the wrong color. But tonight the weight felt different. Tonight the door swung inward almost eagerly, as if it had been waiting too, and the cold air of the curfewed street poured over her like water finding a channel it remembered.

The child stood on the third step.

Not the first step, and not the threshold itself — the third, precisely, as though the first two belonged to some other world and the child would not claim them without permission. Mud streaked the small face in patterns too regular to be accidental: three lines across each cheekbone, a spiral at the throat. The clothes had been fine once — Ines's eye went to them automatically, appraising, the way it went to everything — silver thread gone grey with travel, hemlines cut in a style two kingdoms out of fashion, or perhaps never in fashion anywhere at all. Bare feet. Blue with cold but standing as if cold were a language the child had chosen not to speak.

And the eyes. Ines had catalogued eyes for eleven years. Grief-colored eyes, greed-colored eyes, the flat pewter of people who had sold too much of themselves and were still deciding what remained. These eyes were none of those. They were the color of the sky an hour after sunset, when the light has gone but refuses to admit it, and they looked at Ines with an expression she had no ledger-category for — recognition, but worn smooth, the way a coin wears smooth, as if the child had recognized her so many times that the recognition had stopped being an event and become simply a fact of the world.

Then the child spoke.

*Selu ven atha. Selu ven atha, i maro.*

The words fell into the empty street and Ines felt them land somewhere beneath her ribs, in a place that had no name in any inventory she kept. Her mind — her excellent, orderly, well-paid mind — reached for translation and found nothing. No scribe in the guild would know it. Old Master Corvane himself, who read nine languages and pretended at a tenth, would hear only noise. The tongue was not archaic; it was not a dialect; it was not anything the guild's vast library of borrowed grammars contained. It was language the way the sea is water — older than the containers men built for it.

And yet.

*Selu ven atha.*

Ines heard it and something in her answered. Not understanding, exactly. Understanding was a clean transaction: sound in, meaning out, both parties satisfied. This was rougher. This was like pressing her ear to a wall and hearing, faintly, a conversation she had once been part of — catching the shape of a sentence without its sense, the rise and fall of a voice she should have known. *Atha* meant — her mind offered nothing, and then offered something anyway, unbidden, the way a scar aches before rain — *atha* meant *come home*, or *the door is open*, or possibly *I have walked so far*. All three at once. None of them provable.

"I don't understand you," Ines said, and her voice came out wrong. Too quiet. Too careful. The voice of a woman handling something fragile in the dark.

The child tilted its head — a boy, she thought now, six or seven years old, though age sat strangely on him, the way it sits strangely on very old trees that are also somehow young — and spoke again, slower, the way one speaks to someone deaf rather than dim.

*I maro selu. I maro.* And then, with terrible clarity, he pointed one mud-streaked finger past her shoulder, into the dark hall of the Vellum Guild, at nothing she could see, and said a single word:

*Atha.*

Come home. The door is open. I have walked so far.

Ines's hands were steady — her hands were always steady, it was the first thing the guild had ever praised about her — but behind them, in the architecture of her chest, something shifted. It was not memory. She would have known if it were memory; she kept meticulous accounts of what she remembered, because there were so few entries. Her childhood was a short book. A mother's hands, flour-dusted, gone. A window with rain. The smell of a lamp burning low. That was nearly all, and she had long ago made her peace with the brevity, filing it under *incomplete by nature*, the way one files a ledger with pages torn out — not questioning who tore them, because the question led nowhere and nowhere was bad for business.

But this was not memory. This was the *place where memory would be*, aching. Like a tooth missing so long you'd forgotten the tooth, and then something touched the gap.

"You can't be here," she said. "Curfew began at the ninth bell. If the watch finds you—"

The child simply looked at her, and waited, and the lamplight moved between them, and somewhere across the sleeping city a dog began to howl and then thought better of it.

Ines Ishikawa had rules, and they were good rules, and they had kept her alive and solvent and safely unremarkable for eleven years. Rule the first: the guild buys, the guild sells, the guild does not give. Rule the second: nothing enters the vaults that has not been appraised, priced, and entered in the ledger in her own hand. Rule the third, unwritten but iron: do not wonder. Wonder was how clerks became liabilities. Wonder was how people started asking what happened to the griefs after they were bottled, what happened to the years after they were decanted, what happened to the memories the masters consumed at their private suppers — not that anyone said *consumed*, of course; the ledgers said *processed*, and Ines had written *processed* eleven hundred times without once letting the word open its mouth wider than necessary.

Every rule said the same thing: close the door. Report the anomaly at dawn. Let Corvane's people handle whatever stood on the third step speaking the language before language.

Instead, Ines heard herself say, "You're bleeding."

It was true. She saw it now — the child's left foot, the heel, split and crusted, leaving on the stone step a print that was not quite right. She crouched to look, because looking was safer than thinking, and the lamplight caught the print fully, and her breath went thin.

Mud, yes. But under the mud, in the pressure-lines of the small heel and toes, the stone showed marks that did not belong to any foot. Fine lines, radiating. Deliberate. Like the watermark in guild vellum, the secret mark pressed into every page the guild ever sold — the mark Ines had drawn ten thousand times in margins, in headers, in the tiny colophon at the bottom of each completed ledger, until her hand could make it in the dark.

The Covenant seal. The old sign of the pact between the human realm and the Otherworld, the moonlit country beyond the veil — the thing the guild's oldest ledgers referred to only obliquely, in the way ledgers refer to gravity, as a condition of doing business rather than a thing in itself. Ines had never seen it rendered outside of ink. She had certainly never seen it worn into stone by a child's bare foot, as if the child had been walking on it all night, as if the child *was* it, in some way her ledgers had no column for.

The child watched her look. Then he said, gently, in his impossible language, something short and soft, and though Ines could not translate it, she felt it settle over her like a hand on the head of a frightened animal, and she understood — in the wordless, infuriating, half-way she understood everything he said — that he had said: *you know me. You knew me before. It's all right that you've forgotten.*

"I haven't forgotten anything," Ines said, standing too fast. "There's nothing to forget."

Which was, she realized as the words left her, the most frightening sentence she had ever spoken. Because she did not know it was true. She had never once been able to check.

* * *

She told herself she was following procedure.

That was the story she assembled as she led the child inside, barred the door — the oak sighing shut with what was unmistakably relief — and carried the lamp ahead of him down the corridor toward the counting room. Procedure: anomalies were brought inside, documented, held for appraisal. The guild had forms for this. Somewhere in the deep archive there was a whole drawer of forms for impossible arrivals, printed in three colors of ink, and Ines had processed perhaps forty of them in her time: the man who arrived carrying his own funeral urn, the woman made partly of beeswax, the twin sisters who shared one shadow and quarreled over it constantly. Each had been logged, appraised, priced, and passed upward to the masters, and Ines had never wondered — rule the third — what happened next.

So this was procedure. Except that procedure did not include stopping in the washroom to warm water over the brazier. Procedure did not include kneeling on cold tile washing a child's feet with a cloth soft from years of polishing ledger spines, working the mud loose line by line while the Covenant seal faded from his skin and the boy watched her with those sunset eyes and spoke softly, continuously, in his old old tongue — a running commentary, Ines slowly understood, of *naming*. He named the cloth. He named the water. He named the brazier's coals, and the tile, and the lamp, and finally, with a small pause that felt like a bow, he named her.

*Ines.*

Her own name, in his mouth, sounded like a different word entirely. Older. As if *Ines* were merely the current spelling of something that had been written many times before, in fading ink, on pages now lost.

"How do you know my name?" she asked.

He answered at length, patiently, gesturing — the river, the moon, a shape like a door, a shape like a seed — and she caught fragments the way one catches fragments of a song through a wall: that names traveled, that names were carried, that someone had given him her name the way one gives a traveler bread for the road. He did not say who. When she pressed — "Who gave it to you? Who sent you?" — he grew quiet, and looked at his clean feet, and said only: *Atha. Atha i maro.* Come home. I have walked so far. Which was not an answer, and was, she suspected, the only answer there was.

She dried his feet, found socks — the guild kept a chest of garments recovered from unpaid debts, and she took the smallest pair without logging them, a theft so minor it barely registered, though some part of her noted it anyway, the way a ledger notes even blank pages — and led him to the counting room, because the counting room was where she thought most clearly, and because she needed, badly, to think clearly.

The counting room at night was her favorite lie. By day it was the guild's heart: desks in ranks, scribes bent over their copies, the great master ledger chained to its lectern like a saint's bones. By night, emptied, it was just a room full of paper waiting to be told what was true. Ines lit the second lamp, set the child in her own chair — he curled into it immediately, knees to chest, fitting it exactly, which unsettled her more than anything yet, as if the chair had been measured for him — and pulled a fresh folio toward herself.

Date. Time. Ninth bell plus one hour. Subject: one child, male, approx. six years, origin unknown.

Her pen hovered.

Origin unknown. She wrote it, and hated it, and wrote beneath it: *Language unidentified. Not on guild register of 214 tongues.* True. Beneath that, because honesty was a discipline like any other: *This accountant experiences partial comprehension of subject's speech. Cause unknown. This accountant notes that she has no memories accounting for this facility.*

There. It was on the page. Whatever it was, it existed now in ink, and ink was the world Ines trusted — fixed, dated, signed, unable to take itself back. She had built her whole life on that principle. What is written cannot pretend it was never written.

The child, watching her write, leaned forward and touched the edge of the folio with one finger, and said something brief and wondering.

"What?" she said, before she could stop herself.

He repeated it, pointing at the pen, at the wet lines. *Selu atha maro,* he said — and then, slowly, tasting each piece: *selu... atha... maro.* And Ines felt the half-understanding move through her again, that awful echo-reading, and this time it assembled itself into something almost like grammar: *the writing comes home. The writing walks far. The writing remembers.*

"That's not what writing does," she said.

He looked at her with enormous, gentle pity, the way one looks at someone insisting the sea is a kind of glass, and said nothing, because there was nothing to say to that, and they both knew it.

* * *

Dawn was maybe two hours off when the bell above the counting room door chimed — the inner bell, the one that rang only when someone crossed the guild's threshold with a key, which during curfew meant one person.

Corvane.

Ines was on her feet before the chime finished, body moving on guild instinct, placing herself between the child and the door with a speed that surprised her. The boy did not seem afraid. He watched the door with his head slightly tilted, listening, and his face did something complicated — sorrow, mostly, and underneath the sorrow, patience, the same terrible certainty he had knocked with.

Master Corvane filled the doorway the way he filled every doorway: comfortably, deliberately, a large man grown larger with authority, silver at the temples, rings on six fingers, each ring a little vault in its own right. His eyes went from Ines, to the child in her chair, to the open folio, and he smiled the smile he used when a shipment came in under cost.

"Well," he said pleasantly. "What have we here?"

"A curfew breach," Ines said. Her voice was level. Eleven years of practice. "Found at the door. Unregistered language. I've begun the intake record."

Corvane crossed the room slowly, rings clicking on the lectern as he leaned to read her entry upside down, the way masters read everything — taking possession of it by posture alone. "Unregistered," he murmured. "How rare. How *valuable*." He straightened, and looked down at the child, and Ines watched his face perform appraisal — the guild's oldest trade, the pricing of souls — and she watched, for half a heartbeat, something else flicker underneath it. Hunger, maybe. Or fear wearing hunger's coat.

The child met his gaze without flinching and said, clearly, in the language older than kingdoms:

*Atha i maro, selu ven.*

And Ines, standing in her counting room with her pen still wet, understood it perfectly, completely, horribly — no echo this time, no wall between her and the words, but the whole sentence laid open like a dissected text:

*You are late. The moon is counting.*

Outside the high windows, over the rooftops of the sleeping city, the waning moon hung pale and patient, one turn thinner than last month, sliding toward a number no one had taught it to stop at.

Corvane's smile did not change. But his rings clicked once against the lectern — click, click, click — like a man counting too.
