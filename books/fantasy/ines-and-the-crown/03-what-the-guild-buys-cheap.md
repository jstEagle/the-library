## 3. What the Guild Buys Cheap

The child had not slept. I knew this because I had not slept either, and the two of us had been keeping each other company through the wall — they in the appraisal room with its iron-banded door and its lamp that never guttered, and I at my desk in the counting room with a ledger open to a page I could not make myself write on.

The guild house breathed around us in the way old buildings do: settling timbers, the tick of cooling wax in the seal-room, somewhere below the vaults the soft mineral sigh of bottled dreams shifting against their shelves. I had catalogued that sound for eleven years. *Ambient loss, low register, no action required.* It was the kind of entry I was good at. Precise. Unfeeling. A line of ink that made an enormous thing small enough to file.

The knock came at my door just past the coldest hour, and I knew before I opened it who it would be, because Corvane never sent for me at reasonable hours unless something had arrived that he did not want to think about in daylight.

"Ines." He stood in the corridor with his coat already buttoned, though he had clearly not been to bed. His eyes had the particular brightness of a man who has been awake long enough that sleep has become a rumor. "The child. You'll sit in on the appraisal."

"I'm a bookkeeper," I said. "Appraisals are Master Hullen's—"

"Hullen is ill." A pause so brief it might have been nothing. "You have an ear for languages. You've catalogued acquisitions from four dialects nobody else in this building can read."

"Catalogued. Not spoken."

"You'll manage." He turned before I could answer, which was his way of ending conversations — Corvane never argued; he simply proceeded, and let the world catch up or be left behind. "Bring your ledger. Everything the child says goes down verbatim. Every word. I want it *exact*, Ines. Exactness is the whole of your value."

I brought my ledger.

* * *

The appraisal room smelled of lamp oil and cold iron and, beneath that, something sweeter — the faint green scent of the holding-vaults where we kept perishable goods. Memories went off, you see. That was the first thing they taught you as a junior scribe: memory is meat. It spoils. It ferments. Left too long in a poor vessel it turns, and what comes out of the bottle afterward is not what went in. The guild's entire fortune rested on the science of keeping remembering fresh, the way a fishmonger's fortune rests on ice.

The child sat on the appraisal stool with their feet not quite touching the floor, hands folded in their lap with a composure no mud-streaked curfew-breaker should have possessed. Someone had given them water and bread; both sat untouched on the little table beside them, arranged with a strange deliberate neatness, as if the child had moved them there not out of refusal but out of courtesy — *I will not eat while you are deciding what I am.*

Master Hullen's chair stood empty by the long table. Corvane took it. I took my place at the side desk, opened my ledger, dipped my pen, and waited.

"The acquisition was found at the guild door during curfew," Corvane said, in the flat recitation he used for inventory. "No name offered. No papers. Speaks in a language our on-duty scribes cannot place. Standard procedure is assessment of origin, assessment of nature, assessment of—" he paused, and I watched his thumb move once along the arm of the chair, a small motion, quickly stilled, "—value. Begin."

I looked at the child. The child looked back at me, and there was something in that look that I want to describe correctly, because everything that followed grew out of it. It was not fear. It was not even wariness, exactly. It was the look of someone checking whether a door they remembered was still where they remembered it.

Then the child spoke.

The sound of it went through the room like a struck bell goes through water. The language was nothing like the river-dialects of the south, nothing like the high speech of the capital, nothing like the clipped trade-tongue of the coast. It was older than all of those — older than the idea of kingdoms, I thought absurdly, older than the idea of *speaking*. Each word seemed to arrive slightly before the breath that carried it, the way lightning arrives before thunder, so that listening felt like reading a letter addressed to you that had been posted years ago.

And here is the thing I have never told anyone, the thing I set down now only because this account must be exact, because exactness is the whole of my value:

*I understood it.*

Not entirely. Not cleanly. But underneath the strangeness ran a current of sense, the way you can sometimes follow a melody in a language you don't know and feel the grief in it anyway. Words surfaced in me unbidden — *turns*, I thought, when the child said a word like a stone dropping into a well; *counting*, when they said another like a hand closing one finger at a time. My own mouth wanted to shape answers. I pressed my lips together and wrote instead.

*Subject speaks of a broken agreement,* I wrote. *Subject uses the word* covenant *—*

My pen stopped.

Because I had written that word before. Not recently. Years ago — junior work, transcription duty, copying entries from the deep archive into the master indices. There were vials in the vault whose labels bore terms I had copied without comprehension: *covenant-fragments, provenance uncertain; covenant-matter, unstable; DO NOT APPRAISE WITHOUT SECOND SEAL.* I had filed them the way I filed everything, precisely and without curiosity, because curiosity was a luxury the ledgers did not budget for.

But the child said the word again, and again, and each time it landed in me like a key trying locks I hadn't known I owned.

"What is it saying?" Corvane asked.

"It's—" I chose my words the way I chose ink, carefully, testing each one. "It's hard to render exactly. The structure doesn't map onto trade-speech. It keeps returning to the same phrase." I hesitated. "Something about a pact being cracked. And about the moon."

"The moon," Corvane repeated.

"Counting. It says the moon is counting." I heard myself say it and hated how sane I made it sound, how neatly I folded the insane into a sentence. "Like a tally. Like a debt coming due."

For a moment — a moment I would return to many times in the weeks after, turning it over, examining it under lamplight, hating myself for not seeing it sooner — Corvane's face did something very small. Not alarm. Something more like recognition. The look of a merchant hearing a customer name a price he had hoped no one would ever ask about.

"Ask it," he said quietly, "what happens when the count finishes."

I translated the question into the child's language — badly, clumsily, my tongue finding shapes it should not have known — and the child answered without hesitation, as if they had been waiting all night for someone finally to ask the right thing.

What they said filled nearly two pages of my ledger, and I rendered it as faithfully as I could, and I will render it again here because it deserves to exist twice:

*When the thirteenth turn closes, the door between shuts. What the pact held open stays shut. The borrowed things go home. The given things are taken back. Memory returns to the moon. Mercy returns to the moon. The worlds forget each other completely, and then the worlds forget that there was anything to forget.*

I read my own translation aloud, because Corvane asked, and my voice sounded wrong in the room — too steady, too much the voice of a woman reading freight manifests. When I finished, the silence had a texture. The lamp flame leaned as if listening.

"That's a fable," Corvane said at last. "Moon-stories. Nursemaid cosmology."

"Yes," I said.

"But you believe it."

I looked down at my ledger, at the two pages of careful script, at the word *covenant* sitting in my own handwriting like a splinter working toward the bone. "I believe the child believes it," I said, which was the coward's version of the truth, and Corvane accepted it because cowards recognize each other.

* * *

The appraisal lasted until the lamps burned low. Corvane questioned the child through me for hours — origin, purpose, how it had crossed the city under curfew, whether others of its kind existed — and the child answered every question with the same terrible patience, giving true answers that told him nothing, the way water gives you your own reflection when you demand to see yourself.

But twice, the child said things that were not for Corvane.

The first time, Corvane had stepped to the window to watch the sky — watching the moon, I realized later, though at the time I thought nothing of it, and that thoughtlessness is the wound I keep picking at — and the child turned to me and spoke softly, directly, in that pre-lightning language, and what I understood — what rose up in me unbidden, translated by some faculty I did not know I had — was:

*You have a hole in you shaped like me.*

I wrote nothing. For the first time in eleven years of service, I let a statement go unrecorded, and the blank space on the page felt like a held breath.

The second time was at the end. Corvane was gathering his notes, already half-turned toward the door, issuing instructions — the child would be held "for further appraisal," housed in the holding-vault wing, fed, kept comfortable, kept *available*. And the child looked up at him, then at me, and said a short phrase, three words, that I understood completely and instantly and with a clarity that frightened me far more than any mystery:

*They eat memories.*

I did not translate it. I told Corvane the child had thanked him for the bread. He nodded, satisfied, and left, and his footsteps went down the corridor with the unhurried confidence of a man who has never once been audited.

Then I sat alone with the child and my open ledger and the untouched bread, and I did the arithmetic I had spent eleven years refusing to do.

Because here is what a bookkeeper knows, if she lets herself know it: you can tell what a business truly is by what it buys cheap. Spices, silk, steel — everyone pays fair market for those. But grief? Grief sold by the dram. Borrowed years, decanted from the desperate. First loves, last words, the memory of a mother's voice — these came across our loading dock by the crate, priced at fractions of their weight in silver, and I had entered them all in my neat columns, *acquisition, lot number, storage bay,* and I had never once asked why the guild could get sorrow wholesale. Why people sold their most precious things for almost nothing. Why the buyers were always us, and the sellers were always people with nothing left to lose but the losing.

I had assumed it was simply the world's old cruelty, passing through our books like weather.

Now a child who spoke the language before languages sat on a stool in front of me and told me, in words older than kingdoms, that my employers *ate* — and every cheap lot, every bargain-bin grief, every vial labeled in my own handwriting rearranged itself in my mind into a single coherent picture, the way scattered letters resolve into a sentence once you know the first word.

We were not merchants. We were larders.

And I had kept the accounts.

I looked at the child. The child looked at me, and touched their own chest, and then — gently, the way you'd touch a bruise someone else owned — gestured toward mine.

*You have a hole in you shaped like me.*

"I don't know what you mean," I said aloud, in trade-speech, uselessly.

The child tilted their head. Then they reached out, took the stub of charcoal from the table — the one meant for marking appraisal tags — and drew on the back of my ledger page, on the clean margin where no entry would ever go.

A circle. Pale, ringed, with thirteen marks around its rim like ticks of a clock. Eleven of the marks, the child crossed out, one by one, with slow deliberation, watching my face the whole time.

Two remained.

The child set down the charcoal, folded their hands again, and waited — with the same courteous patience they had shown the bread — for me to decide what I was going to do with what I now knew.

Outside the window, over the sleeping rooftops of the city, the moon hung thin and white and patient, a coin mid-payment, a debt two turns from due.

I picked up my pen.

I wrote, in the margin, beneath the drawing, the first dishonest entry of my career: *Appraisal inconclusive. Subject's language resists full translation. Recommend continued observation.*

Corvane would read it and hear exactly what he wanted: that the child was a puzzle, not a warning. That there was time.

There was not time. There had never been time. There were only turns, and we had spent eleven of them buying memories cheap and calling it commerce, and I had balanced every book without once asking what the columns were feeding.

I closed the ledger on the child's drawing and felt the pages press it flat, preserving it, the way the guild preserved everything —

and somewhere below us, in the deep archive, in a vault I had catalogued a hundred times without ever reading the labels closely, something with my name on it waited in the dark.
