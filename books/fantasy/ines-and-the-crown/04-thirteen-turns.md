## 4. Thirteen Turns

The child had not slept in three days, and neither had Ines.

She told herself it was diligence. The guild's ledgers demanded vigilance; a misplaced entry could ruin a family, sink a shipping concern, void a contract of borrowed luck. But the truth was simpler and less comfortable: every time she closed her eyes, she heard the child's voice again — that language like water moving under ice, syllables that seemed to arrive from somewhere behind her own ribs. And every time, some part of her that had no business existing leaned toward it like a plant toward light.

So she worked. She sat in her narrow office above the counting room with three lamps burning and the shutters latched against the curfew bells, and she reconciled accounts that did not need reconciling, because the alternative was to sit with her hands empty and feel whatever was scratching at the inside of her skull.

The child was locked in the appraisal room two floors down. *For appraisal.* Corvane's phrase, delivered with the mild pleasantness he used for everything, as though the world were a warehouse of goods awaiting his convenience. Ines had translated for four hours while guild scribes took down the child's words in careful script, and the transcript lay before her now, forty pages of nonsense to any ear trained on the trade-tongues.

Except it wasn't nonsense. She could feel that the way you feel weather through a wall.

She dipped her pen. On a fresh sheet, without quite deciding to, she began transcribing the child's phrases into her own notation — the private shorthand she used for entries too strange for the official ledger. Bottled griefs. Borrowed years. The things the Vellum Guild bought cheap and sold dear.

*The moon counts its turns,* one phrase resolved to, when she worried at it long enough. *Thirteen. Thirteen, and then—*

Then nothing. The word after *then* refused translation, sliding away from her mind like a fish in dark water. She wrote the gap itself: a small hollow circle, an entry for an absence.

Her lamp guttered. Outside, through the shutter slats, she could see it — the moon, swollen past full now, beginning its slow decline. It looked wrong tonight. Too close. Too white, like bone held up to a candle.

Ines pressed her palm flat against the transcript and whispered, in the child's language, a word she did not know she knew.

* * *

Sef had spent sixty years learning the sky, and in all that time the sky had never once learned him back. He preferred it that way. Stars were honest creditors: they showed you exactly what they were, took exactly what they took, and never pretended affection. People were another matter. People lied with their faces and stole with their kindnesses, and Sef had buried enough of both to keep his observatory on a cliff a full day's ride from the city, where his only neighbors were gulls and the patient, indifferent dark.

He was grinding lens-glass when the knock came — three strikes, spaced like a heartbeat skipping.

No one climbed the cliff path at night. No one climbed it at all, anymore, except the wind. Sef set down his glass, took up the iron poker from the brazier, and opened the door onto rain and a young woman holding a mud-streaked child against her hip like a bundle of contraband.

"You're the astronomer," the woman said. Not a question. Her eyes had the flat shine of someone who hadn't slept and had decided sleep was a luxury for other people. "They call you Sef. They say you read dead languages and older skies."

"They say a great many things about me," Sef said. "Most of them flatter my silence." He looked at the child. The child looked back, and something in that look — old, old, old behind the young face — made the poker feel foolish in his hand. "Who are you?"

"Ines Ishikawa. I keep the ledgers of the Vellum Guild."

"Then you're the last person who should be standing on my doorstep."

"Yes," she said. "I know."

He let them in anyway. Sixty years of stars had taught him that much: when something knocks three times like a failing heart, you open the door, because the universe rarely repeats its warnings.

* * *

The child sat by the brazier, wrapped in Sef's oldest blanket, drinking broth with the solemn concentration of someone performing a sacred rite. Ines knelt beside them with the transcript pages spread across the floor, and Sef watched her work with growing unease, because what she was doing should not have been possible.

"I only half-understand it," she said, not looking up. "It comes in pieces. Like hearing a song through a wall. But there are patterns — repetitions, structures. It has grammar. Grammar means it isn't madness or mimicry. It's a real language."

"No," Sef said quietly. "It's more than that."

She looked up. Rain ticked against the dome above them. The child went on drinking broth, unbothered, as though the fate of everything were a thing that could wait until supper ended.

"When I was young," Sef said, "before I turned to the sky, I studied under a philologist named Averil Dune. Brilliant, unbearable man. He claimed to have found fragments of a pre-kingdom tongue — inscriptions older than the oldest stones we build with. He called it the Covenant-speech. Said it wasn't a language people invented but a language people *inherited*, the way you inherit blood. The academies laughed at him. He died poor and ridiculed, and I helped laugh, because I was young and wanted to be respectable." His hands tightened around the poker he'd set aside. "I still have his notes. I swore I'd never look at them again."

"Why?"

"Because looking would mean admitting he was right." Sef rose, joints cracking like green wood, and crossed to a chest beneath the star-charts. From beneath forty years of dust he drew a leather folio, cracked and water-stained, tied with string gone gray. "And if he was right about the language, girl, he may have been right about the rest."

* * *

Averil Dune's notes smelled of mold and obsession. Ines read by lamplight while the child slept against her side — actually slept, finally, breath slow and trusting, a weight she found herself unwilling to move even as her legs went numb.

Dune's handwriting deteriorated across the folio's pages, from precise scholar's script to jagged scrawl, as though the material itself had been eating him. He had collected fragments: a line from a standing stone in the northern fens, a verse preserved in a monastery's floor mosaic, a children's counting rhyme that no one remembered teaching to children. And piece by piece, cross-referenced against the child's speech patterns Ines had transcribed, they fit.

"It's the same language," she said. "Or its ancestor. The child speaks it fluently. Natively."

"Which is impossible," Sef said, "unless the child isn't from anywhere the maps cover." He pulled a lamp closer. "Read me the rhyme. The counting rhyme."

Ines found it near the end, copied in Dune's worst handwriting:

*One turn bright and one turn gone,*
*count the waning, sing along.*
*Twelve turns make the year of years,*
*the thirteenth turn —*

The rhyme stopped there. Below it, Dune had written, over and over, in letters pressed nearly through the paper: *what happens on the thirteenth turn, what happens on the thirteenth turn, what happens—*

"He never found out," Ines said.

"He ran out of time," Sef said. "Or it ran out of him." He was quiet a moment. Then: "Ask the child."

* * *

The child woke easily, without the confusion most sleepers carry, as though waking were simply another door they knew how to open. Ines knelt before them and spoke slowly, using the fragments of grammar she'd assembled, feeling her way through the language like a woman crossing a river on wet stones.

*The rhyme. The turns. What is the thirteenth?*

The child answered without hesitation, and this time — whether because the hour was small and the fire low and her defenses worn to lace, or because the language recognized something in her that wanted answering — the words came through almost whole.

*The moon walks a circle that is not our circle. Twelve turns, it keeps faith. On the thirteenth, it forgets us. And everything the forgetting touches—*

The child's hands moved as they spoke, shaping shapes in the air: a circle broken. A door closing. A thread cut.

*—unravels.*

"What did it say?" Sef asked, and his voice had gone thin. "Girl. What did it say?"

Ines found she was shaking. She translated anyway, because translation was the one discipline she trusted, the one act in her life that had never lied to her.

"The moon completes twelve turns and renews the Covenant — the pact between this realm and the Otherworld. That's what the rhyme is. A record. A calendar of the pact." She swallowed. "But the count is wrong this time. Something broke it early. The child says—" she listened again to the memory of the voice, water under ice, "—the child says the Covenant cracked, and the fragments scattered, and unless they're gathered and reforged before the moon finishes its thirteenth turn, the realms sever. Permanently."

"Sever," Sef repeated. "Meaning what, precisely? Borders close? Trade ends?"

"Meaning everything the Covenant sustained comes undone." Ines heard herself say it and felt the words land in her own body like stones dropped into wells. "Magic. Memory. Mercy. The child used those three words specifically. Whatever holds those things together between the worlds — it fails. And what fails here doesn't just stop. It unravels. Backward. People will lose what they can't afford to lose. Names. Loves. The reasons they get up in the morning. And then—" she looked at the sleeping coals, unable to finish, and finished anyway, "—then the losing itself is forgotten, and there's no one left to mourn anything, because mourning is also a thing the Covenant sustained."

Silence, except for the rain and the sea far below, working at the cliff roots.

Sef lowered himself into his chair very slowly, like a man whose bones had suddenly understood their age. For a long time he said nothing. Then he laughed — one dry, terrible bark with no humor in it at all.

"Averil Dune," he said. "You ridiculous, magnificent bastard. You were right, and I helped bury you, and now your ghost has sent a child to my door to collect the debt." He pressed both hands over his face. When he took them away, his eyes were wet and furious and, underneath the fury, awake in a way they hadn't been in decades. "How long?"

"The moon is already waning past full. The child's count—" Ines closed her eyes, listening inward to phrases she'd memorized without meaning to. "Nine turns remain. Perhaps ten. The count is uncertain because the moon itself is uncertain now. It's coming faster than it should."

"Nine turns." Sef stood, and something in the standing was different — the stoop still there, but the spine beneath it remembering an older posture. He began pulling star-charts from their rolls, forty years of meticulous observation, spreading them across the floor among the transcript pages. "Then we don't have time for grief or guilt. We have time for work. Where are the fragments?"

"The child says five. Scattered. Each kept by a guardian who traded away their memory of guarding it — so that even the keepers don't know what they keep." Ines touched the child's hair, gently, an gesture that surprised her with its own naturalness. "The child knows the way. The child *is* partly the way — it carries a fragment inside it. That's why the guild wants it appraised. That's why Corvane—"

She stopped. Sef looked at her.

"Corvane," he said. "The guildmaster. You've brought the one thing in creation he cannot be allowed to want, straight into his reach, and then walked out of his reach carrying it."

"Yes."

"And he'll come for it."

"He'll come for all of it." Ines looked down at the child, asleep again now, cheek against her knee, one small hand curled around the hem of her coat as though even in dreams it knew about letting go. "He's been buying memories for thirty years, Sef. Cheap. In bulk. I've recorded the purchases myself — I thought they were inventory. Griefs, years, childhoods. I thought it was just trade." Her voice went flat and cold, the voice she used for balancing irreconcilable columns. "But nobody hoards that much memory to sell it. You hoard it to *consume* it. To become it. And if the Covenant sustains memory itself, then a man sitting on a vault of stolen remembering might be the only person in the realm who survives a severance intact. King of whatever's left."

Sef stared at her. Outside, the clouds tore briefly apart, and the waning moon poured through — huge, white, wrong — laying a stripe of light across the scattered charts and transcripts like a finger pointing.

"Nine turns," he said.

"Nine turns," Ines agreed. She reached down and smoothed the child's hair back from its forehead. "We need the fragments. All five. We need to move tonight, before Corvane learns his merchandise is missing — and when he does learn it, he won't send clerks. He'll send the retrievers."

"The what?"

"People the guild has fed on. Consumed memories weaponized. I've seen their entries." She stood, lifting the child with a care that belied the steel settling into her voice. "I've filed their purchase orders."

Sef snuffed two of the lamps, leaving one, and began gathering Dune's folio, the charts, the transcript — the whole fragile archaeology of a truth sixty years buried. At the door he paused and looked back at the woman and child silhouetted against the dying fire.

"You understand," he said, "that whatever we do now, we do as thieves and traitors. The Guild does not forgive. The Guild does not forget."

"The Guild," Ines said, "is about to learn what forgetting really costs."

She stepped out into the rain, the child in her arms, and above the cliff the moon slid behind cloud again — one turn darker, one turn closer, counting them all the way down.
