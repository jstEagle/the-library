## 5. Inventory of a Stolen Childhood

The deep archive smelled of cold iron and older paper, and Ines had always found the smell comforting in a way she had never examined too closely. Comfort, like memory, was a thing she preferred to take on faith rather than audit.

She descended the spiral stair with a lantern in one hand and the appraisal ledger for the mute child's effects in the other — though *effects* was a generous word for what the child had carried: a scrap of moon-colored cloth, a river stone worn smooth as a knuckle, and nothing else. No name sewn into a collar. No token of a mother or father. The child had arrived at the guild door like a sentence with its first word missing, and Corvane wanted an inventory anyway, because Corvane wanted everything inventoried, priced, and shelved.

*Appraisal pending,* she had written that morning, her pen steady as it always was. *Subject retains one asset of unknown denomination.*

She did not write down what she suspected the asset was. She had not yet admitted it to herself.

The deep archive occupied three levels below the counting rooms, and each level was colder than the last. The first held the ordinary trade — bottled griefs racked like wine, borrowed years decanted into flasks the color of weak tea, dreams pressed flat between sheets of vellum so thin they wept light when you tilted them. The second level held the restricted stock, behind a door keyed to senior scribes only: memories taken rather than traded, the inventory whose provenance the guild did not discuss in front of clients. The third level —

The third level was hers alone to catalogue, once a season, by order of the masters. Nobody else went down. Nobody else, as far as she knew, could read the oldest shelf-marks, the ones written in a hand that predated the guild's founding charter. She had asked Sef about them once, years ago, over tea gone cold between them, and the old astronomer-poet had looked at her for a long moment and said, "Some shelves are load-bearing, Ines. Don't lean on them."

She leaned on them now, because the child's stone had hummed against her palm all morning like a struck bell that refused to finish ringing, and humming stones made her reckless.

* * *

The third level was lit by no windows and warmed by no hearth. Her lantern threw her shadow long across the racks, and the racks went back farther than the lantern reached — row after row of vials in lead-lined cradles, each tagged with a shelf-mark and a contents-entry in the old notation. Griefs. Years. First loves. Last words. Names, sometimes, whole names, sold by people who had decided they were better off without them.

Ines worked the way she always worked: left to right, top to bottom, entry by entry. She verified seals. She checked weights against the ledger. She noted evaporation — memory evaporated, even in glass, even in lead; the guild called it *angel's share*, a joke she had never laughed at. It was meticulous work and it required nothing of her heart, which was precisely why she had been good at it for eleven years.

Eleven years. She knew the number the way she knew her own hands. What she did not know was anything before it. There was a door in her past with no handle, a smooth stretch of wall where the stairs should have descended into childhood. She had woken — or so the guild's own intake record said — at the Vellum Guild's infirmary at age nineteen, with excellent penmanship, a flawless grasp of ledgers, and nothing before a certain gray morning in autumn. The physicians had pronounced it grief-shock. The masters had pronounced her employable. She had pronounced the matter closed, because a woman who keeps ledgers learns early that some columns do not balance, and the only mercy arithmetic offers is the permission to stop adding.

She had stopped adding years ago.

Tonight, the stone in her pocket would not stop humming.

It began, she noticed, whenever she passed a particular rack — the fourth from the stair, the one whose shelf-marks were in the oldest hand. She told herself it was resonance, some property of moonstone near stored memory, a phenomenon she could look up tomorrow. She catalogued two rows beyond it. Then three. Then she caught herself standing in front of the fourth rack again, lantern raised, telling herself she was checking for seal-failure, which was a legitimate concern, which was part of her duty, which was true in the way most comfortable lies are true.

The vials here were older than the others. Their glass had a greenish cast, like ice over deep water. Their tags were written in the archaic notation, and she read them the way she read everything — quickly, completely, without flinching — until she reached the seventh cradle from the end of the third shelf.

The tag said:

**ISHIKAWA, I. — MIXED LOT, 9 YRS + PARTIALS. PROVENANCE: PRIVATE PARTY. INTAKE AUTHORIZED: [SEAL].**

Ines stood very still.

The lantern flame leaned in a draft that had no source. Somewhere above her, through three floors of stone, the guild's night clerks were scratching their pens, and the sound came down to her like rain on a roof she didn't live under.

*Ishikawa, I.* Her name. Her initial. A mixed lot of nine years plus partials — partials being the trade term for fragments shorn off a larger holding, the scraps left when someone takes the whole cloth. Nine years. She was nineteen at intake. Nine years before nineteen was ten. Ten years old. The age when a child stops being a child's height and starts being their own person's height. The age of—

Of what? Nothing. There was nothing there. That was the entire point of the smooth wall in her past, the door with no handle. She had built her life on the assumption that the missing years were simply *gone*, lost to grief-shock, drowned, burned, whatever merciful catastrophe the physicians had implied. Missing things were sad. Missing things were not *inventory*.

But this was inventory. This was a shelf-mark and a weight and a seal. Someone had carried nine years of her life down these stairs, filled out the intake form in triplicate, and shelved her childhood between a widow's bottled grief and somebody's stolen first snowfall.

Her hands were shaking. She watched them shake with a strange professional detachment — *note the tremor, note the time, note the subject's pulse* — and then the detachment tore straight down the middle like wet paper.

She took the vial from its cradle.

It was lighter than it should have been. Angel's share, she thought wildly. Eleven years of evaporation. How much of me is left in here? The glass was cold, and inside it moved something that was not quite liquid and not quite light — a slow silver turning, like milk stirred by a finger, like moonlight poured through water. When she tilted it, the substance climbed the glass toward her warmth, and for one vertiginous instant she felt it *notice* her.

The stone in her pocket sang one clear, high note.

And the archive answered.

Not in words. In pressure. Every vial on every rack around her leaned — there was no other word for it — leaned toward her like grass toward sun, like the child's face had leaned toward her voice in the translation room when she'd spoken the old language she shouldn't have known. The sealed memories recognized something in her. Or she recognized something in them. The distinction, which had organized her entire adult life, collapsed.

She had spent eleven years believing she was the guild's most reliable instrument: a woman with no past, therefore no loyalties to corrupt her entries, therefore perfect. Neutral. Clean. She had trusted ink more than people because ink had never lied to her, and now the ink itself — the tag in the archaic hand, the seal, the shelf-mark — was telling her that her neutrality had been manufactured. That someone had curated her emptiness the way a curator curates a gallery. That the blank wall in her past was not a wound but a *withdrawal*, entered in triplicate, authorized under seal.

Who?

The intake authorization bore a seal she knew. Of course she knew it; she had catalogued it a hundred times on other lots. The seal of a senior guild officer with vault access. The seal of—

She turned the tag over. On the reverse, in smaller script, almost an afterthought, the kind of marginal note a clerk makes when he thinks no one will ever read it:

*Buyer retained for private consumption. Do not re-list.*

Private consumption. Someone had drunk her childhood. Not sold it onward, not traded it for power or coin in any market she could trace — *consumed* it, swallowed nine years of Ines Ishikawa the way a man swallows oysters, and walked back up these stairs carrying her tenth birthday inside him, and her mother's voice, and whatever face she had loved before she learned to love paper.

The fury arrived without announcement. It did not feel like anger as she had read anger described in the grief-vials' manifests — hot, weeping, loud. It felt cold and enormous and precise, like the archive itself, like a new column opening in a ledger she hadn't known she was keeping. Every careful year of her neutrality rearranged itself around this single entry and became something else: evidence.

She thought of the child. Mute, mud-streaked, humming stone, half-understood language scratching at a memory Ines didn't have. *A memory Ines doesn't have.* She had written that phrase herself, in her own report, in her own steady hand, and it had never once occurred to her to ask where the memory had gone — only that it wasn't there, the way it had never occurred to a fish to ask where the water went.

The child spoke a language older than any kingdom. The vial in her hand turned its slow silver turn. And somewhere in her chest, beneath eleven years of trained stillness, something that had been sleeping sat up and opened its eyes.

She put the vial inside her coat, against her heart, where it beat its faint cold rhythm against her ribs like a second pulse learning to keep time with hers. Theft. She was aware, distantly, that this was theft — guild property, restricted stock, a hanging offense if discovered. She catalogued the crime with the same terrible clarity she catalogued everything: *one vial, mixed lot, stolen by the person it contains.*

Let them audit that.

She climbed the stairs slowly, because running would mean admitting what she had done, and she was not ready to be that honest with the dark. At the second-level door she paused. Through it, muffled by stone, she could hear the night clerks, and beneath their pens, another sound — footsteps descending from above, unhurried, heavy, the tread of a man who owned every shelf in the building including, as of tonight, the one her soul was filed on.

Corvane. Coming down to check his stock.

Ines stepped back into the shadow between the racks, the vial cold against her heart, the stone silent now in her pocket as if it were holding its breath. She had eleven years of practice at being invisible in plain sight, at being furniture, at being neutral.

For the first time, she was grateful for the training.

The footsteps reached the second-level door. The lock turned. And Ines Ishikawa, thief of her own childhood, stood in the dark among the stolen lives and waited to see if the man who drank her past would walk past the woman who had just taken it back.
