## 6. The Night Market

*What follows is not my account, though it lives in my hand. It is the child's — set down as best I could render it, word by word, in that old tongue that scratches at doors inside me I did not know I had. I have kept their cadence where I could. Where I could not, I have kept their silence.*

— I. Ishikawa, marginal note, recovered ledger

The child had never seen a city wear its darkness like clothing.

In the Otherworld — in the before-place, the moonlit country whose name was not a name but a feeling, like the warmth of a stone held too long — night and day were the same color, and nothing needed to hide. Here, everything hid. The houses pulled their shutters closed like eyelids. The streets emptied themselves of people the way a cup empties itself of water, all at once and without ceremony, when the curfew bell rang its three dull notes.

But under the city, beneath the hiding, there was another city. And the other city woke when the first one slept.

The woman with ink on her fingers led them down through it.

Her name was Ines. The child knew this because she had said it once, grudgingly, the way a person gives up a coin they were hoping to keep: *"My name is Ines. If anyone asks, you are my sister's child, and your voice hasn't come in yet. Say nothing. Nod when nodded at."*

The child had nodded. The child was very good at nodding. The child had spent a whole life — a short one, a strange one, a life measured in turns of a moon rather than years — being looked at by people who wanted something, and nodding was the cheapest way to be left alone.

But this woman did not quite leave them alone. This woman watched them sidelong as they walked, the way one watches a door one has locked but cannot remember locking. And when the child spoke — small things, testing things, words in the old tongue like *cold* and *hungry* and *where are we going* — the woman flinched each time, a tiny flinch at the corner of her mouth, as if the words were reaching into her and touching something that wasn't there.

The child wondered what it was. The child wondered if it hurt.

They went down.

Down a stair behind a shuttered chandlery, down past a door painted with an eye that blinked open as they passed and blinked shut again, satisfied. Down into air that smelled of tallow and wet wool and frying onions and, underneath all of it, something sweeter and stranger — the smell the child knew from dreams, the smell of memory just beginning to turn.

Then the dark opened like a flower, and there it was.

The Night Market.

It filled a cavernous space beneath the old riverbed — a vaulted dark hung with hundreds of lanterns, and the lanterns were not lit with fire. Each one held a glow of its own making: bottled light, bottled warmth, bottled hours. Some burned gold as honey. One near the entrance glowed the pale green of a summer remembered badly. A whole row of them along the eastern wall pulsed slow and red, and when the child looked at them too long, they felt someone else's grief settle over their shoulders like a wet cloak, and they looked away quickly.

Stalls crowded the cavern floor in crooked rows, selling everything that could not be sold in the world above. A fence-witch with silver rings on every finger laid out spoons that stirred themselves toward truth. A man with no shadow sold shadows, dozens of them, pinned to boards like moths. Somewhere a fiddler played a tune that made everyone who heard it briefly remember being loved, and the crowd around him stood still with their eyes shining, and he passed his hat among them, and they paid gladly, and forgot again by morning.

The child stopped walking. They had never seen so many *held* things. In the Otherworld, memories lived loose in the air, in the water, in the roots of white trees; no one bottled them, because no one needed to hoard what everyone shared. But here — here people had taken the shared thing and put it in glass, and corked it, and priced it.

*"Don't stare,"* Ines murmured, in her own tongue, which the child was slowly learning the shape of. *"Staring costs extra here."*

Everything cost extra here. The child understood that quickly. That was the nature of markets: they were machines for turning wanting into owing. The child had crossed between worlds carrying a fragment of the oldest pact ever sworn, and even the fragment in their chest seemed to understand market-logic now — it sat heavier, the closer they came to crowds, as if it knew it was valuable and was afraid.

Or perhaps that heaviness was the child's own fear. It was hard, lately, to tell which weight belonged to whom.

* * *

The first fence-witch they needed was called Mother Quill, and she kept a stall shaped like an open book, its awning two great pages of oiled canvas, its counter a spine of black wood worn smooth by decades of elbows.

She saw them coming and smiled with all her teeth, which was most of them.

"Ines Ishikawa," she said. "The Guild's own abacus. Come down from the counting-house to spend a little dirty money? Or—" her eyes slid to the child, and stayed there, and sharpened, "—to make a withdrawal?"

"A purchase," Ines said evenly. "Quietly."

"Everything down here is quiet, dear. That's what the curfew's *for*." Mother Quill leaned across her counter, and the lanterns above her stall dimmed politely, giving her privacy the way well-trained servants give their masters distance. "That child has guild dust all over them. You didn't wash it off properly. You can't wash it off properly — it's ledger-dust, it clings to what's owed." She clicked her tongue. "You're stealing from Corvane."

"I'm auditing him," Ines said. "Early."

The child did not follow all of it — the words came fast, and the woman's tongue was full of hooks and barbs — but they caught the shape of it: two predators circling, deciding whether tonight they were the same kind of animal.

Mother Quill laughed then, a dry sound like paper folding. "Oh, I've missed you, Ishikawa. What do you need?"

"Passage. Through the market, out the river-gate, no names recorded. And a look-away charm strong enough to fool retrievers."

"Retrievers." The witch's eyebrows climbed. "You *have* been busy." She held out her hand, palm up. "Fare?"

Ines reached into her coat — and the child saw her hesitate. Saw her fingers close around something in an inner pocket and stay there a moment too long, the way a person holds a wound.

Then she brought out three small vials and set them on the counter, and the child understood what fare meant here, truly meant, and their stomach turned over.

Memories. Little stoppered pieces of a person, glowing faintly, each one labeled in Ines's precise clerk-hand. The child read the labels upside down, in the old way, reading backward and forward at once:

*A kitchen. Rain on a window. Someone humming.*

*A dog, brown, loyal, dead eleven years.*

*The smell of bread.*

"You're giving them away," the child said, before they could stop themselves, in the old tongue, and both women went still.

Mother Quill looked at the child with new interest. Ines looked at the child with something rawer than interest — something startled, almost accusatory, as if the child had opened one of her vials and drunk it in front of her.

"They're small ones," Ines said at last, to the witch, not to the child. Her voice was perfectly level. Perfectly controlled. The child was learning that the levelness was the tell. "Trivial ones. I have thousands of entries. No one misses the margins."

"Liar," said Mother Quill fondly, and pocketed the vials. "Everyone misses the margins. That's where the living goes." She rummaged beneath her counter and produced two things: a thin gray ribbon, which she knotted around the child's wrist, murmuring words that made the ribbon warm and then forgettable; and a token of black horn, stamped with a crescent. "The ribbon says the child belongs to no one worth noticing. The token buys the ferry. Vane will take you downriver to the gate under the granary — but mind," and here her voice dropped, and the lanterns leaned in to listen, "Vane takes his fare in memories too, and he's particular. He won't take trivia. He takes only what you'd rather keep. That's how you know it's a fair price. It should hurt a little."

The child looked at Ines.

Ines was looking at the empty place on the counter where her vials had been, and her face did the thing the child had already begun to catalog: it closed, like a ledger snapping shut.

*"Come,"* she said in the old tongue, awkward, unpracticed, and the child's heart stumbled — because she was speaking *their* language, badly, on purpose, to be understood. *"We go."*

* * *

They moved through the market's crooked arteries, and the child learned the rules of the dark city the way water learns a channel: by flowing.

Rule one: do not meet the lantern-light directly. Rule two: do not answer when a stall-keeper calls your name, especially if you never told them your name. Rule three: if you hear music, decide *before* you listen whether you want to remember what it makes you remember.

And rule four, which Ines taught without teaching, simply by doing it: walk like you are expected somewhere. The market parted for people who walked like that, and swallowed people who didn't.

The child walked beside her and matched her stride, and somewhere in the matching, without either of them deciding it, their hands found each other. Not holding. Just touching — the child's fingers hooked lightly into the edge of Ines's sleeve, the way ivy tests a wall.

Ines stiffened. The child felt the stiffness travel down her arm like a struck note.

Then, after four steps, five, six — the arm relaxed. Did not pull away.

The child held on.

They passed a stall where a woman sold borrowed years — decanted time, golden and sloshing, twenty-three summers in a jar. They passed a booth where a boy no older than the child traded secrets for sweets, whispering into candy-floss until it turned gray and heavy. They passed a mirror that showed each passerby not as they were but as they were *afraid of being*, and a queue of the brave and the foolish waited to look, and paid, and staggered away changed.

And everywhere, everywhere, the bottled light. Thousands of lanterns. Thousands of held moments, burning to light other people's bargains.

The child thought: *this is what happens when a Covenant cracks.* Not all at once — not thunder and severance — but slowly, market by market, vial by vial, until people forget that remembering used to be free. Until memory becomes currency, and whoever mints the currency owns the past.

The fragment in their chest turned over, heavy as a sleeping animal, and the child pressed a hand to their sternum and kept walking.

* * *

The ferry waited at the market's drowned edge, where the old riverbed still held a finger of black water, deep and slow and utterly silent. The boat was long and low, lacquered dark, and the ferryman stood at its stern with a pole in his hands and a face like a man who had been listening to arguments for a hundred years and found them all equally tedious.

"Vane," Ines said.

"Ishikawa." His voice was gravel poured slowly. His eyes — pale, lidless, patient — went to the child, and lingered, and the child felt them like cold fingers reading ribs. "Out through the granary gate. Curfew ends in three hours. Doable. Fare?"

Ines touched her inner pocket again. Stopped.

The child saw it happen: the arithmetic running behind her eyes, the inventory of what remained, the terrible clerk's precision of a woman calculating her own erosion. She had paid the witch. The pocket was lighter. Whatever was left in it, she was guarding like the last wall of a besieged city.

"I'll owe you," Ines said.

"No," said Vane, without heat, the way one declines a bad coin. "Owing is how Corvane got half this market. Fare in kind, or no passage."

Silence. Water lapped the hull with a sound like a page turning.

Then the child stepped forward.

*"I'll pay,"* they said, in the old tongue, and then — laboriously, word by word, in hers — *"I pay."*

Ines's head snapped around. "*No.*"

But the child was already opening the small bundle they carried — the rag-wrapped bundle Ines had never been allowed to inspect — and taking from it a single object: a smooth white stone, round as a moon, warm as skin. It was not a stone. It was a memory, the oldest kind, the kind the Otherworld made before bottling was invented: a memory grown solid, the way a pearl grows around a wound.

It held the child's first turning of the moon. The first time they had seen the sky count. Lying in white grass, in the before-place, watching the moon swell and thinking — with the vast, serious wonder of the very young — *it is looking back.*

The child held it out to Vane.

The ferryman went quiet in a new way. Even the water seemed to quiet. Somewhere behind them, the fiddler's tune faltered, as if the market itself had paused to watch.

"That's not guild stock," Vane said softly. "That's covenant-grown. That's worth more than this boat, this river, and every debt on it." His pale eyes flicked to Ines, then back. "You'd give this up. For a ride."

The child considered the question honestly, because it deserved honesty.

*It was mine,* they answered, in the old tongue, knowing only that the woman would half-catch it, knowing it didn't matter. *It was mine, and it was warm, and keeping it would not save the moon. Things that are kept do not save anything. Only given things do. I learned that before I came here. I was sent here because I learned it.*

They did not say all of it aloud. They did not need to. Something in the saying — in the offering itself — carried the meaning across the gap between languages, the way light crosses a window whether or not the glass understands.

Vane took the stone.

He held it a long moment, and his tedious, argument-weary face did something complicated — something almost like grief, almost like reverence — and then he tucked it away inside his coat, over his heart, where fares go.

"Board," he said roughly. "Both of you. No charge for the woman. The child paid double."

* * *

The boat slid out onto the black water, and the Night Market fell away behind them — the lanterns shrinking to a scatter of held moments, gold and green and grieving red, a constellation of everything people couldn't bear to carry and couldn't bear to lose.

The child sat in the bow. Ines sat beside them, not touching, but near enough that the child could feel the warmth of her, which was a small fierce warmth, banked like a stove in a shut room.

For a while neither spoke. The pole creaked. Water breathed against the hull.

Then Ines said, quietly, in her own tongue, not expecting to be understood — the way people speak in front of children and animals and gods:

"You shouldn't have paid. That was yours. The first one. You don't get those back."

The child watched the dark tunnel ahead, where the granary gate waited, a gray seam of possible morning.

*I know,* they thought. *That is why it counted.*

And then, because trust is built out of exactly such useless, unrecoverable gifts, the child did the thing they had not done since crossing over: they leaned, very slightly, until their shoulder rested against the ink-stained woman's arm, and let their eyes fall half-shut, and let themselves be, for one boat-length of black water, a child instead of a vessel.

Ines went rigid.

Then — slowly, like a lock accepting a key it had forgotten it was cut for — she did not move away. Her hand rose, hovered, and settled with terrible gentleness on the child's hair.

The child felt her fingers tremble.

Felt her breathe a word into the dark, so soft it barely qualified as sound, in the old tongue, badly pronounced, unmistakable anyway:

*"Mioleth."*

Home-child. Heart-kept one.

The child's eyes flew open. Their heart slammed. *Where did she learn that word? Who taught her? Why does she say it like it hurts her mouth and heals her hands at the same time?*

But when they looked up, Ines's face was shuttered again, the ledger closed, and she was staring ahead at the gray seam of the gate as if it were the only real thing in the world.

Behind them, far back in the market's dark, a bell began to ring — not the curfew bell, not the market's chime, but something thinner and colder, a sound like a blade drawn across silk.

Vane's pole stopped mid-stroke.

"Retrievers," he said. "On the water. Coming fast." His pale eyes found Ines's. "They're not following the child, Ishikawa."

He nodded upstream, toward the sound.

"They're following *you*."
