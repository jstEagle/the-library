## 7. Hounds of the Ledger

The knock came at the hour when even the lamps grow doubtful.

Ines heard it through the floorboards of the safehouse above the tallow-chandler's shop — three soft raps, then silence, then three more. Not the guild's knock. The guild knocked like it owned the door, four blows struck with a signet ring. This was careful. This was someone counting.

She was out of bed before her mind finished waking, one hand finding the child's shoulder in the dark. The boy — she had stopped thinking of him as *it* somewhere on the Night Market ferry, and the word had settled into her chest like a coal — did not stir. He slept the way he did everything: in a language she was only beginning to learn. His breathing had a rhythm to it, tide-like, and lately she had caught herself matching her own breath to his without meaning to, the way two clocks in the same room will slowly agree.

The knock came again.

She moved to the window, keeping below the sill, and looked down into the street through the gap where the shutter had warped. Fog lay over Candlewick Lane like a sheet drawn up to the chin. And standing in it, at intervals along both curbs, were figures.

Six of them. No — seven. They stood too still for watchmen and too evenly spaced for drunks. Each wore the grey traveling cloak of a guild clerk, hood back, faces pale as vellum in the lamplight. And each face was *wrong* in a way Ines could not have described to anyone who hadn't spent ten years cataloguing the guild's inventory. She knew those faces. She had written their acquisition entries herself, years ago, in her neat unbroken hand: *one memory of a mother's voice, consumed; one memory of a wedding day, consumed; one memory of grief entire, consumed.* The clerks who volunteered for consumption were paid triple and promoted within the year. What came back from the consuming room wore that same smoothness, that same absence of weather behind the eyes.

Retrievers. Corvane had not sent clerks. He had sent ledgers with legs.

Ines dressed without light, fingers remembering the work on their own: boots laced loose enough to run, the child's bundle already packed by the door because she had packed it every night since the Bone Orchard road, because hope was a habit you built like any other entry, line by line, until it balanced. She touched the boy's cheek. His eyes opened instantly, fully, no fog of sleep in them at all — and that, too, frightened her a little, how he woke like something surfacing from deep water rather than rising from a bed.

He looked at her. He said a word in the old tongue, low and questioning.

"I know," she whispered, though she didn't, quite. "I know."

* * *

They went out through the chandler's yard, over a wall slick with rendered fat, into the maze of alleys behind the tanneries. Ines had chosen the safehouse for its exits the way she chose everything — methodically, twice-checked — but the city itself had begun working against them. She felt it in the way the fog moved, in the way the bells tolled the half-hour with a hesitation she'd never noticed before. Turn eleven was closing. The moon hung over the rooftops like a coin thinning toward nothing, and everything the Covenant sustained was already going threadbare at the edges. Colors muted. Names slipped. Twice this week she had reached for a word — ordinary words, *spoon*, *Thursday*, *amber* — and found the shelf where they lived standing empty.

And now the guild had sent its hounds.

They made six streets before the first whistle sounded.

It was not a whistle, really. It was a *recitation*. That was what turned her blood to ink-water: she recognized the sound from the archive depths, the cadence of an entry being read aloud — *item retrieved, item retrieved, item retrieved* — pitched high and flung across the rooftops like a thrown knife. The retrievers navigated by ledger-verse. Somewhere behind them, a clerk-consumed throat was reading their description aloud to the night, and the night, thinned as it was, was listening.

"Roofs," Ines breathed, and hauled the child onto the first stack of barrels, up the trellis, onto the tiles.

The city from above was a drowned country. Fog pooled in the streets so thickly the cobbles had vanished; only the rooflines showed, ridge after slate ridge running black under the failing moon. Ines ran the way she had run as a girl — before, before the gap, before whatever Corvane or whoever had carved out of her childhood — feet finding the sure tiles without consulting her. The child kept pace at her hip, silent, weightless-seeming, his small hand locked in hers with a grip that had more than muscle in it. When she glanced down at him, his eyes were fixed backward, on the fog.

Then the fog *broke*.

A retriever rose out of it the way a heron rises from a marsh — no sound, no effort, just vertical arrival — and landed on the ridge twenty feet behind them with a sound like a dropped book. Its lantern-eyes found them. Its mouth opened, and the recitation poured out, and now Ines could hear the words beneath the cadence:

*"Item: one child, mute, covenant-bearing. Item: one clerk, Ishikawa, I., status amended from asset to fugitive. Item: recover all. Consume remainder."*

*Consume remainder.* Her own name filed between an object and an instruction. Something in her chest that had been cracking since the vial in the deep archive — *Ishikawa, I., ages four through nine, sold* — finished coming apart, quietly, the way ice lets go of a pier.

She ran.

The rooftops of the Vellum Quarter were built close, gutter to gutter, and she took them at full sprint while the recitation multiplied behind them — a second voice joining the first, then a third, harmonizing horribly, the hounds calling to one another in bookkeeping. A tile gave way under her heel and she rode the slide three feet, caught the chimney, swung the child past her, kept moving. Below, in the white streets, she saw more of them walking. Not running. *Walking*, unhurried, converging, because they did not need to hurry. They knew where the exits were. They had read the file.

"They know the safehouses," she gasped, skidding down a slope of slates toward the bell-tower of Saint Verre-in-the-Wall. "Every place I've ever—"

Of course they did. She had *written* them down. Every bolt-hole, every favor owed, every name of every fence-witch who owed her — all of it sat in the master ledger in Corvane's vault, in her own immaculate hand, cross-referenced and indexed. She had built the map they were hunting her with. Ten years of precision, and precision, it turned out, cut both ways.

The child tugged her sleeve. He pointed — not at the tower, not at the streets, but *up*, at the moon, and said something rapid and urgent in the old language, and though she still understood only fragments, fragments were enough: *high. higher. the eye of the night is turning away.*

"He can't see us if we're above his sightline," she translated, not knowing how she knew, and climbed.

* * *

The bell-tower of Saint Verre was a skeleton of black oak and green copper, and they went up it hand over hand, through the belfry where the great cracked bell hung silent — the bell whose name, Ines realized with a cold start, she could no longer recall, though she'd passed it a thousand times — and out onto the leaded crown where the fog ended and the wind began. From here the whole city lay below like a manuscript: streets for lines, roofs for script, and moving through the white margins, patient as punctuation, the grey figures. Eleven now. Twelve. They had stopped searching the ground. They stood in rings around the tower's base, faces tilted up, mouths reciting.

*"Item located. Item located. Await retrieval instruction."*

Awaiting instruction. Which meant the reader-in-chief was elsewhere — which meant Corvane himself, or his undermaster, held the pen tonight, and would write the next line when it suited him. There was time. Not much. But some.

Ines sank against the parapet, pulling the child close inside her cloak, and let herself shake for exactly ten breaths. She counted them. Counting was the last discipline she trusted.

"We can't outrun the ledger," she told him quietly. It wasn't despair; it was audit. "Everything I know, they know. Every friend, every door, every road out of this city — it's all written down." She laughed, a small terrible sound the wind took. "I used to be proud of that. Do you understand? I built a cage so well-made I couldn't tell it from a home."

The child studied her with those eyes the color of the space between stars. Then he did something he had never done. He reached up and pressed his palm flat against her sternum, over her heart, and spoke one word.

She understood it completely. Some words needed no translation.

*Mine,* he had said. Or perhaps: *kept here.* The place where things were safe that were never written down.

Her eyes stung. "Yes," she managed. "All right. Yes."

And then, because the audit continued whether her heart was involved or not, she made herself think. The retrievers ran on recitation. Recitation required a source. The source was the master ledger — or a copy of its relevant pages, carried by the reader-in-chief, wherever he stood in the fog below. Every order they followed, every location they converged upon, every *item* they named, flowed from ink. From *her* kind of ink. Guild ink, guild vellum, guild binding.

You could not outrun a page. But you could burn one.

She had nothing to light. The chandler's matches were in the bundle — no. Wait. Her fingers found, in her inner pocket, the thing she had carried since the Night Market without letting herself think about why: the stub of red festival candle the ferryman had pressed into her hand as change, saying, *for when you must choose what your words are worth.* She had thought it sentiment. Fence-witches dealt in sentiment the way banks dealt in copper — small denominations, always in circulation.

Below, the recitation changed key. *"Instruction received,"* sang twelve throats in unison, and the rings at the tower's base began to move inward, and up.

No more time. She had known there wouldn't be.

"Ines," she said — her own name, testing it, because if this worked she might be about to lose far more than a page. "Remember that. Whatever happens. Someone has to remember that."

She struck the candle against the copper flashing until it caught, cupped the flame, and pulled from her coat the thing she had stolen three nights ago, in the sixty seconds Sef's distraction bought her in the vault antechamber: a single leaf of the master ledger. Not a random one. She had chosen it the way she chose everything. It was the index page — the master key of the master key — the page that mapped every entry to every retrieval order outstanding, including, she had noted with a steadiness that had frightened her, the order bearing her own name.

The retrievers' hands appeared over the parapet edge, grey and many.

Ines touched flame to vellum.

Guild vellum was cured to resist age, damp, and rot. It was not cured to resist what happened next. The page caught with a sound like a gasp, and the fire ran across it *reading as it burned* — she could see the entries flaring alight line by line, *order 114, order 115, order 116, Ishikawa, I.* — and with each line that bloomed and blackened, something answered below. The recitation stumbled. Broke. One retriever on the near roof simply stopped, mid-climb, and stood swaying like a man hearing his own name called from very far away. Another made a sound Ines would spend years not describing: the sound of a consumed memory trying to come back through a throat that no longer had room for it.

The page curled to ash between her fingers. The wind took it. Twelve grey figures stood motionless in the fog-rings below, heads bowed, mouths closed, waiting — she understood suddenly, horribly — for an instruction that no longer existed anywhere in the world.

"Run," she said to the child, and meant it as prayer as much as command.

* * *

They came down the river side of the tower, through the rookery, over the tanner's drying-lines, and did not stop until the fog had swallowed three districts and the bells — hesitant, forgetting themselves — tolled the turn of the hour. In a doorway on a street whose name she had to work to recall, Ines finally let her back find the wall and slid down it, the child folding into the circle of her arms, both of them steaming gently in the cold.

She opened her scorched hand. A fleck of ash clung to the crease of her palm, right along the lifeline, or where the lifeline would be if her life had been left intact enough to have lines.

"There," she said softly. "Now I'm a thief."

Not of gold, not of bread, not of any honest thing. A thief of records. An arsonist of truth. Somewhere in the guild's great architecture of ownership, a hole now gaped where her own name used to point, and there was no version of tomorrow in which Corvane did not feel that hole the way a body feels a missing tooth. He would not send clerks again. Clerks were for retrieving items. What came next would come for *her* — personally, specifically, with the patience of a man collecting a debt he had been carrying for twenty years. She had seen his face in her mind all night without meaning to: the apprentice's face behind the guildmaster's, the hand that had signed away a child's ages four through nine to fund a climb.

Let him come, she thought, and was surprised to find the thought had no fear in it, only arithmetic. She had a candle-stub, a stolen child of the Otherworld, two favors unspent, and one direction: east, toward the cliffs, toward the old astronomer who owed the last covenant-bearer a debt he'd been paying interest on for forty years.

The child tugged her sleeve. Pointed east. Said a word she was almost certain meant *the star-keeper* — and smiled, small and certain, as if the whole night had merely been the long way round to somewhere he had always intended to arrive.

"Yes," Ines said, and got up, and took his hand. "We're going to see Sef."

Behind them, far off in the white streets, a single new voice began to recite — low, unhurried, and dreadfully familiar, a voice she had heard give a thousand orders across a thousand ledgers, now reading aloud the only entry that remained:

*"Item: one clerk, Ishikawa, I. Instruction: bring me the original."*
