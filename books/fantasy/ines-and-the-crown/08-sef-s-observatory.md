## 8. Sef's Observatory

The observatory had been Sef's for forty years, and in all that time the sea below it had never once sounded like this.

He noticed it first as a wrongness in the rhythm of the waves — a hesitation, as though the tide itself were counting. He stood at the open shutter of the cupola with his brass quadrant forgotten in his hands, listening, while behind him the child sat cross-legged on the cold flagstones and Ines Ishikawa stood at the door of the stairwell with her arms folded like a woman guarding a ledger against thieves.

The climb from the harbor road had taken them most of the night. The moon hung low over the water, waning still, but thinner now than any almanac could account for — a fingernail of silver where there should have been half a coin. Sef had charted its decline every night since the child arrived at the Vellum Guild, and every night his figures grew more heretical. The moon was not merely waning. It was *spending*.

"Sit him by the south window," Sef said, though he knew the child was a girl; he had simply not yet learned to say so in any language she answered to. "The glass is old. Don't mind the bubbles in it. They were blown into it by a man who believed flaws let the truth through."

Ines did not move immediately. She was watching him with those gray eyes that seemed perpetually mid-audit, weighing what he cost against what he was worth. He supposed he should have been offended. Instead he found it restful, being appraised by someone honest enough to show her arithmetic.

"You said you'd deciphered the words," she said.

"I said I'd *begun*." Sef set down the quadrant and rubbed his hands together, feeling the ache of old joints and older guilt. "There is a difference between beginning and finishing, Mistress Ishikawa, as your guild has presumably never taught you."

Something flickered across her face — not quite a flinch. He filed it away, the way he filed everything, in the vast disorderly archive of his memory that he called scholarship and other people called obsession.

* * *

Sef had been young once, and arrogant in the way only astronomers are arrogant: certain that the heavens were a text and he alone read them properly. Sixty years ago — no, fifty-eight; he had done the arithmetic on his own guilt often enough to wear the numbers smooth — a woman had come up the cliff path in the rain, carrying a bundle wrapped in oilcloth, asking him to look at the sky and tell her whether she was mad.

She had been the last covenant-bearer. He had not known the word then. He had known only that she spoke of the moon making turns like a lock making revolutions, and of fragments scattered like seeds before a winter, and he had looked at her soaked cloak and her burning eyes and decided she was one more pilgrim come to burden an old man's patience.

"I told her," he said now, aloud, to the room, because Ines had gone quiet and the child was watching him with those enormous dark eyes, "that the moon does not turn. That it is we who turn beneath it. I gave her three minutes and a citation from Halvoric's Almanac."

Ines said nothing. The fire ticked in the grate.

"She went back down the cliff in the rain," Sef continued, "and I went back to my charts, and eleven days later the moon skipped a night. One whole night, gone from the record — not clouded, not eclipsed, *absent*. And I understood, far too late and far too completely, what I had refused to hear." He turned to face them. "So when your guild's scribes sent word of a mud-streaked child speaking dead syllables, I came down from this rock for the first time in nine years. Not out of charity. Out of debt."

The child rose. It happened without ceremony — one moment she was seated, the next she was standing at the great orrery in the center of the room, the brass model of the heavens that Sef's master had built and Sef had maintained and Sef had, in fifty-eight years, never once suspected of being anything more than machinery.

She put her small hand flat upon the ring that represented the moon.

"No—" Sef began, meaning *careful*, meaning *that took my master twenty years*—

Light came out of the orrery.

Not lamplight. Not reflected firelight. It rose through the brass rings like sap through a tree, silver-white and slow, and where it touched the little planets they woke — Sef watched Mars kindle, watched the sun-arm flare gold, watched the whole clockwork heaven shudder into motion with a sound like distant bells underwater. And above the orrery, in the dark air of the cupola, stars began to appear where no stars could be.

They arranged themselves.

Sef had spent his life reading skies, and he had never seen a sky compose itself with such deliberate grammar. Points of light drew lines between themselves, and the lines became a coastline — he knew it instantly, the hook of the Grayhaven peninsula, the long scar of the Salt Road — and then the map spread outward, beyond the realm's familiar edges, into territories no cartographer had ever inked. Five points blazed brighter than the rest, five knots of light strung along a curve that arced from the city to the mountains to the drowned places past the maps' torn edges, and each knot pulsed in time with the child's breathing.

"The Covenant," Ines whispered. She had come forward without seeming to decide to, drawn the way iron is drawn. Her hand hovered over the star-map without touching. "It's a circuit. Look — the five points connect. It's not five separate things, it's one thing broken into five—"

"*Fragments*," Sef breathed. The word arrived in his mouth already knowing itself. "The woman in the rain said fragments. Seeds before a winter." His voice cracked on the old memory. "Scattered so that no single hand could gather them — scattered so that the gathering would require—"

He stopped. Because he had seen it now, in the geometry of the light: the five keepers, rendered as small dim figures at their posts, and beside each figure a second shape, fainter, hollow — the shape of something missing. He had spent fifty-eight years learning to read absence in the heavens. He recognized the notation instantly.

"Each keeper traded away the memory of guarding," he said slowly. "That's the warding. You cannot surrender what you do not know you hold. No torturer can extract it, no thief can steal it, no guildmaster can buy it — because the keeper herself doesn't remember there is anything to give up." He laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, ragged at the edges. "Do you understand what that means? The Covenant hid itself inside forgetting. It used oblivion as armor. Your entire profession, Mistress Ishikawa — your ledgers, your vials, your bottled griefs — your guild has been trading in the raw material of the world's last defense."

Ines had gone very pale. He watched her arrive at the same arithmetic he had, watch her lips move slightly as she counted entries she must have catalogued herself — memories bought cheap from bridge-keepers and lighthouse-wards and well-tenders, odd lots, mislabeled lots, *unremarkable* lots, the inventory nobody bothered to audit closely because nothing about them seemed valuable—

"Oh," she said. Just that. A single syllable containing an avalanche.

The child looked up at her, and spoke — that old language, the one older than kingdoms, the one Sef had spent three sleepless nights matching against proto-liturgical fragments and the glossolalia of anchorites. But tonight, under the starlight, Sef found he could follow the shape of it, if not the substance. The cadence was declarative. Enumerative. A list being recited.

"She's naming them," he whispered. "The keepers. Listen — the inflections change at each point of light. She's giving us the locations."

The first point burned over the Grayhaven peninsula. The second sank toward the flooded lowlands south of the city. The third — Sef's breath caught — the third pulsed directly beneath the spire of the Vellum Guild itself, which meant either that the guild sat unknowing atop a fragment, or that Corvane had already found it and was feeding on it, and Sef did not know which answer was worse. The fourth glimmered far inland, in the high country where the Moonwell was said to be less a place than a rumor. And the fifth...

The fifth point did not sit on the map at all. It hovered at the center, above the child's own heart, a knot of light that beat in time with her pulse, and around it the other four curved like petals around a bloom.

Ines saw it too. Sef watched her see it. Watched the gray eyes go wide, then narrow, then settle into something terrible and calm — the expression, he thought, of an accountant who has just discovered the final entry in the ledger bears her own name.

"No," she said quietly, to the child, to the map, to whatever powers kept books on such things. "No. We'll find another way."

The child reached up and touched Ines's hand — just touched it, briefly, the way one touches a door one is not sure one is permitted to knock upon — and said something short and soft, and though Sef could not translate it, he had been married once, long ago, and he knew the grammar of reassurance when he heard it.

* * *

Later, when the orrery had gone dark and the false stars had guttered out and the child slept wrapped in Sef's oldest coat by the fire, the two of them stood at the south window looking at the wounded moon.

"Nine turns remain," Sef said. "Perhaps eight. My figures have a margin of error I no longer trust." He turned the brass sextant over in his hands, not using it, just holding it, the way a penitent holds a rosary. "I want to tell you something, Mistress Ishikawa, and then I would prefer you never mention it again."

Ines waited. She was good at waiting. He suspected it was the only form of violence she permitted herself.

"Fifty-eight years ago I turned away the last covenant-bearer, and the world has been paying interest on my refusal ever since. Every night the moon skips, every name that fades from a signboard, every dream that comes up empty — some portion of that is mine. Compounded." He set the sextant down on the sill with a click that sounded obscenely loud. "I am eighty-one years old. I will not live to see the thirteenth turn regardless of how this ends. Which means that everything I do from tonight onward is, in the strictest sense, worthless to me."

He looked at her then, straight on, letting her audit him if she wished.

"So understand me clearly. I am not helping you out of kindness, or redemption, or any of the other currencies old men spend when they have nothing real left. I am settling accounts. The Grayhaven keeper — the first fragment — I know that coast. I surveyed it as a young man. There is a bridge there that appears on no chart, tolls no passage, and yet the fishermen leave offerings on it every spring tide. I can take you there. I can take you to all of them, if my heart holds out, and if it doesn't, I will die on the road pointing the way, and consider it a fair rate of exchange."

Ines studied him for a long moment. Outside, the sea counted its hesitant waves, and the thin moon laid a blade of light across the floor between them.

"You said the third fragment is under the guild," she said at last. Her voice was level, but Sef heard the strain in it, the sound of a ledger balanced on a knife's edge. "Corvane will have felt it by now. If he hasn't already consumed part of it. And when he realizes the child is gone — when he works out where she'd be taken—"

"He'll come here," Sef agreed. "Or he'll send his retrievers. Weaponized clerks." He smiled thinly. "I've read your guild's procurement records, Mistress Ishikawa. Did you know the budget line for 'memory acquisition, coercive' has tripled in four years? Someone has been doing arithmetic of their own."

"We need to leave before dawn."

"We need to leave *now*." Sef was already moving, pulling charts from their tubes, stuffing bread and dried fish into a canvas satchel, his hands remembering expeditions from forty years ago. "And there's one more thing. The bridge-keeper at Grayhaven — the one who sold her name. When we find her, someone will have to give her back a memory. That's how the warding breaks, yes? The star-map showed it plainly enough. A payment in reverse."

Ines had gone still by the fire. The sleeping child's breath lifted the coat's collar gently, up and down, up and down.

"I know how payments work," Ines said.

"Yes," Sef said softly. "I imagine you do. And I imagine that whoever pays will need memories worth having — not the dross your guild bottles by the gross, but true ones, load-bearing ones." He slung the satchel over his shoulder and met her eyes. "So count yours carefully tonight, Mistress Ishikawa. While you still know what you're counting."

Above the cliffs, the thin moon slid behind a cloud, and the sea went on hesitating, wave after wave after wave, like a tide trying to remember which shore it belonged to.
