## 10. Corvane's Offer

The summons came the way a knife comes: quietly, and already inside you.

A clerk in dove-grey livery found Ines at her desk on the third morning after the bridge-keeper's door had opened for them — three mornings of careful, unremarkable work, of ink and columns and the performance of a woman who had not burned a page of the master ledger or smuggled anything out through the Night Market. The clerk set a card on her blotter without meeting her eyes. The card was vellum, of course. Everything at the Guild was vellum; it was the one material they trusted to hold what flesh could not.

*Master Corvane requests your presence. Tonight. The Amber Room.*

No hour was given. No reason. The Guild did not deal in reasons; it dealt in entries, and an entry either balanced or it didn't. Ines turned the card over twice, looking for a second line, a postscript, some small mercy of context. There was nothing. Corvane never wasted words on paper. He saved them all for the moment they could do the most damage.

She worked until the light went thin and grey, because refusing to tremble is easier when your hands are full. She catalogued a bottled grief from the western provinces — a widow's last afternoon with her husband, decanted and stoppered, the glass fogging faintly when she held it, as though the sorrow inside were still warm. She entered its weight, its provenance, its shelf. Her pen did not shake. She had trained it not to, years ago, in a childhood she could not remember training it in.

That thought followed her down the stairwells like a cat that knew the way better than she did.

* * *

The Amber Room occupied the top floor of the Guild's oldest tower, and it earned its name honestly. Every surface that could be glass was glass, and every pane had been poured with amber resin in which things were caught — not insects, here, not leaves. Memories. Whole memories, mid-motion: a wedding dance suspended in honeyed gold, a soldier's homecoming frozen with his arms still open, a child's first snowfall hanging like a held breath. They were the Guildmaster's private collection, the ones too valuable or too terrible to shelve in the vaults below, and walking into the room felt like walking into the inside of someone else's skull.

Corvane stood at the far window with his back to her, hands clasped behind him, watching the city go dark beneath a moon that had worn itself down to a ragged crescent. He was not a large man. He had never needed to be. He had the particular stillness of someone who had consumed enough other lives to no longer require urgency about his own.

"You've been busy," he said, without turning.

"Month-end audit," Ines said. "There's always inventory."

"Mm." He let the silence stretch until it creaked. Then he turned, and his face was exactly as she remembered it from every guild assembly: mild, scholarly, faintly amused, the face of a man who might ask you to pass the salt or ruin your life, and would use the same tone for both. "Sit, Ishikawa."

There was only one chair, positioned before his desk, and she understood the geometry of it immediately. She sat anyway. Refusing the chair would have been refusing the conversation, and she wanted the conversation. She wanted it the way a starving woman wants a meal she suspects is poisoned.

He poured two glasses of something amber himself — actual drink, not memory — and set one within her reach. She did not touch it.

"The child," he said, settling against the edge of the desk rather than behind it, which was its own kind of message. *We are colleagues. We are reasonable people.* "Where is it?"

"I don't know what you mean."

"Ines." Her name, spoken gently, the way you'd speak to a horse you didn't want spooking. "I have read your ledgers for eleven years. I know your hand better than I know my own signature. I know how your numerals lean when you're lying — leftward, always leftward, as if even your numbers flinch away from the truth." He smiled. It was almost kind. "You wrote the appraisal report on the bridge-keeper yourself. You described a door that opens to a memory freely given. And then the keeper's ledger shows a withdrawal. One memory, returned. Yours, I'd wager, given whose hand countersigned the transfer."

Ines kept her breathing level. Leftward-leaning numerals. Eleven years of being read like a document by the man who signed her wages. She filed the humiliation somewhere cold, where she could examine it later, or never.

"The child is safe," she said. "Which is more than I can say for whatever you intended."

"Intended?" Corvane laughed softly, and crossed to one of the amber panes, laying two fingers against it the way a physician touches a patient. Inside the resin, the wedding dance went on forever, the bride's mouth open around a laugh no one would ever hear again. "You think we meant it harm? Ines. Sit — you are sitting. Good. Then let me tell you what the Guild actually does, since you've been keeping our books so faithfully without once asking what they balance toward."

* * *

He told her standing up, pacing slowly between the amber panes, and he told it beautifully, because of course he did; a man who devoured lives had eaten enough storytellers to have learned the craft.

"The Covenant is dying. You know this now — the child made sure of that, didn't it, whispering in its dead language about turns and tides. Thirteen turns, and then the severance. Two realms torn apart along a seam older than kingdoms. And everything the seam held together comes undone." He ticked them off on his fingers, a merchant's gesture, an auditor's gesture, and she hated him a little for using her own grammar. "Magic, yes. But also memory — the deep memory, the kind that lets a grandmother recognize her daughter's face in her granddaughter's. Mercy — the instinct that makes a stranger wade into a river for another stranger. Those aren't virtues, Ines. They're provisions of the treaty. Clauses. When the Covenant fails, they lapse, like any contract lapses. People won't become cruel. They'll simply forget cruelty was ever avoidable."

"And the Guild hoards," Ines said. "Memories. Bottled years. Griefs and joys by the thousand in the vaults below us."

"Provisions," Corvane agreed pleasantly. "For a long winter. Do you understand what I'm offering you, Ishikawa? Not power. Survival. When the thirteenth turn closes and the world goes quiet and colorless and kind-less, there will be a handful of people who remember what mercy was. Who can teach it again, if teaching is even possible afterward. The masters of the Vellum Guild intend to be those people. Someone has to be. Better us than the dark."

It was a good speech. It was, Ines thought, watching his face in the amber light, possibly even a true speech — true the way a well-cut gemstone is true, all facets and fire and nothing living anywhere inside it. Because she had seen the vaults. She had catalogued what came down into them. A bottled grief was not a provision; grief was not grain to be stored against famine. A grief in a bottle was a grief taken from someone who needed it, because grief was how a widow finished loving her husband, and a woman who never got to finish loving stayed broken forever. The Guild didn't preserve feeling. It amputated it and called the wound an archive.

"You consume them," she said. "The covenant-memories. The fragments' keepers — the ones who traded their memories of guarding. Where do those trades go?"

Something moved behind Corvane's eyes. It was very small and very fast, and she would have missed it eleven years ago. She was not eleven years ago.

"Consumption is such an ugly word," he said.

"It's the accurate one. I've been translating the child, Master. I know what happens to a person who eats what isn't theirs. The retrievers you send after us — clerks who move like cats and don't bleed right. I've seen their eyes. There's nothing behind them but borrowed light." She rose from the chair, because sitting felt like agreeing. "You've been feeding your people fragments. Little ones, splinters, the crumbs the Covenant has been shedding for centuries. Enough to make your hounds strong and your masters stronger. And when the moon finishes dying, you'll swallow the rest and call yourselves the ark."

Corvane regarded her for a long moment. Outside, the crescent moon cleared the tower across the street, thin as a paring, white as bone.

"You always were the best of them," he said. "The only one who read the entries closely enough to be dangerous. Which brings me —" and here his voice changed, shed its silk, became something plainer and therefore more frightening, "— to why you're actually here."

* * *

He opened the desk drawer and took out a single vial.

It was smaller than the ones in the vaults. Older. The glass had gone slightly opalescent at the shoulders, the way old glass does, and the stopper was sealed with wax stamped with a sigil she recognized from the deep archive's oldest ledgers — the mark of the Guild as it had been forty years ago, before Corvane, before the current masters, back when the ledgers were written in a different hand entirely.

Inside the vial, something shifted. Not liquid. Light, maybe. Or weather. It moved the way memory moves when you try to hold it still — sliding sideways, refusing the shape you give it.

Ines knew what it was before he said the label aloud. She knew it the way she had half-known the child's language: not learning it, recognizing it. Some part of her had been waiting for this vial for twenty years without knowing there was a word for waiting.

"This," Corvane said, "is yours."

"My name is on the deep-archive register," she said. Her voice sounded far away to her own ears, flat as pressed vellum. "Entry four-one-one. Sold, not stolen. Provenance: private party. Buyer: guild access required. I found the entry myself, three weeks ago. So either you're lying to me now, or someone lied to me for twenty years, and I confess I'm struggling to imagine which possibility you'd prefer me to believe."

"Both are true," Corvane said. "That's the elegance of it. You were sold — that entry is genuine, and I've never once falsified a ledger; falsification is for amateurs. But the seller was not a private party. The seller was an apprentice with debts and ambition and access to a family foolish enough to trust him." He turned the vial in his fingers, and the light inside it turned with it, and Ines felt the room tilt very slightly, as though the tower were leaning in to listen. "A boy of nineteen. Brilliant. Hungry. The sort of hungry that mistakes appetite for destiny. He sold your childhood — all nine years of it, the whole unbroken run — to fund his first acquisition. A modest purchase, at the time. A minor noble's memory of a coronation. It made his reputation. He built everything since on top of it."

The amber room was very quiet. Somewhere below, the city murmured its evening murmur, unaware.

"You," Ines said.

"Me," Corvane agreed, without shame, without apology, with something worse than either: satisfaction. As though his cruelty were a craftsmanship and he was pleased to have it recognized. "I have regretted many things, Ishikawa, but I will not insult you by pretending to regret that. If I hadn't sold your childhood, I'd have sold someone else's, and then you'd be standing here having this conversation about a stranger, and you wouldn't care half so much. Consider it providence. Consider it the market. Consider it whatever helps you sleep."

"Why show me this?" The question came out steadier than she deserved. "Why now?"

"Because I want to make you whole." He set the vial on the desk between them, precisely centered, precisely aligned with the desk's edge, because he knew — he had always known — that order was her religion. "Every year of it. The mother whose face you've spent two decades unable to picture. The house. The winter you learned to write. The reason your pen doesn't shake. All of it, intact, uncatalogued, yours. No price. No trade. A gift." He smiled again, and this time the smile reached all the way to his eyes, which was the most terrifying thing she had seen all night. "All you have to do is bring me the child."

* * *

The silence that followed was not empty. It was full — crowded with nine years pressing against the glass of their prison, with a mother's face assembling itself out of nothing at the edge of Ines's imagination, with the smell of a house she had never been able to smell, with the specific weight of being a child in a specific bed in a specific city on a specific night, all of it hovering an arm's length away, stoppered, patient, hers.

She made herself look at it the way she looked at any entry: coldly, completely, line by line.

If she took it, she would finally be a person with a past. She would know why she trusted ink more than people. She would know whether the loneliness was temperament or damage, and whether damage could be named could perhaps be mended. She would look in mirrors and see a history instead of a stranger. Twenty years of vertigo, ended with one unstoppering.

And the child —

The child, who had crossed between worlds alone. Who had chosen exile to save realms it would never see grow old. Who spoke a language older than kingdoms and had used its first words in this realm, as far as Ines could reconstruct, to say *the door must hold*. Who had asked her for nothing. Not once. Not shelter, not comfort, not even translation given freely — the child had accepted her help the way water accepts a channel, without entitlement and without surprise, as though being helped were simply what the Covenant promised and the child had never stopped believing the promise.

She thought of the bridge-keeper, who had sold her name and guarded a door she couldn't explain, and who had wept — silently, confusedly, like weather — when Ines gave her back one small memory of her mother's hands. One memory. The keeper had called it *enough*, in the ruined grammar of the forgotten, and had opened the door.

One memory had been enough for a door.

Nine years was not a payment. Nine years was a ransom, and ransoms were paid by people who valued what they had over what they owed.

"No," Ines said.

Corvane exhaled through his nose — not quite a sigh, not quite a laugh. "You haven't even asked what happens to the child."

"I know what happens. It goes into an amber pane. Or a vial. Or whatever vessel holds a fragment of the Covenant while you learn to digest it. And on the thirteenth turn, when you swallow it whole, the last clause of the treaty dies in your stomach, and you get to be king of a silence." She picked up the vial. His eyebrows rose — she saw him recalculate, thinking she'd changed her mind, thinking the hunger had won — and she watched him watch her hold her entire childhood in her palm like a coin she was weighing.

Then she set it back down on the desk, in its exact center, aligned with the edge, untouched.

"Keep it," she said. "It's the only honest thing you own. And I want you to know — write this in whatever ledger you keep behind your eyes — that I will come back for it. Not as payment. As confiscation. When this is over, every vial in this room, every pane, every entry in your garden of stolen lives goes back to the people it was taken from. Including mine. *Especially* mine."

For the first time, Corvane's stillness cracked. It was a hairline fracture, visible only to someone who had spent eleven years reading the lean of numerals, but it was there: the flicker of a man discovering that the document he thought he owned had been writing itself the whole time.

"You're sentimental," he said. "It will get you killed."

"I'm an auditor," Ines said. "You taught me that. Everything balances, Master. Even you."

She walked to the door. Behind her, the amber room glowed with its harvest of suspended lives, and Corvane stood among them like a gardener surveying a blight.

"Ishikawa." His voice followed her, mild again, reasonable again, and all the more chilling for it. "The moon turns tomorrow night. After that, offers expire. And I should mention — Sef is already in my keeping. Has been since the bone orchard. He's a resilient old man, but resilience is just memory with stubbornness, and stubbornness digests poorly." A pause, delicately measured. "Bring the child to the Moonwell road by turn twelve, and we'll discuss the astronomer's release. Bring anyone else, and we'll discuss his consumption instead."

The door closed behind her with a sound like a period at the end of a sentence.

Ines stood in the dark stairwell with her heart going like a trapped bird, and did the arithmetic she had been born to do. Four days until turn twelve. Sef's life priced against the child's freedom. Her childhood locked in a drawer as bait. And somewhere across the city, a small mute figure was waiting for her, trusting her, believing in doors.

She started down the stairs, taking them two at a time, toward the Night Market, toward the fences and the ferryman, toward the only currency she had left.

She was going to need a miracle, a map, and a very sharp knife — and she knew exactly where to find all three.
