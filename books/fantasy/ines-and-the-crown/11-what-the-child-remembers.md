## 11. What the Child Remembers

Sleep did not come easily to Ines anymore. It came the way tide comes to a shore that has learned to distrust it — slowly, then all at once, and always taking something with it when it went.

She had made a bed for the child in Sef's observatory, in the alcove beneath the great brass orrery, where the turning spheres ticked like a clock counting down something no one wanted to name. The child slept there now, curled small beneath a quilt stitched with constellations so old half of them no longer existed. Ines had taken the cot by the window, within reach of the door, within reach of the child, within reach — though she would never have admitted it aloud — of the sound of the child's breathing, which was the only thing in three kingdoms that made the waning feel survivable.

The moon hung low over the cliff. Ninth turn. Two nights since the tide-magic had begun eating names out of the city below, and even here, even this far from the streets, she could feel it working at the edges of her: a softness where certainty used to be, a word on the tip of her tongue dissolving before she could bite down on it.

She slept.

And the child's dream found her the way water finds a crack.

* * *

She knew immediately that it was not her own dream, because her own dreams were ledgers — endless columns of figures, vials labeled in her own careful hand, the vault doors she checked and rechecked in the hours when checking was the only prayer she trusted. This was nothing like that.

This was light.

Not sunlight. Sunlight fell; this rose. It came up out of the ground itself, silver-white and warm as breath, and it moved through everything the way sap moves through a tree — patiently, from the inside. Ines stood in a forest that was not any forest she had ever audited, and every leaf overhead held its own small moon, and the air tasted the way the first line of a poem tastes, before meaning arrives to complicate it.

*You are seeing what I see,* said a voice that was not a voice. It arrived the way the child's language arrived — not through the ear but through some older door, the one behind the sternum. *I am sorry. I tried to keep you out. But you have been carrying my words for weeks now, and dreams are only words that got loose.*

Ines turned. The child stood among the trees — but not the mud-streaked, hollow-eyed child who had knocked on the guild door before dawn. This one wore robes the color of deep water, and their hair was braided with threads of light, and they stood the way people stand in the place they were born: without apology.

*Where are we?* Ines asked, and heard her own voice arrive strangely, doubled, as if the dream translated her even to herself.

*Where I began,* the child said. *Before I had a name anyone here could pronounce. Before I was sent.*

They walked. Ines did not know how she knew to walk, but the dream carried them both forward the way a river carries two leaves, side by side, not quite touching. And as they walked, the Otherworld showed itself to her — and she understood, with a vertigo that had nothing to do with heights, why the Covenant had been necessary, and why its breaking felt less like politics and more like a wound.

There were cities here. Cities grown rather than built, towers of pale wood that sang in the wind, bridges spun between hills by spiders the size of dogs who wove with moonlight instead of silk. There were people — or beings shaped like people, mostly, though some were shaped like weather, like herds, like slow green thoughts — and all of them glowed faintly, all of them carried that rising light in their chests, and all of them, Ines realized as she watched a market square full of luminous strangers trade songs and seeds and years, remembered everything.

That was the difference. That was the whole terrible difference.

*Nothing is forgotten here,* the child said, watching her watch them. *That is what the light is. Memory is not something we keep. It is something we are made of. When someone dies here, they do not leave. They pour back into the ground, and the ground remembers them, and the trees grow a little taller, and the songs get longer.* A pause, and something old moved through the young voice. *Your world forgot us because your world forgets everything. We loved you anyway. That was the first clause of the Covenant, though your scribes never wrote it down: the realm that remembers loves the realm that forgets, and does not hold it against them.*

*What did your world get in return?* Ines asked. She heard the ledger-clerk in her own voice and did not flinch from it. Terms. Every pact had terms. She had spent her life reading the fine print of other people's bargains.

*Continuance,* the child said simply. *Your forgetting is a kind of gravity. Without an anchor in a world that lets things go, memory becomes weight. We would have drowned in our own pasts, eventually. Every song getting longer, every grief kept forever, nothing ever allowed to end.* The child looked up through the leaves at a sky where the moon hung full and close and impossibly detailed, a coin pressed against glass. *The Covenant let us give you our magic and take from you our mercy. Forgetting. Letting go. You lent us your endings, and we lent you our beginnings, and the moon counted the turns between us, thirteen turns to a cycle, and each turn the well was renewed and the pact ran on.*

*And then it cracked.*

The child stopped walking.

The forest stopped too. Ines felt it stop — the light dimming in the leaves one by one, like lamps being shuttered down a long street — and she understood that she was not seeing a memory of the breaking. She was standing inside the moment of it, because the child had never left this moment. The child had been living inside it ever since, the way a splinter lives inside a palm.

*It cracked the way everything cracks,* the child said. *From carelessness. Not malice. Malice would have been easier to forgive.* They knelt and touched the ground, and where their fingers rested, Ines saw it: a hairline fracture running through the glowing soil, thin as a scratch on glass, and through the fracture — nothing. A darkness that was not darkness but absence, the specific black of a page where writing used to be. *A keeper at the Moonwell grew tired. Only tired — do you understand? Not wicked. Tired the way your guildmasters pretend to be important, tired the way mountains are heavy. He skipped one renewal. One turn, uncounted. He meant to catch up. Everyone means to catch up.*

The fracture spread as they watched. Ines saw forests gutter. She saw a bridge of woven moonlight sag and dissolve mid-span, and she heard — this was the worst of it, this was the thing she knew would follow her out of sleep and live under her ribs — she heard the singing towers change key, the way a room changes when someone dies in it.

*When the Covenant cracks, it does not break all at once,* the child said. *It breaks the way ice breaks on a thawing river — in pieces, in groans, in long silences between. And each piece that breaks off has to go somewhere. Memory cannot simply vanish. It falls.* The child looked up at her, and in the dream-light Ines saw for the first time how old the eyes were, how much falling they had witnessed. *Five pieces broke away. Five fragments of the first clause, the oldest clause, the one that says the realms are bound. If they fall all the way — if they come loose entirely — the severance completes, and your world forgets us so completely that we will never have existed, and our world drowns in its own undying past, and both of us go silent. Forever, in both directions.*

*So someone caught them.*

*I caught them.* The child stood. The light in their chest brightened as they said it, and Ines saw — she saw with her auditor's eye, the eye that could not help reading every entry twice — that the child's body was not holding the fragments the way a purse holds coins. It was holding them the way a wound holds a knife. Four points of pressure she could almost see, four places where the borrowed light strained against the small frame containing it, and a fifth place, empty, shaped exactly like the hollow she had felt in herself since the night the child knocked. *I was born near the well. Born of the well, really — the last year of the last cycle, when the keeper's neglect was already showing, the well made me differently than it makes other children. It made me able to carry. No one else could. Carrying kills anything that remembers too completely, and everyone here remembers too completely. But I was new. I was barely written yet. There was room in me.*

*Who chose it?* Ines asked, though she already dreaded the shape of the answer. *Did the elders choose? Your parents?*

The child was quiet for a long time. Around them the frozen forest held its broken note, patient as a held breath.

*I chose it,* the child said at last. *That is the part no one believes afterward. They want it to have been done to me. It is kinder, they think, to be stolen from than to give away. But I stood at the well with the five fragments in five vessels, and I looked at the crack, and I looked at the dark coming through it, and I was — * the voice did something complicated, something between pride and grief — *I was the only one small enough to fit through. The crack was narrow. It is still narrow. A fragment can pass where a whole person cannot. So I took them into myself, and I let the crack take me, and I fell.*

*Fell where?*

*Here.* The dream shifted underfoot like a deck tilting. The forest dissolved upward, the light draining out of it, and suddenly they were falling together through a grey vastness between worlds, and Ines felt the cold of it, the specific cold of a place that belongs to neither country. Below them — impossibly far, impossibly near — two lights: one silver-green, one gold-dim, the Otherworld and the human realm seen from outside, from the gap, from the nowhere the child had crossed alone. *Into your world. Into the mud and the curfews and the guilds that bottle grief and sell it by the dram. I fell for what felt like a hundred years and arrived in a single night, and I walked until I found the one building in your city that smelled of memory, because I thought — * a small, terrible laugh — *I thought a guild that trades in memories would recognize what I am. Would know what to do.*

*They appraised you,* Ines said. The word came out flat and filthy.

*They weighed me,* the child agreed. *Like flour.*

The falling slowed. They descended together toward the gold-dim world, toward a city Ines recognized with a lurch — her city, seen from above, its rooftops familiar as handwriting — and the child spoke again, softer now, and this was the part Ines would spend the rest of her life unable to put down:

*I need to tell you what I never managed to say in your language, because your language has no tense for it. I am not asking you to save me. Do you understand? I chose exile. I chose the falling. I will choose the spending, when the time comes, if it comes to that — a fragment given freely at the well renews the Covenant for another full cycle, and a fragment taken by force buys nothing but silence. Everything Corvane wants to steal, I am already giving away. That is the whole secret of me, Ines Ishikawa. I am not cargo. I am a letter, and I already know what I cost to send, and I regret nothing except that the postage was paid in other people's sorrow.* The child's hand found hers in the dream — small, warm, certain. *You have been asking yourself what I want from you. Why you, why your door, why your dreams. Here is the answer, and I am sorry it is not larger: you were simply the first person who listened. The rest — the mother-word, the recognition, the way your blood answers my language — that is yours to learn and mine to keep quiet about. I ask nothing. I have never asked anything. Asking is what takers do, and I was made to be given.*

*That is not—* Ines started, and the dream began to tear, edges fraying into the grey of pre-dawn, the observatory sounds leaking in around her, the tick of the orrery, the sea against the cliff. *That is not how it works. You don't get to decide alone what you're worth. I kept ledgers for eleven years. I know what things cost. And nothing costs what the taker says it costs—*

*I know,* the child said, and smiled, and the smile was the last thing she saw clearly, luminous and sad and older than kingdoms. *That is why I knocked on your door.*

* * *

Ines woke with salt on her face and did not remember crying, which frightened her more than the tears themselves would have. Her ledger-mind, that faithful machine, was already moving: recording, cross-referencing, filing the dream's testimony in columns she suspected she would need sooner than she wished. Five fragments. Four carried, one hollow. Freely given or worthless. The child had told her the terms of her own quest as plainly as a bill of sale.

Beside her, in the alcove beneath the turning spheres, the child slept on, breathing slow, the quilt rising and falling. And in the grey light Ines saw what exhaustion and urgency had let her miss: the faint glow beneath the child's skin, four small points of straining light, like windows in a house whose owner has gone out and left the lamps burning.

Sef was already awake at his charts, quill scratching, and he looked up when she sat, and whatever he read in her face made him set the quill down.

"You've been across," he said. Not a question.

"She told me where the next fragment is." Ines heard her own voice come out steady, clerk-calm, and was grateful to it. "The second piece fell into water. Deep water. Old water." She closed her eyes and let the dream's geography surface one last time before it faded: a sunken hall, shelves going down into green dark, and something moving between the stacks that was made of every book never written. "There's a library, Sef. Drowned. And it's guarded."

Sef went pale in the way of a man confirming a fear he'd carried for fifty years. "Then may whatever gods are left have mercy on your poems," he said quietly, "because the Drowned Archive does not take coin, and it does not take favors. It takes only what cannot be replaced."

Ines looked at her satchel, where Sef's oldest poem lay folded — the one thing she had brought from her old life besides her hands.

"I know," she said. "I've already read the entry."
