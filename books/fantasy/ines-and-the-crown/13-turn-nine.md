## 13. Turn Nine

Sef had spent sixty years learning to read the sky, and in all that time the sky had never once read him back.

Now it did. He felt it in his teeth, in the old scar along his knuckle where a telescope's brass fitting had bitten him at nineteen, in the marrow-deep ache behind his eyes that no amount of willow bark could touch. The moon hung above the observatory like a coin held under a debtor's nose, and every night it was thinner, and every night the thinning cost the world something.

He had named the phenomenon in his charts with a scholar's cold precision: *tidal bleed*. The Covenant was a wall between two oceans, and the wall was failing, and through the cracks the Otherworld's water — its magic, its memory, its terrible liquidity — was seeping into the human realm the way the sea seeps into a cellar. Not all at once. Drop by drop. Turn by turn.

He was charting the drops.

* * *

The observatory smelled of cold iron, lamp oil, and the ghost of every star he had ever failed to name. Sef sat at his long table with nine days of observations spread before him in columns of his own cramped hand, and he did not like what the columns were doing.

He had built the table himself, forty years ago, from a single plank of storm-felled cedar, and he had built it to hold instruments. Instead it held grief in ledger form. Column one: the date. Column two: the moon's width, measured against a brass ring he had calibrated against the solstice suns of three consecutive years. Column three: anomalies reported in the city below — the fishmonger on Tanner's Row who had sold the same mackerel to the same widow twice and sworn on his mother's grave it was the first time; the ferryman's daughter who had stood at the water stairs for an hour calling a name that no one in the household recognized; the bell-ringer of Saint Aldhelm's who had rung the noon bell at midnight and wept without knowing why.

Column four he had left blank for the first eight days, because he had not known what to write in it. Tonight he wrote: *acceleration confirmed.*

The moon did not wane evenly. That was the first thing. A natural waning was a slope; this was a staircase, and the stairs were getting steeper. Between the first turn and the second, the moon had lost the width of a fingernail. Between the eighth turn and the ninth — last night, the night just past, the night whose light was even now fading off his instruments — it had lost the width of a hand. And the bleed had come through in proportion. What had been a seep was becoming a pour.

He dipped his pen. His hand, he noticed, was not entirely steady, and he was honest enough with himself to admit it was not age. He was seventy-one years old and his hands had been steady through two plagues, one fire, and the death of his wife, because grief, however vast, is at least a known quantity. This was not. This was the sky coming apart in arithmetic, and arithmetic had always been his religion.

*Rate of loss, day nine,* he wrote. *Approximately three times the rate of day four. If the curve holds, the final turns will not be turns at all. They will be a fall.*

He set down the pen and rubbed his eyes, and in the black behind his lids he saw the child's face — the mute child with the covenant-light banked in them like a coal under ash — the way Ines had described the drawing: five fragments, five keepers, each keeper having sold the memory of their own guardianship. He thought: *the world is being unwritten by people who were paid to forget it,* and he thought: *and I am one of them.*

Because that was the guilt he carried up this cliff every evening like a second set of instruments. Thirty years ago — no, thirty-one; he had done the arithmetic so many times the number had worn smooth as a river stone — a woman had come to his door at dusk with a child in her arms and a story on her lips, and she had spoken of the Covenant, and he had looked at her over the threshold of his comfortable, candle-lit, peer-reviewed life, and he had said: *madness. The Covenant is mythology. Good evening.*

She had not argued. That was the worst of it. She had only looked at him with an expression he had spent three decades failing to name, and then she had gone down the cliff path into the dark with her child, and he had returned to his charts of ordinary stars, and the world had continued, and he had told himself for thirty years that he had been right to be cautious, prudent, rigorous.

Now the sky was proving him wrong at a rate of three-to-one and accelerating.

* * *

The knock came at the fourth bell of the morning, which meant it was Ines, because Ines was the only person alive who knocked on Sef's door as though the door might be doing her a favor by existing.

He opened it to grey light and colder wind. Ines stood on the threshold with the child wrapped in a borrowed coat three sizes too large, and behind them the city spread out below the cliff like a bed someone had slept badly in. Sef had lived above the city for forty years and had never tired of the view, but this morning the view tired him. The colors were wrong. Not gone — he would have noticed gone; he was a man who noticed — but *thinned*, as if someone had stretched the city's paint over a larger canvas than it was made for. The harbor water was a grey he did not recognize from any of his color charts. The red roofs of the weavers' quarter were the red of an old wound.

"You see it too," Ines said. It was not a question. She came in without waiting, and the child came with her, and the child stopped just inside the door and turned their face slowly, slowly around the observatory — the orrery, the star charts, the long cedar table — with the expression of someone recognizing a room from a dream.

"They haven't slept," Ines said. "Neither have I. The child keeps — " She stopped. Sef watched her choose her words with the care of a woman defusing something. "They hum. All night. The same four notes, over and over. And every time they finish the fourth note, somewhere in the city, a dog starts howling. I counted. Eleven dogs. The same eleven, I think. I've started to know them by voice."

"Sit," Sef said. "Both of you. I'll make the bitter tea."

"Don't make the bitter tea."

"I'm making the bitter tea. It's the only tea I have, and we are past the point where taste matters." He put the kettle on his small iron stove and turned back to the table, and while his back was to them he allowed his face to do what it wanted, which was to crumple slightly and then reassemble, because he was seventy-one years old and had practice at that too.

When he turned around, the child was standing at his charts.

They could not read — Ines had told him that much, though Sef had his doubts; the child's eyes moved along the columns with the deliberation of someone reading something *other* than words. They stopped at column four, the one Sef had filled in at midnight. *Acceleration confirmed.* The child put one small finger on the ink, and the ink — Sef would swear this to his grave, and had in fact begun drafting the sworn statement — the ink *shivered*, like a puddle touched.

"Tell me what they're saying," Sef said quietly. "You half-understand them. You told me. I want everything. Half of everything, if that's what there is."

Ines sat down heavily in his second chair, the one with the broken cane that no one sat in. "It's not words," she said. "I've tried to translate it as words and it comes out nonsense. It's more like — you know when you walk into a house where someone has been crying, and no one says anything, but you know? It's like that, but with meaning inside it. Last night they were trying to tell me something about the harbor. About water remembering." She laughed, and it was a terrible sound, a sound like a ledger being torn. "Water *remembering*, Sef. I keep books for a living. I know what remembering costs. And a child who can't speak is telling me the sea is starting to do it."

The kettle screamed. He took it off, poured three cups of the bitter tea, and brought them over, and the child took theirs in both hands and held it under their chin and did not drink, and Sef had the sudden, unshakable certainty that the child was warming their hands on the *smell* of it, on the idea of it, the way you'd warm yourself on a memory of fire.

"I want to show you something," he said.

He cleared a space on the cedar table and laid out the maps. Not his star charts — his other charts, the ones he had begun eight days ago and told no one about, the ones he would have been ashamed to show a colleague and could no longer afford shame. They were maps of the city, one for each turn of the moon, and on each map he had marked, in red, every reported amnesia. Not the grand amnesias — the city had no shortage of drunks and fevers and ordinary griefs — but the specific kind. The mid-sentence kind. The kind where a man stopped with his hand on his wife's shoulder and a question in his eyes. The kind where a woman set a table for five and could not have said, under torture, who the fifth place was for.

The first map, from the first turn, had four marks.

The ninth map, from last night, had sixty-one.

He laid them in order and let Ines look, and he watched her do what she did — he had come to admire it, the way one admires a blade — watched her read the maps the way she read everything, completely and without mercy. Her finger went to the marks. They clustered, he had noticed, near water. The harbor. the canal district. The fountains. The wells.

"They're following the tide," she said.

"The tide," Sef agreed, "or the tide is following them. I genuinely no longer know which. But look at the pattern across the nine days." He touched the first map, then the last. "The marks are not only increasing. They are *converging*. Day one, they're scattered like spilled salt. Day nine — " he traced it with his finger — "they're drawing lines. And the lines, Ines, if you extend them — " he did not finish, because the child finished for him.

The child set down the untouched tea, took the pencil from Sef's own hand with a courtesy so gentle it broke his heart, and drew. Three strokes. The lines of the amnesias extended, converged, and met at a point on the map that Sef had marked long ago in a different ink, in a different life, for a different reason: the old quarter. The Moonwell. The place the Covenant had been signed, if it had been signed anywhere, which he had spent thirty years believing it had not.

The child looked up at him. And though they made no sound, Sef heard — he would have sworn on every chart he owned — heard, in the silence, the four notes of the hum, completed.

"Yes," he said, uselessly, in his own tongue. "Yes. We know. That's where it ends. That's where it has to end."

* * *

He sent Ines and the child to sleep in the back room, on the cot he kept for the nights when the observations ran past dawn, and he stayed at the table because he was an old man and old men sleep badly anyway and someone had to watch the sky.

What he watched, in the hour before dawn, was the tide.

The harbor lay below the cliff, and the tide was coming in, and it was coming in *wrong*. He had measured this harbor's tides for forty years. He knew their rhythm the way he knew his own pulse. And this tide came in like a sentence being spoken by someone learning the language — forward, back, forward, a hesitation, forward again — as if the water itself were rehearsing. And each time it hesitated, each time it drew back from the sea-stairs with a sound like an indrawn breath, one of the lamps along the quay guttered.

He began, then, the last entry in the night's ledger, the one he had been avoiding since moonrise, the one that was not arithmetic at all. He wrote:

*Day nine. I have spent my life measuring the distance between stars and calling it knowledge. I believed the sky was a machine and I was its honest auditor. I was wrong. The sky is a promise, and we are the collateral, and I refused to read the terms thirty-one years ago when a woman stood at my door and offered me the contract.*

*The bleed is no longer seeping. Today the fishmonger's widow — no. Today my neighbor, Marta, who has sold me bread every morning for eleven years, called me by her dead husband's name, and when I corrected her gently, she looked at me with the fishmonger's widow's eyes, the ferryman's daughter's eyes, the bell-ringer's eyes, and she said: "I know I know you. I know I know you. I know I—" and could not finish, because the sentence had gone out of her like the tide going out, and I stood there holding bread I could no longer pay for because she could no longer remember the price, or the coin, or possibly her own name.*

*This is what the severance is. Not a wall falling. A tide going out and taking the names with it.*

*I am an old man. I have one poem I have not yet lost, and I mean to keep it. And tomorrow I will go down into the city, and I will write down the names of everyone I meet, because someone has to, because if the world is to be unwritten then let it at least be unwritten over a complete record, and let the record show that we were here, and that we knew, and that we did not look away.*

He set down the pen. His hand was steady now. It was, he found, the writing that steadied it — the same discovery, he suspected, that Ines had made with her ledgers, the same discovery every keeper of records had made since the first fool scratched a tally on a bone: *if I write it, it holds. If it holds, I hold.*

The child stood in the doorway. They should have been asleep. They were holding the coat around their shoulders like a cloak, and their eyes in the lamplight had the banked-coal shine, and they were looking at the ninth map, at the point where all the lines met.

Then the child did something Sef would remember — *would he remember it? would any of them?* — for the rest of his life, however long that proved to be. They crossed the room, took his hand, the old scarred one with the brass-bitten knuckle, and pressed it flat against the ninth map, over the Moonwell, over the convergence of every line of every forgetting in the city.

And through his palm, faint as tide through sand, Sef felt it: a pulse. Slow. Patient. Counting.

*Turn ten,* said the pulse. *Turn ten. Turn ten.*

He looked down at the child, and the child looked up at him, and in the child's silence he heard — clearly now, unmistakably, in the language older than kingdoms that he had refused to hear at his own door thirty-one years ago — the beginning of a word.

Not a name. Not a warning.

A *farewell*, rehearsed.

And down in the grey city below, a dog began to howl — the twelfth dog, a new one — and Sef reached for his pen, and began, with hands that did not shake, to write down everything he could still remember.
