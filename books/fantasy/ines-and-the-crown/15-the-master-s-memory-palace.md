## 15. The Master's Memory Palace

The estate of Guildmaster Corvane sat above the city like a held breath.

Ines had passed it a hundred times in her years of service — the pale stone terraces, the cypress rows planted in perfect arithmetic, the windows that never showed lamplight no matter how late the guild's business ran. She had always assumed the stillness was wealth. Wealth could buy silence, buy privacy, buy the right to be unobserved. Now, climbing the servants' stair cut into the cliff with the child's small hand locked in hers and two covenant fragments warm as coals against her ribs, she understood that the stillness was something else entirely.

It was digestion.

* * *

They had come up through the bone orchard at moonset, when the retrievers' patrol turned like clockwork toward the eastern wall. The ferryman's betrayal had cost them Sef; it had also taught Ines the shape of Corvane's mind. He did not post guards where he expected thieves. He posted them where he expected *fear* — the gates, the walls, the lit approaches. No one, in his long experience of consuming people, had ever come at him from below, from the cliff-face, from the dark wet stone where the sea threw itself against the foundations like a debtor at a countinghouse door.

The child went first along the ledge, sure-footed in a way that made Ines's chest ache. The Otherworld was in those feet. When the child paused and pressed one palm flat to the rock, Ines felt the faintest tremor run up through her boots — the mountain listening, or being asked to listen.

*Which way,* the child signed, hands quick in the starlight. They had built a language between them over the turning weeks: signs borrowed from ledger-notation, gestures invented on rooftops, meanings agreed upon with a look. It was, Ines thought grimly, the only honest accounting she had ever done.

*Up. Through the wine cellar. His vault is beneath the house.*

*How do you know?*

Because I have read every entry he ever filed, she almost said. Because grief files itself in a particular hand. But she only touched the fragment through her coat and said aloud, barely breathing it: "Because men who eat memories keep them close."

* * *

The cellar smelled of dust and vinegar and, underneath both, something sweeter — the smell of a room where time had been poured out of bottles and never quite mopped up. Ines counted doors by touch. Third arch past the press-house, behind the rack of amphorae stamped with the guild's seal, a door of black vellum stretched over iron — paper made, she knew, from rendered memory-skin, the guild's oldest and most shameful craft. It would open for a guildmaster's key.

It would also open, she had gambled, for a senior auditor's blood, because Corvane had never imagined an auditor would turn.

She opened her palm on her knife and let three drops fall. The vellum shivered, tasted her, and — with what she swore was reluctance — unsealed.

Beyond lay warmth. That was the first obscenity of it: after the cold cliff and the cold sea-wind, the vault was *warm*, lit by lamps that burned without oil, glowing the soft gold of a library at evening. And it was green.

Rows and rows of it. Not shelves — *beds*. Raised beds of dark soil stretching away into the lamplight, and growing out of the soil, plants that were not plants: stalks of silvered glass, each one rooted in the earth and rising to a bud of light, and inside each bud, moving, turning, living —

Memories. Growing memories like crops.

Ines stood in the doorway and felt her ledger-craft rise in her like a reflex, the old safe instinct: *count, categorize, do not feel.* There were thousands. Tens of thousands. Each glass stalk labeled with a copper tag in Corvane's precise hand — name, date of consumption, quality grade, remaining potency. A garden of harvested lives, tended, weeded, *pruned*. Some buds glowed bright and full. Others had gone grey and hollow, drunk down to nothing, their tags marked CONSUMED in ink so red it might have been fresh.

The child made a sound Ines had never heard from them before — low, wounded, animal. Small hands rose to cover their mouth.

"I know," Ines whispered. "I know. Look at me. Don't look at them yet."

But of course the child looked. Of course they did. Somewhere in these rows grew the memory of the Covenant's first cracking; somewhere grew whatever Corvane had torn from the world's oldest keepers. The child moved down the nearest bed as if walking among graves, touching each glass stem with one finger, and Ines followed, reading tags, and the auditor in her began — helplessly, horribly — to count.

A fisherman's last morning on his boat. A mother's voice singing in a dialect extinct before the guild's founding. A soldier's reason for laying down his sword. First snows. Last words. Forgiveness, offered and refused. Every entry cross-referenced. Every life reduced to yield.

And then she saw the tag that stopped her heart mid-beat.

ISHIKAWA, M.
ISHIKAWA, R.
Consumed: [date]. Grade: intact. Note: sold by apprentice, C., against future consideration.

Her parents.

She had known they were dead. She had a ledger-entry for it, filed in her own careful hand twenty years ago, terse as all her entries: *Parents deceased. Estate settled. Proceeds to guild custody.* She had never asked why two deaths should generate proceeds to the guild. She had never asked why she could remember their funeral but not their faces — why she could recite the settlement figures but not her mother's laugh. She had told herself it was grief. Grief erodes. Grief is honest erosion.

It was not erosion. It was *harvest.*

Her legs carried her forward without her permission. Two glass stalks stood apart from the others, in a bed of their own near the vault's far wall, as if even Corvane's filing system had flinched from mixing them with common stock. The buds within them glowed warmer than any others in the room — gold shot through with colors she had no names for, and inside the first one, faint as breath on glass, a woman's hands kneading dough, flour to the wrists, humming.

Ines put her fingers against the glass and the hum came through, thin and true, a tune she had been humming all her life without knowing where she'd learned it.

The sound she made then was not auditor's sound. It came from somewhere below craft, below training, below the twenty years of controlled loneliness she had built like a wall around the gap in herself. She heard it and did not recognize her own voice.

"Mother," she said, to a bottle.

* * *

The child's hand on her sleeve brought her back. The child pointed — not at the parental stalks, but at the bed beside them. One more stalk there, smaller, its bud dimmed with age, its tag weathered:

ISHIKAWA, I. Age 6–13. Sold by apprentice, C. Payment deferred against promotion. Grade: exceptional.

Seven years. Her missing seven years, the gap she had refused to examine, the blank she had papered over with ink and routine — here it was, growing in a garden like a sickly bulb, and the note in the margin was Corvane's own young handwriting, and suddenly the whole architecture of her life laid itself bare with terrible bookkeeping clarity.

He had been an apprentice once. Hungry, brilliant, poor in the way clever men are poor — rich in everything except standing. And a senior auditor named Ishikawa had a daughter with an unusually vivid memory, and the guild paid for vividness, and an apprentice named Corvane had seen the arithmetic: one child's childhood, traded against a future. *Payment deferred against promotion.* He had sold her to himself. He had climbed her missing years like a ladder, and then risen high enough to file her parents' deaths as a matter of tidiness, and then risen higher still until he kept the whole garden, and she had served him for two decades, balancing his books, sharpening his ledgers, trusting ink over people because people — people had taken everything and left her a smooth place where a self should be.

And she had never once looked closely at the entries.

The fury did not burn. That was the strangest thing. She had expected fire; she had read enough consumed rage in the guild's inventory to know its flavor. What came instead was colder and far more dangerous: *clarity*. Every number in her life resolved at once, columns aligning, the sum finally legible. Vengeance and duty, which had seemed like separate roads, revealed themselves as a single road viewed from opposite ends. To reforge the Covenant she would have to stop him. To stop him she would have to take back this garden. The same blade, drawn in the same direction.

"Ines." Her own name, spoken aloud, in the child's cracked half-voice — the word they had learned from her, used now like a rope thrown to a drowning woman. "Ines. We take them. All of them?"

She looked out over the garden of stolen lives. Thousands of buds. She thought of the bridge-keeper who had forgotten her own name, of Sef in Corvane's cells reciting a poem to stay alive, of the librarian drowned in unwritten books, of the city forgetting its loved ones mid-sentence as the moon waned. She thought of the toll-mirror waiting at the Moonwell, hungry for a whole life.

"No," she said, and her voice was steady again, auditor-steady, and steadier than that. "We don't take them. We *carry* them. There's a difference, and it's the whole point of everything."

She drew her knife and began, methodically, to cut.

Not the stalks — the *tags*. Copper after copper, she stripped them from the glass and pocketed them, names rescued from filing, while the child worked beside her cradling the dim Ishikawa stalk in both arms like an infant. The fragments at Ines's ribs pulsed in answer, and somewhere above them, through floors of pale stone, she heard — or imagined she heard — a door closing, and footsteps beginning a slow descent.

Corvane's house had known she was coming. Perhaps it had always known.

Ines closed her hand around the last tag — her parents' — and turned to face the stairs, the child pressed to her side, seven stolen years glowing faintly between them.

"Come," she said. "We finish this in the light."

Above them, unhurried, the footsteps kept descending.
