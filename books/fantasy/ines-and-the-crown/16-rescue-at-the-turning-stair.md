## 16. Rescue at the Turning Stair

They kept Sef in a room with no windows, which he supposed was meant as an insult and was in fact a mercy. A man who had spent seventy years reading stars did not need to see them to know where they were. He could feel them through the stone — faint as breath on the back of his neck — and so he knew that Corvane's estate sat facing east, that it was past midnight, and that the moon had turned for the twelfth time since the child first spoke its old tongue at the guild door.

Twelve turns. One left.

He sat on the floor of his cell with his back against the wall and his hands folded in his lap like a man at prayer, though what he practiced was not prayer. It was recitation.

*The moon is a door that forgets it is a door.*

He said it under his breath, barely voicing it, the way one keeps a coal alive by cupping it. The words were all he had left of the poem — *his* poem, written sixty years ago in ink made of ash and rainwater, sold to the Vellum Guild when his daughter was sick and the physicians wanted coin he did not have. He had sold the poem and kept, he'd thought, nothing. But memory is a strange ledger. What you sell, they take; what you love, they cannot fully reach.

*The moon is a door that forgets it is a door,* he whispered again, *and every month it opens—*

And there the line broke off, as it always broke off, into a silence shaped exactly like the missing verse. He knew the shape of it the way a tongue knows a tooth pulled long ago. He pressed against the absence and felt nothing, and felt the nothing keenly, and began again.

*The moon is a door—*

The lock turned.

Sef did not startle. His heart was too old for startling, and besides, he had been expecting her for three nights now. He had heard her in the walls — or told himself he had — a footfall too light for the retrievers, whose boots struck the floors like hammers nailing down a coffin. He opened his eyes.

Ines Ishikawa stood in the doorway with a stolen ring of keys in one hand and a hooded lantern in the other, and behind her shoulder, half-hidden in the corridor's dark, stood the child.

"You look terrible," Sef said.

"You should see the other people I've robbed." She came in fast, low, closing the door behind her with a click soft as a moth landing. "Can you walk?"

"I've been walking to imaginary windows for three days. My legs are eager for something real."

She crouched beside him, and in the lantern's hooded glow he saw what the corridors had done to her: soot on her cheekbone, a tear in her sleeve mended with ledger-wire of all things, and eyes that had gone from careful to *hardened*, like wax that had been held near a flame just long enough to change its nature forever. He had known her for years — the guild's quietest clerk, the woman who trusted ink more than blood — and he had watched her become someone else across twelve turns of a dying moon, the way a coastline watches a storm come in.

"They're feeding on him," she said quietly, nodding toward the door. "Corvane has you scheduled for consumption tomorrow night. Turn thirteen. He wants to be full before the severance."

"Then we are punctual," Sef said, and got up.

His knees cracked like green wood in a fire. The child slipped past Ines and stood before him, looking up with those eyes that were too old for the face they lived in — eyes the color of the sky just before dawn decides anything. The child did not speak. The child rarely spoke in any language the waking world could hold. But it reached out and touched Sef's wrist, two fingers, light as a moth, and warmth went up his arm like the first line of spring.

And in that touch, unasked, the child gave him something: an image. A cliff. An observatory with its dome open to a sky full of patient lights. Home, offered back to him like a promise.

Sef's throat tightened. "Yes," he said, his voice rougher than he intended. "That's where we're going."

Ines looked between them. "It showed you?"

"It shows everyone what they've lost. That's how it loves." He straightened his spine. "Lead on, clerk. I've been reciting poetry to a locked door for three days. I'm ready to insult a richer audience."

* * *

The corridors of Corvane's estate were wrong in a way Sef felt in his teeth.

He had expected wealth — the man had devoured hundreds of lives to buy his ascent, and wealth was the least of what that bought. But this was wealth arranged like a stomach. The hallways curved where hallways should run straight. The lamps burned a color just shy of gold, a color that made him think of broth, of something simmering. And everywhere, everywhere, the walls were lined with doors — small doors, cabinet-doors, each labeled in the guild's precise clerical hand.

He read the labels as they passed, because he could not help himself; a poet reads everything.

*Marisol Vane, 34 years: the birth of her second son.*
*Tomas Ellery, 61 years: forty summers of his orchard.*
*A name worn away, 12 years: learning to swim.*

"He keeps them like a garden," Ines murmured, not slowing. "I saw the vault. Thousands. He walks among them at night. I don't think he even consumes most of them anymore. I think he just likes knowing they're his."

"And you worked for this man."

"I balanced his books." Her voice did not waver, but Sef heard the thing beneath it, the way one hears water moving under ice. "I told myself the ledgers were just ledgers. Numbers about vials. I never asked what was in the vials." A pause, three steps long. "I'm asking now."

"Good," Sef said. "Anger suits you better than neutrality ever did. Neutrality is just fear wearing a very tidy coat."

Ahead, the corridor ended at a stair — a spiral stair of black iron winding upward into darkness, and Sef stopped dead, because he knew this stair. Not from maps. From the poem.

*The turning stair, the turning year,* he breathed, without meaning to, the fragment rising out of him unbidden, *each step a month, each month a sphere—*

Ines turned sharply. "You know it?"

"I wrote it." He stared up the spiral, and the lantern-light moved on his face like water. "Sixty years ago. Before I sold it. This house is built on the shape of my poem, Ines. Or my poem was always the shape of this house. Corvane didn't buy my verses to sell them." His voice dropped. "He bought them because they were a map. The Covenant was written in verse once — the oldest versions, the ones the Otherworld still speaks. He's been collecting poems the way other men collect weapons."

"Then he'll want the rest of yours."

"He can't have it." And here Sef smiled, and it was not a kind smile; it was the smile of a man who had found, at the bottom of his own poverty, one coin the thieves had missed. "Because he took the poem out of my head, but he couldn't take it out of my *stubbornness*. Every day in that cell I recited what remained. And do you know what happened? The missing lines started to grow back. Not as memory — as *defiance*. Memory is possession; they can rob a possession. But a thing you refuse to surrender—" he tapped his own sunken chest, "—that grows roots in a place their tongs can't reach."

The child made a sound then — small, bright, almost a laugh — and pressed both hands flat against the iron rail of the stair.

Above them, far up the spiral, a bell tolled. Once. Twice. Then a third time, urgent, and boots began to hammer somewhere overhead.

"They've found the empty cell," Ines said. "Stair. Now. Climb, old man, climb like the moon owes you money."

* * *

They climbed.

The turning stair wound up through the heart of the house, and with every revolution Sef understood more of why Corvane had built it — or rather, why the house had built itself around the poem's bones, for architecture that greedy stops being design and becomes appetite. Each landing bore a label like the cabinet-doors below, but these were larger, grander:

*The Year of the First Frost.*
*The Drowning of the Harbor Choir.*
*The Last Conversation Between the King and the Sea.*

Whole events. Whole *ages*. Corvane had not merely eaten lives; he had eaten moments out of history itself, and somewhere above them, Sef was certain, waited the shelf where the Covenant's own verses were kept — the verses the guildmasters had been devouring turn by turn, seasoning themselves for a world after magic, where hoarded wonder would be the only wonder left.

On the ninth landing, the retrievers found them.

There were four, and they came down the stair in silence, which was the worst thing about them. Ordinary men shout when they hunt. These had been emptied of everything except purpose, and purpose does not need a voice. Their faces were smooth as overwritten pages. Their eyes had the flat shine of vials.

"Ines," Sef said calmly, "the lantern."

She threw it to him without asking why — trust, he thought, actual trust, earned in twelve turns of fire — and he caught it and hurled it down the stairwell into the dark below, where it burst against the iron and flared up in a bloom of burning oil.

And in the flare, Sef planted his feet on the landing, gripped the rail, and *recited*.

Not the broken poem. Not tonight. Tonight he gave them the whole of what stubbornness had regrown — sixty years of withheld verse pouring out of him at full voice, filling the stairwell the way the tide fills a cave:

*"The moon is a door that forgets it is a door,*
*and every month it opens on a country made of remembering!*
*O merchants of the marrow, O clerks of the consumed—*
*you cannot file the sky, you cannot shelve the sea,*
*and every word I sold you rots inside your vaults*
*because a poem is not a possession, it is a PASSAGE—"*

The retrievers faltered on the stairs. Not because the words hurt them — because the words *woke* them. Sef saw it happen: the swallowed verses of other poets, other keepers, stirring in whatever hollow organ served these creatures for a heart, recognizing kin. One of them put a hand to its breastbone as if something trapped there had turned over in its sleep. Another's smooth face creased — painfully, wonderfully — into something almost like weeping.

"Go," Sef said to Ines, not stopping his recitation, his voice cracking and climbing. "The fragment. Third landing above. Go while they remember how to hesitate!"

She hesitated — one heartbeat, looking at this old man standing in firelight shouting immortality at armed men — and then she went, taking the child with her, and Sef alone on the landing poured the poem out like a man bailing a sinking world.

* * *

They met again at the top, in a round chamber beneath a dome of smoked glass, where the vault's greatest prize hung suspended in a column of pale light.

It was small. That was the first thing. After the drowned archive's vast unwritten librarian, after the bridge-keeper's door, after all the terrible scale of the Covenant's ruin — the third fragment was no bigger than a seed. It turned slowly in its column of light, and it was not glass, not crystal, not anything Sef had a name for. It was the color of the moment just before speech. When it turned, the air around it remembered things out loud: a snatch of lullaby in the old tongue, the smell of rain on the Otherworld's silver grass, a voice saying *I promise* in a language older than promising.

The child walked straight to it, arms out, and the fragment drifted down into those small cupped hands like a bird coming home to a branch it had grown on.

"There were wards," Ines said, slightly dazed. "There were wards worth a kingdom. They just... opened."

"They weren't wards," Sef said softly. "They were *custody*. And custody ends when the rightful heir arrives." He looked at the child holding its own lost piece, glowing faintly now along the edges of its small body, brighter than it had been since the guild door. Two fragments carried. Three gathered. "Where is the fourth?"

"The Moonwell," Ines said. "Its antechamber. There's a guardian there that takes a whole life as toll." She paused, and Sef watched her decide whether to say the next part, and watched her say it anyway, because she was not the woman she had been. "I intend to pay it."

"With what? You've given already — the bridge-keeper—"

"My guild years. All of them. Every entry I ever kept." She smiled, and it was a strange smile, thin and clean as a blade being sharpened. "Do you know what that means, Sef? Twenty years of bookkeeping. Bottled griefs, borrowed years, appraisal orders, Corvane's accounts. If the mirror takes it all, I will finally be free of the only life I never chose."

"And left with?"

"The one I'm choosing now."

Below them, faintly, the retrievers were still standing on the ninth landing. Not pursuing. One of them was humming — badly, brokenly — a few bars of a song some dead poet had sold, and the others were listening, and none of them could say why they could not climb another step.

Sef leaned on the rail and let himself laugh, low and rusty and real, the first laugh since his capture. "Look at them. Corvane builds his empire out of eaten memories, and it takes one stubborn old man with a poem to remind the food it used to be alive." He pushed off from the rail. "Come. The observatory first — I want my charts, my star-glass, and my kettle. If we're going to assault a well guarded by a mirror that eats lives, I intend to arrive caffeinated and correctly oriented."

But as they turned to go, the dome of smoked glass above them lit — not with starlight.

With moonlight. Wrong moonlight, thirteenth-turn moonlight, so heavy and close that it fell through the smoke-colored panes like something poured. And in that light, Sef saw the charts of his own mind rearrange themselves into a single terrible clarity: the moon overhead was no longer counting.

It was leaning in to listen.

And somewhere below, in the garden of stolen lives, a door opened that was not on any of Ines's stolen keys — and slow footsteps began to climb the turning stair, unhurried, familiar with every step, the footsteps of a man ascending his own collection to meet the thieves.

Corvane was coming home early.

And he was bringing the twelfth turn with him.
