## 17. Twelfth Turn: The Waning City

The twelfth turn began not with a sound but with a subtraction.

Ines noticed it first in the ledger ink. She had been copying the route to the Moonwell onto a fresh page — Sef's star-map transcribed into her own careful hand — when she looked down and found the ink had gone grey. Not faded. Greyed, as though the color had been lifted out of it like a stain drawn from cloth. She dipped the pen again. The ink came up from the well the color of ash and dried that way on the page.

She carried the page to the window.

Outside, Ashfall was forgetting itself.

The morning should have been loud with market-day: awnings of madder-red and saffron, the green gleam of copper roofs after rain, the painted signs over shopfronts that had hung there since her grandmother's grandmother. But the reds were going the color of old brick dust. The greens had drained toward pewter. And the signs — she watched, hand pressed flat against the cold glass, as the letters on the chandler's sign across the street softened at their edges, blurred, and slid away entirely, leaving bare wood where *WICK & WAX* had been for ninety years.

The chandler himself stood beneath it, squinting upward, mouthing something. Trying to remember what his shop was called. Ines could see the shape of the question on his face even through the glass.

*One turn left,* said the moon, low and swollen above the eastern rooftops, so pale now it looked like a hole cut in the sky rather than a thing hanging in it.

She turned back to the room. Sef sat by the fire with his charts spread across his knees, an old man made older by three days without sleep, his hands trembling as he marked positions no one would ever need again if they failed. And by the hearth, wrapped in Ines's own coat because the child could no longer hold warmth, sat the one who had crossed the world to save it.

The child was fading with the city.

It had been happening slowly enough, at first, that Ines had told herself it was only exhaustion. But this morning the child's skin had taken on the same ash-grey as the ink, the same drained quality as the sky, and when they turned to look at her their eyes caught the firelight wrong — light went *into* them and did not come back out. The fragment inside them was straining toward the severance, Sef said. Being pulled. The Covenant remembered what the child carried, and the widening dark between the worlds was reaching across the thinning distance like a tide going out, dragging everything loose toward it.

"Tell me what you feel," Ines said, kneeling beside the hearth. She said it in the guild-tongue first, then in the old language, the one that lived half-understood in some locked room of her. Her voice in that tongue sounded strange to her own ears — softer, rounder, as if someone else were speaking through her mouth.

The child considered. They had learned to ration words the way Ines rationed ink.

*"Like being pulled apart,"* they signed, small hands moving in the firelight. *"Like the door is open and I am standing in it."*

Then, after a moment, the smaller truth: *"And I am tired, mother."*

The word still landed in her chest like a stone dropped into deep water. She did not know why the child called her that. She had searched every memory she owned and found nothing — no birth, no giving-away, no face smaller than her palm at midnight. Whatever had been there had been spent or stolen long ago, and now there was only the word itself, arriving each day like a bill for a debt she could not find record of.

But she had stopped flinching from it. That was the thing she would not say aloud, even to herself: somewhere between the bone orchard and the drowned archive, between burning pages and paying poems, the word had stopped being a riddle and become simply true — true the way a load-bearing wall is true, unseen and holding everything up.

She loved this child. She had run out of ways to pretend otherwise.

"I'm here," she said, in both languages, and took their cold hands in hers.

* * *

They went out at dusk, because Sef needed to take the last measurements from the high tower of the old customhouse, and because Ines wanted — needed — to see the city with her own eyes while there was still a city to see.

Ashfall at twilight had always been beautiful in a grimy, stubborn way: lamplighters moving down the streets like slow fireworks, the smell of frying onions and horse and river-mud, ten thousand windows warming gold one by one. Now the lamps lit grey. Not dim — grey, their flames burning the color of fog, throwing light that illuminated without warming. The onion-smell was gone; taste was the first sense the severance ate. People walked the streets with their collars up, and when they spoke to one another Ines heard the conversations fraying mid-sentence, names dissolving out of mouths.

"—and tell Tomas that—" A woman in a fishmonger's apron, gesturing at her son. Then a pause. "Tell— " Her brow creased. "There was someone I was supposed to tell."

"Mama," the boy said, and she looked at him with a flicker of fear, the fear of a person who knows a name has just slipped through their fingers and cannot say whose.

Ines kept walking. There was nothing she could give these people. Nothing but the thing she was already carrying, already spending, already bleeding for — four fragments wrapped in oilcloth against her ribs, warm as live coals, and a fifth walking beside her holding her hand.

At the corner of Ledger Street — the sign had lost its letters too, but she knew it the way you know your own heartbeat — a crowd had gathered around the notice board where the Guild posted its tariffs and proclamations. Someone had nailed up a fresh broadsheet, and even from a distance Ines recognized the seal: the Vellum Guild's wax, the quill-and-key.

She should have kept walking. Instead she pushed close enough to read it, and her blood went cold and quiet.

*BY ORDER OF THE GUILDMASTER,* it began, in Corvane's immaculate hand. *Let it be known that the waning is a sickness of memory, and that the Guild possesses the cure. Bring to our halls your griefs, your grudges, your memories too heavy to carry, and we will lift them from you — freely given, fairly weighed. In the turning dark, be unburdened.*

Below, in smaller type: *The Guild remembers, so that you need not.*

"They're feeding," Sef said quietly at her shoulder. He had come up beside her unnoticed, leaning on his stick, his star-charts rolled under his arm. "Look at the queue."

There was a queue. It stretched down the street and around the corner: ordinary people, frightened people, people who had watched a spouse forget their wedding day or a parent forget their face, lining up in the grey lamplight to sell the Guild the last of what made them themselves. A clerk moved along the line with a ledger open, weighing sorrows on a little brass scale, and every so often he would nod, and someone would walk away lighter — visibly lighter, shoulders easing, eyes going smooth and vacant as river stones — and the clerk would carry a small stoppered vial back toward the guildhall like a man delivering wine.

"He's not curing them," Ines said. Her voice came out flat and terrible. "He's harvesting them. Every memory they surrender makes him stronger for whatever comes after the thirteenth turn. He means to stand in the ruins with a vault full of other people's lives and call himself king of the silence."

"Yes." Sef did not look at her. His eyes were on the moon, which had cleared the rooftops now, huge and white and wrong, its face like the face of something drowning. "And worse than that, Ines. Look at the moon."

She looked. And she saw what three days of charts had shown him: the moon was not merely full. It was *strained*, its edges trembling like the surface of a struck bell, and around it the stars had begun to go out — not occluded, not clouded, but *unwritten*, the way the letters had slid off the chandler's sign.

"The twelfth turn completes tonight," Sef said. "When it does, the pull on the child will double. Perhaps treble. The fragment inside them will begin to leave whether we carry them to the Moonwell or not." He turned to her at last, and in the grey lamplight his face was very old and very kind. "We go tomorrow. At dawn. There is no tomorrow after that one."

* * *

That night, in the safehouse above the disused bindery, the child woke screaming.

Not crying — screaming, and the scream came out in both languages at once, the guild-tongue and the old tongue braided together, and the sound made the grey lamp flames bend sideways as if a door had opened somewhere. Ines was across the room before she was fully awake, gathering the small shaking body against her, and the child clung to her coat with both fists and spoke in a rush of the old language so fast and broken that she caught only fragments:

*"...the door... it opens from the other side... they are all waiting at the door... my mother—"*

Then the child stopped. Went rigid. Pulled back far enough to look at Ines's face, and Ines watched something move behind those light-swallowing eyes — recognition, or its ghost.

*"You are not her,"* the child signed, hands trembling. *"But you are hers. I don't— I can't explain it. When the door pulls me, I hold on to you instead. You are the rope."*

Ines's throat closed. She thought of the vial in the deep archive with her own name on it. She thought of Corvane's offer, still ringing in her skull like a struck coin: *your childhood, whole, returned — for the child.* She thought of how easy it would be, how merciful almost, to set this burden down and pick up her old life like a coat.

"I'm here," she said. "I'm the rope. Hold on."

The child slept again near dawn, exhausted, grey-faced, their breathing shallow as paper. Ines sat beside them and did not sleep. She took out the oilcloth bundle and unwrapped the four fragments one by one in the grey light, because she had learned that looking at them steadied her, the way checking a ledger steadies a clerk in a storm.

They were beautiful. That was the horror of them. Each fragment was a sliver of what looked like moonlight made solid — cool to the touch, faintly singing, and inside each one, if you held it to your eye, a scene moved like a figure in a glass paperweight. In the first, a bridge over a river of stars. In the second, shelves of unwritten books rising through green water. In the third, a stair of light turning forever upward. In the fourth, a mirror, and in the mirror a woman with ink-stained fingers who was and was not herself.

Four pieces of a promise older than kingdoms. One piece missing.

She looked from the fragments to the sleeping child, and the arithmetic assembled itself in her mind with the merciless clarity of a balanced column, and she hated it, and she could not unbalance it.

Five fragments. Four found. The fifth was the one the child carried — the living piece, sent across the worlds in a small body before the first crack widened, exiled to save both realms. To reforge the Covenant at the Moonwell, the fifth fragment must join the others.

And the child was the fifth fragment.

Sef had never said it outright. Neither had she. They had spoken of *carrying* the child to the well, of the child *being present* at the reforging, a conspiracy of euphemism between two people who loved the same small person and could not bear the sentence. But the moon had one turn left, and euphemisms do not balance ledgers.

*Either the child gives the fragment,* Ines thought, watching the grey dawn crawl up the window, *or the fragment takes the child.*

Unless—

Unless there was a sixth thing. Unless the well accepted substitution, exchange, payment in kind — she was a ledger-keeper, she knew that every covenant was at bottom a contract, and every contract had clauses, and every clause had loopholes for those who read closely enough. She had spent her whole life reading closely. It was the only prayer she knew.

She rose, wrapped the fragments, and woke Sef with a hand on his shoulder.

"Dawn," she said. "You said dawn. But I want to go now. Tonight's end. Before the pull doubles. And Sef—" her voice cracked and she let it, just once, "—when we reach the well, I need to know everything. Every word of the rite. Every clause. No kindnesses. No omissions. If there is a way to pay that doesn't cost *them*, I will find it, and if there isn't, I will spend whatever I am to make one."

Sef looked at her for a long moment. Outside, the last grey stars were going out over a city that could no longer remember its own name.

"There may be a clause," he said slowly. "An old one. The well demands a life's memory as toll — we've paid twice already. But the reforging itself..." He hesitated, and she saw him deciding whether mercy or honesty was the greater cruelty. "The reforging wants a *keepsake*. A memory freely given by the one who binds the fragments — the memory dearest to the binder, the one that holds the work together. That is the fifth anchor, Ines. Not the child. The child carries a fragment, yes. But the binding needs a heart's own memory to knot the cord."

Ines stood very still.

She inventoried herself the way she inventoried vaults: quickly, precisely, without flinching. What remained to her? A childhood with holes in it. Guild years already spent at the mirror-toll. A handful of moments — the smell of the archive dust, Sef's laugh, the weight of a small sleeping body against her chest, a voice saying *mother* in two languages.

The dearest one was obvious. The dearest one was sitting asleep by a dead fire, breathing like paper.

"Then we have what we need," she said, and picked up the bundle, and opened the door onto the waning city, where the last lamplighter was going door to door lighting flames the color of ash, keeping his small faithful vigil over a world running out of turns.
