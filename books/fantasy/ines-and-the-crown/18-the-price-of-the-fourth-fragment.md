## 18. The Price of the Fourth Fragment

The Moonwell did not announce itself. That was the first thing Ines understood about it, standing at the tree line where the pilgrim road gave out and the mountain began: the holiest place in either realm had no gate, no spire, no wardens in silver. Only a path of white stone going up through pines that smelled like snow and resin, and a silence so complete she could hear the child's heartbeat against her collarbone, quick as a moth.

She carried him now. He had stopped pretending he could walk an hour ago — his small body burning with fragment-light whenever the moon shifted behind cloud, his breath coming in shallow pulls. The fourth fragment called to the one inside him; she could feel it too, a tugging beneath her sternum, like a hook set in her ribs and reeled slowly home.

"Almost," she told him, though he could not answer. His eyes found hers anyway. They had learned each other's silences on the road — three days from Sef's observatory, two nights hiding from retriever-lanterns in the high passes, one long gray morning when she had held him while he shook with the strain of holding himself together. She knew what his stillness meant. It meant *I am listening.*

The path climbed. The pines thinned. And then the trees simply ended, and there was the antechamber.

It was not a room built by hands. It was a hollow in the mountain's crown, walled in pale stone that seemed grown rather than cut, and open to the sky above — but the sky here was wrong, or rather more right than any sky she had known. The stars were close enough to have edges. The moon hung directly overhead, eleven turns gone, fat and failing, and its light fell into the center of the chamber where the well itself waited: a circle of dark water perhaps ten paces across, utterly still, reflecting not the moon above it but a different moon entirely — whole, unbroken, silver past silver.

*The Covenant's memory of itself,* Sef had said. *That's all the well is. The last place both worlds are remembered at once.*

And between Ines and the water stood the mirror.

She had expected a guardian. A beast, a sentinel, something with teeth. What stood before the well was only a tall pane of glass in a frame of black wood, freestanding, patient, catching the starlight and giving back nothing. No reflection. When she approached, dragging her shadow behind her, the glass remained gray and empty as unwritten vellum.

She stopped an arm's length away. The child stirred against her shoulder.

"I know what you are," she said to the mirror. Her voice came out flat and professional, the guild voice, the ledger voice. "You're a toll-keeper."

The glass warmed. Not brightened — warmed, the way paper warms near a flame, and words surfaced in it like ink rising through blotting sand:

**WHAT BRINGS YOU TO THE WELL?**

"Reforging," Ines said. "The Covenant is broken. I carry fragments. Three." She touched the satchel at her hip where they lay wrapped in Sef's star-cloth, humming faintly against each other like struck bells not yet stilled. "There's a fourth here. I've come for it."

**THE FOURTH FRAGMENT RESTS IN THE WATER.**

"Then I'll take it out."

**THE WATER GIVES NOTHING. THE WATER ONLY RETURNS WHAT IS PAID FOR.**

Ines closed her eyes briefly. Of course. Of course it worked this way. Everything in her life had worked this way — every vault, every ledger, every transaction in the Vellum Guild's gray books. Nothing given. Everything priced. She had spent nine years believing she had escaped that arithmetic when she'd fled Corvane's clerks over the rooftops, and here it was again, waiting at the top of the world, wearing holiness instead of livery.

"What's the price?"

The mirror did not answer at once. The empty glass rippled, very slightly, like the surface of a pond considering a stone. Then, instead of words, it showed her.

Herself. Younger. Nineteen, maybe twenty, sleeves rolled to the elbow, bent over a desk in the guild's counting room with a quill moving in her hand — the first ledger she ever kept, the apprenticeship year, the year she'd arrived with nothing but a head for numbers and a heart locked down tight. The image turned like a page. Herself at twenty-five, sealing a jar of bottled grief with wax and wire. Herself at thirty, auditing the deep archive, lamp held high, cataloguing other people's stolen years with perfect detachment and never once asking why her own felt so thin.

Page after page after page. Nine years of them. Every entry she had ever made, every sum she had ever balanced, every impossible thing she had recorded in her small precise hand — the mirror was showing her the whole of it, and Ines understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, exactly what was being weighed.

"No," she said quietly.

The images kept turning.

"I said no."

**IT IS NOT A QUESTION YET,** the mirror wrote. **IT IS AN APPRAISAL.**

The word went through her like a blade finding the gap in armor. *Appraisal.* Corvane's word. The order he'd given for the child, written in his own hand: *Hold for appraisal.* She had spent months learning what that word meant when men like Corvane used it. It meant: *we look at what you are, and we decide what you're worth to us, and then we take it.*

"You want my guild years," she said. "All of them."

**EVERY ENTRY YOU EVER KEPT.**

"That's—" She stopped. Did the arithmetic she could not stop herself from doing, even now, even here. Nine years. Nearly a third of her remembered life. The apprenticeship. The mastery exams. The night she'd first decoded the covenant-memories hidden in the vault ledgers and said nothing. The night she'd finally read the entries closely and run. Every skill she had. Every craft. The ledger-craft that had bargained with an unwritten librarian, that had burned pages to blind hounds, that had gotten her this far up this mountain. All of it lived in those years.

If she paid, she would climb down from this mountain knowing how to walk and speak and breathe, and nothing else of who she had become.

"And if I refuse?" she asked. "If I turn around and go back down?"

The mirror showed her the child. Not the child in her arms — a child elsewhere, older, alone, walking a road under a moonless sky in a world gone silent and colorless, his mouth opening around words no one could hear because language itself had come apart. Then it showed her the city. Then the sea, flat and dead as poured metal. Then nothing. Gray nothing, page after gray page, a ledger with no entries at all.

**THE TOLL IS SET,** the mirror wrote. **THE WELL DOES NOT BARGAIN.**

*No,* Ines thought. *But I do. It's the only thing I've ever been good at.*

She shifted the child to her other shoulder. He was lighter than he should have been — the fragment inside him spending him, burning him as fuel the way a candle burns itself. Two turns left. Maybe less. There was no version of this where she kept her years and saved him. There was only the version where she chose which self to be.

And underneath the fear — beneath it, quiet as groundwater — was something else. Something she would have been ashamed to name in front of anyone but the moon.

*Relief.*

Not all of it. Not the child, never the child, not the fragments or the fight or the fierce strange love that had grown in her like a seed split open. But some part of her — the part that had spent nine years keeping other people's memories in jars because she could not bear to keep her own in her head — some part of her had been waiting nine years to put the ledgers down.

She thought about telling the mirror that. She decided the toll-keeper deserved honesty. It was, after all, the only currency she had left that wasn't already priced.

"Before I pay," she said, "I want to say what I'm paying with. Out loud. So it's witnessed." She looked up at the broken moon. "You're the oldest witness there is. That'll do."

The mirror stilled. Listening.

"My name is Ines Ishikawa," she said. "Nine years ago I walked into the Vellum Guild because it was the only place that would take someone with no history and a gift for sums. I told myself I was neutral. I told myself ink was cleaner than people. I catalogued bottled griefs by weight and borrowed years by vintage and I never once opened my own accounts, because I was afraid of what I'd find missing." Her voice did not shake. She had spent nine years making sure it couldn't. "I kept perfect books for predators. I know that now. I was good at it, and being good at it was the worst thing about me."

The child's hand curled into her collar. Holding on.

"But the same craft that served them got us out," she went on. "Every trick I know, I learned in those gray rooms. Every bargain I've won since I ran, I won with their tools. So I'm not paying you garbage, mirror. I'm paying you the best of me. The sharpest years I have. And I want it written somewhere — in your glass, in the well, in whatever remembers things here — that Ines Ishikawa paid her debt to the Covenant in full, and paid it *willingly*, and that the woman who climbs down this mountain tomorrow will be poorer and lighter and will owe nothing to anyone."

She paused. The next part cost more than the rest together.

"She won't remember why the child matters to her," she said. "She'll just know that he does. Make sure she knows that much. Whatever else falls out of her head — leave the love in. Take the bookkeeping. Leave the love."

For a moment the antechamber was so silent she heard the stars.

Then the mirror filled with light.

Not the gray appraisal-light of before but a slow silver rising, like dawn coming up underwater, and in the glass she saw her guild years laid out at last — truly laid out, all nine of them, glowing like pages held up to a window — and she saw that they were beautiful. That was the cruelty and the mercy of it. They had been beautiful. The young woman bending over her first ledger had been so careful, so hopeful in her rigid way, building order out of a childhood someone had stolen from her, building walls out of columns of figures. Ines looked at her younger selves turning in the silver glass and grieved them in advance, the way you grieve the living.

"Wait," she whispered. One thing more. One thing she had almost forgotten she was carrying. She set the child down gently against the warm stone, propped him upright, and knelt so their faces were level. His eyes were half-lidded, fever-bright, but they focused on her.

"I don't know if you'll remember this," she said. "I don't know if *I'll* remember it, after. But listen. You were born on the other side of the sky, and you crossed over to save everything, and you have never once in all this time asked me for anything. So I'm asking for both of us." She took his small hands in hers. "When I don't know you anymore — be patient with me. Come find me anyway. however long it takes. The woman I'll be won't know why you matter. Help her learn it again. Can you do that?"

He could not speak. But he raised one hand — trembling, luminous at the fingertips where the fragment shone through his skin like a lamp behind paper — and pressed it flat against her chest, over her heart. Held it there. And in the language older than kingdoms, in the voice he had never used, she heard — or dreamed she heard, and would spend the rest of her life unsure which — one word.

*Mother.*

Ines closed her eyes. Memorized it. Filed it in the one ledger no toll could reach.

Then she stood, turned to the mirror, and said: "Take it."

The glass reached for her.

There was no pain. That was the strangest part — she had braced for pain, for the tearing Corvane's retrievers described when they fed, and instead it was like setting down a load she had forgotten she was carrying. The years lifted out of her one page at a time. She felt the apprenticeship go — the smell of new vellum, the master-clerk's dry praise, the first time her balancing matched to the copper. She felt the examinations go. The promotion. The deep archive, the lamp, the vials, the long gray decades of careful, careful nothing. Each memory rose out of her like heat off summer stone, drifted across the chamber, and slid into the mirror, and the mirror drank, and grew brighter, and passed the light down into the well.

She stayed on her feet through all of it. She had decided she would. It seemed important that someone witness the payment from inside, even if the witness was the payment.

The last thing to go was not a memory at all. It was smaller than that — a habit, a posture, the particular way she squared her shoulders before opening a ledger, the little click of a mind arranging the world into columns. She felt it loosen and lift and leave, and with it went the final weight of the person she had built herself to be, and Ines Ishikawa stood at the edge of the Moonwell with empty hands and a skull full of quiet, lighter than she had been since she was a girl.

The well spoke.

Not in words. In *return*. The black water turned silver from the center outward, and something rose through it — the fourth fragment, a shard of moonlight dense as a seed, ascending through the depths like a bubble of dawn until it broke the surface and hung in the air before her, singing a note she felt in her teeth.

She took it. Her hands knew how, though she no longer knew why. Some things lived deeper than ledgers.

Behind her, the child made a sound — a small exhausted laugh, or a sob, or both — and she turned and gathered him up without thinking, without deciding, the way her arms had learned him on the road. He buried his face in her neck. She held him and looked out over the antechamber, at the true moon overhead with one turn left in it, and felt the strangeness settle over her like a garment cut for someone else.

She knew the child. She knew the satchel, the fragments, the mountain, the mission — these lived in her body, in the deeper ledgers, in whatever the mirror had judged too essential to price. But when she searched for the woman who had begun this — the clerk, the auditor, the keeper of impossible things — she found only smooth blank vellum where nine years should have been.

*I paid,* she thought, and could not remember what for. *I paid everything, and it was worth it.* The certainty remained, vast and sourceless, like faith. *Whatever I bought with it, it was worth it.*

She turned toward the downward path. Dawn was a rumor in the east. One turn left.

The child lifted his head from her shoulder and looked at her — really looked, searching her face the way you search a house you used to live in — and whatever he found there, or didn't find, he only pressed closer and held on tighter.

Ines carried him down the mountain, light as ash, rich as a spent purse, toward the last turn of the moon — and far below, at the tree line where the white path began, lanterns were waiting. Too many lanterns. Arranged too neatly.

Corvane had found the well.
