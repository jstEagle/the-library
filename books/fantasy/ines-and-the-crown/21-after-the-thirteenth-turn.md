## 21. After the Thirteenth Turn

The dawn came up gold.

Not the thin, apologetic gold of the last few months — the washed-out light that had made even blood look like weak tea — but a deep, deliberate gold, the kind of light that seemed to have opinions about what it touched. It poured over the Moonwell's rim and down the terraces of the old city, and everywhere it fell, color returned like a tide coming in. The slate roofs went from gray to blue to a blue so particular it almost had a name. The banners on the customs house remembered they were crimson. A fruit-seller's cart at the bottom of the hill held oranges so violently orange that Ines had to stop and stare at them, her hand pressed flat against her own sternum, as though checking that something inside her was still beating.

She could not remember why she was crying.

That was the first thing she noticed, standing on the well's high terrace with the child's small hand curled in hers: she was weeping without grief, the way a roof weeps after rain — not from any wound, but because it had been holding too much for too long, and now there was somewhere for it to go. Her face was wet. Her chest ached in the specific way an ache aches when its cause has been removed but its shape remains, a keyhole with no key, a glove with no hand.

The thirteenth turn had closed four hours ago. She knew this the way she knew arithmetic — factually, without feeling. Sef had counted it aloud from the observatory steps, his voice cracking on twelve and steadying by thirteen, and then the moon had done something no moon had ever done in the presence of witnesses: it had *let go*. It had released the turn like a breath held too long, and the light that came off it had not waned but *turned*, folding back over itself, and the sky above the well had split along a seam no one else could see, and through that seam had come —

Ines searched herself for the memory of it and found the search coming back empty-handed, politely, the way a clerk returns from the stacks to report that the volume is missing, madam, perhaps it was never catalogued at all.

She had spent something. She understood that much. There was a hollow in her the way there is a hollow in a bookshelf where a favorite volume stood for years — the dust around the absence still shaped like the thing that was gone. She could feel the edges of the hollow when she breathed. Whatever had stood there had been large. Whatever had stood there had been hers.

And the child was looking up at her with eyes the color of the well at midnight, whole again, *whole*, both languages living behind their lips like two birds sharing one cage, and the child said:

"*Máthair.*"

The word went through Ines like a needle finding the one gap in all her armor.

It was not a word from the old language — or it was, but it was also from another one, a warmer one, a kitchen-language, a language of scraped knees and lamp-lit evenings, and it meant — she knew what it meant, the meaning arrived intact even though everything around it had been sold or spent or burned — it meant *mother*.

"I'm sorry," Ines said, and heard her own voice come out ragged. "I don't — I don't know that name."

The child did not cry. That was the terrible grace of it. The child simply looked at her the way you look at a door you have walked through ten thousand times, knowing the door does not remember your hand on its latch, loving it anyway. Then the child said, in the guild-tongue, carefully, as if handling something fragile on Ines's behalf:

"You don't have to. I remember enough for both of us. For now."

*For now.* The words settled over the terrace like frost.

* * *

They came down from the Moonwell as the city woke beneath them, and the waking was a kind of resurrection.

Ines had forgotten — no, that was not right; she had never known, because the forgetting had been gradual and total, a tide erasing footprints — how loud a mended world was. All through the waning turns the city had gone quiet in stages: first the colors, then the names, then the small connective tissue of ordinary speech, people trailing off mid-sentence because the ends of their thoughts had been repossessed. She had grown used to a city that whispered. Now it roared. Somewhere below, a church bell that had been silent since the ninth turn threw its voice across the rooftops, and a dog answered it, and a woman laughed — actually laughed, the sound raw and unpracticed, like someone using a muscle long atrophied — and a man ran through the square shouting a name, just a name, over and over, and Ines understood before she could stop herself that he was shouting the name of someone he had lost to the amnesias and *found again*, found whole, found remembering him.

Her chest did the aching thing again.

"Your hands are shaking," the child observed, in the guild-tongue, with the grave precision Ines had learned to trust more than most adults' fluency.

"They do that," Ines said. "I'm told it passes."

"Who told you?"

"I don't know." She looked at her own trembling fingers, ink-stained, calloused in the shapes a pen makes, and felt the familiar vertigo of a life lived entirely in evidence. Her hands knew things. They knew the weight of a ledger pen and the trick of unsealing wax without tearing it and the exact pressure required to hold a child's hand without letting go. Everything except the why of them. "Someone must have. I seem to be full of other people's advice with the attributions torn out."

The child considered this with the seriousness the old language deserved. "*You gave away the shelf,"* the child said, switching tongues, and the old words moved through the morning air like water moving through light. *"The books remember being held."*

"I don't understand you," Ines said, which was no longer entirely true, and both of them knew it, and neither of them said anything about it.

* * *

Sef was waiting at the bottom of the well-road, sitting on the low wall with his observatory coat buttoned wrong and his eyes red-rimmed, and when he saw them he stood up too fast, the way old men stand up when they have been afraid and will not admit it.

He embraced the child first. Then he held Ines at arm's length and studied her face with the particular horror of a man reading a beloved book and finding pages razored out.

"You did it," he said. "You paid the fifth fragment. I watched you. I *held your shoulders* while—"

"Don't," Ines said. "Please. If you tell me what I gave, I'll only lose it twice."

Sef's mouth worked. He was a poet; silence cost him visibly, like a miser asked to burn coin. But he swallowed whatever chronicle he had been composing and only said, hoarsely, "You're still you. Whatever's missing — the shape of you is still you. I'd know it anywhere. I've been reading your handwriting for thirty years."

"That's kind," Ines said, "and slightly alarming."

"It's the truth. Kindness can manage." He turned, gesturing up the cliff path toward the observatory, its dome catching the new gold like a second moon. "Come. Both of you. There's food, and there's — well. There's work."

"Work," Ines repeated. The word landed strangely. She had been the Vellum Guild's finest auditor; work had been the one fixed star of her existence, the axis everything else turned around. But the Guild was ash now — Corvane unmade at the wellhead, his retrievers scattered like startled crows when the Covenant sealed and the consumed memories tore free of them, his vault-garden of stolen lives flooding open into the streets of the city in a great bright exhalation. People were waking up all over the city with decades in their heads that had been taken from them, weeping in doorways, embracing strangers who turned out to be their brothers. The Guild's ledgers, Ines knew, were at this moment either burning or being read aloud in the square as evidence. She found she could not make herself care which.

"What work?" she asked.

Sef smiled, and for a moment the fear and guilt of thirty years fell away from his face and left the young astronomer-poet he must once have been, the one who had refused the last covenant-bearer and carried it ever since like a stone in his boot.

"A new chronicle," he said. "The old records are corrupted — half of them written by Corvane's people, half of them eaten. If we don't write down what happened, the next age will invent something worse. Someone has to begin." He hesitated, and his voice gentled. "I thought — given what you've lost — that you might want to be there when the first page is opened. Some people find blank books cruel. Others find them merciful."

Ines looked at the child. The child looked back, and in the well-dark eyes there was no pity, which Ines was grateful for beyond the power of language, old or new. Only patience. The patience of someone who intended to stay.

"A blank book," Ines said slowly, testing the words the way she had once tested vials for tampering, "is just a ledger with nothing to hide yet."

"That," said Sef, "is the most romantic thing anyone has ever said about accounting."

* * *

The observatory smelled of cold stone, lamp oil, and old paper — and under all of it, faintly, of starlight, if starlight could be said to smell of anything, which Ines had once catalogued in a vault inventory as *ozone and iron and the moment before rain*, entry four hundred eleven, and the memory of making that entry surfaced whole and useless and dear.

The child ate like the small wild thing they had been on the night of the knock — bread torn, honey applied with architectural ambition — and then fell asleep mid-bite, upright, in a chair, in the manner of children who have saved two worlds before breakfast. Ines eased them down onto Sef's daybed and stood a moment watching the rise and fall of small breathing, and felt the hollow in her ache toward the sleeping figure like a compass needle aching north.

*I know you,* she thought at the child, silently, in neither language. *Whatever I paid — I know you. My body knows you. My hands know the weight of you. Whoever I was kept you, and whoever I am now found you at a door in the rain, and maybe those are the same person wearing different coats.*

The child slept on, trusting her completely, which was either the cruelest thing in the world or the kindest, and Ines suspected — with the precision that had survived every other loss — that it was both.

At the great desk by the dome's eastern window, Sef had laid out paper. Good paper, thick and cream-colored, none of the Guild's slick vellum. A new inkpot, black as the space between stars. And a book — bound in undyed linen, unmarked, its spine stiff with newness — lying closed, waiting.

"The Chronicle of the Thirteenth Turn," Sef said. "Or whatever we end up calling it. First lines matter. Ask any poem." He dipped the pen, held it trembling over the page — and then, with a small astonished laugh at himself, set it down and slid the book across the desk to her.

"You begin," he said. "You paid the most. You should hold the pen."

Ines looked at the blank page for a long time.

She had spent her life on the far side of such pages — the auditor, the appraiser, the woman who weighed other people's memories in grains and drams and recorded their prices in a hand so fine it was said the Guild's masters used her entries as a form of meditation. Every page she had ever filled had been a page about loss. Griefs by the pint. Years by the decade. Names, always names, itemized and shelved and sold.

This page had nothing on it. Nothing taken, nothing owed, nothing priced.

Her hand — the hand that knew things — reached out and took up the pen.

She wrote the date, because dates were load-bearing. She wrote the place. And then, because the hollow in her needed filling and would not be filled from behind, she wrote the only true thing she was certain of, the one entry in all the world's accounts she could verify by direct observation, here, now, in the gold morning light of a mended world:

*The child's name is* — and she wrote it, the old name, the name the child had spoken on the first night in a language older than kingdoms, the name that had survived the severance and the well and the spending of everything else — *and the child calls me mother, and I believe us.*

She sat back. The ink dried, shining briefly, then settling matte and permanent.

Below the window, the city went on roaring softly — bells, dogs, laughter, a name shouted and answered. On the daybed, the child stirred and murmured something in two languages at once, a sentence braided down the middle, and smiled in sleep.

Ines looked at the page, at the single line of her own unmistakable handwriting, and felt the hollow shift — not filled, not yet, maybe never — but *facing*, at least, toward the future instead of the past.

"Sef," she said quietly. "How many pages are in this book?"

He counted the spine with a poet's fingers. "Two hundred and forty."

"Good." Ines turned to the second page, and the third, and found them white as the first snow of a year everyone would remember, and picked up the pen again. "Then we'd better decide what's worth keeping."

Outside, the sun cleared the last rooftop, and the whole mended world turned gold at once — and on the desk, beside the open book, the child's small hand, reaching in sleep, found Ines's sleeve and held on.
