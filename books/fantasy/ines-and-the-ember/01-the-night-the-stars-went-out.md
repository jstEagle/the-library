## 1. The Night the Stars Went Out

There are three rules to a clean inventory night at the Guild of Impossible Things, and I have followed all of them for eleven years without exception.

One: count everything twice, because impossible things breed when unobserved.

Two: never read the entries aloud after sundown, because some of them are listening.

Three: if anything in the ledgers begins to glow, put down your pen, walk out the door, and do not look back until you've had a drink and a long conversation with your better judgment.

I want it on the record that I broke rule three only after checking the locks. Twice. Because whatever else I am—mercenary, bookkeeper, apostate to a god who stopped writing back—I am *thorough*, and thorough people die of curiosity far less often than careless ones.

But I'm getting ahead of myself. The stars went first.

* * *

Inventory night falls on the last dark moon of autumn, which is fitting, because inventory is where light goes to die. The guildhall on Candlewick Row looks like nothing from the street—a narrow brick face between a chandler's and a shop that sells, as far as anyone can tell, hinges. That's deliberate. The Guild of Impossible Things survives by being forgettable. We are the footnote no one reads, the ledger line nobody audits, the door that's always "just storage." For eleven years I have kept us that way: invisible, immaculate, owed nothing by anyone worth fearing.

I was halfway through the third shelf of the reliquary cabinet—the one holding a saint's second-best doubt, a jar of last words, and somebody's grandmother's recipe for weather—when the first star went out.

I noticed it because the skylight above the counting room is old leaded glass, and on clear nights it throws little diamonds of starlight across the floor like spilled coins. One of the coins winked out. Not dimmed. *Out*, the way a candle goes when pinched, instant and total.

I told myself it was cloud.

The second star went while I was still telling myself that. Then a third, and then they started going in earnest, one by one, west to east, like someone walking along the sky snuffing them with wet fingers. I stood in the middle of the counting room with a saint's doubt in one hand and my pen behind my ear and watched the constellations disappear the way a debt disappears when the debtor dies: completely, and with consequences.

Here is what you should understand about me. I have been shot at, cursed by a hedge-witch with genuinely impressive aim, and once held hostage for six days by a man who believed he was owed a miracle. None of it has ever made my hands shake. My hands do not shake. They are the steadiest thing about me, and I have built an entire life on that fact.

My hands were steady when the wards fell.

You've heard border wards break, maybe, if you live near the marches—a sound like a bell struck underwater, a hum that stops. These weren't border wards. These were *our* wards, laid into the foundations before the guild had a name, older than the city's oldest grudge, and when they came apart the sound was glass. All the glass in the world, breaking at once, very far away and very close, so that the panes in the skylight shivered and the inkwell rippled and somewhere upstairs a bottle of preserved thunder cracked neatly down its seal line and did not, thank every indifferent heaven, discharge.

Then silence. The particular silence of a room that has just realized it is no longer protected.

I set down the saint's doubt. I did not run for the doors, because running tells the dark you're prey, and I did not light more candles, because light draws attention, and I did—this is the part I'm least proud of—think, with a clarity that felt almost like relief: *finally.*

Eleven years of invisible, immaculate calm. Some small vicious part of me had always known the bill would come due eventually. Debts always come due. It's the one law even gods keep.

* * *

I locked the doors anyway.

Not because locks would matter—I'd seen what our wards had been keeping out, or rather I hadn't seen it, which was the entire point of wards—but because locking doors is a prayer my hands know how to say. Bolt, chain, salt-line refreshed from the crock by the hearth. The guildhall has nine entrances, which sounds excessive until you remember we traffic in things that shouldn't be owned, and things that shouldn't be owned attract owners. By the time I finished, the stars were gone entirely. All of them. The sky over Candlewick Row was black as fresh ink, black as the inside of a closed ledger, and the city below it had gone quiet in a way cities don't. No dogs. No drunks singing off-key outside the Tin Lantern. Even the river sounded muffled, as though someone had laid a cloth over it.

I lit one candle. Rule-breaker that I am.

The counting room holds the master ledgers: forty-one volumes, bound in leather that isn't leather, chained to their shelves with chains that aren't really for theft. Each ledger records one category of impossible thing. Volume Nine handles debts of gratitude. Volume Seventeen, borrowed time. Volume Twenty-Three is just called *Miscellaneous* and frightens even Marrow, and Marrow is dead, which takes some doing.

I checked them the way you check a wound. Quickly, and braced for worse than you find.

Volumes One through Forty were exactly as they should be: dormant, orderly, faintly smug. Impossible things sleep well when counted properly; it's one of the few mercies of this trade. I ran my thumb down the shelf spines, feeling for warmth, for tremor, for any of the hundred small wrongnesses that mean an entry has woken up hungry.

Volume Forty-One was warm.

Now, Volume Forty-One is the ledger of souls—or rather, of soul-*transactions*, because the guild doesn't deal in souls directly. Souls are currency, collateral, surety. You can pawn one, pledge one, post one against a debt, but owning one outright is the kind of business that gets guilds burned down with their books inside. Our charter is very clear. Our charter is also, tonight, very far from my mind, because Volume Forty-One lay on its shelf glowing through the seams of its cover like a banked fire, soft gold light leaking between the pages as though something inside had caught and was burning slow.

Rule three. Put down the pen. Walk out. Don't look back.

I picked it up.

In my defense—and I am aware of how this sounds—in my defense, I am the keeper of these ledgers. Whatever woke in them woke under my watch. If a debt has come calling, it comes calling to me first, and I have never in my life let a creditor knock twice.

I carried it to the reading table, set the candle beside it, and opened to the entry that glowed.

* * *

The hand was mine. That's the first thing that made my stomach drop—not the glow, not the hour, but the handwriting, neat and slanted and unmistakably mine, dated four years ago. An entry I had written and, apparently, forgotten, which is not a thing that happens. I do not forget entries. Forgetting entries is how guilds end up haunted.

The entry read:

*Received into surety: one (1) soul, male, aged fourteen years, name withheld per seller's request. Condition: intact, unspent. Pledged against debt incurred by the undersigned party (deceased). Term of redemption: five years from date of record. Upon failure of redemption, surety passes to the holder of the original claim.*

Below that, in the column where we record the holder of the claim, someone had written—not stamped, *written*, in ink that still looked wet after four years—a single word:

*Beneath.*

And below that, where the redemption status goes, where I should have written *redeemed* or *forfeited* or *in arrears*, the page said:

*Unpaid.*

The ember-glow pulsed once as I read it, gentle as breathing. Like the entry was glad I'd finally come.

I sat down. I am not ashamed of that; standing suddenly seemed like vanity. I read the entry again, and then a third time, hunting for the details I must have recorded elsewhere—the seller's name, the nature of the debt, the counter-signature of whoever had witnessed it. Guild law requires two signatures on every soul-surety. Mine was there. The other space was blank.

Blank. On a binding contract. Which meant either I had committed gross negligence four years ago, or the counterparty had taken their signature *out* of the book, and there are perhaps three entities in this world with the reach to do that, and I had spent eleven years hoping never to learn their names.

*Beneath.* Everyone in my trade knows the word the way sailors know *down*. The court beneath the mountain. The bureaucracy of the dead. The place where old bargains go to be collected, where the interest compounds in decades and the collectors never sleep because sleeping is for things with souls of their own. Every guild has stories about them. Ours had a policy, which was simpler: *we do not sell to Beneath, we do not buy from Beneath, and if Beneath calls, we are not home.*

And yet. Four years ago, in my own hand, in my own ink: a boy's soul, pledged, term expired, unpaid, held by the court beneath the mountain.

Four years ago. What was I doing four years ago? I cast back and found—nothing. A smooth blank stretch of season, the way a wall looks where a picture used to hang. I remembered that year the way you remember a corridor you've walked a thousand times: functionally, without a single specific step. Something was missing from my memory and something was missing from the ledger's witness line and both absences had the same shape.

I don't scare easily. But there is a particular cold that starts in the wrists when you realize the books have been lying to you, or you have been lying to the books, and either way the lie has been living in your house for four years, eating, growing, waiting for the wards to fall so it could introduce itself.

The candle guttered. Outside, the starless dark pressed against the windows like a palm.

* * *

Here is what I did next, in order, and I record it because order matters when the world stops making sense:

I finished the inventory. All of it. Volumes One through Forty-One, every shelf, every jar, every bottled impossibility, counted twice, entered fair, initialed and dated. Because rule one exists for nights like this, and because if the world was coming apart, it was going to come apart over a completed ledger or not at all. Call it ritual. Call it stubbornness. Call it the only prayer I've trusted since my god went quiet: *the work, done properly, done fully, done anyway.*

Then I banked the fire, trimmed the candle, and took Volume Forty-One off the chain—not out of the building, I'm not mad, just off the chain, into my arms, the way you carry something that might wake up.

Then I sat in the counting room with the book glowing softly against my ribs like a second heartbeat, and I listened.

The city stayed silent. The dark stayed dark. And deep under Candlewick Row, deeper than cellars go, deeper than the city's bones, something turned over in its sleep—or stopped pretending to—and I felt it through the soles of my boots, faint as a pulse in a wrist.

Somewhere beneath the mountain, a claim had matured. Somewhere beneath the mountain, a collector had opened a file marked *overdue* and begun, with infinite bureaucratic patience, to walk.

And somewhere—closer, much closer, close enough that the glow warmed when I thought it—a soul waited in surety, fourteen years old when it was pledged, eighteen now if boys age the way souls don't, unpaid, unredeemed, unclaimed by anyone except a guild that wasn't home and a god that didn't answer and a bookkeeper who had just broken her own best rule and found, underneath the dread, underneath the practiced calm I wear like armor plate, one small unbearable ember of a different feeling entirely.

Not fear.

Recognition.

Because as I sat there in the dark with the ledger breathing against my chest, the front knocker sounded—three sharp raps, past midnight, past the wards, past every lock and salt-line and sensible excuse—and through the door came a voice, young and furious and cracking right down the middle, a voice that had clearly been shouting for a long time at doors less likely to open than this one.

"I know you're in there! My mother signed! You *owe* us!"

Volume Forty-One flared gold in my arms like a struck match.

I looked at the door. I looked at the book. I did the arithmetic every coward does and every keeper of debts must do eventually, and the sum came out the same as it always does:

Owed.

I got up, and I went to answer it.
