## 2. A Girl with an Ember

The trouble with a night like the one I'd just had—stars going out, wards shattering, the whole sky peeling back like old wallpaper—is that it makes you jumpy. And when you're jumpy, you make mistakes. You check locks twice. You count the silver three times. You hear a small noise on the roof at half past two in the morning and instead of doing the sensible thing, which is nothing, you go and open the door.

I want it noted for the record that I did not open the door.

She opened it for me.

There was a sound like a pigeon landing badly, followed by a scrape of small boots on slate, and then the chimney above my office exhaled soot and a girl dropped into my fireplace like an answer to a question nobody had asked. She was eleven, maybe twelve, wearing a coat two sizes too big with the sleeves rolled in furious cuffs, and she stood up out of the ashes with her chin up as if she'd been invited.

"You're the Guild of Impossible Things," she said. It wasn't a question either. Everything about this child was a demand wearing the clothes of a statement.

"We're closed," I said, because it was true, and because it was the only sentence I could find that didn't involve screaming.

"I know. I waited until you were closed." She dusted ash off her coat with two brisk swats, leaving it mostly where it was. "You don't keep honest hours anyway. The sign says *open by arrangement*. I'm arranging."

I looked at her for a long moment. Soot on her cheekbone. A scratch on her knuckles that had been cleaned and wrapped with a strip of linen torn from something—her hem, probably. Eyes the color of strong tea, and just as likely to scald you.

"Chimney's the third-worst way to break into this building," I said. "For future reference."

"There won't be a future reference. There'll be now." She reached into the enormous coat and pulled out a tin box. Biscuit tin, dented, the picture on the lid worn to a ghost of a lighthouse. She held it against her chest the way other children hold cats. "My name is Ines. My mother signed a contract with your guild eleven years ago, and I've come to collect what's owed. Or rather—" her voice caught, just once, and she wrestled it flat again, "—to give back what was taken. Same thing. Probably. You tell me. You're the ones with the books."

Now, I have been threatened by kings' men and gods' messengers and one extremely committed tax auditor from the coastal principalities. None of them frightened me the way this girl did, standing in my fireplace with ash in her hair, because all of them wanted something from me, and she already believed she owned it.

"The guild doesn't honor contracts signed by the dead," I said. It came out colder than I intended. Colder than I meant. Not cold enough, though. Never cold enough; that was always my problem.

"She signed it alive. She died after." Ines set the tin box down on my desk with a click that sounded, in the silent room, like a lock turning. "And before you say anything else—I read your rules. They're nailed up behind the bar downstairs, which is a stupid place for rules, by the way. Rule four: *A debt witnessed is a debt owed.* My mother had a witness. My grandmother. She's dead too, but she told me everything before she went, and she made me memorize the date and the seal and the clause number. Clause nine. *Collateral held in trust, returnable upon presentation.*"

I hadn't moved. I want that understood. I had not moved, had not spoken, had not done anything except stand there being a professional, and yet some part of me—the part that keeps the ledgers, the part that has never once in twenty years let a number go uncounted—had already opened the book in its head and found the page.

Clause nine. Seal of the third circle. Dated the winter the river froze early.

I knew that entry. I had written it.

* * *

Here is what you need to understand about the Guild of Impossible Things: we do not do magic tricks. We are not conjurers. We are, if you strip away the name and the reputation and the truly excessive amount of candle wax we go through, clerks. We keep accounts of things that should not be ownable—last breaths, first loves, the exact weight of a regret—and we make sure they end up where they're supposed to be. When someone sells their shadow, someone has to hold the receipt. That's us. We are the receipt.

It is boring work, mostly. It is holy work, occasionally. It is work I am very good at, and I had spent the years since the god I serve went quiet telling myself that good clerks don't ask questions, they just balance columns.

The girl in my office was a column that would not balance.

"Sit down," I said finally, because my legs had opinions about standing.

"I'd rather not."

"Suit yourself." I sat alone, which somehow made it worse. I unlocked the bottom drawer of my desk—the locked drawer, the one even Marrow doesn't rummage through, though he's dead and rummaging is technically his whole profession—and took out the ledger bound in grey leather with no title on the spine. Some books you don't label. Labeling is advertising.

I turned to the entry dated the winter the river froze early, and there it was in my own handwriting, younger and more careful than I write now:

*Received into trust, per contract of M— Blackfen, one (1) soul, secondary issue, male, aged six years. Collateral against debt outstanding. Held in trust, returnable upon presentation of token and proof of kinship. Clause nine.*

Below it, in different ink, added later, in handwriting I did not recognize:

*Transferred. Paid in full to the Court Beneath the Mountain.*

I had never seen that second line before. I want to be clear about that. I kept this ledger. I balanced it every quarter. That line had not been there last quarter, or any quarter, and ink does not grow on pages by itself unless something very large and very patient wants it there.

"What was the debt?" I asked. "Your mother's. What did she owe?"

Ines's jaw set. "That's not your business."

"It's exactly my business. It's the entire business. It's the only business."

"She owed—" The fury in her face flickered, and underneath it I saw the thing fury is almost always hiding, which is fear wearing a borrowed coat. "Medicine. For Tam. My brother. He was sick when he was little. Fever that wouldn't break. She went somewhere she shouldn't have been able to go and asked for help, and they helped, and he lived, and then—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "Then there was a paper she wouldn't show anyone, and a box she kept on the high shelf, and she told me if anything ever happened to her, I wasn't to open the box, I was to bring it here. To you. Because *they honor their contracts*, she said. She said it like it was the one good thing left in the world."

The candle between us guttered, though there was no draft.

"And then she died," Ines said, "and the fever came back for Tam last month, the same fever, grown-up now and hungry, and the doctors shook their heads, and I opened the box."

She put her hands on the tin lid.

Inside, when she lifted it, was light. Not firelight, not lamplight—light the way an ember is light, deep red-gold, steady, breathing almost, rising and falling in a rhythm I recognized because every living thing keeps it. It sat on a bed of wool scraps in a biscuit tin and it burned without heat, without smoke, without consuming anything at all, and the room got warmer anyway, in a way that had nothing to do with temperature.

"That's him," Ines said. "That's Tam. What's left of him right now. He's asleep somewhere else, wherever souls wait, and this is his—his spark, his pilot light, whatever you want to call it. And somebody bought it. Somebody *bought my brother*, and paid my mother's medical debt with it, and wrote it in your book like it was a sack of flour."

Her voice didn't crack this time. That was worse. An eleven-year-old whose voice doesn't crack when she says *somebody bought my brother* has practiced being angry until anger became load-bearing, and you cannot pull a load-bearing wall out of a child without the whole house coming down on both of you.

I looked at the ember. This was my mistake, or my fate, depending on how you file such things. I looked directly at it, the way you're not supposed to look at anything in our trade, and the ember—

The ember looked back.

I don't mean it had eyes. I mean that the light leaned. The way a plant leans toward a window. The way a dog leans toward the one person in the room who will feed it. It leaned toward me, across the desk, across the wool and the tin and the space between, and it flared—not brighter, exactly, but *closer*, as if distance were a thing it had decided to dispute—and a warmth went through my chest that I had not felt in seven years.

Seven years, three months, and some days. Since the night the prayers started coming back unopened. Since Veth went silent and the guild learned to whisper instead of speak.

The ember flared, and in the flare I heard—or felt, or remembered hearing, memory and hope being difficult to tell apart in the dark—a pressure against the inside of my ribs like a hand pressing a door that has been shut a long time. Not opening it. Just reminding me it was a door.

"Oh," said Ines softly.

I looked up. She was watching me with an expression no eleven-year-old should have mastered: the expression of someone watching a trap close, and knowing they built it.

"It likes you," she said. "Tam always liked people who pretend they don't like anybody."

"That's not—" I cleared my throat. Somewhere in the last minute my professional composure had gone missing, along with, apparently, my ability to finish sentences. "That is not what's happening."

"He never met you. But Mama talked about the guild sometimes. About the clerk who wrote neat numbers and never smiled and gave her tea when she came to sign the papers, even though the rules said don't get comfortable with clients." Ines tilted her head. "Was that you?"

I had given her tea. I remembered it suddenly, completely: a thin woman with river-cold hands and a cough she pretended was nothing, sitting across from me while I explained clause nine, and me breaking guild protocol to pour her a cup because her hands were shaking too hard to hold the pot. I remembered thinking, *this woman is selling something she doesn't understand, and I am the instrument of it, and the least I can do is warm her hands.*

I had filed that memory under *unpleasant but necessary* and never opened it again. Ledgers are good for that. Everything fits somewhere, and once it's entered, you're allowed to stop looking at it.

"Yes," I said. "That was me."

"Then you knew." Her voice went very quiet, which from her was more dangerous than shouting. "You knew what she was signing away. You watched her sign it. And you poured her tea."

The candle guttered again. Outside, the starless dark pressed against the windows like a face.

"Yes," I said. "I did."

* * *

We sat with that for a while. I made tea—it seemed obscene not to, and also seemed obscene to, and I made it anyway, because guilt is a poor advisor but a reliable bartender—and she wrapped both hands around the cup and glared at me over the rim, and the ember sat in its tin between us like a small sleeping sun that had chosen my desk as its altar.

"Here's what happens now," I said, when the tea was half gone and my voice had remembered how to be level. "You take the box, and you go home, and you forget you ever found this place. That's the mercy option. I'll even walk you to the door."

"No."

"The alternative is that I explain to you why the mercy option exists. The court that holds your brother's collateral is not a shopkeeper you can argue with. It is older than the border wards. It is older than the stars, which, incidentally, went out tonight—which you may have noticed, since you were apparently climbing around on rooftops instead of hiding like a sensible person. Whatever is happening out there, it is happening because something enormous has begun collecting. And you are carrying the single most collectible object in this city in a biscuit tin."

"I'm not leaving without my brother."

"You don't have your brother. You have a spark. The rest of him is beneath a mountain, in a court that buys souls the way merchants buy grain, and the road there goes through country that eats travelers, and the tolls are paid in things you can't get back. I've read about it. Everyone in my trade has read about it. Nobody who's read about it goes."

"Then nobody who's read about it gets Tam back." She set down the cup. "Somebody has to go who hasn't. Or somebody who has, and decided it doesn't matter." She looked at me with those tea-colored eyes, and there was no fear in them anymore, and that frightened me more than anything else that night. "You knew my mama. You gave her tea. You watched her sign. So you tell me, clerk—if it was your brother in that tin, and everyone who could help kept saying *too dangerous, go home, forget it*—what would you do?"

What would I do. As if I were a person with answers. As if I hadn't spent seven years being a person whose only answer was *balance the column and don't look up*.

"I would do something stupid," I admitted.

"Good," said Ines. "So would I. We'll do it together. It evens out."

And that was when the ember did the thing I will be explaining to myself for the rest of my life, however long the ledger says that is. It rose. Out of the tin, off the wool, up into the air between us, trailing a thread of gold like a kite string, and it drifted—not toward the window, not toward the mountain I could feel lying out there in the dark like a held breath—but toward me. Toward my chest. It stopped a hand's width from my sternum and pulsed, twice, slow, like a knock.

Like a knock on a door.

Veth, my order says, is the god of debts and mercy, and mercy is just a debt the world owes you that nobody wrote down. Seven years of silence. Seven years of returned prayers. And now a stolen child's soul-spark was knocking on my ribs as if it knew something I didn't—as if somewhere under the mountain, in whatever dark court held it, something had heard my name spoken in a contract and passed the word along.

Or as if the silence itself had just shifted, the way a held breath shifts before it becomes words.

Ines watched the ember hover, and her face did something complicated—young and old at once, hopeful and braced for disappointment, the exact expression of a person who has been handed a gift and is checking it for strings.

"It's never done that," she whispered. "It's never gone near anybody. Mama couldn't even touch the box without gloves."

The ember pulsed again. Knocked again. And far below the floorboards, below the cellar, below the foundations and the old sealed well and whatever sleeps under guild houses that trade in souls—something answered. Not in words. In weight. The whole building settled a half-inch, like a ship changing course, and every ledger in my locked drawer slid an inch toward the edge of the shelf, as if leaning in to listen.

Ines looked at me. I looked at Ines.

"Well?" she said. "Are you going to answer it, or do I have to break rule five too?"

I didn't ask what rule five was. I was afraid I'd written it.
