## 3. Ledgers of Impossible Things

The girl slept in the reading room, curled in the good chair like she owned it, the tin box held against her chest the way other children hold a stuffed animal or a grudge. I had offered her a blanket. She had refused it on principle, then taken it when she thought I wasn't looking.

I sat across from her at the long table and opened the ledger to the entry that had been glowing since the stars went out.

I should explain something about our ledgers before we go any further, because you will not understand anything else that happens if you don't. The Guild of Impossible Things does not trade in gold. Gold is easy. Gold is for people who believe everything has a price, which is a comforting lie we let merchants tell themselves. We trade in things that should not be ownable at all: last words, unspent years, the color of a room you loved as a child, apologies never delivered, names outgrown, shadows sold by desperate men to pay desperate debts. And souls. Occasionally souls. Rarely. Never happily.

Every one of them is written down. That is the whole point of us. Not the buying — anyone with enough hunger can buy. The writing down. A debt that exists only in someone's memory can be denied, distorted, forgotten. A debt in ink is a debt that can be *answered*. Veth taught us that, back when Veth still answered us.

The entry read:

*One (1) soul, male, age nine at time of sale. Sold by: Maren Vasquez-Okonkwo, of sound mind and failing health. Purchased by: [the ink refused to say; it simply pooled into a black dot, the way it always did when the court beneath the mountain was involved]. Consideration received: one winter's mercy, extended. Status: PAID IN FULL. Held in perpetuity.*

Paid in full. That was the part that made my hands cold. Most soul-sales in our books were marked *in arrears*, or *contested*, or my personal favorite, *pending divine review*, which was guild-speak for *we are hoping Veth fixes this because we certainly won't*. But this one said paid in full, and paid-in-full meant the court considered the matter closed, and the court did not reopen closed matters for anybody, including gods.

Except the ember in the tin box said otherwise. An ember that burns without heat is not a receipt. It is a complaint.

I looked up at the girl. Ines. She'd told me her name twice, once as a weapon and once as an afterthought, and both times like it cost her something.

"You knew my mother," she said. She wasn't asleep after all. Eleven-year-olds sleep when they feel safe, and this one hadn't felt safe in what I suspected was a very long time.

"I knew of her," I said. "Maren came to the guild four times. Debts, consultations, one purchase." I turned back a page, because I am a coward with excellent record-keeping. "She bought back her father's shadow. Paid nearly everything she had."

"She never talked about it."

"They rarely do. Talking about it makes it real, and most people prefer their bargains imaginary until the collector arrives."

"What did she sell my brother's soul *for*?"

And there it was. The question I had been circling all night like a moth around a lamp I didn't want to touch. I could have lied. I have lied to clients before; it is one of the few guild-sanctioned activities. But she was looking at me with her mother's exact jaw set at her mother's exact angle, and lying to that face would have been like lying to a locked door.

"Time," I said. "A winter's mercy. Her illness was going to take her before spring. Someone — something — offered to extend it. Long enough to settle her affairs. Long enough to say goodbye."

"To say goodbye," Ines repeated. Her voice did something complicated. "She got three months. And Tam got — forever in a box under a mountain."

"Yes."

"That's not fair."

"No," I said. "It isn't. Fairness is not a currency anyone down there accepts."

* * *

You need to know how I ended up keeping ledgers for a god who no longer speaks, so let me tell you about the worst night of my life, which was also the best one, which is the sort of arithmetic Veth specialized in.

I was twenty-three, and I owed a debt I could not pay. This is not a metaphor. I had signed a contract with a river-spirit — do not ever sign anything with a river-spirit, they consider fine print a form of worship — and the terms were my voice for seven years, and I had signed it to buy passage for forty-one refugees across a flooded border, and I would sign it again, but that doesn't mean I wanted to spend seven years unable to say my own name.

The collectors came on the new moon. River-spirits delegate their collections to things with too many joints and too much patience, and they found me in a ditch outside a town whose name I still can't say without flinching, and they began to take the voice out of me, and it feels exactly how you're imagining, except worse, because they start with the words you love most.

That's when the quiet arrived.

Not silence — quiet. There's a difference, and if you've ever stood in a snowfall at midnight you know it. Silence is absence. Quiet is presence, holding its breath. The collectors stopped. Everything stopped. And a figure stood at the edge of the ditch, small and grey and unremarkable, the way a ledger is unremarkable, and it looked at the collectors the way an auditor looks at creative accounting.

It did not speak. It never speaks, not really. It *writes*. A page appeared between us — I have never learned where the pages come from and I have stopped asking — and on it, in handwriting like frost forming:

*This debt is forgiven.*

The collectors made a sound I still hear sometimes when the wind hits the shutters wrong, and left. And the small grey figure looked at me, and I understood two things with total certainty. First: I had just been given something enormous and free, which is the most dangerous thing that can happen to a person. Second: someday, somehow, I would try to give it back, and it would refuse, and I would spend the rest of my life trying to deserve it instead.

The page turned over. New ink:

*There is work. There are ledgers. Nothing impossible gets bought. Only returned.*

I swore to it there in the ditch. Not out loud — I couldn't, my throat still ached — but Veth has never required words. Veth requires signatures. I pressed my bleeding palm to the page, and the page accepted it, and that was that. Twenty years ago this autumn. I have kept the books ever since, and I have never once heard the god's voice, and I have never once needed to. Until now.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about working for a god of debts and mercy: the two jobs are the same job. Every debt is a story about what someone needed and what they were willing to lose for it. Mercy is just the willingness to read the whole story before you collect.

The guild existed before me and will exist, I suppose, after. We keep offices in a building that is technically a warehouse, technically a chapel, and technically, on certain nights, a door. Our charter is one sentence long, written in Veth's frost-hand on the inside of the front door where every member must read it daily: *What is owned wrongly must be returned; what is owed rightly must be forgiven; write everything down.* Members join by signing. Members leave by being erased, which almost never happens, because Veth forgave so freely that even our worst members got second chances, and thirds, and in Old Brannic's case a seventh, though his sins were mostly drinking and sentimentality.

We traded in what shouldn't be owned because leaving those things unowned meant leaving them in the hands of things that *would* own them. If we didn't hold the widow's grief in trust, the ash-brokers would buy it at famine prices. If we didn't keep the ledger on stolen names, the names would vanish entirely, and a person without a recorded name is a person the world can lose track of, permanently, the way you lose a glove. We were not merchants. We were — I told myself this often enough that I mostly believed it — custodians. Librarians of the unsellable.

And underneath all of it, the rule we never broke, the law I was about to break: *we do not deal with the court beneath the mountain.*

Everyone called it different things. The Undercourt. the Bureaus of the Dead. The Audit. What it was, was this: a bureaucracy that had decided souls were assets, memory was collateral, and grief was a growth market. They didn't steal. Stealing is crude and creates witnesses. They *bought*. They waited at bedsides and crossroads and the edges of battlefields, and they offered exactly what the desperate needed, priced exactly at what the desperate couldn't afford to refuse. And then they filed everything away in shelves of humming dark, and sent no receipts, and answered no complaints, because their entire operation ran on the principle that no one reads the terms.

Veth hated them. I use the past tense carefully. Veth forgave their debtors wherever it could reach, undid their contracts clause by clause, and generally conducted itself as a one-god audit of everything they built. Which, I understood even then, was the sort of behavior that gets a god noticed by things that notice gods.

I just never imagined the noticing would look like stars going out.

* * *

Ines had fallen asleep for real this time, mid-fury, the way children do — outrage is exhausting fuel. I covered her with the blanket she'd officially rejected. Then I took the ledger to my desk, lit the lamp with the low flame we save for delicate work, and did something I had done perhaps six times in twenty years.

I pulled the older book. The First Ledger. The one written in Veth's own hand, or as close to a hand as Veth keeps.

If you want to know who bought a soul, you don't read the sale entry. Sale entries are written by sellers, and sellers lie. You read the consideration — the payment — because payments leave trails the way wounded animals leave blood. And the consideration here troubled me. *One winter's mercy, extended.* Mercy is Veth's word. Mercy is *our* word. The court beneath the mountain doesn't deal in mercy; they deal in extensions, deferrals, forbearance. Cold financial instruments. For someone to have purchased a winter's mercy for Maren Vasquez-Okonkwo, someone with access to Veth's own stores would have had to supply it.

I turned to the inventory of mercies. Mercies are tracked like everything else — granted, spent, outstanding. And there, dated the same week as Maren's sale, an entry that made the lamp flame lean sideways as if avoiding it:

*One (1) winter's mercy, drawn from reserve. Authorized by: A. Blackwood, Keeper. Purpose: compassionate extension, terminal case. Cost borne by: [blank].*

I authorized it. Fourteen months ago. A woman came to the guild door, dying, terrified, asking not for a cure — she was past bargaining for cures — but for time. Time to put her affairs in order. Time to tell her children whatever mothers tell their children at the end. I checked the reserves, found a mercy available, and authorized the draw. Standard procedure. Compassionate case. I remember feeling *good* about it. I remember thinking, this is why the guild exists.

What I did not check — what I have never checked, because checking it means admitting what I suspect — is where the court beneath the mountain fits into it. Because mercies don't come from nowhere. They come from the reserve, and the reserve is fed by *forfeitures*, and forfeitures come from debts collected, and somewhere in that chain, someone converted a god's mercy into a court's collateral. The court didn't buy Tam's soul despite the mercy. I am increasingly, horribly certain the court bought Tam's soul *with* it. They took the kindness I authorized and used it as the coin. My compassion was the purchase price of a child.

I sat with that for a long time. The lamp burned. Somewhere upstairs, Sefa's maps rustled in their sleep, the way they do when the geography is nervous.

Here is the thing about guilt: it is a debt with no counterparty. You cannot pay it. You cannot discharge it. You can only carry it, and carry it, and carry it, and occasionally discover that while you were carrying it, it grew.

I had spent twenty years believing I worked for a god of mercy. Tonight I learned I might be the instrument through which mercy got weaponized. And the girl asleep in the reading room was holding the proof in a tin box, and she had walked through our broken wards and demanded justice from the only institution in the world that keeps honest books, and she had no idea — none — that the honest books were the crime scene.

The ember flared. I swear this happened; I was watching. Through the tin, through the metal, a line of light like a hairline crack, and it pointed — not glowed, *pointed* — south and down. Toward the mountain. Toward the black-dot buyer. The boy's soul, or what remained of it within reach, straining toward the rest of itself the way a compass strains north.

And then, faintly, from everywhere and nowhere, from the pages themselves, I felt something I had not felt in twenty years of service.

The ledger was warm.

Not the ember. The *ledger*. The First Ledger, Veth's own hand, warming under my palms like a body coming back to life, like a held breath finally releasing — and on the blank page at the back, where nothing had ever been written, frost began to form. Letters. Slowly. Painfully, if frost can feel pain, and I think it can.

Two words. Shaky, half-formed, like a voice through a wall, like a shout from the bottom of a well:

*FIND ME.*

Then the warmth guttered out, and the frost melted, and the page was blank, and I was alone with a sleeping child, a guilty conscience, and the first message my god had sent me in twenty years.

I closed the ledger. I woke Sefa, because if anyone could chart a road to a place that isn't on any map, it's her. I woke Brannic, because if anyone owed the guild enough to die for it, he'd already been volunteering for decades.

And I went to the door where Marrow haunts the threshold he refuses to cross, and I asked him the question I'd been dreading:

"Marrow. You worked for them once. Before us. Before you deserted." His translucent face did something I'd never seen it do. "How do we get into the court beneath the mountain?"

He looked at me for a long moment, and then he smiled, and it was the worst smile I have ever seen.

"Keeper," he said, "you don't get *into* the court. You get *audited*."
