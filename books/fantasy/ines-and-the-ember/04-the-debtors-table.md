## 4. The Debtors' Table

*Ada*

There is a particular kind of silence that settles over a room when you ask people to do something stupid on purpose.

I had heard it before—once when I proposed we audit the tax collector of Vell, and once when I suggested, during a lean winter, that the guild simply *stop existing for a while*, which is harder than it sounds when your building is load-bearing in the metaphysical sense. Both times the silence had been the silence of people deciding how to say no politely.

This time it was worse. This time I hadn't even finished asking.

The debtors' table sits in the back room of the guild, behind the ledger hall, and it is not a table so much as an argument between carpenters that someone won by nailing everything down. It has eleven chairs, which is eight more than we have members with standing to sit in them, and the extra seven are there because removing furniture from this guild requires paperwork nobody wants to file. The candles are tallow. The wine is the kind you drink out of obligation rather than pleasure. The atmosphere is, as always, faintly accusatory.

I had called the meeting at midnight because midnight is when the guild does its honest work. Daylight is for appearances: the shopfront where we sell maps to places that no longer exist and charms against misfortunes that haven't been invented yet. Midnight is for the ledgers. And what I'd found in the ledgers required witnesses, or at least accomplices, or at the very least people who would remember afterward that I had asked and they had said no, so that when the guild's founders rose from whatever drawer we keep them in, my name wouldn't be alone on the indictment.

Three came. Out of nine. Two sent apologies written in handwriting so beautiful it was clearly meant to distract from the content, which was "no." Four did not answer at all, which in guild terms is a form of refusal with better manners.

Marrow arrived first, because Marrow is dead and therefore always arrives first; death has excellent punctuality. He drifted through the wall rather than using the door, which he says he does to make a point about doors being for the living, though I suspect he just enjoys watching me flinch. He is a debt-collector ghost, which means he spent his life chasing what people owed and has now spent his afterlife doing exactly the same thing, which tells you something true and terrible about habit. He wears the grey coat of his old office, buttoned wrong, and he keeps a ledger of his own inside his ribcage, which rattles faintly when he laughs.

Sefa came second, wet from the rain and complaining about it in three languages, one of which I'm fairly sure she invented last Tuesday specifically to express dissatisfaction with weather. She is our cartographer of places that move—cities that migrate, rivers that change their minds, roads with opinions—and her maps are the only ones in the world that come with expiry dates. She sat down, put her boots on the table, and looked at me with the expression of a woman who already knew what I was going to ask and had prepared her refusal on the walk over.

Brannic came last, because Brannic is old and old men arrive at the speed of dignity. He owes the guild his death. It was the price of the miracle that saved him from drowning forty years ago—we bought him back from the river, and the river kept the receipt—and every year since, he has come to the table and negotiated another postponement. He is the only debtor in history who treats his own mortality like a subscription he keeps forgetting to cancel. He brought bread. He always brings bread. It is his way of saying that whatever happens, someone should eat.

I laid the ledger open between us, turned to the entry that glowed.

"A soul," I said. "Sold. Unpaid. The buyer is the court beneath the mountain."

Nobody said anything, which was very loud.

"The seller was the girl's mother," I went on, because stopping felt worse than continuing. "She signed before Ines was born. The court collected the brother instead—the mother died before the payment came due, and debts pass down the bloodline like eye color. The girl walked into our hall tonight carrying what's left of him in a biscuit tin. An ember that burns cold. She wants us to honor the contract. Which means getting him back. From *there*."

"Where the stars used to be," Sefa said quietly.

"Where the stars used to be," I agreed.

Brannic tore his bread in half and handed half to me without looking, the way you feed someone you've decided to worry about. "And you're asking us to break guild law," he said. Not a question. Old men don't waste questions on things they can see coming like weather.

"I'm asking you to help me break guild law," I said. "There's a difference. If it goes wrong, I want it on record that I corrupted you all personally."

Marrow laughed, a dry rattle like a drawer of coins being counted by a nervous man. "Guild law," he said. "Ada. Sweet, doomed Ada. You wrote half of those clauses yourself."

"I know," I said. "Clause Nine especially. *No member shall retrieve what has been lawfully sold.* I wrote it after the Halloway affair, when we nearly started a war with the tide-court over a woman's shadow. I wrote it because I watched what happens when the guild starts deciding which contracts count and which don't." I turned the page. The glow of the entry lit my hands from below, making them look like something dredged up. "And now here's a contract that shouldn't count, sitting in my book, glowing like it knows it."

"That's not how ink works," Sefa said.

"No," I said. "It isn't."

Silence again. The good kind this time, the kind where people are thinking instead of refusing. I let it run for exactly as long as I could bear, which was eleven seconds.

"Sefa. Can you map the way down?"

"No."

"Can you map the wards?"

"...Maybe."

"Marrow. You worked collections. You know how courts like that think."

"I deserted that court, Ada." His voice went thin. "You don't ask a deserter to go back. That's not courage, that's cruelty with a schedule."

"Brannic—"

"Don't." He held up a hand, spotted and steady. "Don't ask me to die for this yet. I'm saving it. A man only gets the one, and I intend to spend it on something with better odds than a hole under a mountain full of accountants."

"They're not accountants," I said. "They're worse. They're *believers*. Accountants doubt themselves occasionally."

One by one, then, they said no. Sefa said no while drawing a map of the table itself, absently, her fingers never stopping—she maps things when she's frightened, and she was mapping the exits. Marrow said no twice, once out loud and once in the language of the dead, which sounds like wind through a keyhole and means roughly the same thing. Brannic said no by eating his bread very slowly and looking at me with eyes that had already said yes and were ashamed of it.

And I sat there at the head of a table built for eleven, with four empty chairs staring at me like open mouths, and understood something I had managed to avoid understanding for fifteen years of keeping books for impossible things:

Leadership is mostly just being the person who asks, and being asked-from is its own kind of exile. Everyone in that room liked me. Everyone in that room trusted me, more or less, in the specific way you trust a bridge—not warmly, but enough to stand on. And not one of them would cross with me.

I closed the ledger. The glow didn't go out. It never does. That's the thing about souls; they're patient in a way that makes patience look like a threat.

"Meeting adjourned," I said. "Bread's on the table if anyone wants it."

They stayed anyway. That's the part I didn't expect. Nobody helped, but nobody left. Sefa spread her half-finished map of the table across the real table and started adding rooms she remembered from dreams. Marrow drifted to the window and stood there doing his impression of regret, which is indistinguishable from his impression of fog. Brannic poured wine for four and drank his share and mine.

"You'll go regardless," Brannic said eventually. It wasn't a question either. Old men get two kinds of statements and spend them freely.

"Yes."

"Then we'll need to know what the wards were actually for." He nodded at Sefa. "Before you walk through them. Wards that fall aren't walls coming down, girl. They're hands opening."

Sefa looked up from her map. Something moved behind her eyes—the look she gets when a place starts talking to her.

"The wards," she said slowly, "were drawn on a moving surface. Do you understand? The border isn't land. Land doesn't move. Someone mapped a boundary onto something that *drifts*, and pinned it, and it held for two hundred years, and tonight it stopped holding, and—" She stopped, pen hovering. "I need paper. Big paper. And I need to not be interrupted until dawn."

"You're going to map it," I said. "After saying no."

"I said no to going *down*," she snapped. "Mapping is free. Mapping is what I do when I can't sleep, which, congratulations, will be never again." She was already rolling out a fresh sheet, pinning corners with the candlesticks, her hands moving faster than her mouth, which for Sefa was genuinely remarkable.

Marrow turned from the window. "If she finds what I think she'll find," he said, "you should know something before she says it out loud." His rattle-voice dropped lower. "I collected for the court beneath the mountain for sixty years before I ran. And the thing about that place—the thing nobody writes down—is that it never comes to collect all at once. It takes a star here. A ward there. A name. It lets you get comfortable between payments. So when the whole border falls in one night—"

He didn't finish. He didn't have to. We all heard the shape of it: not a raid. A repossession. Everything, called due at once, because somewhere below, someone had finally read the fine print.

Brannic refilled my cup without being asked. "Drink," he said. "Tomorrow you'll need your hands steady and your head foolish. Those are different requirements. Wine handles both."

I drank. Outside, the starless dark pressed against the windows like a creditor's palm, and somewhere upstairs a girl slept in a chair she refused to surrender, one hand curled around a tin box that glowed faintly through the metal, patient as interest accruing.

Four noes. One maybe. And a map beginning, line by careful line, to show us exactly how deep the hole went.

* * *

*Marrow*

I have been dead for thirty-one years and I still take notes.

It's the coat, probably. You wear a collector's grey long enough and the habits grow into you like roots into a pipe. When Ada spoke tonight—*help me break the law I wrote*—my hand twitched for a pen that rotted off my bones decades ago, and I caught myself drafting the refusal in triplicate. Denial, copy one. Denial, copy two. Denial, filed beneath the sternum for future reference.

I said no. I want that noted properly, because everyone else at that table got to say no like it cost them something, and mine cost more, and nobody could see it, because ghosts grieve on the inside. It's the one advantage of the condition.

Here is what I did not tell them. Here is what I will not tell them until the road forces it out of me, because some confessions you carry to the door of the place that needs to hear them, and no sooner.

I did not desert the court beneath the mountain because of the work. The work was fine. The work was *easy*. You find a soul that's fallen delinquent—you follow the smell of unpaid grief—and you bring it home to be filed. Sixty years. I was good at it. I was, if the ledgers below still exist, and they do, because nothing down there is ever allowed to stop existing—I was *commended*.

I left because of a woman in Marchford who owed one night of her life. One night. That was the whole balance. She'd sold a single evening—her twenty-third birthday, as it happened—to cover her father's burial, and the court dispatched me to collect, and I found her sitting in that evening like a girl sitting in a bath she couldn't afford to leave, and she looked up at me and said, "Is this all it's worth? One night?" And I checked the books, because checking is what I do, and the books said yes. One night. That was the market rate. Her whole birthday, candles and all, priced against a hole in the ground.

I marked the account *uncollectible*. First falsification of my career. Then a second. Then I stopped counting, which for a collector is the same as dying, and shortly afterward I did that too.

So when Ada asked, and I said no, what I meant was: *I have already been down that mountain, and I am still paying it off, and the interest is memory.*

But I stayed at the table. I stood at the window and watched Sefa draw, and around the third hour I saw what she saw, and my dead heart—which does not beat, and yet—did something in the region of beating.

The wards weren't a wall. They were a *schedule*. And the schedule had just been torn up.

I know what that means. I wrote schedules like it. And I knew, standing there in the grey coat I cannot take off, that my no was going to expire before morning. Debts do that. Even the ones you owe yourself.

* * *

*Sefa*

Dawn found me surrounded by paper.

Not a map—a crime scene. Eleven sheets, pinned and overlapped, each one a layer of the border as it had been: the stone wards, the river wards, the wards made of spoken agreements that older maps show as dotted lines because how else do you draw a promise. I had pulled every survey the guild owned, including three that were technically hallucinations, and I'd laid them over one another until the truth underneath stopped hiding.

Here is what cartographers know that kings don't: borders are fictions that agree to hold still. This one had held still for two hundred years because someone very clever had realized the truth—that the world's edge drifts, everything drifts, the ground is just a slow kind of sea—and anchored the boundary not to land but to *obligation*. To debts outstanding. As long as accounts below remained unsettled, the anchors held. The wards were not keeping anything out.

They were collateral.

I came downstairs with the master sheet rolled under my arm and found Ada asleep at the debtors' table, cheek on the glowing ledger, and Brannic asleep in his chair with his hand still near his knife, and Marrow standing watch at the window the way fog stands watch over a harbor. I woke Ada by unrolling the map across their face, which is traditional among cartographers and considered friendly.

"Look," I said. "Look at what the wards were."

Ada looked. I watched the understanding arrive the way weather arrives—first the pressure, then the gray, then the ruin.

"They weren't keeping the court out," Ada said.

"They were *payment held in trust*," I said. "Every unsettled debt below was a peg holding the border down. And last night somebody called in everything at once. The stars going out isn't an invasion, Ada. It's a *repossession notice*. The whole sky. They're taking the collateral back because someone down there has decided the account is closed—or because they're preparing to move house, or because—" I stopped. My hands were shaking. I put them flat on the map, on the border I'd drawn a hundred times, and felt it under my palms like a pulse that had stopped. "Or because something changed in their books. Something big enough to make them liquidate."

Ada was quiet for a long moment. Then, softly, horribly: "The ember. A soul sold and *unpaid*. An open entry. In *my* ledger."

"If their books touch yours," I said, "then that little girl's contract isn't just a tragedy. It's a loose thread in the anchor. And Ada—they didn't fall. Look at the edges of the ward-lines. They didn't tear. They were *released*. Deliberately. Neatly. Like fingers letting go one at a time."

Old Brannic opened one eye. "Then it's not a question of whether the mountain comes collecting," he said. "It's a question of what it wants *first*."

We all looked at the ledger. At the glow under Ada's cheek, rising and falling with their breath, like something sleeping. Like something waiting to be picked up.

Marrow turned from the window. And I swear on every map I've ever ruined, the ghost said:

"I'll draw you the road."

* * *

*Brannic*

I have postponed my death for forty years, and I'll tell you the secret of it: it's easy. Dying is like any other appointment. You simply decline to attend, and eventually the matter is handed to a junior clerk who forgets about you. Rivers, fevers, wars—I've outlived them all through the simple administrative trick of having somewhere else to be.

But I watched the girl sleep in the hall this morning, and I watched Ada fade a little more behind their practiced calm, and I did sums. Old men do sums. It's most of what we're for.

The pass into the mountains—the only pass Sefa's maps agree exists—has a toll. Not coin. Not names. It takes *time*, all the time you have left, every hour of it, and it spends the hours cruelly, and no one who enters with a future comes out with one. A young party loses decades crossing it. Loses marriages, children, the whole harvest of a life.

An old man loses what he was already withholding.

I sat with that arithmetic through breakfast, through Sefa's maps, through Marrow's confession delivered in the tone of a man reading his own indictment. And when Ada finally looked at me across the ruins of the meal and said, "Brannic, you don't have to—" I raised my hand and stopped them, because I've earned the right to finish my own sentences.

"I know what I owe," I said. "I've known for forty years. The guild bought me back from the river and the river kept the receipt, and I have spent four decades negotiating extensions like some merchant haggling over his own coffin." I tapped the table. "Tonight I'm paying early. On my terms. At a time and place of my choosing. That's not sacrifice, Ada. That's *finally honoring a contract*—and if there's one thing this madhouse of ours respects, it's a debt settled in full."

Ada's face did something complicated. I looked away, kindly, and reached for the bread.

That evening I cooked. Real cooking, the kind I learned before the river, before the guild, before any of it: browned onions, cheap wine in the stew, bread from the morning baked hard on purpose so it would soak properly. Marrow couldn't eat and ate anyway, out of courtesy, pushing the food around a bowl like a man visiting his own grave. Sefa cried twice and denied it both times, blaming the onions, which was fair, since I'd also blamed the onions. Ada drank and laughed at my jokes, the bad old ones, the ones they've heard forty times, laughing the way you laugh at a funeral where the deceased got to attend.

The girl came down near the end. Ines. Eleven years old, furious, clutching her tin box, glaring at all of us like we were a bill she intended to dispute. She sat next to me without asking, because children can tell which adults are already halfway gone, and she stole bread off my plate, and I let her, and she said:

"You're the one who's supposed to die."

"That's me," I said.

"For us?"

"For a story worth being in," I said, and she frowned, because eleven-year-olds have no patience for poetry, and rightly so. So I told her the truth instead. "For you. For your brother. Because someone once paid for me, and I've been spending the change ever since, and it's past time I gave some back."

She looked at me for a long moment with those furious eyes, and then she did the worst thing. She patted my hand. Small fingers, flour-dusted, gentle as a debt collector.

"I'll remember you," she said. "Even if the ledgers don't."

I laughed until my ribs hurt, which at my age is a genuine risk, and I thought: *well. Maybe the river can wait one more day.*

But only one.

Because at midnight, Sefa unrolled the final map, and put her finger on the pass, and said the words I'd been waiting for since the stars went out:

"We leave tomorrow. And Brannic—your extension is up."
