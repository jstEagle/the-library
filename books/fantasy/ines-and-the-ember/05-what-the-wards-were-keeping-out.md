## 5. What the Wards Were Keeping Out

I have mapped every kind of place there is to map, and I want you to understand what that means before you trust anything I say.

I have charted the Mire of Second Chances, which relocates itself out of spite. I have drawn the coastline of the Drowned Duchy, whose borders shift whenever someone tells the truth about it. I once spent four months mapping a staircase that only existed on Tuesdays, and when I finished, the Tuesday it lived on was cancelled. Cartography of ordinary places is a science. Cartography of impossible ones is a negotiation.

So when I tell you that mapping the fallen wards was the hardest work of my life, believe me. And when I tell you what I found at the center of them—believe me twice as hard, because I would very much prefer to be wrong.

* * *

The wards fell three nights ago with a sound like breaking glass, and everyone in the guild treated it like weather. Ada locked doors. Marrow drifted through the halls muttering about inventory discrepancies. Brannic drank. Ines sat in the corner with her tin box and glared at all of us like we were personally responsible for the stars going out, which, in fairness, one of us might have been.

But I am a cartographer, and cartographers do not treat catastrophe as weather. Catastrophe has edges. Edges can be drawn. And anything that can be drawn can be understood—or at least survived.

The problem was that the wards had never been mapped. Not properly. The guild's founding documents contained a single sheet, older than any of us, showing the wards as a simple ring around the guildhall: a circle, a few sigils, a note in faded ink reading *as agreed*. No dimensions. No anchor points. No terms.

"As agreed," I said aloud, alone in the map room at midnight, tracing those two words with my finger. "Agreed with whom?"

That is the question I should have asked years ago. That is the question every member of this guild should have asked every day since they took their oath. We are the Guild of Impossible Things. We trade in souls and shadows and borrowed years. We keep ledgers of debts that no honest court would enforce. And we never once asked ourselves why nobody ever came to collect.

Until now.

* * *

Here is how you map something that no longer exists: you map its absence.

The wards fell, but they did not vanish. Nothing truly vanishes; it only moves somewhere I haven't been yet. What remained was a shape in the world—a pressure, a negative space, the way your tongue finds the gap where a tooth used to be. I could not see the wards anymore, but I could feel where they had been, the way a riverbed remembers water.

So I went out into the starless dark with my instruments and my ink and my stubbornness, and I walked the guild's borders for two days, feeling for the ghost of the circle.

It was worse than I expected. It was worse than anyone expected.

The wards were not a ring. A ring is what you draw when you're protecting something small. The wards were a lattice—they ran outward from the guildhall in threads, hundreds of them, thin as spider silk and long as rivers, stretching across the whole border country, over the mountains, down into valleys where no guild member had ever set foot. They passed through villages. Through graveyards. Through the foundations of houses where people had made bargains they didn't fully understand.

And every thread terminated somewhere south, beneath the mountain, in the direction where the stars used to be densest and were now most conspicuously missing.

I stood on the ridge above the mill road with my compass spinning uselessly and understood, with the particular horror available only to people who read maps for a living, that we had never been inside the wards.

We had been inside the net.

* * *

I came back and spread my working maps across the great table, and I will admit my hands were not steady. Marrow floated through the wall to see what I was doing—he does that, appears without knocking, as if death revoked his understanding of doors—and he looked at the lattice I'd drawn and went quiet in a way that frightened me more than noise would have.

"I know these lines," he said.

"You've been dead forty years, Marrow. How could you know my lines?"

"Not yours." He drifted closer, and his translucence seemed to deepen, as if remembering something made him less solid. "The court's. Before. When I collected for them." He touched one thread with a finger that left no mark. "This is a claim line. This is an audit trace. This—" his finger stopped on the thickest thread, the one running straight from the guildhall's foundation stones down toward the mountain, "—this is a lien."

"A lien," I repeated. "On what?"

"On everything," Marrow said. "Sefa. Look at the pattern. Claims don't radiate outward like this unless the whole territory was posted as collateral. Someone put up the entire region—all of it, the guild, the towns, the graves, the births—as security against a debt. And the wards weren't keeping raiders out." He looked up at me, and dead men's eyes are terrible things to be looked at by. "They were keeping the creditor out. Keeping the court from collecting until the debt came due."

"And now the wards are down."

"Now the wards are down," Marrow said, "and the stars are out, and if I still worked for them, I'd call this what it is. This isn't an invasion. This is a repossession."

* * *

I did not sleep that night. Instead I did what I always do when the world becomes unbearable: I drew it more precisely.

By dawn I had overlaid the claim lattice against every record the guild possessed—the old ledgers, the contract archives, the founding documents—and I began matching threads to bargains. Each thread anchored to a specific agreement. A farmer who traded twenty years of good harvests for rain during the drought. A midwife who bought back a dying child's fever. A fisherman who sold his shadow to feed his family through a hungry winter. Small bargains. Desperate bargains. The kind the guild exists to broker, or used to, before we told ourselves we were neutral parties.

Every bargain had a counterparty. Every counterparty traced back, through intermediaries and shell-names and dead men's signatures, to the same place. The court beneath the mountain.

Do you understand what I'm telling you? Every impossible thing the guild ever brokered—the mercies, the rescues, the second chances—we thought we were giving gifts. We were extending credit. And the creditor has come to collect on all of it at once.

The girl's brother. The sold soul glowing in her tin box. He is not a tragedy. He is a payment installment. And there are thousands more like him, filed and shelved and humming under the mountain, because the court does not take coin. Coin is fungible, and fungible things can be forgiven. The court takes what cannot be replaced.

* * *

I presented my findings at noon, to the whole strange assembly of us, and I will render the scene honestly even though it does not flatter anyone.

Ada stood at the head of the table with their arms crossed, wearing the face they wear instead of fear. Ines sat beside them, the tin box in front of her like a place setting. Brannic had brought his cup. Marrow hovered near the ceiling, which is where he goes when he feels guilty—I've never asked why, but I suspect the answer involves whatever he did for the court before he deserted.

I unrolled the master map. The lattice sprawled across it like frost.

"The wards fell because the debt came due," I said. "Not because of raiders, not because of sabotage, not because of anything we did last week. The wards were the guild's original collateral arrangement—a stay of collection. As agreed." I tapped the faded words on the founding document. "Someone, a long time ago, borrowed something enormous from the court beneath the mountain, and put up everything within these lines as security. The wards kept the collector away while payments were made. And the payments—" I looked at Ines, and hated myself for it, and did it anyway, "—the payments were souls."

Silence. The bad kind. The kind with weight.

"That's not true," Ines said, but quietly, the way people say things they already know are true. Her hand covered the tin box. "My mother wouldn't have—that wasn't a payment, that was a sale, she signed—"

"Your mother's signature sits on a thread of this lattice," I said gently. "They all do. Every bargain anyone in this country ever struck with the impossible—the threads all run to the same ledger. Your brother isn't lost, Ines. He's *held*. There's a difference, and the difference is that held things can sometimes be reclaimed."

"Then we reclaim him," Ada said. Flat. Immediate. No deliberation, no weighing of costs, which is so perfectly like them that I could have screamed.

"You don't understand the scale," I said, and I pressed my palms flat against the map. "Look at it. Every mercy the guild ever granted is a debt outstanding. Every soul the court holds is collateral against a principal so large that whoever negotiated our founding agreement must have been buying something enormous. Something worth mortgaging the entire border country for. We are not being raided, Ada. We are being *foreclosed on*. And the repossession doesn't stop with one boy's soul. When the court finishes its audit, it collects everything. The harvests that were promised. The years that were borrowed. The deaths that were postponed—" here Brannic set down his cup very carefully, "—and the names, and the memories, and the children, and the dead. All of it. All at once."

"Then we renegotiate," Ada said.

"With what leverage? They hold every note."

"We've broken better contracts than theirs." And they smiled—not the warm smile, the other one, the one that has preceded every disaster I have witnessed in fifteen years at this table. "Sefa. Can you get us to the mountain?"

I looked at the map. At the threads converging southward like veins into a heart. At the pass where the roads remembered too much and the tolls were paid in worse than coin.

"Yes," I said, because I am a coward in every way except the one that matters, and the one that matters is that I cannot leave a map unfinished. "There's a route. It will cost us."

"Everything costs," Ada said. "That's the first rule of the guild."

"No," I said. "The first rule of the guild is that nothing gets bought that shouldn't be owned. We just learned we've been living inside the largest violation of that rule in history. Whatever we do next, we should at least have the decency to know we're doing it wrong."

Ada held my gaze for a long moment. Then they nodded, once, and began rolling up the maps.

* * *

That evening I caught Ines in the corridor outside the map room, sitting on the floor with the tin box open in her lap. The ember burned without heat, casting its lightless glow on her furious little face.

"You knew," she said. "You map people. You must have known the wards were wrong."

"I suspected," I admitted. "Suspecting and knowing are different countries, and I've mapped both."

"What else aren't you telling me?"

I crouched down to her level. My knees objected. Everything about me objects these days; it's how I know I'm still alive.

"There's a thread on that lattice," I said, "thicker than the others, older than the others, running straight out of the guildhall's foundations. It anchors to a bargain no one has ever been able to find in our records. A founding debt. Whoever owes it signed with no name—just a mark, like someone pressing their palm into ash." I watched her face. "Ines, the ember in that box reacted to Ada the moment you walked through our door. I've been wondering ever since whether it recognized a friend."

"Or a debtor," Ines said.

She is eleven years old, and she cut to the truth faster than the Auditor himself could have. I said nothing, which was its own admission, and she closed the tin box with a snap like a small verdict.

At midnight I returned to the map room to check my work, because checking my work is the only prayer I know. And I found something new on the master map—something that had not been there when I rolled it up that afternoon.

A single line, fresh, in ink I did not own, drawn from the guildhall's foundation stones straight south to the mountain. And beside it, in handwriting I almost recognized and could not quite place, a notation in the old accounting script:

*Principal called due. Collection begins.*

Underneath, smaller, almost apologetic:

*One debtor remains unaccounted for.*
