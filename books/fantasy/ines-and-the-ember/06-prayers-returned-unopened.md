## 6. Prayers Returned Unopened

There is a particular kind of silence that comes from a god, and it is not the same as the silence of an empty room. An empty room doesn't know you're in it. A god's silence does.

I stood in the guild's back chapel at the hour when even the mice take inventory of their sins, and I listened to that silence with my whole body, the way you'd listen for a footstep behind you on a dark road. The chapel was small and unlovely—a converted storeroom with a shelf where an altar should be, a candle stub, a bowl of water for washing debts clean. Veth had never wanted grandeur. Veth was the god of the unpaid and the unforgiven, the patron saint of IOUs scrawled on bar napkins, and its houses were always built like afterthoughts.

I hadn't prayed in six years. Not since the night I signed my name into the guild ledger and felt something in my chest go quiet, like a door closing on a warm room. Gods don't require prayer, Veth had told me once—or rather, hadn't told me, because Veth communicated mostly in the grammar of things going slightly less badly than they should have. But they notice it. And I had stopped offering mine the way you stop writing to a relative who never writes back: not out of anger, exactly, but out of a pride so brittle it masquerades as indifference.

Now there was a girl asleep two floors above me with a tin box under her arm, and inside that box was a soul that burned without heat, and somewhere beneath a mountain a court of the dead held the deed to it, paid for—according to our own books—with something I once lost. And the god whose ledger I kept had gone silent as a stone at the bottom of a well.

So I knelt. Knees on cold flagstone, hands folded the way my mother taught me before she taught me anything useful, like knife work and contract law. I felt ridiculous. I want that on the record. Even alone, especially alone, I felt like a merchant bowing to an empty stall.

"Veth," I said.

The word came out rusty. Six years of rust, flaking off in my mouth.

Nothing. Of course nothing. The candle didn't gutter. The water in the bowl didn't tremble. Somewhere in the walls, the guild house settled its old bones and went on ignoring me, which was more or less what it did best.

I tried again, because stubbornness is the only prayer I've ever been good at.

"I don't know if you can hear this. I don't know if you could ever hear this, or if I spent twenty years talking to a habit." I stared at the candle flame, because it's easier to address a flame than a void. "But there's a girl upstairs. Eleven years old. She walked through our wards like they were cobwebs carrying her brother in a biscuit tin, and she thinks we're going to fix it because we're the Guild of Impossible Things, and I let her think it."

The silence sat there, patient as a creditor.

"And I need to know—" My voice caught on something. I pushed past it. "I need to know if you're still *there*. If the ledger still balances on your end. Because tomorrow I'm going to break every law we have, and I'd like to do it with authorization. Even silent authorization. Even just... a feeling. A nudge. One of your famous almost-coincidences."

Nothing.

"You went quiet," I said, and heard the accusation creep into it despite myself, "the same night the stars went out. The same night the wards fell. And I'm not a cartographer, but I can do arithmetic. Whatever took the stars, whatever's collecting out there—it happened on your watch. So either you're gone, or you're hiding, or you're *held*, and all three of those mean the same thing to me, which is that I'm about to do something insane without my god."

The candle burned. The water lay flat. The silence had texture now, a weight, like snow on a roof.

I laughed, short and bitter, and it echoed wrong in the little room.

"You know what the worst part is? I used to feel you. Not hear—feel. Like a hand on the back of my neck when a deal was about to go bad. Like the specific mercy of a debt forgiven quietly, no one the wiser. Twenty years of that. And then one day it stopped, and I told myself you were testing me, and then I told myself you were busy, and then I stopped telling myself anything, because that's what I do. I keep accounts. I don't chase deadbeats." I rubbed my face with both hands. "Even when the deadbeat is a god."

I got up. My knees cracked in protest—I was forty-one years old and kneeling on stone, and some prices you pay immediately. I blew out the candle, and in the dark I said the last part, the part I hadn't planned, the part that came out low and fast like something slipping off a high shelf:

"If you're being held—if someone has you down there the way that court has souls down there—then hold on. Because I'm coming. Not as your servant. As your bookkeeper. And I am *going* to find the discrepancy."

And the ember flared.

Not the candle—the ember. Upstairs, two floors up, through old timber and plaster, light bloomed against the ceiling of the corridor outside Ines's room, a warm orange pulse like a heartbeat seen through skin. I stood frozen in the chapel doorway watching it, and it pulsed again, brighter, and faded slow, like something exhaling.

Like something listening.

"Oh," I said, to nobody, to everybody, to the ember and the empty chapel and the god-shaped hole in the middle of both. "Oh, that's not good."

Because here's the thing about a soul burning in a tin box: it shouldn't hear you. It shouldn't answer. It certainly shouldn't lean toward your voice like a plant toward a window. Souls sold to the court below are supposed to sleep until they're filed. This one was awake, and it had heard me say *hold on*, and it had answered the only way it could.

It had believed me.

That, more than the fallen stars, more than the glowing ledger entry, more than any of it, was the moment I understood the shape of what I'd taken on. Not a contract. Not a quest. A child's brother, awake in the dark, trusting a stranger's voice because it was the only voice that had spoken kindly into his night in who knew how long.

I climbed the stairs slowly, telling myself I was just checking on her, knowing perfectly well I was doing no such thing.

* * *

She wasn't asleep. Of course she wasn't asleep. Eleven-year-olds whose brothers live in boxes do not sleep; they lie in the dark conducting surveillance.

I found her sitting up in the narrow guild bed, the tin box open on her knees, the ember's glow painting her face gold from below so she looked like a gargoyle in a cathedral, if gargoyles were furious and undersized. She'd heard everything. I knew it before she opened her mouth, the way you know rain is coming by the smell of the air.

"You talked to him," she said.

"Ines—"

"Not to him." She pointed at the floor, at the chapel below us, her finger trembling with the effort of staying pointed instead of jabbing. "To *your* god. You talked to your god and he didn't answer, and then you talked to *Tam*, and Tam answered. Tam answered *you*." Her voice cracked down the middle. "He hasn't answered me in three weeks. Three weeks! I talk to him every night and he just sits there, and you come down and say *hold on* one time and he lights up like—like a festival lamp!"

The injustice of it was enormous and eleven years old and completely correct. I stood in the doorway and took it, because there was nowhere to put it down.

"Ines," I said carefully, "souls aren't—"

"Don't." She was crying now, furiously, the way children cry when they've decided crying is a tactical error but their body has overruled them. "Don't explain him to me. You don't get to explain him to me. You people—" she swept her arm around the room, the guild room, the shelves of impossible collateral, the whole crooked edifice of us, "—you *keep* things. That's what you do. You keep other people's things in drawers and ledgers and you write numbers next to them, and my mother signed something because she was scared and dying and they told her it would protect us, and everyone said it was fine, everyone said the Guild of Impossible Things keeps things *safe*—"

"We do keep things safe," I said. "That's literally the charter."

"Then where were you?" It came out raw. "Where were you three weeks ago? Where were you when the men came with the paper? Where were you when Mama died? Where was your god?" She hugged the tin box to her chest, and the ember glowed through the gaps in her fingers, steady, patient, horribly alive. "Everyone abandons everyone. That's the whole world. Mama abandoned us by dying. The neighbors abandoned us by pretending we weren't there. And your god abandoned *you*, I heard you say it, six years, you said six years—and you're still here. Still keeping his books. Still lighting his stupid candle." She wiped her nose with her sleeve, viciously. "Why?"

And there it was. The question I'd been not-asking myself for six years, delivered by a child in a borrowed nightshirt, with the precision of an auditor.

I could have lied to her. I'm good at lying; it's most of contract law. I could have given her the guild answer, the polished one: *faith is a practice, not a transaction.* I could have given her the mercenary answer: *because he pays.* Instead I sat down on the edge of the opposite bed, which groaned under me like it resented the assignment, and I told her the truth, because she'd earned it, and because bitter honesty was apparently the only currency either of us had left tonight.

"Because abandoning him wouldn't make it hurt less," I said. "Believe me. I considered it. Professionally. I drew up the paperwork in my head—resignation, severance, the whole thing. And then I thought about what I'd actually be resigning *from*, and it wasn't a job. It was the only relationship in my life where somebody asked nothing of me. Do you understand how strange that is? Everyone wants something. The guild wants my hours. My clients want my discretion. The world wants me invisible. And this god—this quiet, useless, absent god—looked at everything I owed, everything I'd done, everything I was, and forgave it. No interest. No terms. Just..." I made a vague gesture, because the word had rusted too. "*Grace.*"

"That's not an answer," Ines said.

"No," I agreed. "It isn't. Here's the actual answer: I don't know why I stay. Maybe because leaving would mean admitting the silence means what I'm afraid it means. Maybe because a bookkeeper doesn't abandon a ledger mid-audit. Or maybe—" and this was the part that cost me, the part I said looking at the ember rather than at her, "—maybe because when I was small, someone stayed for me when they had no reason to, and it saved my life, and I've been trying to pay that forward ever since, badly, to a god who won't confirm receipt."

Ines stared at me. The fury in her face was doing complicated arithmetic, subtracting years, adding context, arriving at sums she didn't like.

"You're scared," she said finally. Not an accusation. Worse—an observation.

"Terrified," I said. "Since the stars went out. Since before that, honestly. Fear is mostly what I'm made of, held together with bookkeeping."

"Tam's scared too," she whispered, and looked down at the box, and her thumb moved over the lid in a small circular motion, unconscious, habitual, the ghost of a gesture from when the lid had been a forehead and the motion a comfort. "He gets scared of the dark. He's eleven too. He was eleven." Her voice wobbled. "When I talked to him just now—after you left—he felt... warmer. Like he heard something. Did you really say *hold on*?"

"I did."

"To your god. About him."

"To my god. About him. About you. About all of it." I leaned forward, elbows on knees. "Ines, I need you to hear something, and I need you to believe it even though you have every reason not to. I have broken contracts for clients. I have honored them when I shouldn't have. I have watched people sell things they didn't know they owned and buy back things they should have left lost. In twenty years, I have never once seen love survive a court's paperwork." I met her eyes. "Tomorrow, we're going to try anyway. Not because I believe we'll win. Because your brother heard someone say *hold on*, and he believed it, and I will not be the second person tonight who breaks faith with him."

The ember pulsed. Once. Soft. Like a hand squeezing back.

Ines looked at it, and then at me, and something in her face shifted—not trust, not yet, nothing so generous—but a decision to suspend disbelief, the way you do with a bridge you have to cross anyway.

"If you abandon me," she said, "I will find you. I found the guild, didn't I? Nobody finds the guild."

"Nobody finds the guild," I agreed. "It's in the charter."

"Good." She lay back down, pulling the blanket up, tucking the tin box beside her pillow with the tenderness of someone tucking in a living thing, which, God help us, it was. "Then we understand each other."

We did. It was the clearest contract I'd signed in years, and neither of us had written a word of it down.

I stood to leave, and at the door I paused, because there was one more true thing and it seemed cruel to withhold it.

"Ines. What I said downstairs—about being held. About someone having my god the way the court has your brother." I waited until she looked at me. "If I'm right, then this isn't two separate problems. It's one problem wearing two faces. And I've been reading our oldest ledgers since midnight, and there's a pattern in the old acquisitions, payments made in things that shouldn't be payable, entries that trail off toward a mountain. Tomorrow I'm convening the debtors' table. Marrow. Sefa. Brannic. The strangest collection of failures this side of the border."

"They'll help?" she asked.

I thought of Marrow's hollow patience, Sefa's moving maps, Brannic's postponed death, and the particular loneliness of asking people to break the only rules that keep them safe.

"No," I said. "Probably not. But I'm going to ask anyway. Turns out that's the family business—asking for impossible things."

Down in the tin box, soft as a banked fire, the ember glowed on. Listening. Waiting. Holding on.

And far beneath us, beneath the starless sky and the sleeping mountain, something else held on too—in the dark, in the chains, in the silence that was not emptiness but captivity—waiting for a bookkeeper to find the discrepancy.

I blew out her candle and went to draft the agenda.
